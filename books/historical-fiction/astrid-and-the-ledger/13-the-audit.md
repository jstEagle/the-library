## 13. The Audit

The auditor came eleven days early.

Elias learned of it the way he learned most things in Broome — secondhand, half-truthed, delivered by a man with no interest in whether the truth survived the telling. A Malay deckhand named Yusuf, whose brother worked the Meridian stores, caught Elias at the foot of the customs stairs just after dawn and said, breathless, as though the words were hot: "They're burning paper at the station, tuan. Big fire. Smoke you can see from the point."

"Burning what?"

"Everything." Yusuf shrugged, and the shrug said more than his English could: *what does it matter, it is not our paper.* "Mr. Calloway's man come on the morning boat. No notice. He go straight to the office and they start carrying out boxes before the tide turn."

Elias climbed the stairs two at a time.

From the customs office window — the one with the warped shutter that never closed flush, through which he had spent four years watching the comings and goings of a town built on shells and silence — he could see it plainly. A column of smoke rose from behind the Meridian administration building, thin and white and orderly, not the black chaos of an accident but the deliberate grey of a planned fire. Even at this distance he could make out figures feeding it. Two men at the boxes, one at the flames, one standing apart with his hands clasped behind his back, watching the water instead of the fire.

That one would be the auditor.

Elias had met auditors before. They came twice a year, punctual as the mail, stayed three days, dined at the Residency, signed whatever the company put under their pens, and left on the next boat. They were ritual, not scrutiny. The whole coast understood this. An audit was a ceremony performed for the benefit of shareholders in London who would never smell a shell heap or see a diver come up bleeding from the ear.

But this was different. This was early, unannounced, and accompanied by fire.

He took down his hat and went out.

* * *

The road along the foreshore was soft with last night's rain, and Elias walked it fast, his boots throwing up red mud, his mind moving faster than his feet.

An audit ahead of schedule meant only one thing in his experience: someone above had reason to believe the books below would not bear waiting. Either money had gone missing — possible; Calloway skimmed like every overseer since Cain — or something worse had gone *visible*, and the company wanted the loose ends ash before any official eye arrived.

And there was only one loose end Elias knew of that mattered.

He thought of Gideon. He thought of her constantly now, in the way of a man carrying something breakable through a crowd — always aware of it, always adjusting his elbows. Three weeks since she had shown him that single page in the dark of the net shed, her candle shielded by her cupped hand, and he had seen the inspector's signature — *R. Halloway, Inspector of Shipping* — written neat as a prayer across a line recording the purchase of nine children from a station up the coast. Halloway. His superior. The man who signed Elias's leave passes and drank at his table and had once, drunk, told him the secret of advancement on this coast: *"Sign what you're given, boy, and ask what things cost later, when you can afford the answer."*

Three weeks of covert meetings in the net shed and behind the church and once, recklessly, on the beach below the telegraph line where anyone with a glass might have seen them. Three weeks of planning a route past the third checkpoint. And now fire.

At the gate of the Meridian compound a Kanaka watchman barred the way with a boar spear held more from habit than conviction. "No entry, boss. Audit."

"I'm customs," Elias said. "I have statutory right of inspection of company records pertaining to shipping."

The watchman looked at him with mild, unblinking patience, the look of a man who has been told many rules by many whites and has learned that all of them dissolve at the same word: *no*. Behind him, past the gate, the smoke rose straight into a windless sky. Somewhere beyond the office block, women's voices carried from the shell-sorting sheds — ordinary sounds, work continuing around the fire as though it were a funeral no one would name.

"Wait here, boss," the watchman said, which meant *go away slowly*, and went inside to fetch someone who outranked both of them.

While he waited, Elias did what he had trained himself to do over four years of watching without appearing to watch: he counted. Two men at the fire, as Yusuf had said. One of them he recognized — Prosser, the storekeeper, a small damp man who sweated even in dry heat and who had once wept at his own wedding, according to town gossip, from relief. Prosser fed papers to the flames with the mechanical diligence of a man shoveling coal. The other was younger, harder, dressed in clothes too good for Broome: grey serge, a stiff collar unstained by the climate, a watch chain that caught the light when he moved. Not local. Not coastal. That would be the auditor himself, or the auditor's knife.

The one standing apart, watching the water, had turned slightly, and Elias saw his face. Heavy jaw, sun-reddened, the expression of a man perpetually smelling something off. Calloway. The overseer himself, attending his own audit's bonfire like a mourner who had chosen cremation.

Then Gideon came around the corner of the sorting shed, a basket of shells on her hip, walking the well-worn path from the sheds toward the washing troughs — and everything in Elias went still.

She did not look at the gate. She did not look at him. That alone told him how much she knew, because Gideon Eriksen missed nothing, and a woman who missed nothing and did not look was a woman performing non-looking with her whole body. Her stride was even, her head appropriately bowed, her face arranged into the flat endurance he had first mistaken for peace and now understood was armor. Only her eyes moved — one flick, half a second, gate-fire-auditor-gate — and then down again at her shells, and she passed within ten yards of him as though he were a fence post.

She was counting. He could nearly hear it, the way she counted breaths underwater, the way she counted everything: the men at the fire, the boxes remaining, the distance between herself and the smoke.

The watchman returned with a clerk who informed Elias, with great courtesy and zero content, that all records were presently undergoing internal reconciliation and that Mr. Halloway's office would be notified if customs assistance became necessary.

*Halloway's office.* So the inspector knew. Of course he knew. The morning boat had brought the auditor, and the morning boat's manifest passed through Halloway's desk before it touched anyone else's. Which meant this fire was not merely sanctioned from Perth. It was choreographed there.

"Tell your Mr. Calloway," Elias said pleasantly, "that customs will require certification of destroyed shipping documents. It's a formality. We do it for every firm."

The clerk's smile did not move. "I'll pass along your regards, Mr. Ward."

Elias tipped his hat to the watchman and walked back along the foreshore, unhurried, a man with nowhere particular to be, while inside his chest something ticked like a clock dropped down a well.

* * *

He found her that evening at the washing troughs behind the divers' barracks, where the women rinsed the sorting rags and where, by long unspoken custom, no overseer came after dark because the smell of brine offended them. She was alone. Nell sat in the sand nearby, solemnly filling a tin with shells and emptying it again, the great project of her second year of life.

"The auditor's name is Merrick," Gideon said, without turning. News traveled fast through the barracks; it always had. "He came up from Fremantle on the *Kestrel*. He burned papers from midday till the tide. Sefu says he burned for six hours."

"Sefu's back?" Sefu had kept his distance lately, ever since the night he'd guessed what Gideon carried and sworn to help — a guess Elias had been carefully not confirming for weeks, knowing knowledge was dangerous to its holder.

"He carries water to the sheds. He sees everything." She wrung a rag, and water ran grey into the sand. "Six hours, Elias. Do you know how much paper a company burns in six hours?"

"Yes."

"Then you know what I know." She turned at last. In the failing light her face was all planes and shadow, and her voice was level in the way he'd come to dread, because it was the levelness she used when the thing underneath was anything but. "Whatever copies existed are gone. Whatever originals were loose are ash. Everything now depends on—" She stopped herself, glancing at Nell, and finished with her hands, pressing them flat against her own chest, against the place where for months a slim ledger had lived its hidden life between hiding places.

"The original," Elias said quietly.

"The original. Which I have sewn into a diving suit lining inside a sealed oilskin, buried in a cache among the shell heaps that floods at spring tide so nothing will dig there." She said it flatly, reciting the inventory of her own survival. "And which is now, as of today, the only copy in existence of evidence that could hang half the company's directors and free four hundred people. Do you understand what that makes it?"

"Lethal."

"It makes it *everything*. To them and to us." She knelt suddenly and pulled Nell into her lap, and the girl went willingly, sucking her thumb, one hand closing in Gideon's collar with the absolute trust of the very young. Gideon looked down at her and Elias watched the tenderness and the arithmetic happen together in her face, the two operations she could no longer perform separately. "Merrick didn't come early to count beans. Someone told Perth something was loose. Maybe the informer. Maybe Calloway guessed. Maybe Halloway simply wants the ground cleared before the quarterly audit makes him sign things in front of witnesses. It doesn't matter which. What matters is that they're frightened enough to burn their own records, and frightened men don't stop at paper."

"You think they'll start looking harder."

"I think they've already started." She lifted her chin toward the barracks. "Calloway posted a reward notice this afternoon. For information regarding 'missing company property.' Five pounds." A short laugh with nothing in it. "Five pounds is two months' diving wages. He's buying eyes."

* * *

Elias walked home along the beach because he needed the walk, the long cold slap of the shallows against his boots, the darkness gathering over the water where the pearling fleet rode at anchor with their riding lights strung out like a low constellation.

His father had taught him to read manifests at a kitchen table in Fremantle, thirty years ago, before the ruin. Before the bank took the house and the shipping line took the license and the family name became a cautionary tale told in other men's kitchens. *Every ship tells the truth somewhere, Eli,* his father used to say, tapping a ledger line with a cracked forefinger. *Cargo in must equal cargo out, or the difference is a story somebody's paying to keep untold.*

Elias had built his career on that sentence and betrayed it nightly, signing what he was given. But in the last three weeks, since the page in the net shed, the old arithmetic had started screaming at him. Cargo in must equal cargo out. Nine children purchased at a northern station; how many landed at Broome? He had pulled the manifests twice in the past fortnight, carefully, one folio at a time, re-shelving each exactly. The numbers did not close. They never closed. Four children unaccounted for across eighteen months, entered as "transferred" with no receiving station named — and transfer entries initialed, all of them, by R. Halloway.

Now the fire. Now the reward. Now the clock.

Because here was the thing about an audit that burns its own records: it establishes a date. After today, any document dated before the fire that surfaces anywhere becomes impossible to dismiss as clerical error. The company had, in its fear, authenticated its own guilt in advance. Every entry Gideon carried was now, retroactively, the thing they had tried to destroy — and courts understood destruction of records better than they understood almost anything.

Which made the book in the shell heap more valuable than it had been yesterday.

And more lethal. Because a company that would burn its own archives in daylight would do considerably more, in darkness, to a customs officer and a diver's daughter standing between it and a hanging offense.

He stopped at the tideline and stood a long moment looking at the anchored lights. Somewhere out there, past the channel, lay the route she would have to dive — the old channel, the one the charts marked disused, silted, treacherous. He hadn't known until last week that she was planning to swim it. She had told him the way she told him everything, flatly, already decided, offering him not consultation but the courtesy of knowledge. As if he were another checkpoint to be passed rather than a man who loved her.

He was going to have to insist on coming. Or going in his stead. Or — no. He knew her. She would go, or no one would, and the book would rot in the shell heaps until a spring tide or a rat found it, and four hundred names would stay buried with it.

A bell began ringing from the direction of the telegraph office — the night bell, rung only when a priority wire came through outside business hours. Then a second bell, answering from the Residency. Elias turned. Down the beach, a lantern was bobbing toward the jetty, and men were shouting softly, the particular hushed urgency of officials summoned at night.

He walked toward the lights with his heart ticking faster, and reached the jetty foot in time to see the telegraph clerk hurrying past with a flimsy copy in his fist, and to hear — called from one man to another along the boards, passed like a live coal — the news that had come down the wire from Perth:

The quarterly audit was cancelled. Cancelled and replaced. A commissioner was being sent north on the next steamer with full powers of inquiry into "irregularities in the pearl-shell trade."

Someone in Perth was frightened too. Someone had talked.

Elias stood on the jetty in the wind coming off the water and understood, with terrible clarity, that the race had changed shape. It was no longer a smuggler's game against checkpoints and seasons. The steamer would arrive in nine days. The company had eight days to find its loose paper, and one diver's daughter standing between them and it.

Behind him, far up the dark beach, a child's voice rang out thin and clear from the barracks — Nell, laughing at something, the sound carrying impossibly over the water.

Eight days.
