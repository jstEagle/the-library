## 14. Nell Taken

The summons came at first light, delivered by a boy too young to know what he was carrying.

"Overseer Brack wants the child brought up to the company house," he recited, staring at his boots. "For care. He says she's been looking poorly."

Gideon stood in the doorway of her barracks room with Nell on her hip, warm and heavy with sleep, one fist curled against Gideon's collarbone. The morning was grey and windless, the kind of stillness that came before heat, and the boy's words sat in the air between them like something dead.

"She's not poorly," Gideon said.

The boy shrugged. He was maybe ten, barefoot, already wearing the flat look of someone who had learned that messages were messages and questions were trouble. "He says to bring her before the tide turns. He says—" the boy's voice dropped into an imitation of Brack's gravel, and it was good enough to make Gideon's stomach tighten— "*the company looks after its own.*"

He turned and trotted off across the compound, and Gideon stood there with Nell breathing against her neck and thought: *they know.*

Not everything. Not yet. But they knew the child mattered, and that was worse than anything.

* * *

She had known it was coming for days, if she was honest. She had felt it the way divers feel a change in the water before any sign shows — pressure shifting somewhere below the surface of things.

It started with small eyes. The cook watching Nell eat her breakfast in the barracks kitchen and asking, too casually, whose brat that was supposed to be. A tally-clerk pausing outside her door longer than a man had reason to. Sefu's warning, three nights ago, spoken low while they mended nets: *Brack asked me where you go on your free Sundays. Asked who minds the girl when you dive.* And then yesterday, the worst of it — Brack himself, crossing the compound, stopping within arm's reach of her, looking down at Nell playing in the dust with a shell, and saying nothing at all. Just looking. That long, weighing look, like a man appraising a pearl and calculating what it would fetch.

A child abandoned on a diver's doorstep during a storm was charity. Charity was tolerated, even admired, in the way the company admired anything that cost it nothing. But a child that made a woman careful — a woman who had never been careful in four years of indenture — that was different. That was a lever waiting for a hand.

Gideon carried Nell inside and set her down among the blankets and began, very calmly, to think about running.

There was nowhere to run. She turned the map of the coast over in her mind the way she'd turn a shell in her fingers, looking for the flaw, the opening. North: mangrove swamp and no water a child could drink. South: the road checkpoint, watched day and night since the audit rumors started. West: open sea, and she was strong but she was not strong enough to swim a toddler through shark waters to the pearling luggers anchored beyond the jetty. East: the company house itself, and Brack standing in it like a spider in the mouth of his web.

Every direction ended the same way. With her caught, or with Nell caught, or with both of them face-down in the shallows and the ledger rotting under a shell heap, having freed no one.

Nell tugged at her skirt and held up the shell. "Boat," she said. It was her word for everything round and pale. Gideon picked her up instead of answering, because if she spoke she would have to keep her voice level, and she did not trust herself to do it.

* * *

She went to see Sefu at the midday break, when the divers sat in the strip of shade behind the drying sheds and the overseer's men were far enough away to make low talk possible.

Sefu was older than her by fifteen years, a Malayan-Moorish man with hands scarred white from salt, and he was the closest thing to family the station had left her. He looked up when she came, read her face in one glance, and moved down the bench so she could sit.

"He's taking her," Gideon said. "Today. Before the tide turns."

"For care." Sefu said the word the way you'd spit out a fish bone.

"I could run tonight. Take the dinghy from the north rack—"

"They count the dinghies." He said it gently, which made it worse. "You'd be reported missing by midnight, and the girl with you. And then whatever you're carrying, girl, they stop looking for it quietly and start looking for it loudly, and loud is the one thing we cannot afford." He glanced toward the sheds. "How much longer?"

"A week. Maybe less. Elias says Corrigan will hear it once the circuit sits."

"Then you hold. You hold and you give them nothing new to look at." Sefu took her wrist, his grip dry and hard as driftwood. "Listen to me. Brack wants something from you or he'd have taken the child already without asking. Asking is a test. He wants to see what you do. So you do the ordinary thing. You walk her up there yourself, you thank him for his kindness, you kiss her cheek, and you walk away like a woman going back to work. Nothing in your face. Nothing in your hands."

"And if he doesn't give her back?"

Sefu didn't answer, and the not-answer told her everything.

* * *

She walked Nell up to the company house at half past two, because the tide turned at three and she would not give Brack the satisfaction of lateness.

The house sat on the rise above the compound, wide-verandaed and cool-looking, built of imported timber the divers' wages had paid for plank by plank without ever touching. Frangipani trees lined the path. Someone swept it every morning. Gideon had lived four years within sight of this path and had never once been invited up it.

Brack met her on the veranda. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with a red neck and small patient eyes, and he wore his authority the way other men wore sweat — easily, constantly, without shame.

"Ah. The little one." He crouched with a grunt and put out a hand toward Nell, who pressed her face into Gideon's shoulder. "Shy. That's all right. We'll soon fix that." He straightened. "Wife'll look after her. Good food, clean bed. Better than a barracks, eh? Company looks after its own."

"You're kind, Mr. Brack," Gideon said, and heard her own voice come out flat and grateful and stupid, exactly as Sefu had drilled it.

"Kindness costs nothing." His small eyes stayed on her face while he said it, and she understood that every word from here to the gate would have a second meaning, and that he would be reading hers for the same. "Mind you, a child's a responsibility. Wandering about, picking things up. Saying things." He smiled. "Children say things, don't they. Whatever they hear."

The floor of the world tilted very slightly, and Gideon kept her feet.

"She doesn't say much," Gideon said. "She's slow with her words."

"That so." He held out his arms. "Give her here, then."

And this was the moment — she knew it even as she lived it — this was the moment the whole thing balanced on. She could refuse. She could clutch Nell and make a scene and confirm in front of the wife and the houseboy that this child was worth making a scene over. Or she could let go.

She let go.

Nell cried — one short, outraged wail, then a bewildered silence as Brack swung her up onto his hip with a practiced ease that told Gideon he had handled children before, taken them from mothers before, and she filed that knowledge away in the place where she kept the ledger's names, another entry in the account. Nell looked back over the big man's shoulder, lips trembling, and Gideon smiled at her. Made herself smile, wide and easy, and wave her fingers the way she did at bedtime.

"Be good," she said. "I'll come tomorrow."

Brack's wife appeared in the doorway, a thin woman with a tired face, and took the child inside without meeting Gideon's eyes. The door closed. The frangipani scent was suddenly unbearable, sweet and thick as something rotting.

"Tomorrow," Brack agreed pleasantly. "Or whenever suits. No hurry." He walked her to the top of the steps and stood there while she descended, and she felt his gaze on her back the whole way down the path, past the frangipanis, through the gate, across the compound, all the way to the barracks door, which she closed behind her before her legs gave out and she sat down hard on the cot.

Her arms ached. Fourteen months of carrying a child, and her arms ached with the weight of not carrying her.

* * *

Night came, and with it the arithmetic.

She lit the candle stub and did not touch the ledger — it lay wrapped in oilskin under the third row of shell heaps, exactly where she'd cached it after the informer's report, and she did not go near it, because near was how people got caught. Instead she sat on the cot and did sums, because sums were all she had.

One ledger. Two hundred and eleven names. One magistrate, seven days out. One child, fourteen hundred yards away, asleep or not asleep in a strange bed in a house built on their drowned fathers' wages.

The trade was simple. That was the obscenity of it — how simple. She could walk up the path tomorrow and say: *give me the girl and I'll tell you where the book is.* Brack would take the bargain in a heartbeat; she had watched him weigh the ledger's existence for weeks now, seen the fear under the patience. A burning barrel, a quiet morning, and the company's whole rotten account settled in ash. Freedom papers for her, maybe. Silence guaranteed by the only law that ever enforced anything out here, which was mutual ruin.

Two hundred and eleven names against one child who called every round white thing a boat.

She tried to hold the ledger in her mind — her father's handwriting marching down the columns, the dates, the payments, the deaths entered as losses the way you'd enter spoiled cargo. *J. Eriksen, lost at sea, compensation nil.* Her father, who had written the truth in secret and died for it, who had left it to wash up at her door wrapped around a living child. If she traded it, she traded every name in it. She traded Sefu, who had said *hold* and meant *I'll pay for your holding*. She traded the women in the barracks who had taught her which breaths to count. She traded the ones not yet sold, the children listed in the back pages under transfers, prices beside their ages.

But Nell.

Here was the thing she could not get past, the reef that wrecked every calculation: the ledger recorded what had been done. Nell *was* what had been done. The daughter of Ada Moreau, diver, who had tried to testify and been transferred to a station she never reached. The last entry in Ada's line, in her father's hand: *one infant, disposition pending.* Disposition pending. As if the child were cargo awaiting a buyer. And Gideon had been the disposition, fourteen months of it, and she loved the girl with the whole blunt animal force of her body, and love did not appear anywhere in the columns, because love was the one entry the company had never learned to price.

Survival. She had built four years on that word. Keep your head down, count your breaths, survive. And survival, she saw now, sitting alone in the candle-dark with her empty arms, survival was just the company's arithmetic wearing her face. Survival said: one child for two hundred names, clear profit, walk away. Survival said: you can't save everyone, save yourself. Survival had a column for everything and a total at the bottom, and the total was always the same — them.

Her father hadn't survived. Her father had *written*, and died, and the writing had outlived him and washed up on her step. There was a kind of arithmetic he'd understood that she was only now learning, the kind where you didn't count what you'd lose but what you'd refuse to surrender.

Still. Still. The candle burned low, and the trade sat there in the dark with her, patient as Brack, and she could not swear she wouldn't make it. That was the truth she arrived at near dawn, the truth that frightened her more than the overseer did: if it came to Nell's skin against the paper, she did not know — she honestly did not know — which way she'd break.

She needed it not to come to that. Which meant she needed the ledger out of the shell heaps and into Corrigan's hands before Brack decided to stop asking and start searching.

Seven days. Maybe less.

She pinched out the candle and lay down, and in the grey before sunrise she heard the compound wake — the shed doors, the gulls, the bell — and beneath it all, faint from up the hill, a thin sound that might have been a gull and might have been a child crying, and she lay with her fists clenched at her sides and counted her breaths until it stopped mattering whether she'd heard it at all.

At the gate, on her way to the boats, the tally-clerk handed her a folded note. Brack's hand, block letters, no courtesy in it:

*The child settles in well. She'll stay as long as need be. Come and see me when you're ready to talk business.*

Gideon read it twice, folded it small, and put it in her pocket next to her heart, where it weighed more than the ledger ever had.

Business. He'd named the price of admission, and given her six days to decide what a child was worth — and somewhere south of the compound, wrapped in oilskin under a million shells, the answer lay buried, waiting for her to dig it up or leave it in the ground.
