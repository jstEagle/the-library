## 15. The Bargain Refused

She waited three days before she went to him. Not because she needed the time to decide — she'd decided the moment Brack's note touched her hand — but because a woman who comes running looks like a woman with something to lose, and Gideon had spent four years teaching herself to look like a woman with nothing at all.

On the third morning she walked up the shell path to the company house in her good dress, the one without salt stains at the hem, and let the tally-clerk announce her like a visitor. The absurdity of it almost made her smile. Nineteen years old, indentured, property in all but the paper they hadn't bothered to write — and shown into the overseer's office as though she'd come about an account.

Brack sat behind his desk with the window at his back, so that the light came off Roebuck Bay and put his face half in shadow. He was a careful man. He'd chosen the chair's position the way he chose everything: so that other people squinted and he did not.

"Miss Eriksen." He gestured at the chair across from him. "Sit down."

She sat. She folded her hands. She let the silence do its work.

He studied her for a long moment, the way he might study a shell of uncertain grade, turning her over in his mind before committing to a price. Then he opened a drawer and laid two documents on the blotter, side by side, squared to its edges.

"You know what these are," he said.

She looked. She didn't touch them. Indemnity papers — the kind Meridian issued perhaps once a year, when a diver's lungs gave out and the company preferred gratitude to litigation. Freedom papers. A name filled in, a seal, a signature. Except the name line on the first one was blank, waiting for ink.

"And the second?" she asked, though she knew.

"An affidavit." He turned it toward her with one finger. "Standard form. Sworn statement that you found no document of any kind on your doorstep in March of this year. That any rumor of such a document is malicious invention. Signed, witnessed, filed — and forgotten by everyone including you."

"And the child."

"Returns to your keeping the same day." He said it smoothly, but she caught the seam in it, the place where the words had been rehearsed until they lay flat. "The girl's mother is dead, her father unrecorded. Legally she's nobody. The company has been charitable. Charity can end, or it can be made permanent. Your choice."

*Your choice.* As if there had ever been a choice. As if Nell had not slept against her ribs every night since March, hot as a coal, gripping her collarbone in her sleep with fingers that knew exactly what they were holding.

Gideon looked at the papers and did the arithmetic Brack wanted her to do. Papers against paper. One signature against another. Her whole life weighed against a book of names — and on the surface of it, any sensible person would sign. Any sensible person would take freedom, take the child, take the boat south and never say the word *ledger* again as long as they lived.

But Brack had made one error, and it was the same error every man at Meridian made, because they had all learned their arithmetic from the same teacher. He believed the ledger was leverage. A thing to be traded. He thought it contained information.

It didn't. It contained people.

"May I ask a question first?" she said.

Brack's mouth tightened a fraction. He had expected weeping, or bargaining, or at minimum a counter-offer. Questions were not part of the script. "Ask."

"The man who wrote that ledger. Anani Eriksen. My father. What did you offer him?"

Something moved behind Brack's eyes — not guilt; she doubted he had any left — but recalculation. The moment when a trader realizes the goods are not what he was told.

"That has no bearing—"

"He kept the books for eleven years," Gideon said. Her voice stayed level. She was surprised by that. "Eleven years of names and payments and losses. And then one day he tried to testify, and three weeks later he went down in company waters with a fouled line, and the loss column gained one entry. So I'm asking what you offered him. Because whatever it was, it must have been generous. And it must have failed. And I'd very much like to know why you think yours will do better."

The room was quiet enough that she could hear the bay through the window — water slapping pilings, the far cry of a boat crew counting shells. Brack's hands came together on the desk.

"Your father was a fool," he said. "He had a position most men would kill for. Trust. Wages. He threw it away for—" he flicked two fingers, dismissive, "—conscience. Conscience doesn't keep a roof on. It doesn't feed a child. You of all people should understand that. You've kept your head down four years. Four years, Eriksen, and never a word of trouble out of you. Don't tell me you've changed your nature over some old book."

And there it was — the thing he could not see, standing in front of him wearing her face. He thought her silence had been loyalty. He thought the stillness of the water meant there was nothing underneath.

"My father taught me to count breaths," she said. "Before every dive. You count them so you know how many you have. So you don't go down greedy and come up dead." She stood. "I've counted mine, Mr. Brack. I know exactly how many I have left, and I know what they're for."

She left the papers where they lay.

Behind her, just before the door closed, Brack said, quietly, without heat — which was worse than any threat — "Think hard tonight. Tomorrow the offer changes shape, and not in your favor."

Elias was waiting where he shouldn't have been — the gap between the cooper's shed and the shell store, a slot of shadow wide enough for two people who were not meeting. He'd grown skilled at finding these places in six weeks of covert planning, and each time she saw him waiting in one, something in her chest pulled tight and refused to loosen. There was no time for it to be anything but what it was. But it pulled anyway.

"He offered you papers." Not a question. Word traveled through Meridian faster than weather.

"Freedom and silence, both signed and sealed." She kept her voice low. "Nell back by nightfall."

"And you said?"

"I asked him what he offered my father."

Elias closed his eyes briefly, the way a man does when he's been struck somewhere he can't rub. When he opened them, they were flat and grim in the shadow. "You know what happens now. If he offered once, he'll escalate. Brack doesn't lose. He reprices."

"Then we stop letting him set the price."

"Gideon." He stepped closer, and his voice dropped to almost nothing. "I've spent the day pulling manifests. Corrigan has agreed to hear preliminary testimony — quietly, before the audit finishes burning everything loose. We're close. Weeks, maybe days. If you can hold—"

"Hold what?" It came out sharper than she intended, and she didn't take it back. "Hold Nell in that house another week while they teach her to forget me? Hold Sefu on a transport heading north? Hold my father's name in a loss column one more season?" She breathed. Counted. One, two, three, four — the rhythm her father gave her, the rhythm that had kept her alive at forty feet and would keep her alive now. "I'm done holding, Elias. I'm done being patient for men who use patience against me."

"Then we move now. Tonight if we have to." His jaw set. "But you need to understand what you're carrying. Not the book. You. If this goes wrong — if Brack decides a drowned indentured girl is cheaper than a hearing — there'll be no inquiry. No magistrate. Just a line foul, a sad report, a widow in Perth collecting nothing. I've read those reports. I file them." His voice cracked on the last word, barely, and he pressed on. "So if there is any version of you that wants the papers — any version at all — take them. I will not think less of you. I couldn't."

She looked at him for a long moment. Six weeks of meetings in gaps and doorways, and neither of them had said anything that could be quoted, anything that would hang them if overheard. Everything important had been carried in the spaces between the operational words. But this — *I couldn't think less of you* — was the closest either of them had come to the true thing, and they both heard it land.

"There isn't a version," she said simply. "You should know that about me by now. There's only the one, and she watched a man drown for signing the wrong page, and she's not going to stand at his daughter's grave someday and explain why the daughter sold the same names he died protecting." She paused. "Besides."

"Besides?"

"Brack thinks the ledger is a list." She felt the cold certainty settle in her the way ballast settles a hull. "He thinks it's leverage because he thinks everything is leverage. He doesn't understand that I've read every entry. Forty-one names, Elias. Forty-one divers recorded as *losses* between '86 and last year. Eleven children transferred between stations with prices beside them. I don't carry a document. I carry a congregation. And congregations don't trade."

For a moment Elias said nothing at all. Then he reached out — quickly, before sense could stop him — and took her hand. His palm was dry and warm and steady, a customs officer's hand, a stamping hand, and it closed around hers like a decision.

"Then here's what happens," he said. "Tomorrow morning I walk into Brack's office in my official capacity. Manifests, quarterly inspection, the whole tedious ritual. While I have every clerk in that building looking at me and resenting me, you go to the shell heaps and lift the ledger. Corrigan sits at ten o'clock. By noon, forty-one names are on the record and no offer on earth buys them back out of it."

"They'll know it was you."

"They already will, the moment you testify." He almost smiled. It was not a happy expression; it was a free one. "My father lost everything challenging a company, and I swore I'd be cleverer than he was. Cleverer means knowing the difference between a career and a life. I've been slow about it. I'm done being slow."

She squeezed his hand once, hard, because there wasn't time for anything longer, and because some things survive better unspoken.

"One condition," she said.

"Name it."

"Nell comes with me tomorrow. Whatever happens at that office, whatever repricing Brack does — I'm not leaving her behind a wall I don't control while I'm digging up proof. If we run, we run together. If they take her again after the testimony, at least the world will know her name and where she came from." She met his eyes. "They only win while she's a secret."

Elias nodded slowly. "Corrigan can have the aunt identified by afternoon. Sister Bernadette's ready. It ends tomorrow one way or the other."

"Yes," Gideon said. And found that the word had no weight at all — that after four years of carrying everything, the decision to stop carrying it made her lighter than she had been since her father went into the water. "It ends tomorrow."

*

That night she did not sleep. She sat on her bunk with Nell curled against her, breathing the small fast breaths of the innocent, and she went through the ledger in her head the way other women said prayers. *Amani Okello, loss, February '87. Tomas Reyes, loss, August '88, payment to inspector, four pounds. The girl called Little Bit, transferred to Lagrange station, eleven pounds.* Names, dates, sums. A congregation.

Near midnight she heard footsteps on the compound path — unhurried, deliberate, stopping outside her barracks long enough to make sure she heard them, then moving on. Brack's message, delivered in boots. *We know you're awake. We know what you're choosing.*

Let him listen, she thought, lying in the dark with the child's weight warm against her side. Let him hear nothing but a woman sleeping, because that was all he would ever understand — and tomorrow he would learn, too late, that the quietest thing in Broome harbor was not the sea.

At dawn she braided her hair tight for diving, dressed in the clothes she'd wear to a courtroom, and woke Nell with a kiss on the forehead.

"Big day, little one," she whispered. "Time to give everybody their names back."
