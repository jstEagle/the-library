## 16. Checkpoint Three: The Wire

*Gideon*

The telegraph office sat at the corner of Hamersley and Napier like a man with his back to the wall — a narrow brick building with one window, one door, and one clerk who had worked there long enough to know every face in Broome and the price of forgetting any of them. Gideon had walked past it perhaps four hundred times since she'd come of age under Meridian contract. She had never once looked at it directly.

Now she stood across the street in the shade of Ah Toy's verandah and studied it the way she studied water before a dive: for what moved beneath the surface.

Two men loitered near the door. They wore no uniform, but they wore their idleness badly — weight on both feet, eyes working the street while their hands pretended to roll cigarettes. Company men. She knew the breed by the way they stood still without comfort.

"They've been there since morning," she said.

Beside her, Elias kept his gaze fixed on the harbour as though the tide were the only thing worth watching. He'd grown a beard since the audit began, a short dark thing that changed the geometry of his face enough that she sometimes startled at him. "Rotating," he murmured. "They swap out at noon. I watched it yesterday from the customs shed. Same pair comes back at six."

"Then we go between?"

"We go when everyone's looking." He turned his head slightly, not toward her — never toward her on the street; that discipline had been beaten into them both by now — but toward the jetty, where a lugger was unloading shell. "That's the whole design of it, Gid. You keep thinking about how to slip past three men. Stop thinking that way. We can't slip past anything anymore. The overseer's got watchers on your barracks, my clerk reports to the auditor, and half the town would sell us for the price of a drink." His voice stayed level, almost bored. "So we don't sneak. We make so much noise they can't watch all of it."

She let that settle. It went against every grain of her survival — nineteen years of smallness, of being a woman whose footsteps made no sound on company boards. Every instinct she owned said: be quiet, be less, be nothing. And every instinct she owned had been wrong since the day a bundle appeared on her step wrapped in a blanket that smelled of salt and another woman's milk.

"Tell me again," she said. "All of it. Slowly."

He spoke to the harbour, quiet as a tide chart. "Tomorrow night, the Perth mail steamer takes the last wire window before the quarterly close. Corrigan's clerk has confirmed the magistrate will receive us Friday morning, first hour, before the office fills. So Thursday is everything." A breath. "The newspaper copy goes first. Fourteen pages, reduced to the essentials — names, dates, payments, the death columns. I've written them out in cipher, simple substitution, key word's *meridian*, which will please the editor when he works it out. The telegram goes through the Broome office addressed to the *West Australian*. Once it's transmitted, it exists in Perth whether we live or die. They can burn this town down and the words are already sitting in a newsroom four hundred leagues south."

"And the original?"

"The original walks in the front door of the same office, inside your diving suit, with me beside you wearing my customs badge, escorting a prisoner." He almost smiled. "You're the prisoner. Drunk and disorderly, resisting arrest. I'm marching you to the lockup, which happens to pass the telegraph office, where I have legitimate business filing my weekly report. Every eye in Broome will be on the spectacle. Nobody searches a prisoner a constable is dragging by the arm — they just enjoy the show."

"Nobody," Gideon repeated.

"The risk isn't the search. The risk is that someone recognizes you. That's why you'll have your hood up, head down, and I'll keep moving. Thirty seconds inside the door while I file. Then we walk out the back lane, and you're nobody again until Friday."

She was silent a moment, watching the two smokers. One of them had finished his cigarette and immediately rolled another, hands too busy, eyes too idle.

"Elias," she said. "If it goes wrong tomorrow — if they take the ledger off me — what happens to Nell?"

He didn't answer right away. Below them the lugger's winch groaned, and a heap of mother-of-pearl spilled across the jetty boards, flashing like a spill of moons.

"Sister Brigid keeps her tonight and tomorrow night," he said finally. "At the convent, behind a locked gate the company has no authority to cross. If we fall, Corrigan already holds my sworn statement and the cipher key. It won't hang them without the book, but it will make them afraid, and frightened companies do stupid things in daylight." He paused. "And if we fall, Gideon, you need to know it was worth attempting. Not for absolution. For arithmetic. There are two hundred and eleven names in that ledger. Two hundred and twelve counting yours."

"Two hundred thirteen," she said. "Counting Nell's mother. I found her column in March. 'Transferred — Roebourne station.' No date of return."

Elias closed his eyes briefly. When he opened them, something had hardened in them that she recognized from her own mirror.

"Thursday, then," he said. "Sunset. Wear the suit."

* * *

*Elias*

He spent the evening before the run doing the thing he hated most: waiting in rooms where other men decided things.

Magistrate Corrigan's chambers smelled of lamp oil and old paper, and the magistrate himself sat behind a desk piled with the debris of twenty years of cases no one in Perth had wanted to hear. He was a spare, grey man with a hearing trumpet he used selectively, and Elias had learned over three visits that he missed more with his ears than he ever did with his eyes.

"You understand what you're asking me to do," Corrigan said, not looking up from the cipher sheets Elias had laid out. "A telegraph transmitting evidence of conspiracy through an office the company effectively controls. If the operator is theirs, your fourteen pages become a confession delivered straight to the men you're accusing."

"I've considered it."

"Have you? Because I've considered it, and I think you haven't considered it nearly hard enough." He set the sheets down. "Who operates the Broome wire at night?"

"A man named Fennelly."

"Who is paid—"

"By the Eastern Telegraph Company, not Meridian. I checked his employment record myself, twice. But his brother-in-law manages the company store, and his rent is paid to a house Meridian owns." Elias met the old man's gaze. "So yes. He could be bought. He probably has been bought, in the small ways, for years. Which is why the plan doesn't depend on him."

"No?"

"It depends on the room." Elias leaned forward. "Thursday at sunset, the mail steamer's wire window opens, and half of Broome wants to send messages south — merchants, pearlers, the bank, the church. The office will be full. Full of witnesses, Your Honor. A bought operator can delay a message or lose a flimsy, but he can't very well refuse to transmit in front of fifteen respectable citizens, one of whom will always be a rival of Meridian's and delighted to carry gossip to Perth. And once the words leave the Broome wire, they're in Adelaide within the minute and Perth within five. There is no pulling them back. Even if they arrest every person in that room."

Corrigan took off his spectacles and polished them with a handkerchief gone grey at the folds. "And the original book. You propose to carry it into the lion's mouth because—"

"Because the lion watches its own mouth best. Every watcher in Broome will be positioned at the barracks road, the jetty, and my own customs shed. No one guards the place we're supposed to be going anyway." He paused. "Also because it needs to reach you by Friday morning, and there is no other way left. The audit burned the copies. This is the last one in existence, and it's been living in a diving suit in a convent cellar for nine days."

"That suit," Corrigan said mildly, "is the single most dangerous garment in Western Australia."

"Yes, sir."

The magistrate looked at him for a long moment. Outside, a nightbird called from the mangroves, and somewhere down the street a piano stumbled through a music-hall tune.

"When I came north," Corrigan said, "I was told this posting was quiet. Eleven years ago. I wrote four reports in my first two years documenting irregularities in the labour contracts — indentured divers signed for terms that were extended without consent, wages held against debts that never shrank, deaths recorded as losses of equipment. Do you know what happened to those reports?" 

"I can guess."

"They were acknowledged. Filed. And I was informed that my annual assessment would benefit from, and I quote the Chief Secretary's secretary, 'a broader appreciation of commercial realities.'" He put the spectacles back on. "I stopped writing reports. I started keeping my own ledger instead. Names, dates, everything I couldn't officially see. For eleven years I have been a magistrate with a drawer full of crimes and no evidence that would survive a courtroom." He tapped the cipher sheets. "This survives. If the girl testifies. Will she?"

"She'll testify."

"She'll be destroyed by it. Her livelihood, her name, possibly her liberty — the company's lawyers will argue she stole the book, forged the entries, conspired with you. They will make her out to be exactly what they call her already. And this town will believe them, because believing them is cheaper than believing her."

"I know."

"Do you?" Corrigan's voice wasn't cruel; it was tired, the tiredness of a man who had watched courage arrive in his courtroom before and watched what became of it after. "Because I've seen men stand where she'll stand, Ward. Myself included, once, in a smaller room, in front of a smaller company. The standing up is not the hard part. The hard part is the morning after, when you're ruined and they're merely embarrassed, and you have to decide whether it was worth it with no one applauding."

Elias thought of his father, who had challenged a shipping combine in Fremantle and died ten years later a clerk in a warehouse, poor and unbowed and bitter in equal measure. He thought of the look on Gideon's face when she'd shown him that first page — not triumphant, not afraid. Certain. The certainty of someone who had done the arithmetic of her own life and found the sum unacceptable.

"She already decided," he said. "Before I ever met her. Everything else since has been the rest of us catching up."

Corrigan grunted — the closest thing to approval Elias had extracted from him in three visits. "Friday, first hour. Bring the book and bring the girl. And Ward—" The magistrate's eyes flicked to him, sharp behind the lenses. "If you love her, tell her now, before Thursday. Not after. Men who say such things after the ruin think they're offering comfort. All they're offering is debt."

Elias stood, collected his hat, and found that his throat had tightened around whatever answer he might have given.

"Goodnight, Your Honor," he managed.

"Goodnight. And Ward — wear a coat over the badge. Sunset's cold this time of year, and I want my witness alive."

* * *

*Gideon*

The convent cellar smelled of candle wax and brine, and Nell slept in a real bed for the first time in her life, a small furious knot of a girl who fought sleep the way she fought everything, and lost the same way, suddenly and completely.

Gideon sat on the floor beside the bed and sewed.

The suit lay across her knees, inside-out, its lining opened along the spine. Fourteen oilcloth packets sat in a row on the flagstones, each one flat as a wafer, each holding two pages of her father's handwriting sealed in wax against the wet. She stitched them in with sailmaker's stitches she'd learned at twelve, tight and even, and then she did it again, because her hands needed the work or they would shake.

Sister Brigid sat nearby mending a cassock that belonged to some priest who was probably not owed the mending. She'd been silent for an hour, which Gideon had come to understand was the nun's native tongue.

"You'll drown," the sister said at last, conversationally, as if remarking on the weather.

"Not planning to."

"No one plans to. That's rather the definition." She bit her thread. "The channel past the point hasn't been dived since the old days. The current runs two ways at once through there, and the silt has shifted since the dredge. A strong swimmer could do it in daylight with a support boat. You mean to do it at night, alone, carrying a book."

"I mean to do it carrying the truth," Gideon said, and heard how it sounded, and grimaced. "That's not an answer."

"It's the answer most people give," Sister Brigid said. "It's why so few of them surface." She set down the cassock. "The Father can row you to the point. He owes the company nothing and enjoys saying so. Beyond that, you're alone, and God help you, child, because I'll be praying, but prayer travels slower than current."

Gideon tied off the final stitch and turned the suit right-way round. It hung on her frame like any other diving suit, patched at the knee, stiff with salt — except that now it weighed eleven pages more than it should, and those pages weighed more than lead.

Nell stirred and whimpered, and Gideon reached out without thinking, found the small hot hand, and held it until the whimper subsided. In the dark behind her eyelids, the girl murmured a word. Gideon caught only the shape of it, but she knew it — she'd been hearing it for weeks. *Bapa.* Bapa, bapa. Her father's name in the ledger was Baptiste. Baptiste Okafor, transferred to Roebourne, no date of return. The child remembered a voice, and the voice had a name, and the name was inked in a dead man's handwriting in a book sewn into the suit hanging on the chair.

"Her aunt comes Friday," Sister Brigid said softly. "The one from La Grange. She'll want to take her."

"I know."

"You'll let her go."

Gideon looked at the sleeping child — the stubborn jaw, the fist curled even in sleep around nothing, around everything. "I'll do what keeps her alive," she said. "That's the whole of my policy. It's been the whole of it since the night someone left her on my step and told me to forget her face."

"And yourself? What keeps you alive?"

Gideon stood and lifted the suit, feeling the true weight of it settle against her arms. Tomorrow at sunset she would dive a drowned channel in the dark with the only copy of a hanging offense strapped against her heart. Tomorrow night she would stand in a telegraph office full of strangers while a man she loved filed papers with steady hands, and she would count the seconds, the way she counted breaths underwater, one and two and three, trusting that the surface was there even when she couldn't see it.

"Honestly, Sister?" she said. "I don't know yet. Ask me Friday."

Sister Brigid snorted — a distinctly un-nunlike sound — and blew out the candle. "Get some sleep. Divers who argue with the sea tired drown faster than divers who argue rested."

In the dark, Gideon lay down on the pallet beside Nell's bed and did not sleep. She listened to the child breathe and counted the breaths, one and two and three, and somewhere past two hundred the bell of St. Mary's tolled midnight, and Thursday had begun.

Across town, in the dark, the telegraph office stood shuttered and guarded, its wire running south through a thousand miles of bush and silence toward Perth, carrying nothing yet.

By this time tomorrow, it would be carrying everything.
