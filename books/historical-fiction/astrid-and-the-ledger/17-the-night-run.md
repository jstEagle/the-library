## 17. The Night Run

The warning came at dusk, nailed to the station board in the overseer's own hand: *CYCLONE EXPECTED. ALL DIVES SUSPENDED. REMAIN BARRACKS.*

Gideon read it twice and felt something she hadn't felt in years — gratitude toward a storm.

By company law, no one moved during a cyclone warning. By company habit, everyone stayed where they were told, because the last woman who'd wandered during a blow had been docked a month's pay and half her teeth. Which meant that when the wind arrived an hour after dark, dragging its freight of rain across the flats, the barracks would be shutters-down and shuttered-in, and the road checkpoint would be a lantern and two men under an awning who wanted nothing more in the world than to be somewhere else.

Nobody walked the coast road tonight. Nobody would look for a diver in water that could kill her.

That was the whole plan, and it was a terrible plan, and it was the only one they had.

* * *

She dressed in the shell room with the door barred and a single shielded lamp turned low. Elias had brought the suit over in a flour sack three days ago, and she'd spent every night since re-lacing it, checking it, hating it. It was her old working suit, patched at both knees, stiff with salt, and sewn into its lining now — flat against the small of her back, wrapped in oilskin and waxed thread — lay the ledger. Forty pages of names. Forty pages of the dead, filed neatly by season.

Elias stood at the door with his back to her, watching the lamplight through the seam. He'd argued for the road. He'd argued for waiting a week, for a second copy, for anything but this.

"You'll be diving alone," he said, "in a cyclone, through a channel nobody's run since the old days."

"I've been diving alone since I was twelve."

"Not like this. Not blind, at night, on an ebb tide." He turned. His face in the low light was the face of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like. "The channel shifts, Gideon. The old divers used to say it ate a man every third year."

"The old divers," she said, "said a lot of things." She pulled the corselet up over her shoulders. "They also said the company would never fall. And here we are, about to hang them with their own arithmetic."

He came to her then and put his hands on either side of her helmet, not lifting it yet, just holding it between them like something breakable.

"If you're not at the jetty by four," he said, "I go to Corrigan alone, and I tell him everything, and I spend the rest of my life explaining why I let you do this."

"Then be at the jetty by four."

"That's not what I—"

"I know what it's not." She looked up at him. Her voice went quieter than she meant it to. "Elias. Say the other thing. In case."

For a long moment he didn't. Then: "Come back." He set the helmet on her head and began the bolts, his hands steady even though his voice wasn't quite. "Nell asked for you at supper. She wouldn't eat until Sister Agnes told her you were counting shells underwater and would count your way home."

"She believes that?"

"She believes everything you say. God help us all."

The last bolt seated. The world shrank to the four small windows of the faceplate and the sound of her own breath, loud as surf. She counted one breath, the way she always did, the way her father taught her before he taught her anything else — *the sea keeps what you don't count* — and Elias pumped the air hose twice, the signal, and opened the door onto the rain.

* * *

The storm hit her like a wall of wet wool.

Wind came off the water in long flat gusts that pushed her sideways on the mud, and the rain was so hard it sounded like gravel against the copper helmet. She kept to the lee of the shell heaps, following the line Elias had walked with her twice by daylight, memorized three times over: past the heaps, past the broken jetty stub, down the mud ramp to where the old channel began.

The old channel. Her father's channel. Before the company dredged the deep-water cut for its luggers, the divers had run their catch through this winding gutter in the reef — a mile of dark water that threaded beneath the point and came up inside the town jetty, past the customs sheds, past the road checkpoint without ever touching a road. When the new channel opened, the old one silted. The maps stopped showing it. Only men like her father, men who read water the way clerks read ledgers, kept it alive in their heads.

He'd shown her once, when she was fourteen, standing on the point in a dry-season calm. *In, along the white stone, down through the gut, hold left at the dead coral, come up under the pilings.* He'd laughed and said no one would ever need it again. He'd been wrong about a great many things, and she carried all of them now — his handwriting, his channel, his daughter — out into the black water to finish his work.

At the ramp, the air hose ended. That was the bargain she'd made with herself and hated: no tender, no line, no pump. A free swim in a borrowed suit, weighted, breathing off the small reserve tank rigged from a lugger's spare — forty minutes of air if she was lucky, less if she panicked, none at all if she tore the hose fitting on the coral.

Forty minutes. A mile. In a cyclone.

She checked the knife at her belt. She checked the oilskin bundle's strap one last time, pressing it flat against her spine through the canvas. Then she stepped off the ramp into the channel mouth, and the sea took her.

* * *

Down was easy. Down was always easy; the weights did that work. She sank through water the color of ink, past the first white marker stone she couldn't see and had to find with her feet, and the storm above became a rumor — a rolling pressure in her chest, a distant drumming, the sea shrugging in long swells that lifted her and set her down again like a hand testing fruit.

She counted breaths. One. Two. Three.

*The sea keeps what you don't count.* Her father's voice, warm in the helmet with her. She'd have given anything for it to be true tonight, for the sea to keep only the counted things, the numbered things, the things she could afford to lose. But the sea kept what it liked. It had kept her father. It kept Sefu's health and Nell's mother and a hundred names in forty pages, and tonight it might keep her, and the ledger with her, and then the whole rotten column of figures would balance forever in Meridian's favor.

No. She kicked forward into the current and refused the thought the way she'd learned to refuse all thoughts that didn't help. There was only the next breath, and the next stone.

Along the white stone. She found it with her shin, a bone-white lump of coral the size of a barrel, and turned as her father had shown her, letting the ebb take her down the throat of the channel. The walls rose on either side — she felt them more than saw them, ridges of reef breathing cold against the suit. Sand hissed along the bottom in ribbons. Somewhere above, the storm dragged the whole ocean sideways, and the swell drove down here in pulses, squeezing the channel like a throat, pushing her faster than she wanted to go.

Faster was fine. Faster was air saved. What wasn't fine was the moment the pulse slackened and sucked back the other way — the surge reversing, hauling at her like a hand on her ankle, trying to drag her back up the gut toward the open sea. She dug her fingers into a coral head and held on, boots trailing, body streaming out behind her like a flag, while the channel exhaled and inhaled around her.

Hold. Count. Hold.

On the fifth surge it relented and pushed her onward instead, tumbling her around the bend at the dead coral, and she was through the worst narrows and into the long straight run under the point, and for a little while there was nothing in the world but breath and black water and the weight of names against her spine.

* * *

It went wrong in the last quarter mile, because it was always going to go wrong somewhere.

The silt had shifted. She knew it before she understood it — the channel floor rising to meet her, the ceiling of water thinning, the walls closing in with new sandbars thrown across the old path like a door half-shut. She tried to climb over the first bar and the current peeled her off it and slammed her against the reef wall. Copper rang against coral. She heard the crack through her own bones.

Then the bar again, and this time she got a knee over it, hauled herself across on her forearms through water shallow enough that her helmet broke the surface and filled with storm roar — and dropped down the far side into a channel that wasn't there anymore.

The map in her head said: *come up under the pilings.* The channel in front of her said otherwise. It forked. Two black throats, one going the way she remembered, one going somewhere else entirely, and the sand between them was moving, the whole bottom crawling seaward with the ebb, and her gauge — she twisted to see it in the lightning-flash glow through the water — showed air she didn't trust.

She chose left, because her father had said left, because left was all she had.

Two hundred yards later the left fork died. It simply ended, choked with rubble from some storm years ago, a wall of fallen coral and tangled mooring chain, and the current pressed her against it and held her there, and her air gauge ticked down, and for the first time since she was twelve years old, since the day they pulled her father's hose up empty, Gideon Eriksen understood that she was going to drown.

Not dramatically. Not even painfully, at first. Just — the gauge falling. The breaths getting shorter in her ears. The darkness total. The ledger heavy against her back, forty pages that would sink with her and rot in the silt and never hang anybody, ever, and Nell waking tomorrow in a barracks where Gideon had promised, had *promised*, to count her way home.

She screamed into the helmet. It was a useless animal sound, and it used up air, and she stopped.

Then she made herself think, the way she'd made herself think in the overseer's office, in the courtroom of her own fears, every time survival demanded silence and she'd had to decide which silence. The current was pressing her against the rubble wall. Currents came from somewhere and went somewhere. This one was pouring *over* the wall — she could feel it, a smooth cold sliding above her shoulders, water finding its way through the choke the way water always did.

Where water went, she could go. Sideways, maybe. Low.

She got her fingers into the chain. She pulled herself along the rubble, flat as a flounder, feeling the current try to peel her up and over, feeling coral open a seam in her sleeve, feeling — she would swear to it later — her father's hands over hers on the chain. *In, along the white stone, down through the gut, hold left.* Left had lied. So she held right instead, into the narrowest gap, the one too tight for sense, and dragged herself through a throat of stone that scraped the corselet and nearly took the air fitting and very nearly took her, and then the pressure eased, and the water opened, and below her, vast and swaying, hung the pilings of the town jetty.

Barnacled columns marching up into storm-dark. Home side. Town side. The checkpoint a hundred yards inland and a world away.

Her gauge showed almost nothing. It didn't matter. There was nothing left to do but rise, and rising needed no air she owned — she let the weights carry her down one last yard, then shrugged them off her shoulders and let the suit take her, and she shot upward through the black water faster than she'd ever risen in her life, breaking the surface inside the jetty line with a gasp that tore her throat.

Rain hammered the water. Wind screamed through the pilings overhead like a choir with grievances. Lightning turned the world white — the jetty planks, the customs shed, the moored luggers bucking at their lines — and Gideon floated on her back in the wreckage of the channel, breathing raw storm air in huge ragged pulls, and laughed.

It was not a sane sound. It was the sound of a woman who had been certain she was dead and discovered herself otherwise, and it mixed with the thunder and vanished, and no one heard it, and she didn't care.

* * *

Getting out was its own small war. The ladder on the harbor side was slick with weed; the rungs bit her gloved hands; her legs shook so badly on the fourth rung that she simply hung there for a ten-count, forehead against the wet timber, letting the jetty hold her up. Below her, the suit streamed channel water and, somewhere in its lining, forty pages of proof that the Meridian Trading Company bought inspectors and buried divers and sold children — dry. Bone-dry. Elias's oilskin and her father's stitching had done their work.

She stripped the helmet on the planks and stood bareheaded in the cyclone, hair plastered to her face, and counted her breaths until they slowed. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen. Hers. Every one hers.

A shape detached itself from the shadow of the customs shed — tall, oilskinned, lamp hooded — and came fast along the boards, and she knew the walk before she could see the face, and something in her chest that had been clenched since sundown finally let go.

Elias reached her and grabbed her arms and held on, and for a moment neither of them said anything at all, because the storm was shouting enough for both of them.

"You're late," he shouted at last.

"I took the scenic route."

His hands found the tear in her sleeve, the blood thinned pink by rain, the shaking she couldn't stop. His voice dropped even though no one alive could have heard them. "Gideon. Are you—"

"It's dry." She turned her back to him and pulled the collar wide, and his fingers found the bundle through the slit she'd cut, and he went very still.

"Dry," he repeated, like a man confirming a miracle.

"Every page." She faced him. Rain ran down her face and she was grinning like a fool and couldn't stop. "Now get me out of this suit and get that book to Corrigan before the storm decides it isn't finished with me."

Elias looked at her a moment longer — the way, she would remember all her life, a man looks at something he has already decided not to live without — and then he unhooded the lamp, and together they walked the length of the jetty into the teeth of the wind, toward the magistrate's door, carrying the dead man's ledger home.

Behind them, out in the dark, the storm rolled on toward the town — and ahead of them, unknown to either of them, a light was already burning in the overseer's window, and it was not the light of a man sheltering from weather.
