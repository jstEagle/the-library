## 18. Magistrate's Door

The rain had stopped an hour before dawn, and Broome smelled of wet iron and rotting mangrove — the smell the town wore after every storm, like a coat it couldn't take off.

Elias Ward walked the last three streets with the ledger against his chest, wrapped in oilskin, wrapped again in his own coat, held there by both arms as though he were carrying a sleeping child. In a way he was. It had a heartbeat. He'd felt it since Gideon pressed it into his hands at the jetty pilings, her fingers blue with cold, her eyes burning in a face gone grey with salt water and exhaustion. *Take it to Corrigan. Don't stop for anyone.*

She had swum the old channel under a cyclone warning to get it that far. The least he could do was walk six streets without being shot.

There was a constable at the corner of Hamersley and Napier, sheltering under the verandah of the closed chandlery, and Elias gave him a nod so ordinary it bored even himself. *Customs officer, off duty, going home.* That was the trick he'd learned in nine years of inspections: the best disguise was tedium. Nobody looked twice at a tired man in a government coat.

Nobody except the men Meridian paid, and they weren't looking for him yet. They were looking for a ledger that no longer existed anywhere but inside this coat and inside the mind of a woman who could recite its columns from memory — names, dates, sums, the small neat handwriting of a dead diver named Eriksen who had known exactly what he was writing down and who would read it.

Elias turned onto Corrigan's street. The magistrate's house sat behind a low wall furred with bougainvillea gone riotous in the wet season, purple flowers drowning in puddles along the gutter like something drowned there on purpose. One lamp burned upstairs. Corrigan kept colonial hours: awake before the heat, asleep before the flies.

Elias stood at the gate a moment and let himself feel what he had refused to feel all night — the fear, cold and specific, sitting in him like swallowed seawater.

If this went wrong, he wasn't losing a posting. He was losing everything he'd spent nine years building, brick by careful brick: the clean record, the promotion due at Michaelmas, the name Ward scrubbed finally clean of his father's disgrace. And more than that. He was handing Meridian a reason, and Meridian did not need much of a reason. His father had taught him that lesson at eleven years old, across a kitchen table, explaining why a man could lose everything for asking one question too loudly.

He opened the gate anyway.

* *

Corrigan answered the door himself, which told Elias something before a word was spoken. A man with servants didn't answer his own door at half past five unless he'd been waiting up.

The magistrate was older than his reputation — mid-sixties, maybe, with a face like a dried riverbed and eyes that moved over Elias once, top to bottom, cataloguing. He was dressed, not in nightclothes but in shirtsleeves and waistcoat, as if sleep had been a formality he'd abandoned hours ago.

"You're Ward," he said. "Customs."

"Yes, sir."

"And you're standing on my step in a cyclone's afterbirth holding your coat like it's a baby." Not a question. "Inside. Quickly."

The hall smelled of pipe smoke and camphor. Corrigan led him through to a study lined floor to ceiling with law books, most of them swollen with damp, spines cracked from use rather than neglect. On the desk lay a cold teapot, two cups, and a map of the northwest coast marked with pins. Elias noted the pins. There were more of them than any honest map needed.

Corrigan shut the door. "Now. You have thirty seconds to tell me why I shouldn't call my houseboy and have you escorted out."

Elias unwrapped the ledger and set it on the desk between the cups.

For a long moment Corrigan didn't touch it. He looked at it the way a man looks at a snake that might be dead or might not — with respect, and with his hand well back.

"Where did this come from?"

"A diver," Elias said. "Her father kept it. He worked Meridian waters until they killed him for it."

"Killed him."

"Recorded it as a loss. Four pounds, ten shillings, written against the station account." Elias heard his own voice go flat and precise, the voice he used for manifests. "That's what a man cost them. Four pound ten. There are forty-one deaths in there, sir. Recorded as losses, or not recorded at all. Bribes to inspectors — two of whom I know by name. Children transferred between stations with prices beside their ages."

Corrigan picked up the ledger then. He turned pages slowly, and Elias watched the man's face change by degrees — the professional neutrality first, then attention, then something older and colder surfacing underneath, something that had been waiting down there for years like a body in deep water rising at last.

"Page forty," Corrigan said quietly. "Read me page forty."

Elias took the book, found the page, and read. An inspector named Halloran. A sum. A date. A note in the margin in that small patient hand: *paid to look away re: girl aged 9, station 4.* He read it aloud because Corrigan asked him to, and saying it out loud made it real in a way that reading silently never could. His mouth went dry around the words.

When he finished, the room was very quiet. Outside, a crow called once in the wet grey light and thought better of it.

Corrigan sat down heavily behind his desk. For a while he only looked at the ledger, lying open between them like a wound.

"Do you know how long I've been in this town, Ward?"

"No, sir."

"Nineteen years. Nineteen years of hearing things I couldn't prove. Men lost in company waters who were strong swimmers. Indentures extended by 'administrative error.' Girls moved between stations and never heard of again." He tapped the ledger once with one blunt finger. "I've written to Perth four times. Do you know what came back? Acknowledgments. Polite ones. Once, a request that I forward my sources' names — to the Colonial Secretary, whose brother-in-law, I might add, sits on Meridian's board." His laugh had no humour in it at all. "So yes. I've been waiting for this book. I'd begun to think I'd die waiting, or worse, stop believing I should be waiting at all. That's the thing about a place like this. It doesn't beat you. It just wears you smooth."

"I understand, sir," Elias said. "Better than you'd think."

Corrigan looked up sharply. "Your father."

It wasn't a question either. Everyone in the service knew the shape of the Ward story, worn smooth in its own way: the father who'd challenged a shipping combine in Fremantle twenty-five years ago, the inquiry that went nowhere, the licence revoked, the family scattered on the tide of one man's ruined name. Elias had built his whole life on the far side of that story.

"He asked questions loudly," Elias said. "And when nothing happened, he thought he'd won. Then everything happened at once, quietly, and he never recovered. So yes. I understand wearing smooth."

Something passed between them then — not warmth, exactly. Recognition. Two men who had learned the same arithmetic from opposite ends.

Corrigan closed the ledger. "Then hear me plainly, because I'll only say this once, and I want you carrying it out of this room with your eyes open." He leaned forward. "This book is worth nothing without testimony. Documents can be dismissed as forgery — and they will be; Meridian's lawyers will stand up in my court and call this the fantasy of a disgruntled native labourer or a thieving clerk, and half the gallery will nod along, because it's comfortable to nod along. What makes it true is a living witness who stands up and says *I saw, I counted, these are my people.*" He paused. "You intend that witness to be you."

It wasn't a question, and Elias didn't treat it as one. "Yes."

"And the diver? The daughter? Eriksen's girl?"

"She'll testify if I don't stop her. I'm not sure I could stop her with rope." He thought of Gideon at the pilings, shivering, pushing the bundle into his arms — *go* — and then turning back into the water-street dark toward the station where Nell was, because the child mattered more than the book, more than the plan, more than her own skin. "She's the bravest person I've ever met, sir, and she learned bravery the hard way, which is the only way it lasts."

Corrigan studied him. "You're in love with her."

Elias felt the colour climb his neck and despised it. "That has no bearing—"

"It has every bearing. Love is the finest motive in the world and the worst evidence." Corrigan rose and went to the window, where the bougainvillea dripped. "Let me tell you what happens if you testify. You lose your commission. They'll find something — they always find something; there's a file on every officer thicker than he knows. You'll be lucky if it stops at dismissal. Meridian owns ships, warehouses, half the pearling fleet, and the goodwill of men in Perth who dine with judges. If they decide to make an example of you, they can. And the girl—" he turned, "—they'll try her for theft first. Company property, they'll claim. They'll say she stole the ledger. It's elegant, really. The evidence becomes the crime."

"I know."

"Do you?" Corrigan's voice hardened. "Knowing isn't the same as standing in it, young man. I've watched men swear oaths in my courtroom with brave faces and watch the faces change when the bill arrives. Ruin is slow. It comes as small refusals — credit denied, references withheld, doors closing one at a time until you're knocking on doors that were never locked before. That's how they kill a man here. No drama. Just weather."

Elias looked at the ledger on the desk. Forty-one dead. Two inspectors bought. Children priced by age. Somewhere in those columns, a line for Nell's mother — he hadn't seen it, but Gideon had told him what she believed, and belief backed by this book was close enough to knowledge to carry weight. A girl aged three, transferred. *Station 4.*

"My father taught me to be careful," Elias said slowly. "Careful the way ice is careful. I've spent nine years being careful, and here's what careful got me: I watched the manifests lie and filed them anyway. I inspected Halloran's stamps and saluted him at the club. Last month I let a shipment pass that stank to heaven because the paperwork was correct, and paperwork, I told myself, was all I was responsible for." He met Corrigan's eyes. "I'm done being careful, sir. I'd like to be useful instead. It's a poorer living, but the hours are better for sleeping."

For the first time, the old man smiled — a dry crack in the riverbed face, quickly gone.

"Well," Corrigan said. "Well." He came back to the desk and laid his palm flat on the ledger, as if taking its temperature, or swearing on it. "Here's how it will go. I convene a hearing under the Pearl Shell Fisheries Act and the indenture statutes — narrow grounds, boring grounds, the kind of grounds that give Meridian's lawyers nowhere pretty to stand. I'll subpoena the station records through customs — your department, Ward, which means your signature starts appearing on paper whether you testify or not, so understand there's no halfway door left to you. I'll need the diver. I'll need the child identified, if she can be. And I'll need you to bring me every scrap of corroborating manifest within the week, because once I move, they'll burn faster than you can imagine. They're already burning. Am I right?"

"The auditor arrived early," Elias said. "Loose papers went into the furnace Tuesday."

"Then we're racing a fire with a book of matches." Corrigan straightened. "One more thing, and this is the part men forget. When you stand in that court and read those names aloud — and you will read them, all of them, slowly, so the gallery hears every one — you will not be destroying Meridian. Companies don't die in courtrooms; they die afterward, in boardrooms, when the money gets frightened. What you'll be doing in my court is smaller and greater. You'll be making it *said*. Out loud. On the record. Names attached to names. After that, whatever happens to you — and things will happen — no one can ever say it wasn't said. That's the whole prize, Ward. Is it enough?"

Elias thought of his father, ruined and unrepentant, teaching him chess by lamplight in a rented room in Fremantle: *the point was never to win the game, boy. The point was to leave the board better than you found it.* He'd been twelve and hadn't understood. He understood now, standing in a damp study with a dead man's handwriting between them.

"It's enough," he said. "It's more than I expected to be offered in this life."

Corrigan nodded once, briskly, business resumed. "Then swear it properly. Not to God — God's got enough on his docket in this town. Swear it to each other and to the record. Hand on the book."

Elias put his hand flat on the ledger's cover. The oilskin was still damp from Gideon's swim. Under his palm he could almost believe he felt the pulse of it — forty-one names and one living girl, all resting in the same binding.

"I, Elias Ward, customs officer, will deliver this evidence and speak it true," he said, "whatever it costs."

Corrigan placed his own hand atop Elias's, an old dry weight. "I, Ambrose Corrigan, magistrate, will hear it true, whatever it costs me. And it will cost me, so let neither of us pretend otherwise." He lifted his hands away. "Done. Now get out of my house before daylight makes your visit interesting, and send me the diver. Not tomorrow. Today. Before Meridian remembers she exists — and Ward—"

Elias paused at the door.

"—when they come for her, and they will come, don't be clever. Be there. Clever men write good reports. Present men keep people alive."

* *

The streets were fully light now, the town shaking itself awake — cart wheels in the mud, smoke rising crooked from kitchen chimneys, the pearl luggers' masts swaying at anchor like a forest deciding which way to fall. Elias walked fast, the ledger gone from his chest and the space where it had rested feeling strangely hollow, lighter and heavier at once.

He was three streets from the jetty road when he saw the trap.

A hired carriage stood outside the Customs House, horses steaming, driver paid and waiting. And coming down the steps two at a time, still buttoning his coat, was Inspector Halloran — the signature Elias had watched Gideon's finger land on, the margin-note man, *paid to look away.* Halloran was climbing into the carriage with a leather dispatch case under his arm, and the direction of travel, when the wheels turned, was north. Toward the station. Toward Gideon.

Toward the girl who remembered.

Elias stood in the mud and watched the carriage shrink up the road, and did the arithmetic he'd sworn, ten minutes ago, he was done avoiding: he could wire ahead and warn no one, stay clean another day. Or he could spend his career right now, this morning, in one motion — seize the dispatch case on a technicality, raise Halloran's fury, start the avalanche early and badly, with Gideon unready and Corrigan's subpoenas unwritten.

Or there was a third road, the one Corrigan had just drawn for him: not clever. *Present.*

Elias turned and ran for the telegraph office — past it, actually, to the back stair where the night operator took private wires and forgot them for a fee — already composing the message in his head, already hearing Gideon's voice in it:

*They know. Come now. Bring the girl. Trust no gate but mine.*

Whatever it cost. He'd signed his name to that in a magistrate's study, and Elias Ward had inherited exactly one thing of value from his father: a man who signed was a man who arrived.
