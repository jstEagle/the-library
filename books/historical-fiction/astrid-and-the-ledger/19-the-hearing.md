## 19. The Hearing

They made me wait outside the courtroom for two hours, and I spent them counting my breaths the way I'd learned to count them underwater. In for four. Hold for six. Out for eight. The old arithmetic that kept panic from rising like floodwater in the chest.

Except this time I wasn't thirty feet down with a shell bag dragging at my hips. I was in a corridor in Broome's courthouse, in a dress Elias had bought me — grey wool, high collar, a woman who might be anybody's clerk or anybody's widow — and through the double doors I could hear the room filling up. Divers came in off the boats still smelling of brine. Pearlers stood at the back in their good coats, arms folded, faces arranged into expressions of polite boredom that fooled nobody. A reporter from Perth sat near the front with his pencil already moving, writing down the names of people as they arrived, as though the audience were part of the evidence.

Elias found me there, ten minutes before we were called. He was in his customs uniform, the one they'd told him not to wear, and he wore it anyway.

"Corrigan says you read the names yourself," he said quietly. "He could have a clerk do it."

"I know what I said."

"I know." He looked at his hands, then at me. "Gideon. When you get up there, don't look at Hargreaves. Look at the magistrate. If you look at Hargreaves, he'll find your eyes, and he'll hold them, and he'll make you feel nineteen again and underwater with no air coming."

I almost smiled. "You've thought about this a lot."

"Since the night you showed me that page. Every night since." His voice dropped further. "Whatever happens in there — whatever they say about you, about us —"

"Don't," I said. "Not yet. Say it after."

He nodded, because he understood. Some words are too heavy to carry into a fight. You set them down on the far side of it, where both of you can reach them.

The doors opened. The bailiff called the matter. And I walked into the loudest silence I had ever heard.

* * *

Magistrate Corrigan sat beneath the coat of arms like a man who had been waiting years for this morning and intended to enjoy none of it until it was done properly. He was older than I remembered from the jetty, thinner, with the kind of face that had been weathered by patience rather than sun. He did not look at the pearlers' barristers when they rose. He looked at me.

"State your name for the record."

"Gideon Eriksen. Diver, indentured to the Meridian Trading Company, contract number—" I stopped. "No. Not anymore. That contract is voided as of last week. Just Gideon Eriksen."

A sound went through the room — half laugh, half gasp — and Corrigan let it live for exactly three seconds before silencing it with a look.

"You hold a ledger," he said. "Original document. Where has it been kept?"

"In three places over four months. A shell heap, an oilskin wrap inside a channel marker, and for the last nine days, in the magistrate's own strongroom. I have not been out of sight of it since the night it came to me."

"And how did it come to you?"

Here it was. The part I had rehearsed a hundred times against the wall of my borrowed room, saying it aloud until it stopped sounding like someone else's life.

"A child was left at my door during the September storm. A girl, perhaps two years old. Wrapped in her blanket was this book. There was a note." My voice held. I made it hold. "The note said: *forget the face.*"

"Who left her?"

"I don't know. But the ledger is in my father's hand. Tomas Eriksen, diver, drowned in company waters in 'eighty-nine. He kept accounts for Meridian because he could write, and they liked a literate man among the divers because a literate man could be blamed for anything the numbers got wrong." I turned to the bench. "My father died trying to get this book to someone who would read it. I'm finishing his errand."

The barrister for Meridian — a soft, pale man named Quill whose hands never stopped moving, smoothing his cuffs, aligning his papers — rose like something surfacing.

"Magistrate, before my learned friend continues down this romantic channel. This witness is a former indentured servant with an obvious grievance, presenting a document of unknown provenance, unauthenticated, which she claims—"

"Sit down, Mr. Quill." Corrigan didn't raise his voice. "Authentication is precisely what this hearing is for. You'll have your turn to cross-examine. It will not go well for you, but you'll have it." He turned back to me. "Read."

I opened the ledger on the stand.

I had expected my hands to shake. They didn't. They were steady the way they were steady on the ladder of a diving boat — because they had been trained by worse mornings than this.

"The entries run from March 1887 to August 1889," I said. "Payments column first. Twelve pounds to Inspector Renwick, quarterly, marked *inspection satisfied*. Eight pounds to Sergeant Doyle of the water police, marked *quietness*. Fourteen pounds, six shillings to the harbourmaster's clerk for manifest corrections." I read them all. Names, dates, sums. The room was so quiet I could hear the pencil of the Perth reporter scratching like a crab on sand.

Then the second column.

"The losses column," I said. "That's what they called it. Losses." I read the names. Anselm Kotto, lost, March 1888, no inquiry ordered. Daniel Ferreira, lost, June 1888, replacement cost noted at nine pounds. Maryam Sefu — no relation to our Sefu, but the name went through the room like a current anyway — lost, November 1888, *no inquiry; family paid off, four pounds.*

Forty-one names. I read every one. My voice cracked only once, on a name near the end — a boy of fifteen recorded as *lost, equipment value recovered* — and I let it crack. Let them hear what a loss sounded like in a living throat.

When I finished, Corrigan said, "The third section."

"The transfers." I turned pages I knew by feel now, the way you know a reef by its shadows. "Children of deceased divers, entered as *apprentices*, moved between stations. Sale prices disguised as transport fees. Here—" I put my finger on the line I had read so many times it was worn soft under my thumb. "September 1889. *One female child, approx. 1 year, daughter of diver R. Nakamura, deceased prior to testimony scheduled re: losses. Transferred to Meridian house staff, Broome. No fee recorded. Note: forget the face.*"

The same hand. The same phrase. The note wrapped around Nell's blanket matched the margin note in my father's ledger, and everyone in that room understood it at the same moment, the way a wave understands the shore.

"Is the child present?" Corrigan asked.

"She is," said Sister Bernadette from the front row, and stood, and lifted Nell onto her hip.

* * *

They had warned me about this part. Corrigan himself had warned me: *the identification may fail, Gideon. Two years is young. Memory at that age is water.* I had prepared myself for Nell to cry, or to stare blankly, or to reach for me instead of the stranger in the gallery, and for the whole thread of it to come apart in front of the pearlers' lawyers, who would smile their soft smiles and say *you see? Nothing here but sentiment.*

What happened instead was worse and better than anything I had prepared for.

A woman was brought forward from the gallery — brought, actually, by the water police sergeant who was not Doyle, Doyle having declined, with remarkable foresight, to appear. The woman was Japanese, maybe forty, dressed in mourning black gone grey at the seams. She walked like someone carrying a full shell bag on each shoulder. She had walked, I later learned, from a camp south of town, eleven miles, on feet that had not fully healed from a beating she'd taken in 'eighty-nine for asking where her granddaughter had gone.

Her name was Mrs. Okonkwo-Nakamura by marriage — the aunt. Reiko. She stood in the well of the court and looked at Nell the way divers look at the surface from the bottom: like something you can see and cannot reach.

"Nell is a name given by strangers," Corrigan said gently. "Do you know her by another?"

Reiko's mouth worked. Then she did the thing that broke the room open. She began to sing. A lullaby, low and cracked, four notes rising and falling, and Nell — who had been solemn and wide-eyed through all of it, my grave little girl in Sister Bernadette's arms — Nell's whole body changed. She went rigid, then trembling, then she reached out with both arms and made a sound I had never heard her make, not in all our months together, a sound that had no word in it, just recognition, pure and animal, a sound from before language, from before the storm, from before anyone told anyone to forget any faces.

Reiko crossed the floor and took her, and Nell buried her face in the grey seam of that black dress, and sang the next line herself. Half the words wrong. All of the tune right.

Sister Bernadette sat down slowly, her hands empty in her lap, and I saw her press her knuckles to her mouth, and I understood that giving the child up had cost her more than she would ever say, and that she had known from the beginning — from the jetty, from the oilskin bundle passed between us in the rain — that this day might ask it of her.

Quill rose. Of course he rose.

"Magistrate, sentiment is not evidence. A toddler's response to a song proves nothing—"

"It proves everything," Corrigan said, "when combined with a ledger entry recording her transfer, signed by your client's overseer. Sit down, Mr. Quill. Or rather — don't. Call your client's overseer. Mr. Hargreaves. Let's hear him explain the entry in his own hand."

* * *

Hargreaves took the stand like a man walking into shallow water that he believed was deep enough to drown in safely. That was always his method — wade in confident, let the other fellow assume there was bottom under him. For four years at the station he had used it on divers, on inspectors, on me. He was tanned and silver-templed and he addressed the court in the warm reasonable voice of a man who had never raised a hand he couldn't later describe as necessary.

He denied the ledger's authenticity. He denied knowing my father beyond "a competent account-keeper." He denied the transfers were sales — *administrative reallocations, your worship, standard practice across the industry.* He was doing well. He was doing exactly as well as a man does when he has rehearsed his lies in order.

Then Corrigan handed him the ledger, open to the September 1889 page, and asked him to confirm the handwriting.

Hargreaves looked at it for a long moment. Too long. I watched his thumb move along the edge of the page — my father's page — and I watched him arrive at the margin note, the one in his own hand, *forget the face,* written in the same ink, the same slant, the same impatient crossbar on the *t* that appeared in every work order he'd ever posted on the barracks board.

And I watched him understand that the note and the ledger had met again, and that between them they had closed around him like a net.

"Is that your handwriting, Mr. Hargreaves?" Corrigan asked. Third time. Patient as tide.

The courtroom waited. Quill was on his feet objecting to something — procedure, relevance, the weather, I don't know — and Corrigan waved him down without looking at him.

Hargreaves looked out at the room. At the divers packed along the walls, forty faces he had counted every morning for years, the way you count shells, the way you count money. At the reporter, writing fast now. At Reiko, standing in the aisle with Nell on her hip, and Nell looking back at him with those dark serious eyes that remembered more than any of us had guessed.

Something went out of him. I saw it go. It was like watching a man take off a weight belt underwater — suddenly too light, suddenly rising, no longer in control of his own direction.

"Yes," he said. "It's mine."

Quill stopped mid-sentence.

"The entries are mine. The payments were authorized from Perth — from Director Ashcombe's office, countersigned by his secretary, Voss. The inspection bribes went where the ledger says. The losses—" his voice caught, and he steadied it with visible effort, the way a man steadies a boat in chop, "the losses were not losses. We were losing too many men to call them deaths. Deaths require inquiries. Inquiries require answers. Ashcombe's instruction was to keep the columns clean." He looked down at the ledger in his hands as if he'd forgotten he was holding it. "Tomas Eriksen kept the true figures. He was going to testify. He drowned eleven days before the hearing date. I wrote *lost* in the column myself. I have written it every night since, if not in ink."

Nobody moved. Even the reporter's pencil had stopped.

"Mr. Hargreaves," Corrigan said, very quietly, "you understand you have just implicated yourself in matters that carry penal consequences."

"I have carried them for five years, your worship. Today is the first morning they've weighed less than the alternative."

They took him down. As he passed the end of the dock he paused — one second, no more — beside me, and did not look at me, and said, in a voice pitched for me alone:

"He asked me once if the sea keeps records. I told him no. I've regretted the answer ever since."

Then the bailiff had him by the arm, and he was gone through the side door, and the room exhaled all at once, a long ragged breath like a swell finally finding the beach.

* * *

It took Corrigan until nearly sundown to finish. Warrants for Ashcombe and Voss. The contracts voided en masse — he read the order aloud and the divers in the gallery did not cheer, because cheering hadn't occurred to any of us; we simply sat there while the words *voided* and *null* and *of no effect* fell over us like fresh water after years of salt. The losses reopened as inquiries, one by one, forty-one names to be answered for. Doyle's warrant issued separately. Renwick's too.

Outside, the crowd on the courthouse steps was so thick that Elias had to put his hand at my back to get us through, and even then it was less pushing through than being carried, borne along on voices saying my name — my name, not my contract number — and hands touching my sleeves like I was something lucky, a charm, a figurehead.

Nell was nowhere in sight. Reiko had taken her home — home, the word still new, still soft in the mouth — to wash her and feed her and begin the long work of teaching her the rest of the song. I felt the absence of her weight on my hip like a phantom limb. Four months of carrying a child, and now my arms didn't know what they were for.

Elias got me as far as the corner, out of the crush, under the fig trees along Hamersley Street, and there he stopped, and there was nothing left between us and the evening and the gulls screaming over the jetty.

"After," he said. "You said say it after."

The street noise washed over us. Somewhere behind us the courthouse windows glowed gold with lamplight, and clerks were still inside copying the order that had ended a company.

"I love you," Elias said. "I've loved you since the gate, since you lied to me with your chin up and your heart going like a hammer, and I let you pass because I couldn't bear to be the wall you broke against. I resigned this morning. Before the hearing. Whatever comes of today, I wanted it done clean — I wanted to walk in there as a man choosing, not a man falling."

"Elias—"

"And I know what you've lost today," he went on, faster, the way men talk when they're afraid the door will close. "Your station, your trade, every wall you ever slept inside. I know I'm asking you to step off the edge of the world with a man who has even less than you do. So don't answer now. Answer tomorrow, or next week, or—"

"Elias Ward." I took his face in my salt-stained hands. "I've counted my breaths my whole life so I wouldn't waste them. Don't make me waste this one."

He laughed — a broken, astonished sound — and kissed me under the fig trees while Broome went on shouting my name behind us.

Later, much later, walking back through the dark toward the nunnery where I'd been given a cot, I heard running footsteps and a voice calling my name, and it was Sister Bernadette, breathless, her habit pinned up for speed, and the look on her face stopped my heart before she said a single word.

"It's Nell," she said. "There's been trouble at Reiko's house. Come quickly."
