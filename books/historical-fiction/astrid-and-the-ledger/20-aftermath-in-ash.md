## 20. Aftermath in Ash

### Gideon

I ran the whole way to Reiko's house with my lungs full of broken glass.

The nunnery sat on the far side of town from the Japanese quarter, and Broome at night was a maze of sand tracks and lantern light and dogs, and I ran through all of it with Sister Bernadette's words beating in my skull like a second heart. *Trouble.* Trouble was a word that had followed me since a bundle appeared on my step in the rain. Trouble had taken Sefu. Trouble had nearly taken Nell once already, when the overseer carried her into the company house and called it care.

Reiko's door stood open. Lantern light spilled across the veranda boards, and inside there were voices — Reiko's, high and furious; another woman's, weeping; and beneath them, small and unmistakable, Nell's cry.

I went up the steps two at a time and into the front room, and there she was.

She was sitting on the floor in her nightdress, hair wild, face streaked with tears and dust, clutching the rag doll Sister Bernadette had made her by its one remaining arm. She saw me and her whole body changed — the way a sail changes when the wind finds it — and she held out both arms and screamed my name, or the word she used for me, which was not quite Gideon but close enough to break me where I stood.

I gathered her up. She was solid. Warm. Whole. I pressed my mouth against her hair and breathed her in, shell-smoke and soap and child, and only then did I look around the room.

A lamp lay shattered near the window. The shutter hung crooked on one hinge. Reiko stood with a broom handle still raised, breathing hard, her knuckles white around the wood. By the wall, a young man sat on the floor holding a cloth to his eyebrow, blood threading between his fingers. One of the pearling masters' men — I knew his face from the station, a tally-keeper named Croft who had always looked at me the way men look at things they believe they own.

"He came for the girl," Reiko said flatly. "Said the company's finished but its debts aren't, and the child is an asset of record. He had papers." She kicked a crumpled sheet across the floorboards toward me. "Signed by nobody who matters anymore."

Croft lifted the cloth from his brow and gave me a smile that had no fear in it, which told me everything about how little the hearing had frightened him yet.

"You heard the magistrate," he said. "Contracts voided. Company property returned to creditors pending liquidation." He nodded at Nell, at the doll, as if he could see the ledger entries written on her skin. "Indentured persons are assets. That includes foundlings registered under company welfare provisions. I've got the registration right here, missy. You've been keeping another man's property warm."

Nell tightened against my shoulder. I felt it go through her whole small frame like a current.

"The hearing isn't finished," I said. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "Corrigan sits again Thursday. And every name in that ledger is going to be read aloud, and yours is going to be among them if you don't walk out of this house now."

"Ledger's been burned," he said. "Auditor saw to—"

"Auditor burned the *company's* copies," said a voice from the doorway, and Elias stepped in out of the dark, hatless, coat over shirtsleeves, looking like a man who had run most of the way across town himself. "There's exactly one ledger left in the world, Croft, and it's sitting in Corrigan's strongbox. Your signature appears on page forty-one. I'd start thinking about what you'll say when they read it to you."

I had never seen Croft move fast before. I would not have believed a tally-keeper could fold himself through a doorway that quickly. His footsteps faded down the track, and then it was just us — the four of them, the ruined room, the child heavy and trembling in my arms.

Reiko lowered the broom. "They'll come back," she said. "Not him. Worse ones. When the liquidators arrive, everything with a company stamp becomes a claim, and that—" she touched Nell's foot, gently, "—that has a stamp on it somewhere. Paper doesn't know it's wrong, Gideon. Paper only knows it exists."

Elias met my eyes over Nell's head. Neither of us said it. We didn't have to. We had won the hearing and lost the war's last skirmish in the same week, and the thing we'd fought for was still, on paper, someone's property.

"She stays with me tonight," I said. "And tomorrow we put her name in front of Corrigan ourselves. If paper is the problem, then paper will fix it. A declaration of guardianship. A new record to drown the old one."

"And who'll swear for you?" Reiko asked. "You're a contract diver with a voided contract. In their eyes you're nobody."

"I'm somebody who reads," I said. "It turns out that counts for more than they thought."

### Elias

The letter dismissing me from His Majesty's Customs Service arrived on a Tuesday, in an envelope so thin it weighed nothing, which struck me as the single honest thing about it.

I stood in the customs house doorway and read it twice. *Conduct prejudicial to the proper execution of duty.* *Unauthorised removal of documents.* *Fraternisation with parties under investigation.* They had managed to make saving three hundred lives sound like embezzlement. There was no signature, only the stamp of the Department, blue and official and utterly without a face.

Behind me, Inspector Hargreaves cleared his throat.

"You'll want to hand over your keys," he said. He wouldn't look at me directly. He had spent eleven days in that courtroom listening to his own superior's signature read aloud in Gideon Eriksen's flat, unlovely voice, and he had not yet decided whether he despised me or envied me, and until he decided he would keep his eyes on the middle distance where such questions couldn't reach him.

I laid the keys on the desk. All of them, including the one to the manifest room I had never officially been issued and had copied years ago out of habit, because habit was the only inheritance my father had left me besides ruin.

"My father challenged a company once," I told Hargreaves, because I found I wanted to say it to someone, even this man, even now. "One letter to one minister. It took them eight months to destroy him and they did it with paper thinner than the one in my hand. I swore I'd never be that stupid."

Hargreaves shifted his weight. "And?"

"And I was," I said. "Gladly. It took longer, and it cost more, and it worked. Tell the Department whatever makes it easiest for you to sleep."

I walked out of the customs house at nine in the morning with nothing but my hat and my good left hand, and the sun came off Roebuck Bay so bright and indifferent it felt like mockery. Twenty-two years old when I joined. Thirty-one now. Ten years of tides and manifests and quiet corruption endured one compromise at a time, and the sum total of my career was a thin envelope I could have posted through a keyhole.

I ought to have felt ruined. Everyone in town clearly expected it; I could see it in the way the shopmen spoke to me, careful and slightly too kind, the tone reserved for men who had come down in the world. But walking along the jetty road with the wind coming in off the water, I kept catching myself almost laughing.

Because the strange truth was this: I had spent ten years being respectable, and I had never once felt clean. Now I was disgraced, unemployed, half in love with a penniless diver, legally entangled in a scandal that would follow me to every port in the colony — and I felt lighter than I had since boyhood. Ruin, it turned out, was mostly a matter of whose ledger you were entered in.

I stopped at the nunnery on my way through town, because I had learned in the past weeks that a day without seeing Gideon was a day spent holding my breath, and I was done counting breaths for other people's reasons.

She was in the convent yard with Nell on her hip and a stack of Corrigan's forms under her arm, arguing cheerfully with Sister Bernadette about ink. The late afternoon light turned everything the colour of brass. Nell saw me first and shouted a word that was either my name or an order, and Gideon turned, and the look on her face was worth ten dismissals.

"They let you keep the hat, then," she said.

"They can have the hat back too if they ask nicely." I took the stack of forms from under her arm and weighed it. "What's all this?"

"Guardianship petitions. Declarations of character. Affidavits." She ticked them off with a finger. "Corrigan says the law moves slowly unless you feed it. So we feed it. Reiko's swearing one. The nun's swearing one. Half the divers off the boats are queuing up to swear they've seen me with the child since the night she arrived, which is true, and the other half are swearing things that aren't true at all, which is kinder."

"And the company's claims?"

"Corrigan quashed the registration Thursday. Turns out a foundling registered 'for welfare purposes' was never legally an asset to begin with — the lawyers wrote the papers too greedily, back when no one dreamed anyone would read them." Her mouth curved. "Their own ledger language saved her. Every entry they made is a rope they tied around their own necks. We're just pulling them tight, one at a time."

I looked at her — salt-haired, form-stacked, victorious and homeless — and something in my chest went tight and sore.

"You know what happens next," I said quietly. Nell had become absorbed in feeding crumbs to a hen and was out of earshot, or pretending to be. "The liquidators finish. The station closes. The barracks are company property; you'll be turned out within the month. And I'm a dismissed officer with no references and a reputation that will follow me to Fremantle and back."

"I know."

"There's work on the luggers. Hard, badly paid, and the skippers will know your name from the papers — some of them won't touch you."

"I know that too."

"And none of it will be safe. Companies talk to each other. Men like Croft hold grudges the way other men hold religion."

"Elias." She stepped closer, and lowered her voice to the register she used for true things. "I lived nineteen years being safe. It looked like silence, and tasted like salt water, and it killed my father and bought me nothing. I'm done being safe. What I want to be is free, and free is going to be poor and difficult and possibly short." She glanced at Nell, and her voice caught just slightly on the next words. "But it will be ours."

### Gideon

The barracks fell silent the way a struck bell falls silent — all at once, and then ringing for a long time after.

They emptied us out over six weeks. Liquidators came with clipboards and chalk, marking crates and cots and diving helmets, everything stamped MERIDIAN TRADING CO., and we watched our own lives inventoried like cargo. The storeroom where Sefu had first guessed what I carried was stripped to the boards. The shell heaps were carted away by the ton, sold to pay debts to men in Perth who had grown fat on drowned divers and would lose nothing now but their appetites' supply.

My contract was voided with a line of a pen, which meant my wages ended with it, which meant the eleven shillings owed me for the final fortnight became, through some clerical alchemy I never fully followed, a debt *I* owed *them* for equipment depreciation. I signed the paper because refusing would cost more than eleven shillings. I signed it and walked out of the office and stood in the yard a long moment, looking at the drying racks where I had hung my suit for nineteen years, and understood that grief arrives late and dressed as ordinary things: a rack, a hook, the shape of a space where your own weight used to be.

The others scattered. Marta took work in the cannery down south. Old Yani, whose lungs had been giving out for years anyway, went to his sister in Derby and died in October, free, which everyone agreed was better than the alternative and nobody quite believed was enough. Some of the younger ones signed with the new companies moving in already, because hunger does not wait for history to sort itself out, and I did not blame them. I had been them. Silence buys bread the same as honesty does, and bread comes due every single day.

Sefu's berth stood empty. His hammock was gone from the barracks before the liquidators ever reached it. No one could tell me which ship had taken him north, or when, or whether he'd live out the year. I kept setting aside a share of every coin I earned anyway. I didn't examine why. Some accounts you keep open regardless of the arithmetic.

We lodged where lodging was cheap. For three weeks that meant the nunnery's charity ward, Nell sleeping between me and three feverish old women, learning hymns in two languages and neither properly. Then Reiko bullied her cousins into subletting us a room behind their laundry — one room, a shared stove, walls thin as prayer — and it felt, after the barracks, like a palace with a rent attached.

Elias took work loading coal. A former customs officer, hauling sacks down gangplanks in the heat while men who had taken Meridian money for years strolled past him in clean linen. He never complained. But I saw him sometimes in the evenings, washing the black off his hands, looking at them as though reading something written there, and I knew the taste of that particular water. Endurance looks like peace from the outside. I had worn the disguise myself for nineteen years.

"You regret it," I said one night. Not a question.

He considered it honestly, which was one of the thousand reasons for him. "I regret that it was necessary," he said finally. "The rest of it—" He shook his head. "No. Ask me what I regretted *before*. That list was longer."

The trial's shadow lengthened over everything, even as the verdicts came down. The overseer got seven years. Two inspectors got less and complained loudly about it. The directors in Perth paid fines calculated, Corrigan observed acidly from the bench, to be roughly equivalent to a bad season's pearls, and the newspapers called it justice with a straight face. Three hundred names read aloud in court had bought convictions for perhaps twenty men, and freedom for hundreds, and ruin for two people foolish enough to do the reading.

Some nights I lay awake doing that arithmetic and it came out wrong no matter how I arranged the columns. Nell asleep against my ribs. Elias's coat hanging on the nail. The empty space where a livelihood should be. Win, win, loss, loss. A ledger balances or it doesn't, and ours refused to.

Then one evening in November, Reiko came to the laundry room with a newspaper folded under her arm and an expression I hadn't seen on her face since before the hearing — something sly and bright, like weather about to change.

"Read the shipping notices," she said.

I read them. Most were the usual — arrivals, departures, auctions of wrecked hulls. And then, third from the bottom, a small advertisement that had cost someone sixpence:

*Lugger available for lease, 28 ft, sound hull, mooring at Town Jetty. Enquire W. Tanaka, chandler.*

Below it, in pencil, in Reiko's handwriting: *Tanaka owes me. He'll take less from friends of friends.*

I looked up. She was watching me with her arms crossed.

"Six shares," she said. "Me and mine take two. The freed divers who stayed in town — Dara, the Okafor brothers — they've been asking me every week what happens now, whether we just scatter and let them write the ending. Say the word and we pool what's left of everyone's wages and take that boat out ourselves. Independent. No company, no contract, no overseer. Just shell prices and weather, same as God intended and the government taxes."

"It's a hard life," I said. "Harder than the station, some seasons. And we'd be diving waters the companies still think belong to them."

"Yes," Reiko said. "That's rather the point."

From the corner, where she had been solemnly folding wash-cloths into shapes that resembled nothing on earth, Nell looked up at the two of us, then at the newspaper, and patted it once with a small sticky hand, as though placing a bid.

Elias came in an hour later, coal-dusted, tired, and found the advertisement pinned to the wall above the stove with a sewing pin, surrounded by a list of names and sums in three different hands — mine, Reiko's, Dara's — totalling, when I showed him, eleven pounds, four shillings and sixpence.

He studied it for a long moment. Then he took the pencil stub from the shelf, and beneath the column of names, in a customs clerk's precise copperplate, he added his own — and beside it, the figure of everything he owned in the world.

"That's not much," he said.

"It's exactly enough," I said. "It's what we have. That's what enough means now."

Outside, the tide was coming in over the flats, and somewhere down at the jetty a boat creaked against its mooring, empty, waiting, already ours if we could raise the difference by Friday.

I counted my breaths — one habit I would never give up — and for the first time in my life, I counted them toward something instead of away from it.
