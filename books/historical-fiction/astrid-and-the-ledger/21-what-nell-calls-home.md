## 21. What Nell Calls Home

The *Pearl of Broome* was a lie of a name for a boat — a forty-foot lugger with a cracked mast step, a hold that smelled of old shell and older fish, and a hull the colour of a drowned man's lips. She'd been laid up two seasons behind Ah Toy's yard, and every plank of her showed it.

Gideon loved her on sight.

"She leaks," said the yard owner, a Chinese man named Wing who had watched the company collapse from behind his fence with the expression of a man eating slowly at a banquet he'd been denied for years.

"Everything here leaks," Gideon said. "Water. Secrets. Blood."

Wing studied her. "You want her cheap."

"I want her honest. She leaks where I can see it."

He laughed then, a short bark, and named a price that was almost fair, and they shook on it before either could think better.

It took everything. That was the truth none of them said aloud in those first weeks. The wages the court had ordered paid out from Meridian's frozen accounts came to less than anyone had hoped — the lawyers took their share first, then the liquidators, then the tax man, each cutting slices off a carcass that had already been picked over by vultures wearing better suits. What remained for the divers who had given four, six, ten years of their lungs to the company was enough to live on for perhaps three months if they were careful, and not careful men and women were what most of them were.

So they pooled it. It had been Marta's idea first — Marta who had dived beside Gideon since they were both sixteen, whose hearing had gone thin on one side after a bad squeeze off Cable Beach, who had stood in the courtroom with her hands folded and her chin up while the lawyers tried to make her sound like a liar. "One boat between us," she'd said. "We know the grounds better than any master they ever shipped in from Singapore. We know where the shell lies. All we were ever missing was owning the thing that floats above us when we go down."

And so: the *Pearl of Broome*, leased-to-own from Wing, crewed by seven divers and a skipper, and a child.

Nell was three now, or near enough to three — no one knew her true birthday, so they celebrated it on the day the ledger arrived on the doorstep, which Gideon thought fitting. The child who had been left like an accusation had become something else entirely: a small loud creature with a gap between her front teeth and an opinion about everything. She had grown up these past two years among people who spoke softly around ledgers and magistrates, and she had learned exactly none of their caution. She walked the jetty like she owned the deed to it. She called the nun Sister Brigid "Bridie" to her face. And she had decided, sometime in the last month, that boats were the finest thing God had made.

* * *

They worked the old grounds at first, south toward the beds off Entrance Point, because the new crews didn't know them well and there was still shell to be had. The work hadn't changed. You went down on the rope with a breath counted out in your head — Gideon still counted, would always count, four strokes down, thirty seconds working, four strokes up — and you came back with shell and nothing else, and you prayed your ears held and your luck held and the tide didn't turn against you while you were blind in the green dark.

But everything else had changed, and the changes were small and enormous.

Nobody shouted at her anymore. That was the one she noticed first, floating on her back between dives, looking at the sky. On the Meridian luggers the overseers had patrolled the decks with canes and clipboards, counting shells and counting divers and counting the seconds you wasted breathing. Now Marta ran the deck with a voice pitched for conversation, and when Gideon surfaced, someone was always there with a hand out — not to haul her aboard fast enough to please a schedule, but just a hand, offered.

She caught herself waiting for the cane. For weeks, she flinched at raised voices on the water. Once, when a squall came up sudden off the point and Marta yelled for everyone to get the sail down, Gideon found herself braced for a blow that never came, and stood in the rain with her arms full of wet canvas and understood that some things take longer to free than others.

Elias came out with them when he could. He'd found work clerking for a shipping agent — a step down from customs, a long step down in pay, and he never complained about it except once, late, when he'd had a drink and he said, "I spent nine years learning to read manifests, and it turns out that's all I'm good for," and Gideon had taken his face in both her hands and told him he was good for considerably more than that, and they'd left it there, mostly.

On the boat he was useful and slightly ridiculous, a landsman among divers, but he kept the books — of course he kept the books — and his handwriting was beautiful, and when the buyers at the shell markets tried to shortchange them he argued figures with them until they gave in, because nobody argues arithmetic with Elias Ward and wins.

"He counts like you breathe," Nell informed Gideon solemnly one evening, having observed them both for some time. "All day."

Gideon laughed until her ribs hurt.

* * *

Sefu came back on a Tuesday in the wet season, walking up the jetty like a man returning from the moon.

Marta saw him first and went still, and then she was running, and then half the jetty was running, because word travels faster than feet and everyone on that stretch of planking knew what Sefu Okonkwo had endured and what he had refused to say. He was thinner. That was the first thing — thinner than Gideon remembered, and he had never been a large man. The harsh station up at Cossack had taken the flesh off him and left the bones, and his walk had changed too; he moved carefully, as though the ground might be pulled out from under him at any moment, which Gideon supposed it had been, more than once.

But his eyes were the same. That was what undid her. They put him in the same eyes they'd taken away.

"You're late," Gideon said, when she could speak at all. Her voice came out wrong, thick, and she didn't care.

"I heard the food was better here." He smiled, and the smile cost him something, and he paid it anyway. He looked past her shoulder at the lugger riding at her moorings, patched and ugly and theirs. "That yours?"

"Ours. If you want it."

He was quiet a moment. Gideon knew what the quiet was — she'd heard it from other men coming back from the northern stations, men who had to relearn that a question could be answered honestly without a price attached.

"What work?" he asked finally.

"Diving, if your chest allows. Deck work if it doesn't. Books, if you've a head for figures. Whatever you can do, and nothing you can't."

Sefu looked at the boat again for a long time. Then he said, "I'll try the water," and Gideon nodded, and that was the whole of the contract, and it was worth more than anything Meridian had ever put its seal to.

He dove within the fortnight. Not deep, not long — Marta set the limits herself and enforced them with the flat authority of a woman who had buried friends — but he went down, and he came up, and the first time he broke the surface with shell in his bag, working for himself and his own, he stayed up there treading water longer than he needed to, just looking around at the sky like a man confirming it was still there.

* * *

Nell learned the boat the way other children learned prayers.

She knew every board by feel, every rope by name — not their proper names, hers: the halyard was "the tall one," the tiller was "the steering bone," and the anchor, to her lasting delight, was "the fish hook." Sister Brigid despaired gently of her vocabulary. Gideon did not correct a single word of it.

The child had been two when she arrived on the doorstep with her father's ledger wrapped in her blanket, too young to remember the woman who had carried her there in the storm, too young to know why. But she remembered other things. She remembered the sea. Sometimes, standing at the rail with the wind pushing her hair back, she'd say things that stopped Gideon's heart: *my mama liked the big waves*. Or once, watching Gideon dress for a dive: *you go under like my mama.* Said plainly, without grief, the way children report weather.

Gideon never pried. She wrote the words down afterward, though, in the back of her own notebook, adding to a list she kept without quite admitting why. Somewhere out there was a woman named Adaeze Okafor, entered in the ledger's columns as *loss — diver, female, 1889*, who had tried to testify and been silenced for it, and somewhere out there might still be a family, an aunt, a mother, someone who had a right to the girl Nell had become. Gideon had searched the passenger lists twice through Elias's old contacts. So far, nothing. She did not stop looking. Some debts you don't get to declare settled just because the collection is hard.

But that was a weight for evenings. Days belonged to the water.

It was on a bright morning in March, running home before the wind with the sail full and the spray coming over the bow, that Nell planted herself at the tiller beside Marta, gripped the wood with both small hands, and announced to the entire crew:

"This is home."

Everyone laughed, because it was sweet, because she was three, because children claim things. But Gideon, coiling line at the rail, felt the words land somewhere deeper than cute. She looked at the child — salt-crusted, barefoot, sunburnt, utterly certain — and then at the people around her: Marta at the helm with her bad ear to the wind, Sefu hauling canvas with his careful careful movements, old Tomas singing something tuneless from the foredeck, the whole ragged crew of them, bought and sold and broken and now, improbably, afloat.

Home. Not the barracks she'd been indentured into at fourteen. Not the company house where the overseer had kept his ledgers and his lies. A leaking lugger with a cracked mast step and seven people who chose each other daily, on the water, where the old world couldn't follow.

"Yes," Gideon said, and Nell looked up at her, and Gideon said it again, louder, over the wind. "Yes, it is."

* * *

The money came slow and went slower. That was the rhythm of it. Good shell fetched fair prices now that the market wasn't cornered by a single buyer, but fair prices don't make rich men, and the *Pearl of Broome* ate repairs the way the sea eats shorelines. By the end of the first year they owned her outright — Wing took pity, or pleasure, and knocked the last instalment off with a wave of his hand — and by the end of the second they'd replaced the mast step and half the deck planks and bought a second smaller boat, the *Nell*, worked by two of the newer freed divers who wanted in.

Word spread, the way it does. Freed divers from the other stations started finding their way to Broome, and finding their way to Gideon's table, and asking — always hesitantly, always watching her face — whether there was room.

There was room. There was only so much room. Gideon turned no one away hungry, but she couldn't lease a fleet, and every refusal sat on her chest like ballast.

It was Sefu, of all people, who named the thing that was growing among them. They were mending net on the jetty one evening, Nell asleep against Marta's side with her mouth open, when he said, without looking up from his knots:

"You know what we are, don't you?"

"A crew," Marta said.

"We're a company." He tied the knot, tested it, looked up. His eyes were calm and very tired and very certain. "Small. Poor. Honest, so far. But a company. We buy shell, we sell shell, we pay wages, we keep books." He nodded at the ledger-sized notebook where Elias recorded their catches. "The difference is who holds the pen."

Gideon sat with that a long moment. She thought about the slim book that had arrived on her doorstep wrapped in a child's blanket — the original of which now lay open on a shelf in her rooms, not hidden, never hidden again. She thought about names in columns, and about who got to write them.

"Then we hold it carefully," she said.

"That," said Sefu, "is the whole job."

* * *

The letter came at the end of the wet season, forwarded twice and stained with salt, and Gideon read it standing at the galley window while the harbour went gold and quiet outside.

It was from a woman named Grace Okafor, writing from Thursday Island. Her sister Adaeze had signed a contract with Meridian in '88 and been listed lost the following year; the family had written eleven letters and received one answer, a form letter, *regret to inform*. Then, last year, a Perth newspaper had printed extracts from a certain ledger, and Grace had spent eighteen months writing to every port on the coast with one question: is there a child?

*My sister's daughter would be four now,* the letter finished. *Her name was Elna. We call her Nellie in the family. If your child answers to that name — if she has a scar on her left arm from a cooking fire when she was a baby — then I am her aunt, and I am coming to Broome, and God forgive me, I do not know yet whether I am coming to thank you or to take her.*

Gideon read it three times.

Then she went and knelt beside Nell's cot, where the girl slept with one fist curled under her chin and her hair stuck to her forehead with sweat, and very gently, with a heart going like a diver's hammer, she took the small arm and turned it to the moonlight.

There, on the soft skin above the elbow, pale and old and shaped like a crescent moon: a scar.

Gideon sat down on the floorboards and held the sleeping child's hand and did not sleep at all.
