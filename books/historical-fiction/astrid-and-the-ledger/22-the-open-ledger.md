## 22. The Open Ledger

The house has three rooms and a verandah that sags in the middle like an old diver's shoulders, and Gideon Eriksen would not trade it for the Meridian company house with its cool floors and its shutters against the wet. She built the shelves herself, from packing crates the *Pearl of Broome* came back with one season — jarrah, salt-bleached, holding nails that squeak when the wind comes up off the bay. On the second shelf, at eye level, where anyone who walks through her door can see it, sits the ledger.

Open. Always open.

It was Elias's idea first, though she had been turning it over for months before she said it aloud. A thing hidden stays a secret, and a secret is only ever worth what someone will pay to bury it. But a thing displayed — a thing with a lamp left burning beside it so no visitor can pretend they didn't see — becomes something else entirely. A record. A warning. A promise that it could happen again and be written down again, in the same steady hand, by whoever was brave enough or stubborn enough to hold the pen.

The handwriting is her father's. She knows that now the way she knows the sound of her own pulse. For years after the trial she told herself the book had come to her by accident, blown onto her step like storm wrack, but she stopped believing that somewhere around Nell's fifth birthday. Her father had copied those pages carefully, entries and dates and payments, in a hand that never slurred even when he must have known what the copying would cost him if he were caught. Men who write things down intend them to be found. He simply hadn't lived long enough to choose the finder. So the sea chose for him, and the sea, in the end, put the book in front of the one person alive who would never sell it back.

She dusts it every morning. That is the whole ceremony. A cloth across the cover, a check that the pages haven't curled in the humidity, a moment standing before it with her hands empty. Some mornings she reads a name at random and says it out loud, because a name spoken is a name kept. *Amina Osei, lost 1889.* *Tomas Reyes, sold to the Thursday Island station, age eleven.* *Sefu Adeyemi, transferred, returned.* The last one still makes her smile, small and private, the way you smile at an old scar that finally stopped aching.

* * *

Nell is nine now and tall for her age, all elbows and questions, with her mother's dark eyes — the mother whose name Gideon found in the ledger's cruelest column, *Maria Vasquez, deceased, child remanded to station care*, which was the company's way of saying *sold*. Gideon told her the truth of it early, earlier than most would have, because lies had nearly drowned this family once and she would not raise a girl on ballast. Nell took the story the way children take the sea: seriously, without flinching, already planning how she'd swim in it.

"Again," Nell says now, sitting cross-legged on the floor with the reading primer Elias brought back from Perth balanced on her knees. "Start again."

Gideon crouches beside her at the shelf. "You know how it starts."

"I know how *I* start." Nell points at the open page, at the first entry in her father's careful script. "But you said the names come first. Before stories. Before anything."

"The names come first." It has become liturgy between them, repeated every evening. Gideon taps the top line. "Sound it out."

"A-mi-na." Nell's finger moves under the letters, slow as a crab across wet sand. "Amina. O-sei. Amina Osei."

"And?"

"And she was lost in eighty-nine, and we say her name so she isn't lost twice." Nell recites it flat, bored, the way children recite things they've said a hundred times — and then, because she is nine and merciful in the way only children are, she adds, "Good morning, Amina," to the shelf, polite as a hostess, and Gideon laughs despite herself despite everything, the laugh coming up out of her chest like a breath she didn't plan.

That is the trick of it, she thinks. You teach a child the weight, and the child teaches you the lightness. She had spent four years carrying this book like a stone sewn into her suit lining, feeling it against her ribs even when it sat safely on the magistrate's bench, even after Corrigan returned it to her with the words *it belongs with the ones it names*. It took a girl who called a leaking pearling lugger *home* to teach her that a record can be carried openly or not at all.

"Now your letters," Gideon says, and moves to the table, and the morning goes on.

* * *

Elias marries her on a Tuesday in October, at low tide, because Gideon insists that anything important should happen when the sea is honest about what it is. There is no church within forty miles that will have them — Elias's dismissal from Customs followed him like a smell, and half the town still crosses the street when Gideon passes, uncertain whether they owe her gratitude or resentment, since both arrive wearing the same face. Magistrate Corrigan offers his garden. Sefu offers to cook. The freed divers offer everything else: flowers cut from the roadside, a fiddle played by a man missing two fingers to the bends, a wedding cake baked in a camp oven on the beach and tasting gloriously of woodsmoke.

"You know what they'll say," Elias murmurs as they stand in Corrigan's garden, frangipani dropping white coins at their feet. "The disgraced officer and the diver. They'll say we married out of the scandal."

"They'll say we married out of spite," Gideon agrees. "Let them. Spite kept me alive for six years. It's as good a foundation as any."

He laughs, and then goes quiet, and takes both her hands in his — customs man's hands, ink-stained, a faint tremor in the left that never quite healed from the night the overseer's men caught him at the telegraph office and worked him over in the alley while Gideon dove the old channel with the book strapped to her chest. "I meant to do this properly," he says. "With a ring I couldn't afford and words someone else wrote. I had a whole speech."

"Spare me the speech."

"Gideon." His voice drops to the register he used across checkpoints and search tables, the voice that meant *listen, and mean it*. "I spent my whole life watching ledgers. My father ruined himself challenging one, and I swore I'd learn to read them instead of fight them, and then a woman looked at me across a station gate like she was measuring exactly how much of a coward I was, and I—" He stops. Starts again. "You never let me lie to you. Not once, not even the kind lies. Marry me and keep doing that. Keep me honest. It's the hardest work I've ever loved."

She looks past his shoulder, just briefly, to where Nell sits on Sefu's shoulders in the crowd, solemn as a magistrate herself, and to the divers standing easy in clothes without company numbers stitched at the collar, and to the sea beyond, lying flat and silver under an October sun, keeping none of its secrets today.

"Yes," she says. "All of it, yes."

Sefu whoops so loudly the frangipani sheds another coin, and the fiddle strikes up, and the tide, honest to the last, begins its slow return across the flats.

* * *

The boat is twenty-two feet of stubbornness held together with jarrah, canvas, and argument. They named her the *Open Ledger*, over Gideon's objection that naming a working vessel after evidence was asking for trouble, over Sefu's objection that it was morbid, over everyone's objection until Nell — six years old at the time, standing in the shallows with her arms crossed — declared that books shouldn't have to hide and neither should boats, and the vote was carried by acclamation of one very small tyrant.

She is slower than the company luggers and draws too much water for the shallow grounds, and she has never once failed to bring them home. Gideon dives from her rail the way she dove under contract, counting breaths — but the count means nothing now except how long until the next shell. No foreman times her. No tally clerk records her bottom-time against her debt. When she surfaces, Sefu hauls the net, Nell bails the well, Elias works the winch he rebuilt twice with his own hands, and the shells go into their own hold, sold at their own price, to buyers who sign their own papers.

Some mornings, treading water in the green light before the drop-off, Gideon thinks about the girl she was at nineteen, counting breaths in Meridian waters, believing silence was the same thing as peace. She wants to tell that girl: *you were wrong, but not foolish. Silence kept you breathing until breathing could mean something more.* She wants to tell her about the bundle on the step, the candle-lit nights, the gate and the jetty and the wire, the courtroom where she read names until the lawyers stopped interrupting, the overseer breaking apart on the stand like bad shell. She wants to tell her that survival is not the finish line. It's the starting buoy.

Then she kicks for the surface, because there are shells to gather and a life above the waterline that won't wait.

* * *

Sefu came back in the third year, thin as a fish spine and walking with a stick, off a ship out of Derby with no papers and no explanation of how he'd got free of the De Grey station except a shrug that said some doors are only locked from the outside if you agree they are. He walked down the jetty without hurrying, found them mending nets, and said, "I hear the food here is terrible and the company worse," and Gideon put down her needles and hugged him hard enough to crack a rib, and that was the entire negotiation.

He sleeps in the loft now, when he isn't at sea. He taught Nell to gut fish and to read currents and to swear in three languages, and every Sunday he stands at the shelf with the ledger and reads one name aloud, whichever falls under his finger, and says, "I knew his brother," or "She sang better than anyone on the boats," or sometimes, quietly, "I don't remember why I know this one. But I know it." The book collects memories the way a shell collects nacre, layer over layer, until the grain of it shines.

* * *

There is a letter on the table tonight, and Gideon reads it twice before she lets herself feel anything about it.

It is from Perth. From the newspaper — the same one that ran the telegraphed extracts, seven years ago, and set the colony alight. A young journalist, by the signature; the old editor who took their wire has gone. The letter says that other companies, other stations, other colonies are stirring. That a parliamentary committee is being seated to look into indenture practices across the whole northwest coast. That the writer has heard — everyone has heard; the story travels with the tides — of a woman in Broome who keeps the original record open on her shelf, and would she consider lending it? Copying it? Speaking to the committee?

Would she come to Perth and testify again?

Gideon sets the letter down. Through the window, dusk is coming up violet over the bay, and the *Open Ledger* rides at her mooring, lantern lit, Nell's voice drifting off her deck where the girl is supposed to be washing shells and is audibly telling Amina Osei's name to the gulls instead. Elias is at the stove, burning rice the way he burns rice every single time, and Sefu is laughing at him, and the smoke smells like every evening they have ever survived together.

She thinks about the cost. She thinks about it honestly, the way she taught Nell to think — no ballast, no sentiment. Testimony in Perth means weeks away from the boat in season. It means her name in the papers again, the resentful half of Broome sharpening its knives, the buyers who deal with her politely going cold. It means digging up Maria Vasquez and Tomas Reyes and every diver who ever went into the water owing a debt that drowning didn't cancel, and handing their bones to men in suits to be weighed and argued over.

She looks at the shelf.

The ledger sits open in the lamplight, patient as it has always been, pages holding their columns of the dead and the sold and the stolen. It did not ask to rest on this shelf. It did not ask to stop at anything — not at a magistrate's door, not at a newspaper's front page, not at a committee room in a capital city a thousand miles south. Books like this one only ever want one thing: to keep being read, further and further, until the reading changes something.

"Nell," Gideon calls through the window. "Come in and bring your primer."

The girl scrambles up the steps, salt-haired, grinning, and slides the primer onto the table next to the letter without asking what the letter is, because Nell can read a room the way her mother reads water.

"What is it?" she asks anyway.

Gideon turns the letter toward her. "It's a question. Someone far away wants to hear the names again."

Nell considers this with the terrible gravity of the nine-year-old judge she is. "Well," she says, "we say them every day anyway. Might as well say them where it counts."

From the stove, Elias has gone quiet, spatula in hand, watching Gideon with the expression he wore across a customs table seven years ago — the one that says *I'll follow whatever you decide, and I decided a long time ago*. Sefu leans in the doorway, stick under his arm, and nods once, slow, like a man agreeing to weather he can already feel coming.

Gideon picks up the pen.

Later, after the rice is rescued and Nell is asleep and the lamp is trimmed low, she stands a moment at the shelf, as she does every night, and reads the last line of her father's last entry, the one she has never needed to look at to know: *If any find this, let them count what was taken.*

Outside, the tide is turning, and the bay is full of moonlight, and tomorrow there will be shells to gather and names to read and, now, a long road south with a heavy book wrapped in oilskin — but carried in the open this time, in daylight, in the sight of anyone who cares to look.

She counts her breaths.

They are her own.
