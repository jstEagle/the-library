## 1. The Weight of Lead

The lead came to us in three forms: as scrap, as pigs, and as grief.

I learned the order of things before I learned my letters. Scrap first—old type worn past use, misstruck sorts, the slugs my father pried from locked-up pages that had never earned their ink—all of it into the melting pot, where it went soft and silver-skinned at six hundred degrees and gave off a smell like rain on hot slate. Then the pigs, the clean ingots from the smelter upriver, each stamped with the assay mark, each one a promise of new letters not yet born. And last, grief, which was what my father called the lead we melted down in secret, the matrices and punches of faces he had cut and then unmade, letter by letter, until no proof remained that they had ever existed at all.

That morning in February of 1844, I was sorting sorts at the long table by the north window while the pot hissed on its iron trivet behind me, and outside the glass the river lay locked under ice the color of old pewter.

Sorting is work for hands that want to think about something else. The case sits before you with its hundred and eighty-eight compartments, and you take a fistful of mixed type from the composing stick's leavings and let it run through your fingers into the right boxes—the lowercase e's to their deep middle drawer, the f's and i's and l's tall in their upper ranks, the em-quads fat in the corner like sleeping cats. Your fingers learn the faces by their shoulders, by the weight of them, by the small nicks some founders cut into the shanks so a compositor could tell one face from another in the dark. My father cut no nicks. He said a face should be known by its features or not at all. He said this the way he said most things, quietly, to the metal rather than to me.

"Jonah." His voice from across the shop, low, half-swallowed by the sound of the graver biting steel. "The e's are drifting."

I looked down. He was right. In the lamplight I could see I'd been letting the e's slide toward the c's, a sloppiness of perhaps a point's width, invisible to anyone but him, and to me now that he'd named it.

"They're drifting because they're tired," I said. "It's been e's since dawn."

"Then be glad you aren't an e." But there was the ghost of warmth in it, the almost-smile he permitted himself when the Ministry wasn't listening, and I bent back to the case and coaxed the letters home.

* * *

The Vasquez Type Foundry occupied the ground floor and cellar of a narrow stone building on Saltpetre Lane, four doors down from the chandler's and directly across from the wall of the dye-works, whose brick had gone the color of dried blood from a century of madder smoke. From the street you saw only a door without a sign, a brass bell worn smooth as a knuckle, and a single window through which, if you pressed your face to the glass—and no one did—you might see racks of type cases receding into shadow like the shelves of some library for the illiterate. We had no sign because signs invited reading, and reading invited questions, and questions were the one commodity the Vasquez family had spent thirty years learning to live without.

My father bought the shop the year before I was born, back when Velmora still pretended to be a city of merchants instead of a city of permissions. In those days a founder could hang his name over the door and take pride in it. Marek Vasquez had cut faces for three of the four newspapers then published on the riverfront, for the university press, for the hymnal commission of the Cathedral of Saint Aldric. There were books in the cathedral sacristy set entirely in his letters, and if you knew how to look—if you knew the way his g's dropped their lower bowl a hair too far, the way his terminals ended in a small deliberate blunt-ness, like a sentence interrupted—you could find his hand everywhere in the printed city, the way a mother finds her child's handwriting on every wall.

You cannot look for it anymore. The newspapers are two now, both owned by the Ministry of Information, and the university press prints only what the Rectorate approves, and the hymnals have been reissued in a face commissioned from a founder in the capital who has never seen our river and does not know that ice makes a sound before it breaks, a groan like a door in a house where no one is awake.

We survived by becoming small. That was the strategy, though my father would have flinched at the word *strategy*, because strategies implied ambition and ambition implied visibility. We simply made ourselves uninteresting. No portraits hung in our rooms above the shop—not because we couldn't afford a painter, but because a face on canvas is a face that can be recognized, and recognition is the first step down a road we did not walk. No petitions, ever, to any office, for any reason; a family that petitions is a family that exists in a file. No opinions offered in the market, at the well, in the pew. When the tax assessor came, we paid promptly and precisely, neither a coin more nor less, because generosity invites scrutiny and stinginess invites resentment, and both invite a second visit. When the parish priest asked after our health, we answered *well, thank God* in a tone so flat that even God might have taken offense, and Father Anselm, who was not a stupid man, learned within a year to ask us nothing further.

My brother Tomas called it cowardice once, when he was sixteen, standing in the doorway of the shop with river mud on his boots and some grievance fresh in his mouth. *We live like moles,* he said. *We dig and we keep our eyes shut.* My father did not raise his voice. He took Tomas's hand—he was a gentle man, my father, in the way of men who work with sharp tools all day—and he pressed Tomas's fingertips against the edge of a freshly cast sort, a lowercase k in the old Aldric face, still warm from the mold. Not hard enough to cut. Just enough that Tomas felt the heat, and the crispness, and the terrible small perfection of the thing.

*Everything sharp is also fragile,* my father told him. *And everything fragile survives by not being noticed. Now go wash your hands before you touch anything.*

Tomas washed his hands. But I saw him looking at the k afterward, turning it in the light, and I understood that something in him had been reached that the lesson was meant to close off. You cannot show a boy a beautiful dangerous thing and expect the beauty to die of the warning.

* * *

By midmorning the shop had warmed to the temperature of work. The stove in the corner threw its thin heat against the damp rising from the cellar, where the casting room lived, and the two temperatures met somewhere around my elbows and stayed there, uneasy, all day. The smell of the place was itself a kind of weather: hot lead and lampblack, the tannin tang of the oak gall ink we mixed for proofs, the horsehair-and-beeswax polish my father rubbed into the wooden handles of his gravers until they gleamed like chestnuts. Underneath it all, fainter than everything, the river—that cold mineral breath that comes off the ice and seeps under doors and tells you, whatever the calendar says, exactly what month your body believes it is.

At the back bench, my father worked on the Ministry's commission.

He had received it eleven days earlier, delivered not by messenger but by a clerk named Herr Fossen who arrived in a government gray coat and stood in our shop for forty minutes without removing his gloves, as though our air might contaminate him. The commission was for a commemorative text face—to be used in official publications of the Regent's jubilee, two years hence—and the specifications ran to nine pages of dense bureaucratic prose that managed, through some alchemy of phrasing, to communicate a single instruction: *the face must be dignified, modern, and in no way distinctive.* It must carry authority without personality. It must be legible at any size and memorable at none. It must, in short, be everything my father had spent his life refusing to make.

He had accepted it the way we accepted everything. He read the specifications twice, nodded, and said, "The delivery date will be met," and Herr Fossen wrote that down in a leather notebook as though recording testimony, and left, and only after the bell above the door had finished swinging did my father exhale, long and slow, the way a man exhales when he has been holding his breath since the knock.

Now he bent over the cutting block, and I watched him sidelong between handfuls of type, because watching my father cut steel was the closest thing to worship our household permitted.

He worked in punches first—a mirror image of the letter, cut backward into a bar of hardened steel finer than a needle's width at its thinnest strokes. His left hand held the punch against the block; his right hand drove the graver in strokes so small they seemed less like carving than like the movements of a man writing a letter too precious to finish. Between strokes he raised the punch to the loupe screwed into the bench, and the lamplight caught the steel, and for a moment his whole face changed—the caution fell away, the flattening, the careful dullness he wore like a coat in public—and what remained was a man in love. There was no other word for it. My father loved letters the way other men loved women or cards or God, hopelessly, specifically, with an attention that would have been embarrassing if anyone had ever been allowed to see it.

The face he was cutting for the Ministry was deliberately, painstakingly dull. He was spending the finest craft in Velmora on the production of nothing. And he did it anyway, stroke after stroke, hour after hour, because the alternative to serving the Ministry's mediocrity was to be interesting to the Ministry, and interesting was a word that got people taken away in closed carriages.

I sorted sorts and thought about the word *taken*. It was a word we used in our house the way other families used names for diseases—rarely, precisely, and always in a lowered voice. People were *taken* in Velmora. Not arrested; the courts had not been abolished, technically, but their dockets had grown strange, and the word that traveled through the markets and the docks and the chandler's back room was simpler and colder. Taken. A printer from the Weft district, taken in autumn. A schoolmistress who had taught geography with maps that showed borders the Ministry did not recognize, taken before the first frost. Each time, my father would hear the news at the exchange where he bought his lead, and he would come home and say nothing at dinner, and later, after Tomas and I had gone up to bed, I would hear the cellar door open and close, very softly, and know that he was downstairs melting something.

In the morning there would be a little less of him in the world. A face erased. A drawer emptied. The lead returned to the pot, formless again, innocent again, ready to become someone else's harmless letters.

* * *

The noon bell rang from Saint Aldric's, muffled by the fog that had come up off the ice, and my father straightened his back with the sound of a man cracking green wood, and we ate bread and hard cheese and pickled beet standing at the side bench, because sitting down fully meant the afternoon would feel longer.

"Tomas was out late," I said. It was not quite an accusation. Accusation required a position of authority, and in our family authority was a currency so carefully hoarded that no one spent it.

"He was at the docks."

"The docks close the gates at ten."

"Then he climbed one." My father chewed, looking at the window, where the fog had thickened until the dye-works wall was only a rumor. "He's nineteen, Jonah. Nineteen-year-olds climb things. I climbed things."

"You climbed things before," I said, and stopped, because the sentence had a second half and we both heard it waiting there. *Before.* Before the tightening. Before the two newspapers became one voice. Before the word *taken* entered our vocabulary. Tomas had been born into the tightening; he had never known the loose years, and so he did not fear them the way we did, the way you do not fear a fever you have never had.

My father set down his bread. For a moment he looked older than his fifty-one years—older than the shop, almost, older than the river—and then the look folded itself away, as it always did, into the mild competent blankness of Marek Vasquez, founder, taxpayer, man of no opinions.

"When the census comes," he said, "you'll answer the door."

"I answered last time."

"And you'll answer this time. You're better at it. You don't fill silences." He picked up his bread again, and this was the whole of the conversation, because in our house the important instructions were always embedded in ordinary ones, like the fine white hairlines in a bar of lead that tell the founder where the impurities lie.

The census. I turned the word over while I worked through the afternoon, sorting the morning's scattered sorts into their cells, and felt the small cold weight of it settle somewhere beneath my ribs. The census had come every third year for as long as I could remember, and every third year it had been the same: a bored functionary with a damp notebook, questions asked in a monotone, answers given in mine. Names, ages, trades. *Vasquez. Two children. Typefounder. Deceased: Livia Vasquez, wife, 1841.*

My mother had died in the winter of 1841. That was what the household record said, in my father's hand, in ink he had mixed himself. Fever, the record said. Buried in the parish ground at Saint Aldric's—or no. I searched my memory the way I searched a type case, finger by finger, compartment by compartment, and found the strangest thing: the compartments where her death should have been stored held shapes but no details. I remembered that she was gone. I remembered my father's face at the funeral, which had been small and private, held in the chapel's side room with only Father Anselm and the four of us—no, the three of us—present. I remembered coming home to a shop that smelled the same and yet was emptier, the way a case is emptier after a sort has been removed, leaving a cell that your fingers keep reaching for out of habit.

What I could not remember was the fever. Whose cough, whose doctor, what was written on the certificate. It was as if the memory had been melted down along with everything else my father had carried to the pot in those grey months, poured back into the common stock of lead, recast as something neutral.

She had been dead three years. Three years was long enough that the grief had settled into its final form, which is not peace but architecture: a shape you build your days around without looking at it. Her chair by the kitchen fire had been given to the chandler's widow. Her sewing box held my father's spare gravers now. Only upstairs, in the narrow room that had been hers, did anything remain untouched—the bed made, the shutters closed, the dust accumulating with the patience of snowfall—and we did not go in, and I had stopped asking why sometime in the second year, because the asking made my father's hands go still in a way that frightened me more than anger ever had.

The pot needed feeding. I gathered the day's scrap—the misstruck sorts, the worn e's and a's retired from the commercial face we kept for jobbing work, a slug of lead my father had pried from a locked page that morning—and carried it to the melting pot, and tipped it in, and watched the metal take the heat.

This was the part of the work I loved best and trusted least. The lead went down soft, lost its edges, forgot what it had been. A letter that had printed ten thousand copies of a grain merchant's bill, a letter that had sat in a case beside my fingers for years, known to me by its shoulder and its nickless shank—all of it slid into the silver pool and became undifferentiated, anonymous, ready to be anything. There was mercy in that. There was also, I thought, skimming the dross off the surface with the iron ladle, a lesson the Ministry had learned before we did: that it is easier to destroy a thing by melting it than by breaking it, because broken pieces can be counted, and counted pieces can be missed, and missed pieces can be mourned. Melted things leave no inventory. Melted things leave only a slightly larger silence.

Outside, the light was going the color of tarnished copper, and the ice on the river groaned—a long, low complaint, the sound of a giant shifting in a sleep that would not last forever. Spring was six weeks away and already announcing itself. In six weeks the ferries would run again, the ice road would close, the river would belong to the water and the water to whoever held the permits.

I raked the coals and banked the pot and wiped my hands on the leather apron, and my father blew out the lamp over the cutting block, leaving the day's punch clamped in its vise—the new Ministry letter, half-born, its unfinished curves catching the last window light like something drowning.

"Early night," he said. "The census man may come tomorrow. They're working the lane."

"How do you know?"

"Aldric's bell rang eleven times today." He said it mildly, gathering his tools, not looking at me. "It rings twelve on Thursdays. Someone wanted the lane to think it was Thursday."

I stood a moment with the ladle still in my hand, feeling the truth of it arrive slowly, the way cold comes up through boot soles. Somewhere in the fog beyond the dyeworks wall, a man with a notebook was counting houses, and my father had counted him first.

"Go up," my father said. "Wash the lead off. And Jonah—" He paused, and in the pause I heard him decide, deliberately, to say the smaller thing instead of the larger one, as he had done all my life. "Answer the door politely. And don't fill the silences."

I climbed the stairs to our rooms above the shop, and washed, and lay in the dark listening to the river groan, and did not sleep, because tomorrow a stranger would stand in our doorway with the state's questions in his mouth, and I would give him our rehearsed answers, name by name, year by year—

*Livia Vasquez. Wife. Deceased 1841.*

—and for the first time in three years, I found I could not remember whether the words had ever been true.
