## 2. A Question Too Many

She came at half past nine, when the frost on the windows had begun to weep and the foundry smelled of hot metal and yesterday's ink.

I heard her before I saw her—the particular knock of a person who has been trained to knock, two raps and a pause, neither timid nor insistent. Father's hand stopped over the composing stick. Tomas, who had been hauling a crate of type metal toward the melting furnace, set it down without a sound. In our house a knock was never just a knock; it was a sentence to be parsed.

"Ministry of Records," said a voice through the door. A woman's voice. Clear, unhurried, with the flat northern vowels of someone educated past her station. "Census enumeration. I'm expected."

We were expected. The notice had come eleven days ago, printed on gray stock in a typeface I did not recognize—some government contract farmed out to a cheaper house—and it had sat on Father's desk like a stone ever since. *All households within the Third Ward will submit to enumeration. Accurate records are the foundation of good order.* Eleven days of rehearsal at the supper table: name by name, year by year, trade and wage and bed count, until even Tomas could recite it in his sleep, and he resented every syllable.

Father wiped his hands on his apron, which only spread the ink, and opened the door himself. That was deliberate. A man answers his own door; a household that hides sends its children.

"Good morning," she said. "Astrid Lindqvist, enumerator for the Third Ward." She held up a leather folio stamped with the Ministry seal, and beneath it I saw the edge of a printed schedule, columns ruled fine as hair. "I'll need perhaps twenty minutes of your time. Perhaps less, if your accounts are kept well."

"They're kept," Father said.

"I'd expect nothing less from a foundry." She stepped inside, and that was the first thing wrong with her—not rudeness, not suspicion, but the ease of it, as though she had already decided what she would find and was merely collecting confirmation.

I stood at the sorting bench with a handful of sorts in my palm and did not put them down. She was younger than I had imagined from the voice—younger than me by a year or two, perhaps—with pale hair scraped back so severely it looked like punishment, and a face built for forgetting: ordinary brow, ordinary mouth, eyes the color of river water in March. She wore a gray coat buttoned to the throat and carried no umbrella against the dripping eaves, as if weather were something that happened to other people.

"You'll be the daughter," she said, finding me across the room. Not a question.

"Jonah Vasquez."

"And the son?"

"Tomas," Tomas said from the furnace, his voice pitched carefully between boredom and deference. He had learned that trick young. We all had.

* * *

She worked the way a compositor works, I noticed later—systematically, left to right, never skipping a line, never going back except to verify. She began with the building itself: date of construction, square footage, whether the dwelling above was occupied by the same household. Then the business: licenses, apprentices registered or unregistered, output categories. Father answered everything in the flat recitation he used for customs men, each fact polished smooth from handling.

"Tonnage of lead purchased annually?"

"Varies. Fourteen hundredweight in a lean year. More when the Ministry commissions run large."

"The Ministry is a good customer, then."

"The Ministry is a customer," Father said, and something in his tone made her pen pause for half a heartbeat before continuing.

Tomas answered his questions about dock work in monosyllables. I answered mine about the foundry in slightly more, because a daughter who talks too little is as strange as one who talks too much, and I had been taught to be forgettable in the way water is forgettable—present everywhere, remarkable nowhere. I told her about sorting sorts, about the melting pot, about how you can tell a good alloy by the sound it makes cooling. This was true, and true things wear best under questioning.

Through all of it she wrote in a small, fast hand, upright, economical, every letter formed exactly like its neighbors. I watched her write my name and thought: *she would make a fair typesetter.* Then I thought: *what a strange thing to notice,* and stopped noticing.

The stove ticked. The river groaned somewhere beneath the floorboards, ice shifting in its long slow argument with spring. Frost came down the window glass in ferns.

And then, twenty minutes in, she turned to a new page, ruled differently from the others, and said, without any change in her pleasant, procedural voice:

"Now the deceased members of the household."

The room did not go silent. That would have been too easy, and rooms in our family never gave anything away so cheaply. The fire crackled; the melting pot murmured; Tomas went on stacking crates with the same careful rhythm. But inside me something went very still, the way the river goes still under ice—not calm, but listening.

"My wife," Father said. "Livia Vasquez. Deceased 1841."

"Of what cause?"

"Fever. The winter fever."

"Were there children born who did not survive?" She asked it gently, the way one asks about weather damage. It was a question designed to be answerable. Grief, properly administered, loosens tongues; every interrogator knows this, and apparently every census taker does too.

"One," Father said. "A girl. Unnamed. 1836."

"And your wife's death—" Astrid Lindqvist turned the folio around on the bench so that we could see the page, though none of us moved to look. "Was entered in the parish register."

It was not a question. That was the second thing wrong with her. Questions give you room to move; statements leave you standing on ice and hearing it crack.

"It was a fever winter," Father said again, as if repetition were mortar. "The parish clerk was overwhelmed. Half the ward buried that month."

"Half the ward," she agreed pleasantly. "Yet the register shows four hundred and eleven entries for February 1841, and no Vasquez among them. I checked before I came." She said it the way she might have said *the frost is heavy this morning*. "Burial entries are rarely missed, Mr. Vasquez. Even in a bad winter. Especially for a woman with a named grave plot—which yours appears to have, in the ledger of the Old North Yard, purchased 1839, though the interment record is blank."

The sort in my hand had grown warm. Or my hand had gone cold; I couldn't have told you which. I looked at my father's back—at the slope of his shoulders over the composing stick, at the ink stain shaped like a swallow on his left cuff—and I performed the arithmetic of a lifetime's training: *do not speak, do not fill silence, silence is where they fish.*

But Astrid Lindqvist was not fishing. She closed the folio, and laced her fingers over it, and looked at the three of us with an expression of such mild, professional regret that it was somehow worse than accusation.

"I want to be clear," she said. "Discrepancies of this kind are extremely common. A death at home, a hasty burial, a clerk's oversight—it happens dozens of times in every ward, every year. My duty is simply to note the discrepancy and refer it to the registrar, who will send someone round with a correction form. It's tedious, not dangerous." A small pause. "Unless, of course, it isn't tedious."

"What would make it not tedious?" Tomas asked. His voice had an edge on it now, the dockworker's edge, and I wanted to close my hand over his mouth the way you stop up a leaking seam.

"A pattern," she said. She looked at me then—directly, fully, the first time she had granted me her whole attention—and I understood that everything before had been prologue, the tuning of an instrument. "Households where the paperwork is immaculate are interesting, Miss Vasquez. Most people keep their papers badly. They lose receipts, miss dates, guess at their own ages. Your father's ledgers could serve as a model for the Ministry's own clerks. And in my experience—four wards, six years—immaculate papers are either the mark of a very honest man or a very careful one." She smiled, and it reached her eyes without warming them. "Velmora has both kinds."

"The census asks for the living," Father said quietly. "My wife is dead. Whether a clerk wrote it down is between him and his God."

"Entirely," she agreed. "And between me and my form forty-one-B, unfortunately. Bureaucracy devours us all, Mr. Vasquez. It's the one faith we share." She reopened the folio and wrote for a moment—I watched her pen move, quick and certain—and then asked the remaining questions in the remaining minutes: births since 1841 (none), marriages (none), persons lodging or boarding (none), and each answer fell into her columns like lead into a matrix, snug and final.

At the door she paused. Outside, the street had brightened; sleet had given way to thin, hard sunlight that lay along the cobbles like spilled tin.

"Thank you for your time," she said. "You've been very helpful." And then, turning back with her hand still on the frame, in exactly the tone she might have used to mention a delivery: "Miss Vasquez. Do you resemble your mother?"

The question went through me like a punch through wet paper—no resistance, straight into the soft place behind everything.

"People say so," I heard myself say. My voice sounded borrowed.

"Do they? That must be nice." She studied my face for one more second than politeness required—not my features, I realized afterward, but something else, some flicker, some failure of the rehearsed calm—and then she nodded to us all, briskly, kindly. "Good morning. You may hear from the registrar's office within the fortnight. Nothing to worry about. It's only a form."

She went down our steps and along the quay, folio under her arm, coat dark against the light, and she did not look back once, which I noted, because people who are finished looking do not look back, and people who intend to return save themselves the trouble.

* * *

For a long moment none of us moved. Then Tomas let out a breath and said, "She checked the register. Who checks the register?"

"Somebody thorough," Father said. He had returned to the composing stick as though the morning were a thing that happened to other people, but I saw his hands, and his hands were not right. A compositor's hands are sure even in sleep; his were setting the same line twice, pulling sorts he didn't need, laying them down crooked.

"She knows something," Tomas said. "That last question—"

"It was nothing."

"It wasn't nothing, Papa. *Do you resemble your mother?* Who asks—"

"A census taker asks whatever the form leaves out." Father's voice had the terrible evenness it took on when the ground beneath him was moving. "Finish the crate, Tomas. Jonah—the Havel commission wants its proofs by Thursday, and I'll not lose the Ministry's business over one woman's form forty-one-B."

He bent to his case. Tomas glowered and hauled his crate. The foundry resumed its noises, the murmur and tick and tap, the whole familiar machinery of our invisibility grinding back into motion.

And I stood at my bench with my handful of sorts—M's and N's, I saw now, I'd been holding them the whole time, the two letters every apprentice confuses—and I thought about her pen moving across the page, quick and certain, writing down the discrepancy. *Refer it to the registrar.* Within the fortnight. A correction form, she'd said. Only a form.

But I had grown up in a house made of forms. I knew what paper could do. Paper had erased my mother once already—no register entry, no funeral we spoke of, a blank where an interment should be, three years of silence sealed over like a wound under wax. If the state's paper had swallowed her once, why not again? Why not us?

And underneath that, quieter, in the place where I kept the things I did not say even to myself: *she looked at my face when she asked it. Not at my features. At my reaction. As though she already knew what resemblance meant here. As though she hadn't come to count us at all.*

The melting pot murmured. The river groaned. And in the drawer beneath my mother's old sorting bench—a bench Father had never let anyone clear, these three years, though it served no purpose but to hold dust and grief—something waited that I would not find for another week, and would not survive unchanged after finding it.

I set the M's back in their box. I set the N's beside them. And I told myself the woman from the Ministry was nothing, only a form with legs, only weather passing through—

—but all that day, and long into the dark, the river groaned, and I listened.
