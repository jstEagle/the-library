## 3. What Father Cut Away

The punchcutter's lamp threw a circle of light no wider than a dinner plate, and Marek Vasquez had spent thirty years learning to live inside circles that small.

He sat at the graver's bench past midnight, the foundry below him silent except for the tick of cooling metal and the river's low complaint against the pilings. In his hands: a steel blank the size of a thumbnail, and in his mind: the letter *g* the Ministry wanted for their new face—a *g* with a closed, obedient loop, a letter that would never dream of opening itself into anything else. He had cut four hundred letters in his life, drawn them first on paper, argued with himself over terminals and stress, signed his name to proofs. This one he was cutting from another man's drawing, unsigned, uncredited, and he had already caught himself twice trying to improve it.

Improvement was a form of opinion. Opinion was a form of signature. Signature was how people ended up on lists.

He set down the graver and rubbed his eyes until sparks swam there. From the shelf above the bench, where it had stood for three years between a jar of quill splitters and a tin of lampblack, the box looked back at him. Birchwood, brass corners gone green at the seams, the lid warped by damp so that it never quite closed—so that its contents breathed out at him whenever the wind shifted through the shutters. He had meant to burn it long ago. He had told himself every winter since that this would be the winter.

Tonight the census taker's visit sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

*And your late wife? Deceased 1841?*

The girl had asked it gently. They always did. That was what made them dangerous—not cruelty but courtesy, the soft voice that made you want to fill the silence with more than you'd rehearsed. Jonah had answered well. *My mother died in the winter of '41. Fever.* Clean as a pulled proof. But then the woman had tilted her head, just slightly, the way a compositor tilts his head at a line that doesn't justify, and said: *I don't find her in the parish register.*

Marek had laughed at the time—no, not laughed, he had made a sound adjacent to laughter—and said the curate of St. Aldric's kept his books like a drunk keeps his cups. The census woman had smiled, written something, thanked them, gone. And all evening afterward the house had felt like a page held too close to a flame: nothing burning yet, everything warm.

She wasn't in the parish register because Marek had paid the curate of St. Aldric's forty crowns to keep her out of it.

That was the arithmetic of his life now, the sum he carried under his ribs: forty crowns, one absence, one daughter who thought her mother's body had been given to the frozen ground behind the fever house, one son who had been eleven years old and had cried at a funeral with no coffin in it. One wife, alive or dead—he still did not know which, and the not-knowing was the cruelest cut he had ever made, and he had made it himself, with his own hand, the way he made everything.

* * *

Livia had come to the foundry in 1833 to buy type.

Not cast type—the finished sorts any stationer could buy—but matrices, the struck bronze mothers from which lead children were born. She wanted a hand that didn't exist. A clerk's hand, she said, ordinary enough to pass, distinctive enough to be hers alone, and when Marek asked why a woman needed her own private alphabet she had looked at him with those gray eyes that never quite finished a sentence and said, "Because I write a great many letters, Master Vasquez, and I should like some of them to be nobody's."

He had cut her twenty-six punches over six weeks. It was the best work of his life, though he could never say so, could never show it, because the letters themselves were contraband the moment they existed: a complete alphabet belonging to a person the state had no record of wanting. He remembered cutting the lowercase *a* by lamplight while Livia sat across the bench sharpening her pens and watching his hands, and he remembered thinking, with the simple vanity of a craftsman, *she will remember me every time she writes.*

She had married him within the year. He had told himself it was love. It was love. It was also the only way two people who lived outside the ledger could build a household the ledger would accept: a foundry, a marriage license, two children entered in the register with dates and godparents, a family like a well-set page, justified at both margins.

What he had not understood—what he had chosen not to understand, and this was the wound he picked at nightly—was that the alphabet had been the beginning of her work, not the end.

He learned it in pieces, the way you learn a leak in a roof: a stain here, a drip there, long before the ceiling comes down. A man from the river district came at dusk once, paid cash for a font of the private face, and Marek assumed a merchant wanting discretion. A bundle of blank passes appeared in the scrap bin, printed on both sides, and Livia said the paper mill sold seconds cheaply and she'd bought them for practice. Their second press—the old Albion in the cellar, "for hobby work," she called it—smelled on certain mornings not of ink but of the acid she used to age documents, and when he came down the stairs she would be standing over it with her sleeves rolled, calm as a woman kneading bread, and she would kiss him and say, "Don't ask before breakfast, Marek. You'll spoil your appetite for the whole day."

So he hadn't asked. For six years he hadn't asked.

He told himself it was trust. Some nights, in the honest dark, he knew it was cowardice wearing trust's coat. Because he had known—had known the way a man knows his own gait in a crowd of footsteps—that the passes in the scrap bin bore his letterforms, that the documents aging in the cellar smelled of his household, that every forged border crossing, every false baptismal record, every dissident slid out of Velmora under a name that didn't exist had been set, in part, in type he had cut with these hands. He was not her accomplice. He was her instrument. And an instrument that chooses to stay on the bench is no longer innocent of the music.

There had been one night, only one, when he had tried to stop it. Autumn of 1840. He had come home early and found the cellar press running and a young man—barely older than Tomas was now—hunched over the forme, inking it with shaking hands, and Livia standing beside him guiding his wrist, and on the stone floor between them lay forty railway passes, each one a small paper door out of the country. Marek had stood on the stairs and said, "Livia. Not in my house."

She had wiped her hands and come up to him and taken his face in both palms, ink-smudged thumbs against his jaw, and said, "Where else? Where else in this whole gray city is there a room with a lock and a man who knows how to keep quiet?" And then, softer, the sentence he would carry to his grave: "You cut the letters, my love. You've been in this since the day you cut the *a*. All I'm asking is that you keep being brave enough not to look."

He had gone back upstairs. He had eaten his supper. He had slept beside her. And in the spring the Ministry came.

* * *

They came at four in the morning, which he later learned was standard—not for tactical reasons but psychological ones; men are weakest before dawn, the light least trustworthy, the mind least defended. Six of them. No warrant shown, though one existed; no names given, though he memorized their faces the way he memorized letterforms, stroke by stroke, in case he ever needed to identify them again. They turned the foundry inside out with a thoroughness that was almost professional respect: drawers lifted whole out of cases, matrices fanned like cards, the cellar press dismantled to its screws.

Livia dressed slowly. She brushed out her hair. She put on the gray wool dress she saved for church and funerals, and Marek understood, watching her button it, that she had chosen it deliberately—that she was telling him she knew this was one or the other.

At the door she stopped. Tomas was crying; Jonah, four years old, was holding her skirt with one fist. Livia crouched, gathered them both, and said into their hair, "Papa will tell you a story about Mama someday. Make him tell it true." Then she stood, kissed Marek once, briefly, on the mouth—the way you stamp a seal—and said, quietly enough that only he could hear:

"The box under the floor of your bench. Burn it. Whatever happens, whatever they promise you, whatever they threaten—burn it, and forget my hand."

Then the door closed, and the street took her, and Marek Vasquez went and sat at his bench until the lamps were lit, and then he pried up the floorboard.

Inside: the box. Birchwood, brass corners. Inside the box: her sketches.

* * *

Now, three years later, he finally opened it.

The lid came up with a sigh of trapped air, and the smell of her rose out of it—lampblack and vinegar and the lavender she'd sewn into the lining against insects—and Marek had to grip the edge of the bench until the wood bit his palm, because for one white moment he was certain she was dead, that this was all that remained of her, that he was a widower opening a reliquary, and the grief hit him with such force that he heard himself make a sound, low in his throat, like a press taking too great an impression.

Then the moment passed, as moments do, and he was only a man looking at papers.

There were hundreds of them. Sketches of letters, mostly—her alphabet in evolution, the *a* tried nine ways, the *g* fought over across a dozen sheets, marginal notes in her upright hand: *too much contrast, the Ministry clerks squint; heavier hairlines; this loop is vanity, cut it away.* There were practice pages, columns of invented names in invented lives: *Anna Reiter, laundress, born Velmora 1816; Josef Brandt, wheelwright, born Ostrava 1809*—whole populations of the nonexistent, each one a person she had built from nothing, letter by letter, to smuggle someone real out of the country. There were pressed flowers between two of the sheets, brown now, river willow by the look of them. There was a child's drawing, Jonah's, a wobbly house with smoke curls, which Livia had kept for reasons Marek could not bear to examine.

And at the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, a matrix. Bronze, warm-looking even in the cold, the letter *A* struck deep and clean—her capital, the first punch he had ever cut for her, the one she'd said she wanted "to sign things that matter." He had believed it lost with the rest of the confiscated equipment. She had hidden it under his own bench, in his own floor, without telling him—trusting him with it precisely by not trusting him with the knowledge, because a man who doesn't know a secret cannot surrender it.

She had always been better at this than he was. That was the truth under all the other truths.

Marek sat for a long time with the matrix in his hand. Below him the river groaned against the pilings, the slow tectonic complaint of ice that would break in six weeks, eight, and carry the winter's filth north toward the sea. Somewhere across the city, in a Ministry archive, in a prison registry, in a file with a number instead of a name, there might exist—one line, one entry—the answer to whether his wife was alive. He had never looked. He had told himself that not looking was protection: for the children, for the foundry, for the careful gray invisibility that had kept the Vasquez name off every list since the night the soldiers came. But the census woman's voice was in his head now, polite as a blade going in: *I don't find her in the parish register.* Someone else was looking. Someone else was pulling threads he had spent three years knotting down, and a thread, once pulled, does not care what it unravels.

He fed the stove.

One by one he fed the sketches to it—the invented names first, Anna Reiter and Josef Brandt and all their paper children, the flames taking each column with a sound like small applause. The practice alphabets went next. He worked steadily, without ceremony, the way he did everything, and if his hands shook it was only the cold of the room, and if his eyes stung it was only the smoke, and he did not permit himself to think of the word *complicity*, or of the difference between protecting a family and burying a woman, or of how thin the wall was between those two acts, thin as a sheet of paper, thin as the lie he had told his children at a funeral with no coffin in it.

Jonah's drawing he held over the fire longer than the rest. The smoke curled. The house in the drawing darkened, curled, went.

Last, the matrix. He weighed it in his palm. *Burn it, and forget my hand,* she had said. But bronze does not burn in a parlor stove; it would sit in the ashes tomorrow morning, staring up at him out of the char, her capital *A*, patient as only metal is patient. To destroy it properly he would have to melt it, and melting it meant the crucible, and the crucible meant the foundry, and the foundry meant doing it in the place where he had made it, in front of the furnace that had given it birth.

He could not. He told himself he would, next week, when the Ministry's commission was delivered and the shop was quiet. He wrapped the matrix in the oilcloth, and he did not put it back under the floorboards, because a hiding place once known is a hiding place forever dangerous. Instead he dropped it into the drawer of his graver's bench, beneath the trays of punches, among the tools of his trade, where it lay against the steel like a seed against stones.

The stove burned down. The sketches became ash, and the ash became indistinguishable from every other ash, and Marek told himself that this was grief's proper paperwork: filed, sealed, destroyed. Protective silence. He repeated the phrase to himself the way a man repeats a password at a gate he no longer remembers wanting to enter.

Upstairs, faintly, a floorboard creaked—Jonah, awake past midnight, or turning in her sleep. His daughter, who had her mother's gray eyes and her mother's steadiness and, God help him, her mother's habit of asking questions after breakfast, before breakfast, at every meal, questions with edges on them. *Why isn't Grandmother's grave marked, Papa? Why won't you speak her name?*

He banked the fire. He climbed the stairs. And in the drawer below, in the dark of the bench, wrapped in oilcloth, the letter *A* waited—cut by his hand, hidden by hers—for the day someone would come asking, as someone always comes asking, what exactly it was that Father cut away.
