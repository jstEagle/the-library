## 4. The Letter in the Drawer

The matrix was not where it should have been.

Jonah knew the foundry the way other people knew their own hands—every drawer's weight, every shelf's complaint under load, the particular way the third floorboard near the casting bench dipped if you stepped on its left edge. She had spent eleven years learning which silences in this building were ordinary and which ones meant her father had moved something. Tonight the silence around the punch cabinet was the second kind.

She had come down for a specific reason, and the reason had a name: the double-v ligature. Marek had been cutting his new commission for the Ministry of Information all week—a clean, obedient face with no flourishes, nothing that might be called ornamental or, worse, expressive—and he'd left the drawing of the ligature pinned above his bench when he went to the guild meeting. Jonah had looked at it for perhaps four seconds before she understood that she had seen that exact curve before, years ago, in a typeface that did not officially exist. Her mother's private alphabet. The one Livia Vasquez had cut by hand on winter evenings, humming, saying *this one is only ours*.

Her father had stolen from the dead. Or borrowed. With her father there was often no difference.

So now, past midnight, lamp turned low, Jonah stood before the punch cabinet and began the slow work of finding what he had hidden.

The cabinet itself was honest—punches and matrices sorted by size, the brass gleaming dully in the lamplight, everything a stranger would expect. But her father hid things the way he set type: with an eye toward the reader's assumptions. He would never hide something in the obvious place. He would hide it in the place a searcher would check *second*, because everyone checked the obvious place first and then, satisfied, stopped looking.

She started with the drawer behind the drawer—the false back in the lower case that had held Tomas's contraband playing cards when they were children. Empty now except for a curl of wood shavings and the smell of beeswax. She tried the hollow leg of the sorting table. The space beneath the hearthstone where the crucible sat; too hot a hiding place for anything but metal, and indeed there was nothing but a scorched tin box containing buttons. Buttons. Her father hiding buttons as though buttons were worth hiding, which meant the buttons themselves were a message to anyone who found them: *nothing here, move along.*

Jonah sat back on her heels and rubbed her eyes. Ink came away on her fingers, as it always did, as it had since she was six years old and allowed, for the first time, to pull a proof sheet off the hand press. Her mother had held her up to see it—that memory arrived unbidden, sharp as a burin—Livia's arms strong under her ribs, both of them looking at the wet black letters standing crisp on white paper, and Livia saying, *Look, little one. We made words stand still.*

Words standing still. That was the whole trade, her mother used to say. Everything else in the world ran and shouted and burned, but a compositor could make words hold their position long enough to be believed. It was why the Ministry wanted her father's typefaces. Words that stood still looked like truth.

She got up and crossed to the type cases themselves, the great slanted wooden trays lining the north wall, each compartment labeled in her father's cramped hand. And there—she almost laughed—the third case from the end, the one holding the obsolete long-s characters that no modern compositor had used in twenty years. Nobody would search a dead alphabet.

The bottom of the long-s compartment was shallower than the ones beside it. She measured it with her little finger, then with the brass rule from her apron pocket. Half an inch of missing depth. Her heart began a small, stupid, hopeful drumming.

It took her ten minutes and a flat pry bar to ease the false bottom free without splitting the old wood. Beneath it lay a shallow tray lined in felt, and on the felt:

Three matrices. Not Ministry work—these were cut by hand, irregular, alive, each letter leaning slightly as though caught mid-breath. An *f*. A *d* with a descender like a river bending. And a ligature she recognized instantly from the drawing upstairs: the double-v, exactly as her mother had once cut it, exactly as her father was now selling to the men who had taken her mother away.

And beneath the matrices, wrapped in oilcloth, a letter.

Not a letter—an envelope, sealed, the wax unbroken. Her name was on it. *Jonah.* Just that, in a hand she knew better than her own reflection: the forward-leaning script with the peculiar open-topped *a*, the crossbar of the *h* drifting loose like a bird taking off. Her mother's handwriting. She had seen it last on the labels of jam jars, on the margins of her school copybooks, on the note tucked into her lunch of bread and dripping on the morning—

On the morning of the day her mother died. Three years ago. Nearly to the week.

Jonah stood very still in the cold foundry and let the lamp hiss.

The date on the envelope was written beneath her name, small and precise, in the way business correspondence is dated. *March 1844.*

March 1844. Three years after the parish record said Livia Vasquez had been carried out of this house feet-first, three years after her father had drunk himself silent for a month and then never spoken of her again, three years after Jonah had sewn the black band onto her sleeve with stitches so tight they cut the cloth.

The dead did not write letters. The dead did not date them.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed this the way you notice rain starting—not immediately, but all at once, everywhere. She set the envelope on the sorting stone and pressed her palms flat against the cool slate until the shaking transferred itself into the stone and, presumably, into the earth beneath the city, into the bones of Velmora, which had swallowed enough secrets by now to choke a cathedral.

*Open it,* said the part of her that was her mother's daughter, the part that pulled proofs and made words stand still.

*Don't,* said the part that was her father's daughter, the part that kept the family invisible, that had learned at nine years old that some questions were doors you did not open because behind them stood men from the Ministry with lists.

She thought about the census taker. Astrid Lindqvist, with her careful voice and her pen that paused half a beat too long over the column marked *deceased members of household*. *The death was never entered in the parish register, madam.* The hairline crack. Jonah had papered over it with rehearsed grief—*we were poor mourners, we buried her quiet*—but cracks do not close because you paste paper on them. They wait.

She picked up the letter knife from her father's desk. It was bone-handled, worn smooth, older than she was. She slid it under the wax flap and stopped.

Through the paper, thin as the envelope was, she could feel something inside besides a folded sheet. Something small and flat and slightly springy. A sprig of something dried. Willow, maybe. River willow grew along the embankment below the foundry; her mother had always kept a vase of it in spring, saying it was the only plant that listened to water all day and never complained.

Her mother had pressed a sprig of river willow into a letter dated three years after her death.

Jonah opened it.

The wax cracked with a sound like a knuckle. Inside: one sheet, folded twice, and the willow sprig, its leaves gone the color of old brass, lying across the fold like a signature. She unfolded the page with the care of a woman handling a matrix—one slip and the impression would be ruined forever.

One sentence. Her mother's hand, unmistakable, unhesitating, the ink brown-black and unfaded, as though written recently, as though written *yesterday*:

*If you are reading this, the ice has begun to break, and you must decide what you are willing to carry.*

Jonah read it four times. The words did not change. That was the terrible gift of print, of writing, of everything her family had given their lives to: words stood still even when the world did not.

*You must decide what you are willing to carry.*

Carry what? Carry whom? Carry the letter itself, the matrices, the knowledge? Carry guilt, or hope, or a person? The sentence was a door with no room attached—or rather, a door with every possible room behind it, and no way to know which one opened until you walked through.

And below the sentence, in the corner of the page, so small she nearly missed it, a mark. Not a word. A symbol: two strokes crossing like a tally, with a third stroke laid horizontally across them—the printer's sign for a correction pending. *This text is not final. Wait for the revised edition.*

Her mother had written her a letter in printer's marks. A letter that said, in the language of the trade, *there is more coming; do not act on this draft alone.*

Jonah became aware, gradually, of sounds: the river muttering under its skin of ice, the settling tick of the cooling stove, her own breath. She became aware of the matrices sitting in their felt bed, her mother's living letters, and of the drawing pinned upstairs, her father's theft of them, and of the census taker's pen pausing over the register that had never recorded a death because—

Because there had been no death to record?

No. She had seen the coffin. She had been eleven; she remembered the weight of it, six men, her father's face like a wall with the plaster stripped. You did not imagine a coffin. You did not imagine your father's silence, which had lasted three years and counting.

But you also did not imagine your mother's handwriting. Jonah had spent her life among letterforms; she could distinguish a thousand hands the way a musician distinguishes instruments. This was Livia Vasquez's hand, and it was not aged, not tremulous, not imitated. Imitation was possible—her mother had been a forger, the thought arrived and she flinched from it—but who would forge a letter to a child, seal it in wax, hide it under a dead alphabet, and leave it to be found? Forgers forged for money or mercy, and this letter offered neither.

Unless it was a test. Unless her father had written it, in her mother's hand, to see whether Jonah would go looking. To see how much she remembered. To see whether the family armor held.

She looked at the willow sprig. Her father did not know about the willow. Her father did not notice plants; he noticed kerning. The willow was her mother's, or it was nothing.

Jonah folded the letter along its original creases, replaced the sprig, and then—her hands moving with a compositor's automatic precision—did not put it back. Instead she took the matrices, all three, and the letter, and wrapped them in the scrap of linen she kept for polishing punches, and slid the bundle inside the front of her bodice, against her skin, where it lay cold as coin and just as heavy.

She eased the false bottom back into the long-s case. She swept the wood shavings from the floor. She trimmed the lamp.

Upstairs, the house slept. Her father's door was shut, and beneath it no light showed, but Jonah stood in the hallway a long moment anyway, listening, the way she had learned to listen at nine years old. His breathing, when it came through the door, was the breathing of a man asleep or a man pretending very well, and with her father those had always been difficult to tell apart.

In her own narrow room, she lit a candle stub and unwrapped the bundle on her blanket. The matrices caught the flame-light and threw it back in small bright pieces. The letter lay open. The willow leaf had detached and curled on the wool like a question mark.

*What are you willing to carry.*

She thought of her brother, who had come home last week smelling of cheap tobacco and other people's excitement, with ink on his cuffs that was not the foundry's ink. She thought of the census taker's eyes, gray and patient as a scale waiting for weights. She thought of her father's hands, which could cut a letter finer than any man in Velmora and had not cut anything beautiful since 1841.

Someone in this house was keeping a secret large enough to require a coffin, and tonight Jonah had learned that the secret could write.

She took a blank card from her stock, dipped her pen, and practiced—once, twice, a dozen times—until she could reproduce the open-topped *a* and the drifting crossbar of the *h* well enough to fool a stranger. She told herself it was habit, discipline, the idle exercise of a craftswoman's hand. She told herself she did not know yet why she was doing it.

Then she blew out the candle, and in the dark she heard the river groan—a long, deep complaint of ice shifting against ice, the sound the whole city pretended not to hear in late winter—and she held the letter against her chest and waited for morning, and for whatever revised edition was coming.

Below her window, far down the frozen embankment, a figure stood at the rail where the ferry docked in summer. Small. Still. Watching the house.

By the time Jonah reached the glass, the street was empty, and the snow held only one set of footprints—leading toward the river, not away from it.
