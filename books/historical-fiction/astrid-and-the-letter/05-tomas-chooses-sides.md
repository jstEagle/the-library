## 5. Tomas Chooses Sides

The river gave Tomas Vasquez his wages and took them back twice over.

He learned this arithmetic young, hauling crates up the frozen ramp at Dock Four, where the ice had been cut into a channel for the winter barges and the water beneath lay black and patient as spilled ink. The stevedores worked in layers of wool and sacking, breath rising in columns, hands going numb around the hook-handles until the pain became its own kind of warmth. Above them, gulls stood on the pilings like clerks waiting for a ledger to open. Below, the barges sat low with grain and salt and—this season more than any other—crates stamped with the double eagle of the Ministry of Information, which paid double freight and asked no questions about weight.

Tomas was twenty-three and strong in the way of men who have never been allowed to be anything else. His shoulders had been built by this dock, his silence by his father's house. He could carry two hundred pounds down a plank slick with frost without letting his face change, which was the skill that mattered most in Velmora now: not dropping what you carried, not letting your face change.

"Vasquez." The foreman, Brann, jerked his chin toward the office shed. "Man asking after you."

His stomach performed the small cold drop it had learned to perform whenever anyone *asked after* him. In Velmora, being asked after was rarely good news. It meant lists. It meant the census, or the tithe rolls, or the Ministry's quiet men who came to workshops with soft voices and hard eyes and left behind a chill that took days to burn off.

But the man in the shed was no one's quiet man. He sat on an overturned flour barrel as if he'd been invited, drinking tea from his own tin cup, a broad-shouldered fellow of perhaps forty with a beard trimmed close and a scar running through one eyebrow like a dropped stitch. His clothes were dockworker's clothes, good ones, mended well. He looked at Tomas the way men look at horses they are thinking of buying—and then, catching himself, the way they look at horses they already respect.

"Tomas Vasquez," he said. Not a question. "I'm called Ferro. I load at Seven."

"I know you," Tomas said. "You threw a customs man into the channel in November."

"He slipped." Ferro smiled without much of anything in it. "Ice is treacherous."

"It is."

They regarded each other across the shed's small stove. Somewhere outside, a crane groaned under a load of ministry crates, and both men glanced toward the sound, and in that shared glance something passed between them that neither would have put into words: *you count those crates too.*

Brann, wisely, had found business elsewhere.

"You've a reputation," Ferro said, turning the tin cup in his broad hands. "For discretion. For keeping your head where your shoulders can't protect it. And—" he paused, and here he changed, subtly, the way a current changes before a bend, "—for knowing letters."

"I can read."

"More than that. Your father's a type founder. Half the official documents in this city come out of his matrices. You grew up with lead in your blood."

"My father works for the Ministry," Tomas said carefully. "We don't discuss his commissions."

"No," Ferro agreed pleasantly. "You don't discuss anything. That's rather the point of you."

* * *

He thought about it that night, walking home along the embankment with the wind coming off the ice flat and gray as unpolished steel. He thought about it through supper, where Jonah sat too still and their father carved his stew bread into smaller and smaller pieces without eating any, and nobody spoke, because in the Vasquez house nobody spoke—that was the family art, the inheritance older than any typeface: *say nothing, want nothing visibly, be a gray stone among gray stones and the flood will pass you by.*

It had kept them alive, that strategy. He believed that. He also believed, some nights, staring at the ceiling of his narrow room while the river groaned below, that it had kept them alive the way a man keeps a dog alive by keeping it in a cellar.

Ferro had pressed nothing further that day. He'd simply finished his tea, set the cup upside down on the barrel—the way dockmen did when they were done but might return—and said, "There's work Thursdays and Sundays, evenings, at the ropewalk off Tanner Street. Heavy lifting. Pays in coin, not questions." And then he'd gone out into the white afternoon and left Tomas standing in the shed with his heart beating strangely, the way it does when a door opens in a wall you'd stopped seeing as a wall.

Thursday came. He told himself he was only curious. Curiosity was almost permissible; it wasn't opinion, it wasn't petition, it wasn't portrait. He walked past the ropewalk once, twice, telling himself he was merely taking a longer route home.

The third time, Ferro was leaning against the gatepost as if he'd been standing there all week.

* * *

The print shop hid inside the ropewalk the way a knife hides in a coat. The long shed where rope-makers walked their hemp back and forth by day smelled by night of ink and lamp oil, and behind a screen of hanging cordage Tomas saw the press—an old hand-press, iron-jawed, its screw freshly greased—and around it six people moving with the economy of workers who have done a dangerous thing so often it has become craft.

A woman with ink to her elbows set type in a composing stick, her hands quick and sure as a surgeon's. A boy of maybe fifteen fed dampened paper. Two men he recognized distantly from Dock Seven sorted sheets as they came off the stone, laying them in rows to dry like fish on a flake. No names were offered. Ferro introduced him only as "our new muscle," and someone laughed softly, and the strange thing—the thing Tomas would turn over in his mind for weeks afterward—was how warm the laugh sounded in the cold shed. How *included* it made him feel, before he had done anything at all to earn it.

He had not been included in anything since childhood. That was the seduction, though he didn't yet know the word for it. Not the politics—he understood the politics only glancingly, a pamphlet here, an overheard speech there, all of it fog to him—but the *belonging*. These people looked at one another when they spoke. They trusted each other with their faces. In his father's house love was expressed as silence, protection as erasure; here, trust was expressed as *letting a stranger see you work*.

"Read this," the woman said, handing him a proof sheet, testing him, he understood later. "Tell me if it sets true."

He read it. It was a broadsheet, dense with figures: the cost of the Regent's coming jubilee set against the price of bread, against the winter dead pulled from the river, against the taxes levied on the very docks where he broke his back. The numbers were damning. The prose was plain and hard as a keel.

"Well?" she said.

"The figures are right," he said slowly. "I haul these crates. I know what the ministry pays in freight."

"And the setting?"

And then he saw it.

He had been looking at the words. Now he looked at the *letters*—the way a man who has lived beside a river all his life finally looks at the water itself—and his breath stopped somewhere below his ribs.

He knew that lowercase *a*. He knew the slight forward lean of it, the tail cut short as if the designer had run out of patience with ornament. He knew the *g* with its flat ear, the numerals with their old-style descenders that dipped below the baseline like feet stepping off a stair. Every apprentice learns his master's hand; every son learns his father's. This was his father's private face—the experimental cut Marek Vasquez had worked on for three winters at his own bench, late hours, no commission, the typeface he called *Livia* and never showed to a client and swore his children never to mention, because a private design was the one thing in a founder's life that belonged entirely to himself.

Someone was printing sedition in his father's stolen soul.

"Where did you get this type?" he heard himself ask. His voice came out level, which surprised him. Underneath, everything was falling.

"Cast for us," the woman said. Too smoothly. She hadn't expected the question—or she had, and had prepared the answer, and the smoothness itself was an answer.

"Where."

Ferro moved slightly, not threatening, just present. "Does it matter?"

"It matters," Tomas said, "because I know whose letterforms those are. And if he sold them, he's a fool who'll hang for it. And if he didn't sell them, then someone stole from my father's house, or cast from his matrices without leave—and either way, whoever cut that type is already on a list somewhere, and everyone who prints with it is holding his hand over a flame he can't see."

The shed went quiet except for the tick of the cooling stove. The woman set down her composing stick. She studied him for a long moment, and whatever she saw in his face made her glance at Ferro, and Ferro shrugged: *he's right, isn't he?*

"Your name," she said at last. "Truly?"

"Vasquez. Tomas Vasquez. Marek Vasquez is my father."

She exhaled through her nose, half laugh, half prayer. "Then you're either the greatest gift the cause received this winter," she said, "or the trap we always feared. Come Sunday and we'll find out which."

* * *

He walked home the long way, along the embankment, and the river groaned beneath the ice—a vast tectonic complaint, the sound of a thing too long held beginning to shift—and Tomas matched his stride to the sound and felt something crack in himself along a similar seam.

His father had taught him that safety was subtraction. Remove yourself from every ledger, every register, every eye. Be no one. His mother—dead these three years, a grief worn smooth as river glass from handling—had died, so far as Tomas knew, of fever and bad luck, and the family had closed over her absence like water over a stone, and spoken of her less each year until speaking of her at all felt like opening a wound in the floorboards.

But now he stood on the embankment in the dark and understood, with the certainty of lead settling in a mold, that his mother's death and his father's hidden typeface and the pamphlets drying in a ropewalk off Tanner Street were all one story. He didn't know how the chapters connected. He knew they were the same book.

And the terrible thing, the thing he would carry like a splinter working toward the surface, was that part of him—the part that had stood in the warm lamplight among people who used each other's faces freely—did not want to warn his father off. Did not want to retreat into the family silence like a crab backing into its hole.

Part of him wanted to go back Sunday. Wanted to stand at that press and pull the bar and feel the type bite into paper and know that his own two hands had put truth into the world, in his father's stolen letterforms, whether his father willed it or no.

The house was dark when he reached it, all but one window—Jonah's, candlelit, where his sister sat late over something she turned and turned in her hands. He watched the light a moment from the street, this small stubborn flame in a city of shuttered windows, and felt the first clean pull of a choice being made far down inside him, deeper than argument, deeper than fear:

The ice was going to break. Everyone felt it coming. The only question left was who would be standing on it when it did.

He went inside to sleep, and in the morning there was snow on the sill, and under the door, a folded sheet of cheap paper he must have stepped over in the night.

It was printed. One column, one paragraph. And the type—the lean *a*, the flat-eared *g*—was unmistakably his father's hand, addressed to the whole city, sitting on his own doorstep like a summons.
