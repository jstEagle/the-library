## 6. Astrid's Second Visit

Astrid Lindqvist had learned, in eleven years of counting the Empire's citizens, that a household revealed itself in its silences before it revealed itself in its answers.

The Vasquez foundry was loud with work—she could hear the clatter of the sorting sticks, the low ring of metal on metal, a man's voice giving instruction in the flat cadence of someone who had given the same instruction ten thousand times—but beneath the noise there was a silence with a shape to it. She had noticed it the first time, and she had spent three weeks failing to forget it.

She stood across the street in the grey light of a February afternoon, her census satchel heavy on her hip, and let the cold work its way through her gloves. Across the river, the ice had begun to murmur at night; the ferrymen said it was nothing, that the real break was six weeks off, but Astrid had grown up on a river and knew the sound of a thing deciding to change. Velmora was full of things deciding to change. The Ministry's new proclamations went up on the walls faster than the rain could peel them down. The bread queues had grown a second queue beside them, of people who had stopped pretending they were there for anything else.

She crossed the street and knocked.

The door was answered by the daughter. Jonah Vasquez. Twenty-two, perhaps twenty-three, ink at the cuticles, a smudge of lead-grey along one jaw that she had not bothered to wipe because wiping it would have meant acknowledging that someone might look at her face. Her eyes did the thing Astrid had been turning over in her mind for three weeks: they assessed, they catalogued, and then they arranged themselves into something pleasant and slightly dull, like a shopkeeper arranging goods for a customer she did not expect to buy.

"Miss Lindqvist." No surprise. That was the first mark against her. A census taker's second visit was a thing people feared or resented or greeted with the anxious hospitality of the innocent; nobody greeted it with the calm of a hostess who had set a place.

"Fraulein Vasquez. I apologize for the intrusion. The Ministry requires a correction to the December enumeration—there was an irregularity in the district's returns, nothing to do with your household specifically, but I'm obliged to re-verify certain entries." She had written the sentence herself, in the Ministry's own bureaucratic dialect, and she watched it land the way a stone lands in still water: without a ripple.

"Of course. Will you come in, or—" Jonah glanced past her shoulder at the street, at the two boys hauling a handcart of scrap iron, at the proclamations flapping on the far wall. "It's warmer inside. Father's at the furnace, but the front room—"

"The front room will do admirably."

* * *

The front room of the foundry was a room that had been arranged to be forgotten. Astrid noted this with professional pleasure. There was a table, four chairs, a sideboard with a cloth runner worn to translucence in the middle. On the walls: nothing. Not a calendar, not a print of the Regent, not the small framed genealogy that even modest households displayed as a shield—*we have been here, we belong, count us kindly.* No portraits. No photographs. In a city where the Ministry had spent two years teaching citizens to hang the Regent's face in their parlors like an icon, the absence was not poverty. It was policy.

"You'll take tea," Jonah said. It was not a question, and it was not quite an offer. It was the voice of someone managing a situation.

"Thank you."

Jonah went out and Astrid stood at the window, hands folded on her satchel, and read the room the way she read all rooms: in the direction of the wear. The chair nearest the furnace wall had its seat polished pale; that was the father's. One of the other chairs stood at a slight angle to the table, dragged back in haste or habit, and its cushion was flattened in a way that suggested a third person had sat there recently, or had sat there for years, and the household had not yet learned to stop setting the place.

She heard Jonah in the passage, speaking low and fast to someone—the father, presumably—and caught only the shape of the words, not their content. Then the kettle. Then footsteps returning, and Jonah carrying a tray with the careful economy of a woman who had decided that hospitality, like everything else, would be performed correctly and without excess.

"Father says to give you whatever you need," Jonah said, setting down the tray. "He's mid-pour. The Ministry's commission—he can't leave the crucible."

"The typeface for the Information Office. Yes, I know of it." Astrid let that sit for a moment, watching for the flicker. There was none. The daughter poured the tea with a steady wrist. "You have a good hand for this work, I'd think. Foundry work. The sorting of small things."

"Everyone here has a good hand for it. You don't last a week otherwise."

"No. I imagine not." Astrid opened her satchel and drew out the district ledger, the December return, and laid it flat on the table between them like a card turned face up. "Now. The irregularity. It concerns the deceased members of the household."

The teapot's lid clicked, very slightly, as Jonah set it down. A sound so small that a lesser observer would have called it the porcelain settling. Astrid called it what it was.

"In December," Astrid continued, in the mild tone she used for reading train schedules, "you reported the household as four persons living and one deceased. Livia Vasquez, born 1809, entered as deceased, year 1841. Cause of death unrecorded. Place of burial unrecorded." She turned the ledger toward Jonah. "The correction required is not to your entry, Fraulein Vasquez. Your entry was perfectly formed. The correction required is to the parish register, which contains no record of the death at all."

The room held its breath. Somewhere behind the wall, the furnace roared and subsided, roared and subsided, like a large animal sleeping.

Jonah looked at the ledger. She did not look at it the way the guilty look at a trap—too quickly, or too long, or with the elaborate casualness of the rehearsed. She looked at it the way a compositor looks at a line of type that has been set by someone else, checking for errors, and Astrid felt a small cold pleasure and a small cold alarm arrive together, because she had come here expecting to catch a lie, and instead she appeared to be catching something far more interesting: competence.

"You're right that it's not in the register," Jonah said. "I've wondered about that myself. She died in the winter of '41, in the fever that came up the river—half the tannery quarter lost people that month. I was nineteen. My father handled the arrangements." A pause, measured to the grain. "I assumed he'd filed everything. He isn't a man who leaves paperwork undone. But grief makes gaps in a household, Fraulein Lindqvist, even in the paperwork. Especially there."

*Grief makes gaps.* Astrid turned the phrase over. It was a beautiful answer. It was the kind of answer that a person gives when they have had three weeks to compose it, and it had the additional quality—rarer, and more damning—of being an answer that made the questioner feel cruel for having asked.

"I'm sure that's so," Astrid said. "And yet the burial. A fever death in '41 would have had a burial society, a grave, a stone or at least a marker. The parish of Saint Aldric covers this district. Their register is thorough to the point of tedium. I've been through it twice."

"Have you." Jonah refilled Astrid's cup, unasked, and Astrid understood the gesture perfectly: *you have been busy, and I am not afraid of your busyness, and also your cup is empty and I am watching your hands while you decide what to do about that.* "Then you'll know the register was reconstructed after the fire of '39. Whole years were lost. My father says the re-entry clerks were paid by the page, not the name, and it shows."

Astrid had, in fact, verified the fire of '39 within an hour of her first visit. It was real. It had destroyed exactly the volumes that would have contained a death entry for the winter of 1841. Either the Vasquez family had been extraordinarily fortunate, or someone in this household understood the archival history of Velmora's parishes well enough to build a lie on the one foundation the fire had left them.

She wrote a small mark in her ledger—nothing, a tick, a clerk's habit—and said, "You know your archives."

"I know my father's stories."

* * *

They drank their tea. Astrid had long ago learned that the interrogation was not the instrument; the conversation was. The interrogation produced rehearsed truth and rehearsed falsehood, indistinguishable. But conversation—loose, lateral, apparently idle—showed the grain of a person the way planing shows the grain of wood.

So she spoke of the river, and the ice, and the ferry schedule that would be posted in March. She spoke of the jubilee preparations, the scaffolds going up along the Processional Way, and watched Jonah's face for the micro-expression of contempt that the Ministry's surveyors had been trained to flag. There was none. There was nothing. Jonah Vasquez's face was a well-run type case: every character in its compartment, nothing out of place, nothing wasted.

And yet.

"You've lived above the foundry all your life?" Astrid asked.

"Except two years at the Meridian School. Girls' academy, on the hill. My mother insisted, before—" The sentence stopped, cleanly, at the place where a sentence about a dead mother would naturally stop, and Jonah looked down at her tea with an expression of such perfect, ordinary sadness that Astrid felt the hair rise on her forearms.

Because it was perfect. It was *too* perfect, and Jonah Vasquez knew it was too perfect, and Astrid understood, with the certainty of a woman who had spent a decade listening to liars, that the daughter was not lying to her. She was *performing* honesty—and doing it so well that the performance itself was the message. *I know you are watching. I know what you are looking for. Here it is, delivered to specification.*

"You were close to her," Astrid said quietly. "Your mother."

"I was fifteen when I understood her," Jonah said. "Which is a different thing from being close." She set her cup down. "She had a way of looking at a page. Like it was a door. I was grown before I realized most people don't see pages that way."

"What way is that?"

"Asking what the page could be made to say." Jonah met her eyes then, directly, and for the first time the shopkeeper's arrangement fell away and Astrid saw the thing underneath: a mind working at speed behind a face that had been trained to stillness since childhood. "That's what type is, isn't it. A page's permission. My father cuts the permission. Someone decides what to ask it to say."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Down in the works, the furnace breathed. Astrid became aware that the conversation had left the census entirely behind, that they were now two women speaking in the plain text of a cipher neither had acknowledged, and that she could not remember, precisely, which of them had turned the wheel.

"That is a poet's way to describe a trade," she said.

"It's a foundry way. You'd know, if you'd sorted sorts." Jonah almost smiled. "You have ink on your fingers, Fraulein Lindqvist. Under the nails. Left hand. You don't get that from ledgers."

Astrid looked at her own hands and found, with genuine surprise, that the girl was right—a ghost of the violet-black that no amount of pumice ever fully lifted, from the Ministry's copying inks, from her own private correspondence, from the letters she wrote at night to no one she could name. She had not thought of herself as legible for years. It was, she found, not entirely unpleasant to be read.

"Quills," she said. "I write my reports by hand. The Ministry trusts a hand more than a machine. A machine can be stolen; a hand is hanged with its owner."

"That's a grim thing to say over tea."

"I'm a grim thing to have over tea. I'm the second visit."

For a moment—half a second, no more—Jonah Vasquez laughed. It was a small laugh, surprised out of her, and Astrid understood that she had just been shown something no Ministry form had a column for: that the daughter was funny, and tired, and twenty-two, and carrying something heavy enough that even her excellent machinery had let a gear slip.

Then the door opened and Marek Vasquez came in, wiping his hands on a leather apron, and the room reorganized itself around him like iron filings around a magnet.

* * *

He was a big man gone spare, with the forearms of the furnace and the eyes of a man who had spent thirty years looking at things smaller than a fingernail. He did not apologize for the delay. He did not offer his hand. He stood at the edge of the room the way a man stands at the edge of his own property when a surveyor has come to measure it, and he said, "You've found what you need, then," in a voice that made it not a question.

"There remains the matter of the parish register," Astrid said. "The death entry for your wife."

"The register burned in '39." He said it the way a man reads his own confession, flat and complete, and Astrid watched him and understood that this was the source of it—the daughter's polish, the household's armor. It had been forged here, in this man's silence, the way his type was forged: under heat, under pressure, to a tolerance measured in thousandths.

"So I'm told," Astrid said. "I'll note the discrepancy as an archival loss. It will require a supplementary declaration, countersigned, and a search of the burial societies. Routine. A week, perhaps two."

"Whatever the Ministry requires." His eyes went to his daughter—one flicker, one message sent and received in less than a heartbeat—and then back to Astrid, and there was something in them that Astrid had seen before, in other houses, in other years, in the faces of men who had made their peace with the state the way sailors make peace with the sea: without love, without illusion, and without any belief that the arrangement was permanent. "We are a quiet household, Fraulein Lindqvist. We have always been a quiet household."

"Yes," Astrid said, closing her ledger. "I'd noticed."

* * *

She walked back across the district as the light failed, her satchel heavier than it had been on the way out, and she did her work the way she always did it: walking, watching, sorting. The lamplighter was making his rounds along the Tanners' Walk. A queue had formed outside the provisioner's, silent and orderly, the orderliness itself a kind of accusation. On the wall by the bridge, someone had pasted a new broadsheet over the Regent's jubilee proclamation—torn down already at one corner, but legible: *BREAD FOR THE JUBILEE TABLES, CRUMBS FOR THE CITY.* The lettering was bold, clean, confident, cut by someone who knew exactly what a page could be made to say.

Astrid stood before it for a long moment, and her breath made small clouds, and she thought about pages and doors.

Here is what she had not written in any report, what she had told no one, what she carried in the sealed room of herself where she kept the things that would ruin her: she did not believe Livia Vasquez had been a criminal. The file was thin and the file was wrong. She had read it eleven times in the Ministry archive, and every reading left the same taste. A forger of Livia Vasquez's caliber—a woman whose work had passed three border inspections and a bank audit before anyone caught the tell in the flourishes—did not get caught forging a grain permit for a tanner's clerk. That was apprentice work. That was the work of a woman who had been *made* to sign something, or made to be found with something, or made into the answer to someone else's arithmetic. And the file's author was redacted, and the arresting officer's report was two pages shorter than the inventory of evidence, and somewhere in that missing length, Astrid was quite sure, was the truth: that Livia Vasquez had been caught the way one catches a fish one has already decided to eat.

Coerced. Or sacrificed. The distinction mattered less each year.

And now there was a daughter who performed honesty like a virtuoso, a father who had memorized the archive of a fire, a household with no portraits and a chair still set for a dead woman, and a death that existed everywhere except in the one place the Empire kept its dead.

Astrid reached her lodgings after dark and lit the lamp and took out the day's report form. The Ministry wanted a correction confirmed: *household Vasquez, deceased member, entry verified or discrepancy noted.* She dipped her pen. She wrote *discrepancy noted—archival loss, fire of 1839, no action required,* and her hand did not shake, because her hand never shook, and she set the page aside to dry, and she sat looking at it for a long time.

Then, in her private notebook—the one that lived under the floorboard, the one that would hang her if it were ever found—she wrote a single line:

*The daughter knows the parish archives better than the parish. Find out where she learned it.*

She blew out the lamp. Down by the river, faint through the walls, the ice groaned like a door being forced.
