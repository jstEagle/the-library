## 7. Ink Under the Fingernails

The letter did not go back in the drawer.

Jonah meant to return it—she told herself she would, that very first night, after she had read the single sentence until the words stopped being words and became a sound, a ringing, a pressure behind her eyes: *If you are reading this, then you have your father's caution and my hands.* She folded it along its original creases, pressed the withered sprig of river willow flat against her palm one last time, and stood before the drawer with the letter in her hand.

She could not do it.

Putting it back would be agreeing to pretend. And pretending, she understood now, was a craft like any other—one you got better at the more you practiced, until you couldn't remember what your own unpracticed face looked like. Her father was a master of it. She had grown up inside his masterpiece.

So instead she hid it where no one hides anything: in plain sight, among the blank endpapers in the foundry's paper stock, slipped between two sheets of the same heavy rag paper the letter itself was made of. If Marek ever went looking—and he would not, because looking would mean hoping—he would find only paper. Paper was everywhere in their lives. Paper was the family business's shadow twin: they cast letters in lead so that other people could put them on paper, and in eleven years Jonah had never once thought of paper as evidence.

Now she thought of little else.

* * *

The stationer's shop on Weavers' Lane was called Brandt & Sons, which was funny because old Brandt had no sons, only a daughter who kept the ledgers and a parrot that swore in three languages. Jonah had been sent there a hundred times for stock—the good German rag for the Ministry commissions, the cheaper stuff for handbills and playbills. Brandt knew every paper that came through Velmora the way Marek knew every typeface, and he liked to talk about both.

She went on a Tuesday morning, when her father was at the Ministry offices delivering proofs of the new commemorative face, and Tomas was at the docks before dawn and would not surface until evening. She wore her working apron under her coat still, so that she smelled of ink and solvent, so that she was only what she appeared to be: the Vasquez girl on an errand.

"Weaver's rag," Brandt said when she showed him the corner of the letter's paper—not the letter itself, never the letter itself, just a torn strip the size of her thumbnail, which she claimed was from an old order her mother had placed. "Haven't seen anyone ask for that in years. Fine stock. Expensive." He turned the scrap over, held it to the window light. "Watermark's mine, though. See the braid? That's my mark."

"When would Mother have bought it?" Jonah asked, keeping her voice at the level of bookkeeping.

Brandt squinted. "Before your time running errands, I'd think. Your mother—" he said it easily, the way everyone in Velmora said it, the way the dead are mentioned in a city that has too many of them, "—she used to come in herself. Every few weeks. Always the same order. Weaver's rag, and the good iron-gall ink, the one that doesn't fade."

"Every few weeks," Jonah repeated.

"For years. Then she stopped." He shrugged and set the scrap down. "After that it was you and your brother coming in, wasn't it? Children grow into their errands."

Jonah looked at the wall of paper drawers behind him, each labeled in faded copperplate, and felt the vertigo open beneath her again like a trapdoor. Every few weeks. A woman buying fine paper and unfading ink, regularly, for years—a woman stocking a workbench. Not a hobbyist. Not a dabbler. A practitioner.

"Did she—" Jonah began, and had to start again. "Do you remember anything else about her orders? Anything odd?"

Brandt laughed, and the parrot screamed something obscene in Velmoran dialect. "Odd! Everything about Livia Vasquez was a little odd, child, if you don't mind me saying it now that she's gone. Lovely woman. Quiet. But she'd stand at that counter turning a sheet over and over like she was reading it with her fingers. And once—" he leaned across the counter, enjoying himself, a man offered a rare audience for an old story—"once she asked me if I'd ever sold paper to anyone from the Women's House at Kessel. Asked it casual, like asking after the weather. I said the House buys its own supplies through the Ministry, thank you very much, nobody from there comes to me. And she smiled at that. A strange smile. Like I'd confirmed something she already knew."

The shop went very quiet around Jonah. Or perhaps it didn't; perhaps it was only her blood, stopping in her ears and starting again.

"Why would she ask that?" Jonah heard herself say.

Brandt spread his hands. "I assumed she knew someone inside. Everyone knows someone inside, these days. But your mother—" and here his voice gentled, the way voices gentle for grief, and Jonah wanted to scream at him not to, not for that reason, not anymore—"your mother had been gone near a year by then. Maybe she was remembering. The dying do that. Reach back toward their work."

"Her work," Jonah said.

"Her letters. Beautiful hand. You have it, a bit—the Vasquez slant." He tapped the counter. "Well. What'll it be? Two quires of the standard?"

Two quires of the standard. As though the world could continue to be ordinary, as though a shopkeeper could say *the Women's House at Kessel* and *your mother* in the same breath and then sell you paper.

"The standard," Jonah said, and paid, and walked out into the gray morning with her hands cold inside her gloves.

Kessel. She said the name to herself as she crossed the Weavers' Bridge, the river sliding black and slow beneath the ice below. The Women's House of Correction sat upriver, past the tanneries, behind walls the color of old teeth. Everyone knew what it was the way everyone knew what the plague was: a fact you organized your life around avoiding. Political women went there. Forgers went there. Women whose names appeared on lists went there, and mostly they did not come out, and their families learned to use the past tense.

Her mother had asked about it. Had smiled when told no supplies came from there—smiled like a woman whose question had been answered *wrong*, or answered *right*, Jonah couldn't decide which was worse.

And underneath everything, steady as a watermark, ran the sentence from the letter, dated 1844, three years after the parish recorded her death: *you have your father's caution and my hands.*

My hands. Present tense. A dead woman claiming her own living fingers.

* * *

That evening she watched her father across the supper table and understood that she had never actually looked at him before.

Marek Vasquez ate the way he worked: precisely, without waste, without pleasure. He had cut the stew's meat into equal portions and arranged them at the edge of the plate, and he ate them in rotation, bread between, and his eyes rested on nothing. Eleven years of this table. Eleven years of his silence shaped exactly like grief, and she had believed in it completely, the way you believe in a well-made thing—because it bore weight, because it did not creak.

But a forgery bears weight too. That was the whole art of it.

Tomas was out again—docks, he said, though the docks had been letting men go all winter and Tomas came home smelling less of tar than of lamp oil and other men's tobacco. So it was only the two of them, and the clock, and the sound of the ice on the river carrying through the foundations like a word trying to be spoken.

"Father," Jonah said.

He looked up. His eyes were his wife's eyes—that was another thing she had never let herself notice, the brown of them, warm at the center and cool at the rim—and for one instant, before the calm settled over his face like a plate of glass, she saw him know. Fathers know. He saw the question in her before she had built any of its scaffolding, and he set down his spoon, and he waited.

"I went to Brandt's today," she said. "For stock."

"Mm."

"He remembers Mother. He says she bought paper from him for years. Good paper. And iron-gall ink that doesn't fade." She laid it out piece by piece, the way her father laid out sorts in a composing stick, each element in its place. "And he says that near the end, she asked him whether anyone from the Women's House at Kessel ever bought paper from him."

The clock ticked. Somewhere below, in the empty type cases, a draft moved paper the way a breath moves.

"You've been going through drawers," Marek said.

It was not a denial. It was not even a deflection, really—it was a sentence constructed with such terrible economy that it contained, folded inside it, the confirmation of everything else. He did not say *what nonsense*. He did not say *your mother is dead, Jonah*. He said: *you've been going through drawers,* which meant: *there are drawers worth going through, and I have known it for eleven years.*

"Is she alive?" Jonah asked. Her voice did not shake. She was proud of that, later, in the way one is proud of surviving a fall one hasn't landed from yet. "Father. Is she alive in Kessel?"

Marek Vasquez picked up his spoon.

He finished his supper. He ate the remaining portions in rotation, bread between, exactly as he had begun, and his hands did not tremble, and his face did not change, and it was the most frightening thing Jonah had ever watched a human being do. When the plate was clean he set down the spoon, aligned it parallel to the knife, folded his napkin, and looked at his daughter with an expression of such perfect, practiced mildness that she understood she was looking at the finest piece of work he had ever cut: a man with no interior, a face with nothing behind it, a matrix struck so deep and so smooth that even feeling could not take hold in it.

"No," he said.

Just that. No.

"Then explain the letter," Jonah said. "Dated 1844. In her hand. Explain why the parish register has no entry for her death—I checked, Father, I went to the archive, I read every page of those years. Explain why there's no grave we visit, no stone, no—"

"There's no stone," Marek said quietly, "because stones can be read."

The words dropped into the room and lay there.

He rose, gathered his plate and hers, carried them to the basin, and began to wash up, sleeves rolled, forearms slicked with water and tallow smoke, a widower at his evening labor, and if his back was a fraction too straight and his movements a fraction too deliberate, only a daughter who had spent her life watching hands at work would ever have seen it.

"Jonah." He spoke to the dishes. "Whatever you found, whatever you think you found—you will put it back, and you will forget it, and you will never say that name in this house again. Not to me. Not to Tomas. Not to anyone who comes to this door with a census book and a polite smile."

"Astrid," Jonah said. "You know her name."

"I know what she is." The water shut off. He dried his hands on the cloth, each finger individually, unhurried. "I have known since the day she walked in. Do you imagine the Ministry sends a woman to count our spoons because they've miscounted? They send her because someone, somewhere, kept a record that should have burned. Because somewhere in this city there is a page with our name on it that I have spent eleven years failing to find." He hung the cloth on its hook, square with the edge of the basin. "Your mother is dead. Say it to yourself until it's true. Grieve her properly, once, and be done. That is the only mercy left in this house."

"She isn't dead." Jonah's voice cracked at last, and she hated it. "You're not grieving her, Father. You're guarding her. There's a difference, and I finally see it, and you can't un-teach me now."

For a moment—less than a moment, the length of a single drop of water falling from the tap—something moved behind the glass of his face. Not softness. Something older and more afraid. Then it was gone, smoothed away by hands that had spent forty years making surfaces true and level.

"Go to bed," he said.

* * *

She did not go to bed.

She climbed instead to the loft above the casting room, where the heat from the furnace pot still rose faintly through the floorboards, and she sat in the dark with her knees drawn up, and she let herself do the thing she had refused to do for six days: mourn.

Because that was the joke of it, the cruel, exquisite joke. She had grieved her mother at nine years old, cleanly, completely, the way children grieve—storm and wreckage and then, slowly, a shoreline where the grief used to be. She had built her whole self on that shoreline. And now the sea had come back, but wrong, inverted, a tide running backward: grief for a woman who might be alive, mourning that had nowhere to settle, love with no object it was permitted to touch. How did you miss someone standing in a cell sixty miles upriver? How did you weep for a death that maybe hadn't happened, knowing that your tears, if she lived, were the cruelest thing you could give her?

*You're the one with her hands,* the letter said. *Use them.*

Jonah looked down at her fingers in the dark. Ink in the creases, as always—no amount of pumice ever cleared the last of it, and she had stopped trying years ago. She had thought it was the foundry marking her. Now she wondered if it was inheritance.

Her mother had stocked fine paper and unfading ink. Her mother had asked careful questions in a stationer's shop and smiled when the answers were wrong. Her mother had, by every sign, gone on practicing her forbidden craft right up to the edge of whatever happened to her—and then, impossibly, from inside the place where the Ministry buried its mistakes, she had gotten a letter out. Through what hands? Past what eyes? A letter dated three years after her own death, delivered into a drawer her husband controlled, reaching a daughter she could not have known would ever find it—

No. Jonah sat up straight in the dark.

Not *could not have known*. Her mother had known exactly. *If you are reading this, then you have your father's caution and my hands.* Livia Vasquez had counted on two things: that Marek would hide the letter rather than destroy it, and that her daughter would disobey him. She had wagered on the family's whole architecture of silence having one crack in it, and she had bet that the crack would be Jonah.

A knock sounded at the street door below. Soft. Twice. Then, after a pause, twice more—the rhythm of someone counting, or signaling.

Jonah went cold and still on the loft floor. Below her, she heard her father's chair push back, unhurried, and his footsteps cross the shop floor to the door, and the bolt draw back.

And she heard Tomas's voice, low and fast and wrong—too quick, too bright, the voice of a boy carrying news that is burning a hole in him:

"Papa. Papa, you need to wake up. There are soldiers at the print shops on Tanner Street, and the type they're smashing in the windows—" a breath, a swallow— "it's yours. Papa, it's your face. Someone's been printing with your letters."
