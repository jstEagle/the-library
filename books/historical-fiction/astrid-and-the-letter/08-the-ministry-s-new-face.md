## 8. The Ministry's New Face

The summons came on a Tuesday, delivered by a boy in a grey coat who would not step across the threshold of the foundry. He held the envelope out at arm's length, as though the paper itself might dirty him, and when Marek took it the boy was already walking away, his heels striking the cobbles in a rhythm too quick for his years. Trained, Marek thought. Even the messengers were trained now.

He did not open the letter until he had climbed to the workroom above the casting floor, where the light came clean through the north windows and no one could watch his face. The seal was the Ministry of Information's: a closed eye over an open book. Someone with a sense of humor had designed that mark years ago, or someone without one; Marek had never decided which.

*The Ministry requests the services of Master Typefounder M. Vasquez for a commission of national significance. Present yourself at the Palace Annex, Thursday next, ten o'clock. Bring specimens of your finest work.*

*Requests.* The word sat wrong on the page, the way a borrowed coat sits wrong on the shoulders. In forty years of cutting letters Marek had learned that language betrayed intention before action ever did. A request from the Ministry was a summons wearing gloves.

He set the letter down and looked out over the river. The ice had begun its late-winter muttering—long groans that traveled through the foundry's stone foundations and up through the soles of his boots, as if the building itself were restless. Spring was coming. Spring always brought the Ministry out of its winter lethargy, hungry for ceremonies, for commemorations, for new ways of printing its name on things.

"Father?" Jonah stood at the foot of the stairs, her sleeves rolled, lead dust silvering her forearms. She had her mother's habit of reading him from a distance—the tilt of his shoulders, the set of his hands. "Bad news?"

"A commission," he said.

"You don't look like a man who's been commissioned."

"The Regent's jubilee." He watched her face for the flicker it gave—the small tightening around the mouth that she thought she hid from him and never had. His children had inherited Livia's face and none of her talent for concealing what moved behind it. "They want type cut for the commemorative editions."

"That's good work," Jonah said carefully. "Steady work."

"It's their work." He picked the letter up again, folded it along its crease. "There's a difference, and they know I know it, which is why they've sent for me personally instead of writing to the guild."

Jonah said nothing. She had learned that too—from him, he supposed, or from the years of silence he'd taught her. She climbed the last two steps anyway and put her hand briefly on his arm, and the touch was so like Livia's that he had to turn away toward the window until it passed.

* * *

The Palace Annex had been a merchant's house once, before the Regent's grandfather confiscated it, and it still wore its grandeur awkwardly, like a soldier dressed in a dead man's clothes. Gilt frames held portraits of officials; the parquet had been scuffed by a decade of government boots. Marek waited forty minutes in an anteroom among clerks who did not look up from their ledgers, and he used the time as he used all waiting: studying letterforms. The Ministry's stationery. The engraved notices on the walls. The way the clerk nearest him formed his figures—open fours, a lazy seven. Everything told you something, if you read it.

At eleven minutes past ten by the annex clock—which ran fast, another small dishonesty—he was shown into the office of Undersecretary Corvus.

Corvus was younger than Marek expected, soft-bodied and precise, with the kind of face that would photograph well on a proclamation. He did not rise. He gestured to a chair with two fingers, the way one gestures at furniture rather than people.

"Master Vasquez." He pronounced the name correctly, which meant he had rehearsed it, which meant Marek's file lay open somewhere within reach. "You come highly regarded. The guild speaks well of you. Your *Garamond revival*—" he consulted a paper without appearing to consult it, "—won the triennial prize, I believe. Before my time."

"Before most people's time," Marek said.

A smile touched the undersecretary's mouth without reaching anywhere near his eyes. He slid a portfolio across the desk. Inside was a design—not a finished drawing but a study, confident and vulgar, all vertical emphasis and heavy serifs, letters built like monuments to themselves. A jubilee face. Letters for shouting.

"This is the concept," Corvus said. "Approved at the highest level. What we require is execution. A complete face—roman, italic, the full range of figures—with matrices cut and cast, delivered to the Government Press by the first of March."

Marek turned the study toward the light. It was competent. It was also, in its bones, derivative—there was a Didot in the thinning of the horizontals, and something of Bodoni's arrogance in the ball terminals—but it had been drawn by someone who understood spectacle if not craft. The counters were too tight. At small sizes the letters would close up and choke.

"There are problems with the counters," Marek said.

"I beg your pardon?"

"If you cast this as drawn, the lowercase will fill in below twelve point. Ink squeeze. You'll lose the interiors of the *a*, the *e*, the *o*. Every proclamation printed from this face will look smudged." He laid the sheet flat and tapped it once with a blunt finger. "Whoever drew this has an eye. They don't have hands."

For a moment something moved behind Corvus's smooth expression—irritation, quickly filed away. "The design is approved. Approved designs are not altered."

"Then your proclamations will be smudged."

"They will not be smudged, Master Vasquez, because you will correct the technical deficiencies while preserving the approved character of the design. That is what execution means." Corvus folded his hands on the desk. "You will, of course, be compensated at the standard rate."

"The standard rate." Marek kept his voice level. "And the credit?"

"Credit?"

"My name. On the specimen sheets. In the colophon of the commemorative edition. Founders sign their faces, Undersecretary. It's how the trade knows whose work it's buying. It has been done for every face cut in this city since before your Ministry had a building."

Now Corvus did smile, fully, warmly, and it was worse than the two-fingered gesture. "Ah. I see the misunderstanding." He rose, moved to the window, hands clasped behind his back, a man admiring the view of the frozen river. "This is a national commission. The face belongs to the nation. It would be—unseemly—for a private craftsman's name to appear on the Regent's jubilee type, as though the Regent's anniversary were a commercial venture. Don't you agree?"

What Marek agreed was irrelevant, and both of them knew it. He looked at the study again—at those swollen, shouting letters—and understood the shape of the thing being offered. Not payment. Surrender. They wanted his hands because his hands were the best in Velmora, and they wanted his name struck off because his name was worth nothing to them except as something to remove. The erasure was not an oversight. The erasure was the point. Power announcing itself by taking a man's signature and leaving him his fingers.

"The first of March is very near," Corvus said to the window.

"It is."

"And the winter has been hard on everyone. On businesses especially. One hears about fuel costs. About inspections." He let the word *inspections* sit in the room like a coin dropped on marble. "The Ministry values craftsmen who can meet a deadline. It remembers them."

Marek looked at his own hands, resting on his knees—broad, scarred at the knuckles, stained at the cuticles with forty years of lead and ink that no amount of pumice ever wholly removed. Hands that had taught two children their letters. Hands that had cut the private face, the one that existed nowhere in any ledger, the one his son was setting pamphlets with in some cellar across the river, though Marek did not let himself think of that here, not in this room with its fast clock and its listening walls.

"I'll need my best apprentice for the finishing," he said. "And delivery of the drawings today. And double the standard rate, in advance."

Corvus turned from the window, surprised—and Marek saw the surprise with satisfaction. The man had prepared for gratitude, for servility, perhaps even for tears. He had not prepared for arithmetic.

"Double is—"

"Half again, then, and I start tomorrow. But in advance. Lead and steel cost money, Undersecretary, and so does my silence about the counters, which you will want kept quiet, since correcting them will mean admitting the approved design was flawed."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below, a door slammed; the annex went on with its business of recording citizens. Then Corvus laughed—a short, genuine bark that seemed to astonish even himself—and pressed the bell on his desk.

"You're everything the file says you are," he said. "Drawings will follow you home this afternoon. Half again, in advance. And Master Vasquez—" the warmth drained back out of his voice as quickly as it had come, leaving only the administrative cold beneath, "—no name. Not on the specimens, not in the colophon, not spoken. If your name appears anywhere in connection with this face, we will assume there has been an error in our records regarding your household. Errors of that kind attract attention. Attention attracts audit. You understand audits, I believe."

*I believe you do,* the words did not say, but said anyway, in the language beneath the language.

Marek understood. He rose, inclined his head the precise degree owed to an undersecretary and not a hair more, and walked out through the anteroom of clerks who still did not look up, past the closed eye and the open book, down the steps into air that tasted of river ice and coal smoke.

* * *

The drawings arrived that evening, carried by the same grey-coated boy, and after supper Marek cleared the big table in the workroom and pinned the study sheets to the wall where the lamplight fell full on them. Jonah watched from the doorway, Tomas's chair standing empty beside her—he was late again, third night running, and none of them remarked on it, which was itself a kind of remark.

"Show me," Jonah said.

He showed her. He took a clean sheet, chalked in corrections to the counters, opened the choked bowls of the lowercase with a few strokes, rebalanced the weight so the letters would breathe at text sizes instead of suffocating. It took him twenty minutes. When he stepped back, the design was still vulgar, still built for shouting—but it would print cleanly, crisply, gloriously legible at any size the Government Press cared to run it.

"So they get a better face than they asked for," Jonah said slowly, "and no one will ever know it's yours."

"No one will ever know."

"Doesn't that—" She stopped herself, the way she stopped herself these days before every second sentence, weighing each word against the family doctrine of invisibility. *No opinions. No petitions. No portraits.* He had made that doctrine; he could not blame her for reciting it. "Doesn't it matter to you? Forty years, Father. The triennial prize. Apprentices who'd give a season's wages to say they trained under Vasquez of Velmora. And they'll hang this face on the Regent like it grew there."

Marek unpinned the corrected sheet and held it up to the lamp. His own strokes, his own judgment, living inside someone else's vanity like a soul in a borrowed body.

"When your mother was alive," he said, and heard Jonah go very still at the tense, "she asked me once why I never signed the punches I reworked for other founders. There was a Dutchman, before the war, who sold my corrections as his own for years. I knew. Everyone in the trade knew. And I let him, because the work was good and the work mattered more than the name." He set the sheet down. "I told her a craftsman's name is just the shadow the work throws. If the light moves, the shadow moves. But the work stays."

"And do you still believe that?"

It was a child's question and a judge's question at once, and it went into him deeper than Corvus's threats had. He thought of the empty space on the specimen sheets where his name would not be. He thought of the parish register with its blank line where a death should have been entered. He thought of a box of sketches burned in the yard three winters ago, ash rising off the coals, and how he had told himself that destroying a woman's drawings was protecting her children, and how the telling had never quite taken.

"I believe," he said, "that a man keeps what he can carry. Come. Hold the lamp steady. We'll start the master drawings tonight—if the first of March is to be met, every stroke must be cut twice, once in pencil and once in steel, and steel doesn't forgive."

They worked until midnight, father and daughter, the scratch of the graver's practice runs on scrap pewter keeping time with the groan of the river ice outside. It was, he had to admit, good work. The letters came alive under his correction, took on a strength the original draftsman had reached for and missed. Somewhere in Velmora, in a month's time, ten thousand copies of a jubilee proclamation would roll off the Government Press in letters that carried Marek Vasquez's hand in every curve and terminal, and not one reader in ten thousand would wonder whose hand it was, and the one person in ten thousand who might have wondered—

He stopped the graver.

The thought arrived whole, the way dangerous thoughts do, uninvited and already grown: *the one person who would know this hand on sight is the person the world agrees is dead.*

Livia had known his letterforms the way she knew her own heartbeat. She had teased him about them, early in their marriage—*you put a little hook on your lowercase g, Marek, like a man pulling a door shut behind him*—and she had been right; the hook was there, unconscious, ineradicable, and he was putting it now into the jubilee face stroke by stroke because a man's hand is his hand whether he signs it or not. Which meant that if she lived—if the impossible were true and his erased wife existed anywhere within reach of a Government Press proof sheet—she would see him in these letters the moment they were printed. She would know he was alive. She would know where.

She would know he had bent.

Marek set down the graver and pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes until green sparks swam in the dark. Jonah glanced up from the lamp.

"Father?"

"Nothing. Cramp." He took up the graver again. Steel does not forgive, and neither do deadlines, and the Ministry's half-payment lay locked in the strongbox downstairs like a bribe he could not refuse to have accepted.

But the thought stayed with him, hooked like his lowercase g, pulling a door shut behind him that he suddenly, terribly, was not sure he wanted shut. Because if Livia could see his hand in the jubilee face, then the face was a message whether he willed it or not—a letter written in steel to a woman the state insisted was dead—and Marek caught himself, at midnight, at his bench, wondering with a hunger that shamed him:

*If she reads it—what would I want her to know?*

Outside, the river answered with a long groan of shifting ice, and in the drawer of the worktable where he had hidden it three years ago, sealed and undisturbed, something waited that he did not yet know his daughter had already found.
