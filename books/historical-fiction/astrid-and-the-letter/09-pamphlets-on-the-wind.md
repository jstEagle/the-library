## 9. Pamphlets on the Wind

The press room above the cooper's shop on Saltpeter Lane smelled of tallow, wet paper, and the particular sourness of men who had not slept enough to be careful. Tomas Vasquez stood at the stone sink washing the last of the ink from his hands when Rell brought the broadsheet in from the drying lines, holding it up against the lamplight like a man displaying a fish he was not entirely sure was legal.

"There," Rell said. "Read it aloud. I want to hear how it sounds in a voice that isn't mine."

Tomas dried his hands on his apron and took the sheet. Two hundred copies, pulled through the night while the cooper below slept or pretended to, and now folded into quarters small enough to vanish inside a coat sleeve. The headline ran across the top in letters taller than his thumb: *THE JUBILEE OF A REGENT WHO FEEDS ON HIS PEOPLE.* Below it, figures—grain requisitions, the cost of the triumphal arch on Founders' Bridge, the number of children entered into the charity rolls of Velmora's third district last winter. Numbers were harder to argue with than slogans. That had been his argument, three nights ago, when they'd argued about what the broadsheet should say, and it seemed someone had listened.

He read it aloud. His voice did the words better than he expected; there was a rhythm to them, a falling cadence at the end of each paragraph like water finding its level.

"Good," said Mirela from the corner, where she sat mending a roller skin by candlelight, her needle moving with the patience of someone who had done this in worse rooms than this one. "Now the hard part."

"The printing was the hard part," said Rell.

"The printing is never the hard part." She bit her thread. "The hard part is tomorrow, when every clerk and constable in this city has one folded in his pocket, and every one of them is asking the same question: *who set this type?*"

Tomas looked down at the sheet in his hands and felt the question land somewhere beneath his ribs.

Because he knew who had set it. Not tonight—tonight they'd used the stolen case, the mixed assortment of bodies that had come to the cell eight months ago through channels no one would name. But the *faces*. He had known them the first night he pulled a proof, before anyone told him anything. He had run his thumb along the serifs and felt his father's hand in them, the way you know your own door in the dark. The lowercase *a* with its shallow bowl. The *g* with that stubborn double-story defiance Marek Vasquez had cut against fashion twenty years ago because, as he put it once over supper, "a letter should stand up straight even when it is being lied about."

The type was unmistakable. That was the trouble with craft. You could steal a face but you could not unmake the maker's fingerprint, and his father's fingerprint was all over two hundred sheets of treason now drying on lines strung across a cooper's attic.

"Tomas." Mirela was watching him. "You've gone pale as a fresh pull."

"It's nothing." He folded the broadsheet along its creases. "It's the fumes. We should open a window before we suffocate."

Rell laughed and went to shove the casement wide. Cold river air poured in, carrying coal smoke and the groan of ice far out in the channel, and Tomas stood at the window with the folded paper pressed flat against his chest like something he needed to keep warm, and understood—for the first time, fully, in the way that understanding arrives only after the door has already closed behind you—that he had brought his father's hands into this room without ever asking their permission.

* * *

The distribution went out before dawn, the way it always did. Tomas took the Market Ward because he knew it best: the fish stalls, the corn exchange, the tavern called the Anchor & Chain where dockmen drank before first shift. He moved through the gray streets with fifty folded broadsheets distributed among three pockets and a satchel, and he had learned the trick of it—you didn't hand them out, you *left* them, tucked under a scale weight, slipped inside a barrel hoop, weighted on a table with a coin so no one could pretend they hadn't been meant to find it. A pamphlet handed from one hand to another made two conspirators. A pamphlet discovered made a hundred readers.

By seven o'clock the city had it. By nine, he heard it read aloud.

He was buying bread—he had learned that a man standing still in a market with nothing in his hands was a man remembered, and a man eating was invisible—when a journeyman printer from the Weft District climbed onto the base of the fountain and began reading the broadsheet to a gathering crowd. His voice cracked on the numbers about the charity rolls. Someone in the crowd shouted *read that part again*, and the man read it again, louder, and the crowd did the thing crowds do when arithmetic becomes outrage: it grew.

Tomas stood at the edge of it with bread in his fist and his heart going like a trip-hammer, and what he felt was not fear. That was the thing he would remember afterward, the thing that shamed him later. What he felt was *pride*—hot and specific and personal. Those are our letters, he thought. Those are my father's letters, standing up straight in front of the whole city, saying what we were never allowed to say at our own supper table. Somewhere under the fear that hadn't arrived yet, some younger version of himself was thinking: *this is what it sounds like when the silence breaks.*

Then the whistles started.

They came from four directions at once, which meant it was planned, which meant it wasn't about the crowd—the crowd was dispersing already, folding into alleys with the instinct of people who knew exactly how much disorder Velmora tolerated and for how long. No. The whistle-lines converged on the reader, and the reader ran, and two constables chased him into the Weft with the crowd cheering and scattering around them like water around stones, and Tomas stood very still with his bread and watched a third man work the fountain's edge, picking up abandoned broadsheets, smoothing each one flat, and studying it—not reading it, *studying* it—with the tilted, forensic attention of someone examining a signature.

The man wore no uniform. He wore a good gray coat and carried himself like an accountant. He turned the sheet toward the light and angled it, and Tomas understood with a cold drop through his stomach that the man was looking at the typeface the way Tomas himself had looked at it the first night. Reading the maker in the letters.

Tomas walked away at a market-stroll pace, chewing bread he couldn't taste, and did not run until he reached the mouth of Tanner's Cut, and then he ran.

* * *

By evening, the news had come in from the other wards, and it was worse than scattered arrests.

Three print shops raided in a single afternoon—that was the phrase going around the cell, passed mouth to ear in the cooper's stairwell because no one dared say it upstairs anymore. Havel's shop off Cordwainer Street: presses seized, matrices impounded, old Havel himself taken away in a closed carriage, which everyone knew meant the Ministry building rather than the watch house, and everyone knew what the Ministry building meant. The Kessler brothers' operation behind the dye works: ransacked, the cases dumped out onto the floor in a spill of loose type that neighbors said glittered in the afternoon light like spilled teeth. And the third—

"The third was a bookbinder," Mirela said. "A bindery on Quay Lane. They took the man's entire stock of endpapers." Her voice was flat in the way she flattened it when she was doing arithmetic she didn't like. "Three shops. None of them ours. Which means they don't know where ours is. Which means they're working backward from the evidence instead of forward from a traitor."

"Is that supposed to be comforting?" Rell asked.

"It's supposed to be accurate. Comfort is your department."

They were gathered in the cellar beneath the cooperage, the real meetings happening down here now, among barrels of brine and hoops of green wood. There were six of them. Tomas stood against the wall near the lamp, half in shadow, saying nothing, doing the other kind of arithmetic—the kind that had begun in the market square that morning and had not stopped since.

Because here was the shape of it, laid out plain: investigators had three seized collections of type and one broadsheet that was multiplying through the city faster than cholera. They would compare. That was what men in gray coats did—they compared. And when they compared the jubilee broadsheet against everything else in Velmora's print trade—and they would, letter by letter, serif by serif—there was one foundry on Ironmonger Row whose faces would stand out from the common stock the way a thoroughbred stands out in a plow team. The Vasquez private cuts. The display roman the Ministry commissioned last autumn. The text face Marek had spent four years cutting and shown to almost no one, because showing was advertising and advertising was visibility and the Vasquez family had survived thirty years on the opposite principle: no portraits, no petitions, no opinions, no names on any list.

And now his father's letters were on ten thousand lists.

"They'll trace it," Tomas said.

The cellar went quiet. Six faces turned toward him, and Mirela raised an eyebrow: *go on.*

"My father's type." He made himself say it plainly, because a thing hidden grows teeth. "The face on the broadsheet isn't common stock. It's a private cut. There's one foundry in this city that cuts like that, and if your investigators know anything about type—and the man in the market this morning knew something about type—then within a week, maybe less, they'll be standing on my father's doorstep asking him to account for every pound of metal in his racks."

"You're certain it's his?" That was Rell, carefully neutral.

"I set a proof with those letters every night for eight months. I grew up sorting them. I'd know his *a* blindfolded, by touch, in the dark." He heard his own voice sharpen and made himself lower it. "I'm certain."

Mirela set down the roller skin she'd been working on. In the lamp shadow her face was all planes and patience. "Then we have a question," she said, "and it's not a comfortable one. Did the Ministry commission that face?"

"Yes. Last year. For information bulletins."

"Then they have specimens. Drawings. Perhaps the punches themselves on deposit, if the contract was written badly." She let that sit. "Which means whoever stole this case for us either didn't know whose type it was—or knew exactly, and wanted the trail to lead somewhere."

The cellar got colder. Tomas felt it in his arms before his mind caught up. Because there had been channels, eight months ago, that no one would name—a crate left at a chandler's, a note in a dead-drop, the whole courteous machinery of anonymity—and he had asked once who arranged it and been answered with a shrug that was too smooth to be honest. He had accepted the shrug. He had been hungry for belonging, for a cause, for letters that finally said something, and he had not asked the second question, the obvious question: *who benefits if the trail leads to Ironmonger Row?*

"You think someone gave us poisoned type," Rell said slowly. "You think this whole broadsheet was a net."

"I think," Mirela said, "that the Regent's jubilee is in eleven days, and the Ministry would very much like to arrest a famous dissident printer before it. And I think a foundry full of imperial commissions, run by a family famous for having no opinions, is either our worst liability—" she paused, and Tomas watched her decide whether to finish the sentence, and watched her decide to finish it—"or our best scapegoat. Possibly both."

Tomas pushed off the wall. "No."

"Tomas—"

"No. My father is a typefounder. He melts metal and makes letters and keeps his head down so completely he's practically underground. He has never—in his life—not once—" He stopped, because his voice had gone up, and shouting in a cellar under a cooperage was how cells died.

"He reported your mother's death to the census himself," Mirela said quietly. It wasn't cruel. That was the worst part; it was delivered with genuine gentleness, the way you tell a man his leg is broken. "I know about your family, Tomas. I make it my business to know about the families of the men who work my presses. Your household is the most carefully invisible household in Velmora. Do you know what that tells me? It tells me your father understands erasure better than anyone alive. And it tells me that when the investigators come—and they will come—he will do whatever he must to keep what's left of his family off their lists. Including giving them us."

"That's not—"

"Or," she said, and now her voice changed, softened, became almost warm, and somehow that was more frightening, "he protects you. Because fathers do that. And then the Ministry has a cooperative witness in the most skilled typefoundry in the city, and every private cut he ever made belongs to the state, and we have lost both a printer and a resource in one afternoon." She picked the roller skin back up. "I'm not your enemy, Tomas. The calendar is your enemy. Eleven days to the jubilee, and every hour of it, someone in a gray coat is comparing serifs."

Nobody spoke. Out beyond the cellar wall, faint through the earth and the brickwork, came the sound the river made these nights—a long, shifting groan, the ice beginning to talk in its sleep. Tomas had heard it every spring of his life and never listened. Now it sounded like something enormous changing its mind.

He walked home through streets gone quiet and blue with early dark, past the Anchor & Chain where a boy was sweeping trampled broadsheet fragments off the cobbles, past the corn exchange where the day's crowd had stood, and every lamp-lit window he passed he imagined a gray coat behind it, comparing letters. At the corner of Ironmonger Row he stopped. The foundry windows glowed the way they always glowed at closing time, furnace banked low, his father probably at the bench finishing the day's last matrices, his sister probably locking the cash drawer and checking the street twice before she stepped out.

He stood there long enough that a lamplighter noticed him and marked it, he was sure of that too now—he noticed everything, that was the whole disease of the last twelve hours.

Through the window he could see his father's silhouette bent over the bench, hands moving with their lifelong unhurried precision, a man utterly unaware that his life's work was at that moment spread across a city in ten thousand copies, testifying in his voice, in his hand, saying everything Marek Vasquez had spent thirty years refusing to say.

Tomas turned toward the door. He had to tell them. Tonight, before the knock came instead.

His hand was on the latch when he saw, across the row, under the sign of the chandler's shop, a carriage standing with its lamps unlit.

Two men sat inside it, motionless, facing the foundry.
