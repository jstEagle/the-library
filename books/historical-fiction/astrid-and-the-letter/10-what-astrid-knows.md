## 10. What Astrid Knows

The summons came at half past seven in the morning, delivered by a boy with a ministry satchel who smelled of cheap tobacco and did not wait for a receipt. Astrid read the note twice while standing in her lodgings with one boot on. *Kessel. The Vasquez woman. Confirm identity against prison rolls. Report by Friday.* No signature beyond the initial of Deputy Director Havel, whose handwriting she knew better than her own father's, having spent two years transcribing his marginalia into clean reports.

She had expected this. That was the trouble with expecting things—they arrived anyway, and they arrived heavier than anticipation had made them.

* * *

The road to Kessel followed the river north out of Velmora, climbing away from the smoke of the foundry districts into country that was still, in late winter, mostly colorless. Astrid rode in a ministry coach with a driver who talked about his daughter's measles and asked no questions, which she took as a small mercy or a small insult—she could not decide which. Through the window the river showed itself in patches between bare willows: grey ice gone soft at the edges, dark water breathing through the cracks like something waking.

Three years ago she would have looked at that ice and thought of nothing but distance. Now she looked at it and thought: *that is how long a lie can hold its shape.* Ice held until it didn't. Then all at once, everywhere, with a sound like the end of the world conducted quietly.

The Women's House of Correction at Kessel sat on a bluff above a mill town, a long yellow building with black shutters, so respectable in aspect that a traveler might mistake it for a sanatorium or a wealthy widow's retreat. The illusion failed only at the wall—a plain stone wall, unremarkable in height, but topped with nothing at all, because the women inside were not considered worth the expense of glass shards or iron spikes. They were not runners, the Ministry's logic went. They were forgers, thieves, debtors, madwives. Where would they go?

Astrid signed the visitors' ledger with the name and office she had been given at birth and by appointment, and felt the familiar small disgust of watching her own hand perform its function without her.

* * *

Warden Brankov was a heavy man with the complexion of a man who ate well on institutional money, and he received her in an office that smelled of ink and boiled cabbage. He was, she noted within the first minute, nervous. Not guilty-nervous—administratively nervous, the fluster of a man whose paperwork was about to be examined.

"Livia Vasquez." He said the name the way he might say *the roof leak* or *the boiler*. "Yes. Inmate 214. Committed 1841. Document forgery, sedition adjacent." He pronounced *sedition adjacent* with care, as though the phrase had been handed to him pre-assembled and he dared not take it apart to see what was inside.

"I'll need to see her," Astrid said. "And before that, I'll need to see the intake record and confirm the identification against the original commitment papers."

"Of course, of course." He produced a ledger from a locked drawer with the eagerness of a man producing a receipt. Astrid turned pages. Intake photograph—the new process, grainy, the woman staring slightly left of the lens with an expression Astrid recognized because she had spent an evening studying that same expression across a tea table: not defiance, not fear, but *accounting*. Livia Vasquez looking at the camera the way she had looked at Astrid, tallying what it saw and what it would cost her.

Height, distinguishing marks, the scar on the left forearm recorded in a clerk's careful hand. All consistent. The Ministry wanted confirmation; here was confirmation, tidy as a hymnbook.

Except.

"The work assignments," Astrid said, running her finger down the column. "She's listed in the print room."

Brankov's face did something complicated. "We have a printing requirement. Prison regulations, forms, the chapel bulletins. The women do the typesetting. It keeps hands busy."

"And Vasquez has experience?"

"She—" He stopped. Started again. "There was some difficulty in her first year. She was assigned to laundry. She requested transfer to the print room. Requests are generally denied."

"But hers wasn't."

"It was... accommodated." He said the word like a bone he'd swallowed sideways. "She is useful. The output of the print room improved measurably after her transfer. Fewer spoiled forms. Fewer discrepancies." He caught the last word a moment too late, and Astrid watched him hear it himself, watched the blood climb his neck.

"Discrepancies," Astrid said mildly. "In prison documents."

"In nothing. In nothing at all." He stood, smoothed his coat, became briefly the warden rather than the man. "You may see her now if you like. Supervised, of course."

"Of course," said Astrid, who had already decided she would prefer very much to be unsupervised, and began composing the request she would make to obtain exactly that.

* * *

It took her two days and a forged pretext of her own—which she noted, with a certain dry internal humor, was the first document fraud of her career, committed in service of the state, which somehow made it worse. She told Brankov that the Ministry required observation of the inmate under ordinary working conditions, unannounced and unaccompanied, per the new protocols. She invented the protocols. She cited a directive number that belonged to a memorandum about coal rations. Brankov, drowning in his own relief at being given instructions rather than questions, agreed to everything.

So on the third morning Astrid Lindqvist stood alone in the doorway of the prison print room, watching eleven women set type by lamplight while snow ticked against the high barred windows.

The room was warm, which surprised her, and quiet, which did not. Typesetting had a sound—she knew it now, had learned it in a foundry on the Velmora embankment, the small dry music of sorts dropping into a composing stick, the breath of the brush across the case. Eleven women making that sound in unison was less like a workshop than like a choir that had taken a vow of silence and expressed itself in lead.

Livia Vasquez worked at the far bench, and she did not look up when Astrid entered, though Astrid was certain—certain the way one is certain of weather—that the woman had registered her before the door finished opening. There was a quality to stillness that announced itself. Livia continued setting a line of the prison form, her hands moving with the economy of long habit, and only when she had finished the line and justified it did she turn her head.

"Well," she said. "The census."

"You remember me."

"I remember everyone who asks questions." She wiped her hands on her apron with terrible deliberation. "You've come a long way to check my spelling."

Astrid crossed the room slowly, aware of the other women—not looking, all of them not looking, which in a prison is a form of looking. "May I?" She indicated the bench beside Livia. Livia gestured with a small ironic courtesy, *be my guest*, and Astrid sat.

For a while neither spoke. Livia returned to her work. Astrid watched the hands.

They were a forger's hands. She understood this now, watching them, in a way she had not permitted herself to understand in the parlor in Velmora. The fingers were stained faintly at the cuticles—not with printer's ink, which washes, but with the deeper discoloration of years. And there was a callus, on the right index finger, in the exact place a compositor develops one from the edge of the composing stick. Three years of laundry would have softened such a callus. Two years of the print room had rebuilt it.

"You're comfortable here," Astrid said quietly.

"I'm imprisoned here." Livia lifted a sort, examined its face against the lamp, rejected it, chose another. "Comfortable is a different country."

"The print room. You requested it."

"I requested a place where the hours pass."

"And the warden accommodated you. A forger, given run of the prison press." Astrid kept her voice level, conversational, pitched beneath the hearing of the other benches. "That's unusual."

"Prisons are full of unusual arrangements. Men get bored. Bored men are generous to women who make their lives easier." Livia set another line. "I correct his reports. His requisitions. I make his numbers balance. In exchange, I set type."

"What type?"

The smallest pause. A pause measured in the time it takes a hand to decide whether to tremble. "Prison type. Forms. Bulletins."

"Mm," said Astrid.

Because she had spent the previous evening doing what she did instead of sleeping, which was reading. She had gone back through the intake records, the disciplinary log, the transfer manifests—and then, because a discrepancy itches like a bite, through the release records. Forty-one women released from Kessel in the past eighteen months. She had pulled the passes, the travel permits, the identity certificates each departing prisoner required. She had laid them side by side under her lamp until three in the morning.

They were beautiful. That was the thing she could not put down, the thing that had ridden with her all night into sleep and out again: the documents were *beautiful*. Consistent hands where consistency was expected, deliberate imperfections where forgery-hunters look for perfection. The dates fell on plausible weekdays. The seals were pressed at the correct depth, the ink aged correctly, the clerk-signatures matched the genuine article down to the hesitation strokes. Whoever made them had access to the prison's own stock, its own seal, its own model books—and had used that access forty-one times.

Forty-one women had walked out of Kessel with new names, and every paper that let them walk had come, somewhere in its making, from the second bench by the north window.

Astrid had checked the releases against their files. Sixteen had completed legitimate sentences; their papers were ordinary clerical work. Twenty-five had been released early—commuted, transferred, pardoned on grounds variously thin. Of those twenty-five, she could find authorization for perhaps nine.

Sixteen women had walked out of a prison on documents that should not exist, and the system had swallowed them whole, and no one had noticed, because no one checks the paperwork of people nobody wants back.

Until now, Astrid had assumed the forger was outside—someone bribing a clerk. Sitting here, watching the callused finger justify a line of type, she revised the geography of the crime entirely.

"Your son writes to you," Astrid said. "Your daughter doesn't. Why do you suppose that is?"

Something moved behind Livia's eyes—not surprise at the question but at its direction, a card player recalculating. "Jonah doesn't write because Jonah is afraid of what I'd tell her to do. Tomas writes because Tomas wants to be told." She set the line, brushed it, slid it into the galley. "You didn't come here to discuss my children."

"No."

"You came because someone in Velmora finally counted."

The words landed with a precision Astrid felt in her sternum. *Counted.* The census. Her own signature at the bottom of the Vasquez household record, her own careful notation—*death not entered in parish register*—which she had written in a moment of professional honesty she had regretted every day since, because she knew what such notations did. They traveled upward. They reached desks. They reached Deputy Director Havel, who underlined things.

"Yes," Astrid said. "Someone counted."

"And now you're here to confirm I'm real. Dead women are so much easier to administer." Livia turned from the case at last and faced her fully, and up close, in the lamplight, Astrid could see what three years had done: sharpened her, the way a tool is sharpened, removing everything not needed for the work. "So. Confirm me. I'm Livia Vasquez, born 1801, committed 1841, resident of this establishment, inmate 214. Every fact correct. Will that satisfy your report?"

"It would," Astrid said, "if facts were what I'd come for."

"And what did you come for?"

Astrid looked at the composing stick. At the case. At the little brass label screwed to the front of the type cabinet, which read, in the prison's own inventory hand, *Assorted faces, mixed, provenance unknown.*

But she had opened that cabinet the evening before, during her invented inspection, with the warden's keys sweating in her own gloved hand. Mixed faces, yes—but among the jumbled sorts, worn nearly smooth with use, she had found a private alphabet. A lowercase *a* with a distinctive hooked terminal. A *g* with a flat ear. Letterforms she had seen once before, in a drawer of a house on the embankment, pressed into wax on the back of an impossible letter.

Provenance unknown. Provenance smuggled. Someone had carried type into a prison piece by piece—in book bindings, in bread, in the hem of a visitor's coat over months, over years—and Livia Vasquez had built a face of her own inside these walls, the way a prisoner grows a plant from contraband seeds, and she was printing freedom with it, sixteen women at a time.

"I came for the truth," Astrid said. "Which is a foolish thing to say in a prison, where truth is the first thing confiscated."

Livia studied her for a long moment. Around them the other women typed and breathed and did not listen, magnificently.

"You have a choice," Livia said at last, and her voice changed—not softer, but closer, the voice of a woman across a table rather than across an interrogation. "You can write what Havel wants: identity confirmed, no anomalies, matter closed. Or you can write what you found, and I will be moved—there's a facility south, for politicals, no press, no windows worth mentioning—and sixteen women's new names will become sixteen old cases, reopened, hunted. Or—" she paused, and for the first time something almost gentle entered her face, "—you can write nothing much at all. Clerks survive by writing nothing much at all. I'm told it's a respected art."

"You're asking me to falsify a government report."

"I'm telling you what the choices cost. I don't ask. Asking is how people hang." She picked up her stick again. "You'll notice I haven't denied anything. That's a courtesy. Denials insult us both."

Astrid sat a moment longer. Somewhere above them a door slammed; snow ticked at the glass; the lamp hissed. She thought of Jonah Vasquez pouring tea with steady hands and frightened eyes, circling her like two animals discovering they were the same species. She thought of Havel's underlined memoranda, of the jubilee preparations swallowing the city's budget, of a regime that counted people the way a butcher counts carcasses.

She thought: *I came here to close a file.*

She thought: *the file is open now, and it opens inward, into me.*

"I'll need to inspect the print room thoroughly before I leave," Astrid said aloud, standing. "Inventory the type. Check the presses against the requisition logs." She met Livia's eyes. "It may take me some time."

"Take all the time you need," said Livia Vasquez, and bent to her work, and the small dry music of the types rose around them both like rain.

* * *

Astrid wrote the report on the fourth evening, in her cold lodgings over the mill town's single respectable inn, with the wind worrying the shutters and the river ice groaning somewhere below in the dark—a long shifting sound, like furniture dragged across the floor of the world.

She wrote: *Identity confirmed against intake record and photograph. Inmate 214 is Livia Vasquez. Conduct satisfactory. Print room labor productive; recommends continuation. No anomalies requiring further inquiry.*

She read it twice. Every sentence true. Every sentence a lie. She signed it, blotted it, and set it in the dispatch case, and then she sat for a long time with her hands open in her lap, looking at them as though they belonged to a stranger—hands that had just, with two days of forged directives and one carefully emptied sentence, become accomplices to sixteen escapes and counting.

From inside her coat she drew the thing she had taken—not stolen, she told herself, *borrowed*, though the distinction was wearing thin—from the prison print room cabinet: a single piece of type, worn smooth, the lowercase *a* with the hooked terminal. Livia's letterform. Livia's hand, cast in lead.

She set it on the table beside the lamp. It caught the light and threw a small reversed shadow on the plaster wall—a tiny black *a*, backwards, the way all printed letters are true only in mirror.

Outside, the ice groaned again, longer this time, and did not stop.

Two days' ride south, in Velmora, a girl was waiting with a letter dated from the grave, and spring was coming, and the ferry lists would open soon. Astrid Lindqvist looked at the small leaden letter on her table and understood, with the clarity of a woman stepping off a ledge she had been walking toward for years, that she had already chosen—that the choosing had happened somewhere between the census page and the prison gate, silently, irreversibly—and that everything remaining was merely the working out.

She wrapped the *a* in a handkerchief and put it in her pocket, next to her heart, where she kept the things she intended to answer for.
