## 11. The Ice Groans

The ice spoke first at night.

I had been lying awake for an hour, counting the cracks in the ceiling plaster the way I counted everything now—sorts in a case, coins in a drawer, days since I'd read a dead woman's handwriting. Then the river made its sound. Not a crack, exactly. A groan, long and low, like something enormous turning over in its sleep beneath the bridge pilings, and it went through the floorboards of my room and into my chest and stayed there.

I got up. I stood at the window with my breath fogging the glass and listened to the Velmora talk in its sleep. The thaw was weeks away still—the willows along the embankment were bare whips, the snow on the warehouse roofs gray as old type—but the ice had begun to move, and once it moved it did not stop until spring took the whole river apart.

In the foundry below, I could hear my father's tread even at this hour. He slept badly since the jubilee commission; he rose badly too, and worked before dawn as if the Ministry might catch him resting. But tonight he was quiet. Only the ice, groaning, and me, awake, and somewhere across the city behind a wall I had no business thinking about—a woman whose posture I remembered better than her face.

I dressed in the dark.

* * *

I told myself it was an errand. The Kessel road passed the chandler's where we bought tallow, and the tallow was low, and a daughter running errands could walk wherever errands required. My father would believe it because he wanted to believe it, the way he believed all my silences were innocence rather than practice.

The morning was hard-bright, the kind of light that shows every flaw in a street. Sled runners squealed on packed snow. A fishmonger argued with a boy over a crate of pike gone stiff. Two Ministry clerks crossed the square with their ledgers under their arms like hymnals, and the whole market seemed to lower its voice as they passed, vendors suddenly fascinated by their own goods.

The Women's House of Correction at Kessel sat where the river bent north, on a shelf of rock above the water. It was not a fortress—it was worse than that, it was respectable. A former convent, whitewashed, its windows tall and narrow, its chapel spire still crowned with a cross that someone in the Ministry had never gotten around to removing. A wall of pale brick enclosed a yard, and beyond the wall you could see, when the wind was right, laundry strung between outbuildings: gray shifts, gray stockings, the washed-out colors of women who had been filed away.

There was a path along the river below the wall. It was not forbidden—that was the cruelty of it. Anyone might walk there. Fishermen did, in season. Children slid down the bank to the frozen shallows. The Ministry understood that visibility was its own kind of punishment, or perhaps they simply didn't care, and either way I walked it with my basket swinging and my heart going like a press at full speed.

Up close, the wall was higher than it looked from town. Mortar had crumbled in places and small green things grew in the gaps, stubborn as weeds in a gutter. At the base of the wall the snow was trampled into a track by the feet of women taking their exercise, and I could see their footprints overlapping, hundreds of them, pressed into each other so that no single step could be distinguished from any other. That was the whole architecture of the place, I thought. That was what the state did with a person. It walked them back and forth until their prints blurred into everyone else's.

Then the door in the wall opened.

It opened without ceremony—just a bolt drawn back and a gray shape stepping through into the cold—and I did not look up. I had been raised on not looking up. I studied my basket, the tallow-wrapped paper inside it, the stitching coming loose at one seam, while my ears did the work my eyes refused.

Boots first. A guard's boots, heavy, unhurried, stopping some ten paces off to smoke. And then softer footsteps, and voices—two women talking in the low, worn way of people who have said everything worth saying years ago—and among them, a laugh. Short. Dry. Tipped upward at the end.

My hands stopped moving on the basket seam.

I knew that laugh. I had heard it over a sorting tray twenty years ago, when my mother held up a mispoured matrix shaped like nothing on earth and laughed exactly that way—short, dry, tipped upward—as if the world were a joke she'd been telling herself all along. Grief is supposed to blur these things. Everyone says so. They tell you memory softens, that the dead become portraits of themselves, that you forget the living texture. No one tells you that sometimes memory keeps one detail in merciless focus and lets everything else go, so that you are left holding a single note of a voice like a single piece of type in your palm, and it is enough to identify the whole body.

I looked up. I couldn't help it. I broke my own rule, the family rule, the rule that had kept us alive through two censuses and three changes of government, and I looked.

Four women came around the corner of the wall in single file, walking their exercise circuit. Gray wool, headscarves, breath steaming. They kept their eyes down as prisoners learn to do. The guard behind them smoked and watched the river instead of them, because watching the river was permitted and watching them was work.

And the third woman—the third one, small, shoulders slightly rounded forward the way a person stands who has spent years bending over a bench, the posture of every craftsman I had ever known, the posture I saw in my own reflection—

She turned her head, just slightly, toward the sound of the ice.

The river groaned again beneath us, that long tectonic complaint, and the woman lifted her face to listen the way my mother used to lift her face to listen to the presses starting up in the morning, chin tilted, eyes half closed, attending to a machine the way other people attended to music.

I did not see her face. The scarf shadowed it; the distance was forty yards; and God knows what four years of prison had done to features I barely trusted myself to recall. It didn't matter. Faces lie. Posture doesn't. Twenty years of bending over lead does not lie, and neither does the tilt of a head toward a beloved sound.

The guard flicked his cigarette into the snow. The door opened. The women filed through, gray shapes swallowed by whitewash, and the bolt went home with a sound like a full stop.

I walked on. I did not run. Running draws eyes. I walked the length of the river path with my legs made of water and my mind made of fire, past the sliding children, past the fishermen's empty holes in the ice, until the prison was behind me and I could stand at the railing of the old ferry dock and put both hands on the cold wood and breathe.

She was alive. She was forty yards away and alive and I had stood there with a basket of tallow like any fool on an errand while my mother walked past me in a prisoner's wool.

* * *

The ferry office was a shed at the foot of the north landing, and it smelled of tar, wet rope, and the particular staleness of rooms where men wait out winter doing paperwork. The harbormaster's clerk was a young man with ink-stained cuffs and the defensive slouch of someone perpetually blamed for things above his station. Behind him, on the wall, hung the sailing schedule for the Velmora–Kessel–Norvik line, and I read it the way Tomas reads a broadsheet: twice, and then again.

ONE CROSSING REMAINS BEFORE ICE-OUT, it said, in letters I recognized, God help me, as a cheap government grotesque—some clerk's idea of authority. DEPARTURE 14 MARCH, WEATHER PERMITTING. ALL PASSENGERS REQUIRING MINISTRY TRAVEL PERMIT CLASS II OR ABOVE. NO EXCEPTIONS.

"Help you?" the clerk asked, without looking up.

"I'm asking about the spring crossing," I said. "For a relative."

"Everyone's asking about the spring crossing." He finally raised his eyes. He had the flat look of a man who has spent February saying no. "Berths are allocated by permit. Class two or better. You apply at the Ministry of Information, Office of Internal Movement, and if they grant it, the berth is assigned by lot among the approved. There's no buying one, before you ask. There's no favoring one. There's a list, and the list is the list."

"How many berths?"

"That's not posted."

"How many berths," I said again, quietly, "is a number. Numbers exist whether they're posted or not."

He almost smiled. Almost. "Eleven," he said, glancing at the doorway as though the number itself might be listening. "Nine are already committed—officials, transfers, a magistrate's household going north for the health of his wife. Two remain open to application. And there's forty-one applications in the tray." He tapped a wooden box on his desk, thick with papers, fat as a ledger. "So you'll understand why I don't encourage hope."

Two berths. Forty-one applications. And somewhere in the arithmetic of permits and lots and Ministry favor, a woman in gray wool behind a whitewashed wall who had once forged documents good enough to move dissidents across three borders.

I thought of her hands. I had watched those hands pour type when I was small—watched them hold a matrix to the light, checking the letter's edges against the flame, patient as prayer. Four years in Kessel had not unmade those hands. If anything, prison was the perfect workshop for her: time, desperation, and a captive population desperate for anything that looked like a signature on the right paper.

"Class two," I said. "What does it take?"

"Property, trade necessity, medical cause, or official sponsorship." He recited it like a catechism. "Or you're being deported, which nobody applies for."

"And if none of those apply?"

"Then you stay in Velmora." He said it without malice, which somehow made it worse. It was simply the weather of our lives. "Ice goes out mid-April most years. After that, the ferries run regular till November. But this crossing—" he nodded at the schedule, at the word REMAINS—"this is the last one the Ministry's chartering before the budget year turns. After March, who knows. Paper's expensive. Boats are expensive. People are not."

I thanked him. He grunted. As I turned to go he added, almost against his will, "If your relative's serious, apply early. The early ones get read. The late ones get weighed." He hefted the box of applications with one hand. It must have weighed fifteen pounds. "Mostly they get weighed."

* * *

Outside, the river had changed its voice.

All winter the ice had been silent or near-silent, a white lid clamped down, and now it talked constantly—cracks running out from shore like lightning frozen mid-strike, groans rolling upriver, sudden sharp reports like pistol shots that sent gulls screaming off the pilings. The ferrymen stood at the landing's edge studying the channel, chewing on pipes, saying nothing, reading the water under the ice the way my father read a proof sheet. One of them spat and said to another, "Three weeks. Maybe less if this warm wind holds."

Warm wind. There it was, faint from the south, smelling of mud and rot and the far-off sea. Spring was not a season here; it was a demolition. Every year the ice went out with a violence that shook windows in the lower town, and every year the ferrymen bet on the day, and every year the river won.

I stood at the rail and let the numbers assemble themselves, because assembling numbers was the only kind of thinking I trusted anymore.

One crossing. Fourteen March. Eleven berths, nine spoken for, two contested by forty-one applications weighted by class and favor. A mother alive behind a wall that opened twice a day for exercise. A brother spending his nights in a print shop with revolutionaries, his name drifting toward lists. A father who had cut type for the Regent's jubilee with his own design and someone else's credit, who had taught me that invisibility was survival, who would sooner burn the foundry than hear the words *your wife is alive* spoken aloud—I knew that about him now, I had known it since the letter in the drawer, since the terrifying calm with which he'd denied everything.

And Astrid Lindqvist, census taker, who asked one question too many and then came back with tea and careful eyes, and whom I had not seen in six weeks, and whose absence I had stopped believing was coincidence sometime around the second week.

The ice groaned again, right below the dock, so close I felt it in my teeth. Somewhere under that white lid the river was already moving, patient, unstoppable, keeping a schedule no Ministry had written.

Hope, I thought, is a deadline. Hope is fourteen days and two berths and a woman who can forge anything except her own freedom, because freedom requires paper, and paper requires access, and access requires a miracle or a traitor.

I had a letter in my possession written in her hand. I had grown up with that hand. I could reproduce its letterforms from memory—the slight forward lean, the open loop of the *e*, the way she crossed her *t*'s low, impatient, left to right in one motion.

I had learned forgery at my mother's knee without ever knowing that was what I was learning.

I pulled my coat tighter and started home through the bright hard morning, and behind me the ice groaned and shifted, and shifted, and would not stop.

At the mouth of Foundry Lane I stopped, because there was a figure waiting by our door, stamping her boots against the cold, and even at fifty yards I knew the straight-backed, deliberate silhouette of Astrid Lindqvist—and she was not alone. She was speaking with my father, who stood in the doorway with his arms folded and his face arranged into the pleasant blankness he wore for officials, and between them on the frozen ground lay the subject of their conversation, invisible but enormous, like the river under the ice.

Astrid looked up and saw me. She did not smile. She inclined her head a fraction—an acknowledgment, an apology, a summons—and I understood, standing in the cold with the tallow melting through its paper against my side, that whatever fragile truce we had built over tea was about to be tested against something harder than ice.

She had come about my mother. And by the look of my father's face, she had brought news that could not be walked back and forth until its footprints blurred.
