## 12. A Bargain in the Archive

The archive of the Second Census District occupied the ground floor of a former chapel on Candlemakers' Row, and it still smelled faintly of incense and old wax, as though the building itself remembered what it had been before the state repurposed its bones. Astrid Lindqvist had worked there for six years. She knew every shelf by its smell—the mildew of the 1810s returns, the iron-gall bite of the war registers, the sweet rot of the ledgers that had been flooded one spring and dried badly. She knew which drawers stuck and which clerks falsified and which hours the supervisor went to his lunch and did not return until the bells rang four.

She was alone in the archive at half past two on a gray Tuesday, collating the Kessel district rolls she had been ordered to verify, when the bell above the door gave its cracked note and Jonah Vasquez came in out of the rain.

Astrid had expected this visit eventually. She had not expected it to come this soon, or with the woman's coat soaked through and her hands shaking—not from cold, Astrid thought, but from something held too tightly for too long.

"The census office is on Fetter Lane," Astrid said, without looking up from the roll. "This is the archive. Closed consultations are by appointment."

"I know what this is." Jonah's voice was low and even, the voice of someone who had rehearsed composure on the walk over and was spending it like coin. "I've walked past this door my whole life. My mother used to buy ink from the chandler two doors down. She said the chapel made her nervous." A pause. "She was right to be nervous, I think."

Astrid set down her pen. She looked at the young woman standing in the aisle between the shelves—soaked wool, ink-stained fingers, eyes red-rimmed but dry—and felt the particular dread of an official who recognizes that the conversation about to happen cannot happen, and is going to anyway.

"You should not have come here," Astrid said quietly.

"No," Jonah agreed. She reached into her coat and drew out an envelope, wrapped in oilcloth, held the way one holds a live coal. "But I did."

* * *

They could not talk in the open archive. Astrid took her through to the back room where the damaged registers were stored—the ones too fragile or too falsified to shelve, stacked in acid-free boxes that no one consulted because consulting them raised questions no one wanted answered. There was a lamp there, a table, a single chair. Astrid took the chair herself; she did not know why except that sitting put her below Jonah's eye level, and some animal part of her wanted that.

"Open it here," Jonah said. "I want you to see the date first. I want to watch your face when you see the date."

Astrid unwrapped the oilcloth. The envelope inside was good stock—laid paper, rag content, the kind that cost more than a laborer's week—and the handwriting on its face was small, slanted, economical: *For J.V., if she asks the right question.*

The date beneath it read the ninth of March, 1844.

Three years after Livia Vasquez had been entered in the foundry's household census as deceased.

Astrid turned the envelope over twice, as if the date might change on the reverse. Her mouth had gone dry. "Where did you find this?"

"In a drawer in my father's workshop. Under the matrices for the old private face." Jonah's voice frayed slightly on the word *father*, then steadied. "He hid it under the letters. Under the alphabet. Where else would he hide anything?"

"Do you know what you're holding?" Astrid asked. "Do you understand what this is, legally speaking? A letter dated three years after a registered death means either the death record is false—which makes everyone who signed it liable—or the letter is forged, which is a hanging offense in this province since the Document Acts. Either way, this envelope is evidence. You have carried evidence through the streets to a Ministry employee."

"Yes."

"And you want me to—" Astrid stopped. She had been about to say *what*, and realized she genuinely did not know what the answer was, and that frightened her more than the envelope did.

"I want you to tell me the truth," Jonah said. "You came to our foundry twice. You asked about my mother both times. The second time you asked whether the parish register recorded her death, and when I said no, you wrote something in the margin of your book and closed it very carefully, the way people close things they intend to open again. I've spent three weeks watching you in my head, Census Taker Lindqvist. You're not hunting my family. You're hunting something else, and you keep hoping my family isn't it."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, water dripped through the old chapel roof onto stone, patient as a metronome.

"You've misread me," Astrid said. It was reflex, nothing more, and she heard the hollowness of it even as she said it.

"Then say so plainly, and I'll leave, and we'll never speak again, and you can write whatever you like in your margins." Jonah stood with her arms crossed, dripping onto the flagstones, and Astrid understood suddenly that this young woman had already lost everything that mattered and was therefore dangerous in the way only the dispossessed are dangerous: with nothing left to protect except the truth.

Astrid looked down at the envelope. At the handwriting. She had spent eleven days verifying the prison rolls at Kessel, cross-referencing numbers against names against transfer orders, and she had told herself every night that what she was doing was procedure. Due diligence. A clerk's conscience satisfied in a clerk's way.

But she had also copied out, in her own hand, on a scrap she kept in her boot, a single line from the Women's House intake ledger of 1841:

*Prisoner 4471, transferred from holding, name withheld per order of the Office of Information. Physical description: female, 34 years, scar right eyebrow, trade—*

And then the word that had kept her awake ever since.

*Compositor.*

A typesetter. In a prison whose records listed every other woman's trade as laundress, seamstress, prostitute, thief. Someone in the Ministry apparatus had wanted that detail buried, and someone else—someone inside—had let it survive in the fair copy.

"Sit down," Astrid said.

Jonah didn't move. "Tell me first."

"Livia Vasquez is alive," Astrid said. "She is prisoner 4471 in the Women's House of Correction at Kessel. She has been there since the winter of 1841. And as of roughly eight months ago, she has been forging documents for other prisoners—passes, transfer orders, discharge papers—with type that someone is smuggling to her piece by piece inside loaves of bread."

* * *

Jonah sat down after all. Not in the chair—on the floor, her back against the shelving, her knees drawn up, the way a person sits when the ground has moved and they need to confirm it is still there.

Astrid watched her absorb it and recognized the stages because she had performed all of them herself, alone, over eleven nights: denial, arithmetic, grief arriving late and sideways, and finally the cold clean anger that asks only one question—*what do I do now*.

"She's been there three years," Jonah said at last. "Three years. We lit a candle for her every year on the anniversary. Tomas wouldn't come after the second year. Father never came at all. He said mourning was—" She stopped. Pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "He said mourning was indulgence."

"Your father signed the death entry himself," Astrid said. It was not a question; she had read the household return. "In the column for deceased members of the household, he wrote her name, the year, and the cause: fever. He wrote it in a hand I would swear was not his natural hand. I noted it at the time. Small inconsistencies are my trade."

"Why would he do that?" Jonah lowered her hand. Her face was terrible and composed. "If the Ministry took her, why would he help them erase her?"

"Because erasure can go two directions," Astrid said. "I've been thinking about this for eleven days, so hear me out. If Livia Vasquez died of fever in 1841, then no one comes asking about her. No one watches her husband. No one wonders whether her children inherited her talents. Erasure protects the living as well as the dead." She paused. "Or it condemns them. I don't know yet which your father intended. But I know this: the order withholding her name from the prison roll came from the Office of Information, and the Office of Information does not hide prisoners it intends to release."

The rain intensified against the high window. Jonah pulled the oilcloth toward her and rewrapped the envelope with slow, precise movements, as though the wrapping were a ritual that kept her hands from shaking.

"There's more," she said. "Isn't there. I can hear it in how you're talking. You're giving me the facts in order of increasing danger, like a physician describing a wound from the skin inward."

Astrid almost smiled. It surprised her, the impulse; she had not smiled in this room in years. "You're your mother's daughter."

"Don't." The word cracked like ice underfoot. "Don't tell me who I resemble. Tell me the rest."

"The rest," Astrid said, "is that I am ordered to file a confirmation report within the week confirming that prisoner 4471 is Livia Vasquez, document forger, so that the Ministry can proceed to trial and execution. The Regent's jubilee is in the spring. They want the lists cleaned before the celebrations. They call it administrative hygiene." She heard her own voice go flat and careful, the voice she used for figures that would not reconcile. "And the rest is that if I file that report, your mother dies before the river thaws. And if I do not file it, I am guilty of obstruction, which in this province carries the same penalty as forgery. Treason, they call it now. They've widened the definition. It fits almost everything."

The lamp guttered and steadied. Jonah stared at her across the little room, and Astrid watched her do the same arithmetic Astrid had done, and saw the moment the girl arrived at the sum.

"So we are both already guilty," Jonah said. "You, for hesitating. Me, for carrying this." She touched the oilcloth bundle. "You realize what you've just done. You've confessed to a stranger."

"You forced the confession."

"I forced nothing. You could have called the wardens. This archive has a bell rope." Jonah tilted her head. "Why didn't you?"

It was a fair question, and Astrid had no prepared answer, and lying to this woman suddenly seemed both impossible and beside the point. She thought about the margin note she'd written at the foundry. She thought about the word *compositor* in her boot. She thought about her father, a customs clerk in Nordvik who had once falsified a manifest to save a smuggler's family from deportation, and who had taught her, without ever saying it aloud, that the law was a net and some fish deserved the knife that cut them free.

"Because a census is supposed to count people," she said finally. "Not erase them. I have spent six years writing down names so that the state knows who exists. Your mother exists. Every day I don't report her, the record I keep becomes a lie, and every day I do report her, the person I am becomes one. I chose the lie I could live with. That's all. Don't make it nobler than it is."

"It isn't noble," Jonah agreed. "It's a bargain. So let's bargain properly."

* * *

What they built in the next hour was not a plan—plans had steps and contingencies and signatures, and this had none of those. What they built was an understanding, assembled sentence by sentence, each woman testing the other's weight before committing her own.

"I delay the report," Astrid said. "I can honestly claim the Kessel rolls require further verification. The archivist will believe me; I'm known for thoroughness. That buys perhaps ten days, two weeks if I stretch it. Not more. After that, my supervisor comes down here himself, and he is not a man who reads margins."

"Ten days for what?"

"For your mother to have a legitimate reason to leave Kessel. Because I cannot forge her freedom—I don't have the skill, and even if I did, I won't hang for paper I made myself. But a transfer order countersigned, a medical commutation, a work assignment north—there are doors, and doors need keys, and keys need to look real." She leaned forward. "That is where you come in, and where I stop being able to help you."

Jonah frowned. "I don't understand."

"Your mother is forging passes from inside with smuggled type. Which means somewhere outside, someone is supplying her. Someone with access to a type foundry." Astrid let that land. "There are three foundries in Velmora capable of casting a body size that small. Two of them are Ministry-supervised. One of them belongs to a man named Marek Vasquez, who cut a private letterface twenty years ago that appears nowhere in any commercial catalogue, and whose daughter sorts sorts with hands that know lead the way priests' hands know their rosary."

"My father doesn't—" Jonah began, and stopped, because Astrid watched her think of the drawer, the hidden letter, the false death entry in a disguised hand, and the sentence died of its own evidence.

"Someone is feeding her type," Astrid said gently. "Maybe him. Maybe through him without his knowledge. Maybe—" she hesitated over the last possibility, having seen it in the docks district gossip that crossed her census rounds, "—maybe through your brother, and the men he keeps company with. I don't know. I'm telling you what I know and what I don't, because a bargain built on guesses is worth nothing."

Jonah was silent for a long time. Rain drummed the window. When she spoke, her voice had changed—it had dropped into something older and harder, and Astrid thought: *there she is, the compositor's daughter.*

"If I find out who's supplying her," Jonah said, "and if a berth or a pass or a door opens—what exactly are you offering?"

"I'm offering slowness," Astrid said. "Bureaucratic slowness. Misfiled forms. A verification that takes eleven days instead of five. A signature that waits. I can't give you your mother, Jonah. I can give you time, and I can fail to notice certain things. Beyond that—" she spread her hands, ink-stained, ordinary, "—you'd be working blind, and so would I, and neither of us can ever acknowledge the other did anything at all. If either of us is caught, the other was never here. Do you understand? Whatever happens, we were strangers. You came to correct a census error about your household's deceased members, and I helped you fill out a form, and you left."

"And if they arrest you and put you in a room with a lamp," Jonah said quietly, "you'll hold to that?"

Astrid considered the question honestly, because this woman deserved honesty even if the state got none. "I don't know," she said. "I've never been in a room with the lamp. Ask me afterward, if there is an afterward." She stood. Her knees ached; she was thirty-four years old and felt sixty. "Now go. Take that letter home, take it apart, burn the wrapper, memorize the sentence and eat the sprig of willow if you have to, but never bring it into a government building again."

Jonah rose more slowly. At the door to the archive floor she stopped, half-turned, and for a moment she looked so much like the woman in the one surviving sketch Astrid had seen in the old parish records—chin up, eyes level, a face built for asking questions—that Astrid's chest hurt.

"One more thing," Jonah said. "The letter says 'if she asks the right question.' I've been turning it over for days. I thought the right question was *is she alive*. But I asked that, and nothing happened." She held Astrid's gaze. "What's the right question, Census Taker?"

Astrid opened her mouth to say she didn't know.

And then, from the front room, the cracked bell above the archive door rang—twice, quick, the signal the night clerk used for official visitors—and heavy boots sounded on the chapel stones, and a voice Astrid knew, her supervisor's voice, flat and unhurried, called out into the incense-and-mildew dark:

"Lindqvist? Are you here? Bring the Kessel rolls. There's been a change of schedule—we sail for the prison tomorrow morning."
