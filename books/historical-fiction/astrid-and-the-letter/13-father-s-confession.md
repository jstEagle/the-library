## 13. Father's Confession

The crucible had gone cold three days ago and Marek had not relit it. He told himself there was nothing worth melting—no scrap lead worth the coal—but the truth sat in the corner of the shop under an oilcloth where he had put it the night the investigators came to the docks: a full case of his private face, the one he called Livia's alphabet, hidden in the shell of a broken hand-press that hadn't worked since before Tomas was born.

He was cleaning punches he did not need cleaning when Jonah came down the stairs.

He heard her before he saw her, the way he always had—the particular weight of her step on the third stair from the bottom, the one with the loose tread he'd never fixed because Livia used to trip on it and swear in two languages, and after she was gone he could not bring himself to silence her.

"Marek." Not *Papa*. Not *Father*. She stood in the doorway between the shop and the sorting room, and the lamp behind her threw her shadow long across the floor, over the cold crucible, up the leg of the workbench where he had spent forty years of his life.

"Jonah. It's late."

"It's not late. It's nine o'clock and you've been down here since supper pretending to work." She came into the light. Her face was pale and set, and he recognized the expression with a lurch of dread, because it was not his face and it was not Tomas's. It was Livia's when she had made up her mind about something and was only waiting for him to catch up.

"I have orders to fill," he said. "The Ministry's jubilee commission—"

"The Ministry's commission is finished. I packed it myself on Tuesday. You haven't touched the bench in three days except to move things around like a man rearranging furniture in a house he's about to burn down."

He set down the punch. His hands were not steady and he folded them to hide it, a trick so old he'd taught it to himself as an apprentice. "Then say what you've come to say."

She pulled something from inside her coat. A letter, soft at the edges, handled many times. He knew what it was before she laid it on the bench between them. He had known for eleven days, since he found the drawer of Livia's desk hanging crooked on its runners and understood that Jonah had been into it, and he had said nothing, and every day since he had told himself that saying nothing was a kind of mercy.

"You've read it," he said.

"One sentence. Dated 1844. In Mama's handwriting, with a sprig of river willow pressed inside like a flower in a hymnal." Jonah's voice stayed level the way a rope stays level over a drop. "I went to the stationer on Weavers' Lane. Old Semyon. He remembered her. He remembered her *orders*, Papa. Special paper. Watermarks. And then I went to Kessel."

The word landed in the cold room like a tool dropped on stone.

"There's a woman in the Women's House of Correction," Jonah said, "with grey in her hair and her left shoulder higher than her right from years at a composing stick, who walks the exercise yard with her head bent like she's reading a line of type on the flagstones. The guards call her the Widow Vasquez. They think it's funny, because everyone knows the Widow Vasquez is dead."

"Jonah—"

"*Is it her?*"

And here was the moment, Marek thought, that he had been rehearsing for three years without knowing it. All those nights lying awake listening to the river ice grind against the pilings, all those mornings setting type with hands that remembered another pair of hands, all that silence he had built like a wall around the family, stone by stone, no portraits, no petitions, no opinions, no names on any list—he had built it, he understood now, not to keep the Ministry out but to keep this conversation in, to keep it forever on the far side of a door he would never open.

Jonah had opened it. She was standing in the doorway of it now, waiting.

"Yes," he said.

* * *

He sat down. It was not a decision; his legs simply ended their argument with him. The stool creaked under him the way it had creaked under his father, under his grandfather. Three generations of Vasquez men had sat on this stool and cut steel and poured lead and believed, with the simple faith of craftsmen, that what they made was honest—that letters were letters, that a word set well was a small good thing in the world.

Livia had never believed that. That was the first thing he needed to tell his daughter, and the hardest.

"She started before we were married," he said. "You should know that. It wasn't desperation that turned her, or me, or Tomas. It was—" He searched for the word and hated how long it took him to find it. "It was appetite. An appetite for usefulness. She could copy any hand she ever saw twice. Any seal, any signature, any official's cramped little scrawl. When we were courting she showed me a letter she'd written in the Provost Marshal's own fist, inviting himself to dinner. I laughed until I couldn't breathe. I should have been afraid. I wasn't. I married her six weeks later."

"Papa—"

"Let me finish. Let me get through it in order, because if I don't do it in order I will not do it at all." He looked at his hands again. Ink under the nails, lead under the skin, the permanent grey crescents in the creases of his palms that no pumice had ever fully lifted. "For years it was small things. Passes for merchants whose papers had lapsed. A baptismal record corrected for a woman whose priest had died mid-entry. Then the purges came, and the small things stopped being small, and she—she couldn't stop. People came to her. Dissidents, mostly. Families. She forged them out of Velmora one document at a time, and I set the type for the false letterheads because I told myself that type was neutral, that lead doesn't know what it spells."

"But you knew."

"I knew everything." The confession came out of him now with its own momentum, like water finding a crack in a dam, and he let it, because stopping it had been the whole of his life for three years and he was so tired. "I knew about the network. I knew about the safe houses. I helped her hide the matrices in the walls of this shop while you children slept upstairs. And I told myself, every night, that we were careful enough. That we were invisible. Your mother was brilliant, Jonah, and brilliance makes you stupid in exactly one way: it makes you believe you are the exception."

* * *

"Tomas," Jonah said quietly.

Marek closed his eyes.

In the dark behind his eyelids he was back in the autumn of 1841, back in the worst hour of his life, and the memory had lost none of its heat. He made himself walk through it anyway, step by step, because Jonah deserved the whole architecture of his betrayal and not just its facade.

"Tomas was seventeen. You were twelve; you remember none of this, which I thanked God for then and thank Him for still. Tomas had fallen in with the river crowd—young men who ran leaflets, who carried messages, who thought revolution was a kind of weather coming and they wanted to be the first raindrops. One of them was arrested. A boy named Corvin, a dockhand, barely older than Tomas. And Corvin, in the cells, frightened out of his mind, gave the investigators a name. Not Tomas's name. Yours."

Jonah went very still.

"They had your name written in a ledger, girl. Twelve years old. Because Tomas had used you once as a courier—a bundle of pamphlets wrapped in a dressmaker's pattern, delivered to a seamstress on Bell Street. He thought it was clever. He thought using a child made it safe." Marek heard his own voice go flat and hard, and did not soften it. "I went to the Ministry myself. Before they came to us. I walked into the Office of Records with my hat in my hands like a tradesman petitioning for a license, and I offered them a trade."

"What trade?"

"I gave them Livia."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them the house settled, a beam ticking as the night cold worked into it, and the sound was unbearable in the silence.

"Not everything," he said. "Not the network—I didn't know all of it, thank God, she'd kept half of it from me by then, protecting me the way I was trying to protect the children. But I confirmed her. I confirmed the forgery work. I told them where the matrices were hidden, the ones I knew about. And I believed—I *believed*, Jonah, I staked my children's lives on believing—that they would give her a sentence. Two years, five years. The Regent's courts were still courts then, still fond of their own procedures, still proud of their mercy the way a fat man is proud of his charity. A sentence. She'd serve it. She'd come home. And your name would be struck from the ledger, and Tomas would be warned off forever by what happened to her, and we would pay for our sins with a few winters instead of a gallows."

"And they erased her instead."

"They erased her instead." He said it the way he might read a line off a gravestone. "No trial. No sentence. No register entry. I went to the parish to make sure her death was recorded properly—I wanted a funeral, Jonah, I wanted to mourn her like a human being—and the clerk looked at me with blank eyes and told me there was no Livia Vasquez on file. Never had been. As if thirty-one years of marriage had happened to a different man. They didn't kill her, you understand. Killing leaves a body. They *unmade* her. And then they came back to me, very polite, very quiet, and explained that my wife had died of a fever, that my grief did me credit, and that a man with two motherless children should be grateful for the state's discretion regarding certain irregularities in his household's past. Grateful. And discreet."

"So you buried her alive," Jonah said. "Twice."

"Yes."

* * *

She turned away from him then. She walked to the window—the one small window, high and barred, that faced the river—and stood looking out at nothing, at the dark where the ice groaned against the shore, and her shoulders did not shake, and somehow that was worse than weeping.

Marek stayed on the stool. He had earned the right to no comfort and he took none.

"Why?" she said finally, to the glass. "Why not tell us she was taken? Why the fever story? Did you think we wouldn't find out someday?"

"Because telling you made it real. Because a widow can be mourned and a prisoner must be freed, and I could not free her, and I could not live in a house with two children planning rescues that would end on the same scaffold as the people she'd saved." He rose, joints cracking, and crossed the floor to stand a few feet behind her, close enough to see her breath fog the pane. "Every day since, I have chosen the lie again. Every morning. It got easier, which is the most shameful part. Grief has an ending, Jonah. Shame doesn't—it just becomes the mortar between the bricks. I told myself I was keeping you safe. And some days that was even true. But most days I was keeping myself safe from your faces. From having to watch you learn what your father was willing to spend."

She was silent a long time. Downriver, faint as a heartbeat, the ice shifted—a long groan, building and releasing, the sound of a thing too heavy to hold itself much longer.

"Does Tomas know?" she asked.

"No. And it stays that way." He reached past her—not touching her, never presuming touch now—and tapped the window frame. "Tomas cannot know that I am the reason he grew up without a mother, because he is seventeen-and-twenty years old and angry, and angry young men with grievances join things. If he learns what I did, his grief will need somewhere to go, and the revolutionaries will be standing there with open arms and a printing press. Do you understand me? This stays between us. Not for my sake."

"For hers." Jonah turned. In the lamplight her eyes were wet and furious and clear. "You're thinking of her. Of what she'd want."

"I'm always thinking of what she'd want. That's the joke of it. That's the whole rotten joke of my life." He laughed, and it came out broken. "I betrayed her to protect you, and then I protected her secret to protect him, and I have spent three years being loyal to everyone in this family except the person standing in front of me. Until tonight."

"Until tonight," Jonah repeated. Something moved behind her eyes—calculation, the quick arithmetic he had watched her do over type cases since she was eight years old, fitting letters to lines. "Then answer me one more question, Papa, and answer it true, because everything depends on it. The spring ferry. The last crossing north. One berth, Ministry permit. Have you known about it?"

He hesitated. She caught the hesitation the way she caught a bad kern, instantly, unforgivingly.

"You've known," she breathed. "You've known for weeks and you said nothing. You were going to let her rot in Kessel rather than spend whatever it costs to buy that berth—because spending it means contact, means risk, means the wall comes down."

"I was going to keep you alive," he said. "All three of you. That was the whole of my strategy for three years and I confess I have run out of cleverness for it."

"Your strategy buried her twice and it nearly buried the truth with her." Jonah picked up the letter from the bench, folded it along its worn creases, and slid it back inside her coat, over her heart. "Well, it's down now. And here's what happens next: tomorrow I'm going to talk to the census taker. Astrid Lindqvist. She knows, Papa. Whatever else she is, she knows, and she hasn't reported it yet, and I mean to find out why."

"You'll hang us all."

"Or I'll save one of us." At the doorway she stopped, her hand on the jamb, and did not turn around. "One more thing. The case under the oilcloth. Behind the dead press. Livia's alphabet—your private face. I know it's there. So does whoever sold a fount of it to the pamphleteers, and so, very soon, will the Ministry. You should decide what you're going to tell them, Papa, before someone decides for you."

Her steps went up the stairs. The third tread from the bottom creaked, Livia's tread, and then the door closed above and Marek Pemberton stood alone in the cold shop among his life's work, listening to the ice groan down at the river like a man turning over in a bed too small for him, and understood that the wall he had built had held for three years and had come down in a single night—and that what came flooding through it was not ruin.

It was his daughter, walking toward Kessel with a dead woman's handwriting over her heart, and he had given her the only weapon he had left: the truth, eleven years too late, and one night ahead of the soldiers.

He banked the cold crucible out of habit. Then he took the oilcloth off the case, and began, with shaking hands, to sort the letters.
