## 14. Tomas's Gamble

The harbormaster's office smelled of tar and tallow, and of something else beneath it—the sour sweetness of money kept too long in a drawer. Tomas stood at the counter with his cap in his hands, watching Dornak weigh a coin that wasn't part of any transaction yet, just to feel its heft. The man had small hands for his bulk, quick ones. A man who counted everything twice.

"You're the Vasquez boy," Dornak said. Not a question. "Foundry family. Your father cut the type for the Ministry's jubilee proclamations."

"My father cuts type," Tomas said. "What he cuts it for is the Ministry's business."

Dornak smiled without warmth and set the coin down. Outside the salt-glazed window, the river lay under its grey shell of ice, and beyond it the ferry *Meridian* sat in her winter cradle like a patient animal waiting for spring. One crossing north before the ice broke. That was what everyone said now, in the markets, in the stairwells of tenements, in the low voices of men who met in back rooms. One crossing. Berths by permit only.

Tomas had spent four days deciding to come here, and every one of those days had cost him something he couldn't name. He thought of it as a debt accumulating interest.

"I need a berth," he said. "On the *Meridian*. For a woman traveling alone. Livia Soren. She'll have papers."

"Papers." Dornak turned the word over like a counterfeit coin. "Papers can be printed by anyone with a press these days. I've seen passes so beautiful they made me weep, and they were worth less than the paper."

"These are good."

"Good how?"

"Good enough that you won't want to look at them twice."

Something shifted in Dornak's face—not offense, but recognition. The look of a man hearing his own language spoken with an unfamiliar accent. He came around the counter slowly, joints cracking, and closed the shutters against the grey light. The office shrank to lamplight and the two of them.

"Sit," he said. "Tell me why a foundry son needs a berth for a woman who isn't his wife, his mother—" he paused, tasting it— "or is she? Is she your mother, Tomas Vasquez? Because I've read the census rolls. I read everything on this waterfront. And your mother died three years ago. Very tidy entry. Deceased 1841."

"The census is wrong."

"Of course it is. The census is wrong about everything except taxes." Dornak lowered himself into his chair, which groaned like a moored boat. "Here's my problem, boy. Berths aren't mine to sell outright. They're allocated by Ministry permit, and the permits come through my ledger, and my ledger gets audited by clerks who would sell their own mothers for a promotion. If I put a name on that manifest, I need that name to be unremarkable. Boring. The kind of name no clerk ever reads twice. What I don't need"—he leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the sweat on his upper lip—"is a name attached to a dead woman whose death was never entered in any parish register. Because that kind of discrepancy is exactly the sort of thing a bored clerk reads twice."

"She'll travel under another name."

"Which you will give me. In advance. Written out, so I can judge whether it bores me." Dornak steepled his small fingers. "And in exchange for this miracle—a berth on the last ferry, which forty people in this city would commit murder for—you offer me what? Beautiful papers I'm not supposed to examine?"

Tomas's mouth was dry. This was the moment he had rehearsed in the dark of his room while his sister slept one wall away, rehearsed until the words wore smooth. But rehearsal was nothing; rehearsal was a pamphlet, and this was the press itself, the iron weight of it coming down.

"I can offer you information," he said.

"Everyone offers me information. Half of it's true and all of it's expensive."

"Not this kind." He heard his own voice drop, and hated how natural the drop felt, how easily the betrayal slid into the shape of a whisper. "Names. Addresses. The cell that's been printing the broadsheets—the jubilee exposé, the ones on the stolen type. I know where they meet. I know who carries what between them."

The silence went on long enough that Tomas could hear the ice on the river, a far-off groan like a giant turning in sleep.

"And why," Dornak said softly, "would a man sell his comrades for a ferry berth?"

"They're not my comrades."

"But they think they are." Dornak smiled again, and this time there was real pleasure in it, the pleasure of a collector acquiring a rare piece. "That's the best kind of merchandise. Loyalty that doesn't know it's been priced." He opened a drawer, took out a sheet of good rag paper and a pencil sharpened to a needle point. "Write. Names first. Then addresses. Then the routes they use—where the type moves, where the ink comes from. And then your woman's travel name, last, at the bottom, so I remember it was the least important thing you gave me tonight."

Tomas picked up the pencil. His hand did not shake, and he wished it would, because a shaking hand might have stopped him.

* * *

He wrote six names.

Not nine. The cell had nine members, counting himself, and he told himself the arithmetic as he wrote, told himself it like a prayer: not nine. Six. He left off the printer, old Halvard, who was half-crippled and knew nothing beyond his own composing stick. He left off Marta, who had a nursing infant. He left off—his pencil hesitated above the paper—he left off the sixth slot entirely, inventing instead a fictional courier named Brenner, plausible enough to check out for a week and hollow enough to collapse when anyone pressed on him. A decoy built of nothing. A lifeboat made of smoke.

Three names saved out of six given. He watched the figures dry into the paper and felt the specific nausea of a man doing evil sums correctly.

"You're hesitating," Dornak observed.

"Remembering if I've missed anyone."

"People always miss someone. It's what makes lists interesting." Dornak took the paper, read it once, and did something worse than smiling: he filed it, unhurried, in a drawer that already held too many papers exactly like it. "Now. The woman. Her travel name."

"Soren. Livia Soren. Widow, seamstress, going north to a cousin in Torshavn." Tomas recited it flat, the biography he and Jonah had built between them over two nights of whispered argument—Jonah wanting to include details, Tomas insisting on blandness. Widow, seamstress, cousin. Grey as river water. Nothing to read twice.

"Livia Soren," Dornak repeated, and wrote it in the margin of his working ledger with the same pencil, in handwriting so neat it looked machine-made. "One berth. Steerage. She boards at the second bell on the morning of the crossing, not before, and she boards with nothing but her own two hands—no trunk, no bundle, nothing that makes a clerk ask questions. You understand? The most invisible passenger on the river."

"And the manifest—"

"Will show Livia Soren, permit number 4471, issued under the general allocation. Which is the pile no one audits because auditing it means work." Dornak closed the ledger. Then he looked up, and his voice changed, lost its transactional smoothness, became almost gentle, which was the most frightening thing in the room. "A word of advice from an old man who has sold things before. Whatever name you crossed out of your heart when you were writing that list—whatever you think you protected—it won't hold. Lists get copied. Lists get compared. Lists breed. You gave me six names, boy, and within a month those six names will be twelve, because everyone I sell them to will add their own knowledge to the pile. That's what information does. It breeds. So whatever you're telling yourself right now about the three you left off—that they're safe, that you were clever—I'd stop telling yourself that. It's kinder."

Tomas stood. His legs worked, which surprised him. "You'll keep her name quiet."

"I'll keep everything quiet right up until keeping it quiet costs more than selling it." Dornak reopened the drawer, dropped in a single coin—the fare, returned—and pushed it across the desk. "No charge. Consider it a gift. Men who pay for their betrayals resent the purchase. Men who receive them as gifts come back."

* * *

The walk home took him past the print shop on Weavers' Lane, and he could not make himself cross the street.

Light leaked through the shutter cracks, and inside he could hear the sounds he knew better than his father's voice: the creak of the press arm, the soft slap of the tympan, Halvard's cough between pulls. They were printing tonight. Of course they were printing tonight—the jubilee was eleven days away, and the new broadsheet was set and locked in the chase, and somewhere in that room was a page proof with his own corrections penciled in the margins, his shorthand, his habits, his hand. Evidence with his fingerprints on it, sitting in a room he had just sold.

He stood in the dark across the lane and did the sums again, because the sums were the only thing holding him upright. Six names. Three spared by omission, one invented. But Dornak's voice followed him down the street like a dog: *lists breed.* And it was true, and he had known it was true when he wrote, and he had written anyway, because the alternative was his mother spending the rest of her life washing prison linen in Kessel while the ice came and went and came again, year after year, until there was no mother left to save.

He had told himself it was different from what his father did. Marek Vasquez had reported his wife to protect his son, and had lived seven years inside that choice like a man walled up alive. Tomas had sworn—had actually sworn, drunk, once, to Jonah, laughing—that he would never make his father's mistake.

And here he stood outside a print shop, having made it larger. His father had traded one person for one person. Tomas had traded six for one, and dressed it up in arithmetic, and called the leftover names mercy.

The light in the shop went out. Footsteps, muffled voices, the scrape of the type cabinet being wheeled against the wall—hiding the type, their nightly ritual, sliding their father's stolen letterforms into the dark like children hiding sweets from a headmistress. He heard Rell's laugh, high and young. Rell, who was nineteen, whose name was third on the list. Rell, who had taught Tomas the courier routes and trusted him with the ink key.

Tomas walked home the long way, along the embankment, where the ice groaned in long slow syllables and the whole river sounded like it was reading aloud something terrible.

* * *

Two days later, the cell noticed.

It began small, the way rot begins—invisible, olfactory. Rell mentioned, casually, over the glue pot, that a Ministry clerk had asked questions at the chandler's on Cordwainer Street, questions about deliveries. The chandler was not on any list Tomas had given. But the chandler's shop was on the route, and routes were attached to names, and names bred. Then Marta reported that a man had watched her building for an hour on Tuesday and left without entering—an hour, exactly the duration of a shift change, exactly the patience of a paid watcher rather than an accidental loiterer.

And on the fourth evening, after the press was covered and the others gone, Halvard did not leave. The old printer sat down across the workbench from Tomas, folded his ruined hands, and looked at him with eyes that had spent fifty years reading type upside down and backward and could therefore read anything.

"You've been somewhere," Halvard said. "Lately. Four nights running. You come in late, and you smell of the harbor."

"Tobacco and tar. Half this city smells of the harbor."

"Mm." Halvard turned a quoins idly in his fingers, click, click, click. "When I was a young compositor, in the Regent's father's time, there was a man in our guild who informed on a strike. Nobody proved it. Nobody ever proves it. But we all knew, the way you know a line is set wrong before you read it—the spacing tells you. Something in the rhythm goes off." Click, click. "Your rhythm's gone off, Tomas."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, in the tenements, a baby cried and was hushed.

"If you have something to say to me," Tomas said, "say it."

"I have nothing to say. If I had proof, I wouldn't be talking—I'd be moving the type." Halvard set the quoins down and rose, joints crackling, and at the door he paused with his hand on the frame, his back still turned. "But I'll tell you what I told that guild, thirty years ago, when they asked me what should be done with a man we all knew was rotten. I said: watch what he protects. A traitor's always protecting something. Find what it is, and you'll find his price—and maybe you'll find you'd have paid it too."

The door closed behind him. Tomas stood alone in the dark among the cases, in the smell of ink and oil, surrounded by his father's stolen letterforms—every character a small leaden ghost—and understood that he had perhaps a week, perhaps less, before Halvard decided the answer to his question, and moved the type, and moved himself, and took the whole cell into the dark with him.

He also understood something else, standing there, and it arrived with the flat certainty of a pulled proof: the raid was coming. Whether through Dornak's bred and multiplied lists, or through some other channel he'd never know, soldiers would come to Weavers' Lane with axes and lanterns. Soon. Days. Maybe tomorrow.

And his mother's name sat in a harbormaster's ledger, one line among hundreds, *Livia Soren, permit 4471*, safe only as long as no clerk read it twice—safe only as long as the world stayed boring.

It was not going to stay boring.

He blew out the lamp and stood in the black, listening to the river ice groan, and began—for the first time since he'd taken the pencil in Dornak's office—to plan how to warn them.
