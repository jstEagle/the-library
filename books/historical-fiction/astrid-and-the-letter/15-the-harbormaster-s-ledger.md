## 15. The Harbormaster's Ledger

The Harbormaster's Office smelled of tar and old paper, and Jonah hated how much it reminded her of home.

She had told herself she would only look. That was the story she'd rehearsed on the walk down from the foundry, past the customs sheds with their new Ministry placards, past the chained bollards where the ferry *Vigilant* lay groaning against the ice like an animal that knew what spring meant. She would present herself as a clerk—her father's clerk, which was even true—and ask to verify a shipping entry for the type consignment the foundry was supposedly sending north with the spring ferry. A plausible lie, built out of true materials, the way her mother must have built all her documents.

Her mother. Three weeks since the letter in the drawer, and Jonah still could not hold the thought for more than a few seconds at a time without it sliding through her fingers like wet lead.

The office was a long room above the customs shed, its windows fogged with river damp, lit by oil lamps that made everything the color of weak tea. A clerk with ink-stained cuffs sat behind a rail, copying entries into a duplicate ledger. Two soldiers stood by the door in the bored posture of men assigned to watch paper. On the wall hung the ferry schedule under glass: *SPRING CROSSING — LAST DEPARTURE BEFORE ICE BREAK — BY MINISTRY PERMIT ONLY.*

One departure. One crossing. And somewhere in that room, a ledger with names in it, and one of those names was going north.

"State your business," said the clerk without looking up.

"Vasquez. From the type foundry on Candlemakers' Row." Jonah kept her voice flat, unhurried—the voice her father used with Ministry men, a voice designed to be forgotten before the sentence ended. "My father sent me regarding a freight entry. Type matrices and cases, consigned north. He wants confirmation the weight classification is correct before he pays the handling fee."

That did it. Money was the one language every clerk respected more than the Ministry. The man sighed, set down his pen, and hauled a ledger from beneath the rail—a fat volume bound in green leather, its corners rubbed to bare board. He flipped through pages of columns with the weary fluency of long habit.

"Vasquez. Vasquez. Foundry freight." His finger stopped. "Here. Fourteen hundredweight, cases and crates, entered under permit 77-K."

"And the passenger berths," Jonah said. It came out before she could stop it, too quick, and she covered it by reaching into her satchel for her father's freight note. "He'll want to know who's traveling with his freight. If there are passengers aboard, the handling rules differ."

The clerk looked at her for the first time. He had the face of a man who had spent thirty years learning not to notice things, and he noticed her noticing anyway. But the soldiers were by the door, and the fee was real, and suspicion was effort. He turned the ledger around on the rail so she could see the passenger page, tapped one line with a blunt fingernail, and went back to his copying.

*Last crossing, northbound. Berths allocated: eleven.*

Jonah read them the way she read proofs—fast, then again slowly, letting each line settle into place. Ministry officials, two names with titles after them. A physician. A widow with an escort allowance. A merchant of grain, a merchant of hides. Each name followed by its permit number, its authorization code, the little architecture of permission that the regime had built over the whole river like a bridge toll.

And then, sixth from the top:

*Berth 6: Marta Havel, seamstress. Permit 81-F. Authorized: Office of the Warden, Kessel House of Correction — conditional release.*

Kessel.

The word went through her like a nail through wet wood. She kept her eyes moving down the column—*Marta Havel, seamstress*—and made herself breathe. A conditional release from the Women's House of Correction, authorized by the warden's own office, three days ago. It was exactly the kind of document that existed in the world because someone with a graver's patience and a criminal's courage had made it exist.

It was exactly the kind of document her mother made.

"I'll need the freight note countersigned," the clerk said. "Leave it and collect it tomorrow."

"Tomorrow," Jonah agreed, and slid the note across the rail, and walked out of the office on legs that felt borrowed.

* *

She did not go straight home. She went to the embankment below the customs sheds, where the ice along the shore had begun to rot into gray plates, and stood looking at the water until her hands stopped shaking.

*Marta Havel.* She turned the name over. It was a good name—plain, common, the kind of name that dissolved into the crowd of a hundred other Havels between Velmora and the coast. Seamstress was a good trade; no one questioned a woman with a needle case, and needle cases held things. Permit 81-F fit the numbering sequence she'd seen on the other lines. The warden's office authorization was bold—audacious—but audacity was camouflage too. No investigator suspected forgery inside the prison's own paperwork, because to suspect it was to admit the prison leaked, and no warden would thank you for the admission.

Every choice in that single ledger line was right. Every one. Jonah had spent three weeks teaching herself, in stolen hours, the rudiments of her mother's craft from the letter's paper stock and the memory of Livia's hands guiding hers over a pen when she was nine years old. She knew now what a perfect forgery cost. It cost everything. It cost the kind of sustained, invisible labor that most people could not give to their own children.

And her mother had done it from inside a cell. With smuggled tools. With whatever passed for ink in the Women's House of Correction, and whatever passed for time in a life measured out in wardens' rounds.

Bitterness and awe arrived together, twins, indistinguishable. Because if Livia could forge her way onto the manifest—if she had already done it—then the berth was spoken for. Then Tomas's gamble with the harbormaster, whatever it had bought or half-bought, had bought nothing. Then the arithmetic Jonah had been dreading for a week—one berth, four names, four people the regime wanted for one reason or another—had just been solved without her, by a woman she had mourned for three years, working quietly in the dark while her family tore itself apart trying to save her.

Her mother had never needed saving. That was the bitter core of it. Her mother had needed only a door left open, and here was the door, and she was walking through it alone.

Unless.

Jonah pressed her palms against the freezing iron rail and made herself think like a proofreader, because grief made bad copy and she could not afford bad copy now. *Conditional release.* Not a pardon—a release. Which meant that somewhere in Kessel, a record showed Marta Havel walking out the gate, and somewhere else, the record showed Livia Vasquez still locked in. The documents were true in Velmora and false in Kessel simultaneously, and that was the flaw. Forgery could make a woman exist on a ship's manifest, but it could not make her exist on the ferry dock unless the prison let her out, and the prison would not, because the prison believed no such release existed.

Unless Astrid Lindqvist's report moved first. Unless the census taker whose conscience had been bending for weeks bent far enough to carry a false confirmation across the river herself.

Jonah pushed off the rail. She had a letter in her satchel bearing her mother's handwriting, dated three years after her death, and she had carried it for three weeks like a coal in her chest, and she had promised Astrid nothing except mutual risk. But promises between watchful women were contracts, and contracts had clauses, and it was time to read this one aloud.

* *

Astrid was not at her lodgings. Jonah learned that from a landlady who answered the door with flour on her hands and suspicion in every line of her face, and who relented only when Jonah produced her father's business card and the magic word *census*. So Jonah waited, in the narrow street where the snow lay gray as cinders, watching the windows of the lodging house, and it was nearly an hour before Astrid came around the corner with her document case under her arm and her collar up, moving with the purposeful stride of a woman who had somewhere to be.

"Astrid Lindqvist." Jonah stepped out of the doorway. In the gray light they faced each other—two women on a cold street, both of them fluent in the language of official papers, both of them now criminals in the eyes of the same state.

"Frau Vasquez." Astrid's eyes flicked once toward the windows, once down the street. Habit. "This is not a good place for—"

"The manifest," Jonah said quietly. "Berth six. *Marta Havel, seamstress.*"

Something moved behind Astrid's eyes—not surprise, exactly. Confirmation. She glanced at the landlady's window, took Jonah's sleeve, and drew her into the recessed doorway of the chandler's shop next door, out of the wind, out of sightlines.

"You've seen the ledger," Astrid said. Not a question.

"My father's freight is on that boat. I had cause to check the passenger list." Jonah held her gaze. "You knew."

"I confirmed a name in the Kessel rolls ten days ago. Livia Vasquez, prisoner, sentence commuted to conditional release, effective the twenty-eighth." Astrid's voice was low and level, each word placed like type into a stick. "I signed the confirmation myself. My superior countersigned it. As far as the Ministry is concerned, Marta Havel exists, her release is legitimate, and she will board the *Vigilant* on the twenty-ninth with a warden's escort to the dock."

"And after that?"

"After that, Frau Vasquez, the record ends." For the first time something human flickered across the census taker's face—fatigue, or fear, or the particular loneliness of a person who has crossed a line and can see no one else has crossed it yet. "After the gangplank goes up, Marta Havel is a free woman in the north, and Livia Vasquez remains a closed file in Kessel. No one will look for a dead woman who has officially been released under another name. The system does not audit its own mercies. It barely audits anything else."

Jonah leaned back against the chandler's door. The relief hit her sideways, unexpected and almost unbearable—that her mother had an ally, that the machinery had oiled itself, that somewhere in the frozen dark a cell door would open in three days and a woman with her father's jaw and her own stubborn hands would walk out into the light of a world that believed she had died in 1841.

Then the rest of the arithmetic caught up, the way it always did.

"One berth," Jonah said. "And my brother's name is in a copied list that reaches the harbormaster's desk by tomorrow night. And my father—" She stopped, because saying Marek's danger aloud made it heavier, and she needed her voice steady for what came next. "Astrid. I need to see that ledger again. Tonight, if possible. Tomorrow morning at the latest."

"Why?" Astrid asked, and it was not suspicion in her voice but dread, because she was intelligent enough to have done the same arithmetic, and intelligent people dread sums that come out wrong. "The berth is taken. Your mother is accounted for. What is left to inspect?"

"That's just it." Jonah pulled her glove off, flexing her stiff fingers, and heard herself say the thing she had been circling since the embankment. "I saw the handwriting. Six inches away, under lamplight, while the clerk wasn't looking. The entry for berth six—*Marta Havel, seamstress, permit 81-F.*" She paused. "I grew up over a type case. I learned letters from a woman whose hand I'd know anywhere, alive or dead, in any alphabet, in any disguise. The slant of the *a*, the way the crossbar on the *t* hooks left instead of right, the little hesitation in the descender of the *p*—she presses too hard on downstrokes, always did, you can feel it in the ink."

Astrid had gone very still.

"It's her hand," Jonah said. "Of course it's her hand. Who else could have written it? She forged her own release, her own permit, her own name—she did all of it from her cell and she did it perfectly, and I stood there in that office feeling proud of her and sick about it at the same time." Her voice cracked and she hated it and kept going. "But Astrid—the *name*. Marta Havel. I keep thinking about why she chose it. My mother thinks in systems. She wouldn't pick a random name. She picks names with doors in them."

"What do you mean, doors?"

"Marta Havel." Jonah watched the words form in the cold air. "There was a Marta Havel who taught music two streets from our foundry until '39. There was a Havel family that ran a dye works on the north bank before the nationalization—they left, emigrated legally, years ago, clean records, clean exit papers. If anyone ever pulls the thread, the name unravels into a dozen real Havels, all of them verifiable, none of them reachable. It's not just a mask. It's a maze." She shook her head slowly. "She's been planning this for years. Maybe from the day they took her. Maybe longer. And we spent three weeks tearing ourselves apart—Tomas selling secrets, my father confessing his betrayal, me trading letters with a census taker in doorways—to save a woman who had already saved herself."

Astrid was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere down the embankment the ice shifted, a long groaning sigh that traveled through the ground and up through their boots, the sound of a river deciding it was finished with winter.

"There's something you're not telling me," Astrid said at last. "You didn't come find me to admire your mother's craftsmanship."

"No." Jonah met her eyes. "I came because I re-read the ledger entry on the walk here, in my head, the way you re-read a proof, and there's a discrepancy I can't put down. Eleven berths allocated. I counted the names twice. There were only ten lines filled." She watched Astrid's face change. "One berth is empty, Astrid. Unassigned. Sitting in that book like a hole in the page. Either the harbormaster hasn't allocated it yet—which means Tomas's bargain bought something after all—or someone removed a name tonight, between the copy I saw and the copy that will be presented at boarding."

Astrid's hand tightened on her document case until the leather creaked.

"If a name was struck," she said slowly, "it was struck by whoever controls the ledger. And the only person with authority to strike a name from the final manifest is the harbormaster himself." She turned her head sharply toward the harbor road, toward the lights of the customs shed, and Jonah saw the fear arrive fully now, no longer disguised. "Or by someone who paid him. And if your brother's contact list is already on that man's desk, Frau Vasquez, then he knows exactly who is desperate enough to buy a berth, and exactly how much they'll pay, and exactly how much it will cost him to sell the same berth twice."

Above the customs sheds, the lamp in the harbormaster's office window still burned. It was very late for a clerk to be working.

"He's amending the book tonight," Jonah said.

"Then we need to see what he writes before the ink dries."
