## 16. One Berth, Four Names

The arithmetic was simple, and that was what made it unbearable.

One berth. Four names. I sat at the kitchen table after midnight with a scrap of paper and a stub of pencil—the same table where we had eaten supper as children, where Mother had once taught Tomas to carve his letters into beeswax—and I did the sum over and over, as if the numbers might tire of being themselves.

*One.* The ferry *Kestrel* sailed north on the morning tide three days hence, the last crossing before the ice broke for good. After that, the river would be a white road no keel could follow until spring, and spring belonged to the new regime and its lists.

*Four.* Jonah Vasquez. Tomas Vasquez. Marek Vasquez. Livia Vasquez.

I wrote them out. Four lines in my own hand, my father's letterforms living somewhere under my skin even when I wasn't thinking of them. Then I drew a single line through one name, and the pencil lead snapped, and I sat there in the dark listening to the house settle around me like something breathing.

The stove had gone cold hours ago. Father had gone to bed without eating, which he never did; Tomas hadn't come home at all, which lately he always didn't; and I kept the lamp unlit because light in the window at this hour was the kind of thing Astrid Lindqvist would notice, and Astrid noticed everything.

I heard her knock anyway. Three soft raps, spaced like punctuation. She had a way of knocking that made you understand she already knew you were awake.

I opened the door to snow and lamplight. She stood on our step with her coat collar up and her census ledger nowhere in sight—she'd left it behind deliberately, I realized later, because a census taker carrying her ledger at midnight was an officer of the Ministry, but Astrid without it was only a woman standing in falling snow, asking to be let in.

"You should be asleep," she said.

"So should you."

"Yes." She stamped the snow from her boots and came past me into the dark kitchen like she'd been there a hundred times, which she hadn't, though it felt suddenly like she had. She looked at the scrap of paper on the table, at the four names, at the broken pencil. She didn't touch any of it. "How many times have you done that sum tonight?"

"I've stopped counting."

"It's still four against one," she said. "It will still be four against one tomorrow. Sit down, Jonah."

I sat. She took the chair opposite, the one that had been Mother's, and I watched her not flinch from it, and loved her a little for that, and hated her a little too.

"The raid comes at dawn," she said quietly. "The print shop on Candlemakers' Row. The one where your brother has been spending his nights."

The cold went through me faster than the open door ever could. "You're certain?"

"I wrote the requisition myself." Her hands were flat on the table, steady, but I had learned to read her steadiness the way sailors read water—what looked calm was often the deepest current. "Twelve soldiers, two investigators, a warrant signed by Undersecretary Havel. They're expecting pamphlets and plates. What they'll find depends on whether Tomas is there."

"He'll be there. He sleeps there now more than here."

"Then his name is in the copied list," Astrid said, "and by noon tomorrow he'll be in a cell, and by the following week—" She stopped herself, which was its own kind of mercy. We both knew what happened to printers who didn't hang: they went north, or they went into the ground, and the regime had grown efficient about choosing between them.

"Can you warn him?"

"I've been warning him for a month," she said, "in the only language he'll hear—that his type is recognizable, that his father's letterforms are a signature, that pride is a scent hounds can follow. He hears nothing. He's twenty-two and he's found something worth dying for, and I remember being twenty-two." Her eyes came up to mine. "But this isn't why I came."

"No?"

"I came because of the manifest." She turned her hand over and produced a folded paper—not official stock, I noticed, not watermarked, the kind of paper you buy loose from a stationer and carry against your skin. "The berth on the *Kestrel*. It's been confirmed in the harbormaster's ledger under the name Vess Halvard. And the handwriting on the confirmation—" she laid it flat between us, and even by the grey light leaking through the window I knew it before my breath finished catching— "is your mother's."

Livia's letters. That forward-leaning *L*, the *a* with its small stubborn tail, the way the downstrokes thickened just slightly at their ends, as if each word leaned toward whoever would read it next. I had spent three years mourning those letters and three weeks learning to forge them, and here they were, alive on an official document, escaping Kessel prison without my help at all.

"She never stopped working," I said. My voice came out strange, half admiration, half grief.

"No. She's been forging passes from inside since autumn. Smuggled type, smuggled ink—I suspect she's been teaching other women to do it, which means when she goes, the whole network goes dark, and the Ministry will finally understand what they lost." Astrid tapped the paper once. "Here is what matters. The berth is real. The pass will hold—for exactly one person, at exactly one moment, at the transfer on Friday morning. One body crosses that gangplank. The guards check faces against the description in the dossier: female, mid-forties, brown hair going grey, a scar on the left wrist from a press accident."

My own left wrist ached sympathetically. I had inherited that scar's story without inheriting the scar itself; Mother used to say the press had taken a bite of her so she'd never forget whose hands these were.

"One berth," I said. "Four names. And now five, if you count the woman who has to walk past the guards wearing that description."

"I don't count myself," Astrid said. "I count consequences. Listen to me." She leaned forward, and the lampless room seemed to lean with her. "Tomorrow at dawn they take Tomas. If he resists—and he will resist—they take him hard. Your father will be questioned within the week; the foundry's commissions are all the pretext they need. You are the daughter of a convicted forger who is currently impersonating a dead woman to steal a government berth. Every one of you is already in the fire. The only question the arithmetic asks is: who walks out of it?"

"And the answer is one."

"The answer is one," she agreed. "And you have three days to decide which name gets written, and two of those names can't choose for themselves."

"Tomas won't leave. He'd give the berth back rather than take it—he's said as much about everything he believes in."

"Then Tomas has chosen already. Men like him always have." Something moved across her face, quick as a fish under ice. "Your father, then."

"Father will claim the forgery himself before he lets me be arrested for it." I said it and heard it land in the room like a dropped weight. "He's been rehearsing his confession for twenty years. He just didn't know what it would be about."

Astrid was quiet a long moment. Outside, the snow ticked against the shutters, and far off—so far off it might have been imagination, or memory, or the earth itself shifting under the city—the river groaned. A long, deep sound, like a giant turning over in sleep, like a door too heavy for its frame.

"The ice is starting," she said softly. "Three days. Maybe less, if the thaw comes early. Everything after the break belongs to them."

"Then tell me what you'd do." I surprised myself asking. I had spent weeks circling this woman, watching her, measuring her silences, and here I was handing her the knife and asking her to cut. "If it were your family."

For the first time since I'd known her, Astrid Lindqvist looked tired. Not weary—tired, the plain animal exhaustion of someone carrying more than her shoulders were built for. "I'd stop doing sums," she said. "Sums are how governments think. One life weighed against another, entered neatly in a column. But families aren't ledgers, Jonah. You don't get to balance us. You only get to choose, and live inside the choosing, and every morning afterward wake up knowing the shape of what you gave away." She rose, buttoning her coat. "Choose the person who can't survive staying. That's the only mercy arithmetic ever offers."

At the door she paused, snow swirling past her shoulder into the hall.

"Dawn," she said. "Candlemakers' Row. If you're going to say goodbye to your brother, say it tonight."

* * *

I found Tomas at the print shop at two in the morning, and he was laughing.

That's what I carried home with me afterward, more than anything else—the sound of it. He sat on a crate beside the press with three other young people I didn't know, ink to his elbows, a broadsheet drying in sheets behind him, and when I slipped in through the door he'd left unbolted (of course he'd left it unbolted; caution was Father's religion, not his), he laughed and held out his blackened hands like a conjurer showing empty palms.

"Sister! Come to see real work done?" The shop smelled of solvent and cold iron and the particular sourness of cheap revolutionary ink. "Look at this. Look." He pulled a proof from the stack and held it to the candle. The jubilee exposé, set bold and beautiful, the headline marching across the top in letterforms I knew better than my own heartbeat—Father's private face, the one he'd cut for Mother the year they married, the one that was supposed to exist nowhere.

"Tomas. You have to leave this place. Tonight."

His laugh died by degrees, the way laughter does when it meets something colder than joke. "Who told you?"

"Astrid."

"The census woman." He shook his head, but slower now. "She works for them, Jonah."

"She's risking her neck for you right now, and she doesn't even like you." I grabbed his sleeve, ink soaking into my fingers, marking me. "Soldiers come at dawn. Twelve of them. Warrant signed. Your name is on a list that got copied when you bought your berth, and every hour you stand in this room is an hour closer to a cell, and Tomas—" my voice cracked and I hated it— "Tomas, there's one berth on the ferry north. One. And I need you alive to argue with me about who deserves it, because I can't do the arithmetic alone."

He went very still. Around us the others had stopped talking; the press stood silent as a headstone; the candle guttered and righted itself.

"One berth," he repeated. "For Mama."

"For whoever we choose."

"And the others?"

"The others stay."

He looked at his hands then, at the ink worked so deep into the creases it would never fully wash out, and I understood that he was looking at the rest of his life. "You know I can't run," he said. Not defiantly. Almost apologetically, the way he'd apologized at nine years old for breaking things, holding the pieces out to Father. "These people trust me. These plates—if I'm gone, they seize everything, everyone. Marta has a child. Old Semyon printed under the last Regent and they never caught him; they'll catch him Tuesday because of me."

"Then burn the type. Melt it. You grew up in a foundry—you know how fast lead forgets its shape."

Something flickered in his face at that. Lead forgetting its shape. Our whole childhood in four words: the crucible, the moulds, Father pouring bright silver threads of metal while we watched and learned that nothing made by hand is permanent except the making.

"I'll melt what I can," he said at last. "The plates go into the crucible tonight. The rest—" he glanced at the press, and I saw that he loved it, this stolen, borrowed, magnificent machine, loved it the way Father loved type and Mother loved her forged lives, helplessly and fatally— "the rest stays. Someone has to be here when they kick the door, or they'll take it out on whoever's left in the neighborhood. Fear needs feeding, Jonah. I'd rather feed it myself than watch it eat Marta."

"That's not bravery, it's arithmetic," I said bitterly. "You're doing the sums too."

"Then we're Father's children after all." He smiled, and it was Mother's smile, crooked and warm and aimed slightly past you, at some horizon only he could see. He hugged me then, ink and all, and pressed something into my pocket—small, heavy, warm from his hand. "In case," he whispered. "Don't look till you're home."

* * *

I walked home through the falling snow past shuttered houses and dead streetlamps, and the river spoke to me the whole way.

Not words. Groans, mostly—a vast slow conversation of ice against ice, pressure finding weakness, the winter's whole architecture beginning to fail from beneath. By the bridge I stopped and listened, hands jammed in my pockets around whatever Tomas had given me, and I thought: *this is what the country sounds like. This is what it sounds like when something enormous decides, all at once, that it can no longer hold its own weight.*

At home I lit the lamp for the first time that night. Father was awake—I knew it by the silence, by the careful quality of nothing moving upstairs—and I sat at the kitchen table and opened my hand.

A piece of type. Single character, lead, worn at the edges from handling. Not from Father's cases; I knew every font in the foundry by touch. This was cruder, cast in a hurry, in some cellar, from a matrix improvised by someone who understood letters the way birds understand flight.

The letter **A**.

Mother's initial. Or Astrid's. Or the first letter of *allons*, of *away*, of *alive*—or simply the first letter of the alphabet, the beginning of everything, handed to me by my brother in a freezing print shop because he believed in beginnings.

I set it on the table beside the scrap of paper with its four crossed-out names, and above me I heard Father's floorboards creak once, twice, and go quiet again, and outside the dawn was coming up grey over the rooftops of Candlemakers' Row, where twelve soldiers were checking their weapons and my brother was feeding a crucible with everything he'd built.

One berth. Four names. Three days.

And underneath all of it, faint but unmistakable, rising through the floor of the world: the sound of the ice, beginning to break.
