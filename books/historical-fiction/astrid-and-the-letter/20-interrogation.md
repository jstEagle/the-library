## 20. Interrogation

The room they put me in had once been a counting house. I knew it by the brass rail bolted along the desk's edge, the kind clerks used to hang their ledgers from, and by the smell of old ink soaked so deep into the floorboards that no amount of scrubbing would ever lift it. Someone had tried, though. The boards near the door were pale, freshly planed, as if a new broom had swept through and wanted no history underfoot.

They sat me in a chair with a straight back and made me wait.

Waiting is a craft too. My father taught me that without ever meaning to—how to stand at the casting bench for six hours while lead cooled, how to let the silence do its work rather than rushing to fill it. So I waited. I counted the cracks in the ceiling plaster. I listened to the building settle around me, the river muttering somewhere below, ice still thick on its back though the nights were softening. Somewhere beyond these walls Livia Vasquez was on the water, moving north with my name folded into her pocket like a prayer she hadn't earned yet and I had given anyway.

I held onto that. When fear came—and it came, it filled the room like cold air off the river—I pressed my tongue against the thought of her until it hurt, and the fear loosened its grip.

The door opened an hour after dark. The man who entered was not a soldier. Soldiers were simple; you could read them like a broadsheet printed in haste. This one was a clerk of some elevated species: gray coat, gray eyes, gray voice, carrying a single folder that he laid on the desk with the tenderness of a man handling eggs.

"Jonah Vasquez." He said my name the way Astrid had said it, weeks ago, in our own kitchen—but where Astrid had worn her questions lightly, this man wore his like a uniform under his uniform.

"Yes."

"You were observed at the Kessel transfer point on the morning of the fourteenth. You will tell me what you observed."

"I went to see the prisoners brought out," I said. "It was permitted. Anyone could stand at the wall."

"Anyone could stand at the wall." He repeated it without inflection, which was somehow worse than if he'd sneered. He opened the folder. "You are listed in the harbor registry as employed. Type compositor, Vasquez Foundry, Ropewalk Lane."

"My family's foundry. Yes."

"Then you can read typefaces."

"I can *set* them," I said. "Reading them is nothing. A child can read them."

Something moved behind the gray eyes—not warmth, but the flicker of a man recalibrating a machine he'd assumed was simpler than it proved. He turned the folder around and slid a sheet across the desk toward me.

It was the ferry manifest. The final crossing. Our crossing—or hers, now. I looked at the line I knew was coming before my eyes reached it, and when they reached it I let myself look puzzled, because puzzlement was the only performance I'd rehearsed.

"The berth allocated under permit number forty-one," the clerk said. "Assigned to a passenger recorded as *Vera Halden*. Do you know a Vera Halden?"

"No."

"The handwriting on the manifest entry has been examined." He tapped the page. "Three examiners. They agree it matches samples in the Ministry archive. Samples attributed to Livia Vasquez, deceased 1841."

The room tilted very slightly, the way a boat tilts when a passenger shifts her weight. I kept my hands flat on my knees.

"That would be remarkable," I said. "A dead woman writing manifests."

"It would be forgery."

"It would be *impossible*," I said, and heard my mother's cadence come out of my mouth, the dry precision she'd used over her matrices, and hated myself for how much of her lived in my voice. "Dead women don't write. That's the whole trouble with dead women, sir—you can ask them nothing and accuse them of everything."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he closed the folder, squared it against the desk edge, and said, almost gently, "Your father is being brought here."

* * *

They didn't put us together. I heard him before I saw him—heard the specific weight of his footsteps in the corridor, the gait I'd known since childhood, heavier on the left since the year the crucible cracked and threw metal across the shop. Then the door opened and there he was, flanked by two soldiers, and I understood in one glance that whatever they had taken from him already, they had taken it from the place where he kept himself.

Marek Vasquez had ink under his fingernails the way other men had calluses; it never fully washed out, a permanent dusk beneath the nails of both thumbs. His hands were clean now. Scrubbed. And that frightened me more than the soldiers did, because my father's hands were never clean, and a man stripped of his dirt is a man stripped of his trade.

They sat him across from me. The clerk stood between us like a hinge.

"Marek Vasquez. Proprietor of the foundry on Ropewalk Lane. Commissioned cutter for the Ministry of Information—" the gray voice paused delicately, "—uncredited."

My father said nothing. He was looking at me, and there was something in his face I had spent my whole life failing to find there, because he kept it behind the same discipline he kept everything: an expression I could only call *witnessing*. As if he were memorizing me. As if he already knew the price of the next thing he intended to say, and was spending the time before saying it looking at what he loved.

"The manifest," the clerk prompted. "Permit forty-one. Vera Halden. Your daughter was seen speaking with a prisoner at the Kessel transfer. She was seen pressing something into the prisoner's hand. She was then observed leaving the transfer point alone, weeping."

"She cries easily," my father said. First words, flat as a slug of lead. "Always has. Her mother was the same."

"The manifest entry—"

"—was mine."

The word dropped into the room and rang there.

I turned to stare at him. He didn't look at me. He looked at the clerk, and his face had arranged itself into the expression he wore when a customer complained about a delivery: patient, faintly bored, immovable as a type case bolted to its bench.

"The entry is mine," he said again. "Vera Halden. It's a name I use for proofs. Any compositor keeps proof names—names that mean nothing, so no real person is troubled by seeing their name misprinted." He recited it smoothly, and I realized with a vertiginous lurch that it wasn't smooth at all—it was *rehearsed*, and I recognized the rehearsal because I'd grown up watching him rehearse answers to census takers, tax assessors, Ministry men. Thirty years of invisibility, and every one of those years had been practice for this hour. "I wrote it. If your three examiners found my wife's letterforms in it, they found what they should have expected to find. I learned to cut letters holding her matrices. Her hand is in mine. It always was."

"That is a romantic explanation," the clerk said. "Forgery carries particular penalties."

"So does treason. So does sedition. So does harboring." My father spread his clean hands on the desk, and I saw the tremor in them—the first tremor, the only one—and watched him master it the way he mastered metal, by applying exactly enough heat and no more. "You want someone to pay for permit forty-one. Take me. But understand what you're taking. You're not taking a forger. Forgers work in paper. You're taking a type founder. Everything I have cut for thirty years—every face, every matrix, every punch in my cabinets—is evidence of the same skill. If my hand can make a manifest, it can make anything. Passports. Deeds. Pardons." He let that last word sit in the air between them. "So take all of it. The foundry, the cases, the punches, the molds. Confiscate the lot. And take me with it, because a man who can forge a pardon shouldn't be left standing at a bench."

"*Father.*"

He still wouldn't look at me. That was the mercy of it, and the cruelty. He talked over my voice as if I were a customer's child making noise near the molten pot—something to be managed, not answered.

"The girl knows nothing," he said. "I kept her ignorant deliberately. Ask your census woman—Lindqvist—she'll confirm the household ran silent. No opinions, no portraits, no petitions. I built that silence brick by brick for twenty years. It will hold. It's the only thing I've ever built that worked."

The clerk studied him the way I'd once watched him study a flawed casting: turning it, weighing it, deciding whether the flaw ran deep or sat on the surface.

"And you'll confess to the forgery. In writing."

"In writing," my father said, "in my own hand. Which your examiners can compare to the manifest. They'll match. I told you—they should match. Her letters live in mine."

There was a silence. The river spoke into it, far below, a long groaning complaint of ice remembering that it was temporary.

"Sign here," the clerk said, and produced a pen.

And that was when I finally understood the shape of the transaction happening across the desk, and understood it the way you understand drowning—too completely, too late. He was not confessing to save me from a charge. Charges could be survived; Tomas had survived arrest; I would have survived questioning, because they had nothing on me but grief and proximity. No. He was doing something larger and more terrible. He was giving them the foundry *on purpose*, handing over thirty years of craft with both hands, because he had understood before any of us what the new regime feared most—not pamphlets, not lists, not revolution, but the *skill itself*, the human hand that could make paper say anything. He was removing himself and his art from the world in a single stroke, buying my freedom with the only currency he had ever valued.

My father, who had taught me that lead remembers every hand that touches it. My father, who slept with a punch in his nightstand drawer the way other men slept with knives. Giving it up. All of it.

"Don't," I said. My voice cracked down the middle like bad type. "Papa. Don't you dare sign that."

"Jonah." Now he looked at me. Only now, when it was done—when the pen was already moving, when the ink was already biting into the paper the way ink bites into everything, permanently, irreversibly. He looked at me and his eyes were wet and absolutely steady, and he said, quietly, just for me, in the voice he used at the bench when he taught me to hold a graver: "*Steady hands. You know the letters better than I ever did. That's worth more than the shop.*"

He signed.

The soldiers took his elbows. As they walked him past me he turned his head, and for one moment—one breath—I smelled the ghost of coal smoke and cooling metal that clung to him even scrubbed, even here, and he said nothing at all, because everything had already been said in the signing.

The door closed. The clerk gathered his folder. At the threshold he paused and regarded me with something that was not quite respect and not quite pity.

"You're released," he said. "The premises on Ropewalk Lane are forfeit to the Ministry, effective immediately. You may collect personal effects within two days. Nothing metallic leaves the building." A pause, thin as a hairline crack in a matrix. "Nothing that casts."

Then he was gone, and I was alone in the old counting house with the smell of dead ink and the sound of the river, and I sat in the straight-backed chair for a long time without moving, because moving felt like agreeing.

Two days. Nothing that casts.

But they had searched the foundry twice already, and searched it as clerks search—looking for papers, for weapons, for seditious print. They had catalogued the type cases and melted nothing, confiscated the punches and understood nothing, because to them metal was metal and cabinets were cabinets.

They did not know about the drawer under the casting bench. They did not know that somewhere in the ash of my mother's burned sketches—if it had survived the confiscation, if my father's confession had bought me two days inside those walls—there might be one small piece of steel, cut in a dead woman's living hand, that could make every letter she'd ever written speak again.

I stood up. My legs held. Outside, the last light was going off the river, and the ice was groaning, long and low, like something enormous turning over in its sleep—like a press being prepared, somewhere in the world, ready to take an impression.

I started walking toward Ropewalk Lane.
