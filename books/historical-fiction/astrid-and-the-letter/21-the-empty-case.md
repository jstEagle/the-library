## 21. The Empty Case

They came for the foundry on a Tuesday, in the gray hour before the shops opened, when the river fog still hung in the streets like something unwashed.

Jonah stood on the pavement across from her father's door and watched four men in Ministry coats carry out the type cases two at a time, their boots ringing on the cobbles, their breath steaming. They handled the cases carelessly—not with malice, but with the worse indifference of men moving furniture—and she heard, each time a case knocked against the doorframe, a small dry sound like teeth chattering. A thousand letters shifting in their compartments. Her whole childhood, rattled loose.

Marek stood beside her. He had not slept; the interrogation had ended only at dawn, and there was still ink in the creases of his knuckles that no amount of washing would take, because it was not ink anymore but something older, worked into the skin itself, thirty years of it. He watched his life carried out into a cart and did not say a word until the last case was loaded, and then he said, very quietly, as if to himself:

"They're taking them out of order. The lowercases first."

And Jonah understood that this was how he mourned: precisely, technically, in terms of arrangement and order, because grief without craft had no shape he could hold.

The confiscation notice went up on the foundry door, pasted over the window where the sample sheets used to hang—*By order of the Ministry of Information, the premises and contents thereof being forfeit under Article—* The rest was legal boilerplate, set in some other shop's type, some inferior face with clumsy serifs and a mean little ampersand. That, more than anything, made her throat close. They had sealed her family's door with bad typography.

Tomas came down the street at a half-run, coat collar up, still carrying the particular hollowness of a man who has been released rather than freed. He stopped when he saw the cart. He looked at it for a long time.

"Everything?" he asked.

"Everything," Jonah said. "Except what they couldn't see."

* * *

What they couldn't see amounted to three things.

The first was the letter, which rode against Jonah's ribs in an oilcloth wrapper, day and night, through the interrogation and after—the letter in her mother's hand dated 1844, three years past death, with its single sentence and its pressed sprig of river willow gone brown and brittle as a moth's wing. She would not describe even to Tomas where she kept it. He didn't ask.

The second was the knowledge, loose in both their heads now like sparks in a rafter: Livia Vasquez alive, north of the ice, free since the morning the river broke with a sound like artillery. No letter had come. No letter could come. But Astrid Lindqvist had falsified the transfer record at the cost of her own career and probably her liberty, and somewhere between Kessel and the northern crossing a woman with a forged name had stepped onto the last ferry, and that fact was a lamp Jonah carried whether or not it ever lit anything again.

The third thing was in the ashes behind the foundry, and it took them nine days to find it.

* * *

The Ministry let them keep the house above the shop—they had not, in the end, wanted the Vasquezes visible enough to make martyrs of—but the yard behind belonged to the state now, or to nobody, which in Velmora amounted to the same thing. It was there, against the wall by the coal shed, that Marek had burned Livia's sketches, years ago, the night after she was taken. Jonah remembered the light of it through her bedroom curtain, orange and wrong, and her father standing over the basin until long past midnight, feeding the fire sheet by sheet, so slowly that she understood even then, half-asleep, that he was making himself do it.

Nine days after the confiscation, Jonah took a trowel and a sieve from the garden and began to go through those ashes.

Tomas found her there on the second morning, kneeling in the cold with black dust to her elbows, sifting the ground like a woman panning for gold in a river of night. He stood over her a while. Then he fetched the second sieve from the kitchen and knelt down beside her, and neither of them said anything about it, because saying something would have made it strange, and it was not strange. It was the most natural work either of them had done in months.

Their father came out on the fourth day. He stood at the edge of the ash patch with his hands in his pockets, watching them, and his face did something complicated that ended in nothing.

"You won't find paper," he said. "I burned it hot. I burned it twice."

"I'm not looking for paper," Jonah said.

He looked at her then, really looked, and whatever he saw in her face made him turn away and go back inside, and that evening he came out with a third sieve, and a soft brush from the composing room that should have gone north with the rest of the type, and he knelt in the ashes between his children, an old man with ruined knees who groaned getting down and did not complain once, and sifted.

They worked through a week of thin sun and one day of rain that turned the ash patch to a black lagoon and drove them all to the window to wait, staring out at it like sailors becalmed. They found nails. They found the fused remains of a button. They found, on the sixth day, the corner of a sketch—one inch of paper, heat-hardened, showing three lines of a capital letter drawn in Livia's sure hand, and Marek held that scrap in his palm the way other men hold a relic, and did not speak, and put it inside his waistcoat, against his heart, where Jonah pretended not to see it.

On the ninth day, Tomas found the matrix.

* * *

It was wedged in the foundation crack where the coal shed met the wall, driven deep and covered with a smear of old mortar that someone—Livia, all those years ago, or Marek after her—had applied to make the crack look natural. Tomas worked it loose with a chisel, swearing softly, and brought it into the lamplight, and the three of them crowded around it and were silent.

A printing matrix. A piece of copper, the size of a thumbnail, struck from a punch and bearing in intaglio the negative of a single letter: a lowercase *a*, in a face none of them had ever seen cast.

But Jonah knew the hand.

Not the letterform—though the letterform was beautiful, a double-story *a* with a generous bowl and a finial that curled at the top like something listening—but the *hand*. She knew it from the letter in her oilcloth wrapping, from the census pages of her childhood, from the grocery lists and the marginal notes in her mother's prayer book. Her mother had cut this punch herself. Had engraved it, by hand, in steel, and struck this matrix from it, and hidden it in the wall of her own home within reach of her own kitchen, the way another woman might hide a ring.

"She was cutting type," Marek said. His voice had gone strange, reverent and broken at once. "Here. In my house. While I slept."

"Not stolen," Jonah said. "Not sold. Hers." She turned the matrix in the light. "She made her own face, Father. And she hid it six feet from where we ate dinner."

"Because your father burned everything else," Tomas said, not cruelly, just as fact, the way he'd learned to say hard things during the weeks in custody. "Because everything she made, you destroyed. So she kept one letter where you'd never think to look—in the house, in plain ground—because she knew you better than anyone alive."

Marek sat down slowly on the coal-shed step. For a moment he looked very old, and then he looked younger than Jonah had ever seen him, the way men do when something long frozen in them finally cracks.

"It's good," he said, examining the matrix with a loupe he produced from nowhere, from thirty years of habit. His thumb trembled slightly against the copper. "God forgive me, it's *good*. The counter is—" He stopped. Took a breath. "Your mother had a better eye than I ever told her."

"You never told her anything," Tomas said.

"No," Marek agreed quietly. "That was my great work. Thirty years of silence, and I executed every piece of it myself."

* * *

They could not cast from the matrix. Casting required metal, a pot, a mold—all of it gone north in a Ministry cart, and buying any of it now would put their names on lists they had spent their lives avoiding. But Jonah discovered, in the nights that followed, that a matrix is not only a tool. It is a proof. Pressed into wax, into soft bread dough, into the damp clay Tomas brought home from the brickyard wrapped in his coat, the little copper gave up its image perfectly, and the letter appeared among them again and again, the way a face appears in dreams.

Livia's *a*. In every kitchen in exile, in every prison cell, in every forged pass and true record, somewhere in the world that letter was being printed right now—on the ferry manifests she'd written herself, perhaps, or on pamphlets Tomas's comrades had scattered before the raid, or in the ledger of some northern town where a woman with a false name was starting a new page. You could burn a woman's sketches. You could empty her cases, seal her door with bad type, erase her name from registers and parish rolls and jubilee commemorations alike. But a single letter, hidden in a wall, survives its own alphabet.

On the twelfth day, the Ministry posted new notices. On the thirteenth, snow fell and filled the ash patch white, and they stopped digging, because there was nothing left to find, and because Jonah understood at last that what they'd been digging for wasn't in the ground.

* * *

It was Tomas who answered the knock, on the last evening of the month, and it was Jonah who saw his face change, and came to the door behind him.

The woman on the step was thinner than the census taker who had once lingered too long over a question about the dead. Her coat was unlined. Her hair had gone from careful to merely clean. There was a bruise-yellow fading at her jaw that she did not attempt to hide, and she stood in the falling snow with the posture of someone who has been interrogated enough times to know exactly how she appears, and has decided not to perform anything anymore.

"Astrid," Jonah said.

"I've been dismissed," Astrid Lindqvist said. "Formally, for irregularities in the transfer records. Informally, they suspect much more, and they are building a case at the speed governments build things, which is to say slowly, which is to say I have perhaps a week before they stop building and start taking." She said it flatly, a report read aloud, and then something moved under the flatness—something almost like embarrassment. "I had nowhere else to be honest. I'm told this household keeps a strict inventory of its silences. I'd like to add one."

Tomas looked at Jonah. Jonah looked at the snow gathering on Astrid's shoulders, at the woman who had sat in their kitchen drinking tea and circling them like a heron circles water, who had bent her conscience a degree at a time until it broke clean through the ice, who had falsified a record while the river detonated behind her and sent a condemned woman north into freedom.

Behind them, in the warm room, on the table, the copper matrix lay in the lamplight with its small dark letter facing upward.

"We were just sorting the ashes," Jonah said, and stood aside to let her in.
