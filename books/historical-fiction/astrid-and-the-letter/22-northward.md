## 22. Northward

The cold in Havnvik came off the water differently than it had in Velmora. In the south, the river's cold had been a held breath—still, patient, waiting under ice for its season. Here the sea breathed out. Wind came through the gaps in the boardwalk and the chinks of the timber houses carrying salt and fish-rot and the cries of gulls that never slept, and Jonah had learned, over one full year of it, to hear that wind as something other than exile. It was the sound of a place where no one checked papers at the door of your own life.

She was sorting type when the letter came.

That was how she measured her days now—in sorts and cases, in the small iron music of the composing stick. The press she and Tomas ran sat in the back room of a former cooper's shop on Havnvik's one crooked street, its stone floor stained with brine where barrels had aged for sixty years before anyone thought to put ink in their place. The press itself had been bought with money Tomas refused to explain, and Jonah had learned not to ask; her brother carried his debts the way their father had carried his shame, close and silent and always in motion.

"Jonah." Tomas stood in the doorway, snow in his dark hair, a bundle of post held out in one hand. "Something from the south."

Her hands stopped of their own accord, a compositor's reflex—the instinct to keep the line true, broken by what could not be composed. Letters from the south were rare and dangerous things. They passed through three postal jurisdictions, two censors' offices, and any number of indifferent hands, and every one of them arrived already read, sometimes resealed with such carelessness that the wax told its whole story. A letter from Velmora was not correspondence. It was evidence.

But this letter was not from Velmora. The postmark, faded and half-smudged, read *Kessel*—the river town downriver of the prison—and the hand on the front stopped Jonah's breath before she even broke the seal.

She knew that handwriting better than she knew her own face.

*To Miss J. Vasquez, presswoman, Havnvik, Northmark Territory.* The letters were upright, unhurried, each one cut clean as if set in lead. Her mother's hand. Her mother's letterforms, the ones Jonah had spent a winter learning stroke by stroke so that a name on a manifest might pass for truth. She had forged her mother once. Here was the original, crossing the sea to find her.

"I'll leave you," Tomas said, but she caught his sleeve.

"No. Read it with me. It's yours too."

He hesitated—he had been hesitating more lately, as if the year had sanded something down in him—and then he pulled up the second stool beside the stove while Jonah slit the envelope along its fold with the tip of a bodkin, the way you opened anything that mattered without tearing what was inside.

There were two enclosures. The first was a pressed sprig of river willow, gray-green and brittle, gone silver at the edges. Jonah did not have to ask which river. The second was a single sheet, written close and economical, in that same upright hand.

---

*My children,*

*I write this from a place with a garden. You will understand what that means better than most: I have spent four years in rooms without windows, so I will not apologize for lingering on the window now, or the garden, or the fact that the frost comes late here off the water and the beans were still standing when I arrived.*

*Your father met me at the dock. We said everything and nothing, which I suspect is how it will be between us for some years, and I find I am content with that. Some marriages are rebuilt like presses—one screw at a time, testing each joint before bearing weight. He is cutting a new face, he tells me, uncommissioned, for no one. He says he will send you proofs. Keep him to that promise; it is the only vanity left him and I intend to let him keep it.*

*Tomas—I know what you gave. Do not tell me it was arithmetic. I raised you; I know the difference between a calculation and a sacrifice, and you have never once in your life mistaken the two. Whatever the cell believes of you, whatever you believe of yourself, I set my hand to this page to say it plainly: you bought my life, and I am spending it carefully, and that is not a debt unpaid. It is an investment, and I expect returns. Print the truth, son. Make it worth what it cost.*

*Jonah. You are the one who remembers the letters. That is what I told you on the dock at Kessel, and you looked at me as though I had given you a sentence instead of a blessing. Let me say it again, plainer, because I have time now to be plain. When they took my hand from me—all the passes, all the names, everything I made go true—I believed the work died with my freedom. Then a manifest crossed my desk with my own letterforms on it, spelled in my own style, and I understood that my hand had survived me. It was in you the whole time. Everything I built, you carry. Set your lines straight, daughter. There is no truer work.*

*Astrid Lindqvist was released in the spring amnesty. The last I heard, she had gone north. If she has not yet found her way to your door, she will. Give her work. She is better at keeping watch than either of you will ever be, and she repays trust the way good paper repays ink—cleanly, completely, without bleeding.*

*The garden needs weeding, so I will end. I am alive. I am well. I am, for the first time in nine years, exactly where I say I am.*

*Your mother,
Livia*

---

Jonah read it twice. The second time aloud, because Tomas asked, and because saying the words made them heavier and more real than lead. By the end her voice had gone rough and Tomas was looking hard at the stove door, jaw working, doing the thing men in their family did instead of weeping—which was nothing at all, very loudly.

"She expects returns," he managed at last.

"Then we'd best show a profit."

* * *

Astrid came on a Tuesday, in the hour before dusk, when the light through the harbor fog turned the color of whey.

Jonah was locking the case of ten-point bodoni they used for headings when the bell above the shop door rang—a bell Tomas had salvaged from a decommissioned ferry, a joke neither of them explained to customers. She looked up expecting the fish-factor's clerk, come again about the price lists, and saw instead a woman standing just inside the threshold, shaking rain off a traveling coat that had been good once and had traveled very far since.

Astrid Lindqvist was thinner than Jonah remembered. The census-taker's neatness remained—it lived in the way she stood, square to the room, hands visible, taking inventory by old habit—but the Ministry grey had been scoured out of her and replaced by something rawer. A scar, new, along her hairline. Eyes that catalogued the exits before they found Jonah's face.

"You've moved the press," she said. "The cooper's shop. It suits you."

"You've walked a long way to critique our real estate."

"I've walked a long way," Astrid agreed, "to do worse things than that."

Tomas came through from the back with a cudgel in his fist and lowered it slowly when he saw who stood dripping on the boards. None of them moved for a moment. In Velmora the sight of Astrid Lindqvist had meant ledgers and lists, the state arriving politely at your door with questions shaped like keys. Here she was only a woman in a wet coat, and somehow that was harder to know what to do with.

"The amnesty didn't hold?" Jonah asked at last.

"It held. I was released. I was even thanked." Astrid's mouth curved without warmth. "I was offered a position in the new Ministry of Records. The revolution kept the files, you see—they always keep the files—and someone competent must maintain them. I declined. I had spent six years learning what happens to people who are well-maintained." She undid the top button of her coat, thought better of mid-motion, and let her hands fall. "I wrote to your mother. She suggested I come north."

"Mother writes to everyone," Tomas said, "as if postage were free."

"Postage is never free. That is rather the whole of what we all learned." Astrid looked around the shop properly now, and Jonah watched her do it: the two cases of type, the battered Washington press, the drying rack hung with the week's run—a plain-sheeted record titled *The Havnvik Account*, four pages, printing what the southern papers would not: the confiscated foundries listed by name, the arrested printers by name, the manifest discrepancy at Kessel dock reconstructed from testimony smuggled out in fish barrels. Watching Astrid read the hanging pages, Jonah felt the particular exposure of a printer whose work is being read by someone who once hunted printers.

"The record is accurate," Astrid said finally. "The dates, the ship movements, the name of the harbormaster who copied the list. I checked against what I know." A pause. "You should know that my testimony is in the southern archives. Under my own name. I confirmed Livia Vasquez's identity in the Kessel rolls; I falsified the transfer record; I engineered the technicality that freed your brother. All of it stands written somewhere, in a file, in a building, maintained by someone competent."

"We know," Jonah said. "It's in the Account. Page two. We printed it months ago."

Astrid went still. "You printed my confession."

"We printed the record. You're in it. So are we. So is Father, God help him." Jonah set down her bodkin. "You can't print true records and leave out the parts that cost you. Otherwise it's not memory, it's advertising."

For the first time, the rawness in Astrid's face cracked into something else—relief, maybe, or its exhausted cousin. "Your mother said you would make me work for it."

"Mother overestimates me."

"On the contrary. Your mother does not overestimate anyone. It's why they needed four years to catch her, and only then because she permitted it." Astrid squared herself, the census-taker once more, presenting herself for enumeration. One name, one trade, one household. "I'm asking for work. I can set type passably. I can keep accounts precisely. And I know—" the pause here cost her something; Jonah watched her pay it "—I know how the other side thinks. If ever a Ministry man walks through that door, I'll recognize him before he finishes his first polite question."

"He won't finish it," Tomas said flatly.

"Tomas."

"He sold us the berth and the cell nearly hanged him for it, and he wants to hire the woman who—" But whatever Tomas had been building, it collapsed under its own weight, and he stood there holding the ruins of it. A year of pamphlets and proof sheets had not taught him how to set this line. "She delayed her report," he said finally, to the floor. "She delayed it long enough. I know that. I've known it for—" He exhaled. "Can you start Monday?"

"I can start now," said Astrid. "It's still raining."

Jonah looked from one to the other. Then she pulled the third stool from under the counter—the one kept for customers, worn smooth at the edges—and set it by the stove.

"Sit down before you drip on the slugs," she said. "We're behind on next week's Account, and you're going to check our figures."

* * *

They worked past dark, the three of them, the stove throwing orange light across the cases. Astrid proved better than passable: her fingers found the letters quickly, and she held the composing stick the way she must once have held her pen, level and exact. Around ten o'clock she paused over the galley of the coming issue, at the blank space at the head of the first column where the masthead of a ledger ought to stand.

"This town has no register," she observed. "No civil record. Births, deaths, land, debts—Havnvik keeps all of it in the harbor-master's head, and he drinks."

"The harbormasters of the world have been the ruin of this family," Jonah said. "Present company's harbormaster included."

"Then someone should cut a new set of faces and rule a proper book. A town ledger. Something with weight to it, that a clerk in the south couldn't alter with a wet thumb." Astrid said it lightly, but Jonah heard the design underneath: a record that could not be quietly corrected. A list that would keep faith.

She thought of her father, cutting faces for ministries that erased his name from his own work. She thought of her mother, whose hand had made false documents true enough to save lives, and true documents false enough to survive a regime. She thought of the matrix in her pocket—she carried it everywhere now, a small wedge of copper, the single letter her mother had cut and hidden in the ashes of her burned sketches: an *A*, in Livia's own forms, the first letter of Astrid's name and of the alphabet and, if you believed the shape of it, of almost everything else.

"Tomas," Jonah said. "How much type metal have we got in the scrap pot?"

"Enough for a title face, if we're not proud of it."

"I've never been proud of anything in my life except the opposite of that."

They cast until midnight. It was crude work, a heavy slab-serif with the proportions slightly wrong in the way of things made by amateurs who love them, and when the sorts had cooled and been dressed and rubbed down, Jonah locked up the first line of the new ledger in the chase and pulled a proof on the good paper they saved for anything that mattered.

The press spoke once, that deep wooden syllable it gave, and fell quiet.

She lifted the sheet and held it to the lamp. The ink was black and complete and it would not wash out.

**HAVNVIK TOWN LEDGER — Being a True and Permanent Record of the Persons and Events of This Place, Begun This Day — Entry the First:**

Below it, ruled and waiting, the empty columns. Room for births. Room for deaths. Room, Jonah thought, for everything the southern archives claimed to hold and lied about. She dipped the pen, and behind her she heard Astrid's stick clicking softly through tomorrow's news, and Tomas humming at the scrap pot, and outside the sea breathing its slow breath against the shore.

She wrote the date. She wrote the town. Then, in the entry line, in her steadiest hand, she set down the first fact of the new record—the one everything else would stand on, the way a house stands on its lowest stone:

*Livia Vasquez, typefounder, of Velmora — living.*

The lamp guttered, steadied, and burned on.
