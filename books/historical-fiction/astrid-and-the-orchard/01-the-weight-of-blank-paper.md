## 1. The Weight of Blank Paper

There is a particular cruelty in finishing a map: the coastline is done, and the country it belongs to no longer exists.

I did not know that yet, on the last morning of October, when I dipped my pen and drew the final stroke of the Breton shore. I only knew that Master Aubertin had been dead for eleven days and eleven nights, that his chair still held the shape of him the way a riverbed holds the shape of water after the water has gone, and that the atlas he had spent forty-one years building was now, at long last, one coastline short of finished.

The ink was iron-gall, mixed fresh because the old batch had gone to jelly. Iron-gall smells of vinegar and rust, of something being eaten slowly — which is what ink does to paper, given enough years. Every map I have ever made has been quietly devouring itself from the moment of its completion. I find this honest. Most things pretend otherwise.

"Coastline of Brittany," I said aloud to the empty workshop, because the master had always insisted one speak one's labels as one wrote them, to catch errors the eye forgave. "Scale, one to four hundred thousand. Soundings corrected per the surveys of seventeen hundred eighty-eight."

No error. The line was true. Forty miles of granite coast, every headland in its place, every shoal marked with the little dotted teeth that mean *here be shipwrecks* without having to say so rudely.

I set down the pen and flexed my hand, which ached in the shape of the nib, and I looked at the atlas open before me on the great table, and I felt — nothing. Or rather, I felt the specific nothing that follows three days without sleep, which is not nothing at all but everything pressed flat, like flowers in a ledger.

*Dominic,* you will say, if you have read this far and are inclined to sympathy, *you must have felt pride.*

Pride. Yes. Somewhere under the flatness there was a small hot coal of it, and I poked it once, experimentally, and then covered it back up with ash, because pride is a luxury for people whose names appear in their own books.

Which brings me to the dedication.

* * *

Master Aubertin had written his dedication in the spring, when his cough was still something the apothecary could shrug at rather than something we both pretended not to hear. He had dictated it to me over supper — bread, hard cheese, and a bottle of the red wine he claimed was for cooking but never once touched a pan — and I had copied it out fair while he paced the length of the workshop, hands clasped behind his back like a general reviewing troops made entirely of paper.

*"To those who came before,"* it ran, *"whose footsteps I have followed; and to those who come after, whose footsteps I shall not hear."*

And then, below, in the space where a man names his patron, or his king, or God:

*"To D—— Delacroix, apprentice of this house, who drew more of these coasts than I shall ever admit."*

He would not tell me what the dash stood for. He said it was a surprise. He said it with the particular twinkle he reserved for withholding things, which was most of his twinkles, and I went to bed that night genuinely curious about the letter that would complete me.

Eleven days ago, I took a razor blade to the dedication page.

Not all of it. I am not a butcher. I lifted the ink of the name itself — *D—— Delacroix* — with careful scraping and a poultice of bread crumb and lemon, the old forger's trick, until the vellum was blank again, and then I burnished it smooth and wrote above the empty space, in an imitation of Aubertin's hand good enough to fool anyone who had not spent six years studying it from the inside: *To the craft, and to all who serve her.*

A dedication to a trade. Safe as a locked door with nothing behind it.

Why, you ask. Why erase myself from the only book that ever wanted me in it.

Because "Delacroix" is not my name, for one thing. Because the real Dominic Delacroix — the nephew Master Aubertin invented on a Tuesday afternoon in 1788, with the same calm he brought to drawing coastlines — exists nowhere but on four sheets of paper and in the habits of a town too polite to look closely at an apprentice with ink-stained fingers and a jaw a trifle soft for a boy. Because a dedication survives. Maps get copied; dedications get copied along with them; and somewhere, someday, some clerk with a revolutionary list and a slow afternoon might read *D—— Delacroix, apprentice of this house*, and go looking for an apprentice who could not answer to that name without answering for everything underneath it.

Maps keep history at arm's length. That is their whole genius and their whole lie. A map says: *here is the world, fixed, known, safe to hold.* A map does not say: *here is a girl who ran away from Lyon with a bruise shaped like a belt buckle and a name she has not spoken aloud in five years.* You can put anything on a map except yourself. That is why I love them. That is why I do not entirely trust them. Both, always, in the same breath, the way you can love a person and know exactly how they will fail you.

* * *

The workshop smelled of him still. That is the cruelty nobody warns you about: not the silence, which you expect, but the smell. Pipe smoke soaked into the beams over forty-one years. The linseed oil he rubbed into the table every Lent. And under it all, faintly, disgracefully, the sour-milk scent of the camphor chest where he kept his best vellum, because camphor keeps off the mice and he had never quite believed I could keep off them myself without supervision.

I worked through the morning with the shutters open to the grey light, because he had hated working by lamplight — *"a map drawn by lamplight lies about itself"* — and there remained the small offices of completion. The atlas was not one book but a gathering of loose plates, sixty-two of them, each awaiting its final collation. I checked each against the index in his own hand. Plate thirty-one, the Loire estuary, had a smudge where his elbow had come down in a fit of coughing two winters ago; I cleaned what I could and left the rest, because a map should carry the mark of the body that made it, even if the body's owner would have sworn at the mark with considerable fluency.

Plate fifty-four was blank.

This stopped me. It should not have — I knew plate fifty-four, knew it the way you know a missing tooth. Vireloup and its valley. The town I sat in. Forty-one years of atlas, and the town he lived in was the one he had never managed to draw. He used to say it was because local knowledge corrupted scale. He used to say a great many things with a straight face while meaning their opposites.

I think the truth was simpler. I think you cannot draw the place where you are dying, because the pencil keeps wanting to draw the death instead.

So I sat down and drew Vireloup. The bridge, first — the old stone bridge with its seven arches, though only six show above water in summer. Then the church, the millrace, the orchard rows climbing the south bank in their patient geometry, the road north that goes to Paris if you walk it long enough and to anywhere else if you don't. I gave the orchards their proper symbols, little circled trees in ranks, and I confess I drew them more lovingly than a surveyor strictly ought. Someone had fed me from those orchards. But that is a matter for later pages, and I am getting ahead of my own narrative, which is the one vice cartography has never cured me of.

By midday the plate was done and I laid it in its place, and the atlas was whole, and I closed it, and the closing made a sound like a door.

Then I did what any sensible person does upon finishing a life's work: I made tea and avoided looking at it.

* * *

It was while the tea steeped that I heard the boots.

Not one pair. Not the scattered clogs of market-day. Boots, plural, rhythmic, coming across the bridge in step with one another, which no one in Vireloup had done in the entire time I had lived here, because Vireloup's relationship to marching was the same as its relationship to hurry generally: theoretically possible, practically unobserved.

I went to the window. Down on the bridge, grey-coated men were walking in formation, muskets slung, and a man on a grey horse rode ahead of them with the unhurried air of someone arriving to collect a debt. At the far end of the bridge, old Guillaume the fishmonger stood holding a basket of eels and watching them come with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he does not like.

Revolutionary soldiers. In Vireloup. Where history had never previously bothered to stop, having found our town a convenient place merely to pass through, like weather.

I want to be precise about what I thought in that moment, because it shames me and shame makes for honest record-keeping. What I thought was: *they cannot possibly want anything here.* Not *how terrible.* Not *the Marceaus.* Not even *the ledger.* I thought: *here*? As if geography conferred immunity. As if the atlas on my table, which placed Vireloup accurately to within a quarter-mile, had somehow also placed it safely outside events.

That is the second lie maps tell, and it took me another day to learn it. A map keeps history at arm's length — but arms are exactly the length of a bayonet, and history crosses bridges in step whether your atlas admits it or not.

The tea went cold. I watched the column reach the near end of the bridge and begin, unhurried, into the town. At their head, the horseman turned once and looked up the hill — not at the church, not at the mill, but at the row of buildings on the rue des Cartes, and among them, at the workshop with its sign: *AUBERTIN, CARTOGRAPHER.* His father's sign. My master's name, hanging over the door of a shop whose master was eleven days in the churchyard ground.

The rider's gaze crossed mine through the window glass. He did not see me — I had stepped back into the shadow of the frame by then, an instinct so old it predated my new name — but I saw him well enough. A young face under the hat brim. Tired eyes. The eyes of a man doing sums, and the sums coming out badly.

I stood in the dimness of the workshop with cold tea in my hand and the finished atlas at my back, and I understood — not fully, not yet, but the way you understand weather by the ache in an old scar — that the blank paper I had just filled with Vireloup was going to cost somebody something.

Behind me, on the great table, Master Aubertin's atlas lay closed and complete: sixty-two plates, every coast of France, every river, every road, and one town drawn for the first time that very morning by a hand that did not officially exist.

Down in the street, the boots stopped at our door.

They knocked the way soldiers knock — that is to say, they did not knock at all, they simply opened it, because doors are for people who believe they will still be asked.

"Records," said a voice with a northern accent, clipped and official. "All civic records of the commune. By order of the Committee."

I set down the tea. I put my hand flat on the atlas cover, the way you'd steady a frightened animal.

And then — because I am, whatever else I am, an apprentice of this house, trained by the best liar in the cartographic trade — I arranged my face into the dull, half-witted politeness of a boy interrupted at his chores, and went downstairs to meet the Revolution.

* * *

What happened at that door belongs to the next chapter of this account, and to Captain Roche more than to me. But I will say this much here, because it matters to everything after: when I opened the door, the light from the street fell across the floorboards, and on the floorboards lay the loose leaf I had pulled from the ledger that morning for copying — the household roll, every family in Vireloup, every true name and every borrowed one, including a line about a certain apprentice that Master Aubertin had entered in his neat secretary hand under the heading *Irregularities, tolerated with affection.*

It lay there in the sunlight like a confession.

I stepped on it. I said, "Come in, citizens," and I stood on my own secret while the soldiers walked past me into the house, and my heart beat so hard I was sure they could hear it over their own boots.

Nobody looked down. Not then.

But the captain, passing, paused — just a moment — and glanced at my feet, and then at my face, with those tired summing-up eyes, and something in the arithmetic behind them shifted, very slightly, like a weight moving across the pan of a scale.

I have thought about that glance every day since. I suspect I always will.
