## 4. The Berth List

I have always believed that most cruelty is really just arithmetic wearing a uniform.

This is not an excuse. I want to be clear about that, at least with myself, in the privacy of my own skull, which is the only parliament I still trust. It is merely an observation, and observations were my trade before the Republic made me its bookkeeper. A man who can add a column of figures without weeping is called competent. A man who weeps is called unfit for service. I was considered very competent.

The requisitioned schoolhouse on the rue de la Fontaine had been given to me as an office because it had long tables, good light, and no windows low enough to be climbed out of. Someone in the quartermaster's chain had a sense of humor, or perhaps only a sense of architecture. On the largest table I unrolled the blank forms that had come up from the department in a crate stamped PROPERTY OF THE COMMITTEE, and I sharpened six pens, and I waited for the town clerk to bring me the civic records, because you cannot sort a town until you know what is in it.

The clerk was a trembling man named Bouchard whose hands had shaken for thirty years, judging by the state of his registers, and he set the ledger on my table the way a man sets down a sleeping animal he does not wish to provoke. The Vireloup town ledger. Baptisms, debts, land transfers, disputes over hedgerows going back ninety years — every family's true shape pressed between covers like flowers nobody meant to keep.

"Everything?" I asked him.

"Everything that's written down," he said, which was either reassurance or a confession, and I chose not to find out which, because finding out would have obligated me to act, and I had a long day of not acting ahead of me.

* * *

Here is how the arithmetic worked, in case posterity would like to check my figures.

The Committee had sent us categories, because categories travel better than people. Category One: émigrés and returned deserters — deportation. Category Two: holders of certificates of civisme judged suspect by local report — deportation pending review. Category Three: priests and their harborers — deportation. There was a fourth category that existed only as a marginal note in Roche's own orders, written in a hand that belonged to a man I had never met: *use discretion regarding skilled tradesmen.*

Discretion. The word had a smell to it, like coin rubbed between greasy fingers. Somewhere above me, someone had realized that a town needs its blacksmith even after it has deported its poet, and had priced this realization accordingly. I noted the tradesmen of Vireloup with a small cross in the margin and felt the grease come off on my own fingers.

The barge. That was the other figure in the equation, and the one that kept me at the table past midnight. One evacuation barge, scheduled twice, delayed once, capable — per the manifest the pilot had signed at Chalon — of carrying sixty souls at a comfortable load, eighty at an uncomfortable one, and perhaps ninety if the river was kind and the passengers were thin. Vireloup, once I had finished counting, held one hundred and forty-three names in my suspect categories.

One hundred and forty-three names. Eighty berths. Sixty-three names too many, and the river would not be argued with.

I write these numbers now and they look so small. In the ledger of my life they are the column I cannot make balance, and I have stopped trying. But that first night they were simply work. Names went into one list or another. A widow with a lame son went into Category Two because a neighbor had reported her for weeping at the wrong execution — I am not embellishing; the informant's actual phrase was *excessive grief, possibly feigned.* A tanner went into the discretion margin because the army needed leather. A whole family of Marceaus, I saw, had three separate denunciations against them, each more creative than the last, and I put them on the deportation draft because three denunciations was a number I could not yet afford to argue with.

That was the trick of it, I learned. You could not afford to argue with any single number. You could only afford to be tired, and move your pen, and tell yourself that the man who replaced you would be worse. Which was true. I had met the men who would replace me. They came to dinner and talked about efficiency.

* * *

It was on the third evening, I think — the days had begun to blur at the edges like ink in rain — that I noticed the apprentice.

Noticed is perhaps generous to myself. What actually happened is that I looked up from the berth list because I had the peculiar sensation of being watched with unusual competence. Being watched is common in occupied towns; being watched *well* is rare, and rarer still is being watched by someone who takes notes.

He — she, I would learn later, though the knowing changed less than you might think — was in the corner of the schoolhouse with the estate inventory, cataloging Aubertin's effects for the sale. The dead mapmaker's apprentice, all elbows and ink stains, with the particular posture of a person who has taught themselves never to take up space: shoulders drawn in, chin down, quill moving. Except that the quill stopped whenever I turned a page of the ledger. Stopped, held, resumed. A rhythm. Anyone who has ever done sums in his head recognizes the rhythm of someone else doing them.

I let several minutes pass and then, casually, I turned to the Marceau page and left it face-up while I pretended to study a window frame that needed nothing from me.

The quill did not resume.

So. The apprentice knew the Marceaus. Cared about the Marceaus, which was more dangerous than knowing them. I filed this under observations, where I filed everything, and I went back to my lists, and I told myself that whatever an apprentice felt about the fates of neighbors was none of my business, in the same way that the weather was none of my business. Both had been decided by larger systems than mine.

But here is what I did do, and it took me years to understand why: when I copied the final draft of the deportation schedule the next morning, I wrote the Marceau entry in my least careful hand, with a smudge over the surname that made it technically ambiguous whether the family was listed under Marceau or Marcenat, a family that did not exist. An error so small that no clerk reviewing it would flag it, but an error, a door left unlatched. I told myself it was fatigue. Fatigue is a wonderful excuse. It is the alibi of conscientious men everywhere.

Then I looked up, and the apprentice was watching me again, and this time there was something in the watching that was not fear. It was assessment. The look of someone measuring a lock to see what it would take.

I should have been alarmed. I was, faintly. But underneath the alarm was something I had not felt since before the Commission gave me my categories, and it took me another moment to name it, because it had been so long.

Company.

* * *

Let me say something about Captain Roche, since Roche is myself and self-portraits deserve honesty even when they flatter.

I had joined the Revolution the way one joins a queue: because everyone respectable was in it, because the alternative was standing outside it in the cold, and because at twenty-four I had genuinely believed that reason, properly administered, could redeem the cruelty of the old world. Ten years later I had administered a great deal of reason and redeemed very little, and I had developed a theory about why. The theory was this: the Revolution had discovered that paper kills at a greater distance than the musket, with less noise and no court-martial afterward, and it had promoted paper accordingly. I was the paper's corporal. I did not fire it; I organized its ammunition.

My conscience, such as it was, had survived by means of a discipline I can only describe as professional. I did my sums correctly. I did not invent denunciations, I did not inflate numbers, I did not accept the bribes offered to me in the first week (three of them; the tanner's silver watch still visits my dreams). Correctness was my moral ceiling and my moral floor and the narrow corridor I lived between, and I had honestly believed, until Vireloup, that correctness was a form of decency.

Vireloup disabused me in four days. Because correctness, applied to one hundred and forty-three names and eighty berths, produced a residue of sixty-three corpses, and no amount of correct arithmetic made the residue go away. The numbers did not care that I had followed procedure. The drowned do not audit.

On the fourth night I sat in the schoolhouse after everyone had gone, holding the pen over the final column, and I did something I had never done in ten years of service. I put the pen down unfinished. I left the sixty-third berth blank. Not assigned, not denied — blank, like a wound left open on purpose, because closing it would mean deciding what filled it.

Then I walked out into the dark, and the river was somewhere below the town making its patient indifferent sound, and I thought: the river gets the last word in every argument. All my lists were just ways of choosing who would be standing on the bank to hear it.

* * *

The apprentice came back on the fifth morning with the completed estate inventory, set it on my table, and said, in a voice pitched carefully between deference and indifference:

"The master's papers will need transport classification. For the sale."

"Classification?"

"Folio atlases are strategic materials, citizen captain. River charts, road surveys." A pause, calibrated to the millimeter. "You'd want the atlas inventoried separately. It has value beyond the paper."

Every sentence was true, which was itself suspicious; lies travel alone, but truth travels in convoys, and this convoy was delivering something. I looked at the apprentice properly for the first time. Young. Watchful. Hands stained the particular blue-black of someone who works with iron gall ink daily, which fit the story. Eyes that did not fit it at all. Eyes that had done their own arithmetic, recently and badly, and were still bleeding from the result.

"I'll classify it myself," I said.

"As you think best, citizen." And then, with the smoothness of a card player laying down a card face-down rather than face-up: "The master spent forty years on it. He used to say a map outlives its makers' opinions." Another pause. "Though I suppose opinions decide what happens to maps."

And the apprentice gathered the empty crate and left, and I sat there understanding perfectly well that I had just been approached, solicited, sounded out — by a person who could not possibly afford to be doing any of those things — and that the approach had been so deft that a stupider man would have missed it entirely, and a wiser one would have reported it.

I did neither. I opened the berth list instead, and I looked at the blank sixty-third line, and I thought: whoever you are, apprentice, you are going to be trouble, and I have missed trouble the way a drowning man misses the surface.

Then I picked up the pen, because the lists would not wait, and the river certainly would not, and somewhere in the ledger under my hand a name was waiting that did not belong to anyone I had counted yet.

It would be some days before I learned whose it was. But the paper already knew. Paper always knows first.
