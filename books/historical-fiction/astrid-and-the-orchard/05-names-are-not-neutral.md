## 5. Names Are Not Neutral

There is a particular smell to a town that has been requisitioned. I had imagined it would smell of gunpowder, or blood, or some other theatrical substance out of a pamphlet. It does not. It smells of wet leather and horse dung and the sour sweat of men who have marched all night and are now standing about in your market square looking important. History, it turns out, smells like a stable.

I learned this on the second morning of the occupation, crossing the Place des Tilleuls with my satchel of pens and my apprentice's coat buttoned to the chin, on my way to the records room where Captain Roche's clerks were busily converting Master Aubertin's life into inventory.

The town had arranged itself into the posture of a dog that has been kicked often enough to know the geometry of boots. The baker still baked — the army must eat — but he baked without humming. Madame Gounod still swept her step at six, but she swept with her eyes down, as if the cobbles had recently become politically sensitive. Even the church bell rang differently, or perhaps I only imagined that; grief and fear will make you hear things.

At the fountain, Odile Marceau was drawing water.

I want to be precise here, because precision is my trade and because what happened next deserves exact accounting. Odile Marceau, wife of Henri Marceau, cooper — not a rich cooper, not a poor one, the kind of solid middling man every town is built out of and no history book ever mentions. She stood at the fountain in her grey apron with her sleeves rolled, and when she saw me she did not lower her eyes. That was the Marceau way. Two winters ago, when I first came to Vireloup with a dead man's name sewn into my collar and nothing else to recommend me, it was Odile who set a bowl of soup in front of me without asking why a boy of fifteen looked like he hadn't eaten since the previous government.

"Little Dominic." She said it the way she always had, as though my name were a joke we were both in on and neither of us found funny anymore. "You look like you've been sleeping in the map room again."

"I have never once slept in the map room," I said. "I sleep under the map table. It's entirely different."

Her mouth twitched. Then it stopped twitching, and she glanced over her shoulder at the soldiers lounging by the well-head, and her face did something I had never seen it do before: it closed. Like a shutter. Like a shop going out of business.

"Tell your mother—" she began, and then caught herself, because everyone knew the old master had no wife living, and everyone knew his nephew had no one, which was the whole architecture of my disguise, load-bearing wall and all. "Take care of yourself, boy."

She said *boy*. She has always said *boy*, and I have always let her, because the alternative was a conversation none of us could afford. But that morning the word landed strangely, like a coin from a country I'd left.

* * *

It was Sylvie who came to the records room that afternoon, and Sylvie who broke everything open.

You should understand something about the records room before I go further: it was mine, in the way that anything can be yours if you are useful enough and invisible enough. When the soldiers seized the ledger and the atlas and all of Aubertin's papers, they discovered very quickly that confiscated paper is worthless unless someone can read it, and that revolutionary clerks from Orléans cannot decipher an old provincial mapmaker's hand, which was cramped, idiosyncratic, and — I say this with love — frequently drunk. So they needed me. An apprentice who knew the archive, who could locate any document by its shelf and its smell, who could tell them which of the master's abbreviations meant *vineyard yield* and which meant *debt unpaid, see priest*.

They needed me the way you need a key. Nobody worries much about the opinions of a key.

So I sat at my own old table in my own old room, cataloguing for the Republic the same papers I had catalogued for love, while Sergeant Vaillant stood by the window cleaning his nails with a knife, and Captain Roche's clerks made their neat lists. And in through the door came Sylvie Marceau, seventeen years old, with flour on her wrists and her chin up the way her mother's chin goes up, demanding to see whoever was in charge of names.

That turned out to be me. There is a sentence I will not soon forget: *the person in charge of names was a girl wearing a name that didn't belong to her.*

"Mademoiselle," said Vaillant, with the elaborate courtesy such men reserve for pretty girls and enemies, "this office is closed to civilians."

"Then I'll talk to him." She pointed at me. "He's not a soldier. He's Dominic. He practically lived in our kitchen."

Vaillant looked at me with mild amusement, the way you'd look at a cat that had wandered into a council meeting. "Well, apprentice? Your kitchen seems to have followed you here."

"Two minutes," I said, as if I were granting the favor, as if my stomach had not already begun its slow cold descent toward my boots.

We stepped into the corridor, into the smell of wet plaster. Up close Sylvie was thinner than I remembered, and there was a bruised look under her eyes that I recognized because I had invented it, once, staring into a canal in another town.

"They came last night," she said, low and fast, the words tumbling like water finding a crack. "A clerk and two soldiers. They read out our names from a paper. Papa's name, Maman's name, mine, little Étienne's. They said we're listed as *suspects*. Suspects! Of what? Papa ships barrels down the river. He can barely read. What does he know about politics that the wine doesn't tell him?"

"Sylvie—"

"They took the paper away and left a receipt. A *receipt*, Dominic, like we were goods. And the neighbors say there's a barge coming, an evacuation barge, and that families on the suspect list go on it and don't come back." Her hands had found my sleeve and gripped it, and I stood very still, because I have learned that when a frightened person grips your sleeve, the wrong movement can shake loose whatever is holding them together. "But you're in there. With them. With all the lists. You know how those rooms work now. You could find out what's written against us. You could find out who wrote it."

And there it was: the request, plain and terrible, hanging in the corridor between us.

Because here is what I had not told her, and what I will now confess to you as though you were a priest, or a page, which amounts to nearly the same thing: I already knew. I had catalogued the deportation schedule three days earlier, in Roche's own hand, in his clerk's fair copy. I had seen the name *Marceau* sitting on the list among forty others, printed in ordinary ink, taking up exactly as much space as any other word. I had read it, felt something twist behind my ribs, told myself that the fate of towns is a matter for larger maps than mine, and copied the schedule accurately, including the Marceaus, because accurate copying is what apprentices do.

Maps keep history at arm's length. Lists do too. That is their entire genius. A list lets you hold a family in your hand without ever having to look them in the face.

"You could ask," Sylvie said again, quieter now, and this quietness was worse than the gripping. "You could just ask. I'm not asking you to steal anything. I'm asking you to read."

She thought I was brave. That is the cruelty of it. To Sylvie Marceau I was the boy who had survived alone, who had a place inside the machine now, who could walk past soldiers and be saluted with bored indifference. She could not see that I was precisely as afraid as she was — only better disguised, which is, when you come down to it, my one consistent talent.

"I'll try," I said. "I promise nothing. But I'll try."

She kissed my cheek, flour and all, and went out through the door into the occupied afternoon. I stood in the corridor for a long moment, doing sums.

Here is the arithmetic I performed, standing on a flagstone worn concave by a century of feet:

Item: the Marceaus fed me through two winters when I had no name worth speaking and less future. Odile's soup. Henri teaching me barrel-gauge arithmetic at their table because he thought a growing boy should learn a trade with numbers in it. Sylvie slipping me end-crusts of bread shaped like boats so I wouldn't feel like charity. Four years of small kindnesses, itemized nowhere, receipted by no one.

Item: my safety depended on being nobody, seeing nothing, copying everything faithfully, and delivering the faithful copies to men who would use them to send the Marceaus downriver on a barge with no return berth.

Item: Master Aubertin, who taught me that a map is a promise — *that the world can be known, boy, that it yields to patience* — and whose life's work was currently being sorted into piles labeled STRATEGIC and SUBVERSIVE by clerks who could not find Vireloup on a map of Vireloup.

The sum did not balance. It has never balanced since. But somewhere between the second item and the third, something shifted in me — I felt it physically, a grinding like ice breaking up on the river, the first thaw after the kind of winter that convinces you spring was a rumor started by optimists.

History does not happen elsewhere. That had been my creed; I had built my survival on it. History happens in Orléans, in Paris, to other people, on large-scale sheets I merely inked the borders of. But the Republic's history had arrived in Vireloup wearing muddy boots, and it was standing in my records room using my tables, and it held a pen that wrote *Marceau, Henri, cooper; spouse; two children — deport* in the same tidy hand it used for requisitioning hay.

Names are not neutral. My master said that once, drunk, apropos of a border dispute he was mapping for a count who had bribed the surveyor. *Every name on a map was put there by somebody, boy, for a reason. Ask who benefits from each one.* I had thought he was talking about geography.

* * *

I went back into the records room and sat down and worked until the light went yellow and the clerks went to supper. Vaillant left at dusk, whistling something martial and flat. Roche came through once, checked a column, looked at me a moment longer than a man looks at a key, and left.

And then I was alone with the lists.

I am not proud of the next hour, but I record it honestly, because honesty is the only inheritance I trust. I did not stride to the drawer marked SUSPECTS and tear it open in a blaze of righteous courage. I sharpened a pen. I tidied the marginalia. I invented two errors in the hay-requisition figures that needed correcting. I procrastinated with the thoroughness of a true professional, and only when the candle was more than half gone did I finally draw the folder across the table and open it and read the Marceaus' entry straight through, top to bottom, including the annotation in the margin.

*Informant's report attached. Loyalty questionable.*

Attached. Which meant somewhere in this room — in these shelves I knew like the veins of my own hand — there existed a piece of paper in which someone had reported on the Marceaus, had weighed their soup and their barrels and their daughter's flour-dusted wrists against the Republic and found them wanting. Someone with a name. Names are not neutral; neither is ink.

I searched until midnight. I found requisitions, tithe rolls, Aubertin's vineyard codes, three letters from a priest to his mistress (I filed those carefully; blackmail may yet prove a currency), and no informant's report. It had either been removed to the captain's quarters or hidden by someone cleverer than a clerk — and I include myself in that category, because I know every hiding place in that room except, apparently, the ones that matter.

What I did find, wedged behind the atlas's mounting frame where I had tucked it days ago and half forgotten, was the loose page I had torn from the ledger before the soldiers came: the page in Aubertin's cramped, loving, occasionally drunken hand that I had stolen without fully knowing why.

I had not read it closely until then. There are things you avoid reading the way you avoid stepping off a cliff: carefully, and with your eyes averted, until the day your foot slips.

By candle stub, alone in the room where my master had spent thirty years turning the world into lines, I read the page he had hidden inside his own great work. It was not cartography. It was a list — of course it was a list; this whole catastrophe is a war of lists — names, and beside each name a note of loyalty, debt, allegiance, payment. Informants. The men who sold their neighbors' names, and what they were paid for them. Aubertin had kept accounts of betrayal the way he kept accounts of everything: precisely.

And near the bottom, in fresher ink, in a hand I almost recognized, a line I read four times before my eyes would accept it:

*Delacroix, D. — identity forged. See appendix. Papers prepared by A. Original name:*

The original name was not given. The line broke off, or the rest was elsewhere, on some other page, in some other place in this room or out of it. But the entry was there. My forgery — the load-bearing wall of my entire existence — was recorded in the master's secret ledger, which was now, by the grace of the Republic, military property.

Someone had written that line. Not Aubertin; the hand was wrong. Someone had known what I was, had entered me in the catalogue of secrets alongside informants and traitors, and that someone's page was missing from a ledger that was missing from a room I was supposed to be guarding.

I blew out the candle and sat in the dark and listened to the river, which was running high with melted snow, carrying ice toward the sea, patient and unstoppable, indifferent to every border it crossed.

Sylvie had asked me to read. Well. I had read.

And now I had three days — the barge came in three days, the schedule said so, my own faithful copying said so — to find a missing page, save a family, protect a forgery, and decide whether the girl called Dominic Delacroix intended to go on being a line drawn safely around empty paper, or whether she was finally going to put herself on the map.

Outside, boots crossed the square. Somewhere a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

Three days.
