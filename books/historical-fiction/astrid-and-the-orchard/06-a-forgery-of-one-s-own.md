## 6. A Forgery of One's Own

There is a particular kind of lie that behaves like a map projection: it is false in every part, and yet, if you are careful, true enough at the center to get you where you need to go.

I have been living inside one for eleven years. Her name was Dominic Delacroix before she became Dominic Aubertin's nephew, and I have not said it aloud since the night I ran from Lyon with my shoes split at the seams and a loaf of stolen bread going stale against my ribs. Names are like coastlines. The more closely you examine them, the more they dissolve into something no chart can hold.

But tonight the ledger sits locked in a soldiers' strongbox, and Sylvie Marceau has looked at me with her mother's eyes and asked me to become a thief on her behalf, and I find that the past has come off its shelf and is sitting in my room like an unpaid creditor. So let me tell you how a girl becomes a boy becomes an apprentice becomes the only person left who can draw Master Aubertin's coastlines the way he meant them drawn. Let me tell you about forgery — not the kind done with ink, or not only that kind, but the slower forgery, the one done with years.

* * *

Lyon, in the winter of 1782, smelled of smoke and wet wool and the particular despair of rivers in fog. I was thirteen, and I had learned in the previous six months that the world sorts people into two categories: those with papers and those without. My father had been a silk dyer with opinions; his opinions got him a cell, and his cell got him nothing at all, because he vanished into it the way a stone vanishes into a well, without even the courtesy of a splash. My mother followed him within the year, not into a cell but into a fever, which was faster and cheaper. What remained was me, a lodger aunt who counted every spoonful I ate aloud so I would know my debt, and a set of documents that named me a ward of nobody in particular.

The aunt's counting did it. I want to be clear about that. It wasn't hunger — hunger you can carry. It was listening to someone itemize your existence as a series of small thefts. On a Tuesday in November I took the bread, took the road, and took, without quite deciding to, a new name. I had heard that apprentices were wanted in the river towns. I had also heard, from a boy on the road, that a girl traveling alone was cargo waiting for a buyer. So somewhere between Lyon and the Loire I bound my chest with linen, cut my hair with sewing scissors in a hedgerow, and practiced saying *Dominic* in a voice pitched lower than mine, until the name stopped sounding like a costume.

It is astonishing, really, how few questions people ask a boy with dirty hands. A girl alone is a problem to be solved. A boy alone is merely a boy alone.

* * *

Vireloup I reached half-dead, in February, with frost in the splits of my shoes and the bread long since eaten and regretted. The town had one main street, a bridge the color of old bone, and — this was the whole of what I knew — a mapmaker. I had chosen it the way a drowning person chooses a floating plank: not thoughtfully, but because it was there. Master Étienne Aubertin, cartographer, late of the Navy hydrographic service, retired to the provinces with his instruments and his silences.

I knocked on his door because it was the only door in Vireloup with a brass plate, and a brass plate seemed to promise that someone behind it believed in order.

He opened it a hand's width. He was perhaps fifty-five then, tall and folded inward like a drafting compass stored wrong, with ink permanently resident under his nails and spectacles pushed up into gray hair that stood off his head as though startled. He looked at me for a long moment.

"You're not selling anything," he said. "Sellers knock louder."

"No, sir."

"You're starving, though. That's written all over you." He stepped back from the doorway. "Come in before the street decides to make you its business."

Inside was the smell I would come to think of as *home* without ever using the word: size, lamp oil, old paper, and the faint iron tang of fresh ink. Every horizontal surface held maps in various states of undress. Charts of coasts I had never seen hung from rails like laundry. On the great table in the center lay a half-finished survey of the lower river, beautiful and precise, and beside it a plate of bread and hard cheese, unattended.

He saw me look at the plate. He pushed it across the table without a word, and sat down, and went back to ruling a line, as though feeding a stray were a matter of no consequence whatsoever.

I ate. I tried not to weep over the cheese and failed partially. He ruled his line and let me fail in privacy, which is, I have since decided, one of the highest forms of kindness available to a certain kind of man.

When the plate was empty he looked up over his spectacles.

"Can you read?"

"Yes, sir."

"Figures?"

"Yes, sir."

"Hold a pen without strangling it?"

"I — think so, sir."

"We'll see." He pulled a clean sheet toward him, dipped a pen, and set it before me. "Copy that column of soundings. Do not improve them. Do not decorate them. Copy them exactly, including my errors, two of which I have put there on purpose."

That was the examination entire. No questions about my name, my family, my papers, my sex. I found out later that he had known within thirty seconds — the way I held the doorframe, some habit of the shoulders, God knows what — and had decided, in the space between opening the door a hand's width and stepping back from it, that whatever I was running from could keep what it was chasing.

The two deliberate errors I found. The third error was mine, and he circled it in red and wrote beneath: *Careful is not the same as accurate.* I still have that sheet. It is in the lining of my coat, and if you are reading this after I am dead, dig in the lining.

* * *

He never asked my story until nearly a year had passed, and then only once, and obliquely.

We were correcting proofs of a harbor chart by lamplight, and he said, without looking up: "The names people give themselves are usually truer than the ones given to them. Remember that when you choose what to write on documents."

I kept my eyes on the proof. "Yes, sir."

"Aubertin," he said, "is a common name in these parts. Very common. There are Aubertins in three parishes whose genealogies I could not vouch for if pressed." A pause, the scratch of his pen. "A nephew, say. A sister's boy from the south, apprenticed young, no reason for anyone to inquire further. Nephews are like rain, in France. Nobody audits the sky."

My heart was doing something unsuitable. "Sir, I—"

"Next week," he said, "the notary Bressac will call regarding my will — I make a new one annually; it annoys him pleasantly. You will be present. You will be introduced as Dominic Aubertin. Practice the surname. It should sit in your mouth like it grew there." And then, finally looking up, over the spectacles, with an expression I would spend eleven years learning to read: "Unless you'd rather not. In which case we will think of something else. But I am too old to learn a new assistant's habits, and you are too useful to lose, and the world is full of girls being wasted by their circumstances. Choose."

I chose. Of course I chose. But I want it recorded that he made it a choice, that first night and every night after. He built my forgery out of Sundays and corrections and a place at his table, out of teaching me theodolite and scale bar, latitude by noon sight and longitude by patient argument with a chronometer he loved more than most people. He built it out of introducing me, in Vireloup's one respectable café, as *my nephew, who does the mathematics* — and watching, with apparent indifference, while the town swallowed the fiction whole.

Papers came later, and properly. He had friends — I use the word narrowly; he had perhaps four — among whom was a defrocked notary in Saumur who owed him a favor of the dangerous sort. Registers were consulted, entries adjusted, a baptismal record acquired a nephew it had somehow always contained. When Bressac stamped my *livret* — apprentice to Master Cartographer É. Aubertin — the document was as real as anything the state had ever said about anyone. That is the joke at the heart of all officialdom, and Aubertin taught it to me without ever quite saying it aloud: *legitimacy is a matter of good draftsmanship.*

* * *

What I have been denying, these eleven years, is not the danger of it. I deny the word *family*, because I used it once, early, carelessly, and it frightened us both.

It was the second winter. I had fever — the bad kind, the kind that makes the walls talk — and for three days I surfaced from it at intervals and each time he was there, in the chair by my cot, mending an instrument or reading, never sleeping that I could see. On the third night, half-dreaming, I called him something. I don't know what exactly — *uncle*, perhaps, or worse, or better. He went very still. Then he said, gruffly, to the wick of the lamp:

"Fever talks nonsense. Drink this."

And I woke the next morning to find that during the night he had moved his own bedclothes down the hall to the room next to mine, and hung there his spare coat, and that he thereafter took his supper at the table with me instead of at his desk, as though these changes had always been the arrangement and he was annoyed at himself for the delay.

We never spoke of it again. We did not need to. Family, in that house, was conducted entirely in marginalia — small annotations in the margins of larger documents, deniable, unofficial, and more accurate than the body of the text.

* * *

He taught me everything in the end except the thing I needed most, which is that dead men cannot protect you and living women must protect each other. Or perhaps he did teach it and I declined the lesson. Perhaps the whole atlas — every coastline, every sounding, every careful red-circled error — was his last marginal note, the place where he wrote me into permanence: *this work, and the one who completes it, belong to each other.*

He made me swear to finish it, in his last month, when his hands had begun to betray him and he dictated bearings from memory while I drew. He made me swear with the same gravity he applied to everything, and I swore, and I meant it, and I have spent every day since discovering what the oath actually cost.

Because here is what the oath did not anticipate: soldiers on the bridge, a ledger in a strongbox, a captain doing arithmetic with human lives, and a Marceau girl with her mother's eyes asking me to steal. Here is what oaths never account for: that the dead do not have to choose between their work and their people, and the living do, hourly, and the choosing leaves scars in places maps cannot mark.

* * *

Tonight I took out the sheet from my coat lining — *Careful is not the same as accurate* — and spread it on the table under the lamp, next to the finished atlas, next to the memory of a man who fed a starving stranger and called it an administrative convenience.

He forged me a name so that I could survive. Fine. But survival, I am beginning to understand, is not the same as loyalty, and loyalty is not the same as accuracy, and I have been careful for eleven years without once being accurate about what I am: a woman made of borrowed paper who owes her life to a dead man's forgery and her future, possibly, to a live one of her own.

Tomorrow I go back to the records room. Captain Roche thinks he requisitioned an archive. He has, in fact, requisitioned a weapon, and the Marceau family is in its path, and I am the only person in Vireloup who knows both how the lock works and where the key is kept.

Master Aubertin taught me that a map is a set of choices about what to show. Tomorrow I find out what I am willing to show — and what I am willing to burn.
