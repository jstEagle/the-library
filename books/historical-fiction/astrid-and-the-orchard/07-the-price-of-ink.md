## 7. The Price of Ink

My mother's silver was the last beautiful thing we owned, and I want it understood that I never intended to sell all of it. That's the sort of detail people skip when they tell these stories later—the part where you stand at the table doing arithmetic with spoons.

There were twelve pieces once: six forks, six knives, with my grandmother's initials worked into the handles in a curling script that meant nothing to anyone anymore except as proof that we had once been the kind of family whose possessions outlived them. Mother had already gone through the linen, the clocks, the good candlesticks. She'd done it without ceremony, the way she did everything now, folding each loss away as if it were a shirt she was tired of.

"The forks," she said, when she saw me wrapping them in a flour sack. "Not the knives. A person can eat with a knife."

"Mother—"

"Sylvie." She said my name the way the priest used to say *dominus vobiscum*, with the weary authority of someone who has said it ten thousand times and expects to say it ten thousand more. "We may need knives before this is over. We will not need forks. Nobody has ever needed a fork desperately."

I left her four knives and took everything else, which was eight pieces of silver and one lie, because I had already decided on the way to the door that I would need all twelve if the man at the guardhouse drove as hard a bargain as I feared. I told myself the extra four were for leverage. I told myself I would bring back whatever wasn't spent.

I have since learned that this is what everyone tells themselves in the last hour before a town changes hands: *I will bring back whatever isn't spent.*

* * *

The man who bought transit passes out of the back room of the cooper's shop was called Gaston, though I doubted even his mother had ever called him that, and his prices moved like weather—worse by the hour, worse when the cannons were audible, worse for women alone because he thought fear made us stupid. He was wrong about me specifically but right about the general principle.

"Eight pieces," he said, turning a fork over in his fingers like a jeweler appraising a crown. "That gets you a pass. One name on it. Not stamped—stamped costs more."

"It's real silver."

"It's heavy metal," Gaston said. "Everything is heavy metal until I can eat it." But he weighed the forks in his palm anyway, and I watched him do the same arithmetic I had done at our table, only in reverse, and I hated him with the clean, uncomplicated hatred of someone who has not yet been forced to hate herself.

"My mother," I said, "kept those through two wars. She buried them under the kitchen floor in ninety-two."

"And now she wants very much to be somewhere else, doesn't she?" He smiled. It was not entirely unkind. That was somehow the worst part—that there was a person in there somewhere, doing this because the alternative was being deported himself, and that neither of us could afford to acknowledge it. "One name. Which name?"

"Marceau."

His smile flickered. Everyone in Vireloup knew the Marceau name by then; it had been read off the berth postings on Tuesday with all the drama of a market price. "The Marceaus are on the suspect list. A pass won't move a listed family. You'd want a berth allocation, and berths aren't mine to sell."

"Then who sells them?"

"The army sells them," Gaston said, "the way the rain sells dryness. You don't buy from the rain. You find the one roof with a hole in it." He counted out my silver, kept eight pieces, and slid a folded paper across the table—a transit pass, genuine enough to fool a drunk sentry, useless against anything soberer. "But since your mother fed half this town through the bad winter, including, I notice, certain apprentices of the late mapmaker—" he said *late* the way people said it about Aubertin, carefully, as though the word might detonate— "I'll tell you where the hole in the roof is. The evacuation manifests are copied fresh every morning in the records annex. Copied by hand. By clerks. And clerks," he said, tapping the pass, "have handwriting."

"I don't understand."

"You will," he said. "Or you won't, and you'll stand on the quay Thursday morning reading names, and understanding will come to you too late, the way it does to most people. Go home, girl. Take the knives."

* * *

The records annex had been a granary before the requisition, and it still smelled of grain dust and mice, as though the building itself hadn't accepted the change in management. I had no business there. My business was at home, packing, sitting with Mother, doing any of the sensible things a daughter of a marked household should be doing with her final days in the town where she'd been born.

Instead I walked past the sentry at the side door with a basket of bread rolls and an expression I had practiced in the reflection of Mother's good mirror—the expression of a girl delivering bread, bored, faintly put upon, invisible. The sentry was maybe seventeen and mostly interested in his own boots. Bread proved sufficient.

The manifests hung on a corkboard inside, three pages pinned under a wooden bar, and I found them because finding things is what you do when you've spent your whole life in a small town learning where everything is kept: who owes whom, which cellar holds brandy, which window a girl can be seen through on winter evenings when the light is good.

I looked for Marceau. That's all I intended. I wanted to see my family's name written somewhere other than the deportation notices—to see it in an ordinary clerk's hand, among ordinary names, and pretend for one minute that we were an administrative problem rather than a tragedy.

The third line of the second page read: **Marceau, Tomas; Marceau, Odile; Marceau, Sylvie — Berth 14, Barge *L'Aigrette*.**

I read it four times. Then I read the rest of the page, because terror makes thorough people of us all, and that was when I saw it.

Berth 9, originally allocated to a *Vasseur, René*—a name I knew vaguely from church, a wool merchant who had fled south in October with his ledgers and his mistress and left everyone else to deal with the consequences—had been struck through. Struck cleanly, with a single confident stroke, and above it, in the margin, in ink slightly fresher than the rest of the page, was written: **Fournier, Père Anselme.**

And I knew the handwriting.

You know handwriting the way you know a voice. You know the particular way a person fails to close their *a*s, the way their capital letters lean. I had seen this hand a hundred times—on the labels of Aubertin's map drawers, on the practice sheets Dominic burned in the stove when she thought nobody watched, on the little notes she left on our kitchen table when she came for soup and wouldn't stay: *Back by dark. Thank you for the bread. —D.*

Dominic.

Dominic had stood where I was standing, or at some desk with this page in front of her, and crossed out a fleeing man's name and written in old Père Fournier's instead—in the berth that should have gone spare, in the arithmetic of survival that the whole town was now doing with spoons. And she hadn't asked anyone. Hadn't told anyone. Hadn't said to me, on any of the hundred evenings she sat at our table eating my mother's soup, *by the way, I am deciding who lives.*

I stood in the granary smelling of grain dust with a forged pass in my pocket—forged by Gaston, but I had paid for it, which is its own kind of forgery—and I understood suddenly, with a clarity that felt like cold water, several things at once and none of them comfortably.

First: that Dominic had access to these rooms. Access enough to alter a military manifest, which meant access she had never mentioned, in a capacity she had never explained, wearing the modesty of an apprentice like a cloak thrown over something larger.

Second: that she had used that access for Fournier—for the old man who sheltered her the first winter, before us—and not, apparently, said one word about the fact that our family's berth allocation was tight, that there were three Marceaus and a grandmother's worth of debts and no margin at all, and that one more name, one small addition in one marginal hand, would have—

Would have what? Would have endangered us all, whispered the reasonable voice, the voice that sounded like Mother. A tampered manifest discovered is a shot squad. She protected us by leaving us off her crimes.

And third, arriving last and worst, quiet as mice under floorboards: that I could not tell whether I was furious because she had risked so much for a stranger's grandfather, or because she had done it without me, or because some small ugly splinter of myself had already been measuring that empty margin, counting the space after *Sylvie,* wondering if there was room.

I had sold my mother's silver for a pass. Dominic had sold something larger and stranger—her master's rules, perhaps, or her own safety—for an old man's berth, and had not even billed herself for the privilege.

"Hey." The young sentry had wandered in from the door, drawn, presumably, by the smell of bread or the sight of a girl standing motionless in front of a corkboard for too long. "You lost?"

"Delivering," I said, and held up the basket, and smiled the practiced smile, and walked out past him with my heart going like a drum in a parade.

Outside, the light was going amber over the rooftops, and somewhere toward the river the barge crews were loading, and I stood in the street holding eleven bread rolls and one dangerous piece of paper and understood that I had to talk to her. Tonight. Before Thursday, before the quay, before any of us signed our names to anything else in anybody's hand.

Because there was one more thing I had noticed on that manifest, the smallest thing, the thing I'd filed away while the bigger betrayals were still shouting: beneath the struck-through name and Fournier's name in the margin, in that same familiar hand, there was a fourth mark. A tiny cross. The kind a cartographer makes on a map to mark a spot worth returning to—or a spot where something is buried.

And it was drawn at the old lock, upriver, the one that hasn't opened since before I was born.
