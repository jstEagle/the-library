## 9. Old Wounds, New Borders

There is a particular quality to the light in a requisitioned map room at four in the afternoon in late October — a grey that comes through the windows already defeated, as though it had been interrogated on its way in. I had spent three days working under that light, cataloguing what remained of Master Aubertin's collection for men who could not tell a rhumb line from a rent receipt, and I had convinced myself it was patience.

It was not patience. It was stalking.

I knew Sergeant Vaillant's habits by then, the way one knows the habits of a rat one has not yet managed to trap. He came to the archive at odd hours when his duties allowed, always alone, always with the air of a man visiting money he had hidden rather than papers he was assigned. He would stand at the map chest with the long drawer of the Loire valley — Aubertin's finest survey — and he would look not at the maps but through them, into whatever arithmetic he kept behind his eyes.

So on the fourth day I did not go to my table. I went to the chest, and I waited among the rolled vellums like a spider who has read about spiders but never met one, and when his boots came up the stairs I stepped out of the shadow of the chest and said:

"You were there. When he died."

I will grant him this: he did not startle. A man who startles does not survive long wearing that uniform in a town that hates him. What he did was worse — he smiled, slowly, the smile of a man confirming an inventory.

"Apprentice Delacroix." He pronounced it *Delacroy*, deliberately wrong, the way he had since the first day, testing each time whether I would correct him. A correction would have been a small thing. It would also have been a tell. "You shouldn't be here alone."

"Neither should you. You're infantry. The records detail belongs to Captain Roche's clerks."

"The captain's clerks," Vaillant said, drifting toward the Loire drawer, "are busy drafting lists of people to put on barges. I thought someone should mind the... strategic materials." His hand rested on the drawer pull without opening it. "You have a question, apprentice. Ask it badly, like apprentices do."

"Master Aubertin fell down his own stairs on the night before the army arrived," I said. "A man of seventy-one, who climbed the tower at Montbrison twice a year to take sightings, fell down thirteen steps he had walked ten thousand times. And the door of the house was found unlatched, though he locked it against the dark the way other men say their prayers." I kept my voice level, because a level voice was all the weapon I had and I could not afford to spend it early. "You were seen on the rue des Orfèvres that night."

"Seen." He turned the word over like a coin he suspected of being clipped. "By whom?"

"By Madame Guesde, who is now on the deportation list, and therefore has nothing left to lose by repeating it."

"Ah." He nodded, as if I had confirmed a delivery. "The widow Guesde. Yes, she saw me. I knocked. Your master declined to answer. I regret the town's doors are so poorly made that a knock becomes an accusation." He opened the Loire drawer at last and drew out the survey, unrolled six inches of it, and studied the river with something almost like tenderness. "He drew this?"

"You know he did."

"I know he was more than a mapmaker. That's what nobody in this town has understood, girl. Not the captain, not your neighbours, not you, apparently, though you inked his coastlines." He rerolled the sheet with a snap. "Aubertin spent thirty years making himself boring. Do you know how hard that is? To be so useful and so dull that no committee ever looks twice at you? He nearly managed it."

"Nearly," I said, because my mouth had gone dry and one syllable was all it would carry.

"Nearly," Vaillant agreed. "And then he died at the bottom of his stairs, and everyone asked the wrong question. They asked whether I pushed him." He leaned against the chest, arms folded, and looked at me with the frank interest of a farmer appraising a horse he intends to work hard. "The question worth asking is what he was protecting. Because a man does not fall downstairs defending a coastline."

* * *

I want to record, honestly, what happened inside me at that moment, because later I pretended it had been courage and it was not. It was vertigo. There is a moment in draftsmanship — Aubertin taught me this in my first year — when you have drawn a long line freehand and you must decide, before lifting the pen, whether the line you have made is the line you meant. Lift too soon and the stroke dies mid-paper; hesitate and the hand trembles and ruins everything. I had begun a sentence and now I had to finish it without knowing where it ended.

"What was he protecting?" I said.

Vaillant took his time. He crossed to the window, looked down at the square where two soldiers were arguing with the baker about flour weights, and spoke with his back to me, which I understood to be a courtesy or a tactic, possibly both.

"When the Committee sent me here, my instructions were simple. Secure the archives, forward anything of intelligence value, and identify local correspondents — people who had written to émigrés, sheltered priests, sold grain outside the requisition. The usual rot. The previous commissioner had compiled a list of such names. A very thorough list. Names, sums, dates, the whole filthy ledger of a small town's small treacheries." He turned. "That list went missing two years ago. Officially, it was lost in transit. Unofficially—" he spread his hands, "—it arrived in Vireloup, in the possession of a mapmaker who had once done surveying work for the Ministry and still had friends in its offices, and who read it, and was so horrified by what a piece of paper could do to a town that he stole it."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, the baker won, or lost; a shutter banged.

"He hid it," Vaillant went on. "Not cleverly — he wasn't a spy, God help him, he was an old man who had only ever lied to rivers. But well enough that a sergeant with no head for geography searched his house twice and found nothing but beautiful, useless drawings." His eyes came back to mine and stayed there. "On the third visit, I was more persuasive. He still refused to tell me where it was. He was on the landing. There was a scuffle — I will not insult you by pretending otherwise — and he fell, and I ran, because I am a coward in matters of stairs and corpses, and by morning the army was on the bridge and the whole thing became a death from misadventure, which is what most deaths are, if the paperwork is willing."

I had known. Let me be clear about that — some part of me had known since the night I found him, since I knelt on those boards and saw the angle of his neck and thought, with a clarity that has never since left me: *this was not gravity.* Knowing and hearing are different countries. I stood in the second one now, and it was colder.

"Why tell me?" My voice came out lower than I intended. "You've just confessed to murder. To the one person in Vireloup with reason to hate you for it."

"Because confession is currency, apprentice, and I'm spending it to buy something." He straightened off the chest. "I need that page. You've had access to this room, to his effects, to his precious atlas — don't look at the frame like that, I've handled enough of his hiding tricks by now to know he'd never leave a loose leaf where anyone honest might find it. You find it, you bring it to me, and in exchange I lose my memory regarding Madame Guesde, regarding a certain apprentice whose papers the commissariat might someday think to examine too closely, and regarding the Marceau family, whom you have been hovering near like a hen that's hatched the wrong chicks."

The world narrowed to the width of that last name. I felt my face do something I could not control, and hated it, and watched him watch it.

"Ah," he said softly. "The Marceaus. Yes. I thought so. Sentiment — the great weakness of people who believe they have none."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the list stays lost, the arrests continue on general principles, and one day a clerk with a good memory wonders aloud why Master Aubertin's nephew has no birth record in any parish within fifty miles." He shrugged, a small ugly economy of motion. "You see, I don't need the page to hurt you. I need the page to save myself. Command believes the informant list exists. If it doesn't surface, the failure lands on me. So it surfaces — through you — or everyone in this town keeps paying for my misfortune at the rate of one barge-load per week."

He let that sit. Then he added, almost gently, which was somehow the worst thing he had done yet:

"There's one more thing you should know about that page, since you'll be looking for it anyway. Your master didn't steal the list out of abstract virtue. He read it, and he found a name on it he couldn't bear to surrender." Vaillant picked up his cap from the table, turning it once in his hands. "The original informants. The people whose denunciations built the list. Their names are at its head. And one of them — the first entry, dated years back, a woman informing on her own household for bread money — shares a surname with a runaway girl who turned up on Aubertin's doorstep smelling of the road and lying about who she was." He settled the cap on his head. "I don't know your real name, Delacroix. But I'd wager a month's pay your master did, and that he died keeping it off government paper. Think on that while you search."

His boots descended the stairs. Counting them was an old habit; I counted all thirteen, and heard in each one the echo of a different staircase, and stopped counting.

* * *

I did not cry. I want that entered into the record, whatever record survives, because I have been accused of many things and weeping is not among them and I am vain enough about it. I sat down at my table instead, before the half-finished plate of the evacuation route, and I dipped my pen, and I held it over the paper until the drop gathering at its nib fell and bloomed into a blot the size of a fingernail, and I looked at that blot for a long time.

My mother's name. On the head of a denunciation list. Informing on her own household — on us, then, on whatever we had been before hunger took the shape of a soldier's pay — *for bread money.*

Here is the cruelty of it, the mechanism I have turned over every night since: I have spent nine years being ashamed of a forged identity. I have flinched at parish records, rehearsed answers to questions no one asked, kept my history folded as small as a map of a country that no longer exists. And all that shame assumed the story underneath was clean — that somewhere there was a true Dominic, a real family, an honest name that forgery had displaced. Now a murderer tells me the honest name sits at the top of a traitor's ledger, bought with our ruin, and I am asked to grieve for its protection. Aubertin died guarding the proof of my family's betrayal. He carried it like a stone for two years, this kind, dry, exacting old man, and never once let me feel the weight of it. Never once looked at me differently. That, more than the stairs, more than Vaillant's smug confession, is what undid me: that the greatest act of love I ever received was a secret kept at the cost of his life, and the secret itself was my shame.

I mopped the blot. I finished the coastline I had been inking, a fictional stretch of the Atlantic, invented entirely, safe, and I signed the plate *del. Delacroix* in a hand steadier than I deserved, and I thought: *he gave me this name too. He gave it knowing what the old one was. So which of us forged me — him, or her, or me?*

Then I blew out the lamp, because the light was failing and because Vaillant would be watching the windows, and I sat in the dark of the map room doing what cartographers do when the territory turns hostile: I drew the ground in my head.

He wanted the page. Very well. He would get a page — but pages can be copied, and copies can be altered, and a man who confesses to one murder has told you exactly how much his word is worth. Roche wanted order. The Marceaus wanted a berth. Sylvie wanted her family whole. And somewhere under the floorboards of a dead man's house — or sewn into an atlas frame, or folded into a ledger I had touched a hundred times without reading — lay the single document that could buy all of it or burn all of it.

I had hidden one loose page already. I could hide another. The difference, this time, was that I would know what I was hiding: not my master's truth, and not my family's shame, but leverage. Living names outweighing dead ones.

I rose, took my coat, and let myself out into the blue cold of the square, where the lamplighter was working his way along the rue des Orfèvres, and past the darkened doorway of the house with the thirteen stairs, and home — if a forged address can be called home, which I have decided, provisionally and tonight, that it can.

Tomorrow I would take the atlas frame apart.
