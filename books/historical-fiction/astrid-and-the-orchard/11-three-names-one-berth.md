## 11. Three Names, One Berth

The requisition notice arrived on a Tuesday, which felt characteristic of Captain Roche. Not a Friday, when a man might be excused for drowning his sorrows in advance, and not a Sunday, when even revolutionaries pretended to have souls. A Tuesday. Bureaucratic to the bone.

*The final barge will depart Vireloup on the morning of the 14th. All persons holding transit authorizations must present themselves at the quay by six o'clock. No exceptions will be granted after this hour.*

No exceptions. I had learned enough about paperwork by then to know that *no exceptions* was a phrase written by men who spent their evenings granting them.

I read it four times in the map room, standing by the window where the light was best, while behind me Roche's clerks scratched away at their manifests like a roomful of very anxious mice. And it was on the fourth reading that I saw it — or rather, that I let myself see what I had been avoiding for two days, ever since the Marceaus' allocation had come through with Madame Marceau's signature still wet beside it.

The allocation covered three names. There were four Marceaus.

I did the arithmetic standing there, because arithmetic is honest in a way that nothing else in Vireloup was that autumn. Three berths: Papa Marceau, whose lungs had been ruined by thirty years at the tannery and who would not survive a deportation march. Maman Marceau, who had fed a friendless apprentice through two winters out of a pot that never seemed full enough for it. And Jean-Luc, sixteen years old and already on a list somewhere because he'd been overheard saying that no country belonged to anybody.

That left Sylvie.

One berth short. One name too many for the paper's mercy.

I put the notice down and picked up my pen, and I am not ashamed to say my hand shook — though I note, with the detachment I once reserved for coastlines, that it shook in a way no drawing had ever made it shake. Maps do not tremble. That is their great lie, and I had believed it for years.

"Apprentice." Roche's voice, from across the room. "A word."

I went to his desk. He had the berth list spread before him, and his finger rested on the margin beside the Marceau entry, where someone — I, as it happens, three nights prior, with ink I had watered to look faded — had adjusted a number.

"You've been correcting my clerks' sums," he said. It was not quite an accusation. It was worse; it was an invitation.

"The count was wrong," I said. "Four in the household. Three authorized."

"And you changed it to—" he squinted at his own document, performing surprise badly— "three. Correctly. So the family is short one."

"The family is short one," I agreed, and hated how steady my voice came out. Steadiness is easy when the stakes are other people's lives. I had been steady my whole life precisely because none of them had ever been mine.

Roche looked at me for a long moment. He had the kind of face that gave nothing away on principle — a customs officer's face, a card player's face — but his eyes moved, and I had learned to read movement if not expression. They moved from me, to the list, to the window, where the barges sat at their moorings like patient animals.

"There are no more allocations," he said. "Not from Paris. The quota is fixed."

"I understand the quota is fixed."

"Do you." He straightened the papers on his desk, aligning their edges with a precision that would have made Master Aubertin nod. "Then you understand that whatever you intend to do about it, you intend to do it without my help."

"I haven't said I intend anything."

"No. You've said nothing at all, very carefully, which from you is practically a confession." He turned back to his manifest. "Six o'clock, apprentice. The 14th. Bring your master's atlas when you come — Command wants it inventoried before departure."

I left the map room with the notice folded against my ribs like a splint, holding something broken in place.

* * *

Père Fournier was mending a net when I found him, because Père Fournier was always mending a net. It was his answer to idleness, to grief, to weather, and possibly to mortality itself; I half expected him to have one in his hands at his own funeral, should anyone be fool enough to hold one for him.

He had sheltered me, the first winter. I want to be precise about this, because I have spent years being imprecise about everything that matters. I had run from — well, from things this account has not yet earned the right to name — and I had slept three nights in the hollow of his woodshed before he found me, and instead of turning me out to the gendarmes, as the law required and as any sensible man would have done, he had looked at me crouched there among the split logs with my stolen compass clutched to my chest, and said:

"You'll take the cold worse in those shoes. Come inside and eat something, and we'll decide what you're running from in the morning."

In the morning he had decided nothing. He had simply never raised the question again. For four months I had slept in his loft and eaten at his table and listened to him complain about the river as though it were a disobedient child, and he had asked for nothing, and I had paid for nothing, because he would have taken offense at payment. When Aubertin took me in, Fournier had shaken the old man's hand so hard I'd heard the bones click, and said, "You've taken a fine weight off my floorboards," and that was the whole of the farewell.

He looked up now, squinting against the low sun off the water.

"Dominique." He used the masculine form without irony and without effort; he was perhaps the only soul in Vireloup who had never needed to be told, and never mentioned it either. Some kindnesses are just accuracy. "You have the face of a girl about to ask for something difficult."

"How would you know my asking face?"

"Because your working face looks at the horizon. Your asking face looks at your boots." He set down the net. "Sit, then. Ask."

I sat on the cold stones beside him. Downriver, the barges knocked gently against each other, conversing in wood.

"The lists are closed," I said. "The last barge leaves on the 14th."

"I know. Half the town knows. The other half is pretending not to know, which is the same thing slower." He picked up a bobbin and turned it in his fingers. "The Marceaus have their papers. I saw Sylvie showing them at the bakery like a christening gown."

"They're short a berth."

His hands went still. Old hands, river hands, hands that had pulled me out of more trouble than I could count and one literal flood. He understood immediately — men who work water understand shortages faster than landsmen — and I watched him arrive at the arithmetic before I could speak it, and watched him begin arranging his face into acceptance.

"One berth," he said. "And two claimants."

"It isn't a claim. It's—" I stopped. Lying to Fournier had never worked and I wasn't going to start succeeding now. "Sylvie doesn't know yet. I haven't told her."

"You will."

"I have to."

"Yes." He resumed mending, the shuttle clicking through the mesh. "You will. Because if you don't tell her yourself, she'll find out on the quay, and finding out on a quay is a bad way to learn anything. Take it from a man who has watched a lot of departures."

"Fournier—"

"Don't." The word was gentle but it stopped me like a hand on the chest. "Don't choose me in this boatyard, boy. I'm seventy-one years old. My hands shake before dawn now, and my eyes aren't fit to thread this net without the good light. Sylvie is seventeen and her mother needs her and that girl has more sense than the entire Committee of Public Safety. If you give the berth to me, you're not saving me. You're burying me with better scenery."

"She wouldn't withdraw."

"She would. You know she would, and you know why, and you know that's exactly what makes it unbearable, so let's not perform the unbearable together, we haven't time." He tied off a knot with infuriating calm. "Besides. Who says I'm staying?"

The river said so. The roads said so. Every list and quota and gray-faced clerk said so. But I looked at him and he looked back, and beneath the weathered pragmatism there was something moving, quick and private as a fish under ice.

"What have you done?" I asked.

"Nothing yet." Which from Fournier meant *something already*.

I left him mending his net and walked the long way back through town, past the shuttered bakery where Sylvie had once slipped me bread on credit that her mother silently forgave every month, past the fountain, past the house where Master Aubertin had lived and died and where his atlas — his life, bound in calfskin — waited for me under lock like a hostage or a treasure, depending on the day and the light.

Three names. One berth. Two people I loved and could not bear to rank, because ranking them meant admitting one of them was going to be taken by the thing waiting downriver, and I did not think I could carry that and stay the person who could still draw a straight line.

* * *

Sylvie knew before I finished telling her. Of course she did. She had grown up above a shop that extended credit in a dying town; she could smell scarcity coming up the stairs.

We stood in the Marceaus' back room among the trunks they'd packed and unpacked three times, paring their lives down to what a berth could hold. Maman Marceau had insisted on bringing the good linens; Papa had insisted on bringing his father's tools; neither would win, and both kept packing anyway, the way couples do.

"So it's me or Fournier," Sylvie said flatly.

"It's not decided."

"Everything's decided. That's what lists are." She sat down on her trunk, hard, as if her knees had submitted a resignation. "You could alter the manifest again. You've done it before."

"I've adjusted numbers. Adding a person is different. The quay has guards with the actual rolls, and Roche checks—"

"Roche." She said the name the way you spit out a fishbone. "Does he check? Or does he look away? Because Marguerite Duval swears he looked directly at her cousin's name on the exemption roll and then developed a sudden interest in the ceiling."

I didn't answer, which she correctly interpreted as an answer.

"Dominic." She used my false name, my true gender, the particular combination she alone managed to make sound like both. "If you tell me it's hopeless, I'll believe you. But you're the worst liar in Vireloup, so try honesty for once. Is there another way onto that barge?"

And there it was, the question, sitting between us like a coin on a table. Was there another way? There is always another way; that is the curse of being someone who reads terrain. I had spent the evening before, unable to sleep, doing what I always did with insomnia: drawing. And what I had drawn, without quite admitting it to myself, was the river below the checkpoint — its bends, its shoals, the old lock at Chenal Mort that the bargemen said had silted shut since before the war.

But that was tomorrow's thought, or next chapter's, and I wasn't ready to say it aloud, because saying it aloud would mean admitting what it cost.

"Not onto the barge," I said carefully. "Maybe... past the checkpoint."

Her eyes sharpened. "Explain."

"Not yet. I need to walk the ground first. And Sylvie—" I reached for her hands, and she let me take them, her fingers cold as the river stones. "Whatever happens, you three are getting on that barge. That part is fixed. I'd forge God's own signature before I'd let otherwise happen."

"That's blasphemy."

"That's Tuesday."

She laughed despite herself, a short wet sound, and gripped my hands back. Then her face did something complicated — guilt crossing purpose like two contour lines meeting at a cliff — and I recognized it, because I had been wearing the same expression for days.

"What?" I asked.

"Nothing. Nothing. Go walk your ground." She kissed my cheek, quick, the way she'd done since we were young enough that it meant only bread and loyalty. "Just promise me one thing. Whatever you're planning — don't plan me out of it. I get to decide what I'm worth too."

I promised. It was the easiest lie I ever told, because I believed it when I said it, and the hardest, because I already knew I didn't mean it.

* * *

I walked to Chenal Mort at dusk, because I wanted to see the lock with my own eyes before hope got ahead of the facts. Hope does that. It drafts coastlines without surveying them first, and I had buried one master already who taught me better.

The old lock sat in its bowl of trees, doors green with a decade of weed, water pressing patiently against the downstream gates. I measured it the way I measure everything — with my eyes, with my stride, with a stub of pencil — and by full dark I had the shape of it in my head: workable. Silted shallow, yes, but a barge draws less than people think, and if the upstream gates could be made to open...

They could. With muscle. With hours. With someone staying behind at the mechanism, in view of anyone sent to check, working the winches by hand while the soldiers' lanterns came down the towpath.

Someone staying behind.

I stood in the dark with the river talking to itself below me and did the terrible arithmetic one more time. Three names on the barge beyond the checkpoint, safe. One name at the lock, exposed. And a choice wearing the faces of everyone I loved.

Fournier would volunteer. That was the horror of it — he'd volunteer before I finished asking, and he'd call it practicality, and he might even survive it, old and clever as a heron. Sylvie would fight him for the post. And I would stand between them choosing, as though love were a scale and I held the weights.

Except.

Walking back along the towpath, a third thought surfaced, cold and clean as the moon coming out from cloud: there was a fourth name available. Unlisted, unallocated, unwritten anywhere except in forged ink. A name that belonged to no household, owed no debts, appeared on no roll in Paris. An apprentice with access to the records room, the atlas, and twenty-two days of practice at making documents say what he needed them to say.

I could take the lock myself.

I stopped walking. The river kept on. And somewhere behind me, up the dark road toward town, I heard footsteps — unhurried, familiar, heavy in the way of a man who has spent his life on wet stones — and Père Fournier's voice came out of the night before I could even turn:

"You've the look of someone who's found a way and hates it. Come and tell me what it costs before you go pricing it alone, boy. I'm too old to chase you, and you're too kind to make me."
