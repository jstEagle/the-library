## 12. What Sylvie Would Do

My mother taught me two things before the world turned upside down: how to make a stew out of nearly nothing, and how to write a letter that says one thing while meaning another. She called the second skill *diplomacy*. It has since occurred to me that what I did in the records office on the fourth of Thermidor was not diplomacy, but we will come to that.

First you should know what I knew, and when.

I knew that the Marceau name sat on Captain Roche's evacuation list with a red line drawn beneath it, which meant deportation, which meant the salt works in the south or something worse, depending on which rumor you preferred and how much wine you'd had while hearing it. I knew my mother had already begun packing as though denial were a form of luggage—setting things near the door and then moving them back, night after night, like a tide of spoons. And I knew that Dominic Delacroix—or whatever name she was wearing that month; I had stopped asking around our second winter, the way you stop counting a friend's debts—had access to the records room where the lists lived.

What I did not know, until the evening my cousin's boy came running up the lane with his shirt untucked and his mouth going like a millwheel, was that there was exactly one berth left beyond our family's allocation. One. Enough for a single body on the last barge out of Vireloup.

And that Dominic had been seen standing outside the harbor office for the better part of an hour, looking at the water as if it had said something rude.

* * *

Let me tell you about Père Fournier, because if you have not eaten his bread you will not understand what I did, and I refuse to be misunderstood by strangers.

Fournier kept the bake ovens at the top of the Rue Basse, and in the winter of '91—when the flour was rationed to a mockery and my father's workshop stood idle because nobody commissions fine cabinetry during a famine—he used to set aside the misshapen loaves. The burned ones. The ones a respectable baker would sell for half or throw away. He sold them to us for nothing and swore us to secrecy about it, because, he said, charity is like a wound: decent people cover it so it can heal.

He never once asked why the cabinetmaker's family had fallen so low. He simply noticed, the way old men notice everything while pretending to be asleep, and he acted.

Two winters later it was Dominic he fed. She had appeared in town that autumn—a narrow-shouldered apprentice with ink permanently under her fingernails and a genius for being unremarkable—and she took a room above the bakery because it was cheap, which is to say free, though Fournier grumbled about the rent loud enough for the whole street and collected none of it. She thought she was fooling him. She thought she was fooling all of us. A girl disguised as a boy is a locked door, and everyone in Vireloup could see the hinges; we simply agreed, as a town, not to knock. That is a kind of love too. Perhaps the most common kind.

So when I heard the arithmetic—one berth, three names, Dominic holding the pen—I did not need to see the list to know how it would break.

* * *

I found her at dusk on the quay, doing a very poor impression of a person with somewhere to be.

"You've heard," she said, without turning. She always knew my footsteps. I choose to believe this is affection rather than surveillance.

"I've heard."

"It's Fournier or me." She picked a wood shaving off the bollard and worried it between her fingers. "Roche's clerk let it slip. One berth past your family's allocation, and the manifest closes tomorrow noon. Sylvie—" She turned then, and her face did that thing it does, that closing of shutters, that arranging itself into the expression of someone who has already decided and merely requires my signature. "It has to be him. He's seventy-three. I'm seventeen and I can pass for a boy in bad light, which as you know is my entire biography."

"And what will you do instead?" I asked. "Stay? In Vireloup? With Vaillant's men counting every head at the checkpoint like coins?"

"Maps are portable," she said lightly, which from anyone else would have meant nothing and from her meant everything: *I have an exit drawn that you haven't seen.* "And somebody should stay who knows where the roads go."

"Dominic."

"Sylvie." She smiled at me, and it was the smile that decided me—not because it convinced me, but because it didn't. Behind it her jaw was set the way it set over the drafting table when a coastline refused to cooperate, and I recognized the look of a person preparing to disappear themselves for the convenience of others. She had done it her whole life. Her whole life was a forgery with her own signature missing.

I kissed her cheek and told her I'd think about it, and I lied to her, and it was the easiest lie I have ever told, which frightened me more than the soldiers did.

* * *

Here is what my mother's second lesson comes down to: a letter that lies must be built honestly. Every joint true, every seal sound. People forgive a false message; they never forgive sloppy work.

Dominic's handwriting I knew better than my own conscience. Two years of watching her label rivers and mark depths along the riverbank. The hand was small and forward-leaning, economical, with a particular cruelty to the letter *r*—she crossed nothing twice, she wasted no stroke, as if paper were bread and ink were flour and both were rationed. Her signature, though. Her signature was the interesting part, because Dominic Delacroix signed almost nothing. An apprentice learns early that documents are dangerous. So her name existed mostly on the flyleaf of the atlas, in that same compressed fist, and once, memorably, on a promissory note to my mother for three eggs, which my mother framed as a joke and which I studied longer than any sermon.

That evening I sat at our kitchen table after my parents slept, with the egg-note before me and a scrap of the clerk's discarded list-paper beside it, and I taught myself to be her.

The trouble was not the letters. The trouble was the hesitation. Dominic wrote like someone who has decided never to be caught hesitating. When I forged her *D*, mine came out apologetic. I tore it up. My second attempt was too confident, a *D* written by someone enjoying herself, which Dominic never was. Torn up. On the third try I thought of her the way I actually saw her—the set jaw, the shuttered face, the girl performing a boy performing an apprentice performing indifference—and my hand went still and mean and exact, and the name came out looking like it had been written by someone in a hurry to have finished existing.

It was very good. That is the confession underneath the confession: I am good at this, and some traitorous cellar of myself enjoyed it.

Above the signature I wrote the alteration itself, in the clerk's blander hand, which posed no difficulty: *Berth reallocated — P. Fournier, in place of S. Marceau, by consent of party.* By consent of party. Six words, each one a small door closing behind me.

Then I sat with the paper for a long time while the fire ticked down, and I examined myself for guilt the way you probe a tooth, and found—almost nothing. What I found instead was fury, banked and patient, and underneath the fury something worse: tenderness. Because I understood her perfectly. She was doing to herself exactly what she'd done her whole life—writing herself out of the manifest, making herself portable, invisible, a map instead of a person. And I could not argue her out of it with words, because she had argued herself into it with twenty years of practice.

But a forged signature, now. A forged signature she couldn't refuse. There is a justice in it that appeals to me even now: I would betray her the way she betrays everyone she loves, quietly, on paper, for their own good.

* * *

Getting the paper into the harbor office required my mother's silver, a lie to my mother about the silver, and a dawn walk through streets where the soldiers' boots made the cobblestones sound like a slow drum.

The corporal on duty was nineteen and hungry and bored, the most conquerable combination known to man. He looked at the candlesticks. He looked at me. I looked at the transit passes on his desk the way you look at something you are pretending not to want, and I said I needed five minutes alone with the manifest to verify my family's listing, because my mother was beside herself, poor woman, and wouldn't eat. Both statements were true, which I have found is the best mortar for any forgery. He took the silver and went to find breakfast, and I had the office to myself and the morning light coming gray through the shutters.

The manifest lay open on the stand. My family's names, all six, the red line beneath. Fournier's name absent. Dominic's—there was no Dominic's, of course; there never is; she exists in the margins of every document that concerns her.

I slid the altered slip in among the clerk's working papers, weighted it with the inkwell, adjusted the manifest's ribbon so it would fall open naturally to the right page at the right hour, and left. My hands did not shake until I was outside, and then only briefly, and I have decided to attribute that to the cold.

* * *

I want to record, because this account may be all that survives of me, that I understood the cost. If Dominic discovered the substitution before noon, she could countermand it, and then we would have a fight, and I would lose the fight but win the war, because she could not re-forge the berth to herself without revealing she'd read my forgery closely enough to copy its methods, and pride is the one document she will never surrender. If she discovered it after noon, it was done, and she would be on the next road south or in a cell, and either way alive until the former.

What I did not anticipate—what I could not have modeled, because I did not know her the way I thought I did—was that Dominic Delacroix does not wait for manifests. Dominic Delacroix makes her own.

I learned this at midday, when the clerk posted the final list and my eyes went straight to the bottom of it and found, appended in a hand I recognized better than my own heartbeat, a note in the margin. Small, forward-leaning, economical, cruel little *r*s:

*S. Marceau remains allocated. Do not amend further. We will speak.—D.*

She knew. Within hours, she knew. And instead of rage or gratitude or anything so simple, she had answered my deception with an instruction, which meant she had a plan of her own, which meant that while I had spent my night learning to write like her, she had spent hers learning something about the river.

We will speak. Two words, and my stomach dropped through the floor of the world like a coin through a grate.

Because here is the thing about loving someone who lies professionally: you celebrate when they catch yours, and you dread it in the same breath, because being seen through is a kind of nakedness, and Dominic sees through everything. She had read my forgery the way she reads coastlines—for the truth underneath the drawing. She knew I loved her. That was never in doubt and never the danger. The danger was that she knew I would do it again, and that knowing it, she had gone to the harbor anyway, alone, at dawn, to look at the water and find whatever I hadn't thought to hide from her.

I folded the posted list's corner over my finger, the way you keep a place in a book you intend to finish, and I went home to pack my mother's spoons properly this time, and to wait.

Night came down on Vireloup like a lid. And down by the river, I later learned, a lantern moved along the banks where no lantern had any business being—small, steady, patient—mapping the dark water for a way through that the lists and manifests and soldiers had not thought to close.

Whatever she found there, I intended to be standing beside her when she used it. Even if she had planned otherwise.

*Especially* if she had planned otherwise.
