## 13. Night Cartography

There is a particular kind of honesty that only comes out of paper after midnight. By daylight, a map is a document — it has opinions, it takes sides, it flatters the surveyor's paymaster. But work on it by moonlight, alone, with cold fingers and an unsteady lamp, and the map starts telling you things you didn't ask it to tell you.

I had been at the river chart for three hours when the Vireloup finally confessed.

Not the town. The river. Though lately I had trouble keeping the two separate in my mind, which is the sort of confusion that would have earned me a rapped knuckle from Master Aubertin. *A map is not the territory, girl,* he used to say, tapping my drawings with his pipe stem. *But neither is it innocent of it.* He said that about maps. He might as well have been saying it about me.

The confession was this: the checkpoint at the Pont Neuf — the one Captain Roche's men had thrown up with sandbags, a brazier, and the bureaucratic confidence of men who believe that geography obeys paperwork — sat where it sat because the old charts said the river narrowed there. And the old charts were wrong. Or rather, they were right for 1761, before the flood of '74 silted up the eastern channel and left the western one carrying most of the water. Every map in Aubertin's atlas showed the deep channel under the bridge, because every map had copied the same dead man's survey, and dead men are famously resistant to correction.

Which meant the soldiers were guarding the wrong water.

I sat back on my stool and looked at what I'd drawn, and felt the peculiar vertigo of a cartographer who has just discovered that her own profession has been lying to everyone, including her, including the Republic, including the dead master whose life's work she was betraying by inches.

* * *

Let me back up, because I have been accused — by Sylvie, mostly, in the tone she reserves for telling me things I already know — of explaining myself only halfway and then acting surprised when people misunderstand.

Three days ago I traded Roche a piece of the truth: the existence of the disused mill channel above the town, in exchange for the Marceaus' names moving up the evacuation list. It had felt like a betrayal and a rescue at once, which I was learning was simply what choices felt like now. What I had *not* told him — what I had barely admitted to myself — was that the mill channel wasn't the whole story. Aubertin's field notes mentioned a lock. The old grain lock at Chenelot, upstream, built to float barges down to the mills and abandoned when the mills burned in '81. Abandoned, but not destroyed. Nothing in Vireloup ever got properly destroyed; we just stopped looking at it.

If the lock still held water — if its gates could be made to hold, or even half-hold — then a second boat could come down behind the first, timed to pass the checkpoint while the guards were busy with the legitimate barge. Two boats through one inspection. It was either brilliant or suicidal, and the difference between those two things, I had noticed, tended to be about forty minutes and a great deal of luck.

So tonight I was going to find out which.

* * *

Getting out of the town after curfew is easier than it should be, if you are small, patient, and have spent six years being invisible on purpose. I went out through the tannery yard, where the smell covers everything including fear, and along the drainage path that appears on no official map because the surveyor in 1761 had refused to walk it. Aubertin had walked it. Aubertin had walked everything, which is why his field notes were worth more than the atlas itself, though I'd have let them burn me before I said so aloud.

The moon was three days off full and generous with it. The river ran silver and indifferent, doing what rivers do, which is carry on regardless — a quality I envied. Somewhere behind me the town slept badly; I could see the checkpoint brazier smoking on the bridge like a bad thought.

Chenelot lock announced itself the way abandoned things do: by being exactly where memory said it should be and slightly worse. The stone walls stood, furred with moss. The lower gate had rotted to lacework. The upper gate —

The upper gate was shut. Shut, and when I put my shoulder against the beam, it moved. Stiff, groaning, complaining in the voice of all neglected machinery, but moving. Someone had kept it greased. Recently enough that the grease smelled alive under the rot.

I stood very still in the moonlight and did the arithmetic nobody wants to do.

Nobody keeps a lock greased by accident. Somebody had been coming here. Somebody had looked at this same dead channel and seen the same thing I was seeing — a way past the checkpoint — and had been preparing it quietly, the way people in Vireloup prepared everything: without witnesses, without thanks, without saying a word that could get anyone hanged.

And there was really only one person in this town old enough to remember the grain barges, stubborn enough to care, and quiet enough to keep his own counsel about it.

Père Fournier.

* * *

I want to say something about Fournier, because the ledger lists him as *Fournier, Étienne, wheelwright, widower, no dependents*, and a ledger line is the cruelest kind of biography.

He sheltered me my first winter in Vireloup, before Aubertin took me in, when I was sleeping in the church porch and stealing bread with the competence of someone who had recently learned how. He never asked my name. He fed me soup that was mostly water and called it stew, and told me long stories about wheels — true ones, about how a wheel bears weight only at the point where it touches the ground, and how the rest of the spokes exist to make that one moment possible. I was fifteen and hungry and thought he was boring. I was fifteen and hungry and memorized every word.

*A wheel bears weight at one point,* he'd say. *Everything else is structure.*

I hadn't thought of that in years. Standing in the moonlight with my hand on a greased beam, I thought of nothing else.

* * *

Here is the terrible part, and I will set it down plainly because setting it down plainly is the only discipline I have left.

The lock works. I spent two hours proving it, hauling the windlass until my palms blistered, watching the black water rise in the chamber and fall again, and it works — barely, rustily, with a shriek that made me flinch toward the dark twice — but it works. A boat could pass. Two boats could pass, one through the main channel under the guards' noses, one through here, silent, downstream of the checkpoint before anyone thought to look.

But the gates cannot work themselves.

The upper gate needs a hand on the windlass to open it, and another hand to close it behind the boat, and the winch mechanism is manual — no gears clever enough to run themselves, no counterweight system, just iron, wood, and muscle. Whoever opened the gate for the escape boat would have to stay on the bank and work it, and then — this is the part I kept trying to make the arithmetic refuse — whoever stayed would be standing at a freshly operated lock at the exact moment the alarm went up, connected to the escape by every splinter of evidence, with a mile of open ground between them and anywhere safe.

One berth on the barge beyond the Marceaus' allocation. That was the number I'd been carrying around for days like a stone in my shoe. One berth: Sylvie, or Fournier. I had lain awake dividing myself between them like a loaf too small for the table.

And now the river had handed me a different sum entirely. Not one berth and two claimants. One lock and one operator. The lock could save everyone — and it would cost whoever worked it their place on any boat at all.

Hope, I discovered, has a price tag, and the price tag is always written in somebody else's name.

* * *

I sat on the lock wall for a long time after that, doing what cartographers do instead of weeping, which is drawing.

In my head, at first, and then — because my hands are traitors and always have been — on the scrap of paper I carry everywhere, the one with Aubertin's last marginal note inked on the back where no soldier would think to look. I sketched the channel. I sketched the timing: the legitimate barge passing the bridge at slack water, the second boat holding in the lock chamber, the gates, the mile of dark water below Chenelot where a hull would be invisible from the road. It was a good plan. It was, in fact, the best plan I had ever drawn, and every line of it required a person standing exactly where I could not bear to imagine a person standing.

I thought: I could do it. I could stay. I am the one who found the lock; the debt is mine; and besides, I am nobody — that has been my entire strategy for six years, my armor, my masterpiece. Nobody would miss Dominic Delacroix, apprentice, because Dominic Delacroix does not quite exist. The papers say so. I forged them myself.

Then I thought about the loose page hidden inside the atlas frame. The informant list. The names — including, if Vaillant's sneer meant what I feared it meant, names that touched my own history, my real family, the one I had spent a decade pretending began the day Master Aubertin found me. If I stayed behind at the lock, the atlas went nowhere, the page went nowhere, and everything Aubertin died protecting burned with the town.

And then I thought about Fournier's hands. Seventy years old, knuckles like walnut burls, hands that had greased this beam in secret because he had decided, in whatever silence old men decide such things, that other people were going to live.

He had already chosen his post. He simply hadn't told anyone, because telling would have made it real, and because — I understood this suddenly, with a pain like a door opening — he was waiting to see whether anyone else would figure it out. Whether anyone would come stand beside him. Whether the girl he'd fed through a winter of stolen bread had learned anything from all those stories about wheels.

* * *

The walk back into town took less time than the walk out, which is the way of walks home when you're carrying something heavy.

The checkpoint brazier had burned low. A sentry nodded at nothing. Above the roofs, the moon was beginning its slow slide west, and I thought about how many maps show the moon and none of them show what it costs.

I climbed through the tannery yard, up the stair, into the map room that smelled of ink and linseed and the ghost of my master's pipe. The atlas lay where I'd left it, open to the final coastline, the dedication still unsigned. I had been avoiding that dedication for weeks. Tonight I picked up the pen.

*For the people of Vireloup, who are not on any map.*

Master Aubertin would have hated it — too sentimental, insufficiently rigorous. Then again, Master Aubertin was dead, killed by Sergeant Vaillant for a page of names, and the dead can spare us their rigor.

I set the pen down without signing anything. There would be time for signatures later, or there wouldn't, and either way the ink deserved better than shaking hands.

Because here was the shape of it, plain as a contour line: tomorrow I would go to Fournier's workshop, and I would lay my map on his bench, and he would look at it and know that I knew, and one of us would say the thing out loud. And then the last arithmetic would begin — who rows, who stays, who tells Sylvie, who tells Roche, who carries the atlas, who carries the pages, who carries the blame.

A wheel bears weight at one point. Everything else is structure.

I blew out the lamp and stood in the dark listening to the river, a mile away and getting louder in my head by the hour, and somewhere downstream an old man was presumably asleep, or awake, oiling a windlass for a boat that didn't yet exist, for people who didn't yet know they'd need it.

Tomorrow, then. Tomorrow I would ask him the question I was most afraid to ask, which was not *will you do it* — I already knew the answer — but *how do I let you.*

Outside, far off, a dog barked once at the bridge and thought better of it. Even the dogs had learned discretion in Vireloup.

I slept, eventually, with the unfinished map under my pillow like a child with a talisman, and dreamed of water rising in a stone chamber, black and patient, lifting something light.
