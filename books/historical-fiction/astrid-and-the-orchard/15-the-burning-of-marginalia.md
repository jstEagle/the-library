## 15. The Burning of Marginalia

There is a particular smell to a town preparing to burn its own memory. It is not the smell of woodsmoke, which is honest and domestic and means somebody is baking bread. It is sharper, chemical almost—the smell of ink releasing itself from paper, of glue surrendering in bindings, of vellum curling like a hand withdrawing from a handshake. I had smelled it once before, in Master Aubertin's workshop, when a lamp guttered too near a shelf of drafts and we lost six years of coastal surveys in eleven minutes. He had stood there watching the flames eat his careful soundings and said, very quietly, *well, the sea will still be there tomorrow.* Then he had sat down and redrawn all six years from memory in nine weeks, because the sea would be there but his measurements would not.

I thought of him that morning as the soldiers stacked documents in the market square like cordwood, and I confess my first thought was not noble. My first thought was: *they had better not have found the atlas.*

* * *

The proclamation had been nailed to the church door at dawn, which struck me as a small theological joke—announcing the destruction of paper on the one surface in Vireloup guaranteed to survive anything. BY ORDER OF THE COMMITTEE OF PUBLIC SAFETY, it read, ALL WRITINGS OF A SEDITIOUS OR COUNTER-REVOLUTIONARY CHARACTER SHALL BE SURRENDERED AND PUBLICLY CONSUMED. *Consumed.* Someone in the Committee had a taste for the elegant word. I wondered if he slept well.

By eight o'clock the square held three barrels of confiscated papers, a squad of soldiers with bored faces, a pyre already laid in the fountain basin, and half the town pretending not to watch while watching with the intensity of people memorizing a face for later identification. Sergeant Vaillant presided over the barrels with the contentment of a man at last given work suited to his temperament. He wore the tricolor cockade as though it were a decoration for valor rather than an issue item, and he smiled at the crowd the way a dog smiles at a meat safe.

"Citizens," he called. "The Republic cleanses itself. No longer shall the old names strangle the new France in their ledgers!"

He had, I noted, used the word *ledgers*. Not *papers*. Not *writings*. Ledgers. Singular in the possessive dark of his mouth, plural in my stomach.

Here I should explain what I had done two nights prior, because I have discovered that guilt arrives late with me—it sends an envoy ahead, but the full delegation takes days. Two nights prior, after the requisition, after Captain Roche's men had carried off the town ledger like a captured standard, I had gone back to the records room under cover of fetching my master's drafting instruments, which was even technically true. The room had been searched, roughly, by men who understood seizure but not order. Papers lay everywhere in drifts, and among them, loose from the ledger's binding where some soldier's impatient hand had cracked the spine, perhaps thirty or forty pages that had once belonged to Master Aubertin's great census of Vireloup. Household by household. Debt by debt. Name by true name.

I had meant to take only one page. I had told myself this with complete sincerity, standing in the dark with my heart going like a mill wheel. One page, the one that mattered, the forged record of one Delacroix, apprentice. But my hands, which Master Aubertin had trained to precision, apparently had their own ideas about what constituted a complete collection, and when I came out of the records room my coat was lined with paper from collarbone to hip, and I was, strictly speaking, the most seditious document in the department.

So when Vaillant said *ledgers*, I felt every sheet against my ribs like thirty-nine small accusations.

* * *

The burning itself was conducted with revolutionary thoroughness and practical incompetence, which is to say they lit the pyre before checking whether the wind favored them, and the smoke went sideways into the café terrace where Madame Brissot served coffee to soldiers who had requisitioned her husband's boat. There is a God of Small Injustices and she has a sense of humor.

They fed the fire in armfuls. Ledgers first—the parish registers, the guild rolls, a merchant's book of accounts whose owner stood in the crowd making a noise like a man watching his own amputation. Then letters. Then, heartbreakingly, a crate of school primers someone had surrendered out of terror or stupidity, so that the children of Vireloup gathered to watch their own catechisms and copybooks go up in flame, and one small boy began to cry, and a soldier cuffed him—not hard, more a gesture—and told him to stop sniveling over trash.

I stood at the edge of the square with my hands folded and my face arranged into the mild blankness of a working man waiting for nothing in particular. This is a skill. Master Aubertin taught me that a mapmaker learns to be furniture; you survey best when no one remembers you arrived. For four years I had been furniture. Furniture did not feel its heartbeat in its teeth. Furniture did not calculate, with a cold spinning arithmetic, exactly how long it would take a sergeant with good eyes to notice that one apprentice in the crowd was built wrong for his coat, or that his pockets made the wrong silhouette.

And then Père Fournier did something either very brave or very foolish, and being Fournier, it was both.

The old priest—or former priest, the Convention having married him to a widow in some ceremony of civic correction, an arrangement both parties treated as a bad joke with excellent housing—stepped forward from the crowd holding a book. It was small, leather-bound, fat with use. His breviary, I realized. Or what had been his breviary before his enforced retirement from the trade of prayer. He walked to the pyre and held the book out over the flames himself.

"I'll do it," he said, in that cracked, unhurried voice that had talked me through the worst winter of my life over bowls of thin soup. "If it must burn, let the hand that sinned carry it. That's fairer."

Vaillant looked at him for a long moment. I watched the calculation move behind his eyes like something swimming under ice: here was an old man offering submission in a form that was actually defiance, dressed as piety, deniable as theater. Arrest him for surrendering his own book? Charge him with the crime of performing his own punishment? The paperwork alone would be humiliating.

"Burn it then, citizen," Vaillant said at last, with a smile like a filed edge, and turned away to supervise another barrel.

Fournier dropped the breviary into the fire. Then he stood there, hands folded, watching it, and I swear on everything I have ever drawn falsely that the old fox slipped two fingers into his sleeve and I saw the sleeve hang heavy afterward. Whether it was the breviary's ribbon or a handful of loose pages fished from the pyre's edge I could not say from where I stood. But his eyes met mine across the square, one conspirator acknowledging another, and the wink he gave me was so outrageously unrepentant that I nearly laughed aloud, which would have killed us both.

It was the laughing that undid my caution. Because when I bit it back, my breath came short, and my hand went instinctively to my coat—to press flat the pages, to still their rustle—and across the square, on the church steps, Captain Roche was watching my hands.

* * *

I want to describe Roche accurately, because accuracy is the only religion I have left. Roche was not cruel. I have since catalogued cruelty with professional thoroughness and can certify he did not keep stock. He was worse in a way, or better, depending on how much you value your life: he was *attentive*. He had the mapmaker's disease without the mapmaker's training—he noticed patterns, he remembered positions, he drew conclusions. From the day he took the town he had watched me the way one watches weather on a horizon: not yet dangerous, but organized.

Now he came down the church steps and crossed the square toward me, unhurried, gloved hands behind his back, and every step closed a distance I could not afford.

"You're Aubertin's apprentice." Not a question. "Delacroix."

"Captain." I bowed the correct amount for a tradesman to an officer, eyes lowered, spine screaming.

"The atlas. The master's atlas of the river approaches." He stopped an arm's length away, close enough that I could see the powder burns the Republic's powder leaves, close enough that he could hear what my breathing was doing. "Command has classified it strategic material. It will be collected. Where is it?"

"Where you put it, Captain," I said. "Your men took it with the ledger. Locked in the records room, unless it's been moved."

Something moved through his face—satisfaction at the answer, irritation at its correctness, and beneath both, a flicker of the thing I would spend the next week trying to name. "It will be inventoried properly," he said. "My clerk complains the requisition was done by savages. Pages loose everywhere."

"Soldiers aren't archivists," I said, and heard only afterward how it sounded.

"No," Roche agreed mildly. "Nor apprentices." He looked at my coat. Not at my face—at my coat, at the line of it, with the gaze of a man who had supervised the searching of two hundred bodies and knew what contraband did to tailoring. The square crackled. Somewhere behind him a soldier kicked a burning log back into the basin and sparks went up like startled birds.

"Those papers are worth a man's neck," Roche said quietly. "Whoever carries them."

"Whose papers, Captain?"

"That's precisely the question I've been asking since Vireloup," he said, and there it was again, that flicker, and I finally identified it: it was doubt. He was a man doubting his own cargo. "The ledger lists loyalties. Names of who informed, who sheltered, who prayed in the wrong direction. Do you know what happens to a town when such a book exists?"

"It gets emptied," I said. "Barge by barge."

"Yes." He said it without flinching, which I hated him for, and without appetite, which confused me. "Well. Mind your pockets, Delacroix. The wind today is treacherous." And he turned and walked back toward the pyre, leaving me standing in the smoke with my heart trying to climb out through my collar.

* * *

He knew. I want to be exact about this, because for years afterward I told myself he hadn't known, and lying to oneself is the one forgery I refuse to practice anymore. He knew. He had watched my hand go to my coat, he had seen the silhouette, he had heard the fear in my voice, and he had named the danger to my face—*the wind today is treacherous*—and then he had turned his back on me and walked away.

Why? I spent that evening in the workshop with the shutters barred, taking the rescued pages out one by one and laying them on the drafting table in the lamplight, and I interrogated the question the way I'd interrogate a suspect coastline. Options presented themselves. He wanted me to lead him to the rest of the ledger's secrets; possible, but he could simply have arrested me. He disapproved of the burning and needed someone to smuggle history out on his behalf; flattering, unverifiable. Or—and this was the option I kept returning to, the one that fit the flicker in his face—Roche had begun to suspect that the paperwork he served did not serve anyone but itself, and a man in that position starts looking for hands other than his own to do the things his conscience is learning to flinch from.

An enemy who looks away is not an ally. I know that. But he is also not merely an enemy anymore, and in the arithmetic I was learning to run—that cold survival sums, debits and credits of living people against dead principles—an uncertain quantity is worth more than a certain subtraction.

Among the pages spread on my table that night were the Marceaus' entry, with Sylvie's mother's maiden name recorded in my dead master's upright hand, and the note in the margin—*sheltered refractory priest, 1791; fed the Aubertin household through the scarcity; loyal to persons, not factions*—which Master Aubertin had written as praise and which the Convention would read as a triple condemnation. There was the Fournier entry. There were dozens more, neighbors whose kindnesses and crimes and ordinary tangled lives were now, by decree, kindling.

And there was the page. Mine. *Dominic Delacroix,* it said, in ink I had helped grind, *apprentice and nephew of the master; papers forged at his instruction, 1789; born —* and then the name I had not read until three nights ago, the name that had knocked the floor out from under me in the dark of the records room, the name belonging to a family I had never known and that the ledger insisted existed, somewhere, traceable, findable by any soldier who could read.

I sat with these pages until the candle failed. Outside, the pyre in the fountain basin had burned down to a red eye that would smolder for days, the way such fires do, patient as a grudge. Somewhere in that ash were the marginalia of a hundred lives, and somewhere in my coat lining were forty pages that had escaped it, and somewhere across town a captain had committed, with a single turn of his back, an act of treason so quiet that only two people in Vireloup would ever know it happened.

I should have felt safe. Instead I felt the specific vertigo of a cartographer who discovers mid-survey that the ground is still moving.

Because there was one thing about that afternoon I had not yet told myself plainly, and it forced its way up now, in the dark, with the truth: Vaillant had not watched the fire. All through the burning, all through Fournier's theater and Roche's slow promenade, the sergeant's eyes had been on the crowd, moving face to face, patient as a bailiff—searching, I now understood, for a particular silhouette. A coat that hung wrong. An apprentice who stood too still.

He had not found me. Yet. But he had been looking, and Vaillant did not stop looking for things; he was the sort of man who found them eventually, the way water finds the crack.

I gathered the pages, wrapped them in oilcloth, and hid them where I hide everything—in plain sight, sewn into the backing of a framed map of the Loire estuary that hung in the workshop, worthless to looters, priceless to me. As I drove the last tack I heard, far off toward the barracks, the sound of a door slamming and boots in the street, and then—a smaller sound, easy to miss—voices arguing low outside the records room. One of them was Vaillant's.

The other was lighter, clipped, official.

Roche's.

And whatever they wanted from that room tonight, I doubted it was the atlas.
