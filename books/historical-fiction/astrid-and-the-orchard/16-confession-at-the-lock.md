## 16. Confession at the Lock

*Dominic*

I had told myself a great many lies in my life, most of them written in a fine, steady hand on good rag paper. Forgeries are only lies that have been practiced. But the lie I had lived the longest was not on paper at all: it was the quiet, daily fiction that Sylvie Marceau was simply a neighbor's daughter to me, a friend of circumstance, a girl whose family had happened to feed me through two winters because the Marceaus were the sort of people who fed people, and not because they had chosen *me*, specifically, stubbornly, over their own flour.

The lock was half a mile downriver from the checkpoint, where the channel narrowed between old stone walls furred with moss. I had found it by moonlight two nights before and told no one but myself, which is to say I had drawn it, which for me amounts to the same thing. Now I stood on the towpath in the blue hour before dawn with my collar up and my breath showing, waiting for Sylvie, who had left a chalk mark on my shutter that meant *come, alone, now*—a code we had invented at fourteen for passing along gossip and had never expected to use for anything that made my hands shake.

She came along the towpath with her hood back despite the cold, which was like her. Sylvie has never once in her life dressed appropriately for the weather or the century. Her mother's shawl was around her shoulders, and her chin was set at the angle she inherited from her father, the one that preceded either an argument or an embrace.

"You found it," she said, nodding at the lock.

"I found it."

"Then you already know what I did."

That stopped me. I had rehearsed this conversation on the walk down—or rather, I had rehearsed the version where I confessed and forgave myself in advance and she wept with gratitude. It is astonishing how often the rehearsal fails to survive contact with the actual person.

"What did you do?" I asked, and my voice came out colder than I intended, which was cowardice wearing bluntness as a coat.

Sylvie looked at me for a long moment. The river said what rivers say, which is nothing and everything, water worrying stone.

"Père Fournier," she said. "The berth. You were going to give it to me. And I gave it to him."

* * *

*Sylvie*

Here is what nobody tells you about forging a signature: it takes longer to decide than to do. I had watched Dominic practice hands the way other girls practice dance steps—the clerk's crabbed italic, the notary's flourish, the soldier's blunt scrawl—and I thought I knew something about it from watching. I didn't. What I knew, sitting in our kitchen at midnight with the altered berth list under my fingers and Mama's silver already traded for the transit pass, was that Dominic's hand was the easiest thing in the world to copy, because she draws letters the way she draws coastlines: precisely, beautifully, and holding herself always just outside the ink.

I signed her name giving my berth to Père Fournier, and then I sat there feeling clever, and then I sat there feeling sick, and then Maman came down for water and saw my face and asked no questions, because Marceaus ask questions only when the answer will not break them.

I will say this for my family: we are terrible liars individually and magnificent ones collectively. When Papa noticed the list had changed, I said the records office had made an error in our favor, and he looked at me the way he looks at a bill he intends to dispute later, and said nothing.

But Dominic—Dominic I could not lie to, which I realized only when I tried. She was standing on the towpath with her arms crossed and her jaw doing that thing, and I could see her assembling the truth out of me sentence by sentence the way she builds a map out of bearings, and I understood that I had never once successfully hidden anything from her, and that she had been hiding things from me for six years, and that I was furious about both.

"You should have taken it," she said. "You and your parents are marked. Fournier is old. He's half-deaf. He *wanted* to stay, he told the requisition officer so to his face—"

"Yes, and how long do you think a deaf old man who contradicts soldiers to their faces lasts in a town run by Vaillant?" I heard my own voice go sharp and was too angry to be embarrassed. "You were going to spend the berth on me. On me, Dominic, when you could have bought Papa's passage outright with whatever it is you're hiding in that map room, and don't tell me there's nothing, I've seen you come out of there looking like you'd swallowed the ledger whole."

"That's different—"

"Everything is different with you. That's the whole trouble." I stepped closer. The lock chuckled behind us, water finding its level, patient as arithmetic. "Six years, Dominic. Six years of bread at our table and never once—"

"Never once *what?*" And there it was, cracking open in her voice, all the things she'd been keeping behind her teeth.

"Never once telling me the truth about anything that mattered."

* * *

*Dominic*

There is a technique in cartography called breaking the graticule—deliberately distorting the frame so that a hidden region can be folded out of view. Master Aubertin taught it to me in my second year, and I remember thinking it was dishonest, and him laughing and saying *all maps lie, girl; the good ones choose their lies*. I had been choosing my lies for six years and calling them mercy.

"The truth," I said, and heard how ridiculous the word sounded in my mouth, "is that I am not who my papers say."

Sylvie waited. She has always been better at silence than I am; she simply stands in it like weather.

"My name isn't Delacroix," I said. "It never was. I came through Vireloup in '88 with nothing but a stolen coat and a stomachache of fear, and Master Aubertin caught me stealing bread from his kitchen—not buying, stealing, let's have that right since we're being honest—and instead of turning me in he sat me down and fed me and asked me my name. My real one. And I wouldn't give it, because the people I was running from—" My throat closed. I hadn't planned on that part. "There are men who would know my family's name, Sylvie. Not because we did anything. Because of what we *were*, and whose side of an argument older than any of us my grandfather happened to be born on. So the master made me a new one. Drew me up papers like a coastline he invented—plausible, defensible, empty. Nephew of Aubertin the mapmaker. A boy, even, because a boy travels safer and asks fewer questions, though God knows I've never managed the second part."

The lock gate groaned. Somewhere upstream a night bird gave up on song.

"He forged those papers himself?" Sylvie asked quietly.

"He drew them the way he drew everything. Beautifully. He said—" and here my eyes stung, because grief ambushes you in the strangest doorways, "he said a name was just a projection, that every projection flattens something true, and the trick was choosing which truths to keep. I thought he was talking about cartography. He wasn't. He was talking about me."

"And you let me believe you were his nephew. All this time."

"You never believed it," I said. "Don't pretend otherwise. You called me by my initials when we were fifteen because you said Dominic suited me better than any boy's name, and you knew, Sylvie, you've known since the winter my beard wouldn't come in, you just had the decency not to say."

"Decency," she repeated, and laughed, and it was not a kind laugh. "Is that what you call it? I called it waiting. I decided that if you ever wanted to be known, truly known, you would tell me, and I would be there when you did. I have been *waiting*, Dominic, like a fool at a station where the train stopped running years ago. And meanwhile you spent your waiting on—forgive me—on cartography. On a berth list. On deciding, alone, in a locked room, who in my family lives."

"That's not fair."

"No," she said. "It isn't. Nothing about this is fair. But it is true, and you taught me that names are not neutral, so let me tell you what yours did when I read it on that altered list. It told me that you love us. It also told me that you think love is something you can draft in private and present finished, like a map, with all the dangerous country folded out of sight. And Dominic—I would rather have your ugly, unfinished, badly-drawn truth than your beautiful mercies. Every time. Do you understand me?"

I understood her. It was the understanding that hurt.

"So now you know," I said. "Delphine. My name is Delphine. There—it's said. It's in the air. It can't be un-said." My hands were trembling and I pressed them flat against the cold stone of the lock wall, as if steadying myself against a chart table. "And there is more, worse, and you may hate me after, but you'll have it honestly. The page I pulled from the ledger—the one Roche hasn't found—it lists informants. Men who sold names to the Committee. And my family's name is on it. Not as informants. As the *sold*. Aubertin died keeping that page out of certain hands, and I have been carrying it around like a stone in my chest, because it means the master didn't die of his heart failing after all, and it means my name is worth killing for twice over."

* * *

*Sylvie*

I want to record, because someone should, that my first feeling was not pity. It was rage—hot, childish, glorious rage—that she had carried all of it alone, that she had thought us so fragile, that she had spent six years deciding on our behalf what we could bear. The Marceaus survived two revolutions and a bad harvest by never letting anyone else do our arithmetic. And here was this girl—we had fed her, we had *made* her ours whether she signed anything or not—rationing herself out to us in safe portions.

"You absolute mule," I said, which in our family is a term of endearment, and grabbed her by the shoulders. "You were going to hand me that berth, sign my life into safety with a stolen name, and die inside without ever once asking to be rescued back."

"There was nothing to rescue—"

"*Everything,*" I said. "Everything, you mule. You don't get to be the only one who bleeds quietly. That's not family. Family is bleeding loudly together where everyone can complain about the mess."

And then she laughed—wet, cracked, real—and then I was crying, and then we were both of us holding onto each other on the freezing towpath like two girls half our age, and the lock went on muttering its one syllable, and I thought: *this*. This is what she has been drawing around the edges of every map. The territory she refused to survey because she was afraid of what she'd find named there.

Us. She would have found us.

When we finally stepped apart, wiping our faces with undignified sleeves, she said, "Fournier keeps the berth. I won't take it back, so don't argue."

"I wasn't going to argue."

"You were absolutely going to argue."

"Well," I admitted, "yes. But I've decided to be gracious instead, and you'll never know how much it costs me." Then, because honesty is apparently contagious and I had caught it bad: "The pass cost Mama's silver. The christening spoons. She insisted."

Dominic closed her eyes. "Tell her I'll forge her a replacement set. In silver leaf. Better than the originals."

"She'll hold you to that."

"Good," she said, and I heard the whole future in it—small, domestic, absurdly hopeful. "Let her hold me to things. I'm done being held by nothing."

We turned back up the towpath, shoulder to shoulder, and it was there, walking, that she stopped so suddenly I overshot her by three steps. The light was coming up gray behind the checkpoint tower, and her face had gone the color of old paper.

"Sylvie," she said. "If I can alter a berth list and you can forge my hand in an evening—then so can someone else. Someone with access, and a reason, and worse intentions." She was already moving, fast, toward the town. "Vaillant has been in that records room twice this week. And last night the manifest was on the desk, and I *left it there*, because I trusted the room—"

"Then we trust faster," I said, and ran beside her.

Behind us the lock settled back into its murmur, keeping our confession the way water keeps everything: briefly, deeply, and ready to carry it downstream.
