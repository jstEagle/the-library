## 19. Water Rising

*Dominic*

There is a particular kind of silence that comes before a thing goes wrong — not the absence of sound but the presence of held breath, as if the whole world were a map waiting for its ink. I had heard it once before, the night Master Aubertin died, and I heard it now standing at the rail of a leaking barge called *La Colombe*, which had been a wine barge in happier years and was now carrying eleven human beings toward a checkpoint that wanted very much to kill them.

The Marceaus were below decks with the other families, because I had insisted, and Sylvie had obeyed me for the first time in her life, which should have warned me something was coming. Père Fournier was not aboard. He was two miles upriver, at the old lock, where he had been since sundown, and every minute that passed without word from him was a minute my hands wanted to shake.

I did not let them shake. A cartographer's hands do not shake; they are instruments, and instruments hold steady even when the person holding them is quietly terrified. This was the first useful thing Aubertin ever taught me, though he'd framed it differently: *A line drawn in fear is a lie, Dominic. The paper deserves better than your cowardice.* He'd been talking about coastlines. He would have been appalled to learn how much of his advice applied to smuggling.

Roche's patrol boats sat at the checkpoint like fat black beetles on the water, lanterns swinging, and beyond them the river ran silver-black toward free water and the sea and everything the Republic could not follow you into. One barge was expected tonight. The manifest said so. The manifest, thanks to Roche's pen and my forgery and Sylvie's worse forgery, said a great many things, only one of which was true.

*La Colombe* was the true one. The second boat — old Tomas's skiff, riding low with nine more souls — was supposed to come through the disused lock after we passed, once Fournier opened it from the far bank, manually, alone, an eighty-year-old man wrestling ironwork that hadn't moved since the last king of France was still a citizen.

It was a mad plan. I want that recorded somewhere. When they write the history of this night — and someone will, because history is just a ledger that outlives its keepers — I want it written that I knew it was madness and went anyway, so that no one can call me brave by accident.

The checkpoint lanterns grew larger. The held breath of the world grew longer.

I thought of the atlas, wrapped in oilcloth under the false floor of the hold, and of the loose ledger pages tucked inside its binding — every true name in Vireloup, mine among them, rescued from a fire that was still, even now, probably sending up smoke over the archive square. I had traded my master's life's work to save a family I'd spent six years pretending wasn't mine. Tonight the trade came due.

The river carried us forward whether I was ready or not. Rivers are like that. So are histories.

* * *

*Fournier*

Old bones have one advantage over young ones: they have already made their peace with most outcomes. When Dominic had come to me three nights ago with her moonlight maps and her terrible plan, she had knelt in my kitchen like a girl asking forgiveness and laid it all out — the lock, the mechanism, the need for hands that knew iron. She had offered me a berth instead. Safe passage, warm boat, done with all of it.

I had told her what I will tell anyone who asks: I am too old to start over anywhere new. But I am not too old to open a door.

The lock house smelled of rust and river weed and sixty years of my own pipe smoke. The mechanism was worse than I remembered — the great gears furred with moss, the windlass seized like a fist that had forgotten how to open. I had spent two nights oiling it with tallow and swearing at it in three languages, two of them dead.

Now I stood at the crank and waited for the signal: a lantern shown twice from the water below the checkpoint bend. Twice means the first barge has passed clean. Once means run, old man, run and don't look back.

I thought about Marguerite while I waited. Forty years dead, and still the woman kept me company better than the living did. She'd have called this whole business foolhardy and then she'd have come down to hold the lantern herself. That was the marriage. You called each other fools and then you helped.

Downriver, through the trees, I saw the lanterns of the checkpoint swing. I saw a small light move along the water — the barge, low and slow, playing the part of a tired wine boat with nothing in its belly but dregs.

Once. The light blinked once—

No. It blinked twice. Then once more. Twice-and-once, which was not any signal we had agreed upon, which meant either that the plan had changed or that the plan had gone wrong, and from an eighty-year-old man's position both possibilities looked identical: dark water, cold iron, and a choice to be made alone.

I put my shoulder to the windlass.

* * *

*Sylvie*

Below decks, in the dark, my mother held my hand too tightly and pretended she wasn't afraid, which is a thing mothers do instead of praying. Papa counted the creaks of the hull like a miser counting coins. Somewhere above us, Dominic walked the deck, and I lay in the dark hating her and loving her in the same breath, which is also a thing families do.

She didn't know yet. What I'd done with the list. I had crossed out my own name and written hers in — no, that isn't honest, even in my own head. I had forged *her* signature giving the berth to Père Fournier, because she never would have taken it herself, because she is the sort of person who would drown politely rather than inconvenience anyone, and because someone in this family had to be selfish on her behalf.

That was before I learned there was a lock, and a second boat, and an old man with a windlass. Before the arithmetic changed.

Because here is what I understood, lying in the hold of *La Colombe* while the checkpoint lanterns swept past overhead like slow yellow eyes: the lock needed someone to close it again behind us, or the soldiers would know within minutes, and they would follow, and none of us would reach the sea. And Père Fournier, God love him, was going to stay to work that iron until we were through, and he was going to be caught doing it, and the berth I had stolen for him — the berth Dominic had given him — was going to be wasted on a man who had already decided how his story ended.

Unless.

I kissed my mother's hand and lied to her about needing air.

On deck, Dominic stood at the stern like a figurehead carved out of guilt. I came up beside her and watched the checkpoint fall behind us, lantern by lantern, and when the last of them was a smudge on the water I said, quietly, "How long between the barge passing and the lock opening?"

"Ten minutes. Maybe twelve." Her eyes never left the dark bend of the river. "Why?"

"Because I'm getting off at the mill landing."

Her head turned so fast I heard her neck protest. "You are not."

"Fournier can't close that lock alone and get clear. Someone has to take the crank off his hands while he runs for the skiff. That was always the flaw in your beautiful map, Dominic. You planned the way in and forgot the way back."

"I planned—" She stopped. I watched her do the same arithmetic I'd done below decks, and I watched it come out the same. "The soldiers will sweep the towpath within the hour."

"Then don't waste the hour arguing." I took her hand. Her fingers were ice and iron. "You got us out. Let me be the one who closes the door."

For a moment she looked at me the way she used to look at blank paper — like something enormous was trying to fit itself into a space too small for it. Then the barge's bell rang once, soft, the signal for the mill landing, and the moment drowned.

"You come back," she said. "Sylvie. Whatever happens — you come back to this boat or you come back to me. Those are the only two directions allowed."

"I'll aim for one of them," I said, and dropped over the side into the shallows, and the cold of the river closed around my ankles like a hand.

* * *

*Roche*

I stood on the checkpoint platform with a manifest in my hand and perjury in my heart, watching the wine barge slide past my own patrol lanterns, and I confess I felt nothing so refined as conscience. What I felt was the specific nausea of a man who has discovered that paperwork is a weapon and has already fired it.

The manifest said one barge. The manifest said thirty-one souls bound for the interior, names checked against the ledger, signatures in order. The manifest was, in the technical sense, a masterpiece. Vaillant had signed it without reading it. Command would sign anything put in front of them. Only I knew that four of those names had been added in ink I had mixed myself, and three erased, and that the erasures were the point.

"Boat's clean," Sergeant Brel said beside me. "Wine dregs and ballast, by the smell."

"Let it pass," I said, and my voice did not shake, which surprised me.

But something else did not pass. Something was wrong with the night itself. I have served in this valley long enough to know its sounds — the river's mutter, the frogs' idiot parliament, the wind in the poplars. And beneath all of it tonight there was a rhythm that did not belong: a groan, distant, metallic, rhythmic. Like a great joint being forced to move after years of sleep.

Toward the old lock.

I knew the lock. Every officer did. It had been sealed since '89, silted, useless, marked on no working chart. Which meant that whoever was turning that windlass was working from a chart that shouldn't exist — from *the* chart, the atlas command had ordered me to confiscate weeks ago and which I had reported as damaged beyond transport value while it sat under my own floorboards.

Vaillant appeared at my elbow the way he always did, as though the darkness had coughed him up. He was listening too. I watched his head tilt, wolfish, toward the sound.

"The old lock," he said. "Someone's running cargo through it."

"It's silted shut," I said. "Has been for years."

"Then someone's un-silting it." He turned to his men. "Six of you. Lanterns. Towpath. Now."

And here is the thing I will have to answer for, someday, to somebody: I gave the order to half his squad instead. "Brel, take two men, walk the towpath, report anything unusual." Two men, moving slowly, on the longest route available. Vaillant looked at me, and I looked at the river, and neither of us said what we both knew.

He took his six anyway, fast, down the eastern path.

The groan of the lock rolled across the water again, and this time I could hear beneath it — faint, absurd, unmistakable — an old man singing. Some harvest song, half-forgotten, pitched to carry over the noise of iron. Singing so that whoever was coming through would know the door was open.

I stood on my platform, holding my beautiful criminal manifest, and listened to an old man sing the Republic a lullaby it would not thank him for.

* * *

*Dominic*

The skiff came through the open gates of the lock like a breath released after six years of holding — nine people who had been furniture in their own houses for months, suddenly moving, suddenly *going somewhere*. Old Tomas poled her through the foam with tears running openly into his beard, and I counted heads from the stern of *La Colombe* like a miser: one, two, three — all of them, all nine, plus Fournier's shape at the windlass, still turning, still singing, closing the great gates behind them so the soldiers would find nothing but sealed stone and silted quiet.

Twelve minutes. Roche had promised me twelve minutes of blind eyes at the checkpoint, and he had delivered, and I would spend the rest of my life deciding what that made us to each other.

Then torches. Eastern towpath. Six of them, moving fast, and the singing stopped mid-verse.

What happened next happened in the grammar of nightmares — everything true and nothing in order. Fournier stepping back from the windlass with his hands raised, unhurried, almost courteous. The torches converging. A voice — Vaillant's, that voice like a file drawn across iron — shouting questions nobody intended to answer. And a smaller figure dropping from the mill landing path onto the lock's upper gate, hands going for the crank, and I knew that silhouette the way I knew my own signature, because I had taught it to write.

"Sylvie!" I screamed it across the water and the water ate it.

She reached the windlass. Fournier shouted something — I couldn't hear it, but I saw him wave her off, wave her *away*, toward the reeds, toward the towpath south, anywhere — and she waved back and kept cranking, finishing what he'd started, sealing the gates, buying the skiff its head start, and the soldiers were twenty yards off, ten, and the last tooth of the gear engaged with a clang I felt in my teeth from half a mile away.

The skiff shot downstream past us, low and silent and full of the saved.

The lock went dark behind them. The torches closed around it like a fist.

I stood at the stern of *La Colombe* with the current pulling us toward the sea and every instinct I owned screaming at me to turn the boat, and I did not turn the boat, because turning the boat would drown forty-one people to chase two, and Aubertin's ghost leaned on my shoulder and said what he always said: *the paper deserves better than your cowardice,* and for the first time in my life I hated him for it.

Fournier was a shape between torches. Sylvie was a shape running south along the towpath, quick and low, and for one impossible heartbeat I let myself believe in the geometry of escape — she was faster than them, the reeds were thick, the dark was deep—

A torch flared. A whistle cut the night. The shapes converged.

And *La Colombe* carried me onward, downriver, toward free water, holding two empty berths and every name I'd managed to save and the two names I hadn't, and the last thing I saw of Vireloup was the lock house window, where some soldier had lit a lamp, throwing one small square of light onto the black water like a page torn from a book and left floating.

They had Fournier. They had Sylvie. And I was alive, and free, and carrying proof of both, and tomorrow — tomorrow I would go back through hell with it.

*La Colombe* slid past the last bend, and Vireloup went out like a snuffed candle, and I turned my face downriver because a cartographer's hands do not shake.

Mine shook anyway.
