## 20. The Cost of the Crossing

I have read that grief and joy are opposites, which is the sort of thing written by people who have never stood on a riverbank at dawn with a dead man's pages in one hand and a living family's future in the other. They are not opposites. They are two inks on the same pen, and you cannot use one without staining your fingers with both.

The barge went through the lock at half past three in the morning. I know because I counted the strokes of the clock at Saint-Aubin across the water — a habit Aubertin beat into me during my first year, *a cartographer who cannot keep time cannot keep bearings, Dominic* — and because I could do nothing else with my hands but count while Fournier's silhouette bent over the lock mechanism like a man arguing with it.

He won the argument. The gates groaned open, the barge slid through carrying Sylvie and her mother and father and eleven other households Vireloup would never see again, and I stood in the reeds with Roche's borrowed lantern shuttered to a coin of light and watched free water take them.

Sylvie did not look back. I had told her not to. *If you turn around, they'll know which of us matters*, I'd said, as though love were contraband to be smuggled past a checkpoint, and perhaps it is, at that.

Then the shouting started upstream, and I stopped being a cartographer and became only a girl in the mud, counting clock strokes that had nothing left to measure.

* * *

They took Père Fournier at the lock itself.

I learned this the way one learns most terrible things: secondhand, in fragments, hours too late for any of it to matter. A fisherman who owed his mooring rights to no one's politics rowed past our hiding place at first light and told us — told Roche, technically, though I was standing close enough to be included in the geography of it — that the soldiers had found an old man at the sluice gate, soaked to the waist, working the crank with both hands like a man closing his shop for the night.

"He didn't run," the fisherman said. "That's the strange part. Could've gone over the weir, maybe. He just stood there when they came. Put his hands out."

Put his hands out. As if he were asking to hold something. As if eighty years of living had taught him that what comes for you should at least be met halfway.

Roche said nothing. He stood at the water's edge in his captain's coat, which looked wrong on him in daylight, the way a signature looks wrong on a document you haven't agreed to, and he watched the lock where the second boat's wake was still flattening into nothing. I watched him watch it, and I understood that whatever happened next between us would happen without witnesses or paperwork, which is the only kind of transaction left worth anything in France this year.

"They'll want names," I said quietly.

"They'll want the man who altered the manifest," Roche said. "Names are secondary. Names are always secondary." He glanced at me then, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen before — not softness exactly; I don't think Captain Roche was built with softness any more than a bridge is built with it — but load-bearing. The expression of a structure deciding how much weight it can hold. "You were never on the manifest, apprentice. Neither was I, in the sense that matters. Remember that."

It was the closest thing to absolution a bureaucrat has ever issued, and I took it, because I am not proud.

* * *

Vaillant reported the escape by noon.

I know because Roche told me, later, in the map room, with the shutters closed and the atlas open between us like a patient on a table. Vaillant wrote it up in his neat sergeant's hand: unauthorized vessel through the eastern lock, suspected collusion of local personnel, recommendation of full inquiry. Three lines. Vaillant believed in three lines the way other men believe in God — sufficient unto themselves, requiring no further revelation.

And beneath those three lines, Roche had written his own report. I saw it before he filed it away; he made no particular effort to hide it from me, which was its own message. *Investigation conducted. Lock found unattended and rusted through; passage determined accidental, likely flood debris displacing the gate mechanism. No evidence of collusion. No further action recommended.*

Flood debris. I thought of old Fournier's hands on that crank, hands that had mended nets and buried two wives and once carried a starving runaway girl on his shoulders for three miles when her feet gave out on the road to Rouen. Flood debris.

"You're lying in an official document," I said. It came out less like an accusation than I intended. More like wonder.

"I'm filing a report," Roche said. "Reports are what actually happened, after the part that actually happened has been weighed against what can survive happening." He blotted it, squared it, set it in the dispatch tray with the small finality of a man closing a door on a room he will never enter again. "Vaillant knows the truth. Vaillant always knows the truth. That is his entire talent. But his version requires proof, and proof requires the inquiry he cannot convene without explaining why he was watching the eastern lock at four in the morning instead of his post at the bridge." He almost smiled. "Even fear has to balance its books, apprentice."

"And Fournier?"

Roche was quiet a moment. Outside, a soldier laughed at something; it sounded obscene.

"Fournier is a prisoner awaiting transfer," he said finally. "Transfer orders are drafted by my office. Transfer orders are subject to delay. Roads wash out in autumn. Wagons lose axles. Paperwork," he added, looking directly at me, "is a river, and some rivers can be dammed. Not forever. But long enough."

Long enough. In 1793, in a country eating its own children, long enough was the largest unit of mercy available, and Captain Roche had just spent his career to buy it.

* * *

The Marceaus reached free water.

I have no letter confirming it — letters are the first casualties of escapes — but I have something better, or worse, or simply different: Sylvie's method. She had promised me a signal, because Sylvie Marceau keeps promises the way rivers keep directions, reluctantly and inevitably. If the boat passed the last checkpoint at Rouvray, she was to pay the ferryman there a single silver button to hang a white cloth on the willow downstream.

Three days later, walking the towpath under a pass I had forged so many times I no longer felt the forgery of it, I saw the cloth.

White. Small. Ridiculous, really — a scrap of someone's shift hung on a branch, and the whole world changed its shape around it. My father's daughter had crossed into Normandy. My mother — Sylvie's mother, my mother in every way that ever counted — was alive on moving water, going somewhere the Republic's arithmetic couldn't reach.

I sat down on the towpath and laughed until I cried and then cried until I laughed, and a bargeman gave me a wide berth, which was sensible of him, since I must have looked like exactly what I was: a young woman coming apart at the seams of her disguise, stitching herself back together with both hands.

* * *

That night, in the map room, I opened the ledger's rescued pages.

I had carried them sewn into the lining of my coat since the burning — nine leaves of Aubertin's cramped hand, pulled from the fire while the smoke still smelled of other people's lives. I had not dared read them. Reading them felt like opening a grave, and I had done enough grave-opening for one season.

But grief braided with victory is a rope, and rope pulls.

By lamplight, I smoothed them flat on the drafting table, weighting the corners with the brass instruments Aubertin had left me, and I read my family whole.

There we were. Page six, the Delacroix entry, in ink gone brown as old blood. My mother's true name — her name before marriage, the name she was born with and that the Republic's lists had never troubled to learn. My father, recorded not as the drunkard the village remembered but as a man who had once paid a neighbor's debt in a bad winter and asked that it be kept quiet. My grandmother. An uncle lost at sea whose loss had been a silence in every family conversation of my childhood, and here was his date, his ship, his port — a silence made into a fact, a fact made into a line, a line made permanent by a dying man's patience.

Aubertin had written us down. All of us. The family I had spent eleven years pretending did not exist, catalogued with the same care he gave coastlines, because to him there was no difference. Every household was territory. Every name was a shore worth charting.

*Dominique Delacroix,* the margin note read, in his smaller hand. *Apprentice. Nephew. Daughter. See: all three true.*

All three true. He had known, of course. He had known from the beginning, or nearly, and he had let me build my fortress of paper anyway, and then he had written the truth down beside the lie so that neither would be lost. That was the whole of him, really. That page was the whole of him.

I held the pages against my chest the way you hold a child, or a wound, and I let myself say it, alone, to a room full of maps that kept history at arm's length no longer:

"My name is Dominique Delacroix. These were my people. This was my town. And I am going to write it all down."

* * *

Which is when I heard the boot on the stair, and knew that saying things aloud in occupied buildings is a habit I would need to break before it broke me.

The step was heavy. Unhurried. Familiar in the way a bad debt is familiar.

Vaillant.
