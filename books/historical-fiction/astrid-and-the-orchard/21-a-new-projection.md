## 21. A New Projection

The trouble with exile, I discovered in the first fortnight, is not the leaving. The leaving is simple. You walk, or you float, and then you are elsewhere. The trouble is that elsewhere has no edges. A mapmaker without borders is a priest without a parish—all the faith, none of the walls.

We had landed, in the end, in a village whose name I will not write here, because the whole point of the exercise was that no one should ever read it. It sat on a river wider than ours, in a country where the accents wrapped around vowels like wool scarves, and the first thing Madame Marceau did upon stepping off the boat was to criticize the fish market.

"It's fine," she said, in the tone of a woman pronouncing a sentence of death. "It'll do."

From Madame Marceau, this was a coronation hymn. I had known her through two Vireloup winters and one revolution, and I had never once heard her call anything *fine*. Sylvie caught my eye over their trunks and grinned, and for a moment we were just two girls laughing at a mother's relief, and then I remembered everything we were carrying—trunks, yes, but also forged papers, rescued ledger pages sewn into my coat lining, and between us a silence shaped exactly like Fournier—and the laugh died somewhere on the riverbank, unmourned.

That was three months ago. We lodged above a cooper's shop, and I earned our bread the only way I knew how: I drew. Survey work, mostly. Estate plans for farmers who wanted their ditches argued into permanence. It paid badly and it kept my hands honest, which is more than most trades can claim.

And every night, after the others slept, I drew something else.

* * *

It began as an accident, the way all my important work has begun. I was sketching from memory to keep my hand loose, and instead of a coastline I drew the Vireloup river—the real one, ours—with its lazy bend below the mill, and then, because my memory is a traitor with excellent recall, the checkpoint. The lock. The reed bank where Père Fournier's second boat had waited in the dark like a held breath.

I should have stopped. Aubertin would have stopped me. *A map is a confession, Dominic,* he used to say, tapping his temple with his pipe before it could reach the paper. *Every line you draw tells the reader what you thought mattered.* And what mattered, apparently, at midnight in a foreign country with my coat lining still smelling faintly of smoke, was the route by which eleven people had slipped out of France while a twelfth stayed behind to open the water for them.

I worked on it for weeks before I admitted it was a map at all. I told myself it was practice. Catharsis, if I was feeling literary. But my hands knew better. They put the scale bar in. They labeled the lock. They marked the hour the gates groaned open—two bells past midnight, though it had felt like a year—and they rendered, in the fine stippled shading I reserved for things I could not bear to draw plainly, the stretch of bank where the lanterns had come out and where old Fournier had stood up straight in the torchlight so the soldiers would find him quickly and stop searching the reeds for the rest of us.

He had stood up *straight*. I keep coming back to that. A man of seventy-odd years, with knees that complained in dry weather, and he stood up out of the mechanism pit like a man answering a summons he'd been expecting since the Directory, and he looked—not at the soldiers, I learned later from Sylvie, who had watched until Roche's men physically turned her head—at the boat. At us. As if to say, *well? Don't dawdle.*

So I drew him standing there. Not as a figure—I'm no portraitist—but as a point on the map, annotated in the margin: *Here the way divides. Here someone must remain.* Every honest route map has such a point, if it's honest about its own history. There is always a toll. Usually it's money. Sometimes it's a man.

* * *

Sylvie found it in the seventh week, because Sylvie finds everything, because I have never once successfully hidden anything from Sylvie Marceau, a fact which has shaped my entire adult life and which I have stopped resenting on the grounds that resentment was getting me nowhere.

She came in from the evening air with her cheeks pink and her arms full of kindling, saw the sheet pinned to my board under the lamplight, and went very still in the way she does—which anyone else would mistake for calm and which I know to be the opposite.

"That's the lock," she said.

"Yes."

"You're mapping it."

"I'm—" I set down my pen. There are conversations you rehearse and conversations that ambush you, and this was the second kind. "I don't know what I'm doing with it."

"May I?" She didn't wait for permission; she never does. She leaned over the board, and I watched her eyes travel the river I had drawn from memory, past the checkpoint, past the reed bank, and stop at the margin note. I watched her read it twice.

"He'd have hated being a marginal note," she said quietly.

"He'd have hated being dead more."

"No." She straightened. "No, I don't think he would have. He'd have hated being *useless*. Being a note on your map—" and here her voice did something complicated, "—that means he got everyone through. Including you. Especially you."

I want to record, for accuracy's sake, that I did not cry. What happened was that my eyes watered due to lamplight fatigue, a well-documented occupational hazard of draftsmen, and Sylvie had the grace to pretend otherwise, and passed me the good drying cloth without comment, and we left it there. This is, I have come to believe, what family is: people who know when to leave it there.

But she wasn't finished, because Sylvie is never finished.

"What will you do with it?" she asked. "When it's done?"

"Nothing. Burn it, probably. It's dangerous. It names a checkpoint, a lock, a route—if the wrong person—"

"Dominic." She said my name—that name—in the particular way she had adopted over the winter, patient and slightly amused, a woman handling a skittish animal. "You've redrawn it four times. I've watched you. Nobody burns their fourth draft of anything."

* * *

My name. Since we're on the subject.

Dominic Delacroix was not, strictly speaking, a lie. Aubertin had been very clear on the theology of it, back when he was teaching me to forge my own baptismal record by candlelight. *We are not inventing you, girl. We are translating you. The original text remains true underneath.*

I had spent six years believing that the translation was the whole of me. It was easier that way. Delacroix, apprentice mapmaker, friendless and unremarkable, could pass through a requisitioned town unnoticed; whoever I had been before was a liability with a name that might appear in ledgers, and I had buried her so thoroughly that even I stopped visiting the grave.

Then came the ledger page, and Vaillant's sneering revelation in the map room: that the informant list Aubertin died protecting carried, among its names, my own birth family. That my master had known who I was all along—had perhaps taken me in *because*—and had kept the proof folded inside his life's work like a pressed flower.

I have those pages now. I pulled them from the fire while soldiers fed documents to the flames, and Roche looked at the ceiling and let me, and I have carried them across a border sewn into my coat, and they sit at the bottom of my trunk wrapped in oilcloth like the relics they are.

I still haven't read them properly. That is, I have read them—I have read every word a hundred times, I could recite them—but I haven't let them become real. There is a family in those pages with a surname, a village of origin, a father who signed something in 1789. Reading them fully would mean deciding what to do about it. Grief is exhausting enough without genealogy.

But the map—the new one, the route map, the fourth draft that nobody was going to burn—had developed, without my quite noticing, a cartouche. The decorative frame in the lower corner where a mapmaker signs her work. It sat empty for weeks. I inked coastlines around it. I shaded the reed bank. The cartouche stayed blank, and every night it got louder.

Because here is the thing about signing a map: it is the one act in the trade that cannot be disguised. You can fudge a survey. You can forge a transit pass—you can forge a whole *person*, believe me, I've done both. But a signature is you saying *I made this, I vouch for it, come find me.* Aubertin signed everything. Even the atlas he hid his most dangerous secrets in bore his name in flourishes, as if daring the future to argue.

And I had signed nothing in six years. Delacroix's signature existed on official documents in Vireloup, tidy and false, an apprentice's hand. My actual name—the one in the oilcloth packet—belonged to a girl who had stopped existing in 1787.

The question the empty cartouche kept asking, night after night, was not *who are you*. I knew who I was. The question was whether I was willing to be findable.

* * *

In the ninth week, Madame Marceau made rabbit.

This requires context. Rabbit meant gratitude, or apology, or celebration, depending on the sauce, and the sauce that night was rich and wine-dark and entirely unlike poverty, which meant celebration. Sylvie had gotten work at the cooper's—actual wages—and there was talk of renting a proper house in spring, and Madame Marceau stood at the head of the table in a borrowed shawl looking like a general reviewing troops she had personally marched out of hell.

"To us," she said, raising her cup. "All of us."

"All of us," Sylvie echoed, and looked at me, and the look had weight to it, an instruction.

I drank. And somewhere between the rabbit and the second cup of thin wine, I decided.

Not dramatically. I have no talent for drama; I save it all for margins. I simply climbed to our room afterward, sat down at the board, lit the lamp, and looked at the empty cartouche for a long time. Below me, the Marceaus' voices murmured over dishes. Somewhere south and east, past rivers I had drawn and rivers I hadn't, a man sat in a prison or a camp or a hole in the ground because he had chosen eleven strangers over himself, and a captain named Roche was presumably filing paperwork that would either save him or bury him, and none of it—none of it—was mine to control. All that was mine to control was a pen and forty square centimeters of paper.

I unwrapped the oilcloth packet. I read the name again. I said it aloud, once, quietly, to hear how it fit after six years in the ground.

It fit like my father's coat would probably fit: unfamiliar in the shoulders, correct everywhere else.

Then I dipped my pen and signed the map.

Not the name on my forged papers. Not Aubertin's name, borrowed like an overcoat. My own. In full. With the date, and beneath it, because honesty is a habit that spreads once started, the words: *Drawn from memory, for the Marceaus, and for P. Fournier, who stayed.*

The letters dried crooked, because my hand shook slightly. I decided to consider this authenticity rather than weakness.

And then—because a signature, once given, demands consequences—I took the ledger pages from their wrapping, and the atlas plates I had copied in the last desperate days in Vireloup, and the loose sheets of the route map's earlier drafts, and I laid them all out on the floor of that rented room above the cooper's shop, and for the first time I looked at the whole of it together. Master Aubertin's life's work, saved in fragments. The town's true names, rescued from the fire. My family's line, folded in oilcloth. And one new map, signed, of a river crossing that should not have worked and did.

It was not an atlas yet. It was the raw material of one—a different atlas than the old man's. His had catalogued a world as it was, every loyalty and debt fixed in place like terrain, and the Revolution had burned through it precisely because fixed things burn well. Mine would have to be something else. A map of motion. Of routes, not territories. Of the ways people get out, and the points along the way where someone must stand up straight in the torchlight so the rest can slip past in the dark.

I sat on the floor amid the papers until the candle guttered, and upstairs the house creaked, and I was—for the first time since 1787, as far as I could calculate—not hiding from anything.

* * *

The knock came at dawn.

Three knocks, measured, unhurried. Sylvie slept through them the way she sleeps through everything short of artillery. I pulled a blanket over the scattered papers—it fooled no one, least of all me—and opened the door on a stranger: a compact man, travel-worn, with mud to his knees and the particular stillness of someone who has walked a long way carrying something heavy.

He looked at me. He looked past me, at the blanket-shaped lump on the floor, and I watched him decide not to care about it, which was somehow more alarming than curiosity.

"You're the mapmaker," he said. Not a question. "Delacroix—or whatever you're calling yourself now."

"And if I am?"

He reached into his coat and produced a letter, sealed, travel-stained, and handed it to me with the gravity of a man handing over a live coal.

"It's from Captain Roche," he said. "About the old man who stayed at the lock."

The seal was unbroken. My hands, I noticed distantly, were perfectly steady—draftsman's hands, trained for exactly this. I broke it, and unfolded the single page, and read the first line, and understood that whatever map I had been planning next, the world had already begun drawing it for me.
