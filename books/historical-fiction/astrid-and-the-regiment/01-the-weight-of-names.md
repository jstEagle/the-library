## 1. The Weight of Names

The floorboard beneath Freya Montrose's bed had a particular creak, and she had learned to love it.

It was the third plank from the window, the one that always complained when she stepped on its left edge but kept a sullen silence if she approached from the right. Every night for eleven months she had made that approach, kneeling in the blue dark with her skirts pooled around her knees, lifting the board by its nail-scarred corner, and lowering into the cavity below a small ledger bound in oxblood leather. Eleven months. The board still creaked when it wanted to, as though it disapproved of secrets on principle — which, Freya thought, was fair enough. So did she. But a woman could not keep a conscience in the open air any more than she could keep bread there without the gulls taking it.

She lowered the ledger now, replaced the plank, pressed her palm flat against the floor as if taking its pulse, and stood. Through the thin wall came the sound of the tide going out at Greyhollow Bay, patient as a housekeeper sweeping up after guests who would not leave.

Downstairs, the schoolhouse smelled of carbolic and barley soup, which was to say it smelled like 1917 everywhere along this coast, except here the two smells had learned to live together without quarreling. Freya's father had taught arithmetic in the long room for thirty years; his chalk ghost still haunted the slate rails where someone — Nurse Ida, most likely — had written *Ward A* and *Ward B* in letters meant to be cheerful and managing only to be enormous. Twenty-two beds where forty desks had been. The desks themselves were stacked in the coal cellar, and sometimes, passing the cellar door, Freya imagined she heard them whispering multiplication tables to one another in the dark.

"You're late," said Nurse Ida, not looking up from her bandage-rolling. Ida was sixty, built like a lighthouse keeper, and had decided sometime around Freya's fourteenth year that punctuality was the whole of morality. "Mrs. Brandel's boy woke at six asking for his mother, and Private Lund has opinions about his porridge."

"Private Lund has opinions about everything," Freya said, tying on her apron. "It's what keeps him alive."

"It's what keeps *me* alive," Ida said. "A man arguing about salt is a man too busy to die."

This was the theology of the Greyhollow schoolhouse hospital, and they observed it faithfully: fuss was medicine. Complaints were vital signs. The morning Freya walked down the ward and heard nothing but gratitude, she would begin to worry.

She began at the far end, as always, with old Mrs. Brandel's boy — Henrik, twenty years old, shot through the shoulder and homesick in a way that no morphine touched. He had his mother's letter propped against his water jug, read so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth.

"She says the herring catch is good," Henrik told Freya, with the desperate brightness of a boy determined to make news out of nothing. "Best in nine years, she says."

"That will show them," Freya said solemnly. "The fish won't know what hit them."

He laughed, and then winced, and then laughed again anyway, because laughing through the wince was also part of the theology.

Private Lund, three beds down, wanted his porridge salted, his blanket shaken, and the government informed that a man who had survived three months in the trenches deserved butter with his bread, not that grey scrape they called margarine, which he suspected — he confided this loudly, leaning toward Freya as though sharing state secrets — was manufactured from old boots.

"I'll write to the King," Freya promised.

"Do," said Lund. "Tell him His Majesty can have my boots back when I'm done eating them."

Further along: a boy named Eklund who never spoke and watched the ceiling as though reading something written there; two men playing draughts with buttons because the army had confiscated anything resembling strategy; and young Sørensen, feverish since Tuesday, whose chart Freya checked twice, whose forehead she cooled twice more, and whose name she did not yet know she would need to write down before the week was out.

At half past ten the mail cart came, and with it the thing Freya had come, without ever deciding to, to dread: the casualty list.

Captain Ellery's office sent them weekly now, printed on the requisitioned press at the harbor office — neat columns, official type, the war translated into bookkeeping. The matron pinned it to the ward door, and the men gathered to read it the way farmers gather to read weather. Freya read it too, standing slightly apart, doing the thing she did every week, the thing no order had yet forbidden because no order knew she did it.

She compared.

Not the numbers — the numbers matched, the army counted well enough. It was the names. The list gave surnames, initials, ranks, regiments. *P. Lund, Infantry.* But the list did not say that Private P. Lund had a wife named Sigrid who signed her letters *your own girl* even though she was thirty-one and he was thirty-four, or that he carried her photograph inside his left boot, or that when the fever took him in March he had called for her in a voice Freya still heard sometimes when the ward went quiet. The list recorded that he had *died of wounds.* It did not record what he said.

What he said was: *Tell her I kept the boot dry.*

Freya had written it down. She wrote all of them down.

That was the ledger. That was the whole of it, though a tribunal, if one ever convened, might call it sedition. Names the reports thinned away. Last words the reports had no column for. Mothers' addresses, so that when the official letters arrived saying only *killed in action*, a second letter might follow from Greyhollow saying *he spoke of you; he was not alone; here is what he said.* Eleven months of them. Forty-one entries in a small oxblood book hidden under a complaining floorboard.

She had started it the night of the first entry — a corporal named Voss, who died at two in the morning holding her hand and reciting, over and over, the name of a village so small it appeared on no map the army bothered with. The official list said *Cpl. A. Voss.* The map of him, the true one, lived nowhere else in the world. Someone had to be the map. And if the army would not do it — if the officers with their neat columns believed a man could be *expended*, that word she had seen once in a returned document and never forgotten, *expended*, as though soldiers were lamp oil — then a schoolmaster's daughter with good handwriting and a stubborn streak a mile wide would do it herself.

Her father, before the paralysis took him, had kept the town register for forty years. Births, deaths, marriages, herring catches, floods. *A record is a promise,* he used to say, tapping the great ledger on his knee. *You write a person down truly, and they cannot be wholly taken.* Freya had inherited the penmanship and the promise both. The army had inherited her brother.

Anders. She let herself think of him only at noon, sitting on the back step with tea she forgot to drink, because thinking of him earlier in the day made the porridge burn. Her little brother in an officer's uniform that fit him like a costume he hadn't grown into, somewhere behind a desk in the district capital, stamping forms. Requisition forms. She'd had a letter last month, stiff with officialese, informing her that coastal districts were to be surveyed for *war-utilizable assets.* She had laughed when she read it, alone in the kitchen, and then stopped laughing quite abruptly, because Anders had always been precise, and precision in a clerk was a kind of loyalty, and loyalty these days wore whatever uniform it was handed.

"He'll come home for Midsummer," she told the cat, who had appointed himself supervisor of the back step. The cat, a scarred grey veteran called Admiral, regarded her with the skepticism of his rank. "He will. There's still an Anders in there somewhere. Underneath the stamps."

The afternoon passed in its ordinary liturgy — dressings changed, charts initialed, Sørensen's fever fought with cool cloths and lying to him about how well he looked. At four o'clock the light went long and gold across the bay, and for a quarter of an hour the ward was almost beautiful, the way a bruise is beautiful if you squint.

It was Matron who brought the paper, at dusk.

Matron Halvorsen was a small woman with a voice like a closing door and the unshakeable conviction that the war was badly organized, an opinion she held with the calm of someone who had nursed through two epidemics and one shipwreck and considered the war merely the latest management failure. She set the telegram on the desk in front of Freya without preamble, the way she did everything.

"Harbor office," she said. "For the district. Read it."

Freya read it. Read it again. The words sat on the page being perfectly ordinary, which somehow made it worse.

*COMMENCING IMMEDIATELY, ALL PROPERTY IN GREYHOLLOW DISTRICT SUBJECT TO REQUISITION SURVEY. ARMY CLERICAL OFFICER EN ROUTE TO SUPERVISE ASSESSMENT AND DOCUMENTATION.*

"Clerical officer," Freya said.

"Arriving Friday," Matron said. "On the morning boat." She paused, and for once her door-of-a voice softened half an inch. "There was a name in the signature block, girl. I thought your eyes should see it before anyone else's."

Freya looked down at the bottom of the telegram, at the neat, precise, unmistakable hand that had typed itself into her chest like a splinter:

*Clerical Officer, District Survey: Lt. A. Montrose.*

The Admiral jumped onto the desk, knocked the telegram askew, and settled on it, as if he had opinions about paperwork too.

Friday, Freya thought. Three days.

She went home in the blue dark, knelt beside the third plank from the window, and lifted the floorboard on the quiet side, the way she always did. The oxblood ledger lay where she had left it, forty-one names deep, keeping its silence.

"Three days," she told it. "And my brother is coming to count the town."

The board creaked when she put it back. For once, she agreed with it entirely.

* * *

That night she did not sleep so much as keep watch lying down, and near dawn she dreamed of her father's study — the smell of pipe smoke and paste, the great register open on his knee.

"A record is a promise, little one," he said, tapping the page. "But mind—" and here he leaned close, and his voice was the tide, going out, "—they are coming to take inventory of everything in this town. Everything. You must decide what a person is worth on their list, and what a person is worth on yours. And Freya—"

"What?" she asked, in the dream, in the grey hour, in the bed where she lay listening to the sea sweep its endless broom. "Father, what?"

But he only smiled the way he had when he'd caught her copying the register at eight years old, and said: "They'll want the numbers. Give them the numbers. Keep the names."

When she woke, gulls were shouting over the bay and the first boat of the morning was coming in — and riding at anchor beyond it, grey against the grey water, far too early and far too large, a naval launch flying the survey pennant, making for the Greyhollow pier.

Two days early. Of course. Precision ran in the family.
