## 2. Requisition

The train let Anders off at Harrow Point an hour late, which meant the war had already been running ahead of him for a year and showed no signs of slowing to let a junior clerk catch up.

He stepped onto the platform with a leather dispatch case, a stamped requisition folder, and the particular posture of a man trying to look official in a uniform that still smelled of the regimental stores. Second Lieutenant Anders Montrose, Adjutant's Department, Provisional Requisitions. The title had eleven words and not one of them, he had discovered, meant anything a person could be proud of at dinner.

The town had not changed, and that was the trouble. He had expected change — craters, barricades, something to justify the folder under his arm. Instead there was the same crooked lamppost at the station road, the same gulls conducting their eternal argument over the fish sheds, the same smell of salt and tar that lived in a person's coat forever no matter how many times it was washed. Home, in other words. Which was precisely what he had been sent to take apart.

"Lieutenant Montrose?" A corporal was waiting at the barrier, young enough that his cap sat on him like a borrowed idea. "Corporal Penn, sir. Captain Ellery's compliments. The survey begins at nine."

"Then we have twenty minutes," Anders said, "in which nothing official can happen," and was pleased to find the boy grin before remembering to stop himself.

* * *

The survey began, as all surveys do, with tea that nobody wanted and everybody drank.

Captain Ellery had established himself in the Harrow Point Hotel — requisitioned that morning, though the landlady was still, with magnificent dignity, charging him for the room — and had spread the town across the billiard table in the form of a map. He was a tall man with the kind of neat, unhurried voice that made destruction sound like tidying.

"Coastal garrison, Lieutenant. The Admiralty wants the anchorage; the Army wants the rail spur; and the Medical Directorate wants—" he tapped the schoolhouse on the map with a pencil worn to a stub, "—this. Four hundred beds by month's end. The town will be billeted, the church will be a storehouse, and the schoolhouse will be a hospital. Standard schedule."

"Standard," Anders repeated. He had read the schedule on the train. It allotted the town's residents two days' notice and one cart apiece, which was the kind of arithmetic that looked reasonable on paper and became, in practice, an old woman weeping over a piano that would not fit on a cart.

"Something troubles you, Lieutenant?"

"No, sir. I was confirming the figures."

Ellery looked at him for a moment with pale, patient eyes, the way a man looks at a ledger he intends to balance later. "Good. The requisition notices go out today. You'll serve them personally. It's better when it's done properly — a signature, a stamp, an explanation. Civilians fear what arrives unsigned."

Anders thought: they also fear what arrives signed, sir, by their own brother. He said, "Yes, sir."

* * *

He served the first notice at the bakery, where Mrs. Aldous read it twice, set it down, and said, "Your mother would have boxed your ears, Anders Montrose," and then gave him two buns anyway, because grief and kindness in Harrow Point had always traveled in the same basket.

He served the second at the fish sheds, where the men listened in silence and one of them, old Halvard, spat precisely between his boots and said, "You'll be wanting the harbor too, then. Take it. The herring left years ago; might as well the rest of us."

He served the third at the church, where the vicar received the paper as though it were a diagnosis, read it slowly, and then asked, with terrible gentleness, whether the Army would be needing the graves as well.

Anders said no, and heard his own voice come out smaller than he intended.

By afternoon he had learned the trick of it — the trick of serving paper to people who had known you in short trousers. You did not apologize, because apology was an insult dressed as courtesy. You did not explain, because there was no explanation that survived contact with a mother's face. You read the schedule, you took the signature, you said *thank you, ma'am*, and you carried the small cold weight of it home in your chest, where it would keep, he suspected, for the rest of your life.

There were eleven notices in the folder. The eleventh was addressed to the schoolhouse.

* * *

The schoolhouse hospital smelled of carbolic and lavender, which was a war all its own being fought between the matron and the nurses. Anders stood in the corridor with his folder under his arm and listened to the sounds he had grown up ignoring: the squeak of the old floorboards, the tick of the clock over the door that had governed his childhood latenesses, and beneath it all the low sea-sound of men breathing in rows.

Freya found him there. She was carrying a basin and a roll of bandages and she stopped as though he were a piece of furniture that had been moved in the night.

"You're wearing it," she said.

"I'm wearing what?"

"The uniform. You're actually wearing it. I kept telling myself they'd give you a desk and a quill and you'd write angry letters in a nice warm office, and instead they've gone and dressed you up as the end of the world."

"It's a clerk's uniform," Anders said. "It's the least martial uniform ever issued. There's a regulation for how I file things, Freya. Three regulations."

"And how many regulations for turning the school into a ward?"

He looked at the floor. "One. It's a short one."

She set the basin down on a windowsill, slowly, the way she did everything when she was deciding how to be angry. He knew the signs. He had grown up reading his sister the way sailors read weather.

"Four hundred beds," she said. "Word travels. Mrs. Aldous sent her boy round before you'd finished your tea."

"Then you know I have to—"

"You have to serve me a piece of paper. Yes. I know how it works, Anders. I've been watching it work all day through other people's windows." She picked the basin up again. "Come to the office. Not the ward. They don't need to see it."

* * *

The office had been the headmaster's room, back when the school had a headmaster and a globe and a cane nobody talked about. Now it held a desk, a lamp, a ledger — and Anders's stomach turned over at the sight of it, a ledger, of course she kept a ledger, she had kept lists since she was seven — and a chair that Freya indicated with a tilt of her head, granting him the visitor's seat in what was, as of that morning, Army property.

He laid the requisition notice on the desk between them, square, corners aligned. He had squared the corners on the train. He had squared them because it was the only thing in the whole business he could make straight.

"Read it," he said. "You have to read it aloud. It's in the—"

"Regulations. I know." She read it. Her voice was level and clear and it did the words a small, terrible kindness, pronouncing *requisition* and *conversion* and *effective immediately* as though they were ordinary words, words that had not spent the morning making a vicar look old. Then she signed. Her signature was the same as it had been on her schoolbooks, the F with its extravagant tail, and something about that undid him more than anything else that day.

"Freya—"

"Don't." She set down the pen. "Don't do the face. I've had the face all day from half the town, all of them wondering how Anders Montrose could stand there in that coat, and I've been telling them he had no choice, which is true, and also not the point."

"What's the point, then?"

"The point is what happens after the paper." She folded her hands on the desk. "Tell me what the schedule says about records."

He had known this was coming. He had known since the train, since the folder, since Ellery's pencil tapping the schoolhouse map. He had rehearsed it on the platform, standing under the crooked lamppost, and every rehearsal had ended with him being brave, and now bravery arrived and it was exactly as heavy as advertised.

"There's a standing order," he said. "On documentation. Any register, ledger, or record not authorized by the Military Directorate is classed as subversive record-keeping."

"Subversive." She said the word the way she'd once said *algebra*, with the particular scorn of the gifted. "A list of names is subversive."

"A list of names that contradicts official reports," Anders said, "is subversive. Freya, listen to me. There's a clause. Confiscation without notice, and the holder is liable to questioning. Questioning, Freya. By officers. Captain Ellery's kind of officers — the kind who think a discrepancy is a conspiracy and a ledger is a weapon." He leaned forward. "Whatever you write in that book — and I know you, you've been writing in it since before the war, you'd have kept a ledger of the Flood — burn it. Tonight. Before the survey teams come through with their inventory forms."

The lamp ticked. Somewhere down the corridor a man coughed, twice, and was soothed by a voice Anders didn't recognize.

"You're asking me to forget them," Freya said quietly.

"I'm asking you to not be *found* remembering them. There's a difference. It's the whole difference." He stood, because sitting felt like surrender and standing felt like something, even if it wasn't. "The survey team comes Thursday. Ellery himself is coming Thursday — he's taken an interest in the hospital, in its *administration*, in what he calls its *parallel documentation*. I don't know what he knows. I know what he suspects, and what he suspects is that somebody in this town has been keeping accounts the Army hasn't authorized."

"Then he's a good guesser."

"Freya."

"Anders." She looked up at him, and her eyes were their mother's eyes, which was the cruelest thing about her, and the truest. "You came here to take my school, my neighbors' homes, and the church where we were christened, and you've done it with your corners squared and your stamps in order, and I'm not angry at you for it, which you should understand is worse for me than anger. But you are not going to stand in this room and tell me that the only way to keep the dead safe is to pretend they were never here."

"That's not—"

"That is *exactly* what you said. Burn it, you said. Forget them, you said. Be found innocent, you said." She rose too, and took the requisition notice, and put it in a drawer with the gentleness of someone filing a wound. "I'll have the beds cleared by month's end. The Army will have its four hundred beds. But the names, Anders. The names stay where I put them."

He wanted to argue. He was, by training and temperament, a man who argued in triplicate. But down the corridor the coughing had started again, and somewhere a bell rang, and Freya was already moving toward the door with the basin and the bandages, back into the ward, back into the tide of it, and there was nothing left to serve her that she hadn't already signed.

At the door he stopped. "Thursday," he said. "Please. Whatever you decide — decide before Thursday."

She looked back at him, and for one moment she was seven years old again, keeping lists of things the world had lost, and he understood with a cold, descending certainty that she had already decided.

"Thursday," she agreed.

He was halfway down the corridor when the coughing stopped, and did not start again, and he heard Freya call softly for a pencil.
