## 3. What the Officers Omit

The pencil Freya called for was never used that night, because Private Oskar Lindqvist died before she could reach his bedside, and what he had said in his last hour she carried instead the way she carried everything — folded small, kept dry, stored against the dark.

She wrote it down at dawn, when the floorboards beneath her cot were cold and the schoolhouse still belonged to the dead rather than the army. The ledger came up from its hiding place under the third plank by the window, wrapped in oilcloth, smelling faintly of the sea it had never touched. She opened it to a fresh page and wrote:

*Oskar Lindqvist, private, second company. Not killed advancing, as reported. Killed retreating, on his own feet, carrying a boy from the fourth who did not survive either. His last words: "Tell my mother I was walking toward her."*

The official report, she knew, would say something tidier.

* * *

By seven o'clock the schoolhouse was no longer hers at all. It belonged to the war, the way the church belonged to the war now, and the harbor, and old Mrs. Vinter's front room with its good china cabinet, which had been requisitioned for map storage on the grounds that maps required a cool, dry place and Mrs. Vinter's house had famously never once been warm.

The ward held thirty-two beds. By seven o'clock it held forty-one patients, because the advance that had been launched at dusk the evening before had broken against the ridge like a wave against a seawall, and the wave had come home all night in carts and on stretchers and in one memorable instance in a wheelbarrow pushed by two indignant corporals who refused all offers of tea until they'd finished complaining about it.

Freya moved down the rows with her basin and her sponges and her particular brand of cheerfulness, which Nurse Marta once described as "the sort that makes you feel better and slightly scolded at the same time."

"You're going to live," Freya told a sergeant whose arm was in a sling and whose chief wound appeared to be boredom.

"I'd rather not, if it's all the same."

"Well. We can't all be ambitious."

He laughed, which cost him something in the ribs, and laughed again anyway, and Freya counted it as medicine administered.

At the far end, by the window, lay the ones the night had used hardest. She saved them for last, not because she feared them but because she had learned that fear made her hands quick and quick hands were careless hands, and these men deserved better than quick.

Private Lindqvist's bed stood empty, stripped that morning, the mattress already turned. In the next bed lay Corporal Enckell, both legs wrapped, his face the color of the bedsheets, alive in the way that a banked fire is alive.

"He talks in his sleep," Enckell said, when she bent over him. "That one. Lindqvist. All night he talked."

"What did he say?"

"His mother's name. Over and over. And something about walking." Enckell's eyes found hers, and there was a question in them that she could not answer yet, or perhaps ever. "Was it true, miss? What the captain read us? That we took the ridge?"

Freya straightened the blanket, though it did not need straightening.

"The captain will read you whatever the captain has," she said, which was true, and evasive, and the best she could do at eight in the morning with forty-one men listening.

* * *

Captain Ellery arrived at eleven, as he always did, punctual as a debt. He was not a cruel man — Freya had decided this early and clung to it the way one clings to any theory that spares one despair — but he was a precise man, and precision, in wartime, could do more damage than cruelty, because cruelty at least knew it was doing harm.

He stood at the head of the ward with the report in his hand and read it aloud, because reading it aloud was policy, and policy was the air Ellery breathed.

"'On the evening of the seventeenth,'" he read, "'elements of the second and fourth companies advanced in good order upon the ridge position, meeting light resistance, and achieved their objectives at a cost considered acceptable.'"

The ward was very quiet. Forty-one men listened, and some of them had been on the ridge, and none of them said anything, because men who had been on the ridge had learned what happened to things said aloud.

"'Casualties were moderate. Morality and discipline remain high. The action reflects credit upon all involved.'"

Ellery lowered the paper. "Questions?"

Silence. Then, from the far window, in a voice like a dry stick snapping:

"My brother was in the fourth."

It was Corporal Enckell. Freya saw Ellery register the voice, turn toward it, assemble his face into something appropriate.

"The fourth fought with distinction," Ellery said.

"The report says light resistance." Enckell's hands were fists on the blanket. "I was there. There was nothing light about it."

"There is a way these things are written, Corporal—"

"I know how they're written." His voice cracked, and he seemed ashamed of the crack, and pressed on regardless, which took more courage than the advance had, Freya thought. "Lindqvist didn't die achieving an objective. He died in the water at the bottom of the gully, holding a boy from his own company, and the water came up past his chest before the firing even stopped, and he *walked* toward the lines, sir. Toward home. That's how he died. You can write that down if you want something to reflect credit."

Ellery's jaw tightened. Freya watched him decide — visibly, like a man counting coins — whether discipline or dignity would serve him better here, and choose discipline, because it was cheaper.

"The corporal is feverish," he said, to the ward at large. "He should rest."

And then, turning to go, his eyes passed across Freya — just passed, lightly, the way a hand passes across a shelf checking for dust — and stopped.

"Miss Montrose. A moment."

* * *

He interviewed her in what had been the schoolmaster's office, which still contained the schoolmaster's desk, upon which Ellery now laid the report like a card he was tired of holding.

"You keep records here," he said. "Of the patients."

"Admissions, treatments, discharges. Yes."

"Discharges." A thin smile. "A delicate word for it."

"It's the word on the forms your office supplied."

He let that stand, which she noted; he was not a man to let things stand without wanting to. He turned the report toward her.

"The corporal's account differs from this document. Materially. Such differences are common in feverish men. What is less common" — and now the lightness went out of his voice entirely — "is when the same differences appear elsewhere. Word for word, Miss Montrose, in places they should not appear. A letter that reached a widow in the south parish last week. Remarks overheard among orderlies. Details no one outside this ward could know — unless someone outside this ward was recording them."

Outside the window, October was doing its patient work on the elm, letting down its leaves one by one as if paying out rope.

"You believe someone is keeping notes," Freya said.

"I believe someone is keeping *unauthorized* notes. There is a difference between record-keeping and—" he searched for the word, and chose it with care — "counter-record-keeping. The first is a duty. The second is a leak. Leaks, in my experience, begin with small kindnesses. A name spelled correctly. A sentence remembered accurately. Nobody sets out to betray anything. They set out to be accurate, and accuracy becomes a habit, and habits become evidence."

"Whose evidence?" Freya asked. "Against whom, and in service of what? A man died walking toward his mother, Captain. Your report says he died achieving an objective. One of those statements is true. Keeping hold of the true one doesn't feel like betrayal to me. It feels like the only loyalty left that hasn't been requisitioned."

The words were out before she had fully weighed them, and she watched them land — watched something shift behind Ellery's eyes, not anger exactly but a sharpening, the look of a man who has been casting about in the dark and has finally felt, under his boot, the edge of the thing he has been looking for.

"You speak," he said softly, "like a woman who has already chosen her loyalties."

"I speak like a woman who writes a great many letters home," Freya said. "Grief is not sedition, Captain. Though I understand the army sometimes finds it difficult to tell the two apart."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he gathered up his report, squared its edges against the desk, and stood.

"My clerk arrives Thursday," he said. "My own clerk, from headquarters — not the local arrangement we've been enduring. He will conduct a full inventory of this facility. Every form, every ledger, every scrap of paper bearing a name. If such records exist, they will be found. If they are surrendered voluntarily beforehand—" he paused at the door, and gave her the smallest of smiles — "the circumstances will be regarded more generously. Good day, Miss Montrose."

* * *

She waited until his footsteps had faded down the corridor, until the ward door opened and shut and the ordinary sounds of suffering resumed their tenancy of the building. Then she went back along the row of beds, past Enckell sleeping at last, past the empty stripped frame of Private Lindqvist's bed, to her cot in the corner, and knelt, and put her hand flat against the third floorboard by the window.

Underneath it, in oilcloth and darkness, lay four years of names. Four years of last words, correctly spelled. A hundred small kindnesses that were, as of eleven o'clock this morning, evidence.

*Thursday,* she thought. *He gives me until Thursday.*

Her brother had given her until Thursday too, though he didn't know yet that he and the Captain had set the same clock. Everyone in her life, it seemed, was converging on Thursday like tides on a shore.

Freya sat back on her heels. Somewhere down the corridor a man called out in his sleep, a name, only a name, the way they all did.

Very well, then. Between now and Thursday there was work to do, and ink was running low.

She would need to write faster.
