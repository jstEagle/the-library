## 4. The Deserter in the Dunes

The storm came in on a Tuesday, which is how I remember it, because Tuesdays were the days Marta Lindqvist boiled the linens and the whole schoolhouse smelled of lye and wet wool. By evening the wind had torn a shutter loose from the old geography room and carried it out over the harbor like a gull with ambitions, and I had stood at the window watching it go, thinking that even our buildings were trying to desert.

I found him the next morning, wedged into the hollow between two dunes where the marram grass grew thick enough to hide a man who very much wished to be hidden.

I had gone out early — before the orderlies woke, before Sister Brenner could assign me to scrubbing — because after storms the sea gave things back. Driftwood. Rope. Once, horribly, a boot with a foot still considering itself part of the arrangement. I walked the tideline with my basket and my eyes down, and it was the boots that caught me first: army boots, mud-caked, one lace snapped, planted in the sand like two small grave markers.

Above them, curled against the dune's shoulder, was a soldier.

He was so young he made Private Holm — whom we had buried in October, aged nineteen — seem middle-aged. His greatcoat was soaked through and his hands were raw as peeled fruit, and when I crouched beside him he did not startle or beg. He simply looked at me with the particular emptiness I had learned to read in the ward, the look of men whose insides had been evacuated ahead of their bodies.

"Please," he said, and then seemed to lose the rest of the sentence somewhere between his chest and his mouth.

"You're freezing," I said, which was true and also beside the point, but it was the only sentence I trusted myself with at seven in the morning on an empty beach with an army deserter.

"I'm not—" He stopped. Started again. "They wrote us off."

I knew what he meant before he finished saying it. I had written it myself, in a manner of speaking, in the ledger beneath my floorboards. *Company written off as expended* — the phrase from the dispatches, tidy as arithmetic, as though ninety-one men had been coins spent rather than boys spent *up*.

"What's your name?" I asked.

And here is the thing I will carry to whatever grave receives me: he hesitated. Not from guilt. From genuine difficulty, as though the name had been packed away with the rest of his kit and he could not recall which crate.

"Tomas," he said at last. "Tomas Vinter."

There was no Tomas Vinter on any roster I had seen. There was no company left to hold him. As far as the army was concerned, he had been subtracted along with everyone else, and his continued breathing was a clerical error walking around in a greatcoat.

"Can you walk?" I said.

He tried. He got halfway up and swayed like a mast in crosswind, and I took his arm across my shoulders — he weighed no more than a bundle of wet kindling — and we went up off the beach together, two figures bent double against the wind, looking for all the world like a woman hauling salvage home.

Which, I suppose, is exactly what we were.

* * *

I put him in the net shed behind Greta Sorel's cottage, because Greta was deaf as a post, blind as justice, and devoted to me with the fierce uncritical love of the very old, and because the net shed had a stove, a cot, and a door that locked from the inside if you knew the trick of the latch, which I did, having hidden there myself at eleven years old to escape piano lessons.

Getting him there required crossing the village green in daylight, which I accomplished by the simple method of draping a blanket over his head and telling anyone who looked our way that I'd found a drowned sailor and didn't want the children seeing. This was close enough to the truth to satisfy the village's appetite for tragedy, and by nine o'clock half of Saltvik believed I had personally dragged a corpse ashore, which bought me a respect I did not deserve and a quantity of soup I very much did.

Tomas slept for a day and a night. I sat with him through the worst of the shivering, feeding him broth spoonful by spoonful, and when the dreams took him — and they took him hard, his whole body flinching at some interior artillery — I held his hands and spoke to him about nothing. The weather. The price of herring. The scandal of Mrs. Aalto's new hat, which resembled a collapsed trifle and had united the village in disapproval more thoroughly than the war ever had.

Talking nonsense to the wounded is a skill the war taught me, and I am not ungrateful for it. Silence lets a man sink. Nonsense gives him something to float on.

When he finally surfaced — really surfaced, eyes clearing, looking around the net shed as though confirming the world still existed — the first thing he asked was not about food or safety or whether he would be shot.

"Did any of them get out?" he said. "Second Platoon. Did anybody write?"

I thought of the ledger under my floorboards. I thought of the names I had collected from dying men while Captain Ellery's reports recorded numbers.

"Some of them are remembered," I said carefully.

"That's not—" His face crumpled and reassembled itself with an effort that cost him visibly. "That's not the same thing."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

The search parties began on Friday.

We heard them before we saw them — dogs first, then voices, then the crunch of too many boots on shingle for it to be anything innocent. From the net shed's salt-fogged window we watched them come over the dunes in a line, six soldiers and a sergeant, sweeping the beach the way you'd sweep a floor: thorough, indifferent, certain that what they sought had no feelings worth consulting.

Tomas went white as the sail canvas stacked against the wall. "If they find me—"

"They won't." I said it with more confidence than I felt, which is, I have come to believe, most of what courage consists of.

"Deserters hang," he said. "Or firing squad. They do it at dawn so the light's good for—" He stopped himself, swallowing the rest.

"Then we'll make sure dawn finds you elsewhere." I looked around the shed. It was, let us be honest, a terrible hiding place: one room, one window, one door, and a stove chimney puffing smoke like a signal fire for anyone who cared to read it. "First, we kill the fire. Cold broth and a cold man, I'm afraid."

"And then?"

"And then you get in the boat."

There was an old dory beached behind the shed, Greta's late husband's, hull sound but for a seam that wept when the tide was high. I had rowed worse. Probably.

"In *that*?" Tomas stared at it the way a man stares at his own coffin being measured. "In open water? In this season?"

"Do you prefer the alternative? Because I can arrange for you to stand very still in the dunes and be found, if that's your preference, but I'll want it in writing, since I intend to be extremely rude at your funeral."

Something happened to his face then — the ghost of a smile, faint and astonished, surfacing through the wreckage like a coin through sand. "You're not afraid," he said.

"I'm terrified," I told him. "But fear is a poor navigator. You learn to give it a job below decks and take the wheel yourself."

This was, I confess, a lie twice over. Fear steered me constantly; I simply lied to it about the destination.

* * *

They knocked at Greta's door within the hour, and I answered it wearing my hospital apron and my best expression of brisk, slightly annoyed competence — the expression that says *I am busy and important and you are neither*, which has repelled better men than these.

The sergeant was polite in the way of men reading from a script. "Looking for a straggler, ma'am. Deserted his unit. Last seen near the coast road."

"A deserter," I repeated, and let my voice do a small, contemptuous dance around the word. "In this weather. Goodness. What sort of man walks away from a warm mess hall into a gale?"

"The desperate sort, ma'am."

"Mm. Well, you've seen my kitchen and my cellar, and Greta's hearing is what it is — she'd sleep through an invasion, and indeed may yet." I stepped back to let them poke at the woodpile, and behind me, folded into the space beneath the net loft where the winter nets hung in their briny curtains, Tomas Vinter held his breath among the smells of tar and herring and did not exist.

The sergeant lingered at the gate. "You're the one keeps the records at the schoolhouse hospital," he said. Not quite a question.

"I keep notes," I said. "Names, mostly. For the families. Someone has to tell a mother which boy it was."

"Official channels handle that."

"Official channels handle *numbers*," I said pleasantly. "Mothers want names. But you know this, Sergeant, because you have one yourself, and I'd wager there's someone somewhere praying it gets spoken properly."

He looked at me a moment too long — long enough that I catalogued the exits, the tide, the boat, the distance to the tree line — and then touched his cap and moved on, and the search party swept east along the shore toward the point, away from the weeping dory, and I closed Greta's door and discovered that my knees had opinions about the morning that they had politely withheld until now.

* * *

I launched him at dusk, two days later, when the wind backed round and the seam in the dory's hull could be stuffed with oakum and prayer. I had planned to row him out to the fishing grounds myself and signal Halvard Olsen to take him aboard the *Petrel*, bound south, but the sea had other ideas and Halvard's boat was laid up with a cracked spar besides, so the plan became simpler and worse: row him past the point, land him below the cliff path, and let him walk the coast road south by night until he reached people who asked fewer questions.

"One berth," I said, checking the oarlocks, the bailer, the tin of biscuits I'd wrapped in oilcloth. "One chance. If you're caught—"

"I know what happens if I'm caught."

"No," I said, and took his cold face in my hands, because some things must be said eye to eye or not at all. "You know what happens to *deserters*. But you are not a deserter. You are Tomas Vinter, and you existed, and you will go on existing, and somewhere there is a woman — a mother, a sister, someone — who thinks her boy is gone, and every day you stay alive is a letter she hasn't opened yet. Do you understand me? Living is the report now. You *are* the record."

His eyes filled and he blinked it away like a soldier, which broke my heart in the ordinary way hearts break in wartime — quietly, without stopping work.

"Your name," he said hoarsely. "I don't even know your name. If I live, if I get clear — who do I thank?"

"Freya," I said. "Freya Montrose."

"Freya Montrose," he repeated, tasting it, committing it, and I understood suddenly that I had just done the thing I did for dying men in reverse — handed a name across the water instead of into the ground — and that this, too, was keeping the ledger, just in ink that breathed.

I rowed him out through the darkening chop with my arms burning and my heart hammering its small furious drum, and at the point where the cliffs rose black against the last grey light, he climbed over the side into the shallows and turned back once.

"They wrote us off," he said. "But you didn't. That counts. Whatever else happens — that counts."

Then he was a shape, then a shadow, then nothing, and I rowed home alone with the tide helping me and the first stars coming out over a sea that kept its secrets better than any ledger.

I was still wringing out my skirts in the schoolhouse scullery, laughing at nothing like a madwoman with relief, when the door opened behind me.

It was Anders.

My brother stood in the doorway in his army uniform, travel-stained, holding a leather satchel under one arm, and the look on his face was the look of a man carrying news that weighs more than luggage.

"Freya," he said. "We need to talk. And not here."
