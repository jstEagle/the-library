## 5. Confiscation

My sister poured tea the way other people loaded rifles.

We were in the back room of Halvard's chandlery, which smelled of tar and old rope and was, according to Freya, "the only place left in this town where a conversation doesn't have ears." She had insisted we come here before she would hear a word. She had also insisted on bringing her own tea, because, she said, army requisition had stripped the chandlery of everything worth drinking and replaced it with something the quartermaster called *extension tea*, which extended chiefly into disappointment.

"Say it," she said now, setting the cup down in front of me without ceremony.

"Freya—"

"You've been rehearsing since you walked in. I could see your lips moving on the walk over. Say it."

I said it. "There are going to be searches. Quarter by quarter, house by house. Ellery's order. He believes someone in this town has been keeping records that contradict official reports." I turned my cup a quarter-turn on the table, a habit from boyhood I hadn't managed to kill. "Unauthorized records. His phrase."

"And?"

"And yours is exactly the kind he means."

She looked at me across the steam rising between us, and I watched her do the arithmetic — the same arithmetic, I realized later, that she would still be doing weeks afterward, though with higher stakes and crueler numbers. My sister has never been stupid. She has only ever been brave in ways that look like stupidity from the outside.

"He won't find it," she said.

"Where is it?"

"That's not a question you get to ask in that uniform."

"Freya." I kept my voice low. The chandlery's back room had one window, shuttered, and a wall thin enough to carry a whisper to whoever owned the tar barrels next door. "Ellery isn't a fool. That's the whole problem with him. Fools can be managed. He reads manifests the way you read faces. When his searches turn up nothing, he won't relax. He'll dig."

"Then let him dig." She said it lightly, but her thumb was pressing a dent into the soft wax of the table, round and round, the way she used to when Father read the shipping news aloud and the news was bad. "Anders. Do you know what's in that ledger? Do you actually know, or have you been imagining something scandalous all this time?"

"I imagine paper with names on it that shouldn't be there."

"It's the opposite of *shouldn't*," she said, with a sudden fierceness that made the tar smell seem mild. "It's the only place some of those men exist at all. Ellery's reports write them as numbers. Expended. Attrited. Absorbed." She pronounced each word as if spitting out a stone. "I write them as men who had mothers and bad teeth and jokes they told twice. Somebody has to. If nobody does, then the war gets to decide what happened, and the war lies."

I wanted to say: *then give it up, burn it yourself, before someone else burns it for you.* What I said instead, because I am a coward in the small moments and apparently only discover my spine in the large ones, was: "Just tell me it's somewhere I'd never think to look."

She smiled then — the family smile, the one we both inherited from Mother along with our stubbornness and our unfortunate chin. "Everywhere you'd never think to look, little brother. Every single one."

* * *

The order came through on Tuesday, typed in triplicate, and I signed for it the way I signed for everything that week, which is to say mechanically, hating my own signature a little more each time.

*By direction of Captain R. Ellery, all private residences within the requisitioned zone are subject to inspection for unauthorized documentation. Inspections to commence Wednesday, 0800. Sector Three: the Montrose residence, the former schoolhouse, and associated outbuildings.*

Associated outbuildings. They'd even thought of the boat shed.

Sector Three was mine. I had assigned it to myself three days earlier, moving my own name up the roster with the quiet forgery of a clerk who knows exactly how much ink matters. There were four men under me for the search: Corporal Voss, who followed orders the way a cart follows a horse; Privates Lund and Beck, who were nineteen and mostly interested in whether anyone's larder had survived requisition; and Private Anselm, who was older, quieter, and had been at the front long enough to have opinions about paperwork that he kept behind his teeth.

At 0758 I stood outside the house I had grown up in, wearing the uniform of the people searching it, and I confess I made myself look at the door for a full minute before knocking. Our father had repainted that door blue the summer I was ten because I had declared blue the superior color with the absolute authority of a ten-year-old. It was still blue. It had outlasted his opinion of the matter, his heart, and now it was going to outlast my principles too, if I had any left after Wednesday.

Freya opened the door already dressed, already composed, already offering tea as if we were neighbors discussing weather. She had dressed plainly, hair pinned severely, and I understood that too: she was making herself hard to pity and harder to rattle.

"Lund, Beck — larder, cellar, and try to leave the crockery where you found it," I said. "Voss, the bedrooms. Anselm, with me."

We went through the house like a slow tide. That is what searches are: a tide. You come in, you lift things, you set them down slightly wrong, and you recede. By nine o'clock the house had the particular violated neatness of everywhere the army had been. Beck found a jar of preserved plums and asked with his eyes; I shook my head; he put it back with the reverence of a man laying down a weapon.

It was Anselm who stopped at the floorboards.

I saw him stop. He was kneeling by the window in Freya's room, running his hand along a plank the way a man checks a horse's leg, and his fingers paused where two boards met a hair's width proud of their neighbors. New work. Or newly disturbed work.

He didn't say anything. He looked at the board, and then he looked at me, and there was a very long moment in which Private Anselm, who had seen what happened to men whose names got written off as *expended*, decided what kind of soldier he was going to be about it.

Then he moved his knee, loudly, onto a loose nail, swore with feeling, and spent the next five minutes sucking his thumb while complaining that civilian floorwork should be a court-martial offense. By the time he was done being loud, Voss had called from the kitchen that he'd found a strongbox, and everyone's attention went with him.

The strongbox held letters from our aunt in the south, a deed to the boat shed, and eleven crowns. Voss recorded it all with tremendous satisfaction, the way he recorded everything, as though finding a deed proved the army right about something.

The floorboard stayed where it was.

I don't know how I did it. That's the honest answer. While Voss inventoried our aunt's opinions on the weather in Torshalla, I crossed the room, knelt as if examining the skirting, and slid my penknife under the proud edge of the plank. It lifted. Beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth, flat and familiar and heavier than any object has a right to be, lay the ledger.

I had never actually seen it before. I had imagined it a hundred times — imagined it as either a treason or a sacrament depending on the hour — and here it was, just a ledger, a stationer's ledger with a cracked spine, the kind sold three for a crown in any port town. Freya's handwriting showed at the top of the visible page in columns. Names. Dates. Small careful notes in the margins, cramped down to fit.

*Private E. Halloran, 2nd Btn. Not killed advancing. Killed refusing.*

That was all I read. One line. It was enough to change the temperature of my blood, and I wrapped the thing back in its oilcloth and did not read another word.

Here is what went through my head, in order, standing in my sister's bedroom in an army uniform holding evidence against her:

First: take it. Hand it to Voss, add it to the inventory, let the machinery grind forward the way machinery grinds. Duty. Order. The whole architecture I had been living inside since I put the uniform on — the architecture that told me a clerk's honesty was the last honest thing left in a dishonest machine.

Second: the machine writes *killed refusing* as *died honorably in action*, and calls it bookkeeping.

Third: my sister's thumb, pressing circles into soft wax, round and round.

I put the ledger inside my coat.

Anselm watched me do it. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only here in the privacy of my own conscience: Anselm watched me do it and then turned deliberately to the window and began inspecting the sash cord for defects, humming, giving me his back like a man closing a door on purpose so the person behind him can slip through.

* * *

The forging took until midnight.

This is the part no one tells you about betraying your uniform: it is mostly paperwork. A search that finds nothing must be documented as finding nothing, and a document that says nothing was found must be believed, and so I sat in the requisitioned customs house with a lamp turned low and wrote the most consequential fiction of my life:

*Sector Three complete. Residence of F. Montrose: no unauthorized documentation recovered. One strongbox, contents civilian (deeds, correspondence, currency, 11 cr.), logged Appendix C. Outbuildings negative. Search conducted per regulation 114-b.*

Regulation 114-b. I cited a regulation to cover a sin against the regulation, which I believe makes me either a hypocrite or a bureaucrat, and possibly those are the same animal.

My hand was steady until the last line and then it wasn't, and I had to blot a small gray cloud where my pen had hesitated over the word *negative*. I blotted it. Ink will forgive almost anything if you catch it in time. That seemed, at midnight, like the profoundest theological observation ever made, and I sat back and laughed once, alone, the way my sister laughs at nothing in the scullery, and understood suddenly where she learned it.

* * *

Captain Ellery read my report on Thursday morning.

He read it standing at his window in the customs house, which he had furnished with a desk facing the door so that every visitor entered into his sightline like a clerk entering judgment, and he read it twice, and then he set it down on the desk with both hands flat on either side of it, the way a man squares a map before planning a battle.

"Sergeant-clerk Montrose," he said. He always used the full rank; it was either respect or a reminder, and with Ellery it was impossible to tell which and probably didn't matter. "Your sector was clean."

"Yes, sir."

"Thoroughly clean."

"To regulation, sir."

"Mm." He turned. He had gray eyes, Ellery, the exact color of the sea before weather, and he looked at me the way he looked at manifests — reading not the entries but the erasures, the spaces, the weight of ink where ink shouldn't concentrate. "Do you know what I find interesting about clean sectors, Sergeant-clerk?"

"No, sir."

"They're rare. In four months of searching this coast, I have found contraband letters in nine houses out of eleven, unauthorized newspapers in six, hoarded sugar in fourteen, and in one case a entire wireless set hidden in a coffin, which speaks well of the undertaker's ingenuity and poorly of his piety." He picked up my report again, delicately, by one corner. "People keep things. It's what people are *for*, apparently. And yet your sector — containing, unless my maps lie, a hospital, a schoolhouse, and a woman whom three separate informants describe as writing constantly — contains nothing. Nothing at all."

"The informants may be describing letters, sir. Family correspondence."

"They may." He set the report down and squared it with the edge of the desk, precisely, so that its borders ran parallel with the desk's borders, and I understood that I was looking at a man who needed the world in parallel lines and could not rest until every edge matched. "Your sister, Sergeant-clerk. You searched your sister's house personally."

The lamp in my memory: the oilcloth, the cracked spine, *killed refusing*.

"Personally, sir."

"And found nothing."

"Nothing at all, sir."

He regarded me for a long moment. Outside, a supply wagon ground past, and gulls wheeled over the harbor screaming their eternal litigation, and somewhere down the coast a hospital ship was loading berths that I knew nothing about yet, thank God, thank God I knew nothing yet.

"Curious," Ellery said softly. "Because I have a witness — a dune keeper, unreliable, half-mad with loneliness, but occasionally useful the way broken things are useful — who swears he saw your sister rowing to the point at dusk two weeks ago. And my casualty returns for that fortnight contain discrepancies I could drive a gun carriage through. And now the one house in this town I'd stake my commission on comes back *clean*, searched by a clerk who shares a surname with its occupant." He folded his hands. "I don't believe in coincidence, Montrose. I believe in arithmetic. And the numbers say pages are missing from my world."

"Sir, if there were pages, the search would have—"

"Yes," he said pleasantly. "It would have, wouldn't it. Which is why, tonight, I'm conducting a second search of Sector Three myself. Unannounced. Unrecorded." He smiled, and it was the worst smile I had ever seen on a man, because it was patient. "Bring a lamp, Sergeant-clerk. Floorboards read differently by lamplight. And afterwards, you and I are going to have a long conversation with your sister — she'll be home by then, I've sent a corporal to collect her — about where a woman keeps the things that matter to her."

He turned back to the window, dismissal complete, and I saluted the gray back of his coat and walked out on legs that belonged to someone else, doing the only arithmetic I had left: how far it was to the schoolhouse, whether I could reach the floorboard before the corporal reached my sister, and what, in God's name, a man was supposed to do with a stolen ledger hidden in his coat when the lamplight was coming.
