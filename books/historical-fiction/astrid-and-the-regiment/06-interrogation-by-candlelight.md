## 6. Interrogation by Candlelight

*Ellery*

Captain Ellery had interrogated smugglers in Hong Kong and deserters in Flanders, and he had developed a theory about lying: everyone lied, but no one lied well after the third question. The third question was where the seams showed.

He arranged his candles accordingly. Two on the table between them, one behind him, so that Mrs. — Miss — Montrose would have to look into light to look at him. An old trick. He was mildly disappointed to note that she seemed to know it too, and had positioned her chair so the draft from the window guttered his back candle every forty seconds. She did not relight it. Neither did he.

"You keep meticulous records," Ellery said. "Your brother tells me you were the town's registrar's assistant for six years."

"I alphabetized things," Freya said. "I would not call it a vocation."

"Alphabetizing is a kind of devotion." He slid a sheet across the table — the requisition inventory Anders had filed, neat as scripture. "Private Hollis, died Tuesday of wounds. The regimental report places his death at four o'clock. Yet Nurse Lindqvist's dosing log shows morphine administered at half past nine, and your own hand wrote the time of death in the ward book as ten minutes past ten. Six hours of discrepancy, Miss Montrose. Where does a man spend six hours that officially never happened?"

"Being missed," Freya said.

Ellery let the silence do its work. It usually did. But she sat in it the way other people sat in church — comfortably, as though silence were an old acquaintance who owed her nothing.

"Somewhere in this building," he said at last, "there is a record. Not the ward book. Something private. Men's names, accounts of their deaths, statements that contradict official reports. Subversive record-keeping, Miss Montrose, is how the War Office phrases it. I confess I'd phrase it more gently. Sentimental record-keeping. Either way, it cannot stand."

"Then it's fortunate," said Freya, "that no such thing exists."

Three questions, he thought. And the seam had not shown. He reached for the fourth and found, to his irritation, that he wanted very much to believe her.

* * *

*Anders*

Two floors up, in what had been the headmistress's office before the war required it to become a records room, Anders Montrose was committing a crime.

It was going poorly.

The transport manifest for Friday's sailing lay open before him, and the trouble was that forgery, like everything else in the army, required forms. He could not simply strike Private Hollis from the berth list; he had to fill out a *Berth Reassignment Notice*, in triplicate, citing a regulation, which cited another regulation, which cited a memorandum nobody had ever seen. His pen hovered. Somewhere below, through the floorboards, he could hear the faint murmur of the Captain's voice and the occasional soft reply of his sister, and each murmur went through him like a bayonet fitted badly to its socket.

He thought — not for the first time, but with new urgency — about the ledger wrapped in oilcloth inside the false bottom of the linen chest in his own quarters, because he had not been able to bear leaving it anywhere cleverer. Clever hiding places were the first place searching men looked. Nobody searched a clerk's sock drawer; clerks were assumed to be made entirely of regulations.

His pen scratched. *Reassigned per authority of…* Whose authority? God's? He wrote *Area Commandant*, initialed it *A. Montrose*, and then, in a flourish of cowardice, added the fictional counter-initials *R.W.* R.W. did not exist. R.W. was going to have a great deal to answer for someday.

"You've been on that page twenty minutes," said Sergeant Dahl from the doorway, and Anders's heart performed a maneuver not covered by any manual.

"Cross-referencing," Anders said.

Dahl squinted at the manifest upside down. "Hollis. Wasn't he the one the Captain's been asking about downstairs?"

"He was reallocated," said Anders, and discovered that his voice held, and that holding it felt exactly like being a soldier and nothing at all like being a brother, and that he could no longer tell which of those was the lie.

* * *

*Freya*

"The deserter," Ellery said.

Freya kept her hands still around the tea she had poured him because it seemed unwise to poison the man interrogating her, even passively. "Which one? There are generally several in any war. It's one of war's few growth industries."

"A private from the Second Company. Whole unit written off at the salient. One man walked out of it alive and kept walking." Ellery turned a candle a quarter-inch, watching her across the flame. "Search parties found footprints in the dunes yesterday. Small ones. Someone has been feeding him, the tracks suggest, or at least not reporting him."

"The dunes are full of rabbits," Freya said. "Rabbits make small prints. I'd caution the search parties against shooting any rabbits wearing boots, however."

Something happened at the corner of Ellery's mouth that in a better man might have been permitted to become a smile. "You are the most difficult witness I have encountered since a Cantonese grandmother who claimed, for eleven hours, to speak no language at all."

"She sounds like a woman of principle."

"She was a smuggler, Miss Montrose."

"All the best people are, in wartime. Smuggling is only requisitioning done privately." She sipped her tea. "Captain. May I ask you a question? You've had six."

Ellery glanced at the candles, tallying, and inclined his head.

"Friday's ship. How many berths?"

"Forty wounded, staff as assigned. Why?"

"And the manifests — are they final?" She kept her voice at the pitch of idle curiosity, the voice she used asking after flour at the shop, but her heart had begun to knock. Because she had seen the harbor charts that afternoon. Because Marta's cough had worsened since morning, and Marta had spoken wistfully, twice now, of her mother's kitchen in the north. Because Anders had gone white at supper and said *supply yard* in the flat way men say things they refuse to feel.

"They are being finalized tonight," Ellery said slowly. "One berth remains unclaimed — a casualty list error, under appeal, unlikely to resolve before sailing." He set down his cup, and his eyes, which had been instruments all evening, became briefly just eyes. "You ask like a woman doing arithmetic."

"I am always doing arithmetic, Captain. I run a hospital."

* * *

*Anders*

The trouble with falsifying documents, Anders reflected, was that it improved with practice. His first forged initial had trembled. By the third form his hand was steady, and steadiness frightened him more than trembling had, because it meant some part of him had decided this was simply work now — his work — and would do it well.

He copied the berth reassignment onto the master list, blotted it, and then sat looking at the name he had chosen to displace. Hollis. Poor Hollis, who had spent six hours dying that officially never happened, and whose death Freya had written down truly because someone had to, and whose empty berth was now, by the alchemy of ink, available.

Available for whom?

Anders did not let himself finish the thought. Finishing thoughts was how clerks got shot.

Below, the floorboards creaked. He heard the scrape of a chair, footsteps crossing the schoolroom below, the Captain's measured tread descending toward the door — and then, horribly, turning. Toward the stairs.

Anders swept the manifests into his case, sat on the case, and opened a completely blank ledger, and began, with the serenity of a man entirely innocent, to rule lines.

* * *

*Ellery*

Ellery paused on the stair, because he was a man who paused on stairs; it was where all the information lived. Above him, pen-scratch. Regular, unhurried, dull as rain. Clerks who were guilty scratched quickly or not at all. Clerk Montrose scratched like a man ruling lines for the pleasure of straightness.

He filed it away — the file in his head was getting crowded — and returned to the schoolroom, where Miss Montrose had not moved except to relight his back candle. She had also, he noticed, moved his chair two inches. He decided not to mention it. A captain must choose his victories.

"Last question," he said, sitting. "If such a ledger existed. Names, last words, truths the reports omit. What would you say to the man who kept it?"

Freya considered him for a long moment over the candlelight, and for once she did not parry.

"I would say," she said quietly, "that a country is not its reports. A country is whoever remembers it. And that the man who keeps such names is doing the country's truest bookkeeping, whatever the War Office calls it." She rose, gathered his cup, and smiled the smile of a hostess ending an evening that had never really begun. "Goodnight, Captain. Do give my regards to the rabbits."

At the door Ellery stopped, hand on the frame.

"One berth," he said, not turning. "Friday's ship. Manifest errors happen, Miss Montrose. They get corrected. If anyone were to attempt to correct one in the other direction — for a deserter, say, under a dead man's name — the penalty is the same as the desertion itself. I mention it purely as arithmetic."

"Arithmetic is my best subject," said Freya.

In the hall, Ellery stood a while in the dark, listening to the house breathe: the clerk upstairs scratching, the nurse coughing two wards over, the sea keeping its own ledger against the breakwater. Three people in this building were lying to him, he was nearly sure. What troubled him, walking home under the cold early stars, was that he could not make himself care in the correct proportion — and that somewhere in the dunes, a rabbit in boots was presumably listening to search parties crash about, and that on Friday morning, when the tide turned, he would be standing at that gangplank with a list of forty names.

He counted the berths twice in his office by candlelight.

Thirty-nine occupied. One open.

He wrote in his pocket diary, beneath tomorrow's date, a single word: *Watch.*
