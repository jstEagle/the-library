## 7. Three Names, One Berth

The arithmetic began, as most terrible things do, with an ordinary morning.

I was restocking the linen shelf in the schoolhouse ward — the one that had been the geography cupboard, and still smelled faintly of chalk and old maps — when the supply-yard whistle went up. Not the short blast that meant a crate had arrived. The long, rising scream that meant someone was caught in the machinery of the war effort.

I knew before I reached the door whose name I would be asked to write down.

* * *

They had brought Anders to the far end of the ward, behind the canvas screen, because a captain's clerk — even a broken one — was apparently owed a dignity the privates were not. I pushed through the screen and found him propped on a cot, his left leg bent at an angle legs are not meant to bend, his face the color of the oatmeal the cooks served on Tuesdays.

"Freya." He managed something like a smile. "Before you say anything — the crate was mislabeled. I was only correcting the manifest."

"You were standing under a crane," I said, "correcting the manifest."

"The crate was mislabeled *from* the crane," he said, and then the pain took the joke away from him, and he gripped my hand hard enough to grind the small bones together, and I let him.

The surgeon — a tired, kind man named Lindqvist who had been a village doctor before the war made him an expert in amputations — looked at the leg, looked at me, and said the thing I already knew. "He needs the ship. There's infection setting in, or there will be by tomorrow night. The *Sovereign* sails on Friday's morning tide. If he's not on it, I can't promise him anything but a long fever and a short prayer."

Friday. The *Sovereign*. The hospital ship I had watched being fitted out in the harbor for a week, its white hull and green stripe going up plank by plank like a promise being painted.

"And the berths?" I asked.

Lindqvist's eyes did the thing that tired kind men's eyes do when they are sorry. "Full. Every one spoken for by the list from headquarters." He paused. "Though there's talk of one berth unclaimed. A sergeant who died Tuesday. The paperwork hasn't caught up with him yet." He said it without judgment, the way one mentions weather. "Paperwork is slow. Men are fast."

I sat with Anders while the laudanum took him, and he held my wrist and said, drowsily, "Freya. If it comes to a choice —"

"Don't be absurd," I said. "It won't come to a choice. Things never come to a choice. They come to a moment, and then afterward everyone pretends it was a choice."

"That's very wise," he murmured, "and very frightening," and then he slept, and I sat in the chalk-smelling dark and did not pray, because I have never been able to pray to anything that keeps ledgers, and God, I suspect, is in the same business I am.

* * *

Tomas was waiting for me in the dunes that evening, in the hollow between the second and third marram banks where I'd made him a nest of sacking and my old green coat. He had gotten better at waiting. The first week he had flinched at every gull. Now he sat still as the land itself, and only his eyes moved, tracking me down the path.

"You're limping," he said.

"I stood on a bedpan. The ward is a hazard to navigation." I sat down beside him and passed the bread and the twist of salt fish, and he ate the way he always ate — quickly, guiltily, as if food were something he'd been caught stealing.

"There's news," I said. "The ship sails Friday. There's one berth that may fall free."

His hands went still around the bread. He looked at me, and I watched him understand it, and I watched him refuse it, both in the space of a breath.

"No," he said.

"Tomas —"

"No." He set the bread down, which for Tomas was a gesture of tremendous gravity. "You have a brother. I've heard you talk of him. A brother who wears the uniform and hates it and loves you anyway. I'm a man whose own army wrote him off like a spent cartridge. If they catch me, they shoot me — that's arithmetic too, and it's simple. But you don't spend your family on a stranger."

"You're not a stranger. You're in my ledger."

"Then cross me out," he said, and his voice cracked on it, and he picked the bread back up and ate it fiercely, staring at the sea, because a man can only be noble for about a minute before his body betrays him with hunger.

I let him finish eating. Then I said, "There's a third name."

He turned.

"Marta. Nurse Marta Lindgren. She took shrapnel in the abdomen during the advance — the same botched one, the one Captain Ellery read his tidy report about. The surgeons can't repair it. She's dying, Tomas, slowly and in great pain, and she has a mother in Uppsala who has already lost two sons and believes — because the army told her so — that her daughter is safe and well and serving with distinction." I heard my own voice go thin and hard. "Marta asked me today, very politely, the way she asks for everything, whether I thought the ship would have room. She asked me as if I were the kind of person who would know. As if I were the kind of person who could do something."

The tide came in below us, unhurried, filling its ledger.

"Three names," Tomas said quietly. "One berth."

"One berth."

He was silent a long time. Then: "Your brother. What's his name? His real one."

"Anders. Anders Montrose."

"Anders," he repeated, as if filing it. "And the nurse?"

"Marta Lindgren."

"And me?"

"Tomas Lindqvist." I said it the way I had written it, months ago, in the ledger beneath the floorboards: the private from the 4th Company who had walked out of the ruins of his unit with his senses shattered and his name struck from every roll. "Though the army thinks you're nobody at all."

"Then nobody can't take the berth," he said. "Nobody doesn't exist. You said it yourself — the ledger is the only place I'm real." He almost smiled. "Give it to someone with a family to break."

I wanted to strike him. I wanted to weep. I did neither; I am a Montrose, and we do our weeping in installments, privately, at inconvenient hours. I took his hand instead — it was a thin hand, a clerk's hand, a hand that had never wanted to hold a rifle — and I said, "Nobody decides tonight. Nobody sleeps. And nobody lets me hear any more of this cross-me-out nonsense, or I'll write you into the ledger twice, under two names, just to spite you."

That got a real laugh out of him, short and startled, the first I'd heard since the search parties came through the dunes in October. It carried a long way over the dunes, and we both went quiet after it, listening, but the night gave us back nothing but wind.

* * *

Marta was propped on pillows in the corner cot by the window that had been the schoolhouse's prize pupil window, back when prizes meant penmanship. The shrapnel had taken her appetite before it took her color, and now it was taking her color, and she greeted me the way she greeted everything — with a courtesy so complete it was its own kind of courage.

"Miss Montrose. Sit, please. The chair by the window still has the sun in it. I've been saving it."

I sat in the saved sun. Her hand in mine weighed almost nothing.

"I've been doing sums," she said. "I do them when the pain is loud. It gives the pain something to compete with." Her eyes, still bright, found mine. "The *Sovereign* sails Friday. There's one berth. Your brother needs it — don't look shocked, Miss Montrose, the whole ward knows whose brother that is, he has your chin. The deserter needs it more, and you know that too, because you know everything, you and your terrible secret kindness. And I need it least of all, because I'm not going to survive the passage no matter how white the ship is." She smiled. "You see? I've done the sums. I'm giving you permission to arrive at the answer I've already reached. It saves you the guilt, and I could use the company of a clear conscience at the end."

"Marta —"

"Please." Her grip tightened, and there was more strength in it than there should have been, the last strength, the emergency strength. "My mother thinks I'm safe. If I die ashore, she'll get a telegram in a week and a form letter and a number. But if I die on that ship — if I die *going home*, with the sea under me and Sweden ahead of me — then somewhere in the last hour of my life I'll be almost there. Do you understand? The berth isn't for my body. My body is past help. The berth is for the last hour of my life, and I am asking you for it, and I have earned the right to ask, because I have changed eleven hundred bandages in this schoolhouse and I never once told a soldier the truth about his odds unless he insisted."

The sun had gone off the chair. I hadn't noticed it leaving.

"Marta," I said, "I can't choose tonight. I need —"

"You need until Friday's tide," she said. "Everyone needs until Friday's tide. That's what tides are *for*, Miss Montrose." She closed her eyes, spent. "But do me one kindness. Whatever you decide — don't decide it by the book. I've seen what the book does. Decide it the way you write in that ledger of yours. As if every name were a person, and every person were someone's whole world."

I sat with her until she slept, and then I walked home through the dark town — past the requisitioned church, past the supply yard with its broken crane standing against the stars like a gallows that had lost its nerve — and I went down on my knees on the schoolhouse floor and lifted the loose board and took out the ledger and held it in both hands like a thing that could weigh three lives, which of course it could not, because the weight had never been in the pages. The weight was in me, and had been all along, and Friday's tide was coming whether I was ready or not.

Three names. One berth. Two days.

And somewhere across the town, in a requisitioned office lit by a requisitioned lamp, Captain Ellery was doing his own arithmetic — and I would learn, soon enough and too late, that he had already arrived at an answer of his own.
