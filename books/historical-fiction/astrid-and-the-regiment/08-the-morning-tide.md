## 8. The Morning Tide

**FREYA**

The tide would turn at ten minutes past six, and I knew it the way I knew the names in my ledger — not because I had studied it, but because it had been written into me by forty years of living within earshot of the water.

I had not slept. Sleep, it turned out, was one of those luxuries requisitioned along with everything else: our homes, our church, our schoolhouse, and now, apparently, my ability to close my eyes without seeing three names stacked like stones on a grave.

Anders. Tomas. Marta.

One berth.

I had spent the evening of the day before doing what I do best, which is making lists and lying to myself about them. *Anders must go* — he was my brother, he was wounded, the army owed him that much. But then: *Tomas must go* — because if he stayed, they would shoot him against the sea-wall, and his name existed nowhere on this earth except in a ledger hidden under my floorboards, which meant that if the ledger burned, Tomas Lindqvist had never lived at all. And then, worst of all: *Marta must go* — Marta, who had nursed four hundred men through this war and caught her own death doing it, who asked for nothing but to die in her mother's house in Falsterbo instead of in a requisitioned schoolhouse smelling of carbolic and other people's courage.

Three names. One berth. The arithmetic did not care how gently you held the pencil.

So I rose in the dark, dressed without lighting a lamp — a skill I had perfected, though I confess I once considered it merely thrifty rather than treasonous — and went down through the sleeping hospital to the cellar where Anders lay feverish under army blankets, his bandaged shoulder rising and falling with the careful rhythm of a man whose body was negotiating hard on his behalf.

He was awake. He usually was, at night. Fever kept its own hours.

"You're going to do something foolish," he said, before I had said a word. His voice was dry as paper. "You have your foolish face on."

"I don't have a foolish face."

"You've had it since you were six and tried to sail Father's dinghy to Denmark to join the circus." He attempted a smile, which cost him visibly. "Freya. Whatever you've decided —"

"The ship sails on the morning tide," I said. "There's one berth. I'm giving it to Tomas."

The silence between us had the particular quality of the silence after a wave breaks — the moment when all the water has gone somewhere else and you are waiting to see where it comes back.

"He's a deserter," Anders said at last, not unkindly. "Under a firing order. If Ellery finds him —"

"Then Ellery finds him on a ship bound for the southern hospital, wearing the identity disc of a dead man, and by the time anyone sorts out the paperwork the war will have eaten another ten thousand names and nobody will remember one more." I sat on the edge of his cot and took his hand. It was very hot. "Anders, listen to me. You'll live. Doctor Halvorsen says so, and Halvorsen has never once lied to me, only to the army, which doesn't count. Marta—" My voice did something I hadn't authorized. "Marta can't be moved. She knows it. She told me last night, quite calmly, between asking after her cat and telling me my soup needs salt. She's chosen to die ashore. That's her arithmetic, and I won't overturn it. But you and Tomas — one of you stays and risks the firing squad, and one of you goes. And I cannot choose my brother over a man who has no one else in the world to choose him."

Anders looked at me for a long moment. In the grey pre-dawn light leaking through the cellar grate, his face seemed made of the same material as the rest of the town — worn, stubborn, salt-scoured.

"You could," he said quietly. "That's the terrible thing, Freya. You could choose me, and no one would ever blame you. Not even Tomas."

"Yes," I said. "That's exactly why I can't."

He closed his eyes. When he opened them again, there was something in them I had not seen since we were children sharing a blanket during the winter storms — not fear, but the strange relief of finally setting down something heavy.

"Then help me up," he said. "If my sister is going to commit a hanging offence at dawn, the least I can do is hold the coat she takes off first."

* * *

**ANDERS**

Here is what nobody tells you about treason: it looks remarkably like paperwork.

By five o'clock I was upright, grey as porridge and leaning heavily on a broom handle I had requisitioned as a walking stick — my first honest abuse of office in eleven months, and I found I rather enjoyed it. Freya had fetched Tomas from the dunes an hour before, moving through the marram grass in the dark like a woman who had done nothing more suspicious in her life than gather samphire. Tomas himself came into the cellar pale and shaking, his shell-shocked hands fluttering, and when Freya told him what she intended, he sat down abruptly on a crate and said, in Swedish, "But I am nobody."

"You are somebody," Freya said, with the tone she used on me when I'd said something particularly dense at the age of nine. "You are Tomas Lindqvist, born in Helsingborg, twenty-three years old, who played the accordion badly and loved a girl named Ingrid and walked out of hell when hell told him he was expended. I wrote it down. I remember it. Now you're going to go and be somebody named Private Einar Dahl, and you're going to carry Tomas Lindqvist with you like contraband, and someday, when it's safe, you're going to say his name out loud to someone who loves you. Do you understand?"

Tomas nodded, weeping without any noise at all, which was perhaps the most soldierly thing I ever saw him do.

Einar Dahl. Company C, written off at the second advance, dead in every report that mattered. His disc had come to Freya's ward unnamed, and Freya — my sister, my quiet, disapproving, jam-making sister — had written his true story down and kept the disc, and now the disc would buy a living man's passage south. There was a symmetry to it that would have satisfied even Captain Ellery, if Ellery had possessed a soul to be satisfied with, which the available evidence suggested he did not.

I forged the manifest myself, upstairs, by candlelight, with hands that shook from fever and something else. *Private E. Dahl, stretcher case, priority evacuation.* My stamp went down on the paper with a sound like a gavel. Eleven months of inventory forms and requisition ledgers, and this was the first document I had ever produced that I would not be ashamed to have read aloud at my trial.

"There," Freya said, watching me. "You're one of us now."

"One of who?"

"Criminals with excellent penmanship."

Despite everything — the fever, the dawn, the berth I was not taking — I laughed, and it hurt, and it was worth it.

* * *

**FREYA**

The gangplank at the harbour was a narrow plank-and-iron affair, slick with morning dew, and walking it beside a stretcher borne by two orderlies who believed every word of Anders' manifest, I felt my heart beating somewhere up around my ears.

The ship — *S.S. Meridian*, white-painted, green-banded, her Red Cross ensign limp in the windless early light — sat low and patient on the black water. Along the quay stood a handful of figures: wounded men shuffling toward the plank, a chaplain, a purser with a clipboard. And near the foot of the gangplank, hands clasped behind his back, cap brim level as a judgement, stood Captain Ellery.

My legs very nearly declined to continue. It was only the weight of Tomas's stretcher-passing protocol, and the memory of Freya Nystrom's foolish face, that carried me forward.

Ellery saw us. Of course he saw us; the man probably counted seagulls by serial number. His eyes moved from the manifest in the head orderly's hand to the stretcher to me, standing at the rear like a woman attending a funeral that had gotten out of hand.

"Name?" he said to the orderly, not to me.

"Private Dahl, sir. Priority evac, clerk's stamp."

Ellery took the manifest. He read it the way he read everything — twice, slowly, hunting for the lie between the lines. And then he looked past the orderlies, down at the stretcher, at Tomas's grey face and bandaged hands, and I watched him do his arithmetic in real time: a man he had never seen before, in a uniform without regimental marks, evacuated under the stamp of a clerk who had been injured in the supply yard two days ago and should have been flat on his back.

For one long moment, the whole harbour held its breath — the tide, the gulls, the chaplain, me.

"Private Dahl," Ellery said. "You are being evacuated to the southern hospital. You will arrive, you will be treated, and you will be entered into their records." A pause, thin as wire. "Do you understand?"

Tomas, to his eternal credit, whispered, "Yes, sir."

"Good." Ellery handed back the manifest, stepped aside, and did not look at me at all. "The tide waits for no one, madam. Not even you."

I have thought about that moment every day since, and I still don't know whether it was mercy, indifference, or simply Ellery's arithmetic arriving where mine already had — one berth, three names, and a captain too clever not to see that the ledger he wanted so badly might be safer lost than found. Perhaps he understood, in his bloodless way, that some records are better kept in flesh than in paper.

Tomas reached the top of the gangplank. Before they took him aboard, he turned — a small, terrible, wonderful gesture — and pressed his ruined hand briefly to his heart, toward me. Toward his name.

Then he was gone up the deck, and the plank came in, and the mooring lines splashed loose, and the *Meridian* gathered herself against the tide like an old dog remembering how to run.

* * *

**ANDERS**

I was not at the harbour. I was in the schoolhouse, burning with fever and holding vigil of a different kind, at the bedside of Nurse Marta Lindgren, who had refused her berth with the same brisk authority with which she had once refused to let me smoke in the dispensary.

She drifted in and out. Between the drifting, she gave instructions — her cat, Pelle, was to go to the baker's wife; her mother in Falsterbo was to be told she had been comfortable, which was a lie, but a kind one, and Marta sanctioned it herself, which made it administrative rather than dishonest. Freya arrived as the tide turned, salt-wind still in her coat, and went straight to Marta's side as though she had never been anywhere else.

"Did it sail?" Marta asked me.

"It sailed."

"Both of them?"

"Both," Freya said.

"Good." Marta closed her eyes. "Then it was a fair trade. Write that down in your book, Freya. Somebody ought to record that the arithmetic came out fair, once."

She died an hour after sunrise, with the light coming gold through the boarded windows, holding Freya's hand, and asking — this is true — whether anyone had fed Pelle yet.

* * *

**FREYA**

We buried Marta that afternoon in the churchyard the army had requisitioned, which meant the army paid for the flowers, and there was a certain grim justice in that. Anders stood through the whole service on his feet, grey and swaying, refusing all assistance with a look I recognized as entirely our father's. The chaplain spoke the words. And then, because I could not help myself — because I am who I am, and the ledger lives in me now whether the pages exist or not — I stepped forward and said her name and her story aloud, to everyone: Marta Lindgren, of Falsterbo, who nursed four hundred men, who loved a bad-tempered cat, who gave her berth away with a joke on her lips.

Nobody stopped me. Several people wept. The baker's wife, holding Pelle in a flour sack, said, "Amen," very loudly, in the tone of a woman daring anyone to object.

It was only afterward, walking home through the dunes in the long gold evening, that Anders took my arm and stopped me, his face gone strange.

"Freya," he said. "When I got back to the office this afternoon — Ellery had cleared my desk. All of it. Manifests, files, my inventory of your quarters. Everything's boxed and sealed, and there's a transport order on his desk dated tomorrow." His fingers tightened on my arm. "He's not finished with us. Whatever he found — whatever he suspects — he's assembling a case. And Freya — the seal on your floorboard was broken when I checked this morning. The ledger wasn't where I hid it. I think Ellery already has it."

The tide sighed against the sand below us, going out and out, taking the day with it.

Tomorrow, then. Tomorrow we would learn what the captain's arithmetic had bought.
