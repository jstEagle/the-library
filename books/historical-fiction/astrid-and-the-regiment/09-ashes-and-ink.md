## 9. Ashes and Ink

The morning after the ship sailed, Captain Ellery sent for me.

Not a summons, exactly — the army did not stoop to summons in towns it had swallowed whole. It was a typed slip delivered by a corporal too young to shave properly, informing me that I would present myself at the requisitioned vicarage at eleven o'clock, and that I should bring "any documents pertaining to the welfare of convalescent personnel." The phrase had the flavor of something drafted at midnight by a man who suspected he was losing.

I put on my good dress. I brushed my hair until it behaved. And beneath my petticoat, pinned against my ribs with three brass safety pins that dug into me with every step like small disapproving fingers, I carried nothing at all.

That was the trick of it. The ledger — the real one, with its two years of names and last words, its ink-blot where Private Hollis had gripped my wrist too hard — had been gone since Tuesday night, when Anders had come to my room white as church linen and said, *Freya, they're going to search your quarters tomorrow. Where is it?* He had hidden it himself; I still didn't know where. But Ellery believed the ledger existed and believed it was mine, and a man who believes a thing exists will accept almost any paper bearing its resemblance.

So on Wednesday evening I had sat at my kitchen table with a blank exercise book from the schoolhouse stores, a bottle of ink, and the strangest task of my life: forging my own diary. I gave it a convincing stain of tea. I smudged the pages. I wrote in it the sorts of things Ellery wanted to find — weather, ration counts, mild complaints about the matron's soup — all dull enough to be innocent, all wrong enough to be found out if anyone looked closely. No one would look closely. That was the wager. A man hunting a subversive does not stop to read a grocery list.

Anders had argued against it. "If he thinks you've handed him a fake—"

"He won't think it," I said, "because men like Ellery never believe a woman's real records are worth anything anyway. They'll search for codes in the margins and find my opinion of turnips."

He laughed despite himself — the first laugh I'd heard from him since the supply yard took his shoulder — and then winced, because laughing pulled at stitches, and then swore, and then apologized for swearing, and I told him that after two years of soldiers dying around me, an oath was practically a lullaby.

Now I walked to the vicarage through streets gone strange with khaki. The baker's boy had been conscripted as a runner and sprinted past me with his bread basket still under his arm, as though the war might be fought on crusts. Mrs. Aldous stood at her gate watering geraniums that the army had already trampled twice, out of what I can only call botanical defiance.

Ellery received me in the vicar's study, which he had improved considerably by ruining. Maps everywhere. The vicar's good chairs pushed into corners. He looked tired in a way that uniform could not hide — a man who had been reading casualty figures the way other men drink, steadily and without satisfaction.

"Miss Montrose." He gestured to a chair. "Thank you for coming."

"You didn't leave me much alternative."

"No," he agreed, and there was something almost honest in it. "I didn't." He opened a drawer and laid the exercise book between us. My false child. "Your brother returned this to the inventory yesterday. It was listed among items recovered from the schoolhouse during the transfer of the ward. Curious, that it wasn't logged when the rest were."

"Paperwork is not my department, Captain. Ask the army about the army's lapses."

His mouth twitched. He might once have been handsome before the war filed his face down to function. "Do you know what this contains?"

"Opinions on turnips, mostly."

He turned pages. I watched him with the serenity of a woman who has spent two years watching men die and cannot be much moved by a man reading. He reached the end. He closed the book. For a long moment he said nothing, and I understood — the way one understands weather changing — that he knew. Not proof. Knowledge without proof, which is the worst kind for men like him, because it cannot be court-martialed.

"The private's account," he said quietly. "The advance that failed. Someone has been recording what my officers omit. Names. Last words. Statements that contradict official reports." He looked up. "I don't care about the sentiment, Miss Montrose. Sentiment is free. I care that a record exists which makes the army answerable — to whom, I don't know, and that is precisely what frightens the people above me."

"Perhaps," I said, "the people above you ought to be frightened of being answered."

The words were out before I could weigh them. He studied me for a long moment, and then — and this I did not expect — he nodded slowly, as if I had confirmed a suspicion he'd been carrying like a stone.

"Where is the ledger?"

"Gone."

"Gone where?"

"Burned, most likely. Paper burns, Captain. It's the one thing paper does reliably."

"And your brother? Your brother who removed evidence from a sealed inventory?"

"My brother," I said, "was carrying crates for your quartermaster when a sling snapped. He is upstairs at this moment with his shoulder sewn shut, and whatever he did or didn't do, he did while concussed. If you want someone accountable, you have me. Take me and leave him his commission. He believes in this uniform more than I ever have. Don't make him pay for my opinions."

Something moved behind Ellery's eyes. I have thought about that moment often since — whether it was mercy, or exhaustion, or the particular loneliness of a man who suspects the war will not reward him and wants, just once, to be on the side of the living.

"The transport manifests are already filed," he said at last, which was an answer to a question I hadn't dared ask aloud — whether Anders's name appeared on them, whether the falsified lists that had put Tomas Lindqvist aboard the hospital ship under a dead man's name would survive scrutiny. Filed meant safe. Filed meant Ellery had chosen not to look. "The matter of the ledger, however, remains open. Evidence must be produced or accounted for. Those are my orders."

"I understand."

"Do you?" He almost smiled. "I think you understand a great deal better than you let anyone see. Eleven o'clock tomorrow. The churchyard. Bring the ledger, or bring ashes that look like one. I find I'm no longer particular."

* * *

I did not sleep. I went instead to the schoolhouse, where Marta lay in the bed by the window she had asked for — the one facing the sea she would never sail on now, though none of us said so. Her breath came in shallow tides. She was twenty-six and had bandaged half the town's wounded and had wanted only, she told me once, to die in sight of water she'd swum in as a girl.

"Did you give it to him?" she whispered, when I sat down.

"Not yet."

"Freya." She said my name the way you set down something heavy. "You can't carry every name yourself. Even the sea gives some things back to the sky."

"I know," I said, and wept a little, and she held my hand with fingers that had grown light as gulls' bones.

In the morning I carried the exercise book to the churchyard, and behind me, uninvited, came half the town. Word travels fast in occupied places — faster than orders, faster than truth. Mrs. Aldous with her geranium-stained hands. The baker's boy. Old Pastor Lund, permitted back into his own churchyard to watch another man's bonfire lit upon his own grass. They lined the low wall in silence, the way crowds do at executions, and perhaps that is what it was.

Two corporals had prepared an iron pail and a dish of paraffin. Ellery stood with his cap off, which surprised me. The exercise book went into the pail. The paraffin went over the exercise book. A match hissed.

It burned quickly, the way forged things do — no struggle in it, no weight, flame taking the tea-stained pages with an eagerness the real ledger would have resisted. I watched the smoke go up past the church tower and thought: *that smoke means nothing.* And then I thought: *no. It means everything. It is the price, paid in full.*

When the last page curled to black, Ellery stamped out the embers himself, knelt and did it, a captain on his knees in wet grass. He rose, dusted his trousers, and said, only for me, quietly: "The manifests stand. Your brother is on the next draft home. Tell him—" He stopped. Started again. "Tell him the inventory was correct throughout."

Then he saluted me — actually saluted, a full hand to brim, held a beat too long for regulation — and walked away, and I understood that I would never learn what ledger he kept, or whose names were in it.

* * *

That evening Marta died, gently, between one tide and the next, with the window open and the sea saying what it always says. We buried her two days later beside the north wall, and after the uniformed men had gone, after the wreaths with their printed ribbons had wilted in their cellophane, the families began arriving at the schoolhouse. Not wounded families — *survivors'* families. Mothers and fathers and wives of men whose official telegrams had said *missing, presumed* or *died of wounds sustained in action* and left it at that, tidy as a receipt. They came because word travels fast in occupied places, and somewhere along the way the word had become: *Freya Montrose knows. Freya Montrose remembers.*

They filled the old classroom on Thursday night. Thirty-some of them, sitting in the children's desks with their hats in their laps, lamplight on faces worn thin with waiting. Anders came down on his good arm to sit in the doorway, refusing his bed, pale but present. Pastor Lund stood by the chalkboard as though resuming a service interrupted mid-sermon.

Mrs. Aldous spoke first, and her voice shook. "My Harold. They wrote to say he fell. That's all it said. *Fell.* Fourteen months I've been falling too, wondering."

I had burned the ledger. Every page. And here was the thing I discovered, standing before them with empty hands: I had never needed the pages.

"Harold Jameson," I said. "Private, second platoon. He carried letters in his boot because he distrusted the post. The last letter was to you. He talked about your apple tree — how the apples would be ready and he'd miss them, and he hoped you'd eat them slowly, one a day, so the season lasted until he came home." I stopped, because Mrs. Aldous had made a sound like a stitch giving way. "He didn't fall, Mrs. Aldous. He was hit going back for a wounded man the report called expendable. He got the man to cover first. His last words were 'Tell her about the apples.' I am telling you about the apples."

She bowed her head over her hands, and it was grief, yes, but grief with a floor under it at last.

I went on. Name by name. Private Hollis, who had gripped my wrist and said *don't let them write it wrong.* Sergeant Byrne, who sang to his men under fire and hummed the tune to me when his voice was gone. The boy from the fishing fleet whose real age I alone had known, seventeen, who'd lied to enlist and confessed it to me at the end and made me promise to tell his mother he was sorry and not sorry. Each name spoken aloud into that lamplit room became, as it left my mouth, somebody else's to keep. I watched it happen — watched memory pass from me into them like a lamp passed down a row, each face it touched lighting the next.

Pastor Lund wrote nothing down either. He simply listened with his eyes closed, and afterward he said, "This is how scripture started, you know. Spoken. Carried. Written down only later, and imperfectly, by frightened people doing their best." He smiled faintly. "We may do our best as well."

"No," I said, and felt Anders shift in the doorway, and saw in his eyes that he already knew what I would say. "No writing. Let them live in us. Paper can be confiscated. Burned. A name carried in thirty hearts needs no berth on any ship and survives any tide."

It was near midnight when the room emptied, the last of them lingering at the door to touch my sleeve, to say my name — *thank you, Freya* — as though it belonged on the same list as the ones I'd given them. When they were gone, Anders eased himself upright with a grunt and looked at me across the little classroom, the chalk dust, the empty desks.

"There's a letter," he said, and drew it from inside his shirt with his good arm. "Came with the evening draft post. Addressed to Miss Freya Montrose — but the postmark's foreign, and Freya, the name it's signed with isn't any name I know."

He held it out. My hands, steady all through the burning, all through the testimony, were not steady now.
