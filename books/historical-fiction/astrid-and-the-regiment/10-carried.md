## 10. Carried

**Freya**

The last soldier left on a Tuesday, and the town did not know what to do with its quiet.

We had grown so used to the sound of boots that their absence was itself a kind of noise — a ringing, an emptiness with edges. Freya Montrose stood in the doorway of the requisitioned schoolhouse and watched the final lorry grind down the coast road, trailing its dust like a bad reputation. Someone had nailed a board over the gate where the sentry box had been. The grass there was already growing back, stubborn and green, as though the war had only ever been a poor tenant.

"You could close your mouth," said Anders behind her. "You'll catch flies."

"You could stop pretending you're not crying," she returned.

"I have ash in my eye."

"For three weeks?"

"The wind is very persistent."

It had taken three weeks for the army to finish its withdrawal — three weeks of manifests and inventories, of Anders standing at gates with his clipboard, waving the war out of his sister's town one crate at a time. He had kept the clipboard, she noticed. It sat on the schoolhouse step now like a retired gun dog, and he touched it from time to time as if reassuring himself it would never again be pointed at anything he loved.

The schoolhouse itself was a ruin of good intentions. Desks stacked in the hall like driftwood. The ward partitions folded and leaning against the blackboards. A smell of carbolic that Freya suspected would outlive them all, seeped into the very grain of the building. In the corner of what had been Ward B, someone had scratched a tally into the wainscoting — days counted by some boy who'd survived, she hoped, whoever he'd been.

"We'll need new floorboards in the east room," Anders said, in the voice he used for inventories.

"We will," Freya agreed, in the voice she used for pretending she didn't know why. The east room's boards were fine, actually. But beneath two of them there was a gap shaped like nothing at all now, because the ledger that had lived there was ash in the churchyard, and the gap was only a gap, and grief is strange about floors.

She missed the ledger more than she had expected to. She had thought surrendering it would feel like cutting off a hand; instead it had felt like setting down something heavy after a long carry, and then missing the weight, because the weight had been hers. Some nights she woke reaching for names the way other people reached for water.

But that was the thing she had learned in the burning, the thing she was still learning: the names had not burned. She had recited them for two hours to a room full of widows and mothers and one extremely attentive postmistress, and afterward the names had walked out of that room in forty different mouths, going home to forty different kitchens, and there they lived still. Marta Lindqvist's name was carved on the churchyard bench. Private Oskar Brandt's name was in his mother's mouth every morning over coffee. Names did not need paper. Paper was only ever a ferry. The passengers crossed without it.

"Come on," Anders said. "If we start on the desks today, we might have a school before winter."

"A school," Freya said. "Imagine. Children. Learning things."

"Don't teach them handwriting. You'll make them dangerous."

She threw her rag at him. He caught it, which even after everything struck her as insufferable.

* * *

**Anders**

Anders had expected the town to hate him, and had prepared a whole philosophy for enduring it. He was surprised to find he didn't need the philosophy. It turned out that when you spent three weeks forging manifests to smuggle a deserter onto a hospital ship, the townspeople developed a certain tolerance for your earlier bureaucratic sins. Old Halversen, whose boat the army had commandeered, shook his hand outside the baker's and said only, "Your sister raised you right after all, in the end." Which was not strictly accurate — Anders had been raised by the same parents who raised Freya, mostly in her shadow — but he had learned when to accept a verdict without appealing it.

The work was good for him. He had not known how much he needed work that ended in something visible. A manifest vanished into a file. A mended desk stood in sunlight. He repaired thirty-one desks, counting, because counting was his native tongue, and Freya teased him for narrating each one — "*Twenty-two*," with satisfaction, like a man naming grandchildren.

On the fourth Thursday, a letter came.

He saw it first because the postmistress delivered it personally, which she never did, and because she was practically vibrating. It lay on the counter between them, ordinary as bread. Foreign stamps. An address in a careful hand that neither of them recognized.

"It's addressed to Miss F. Montrose," the postmistress said, "care of the Schoolhouse, Harrow Point — but you'd better open it together, I think."

So they sat at one of the repaired desks — number twenty-two, as it happened — and Freya opened it with fingers that were not quite steady, and read aloud.

*Dear Miss Montrose,*

*I am writing to you from a farm outside Torsten, where I have work and a room and a view of hills that have never once been shelled. I am told I am called Nils Ekman here, and I answer to him readily enough. But a man can answer to a name and still know his own, and mine I owe to you.*

*I remember everything you wrote down. I have carried it across the sea in my pocket where no one could confiscate it. You gave me back my name and so I give you the only thing I have to give: news of it, alive.*

*The doctor says in another year I may sleep whole nights. I plant apple trees now. It is good work, putting things in the ground that are meant to come up.*

*You once recorded my last words. Here are my first ones.*

*With a gratitude too large for letters,*
*Tomas*

Below the signature, in a shakier line, as if written slowly, deliberately, the words she had once taken down in a dune shelter while search parties swept the ridgeline:

*I am still here. Tell them I am still here.*

Freya put the letter face-down on the desk and pressed both hands flat upon it, the way one presses a wound or a blessing, and for a long moment she could not speak. Anders, who had faced Captain Ellery across an interrogation table without flinching, found his own eyes useless and did not trouble to hide it.

"He plants apple trees," Freya managed finally.

"He answers to Nils," Anders said, "but he knows his own name."

"And he signs himself—" Her voice failed, gathered itself. "He signs himself with what I wrote. Anders. He *carried* it."

"Of course he carried it," Anders said. "Where else would it live? You taught half this town that paper burns." He tapped the desk. "People don't."

* * *

**Freya**

They rebuilt the school through September, and if you had asked either of them whether it was work or penance or prayer they would have said yes.

There were mornings Freya painted walls and heard the ward behind them — the coughs, the murmured names, Marta laughing her dry laugh at some joke about doctors. She let the sounds come. She had learned that memory was not a ledger to be balanced but a house to be lived in, rooms opening off rooms, and some rooms sad, and all of them hers.

Anders hung the bell rope himself, badly, and fell off the ladder exactly once, which by his standards was restraint. On the first of October the school reopened, and eleven children clattered in, and a girl named Ingrid — whose father's name Freya had recited in the churchyard, whose mother wept quietly through the entire first lesson out of gratitude too large for sitting still — raised her hand and asked why Miss Montrose knew everybody's grandfather's name.

"Because I write things down," Freya said.

"She used to," Anders added darkly, from the doorway. "Now she just remembers. It's worse. There's no confiscating it."

In the register, in careful ink, Freya wrote each child's name, and after each name, in the column where the old registers had recorded fathers' trades, she wrote the fathers' names themselves, and the mothers', and where she knew them, the grandfathers'. The inspector would find it irregular. She had decided she could live with irregular.

On the last evening of term she stayed late with the register open before her, reading names like a woman counting sheep who loved every sheep individually. Anders appeared with two cups of tea and sat on a child's chair that groaned under him.

"Do you ever regret it?" he asked. "The berth. Giving it away."

She thought of Marta's hand in hers as the tide came up, gray light on gray water, the nurse's voice going quiet mid-sentence the way a lamp goes quiet — not out, just resting. She thought of Tomas among apple trees. She thought of Anders's fever breaking at dawn while she held it off through the long night by sheer refusal, telling him names, all the names, a litany against the dark until he surfaced muttering "*thirty-one desks*" and she had known he would live, because no one dies while being that pedantic.

"No," she said. "I regret plenty of things. Not that."

"And the ledger?"

"Burned," she said. "Entirely. Every page." She smiled at the register, at the small fierce alphabet of it. "Every single word."

Anders looked at her a long moment — the look of a clerk who knows exactly how many desks there are — and then he laughed, and raised his cup, and drank to whatever it was she had or hadn't kept, because in the end it didn't matter. The words were safe wherever they were. They had already crossed the sea in a pocket. They were planted in hillsides and kitchen conversations and a bench in the churchyard and a register with too many names in it, going up green every spring.

Outside, the tide turned. The town settled into its quiet — not the army's silence, emptied of boots, but its own quiet, inhabited, warm-lit. Somewhere beyond the horizon a man answered to Nils and knew his own name, and somewhere in this room a woman wrote it down anyway, just once more, for the sheer defiant joy of it.

*Tell them I am still here,* she wrote in the margin, beneath the day's date.

*We are still here,* she corrected. And closed the book.
