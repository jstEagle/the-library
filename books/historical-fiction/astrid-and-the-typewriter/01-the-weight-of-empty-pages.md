## 1. The Weight of Empty Pages

The lamp had a trick it played in winter. When the oil ran low, the flame turned blue at its heart and threw shadows like ink blots across the workshop wall, so that the hanging proofs of Master Halvard Renn's atlas — the northern coasts, the archipelagos, the long patient spine of the mountain ranges — seemed to bleed into one another, as if all the maps were trying to become a single country.

Astrid Voss had learned to ignore this. She had learned to ignore a great many things in the four months since the funeral.

"Three more plates," she told the room, because the room was her only colleague now and it was rude to leave it uninformed. "Three, and then we are done, and then I will sleep until spring, and then you may collapse entirely for all I care."

The room did not answer. It never did. But it held its warmth around the stove better than most rooms in Kalvik, on account of the flatiron Renn had insisted on heating every evening even in summer — "A warm room is a courtesy to tomorrow's work," he used to say, as though courtesy were something you could measure in degrees — and tonight, the last night of inking, Astrid was grateful for the habit of a dead man. That was the trouble with habits. They outlived their owners the way names do, stubbornly, uselessly, asking to be kept up.

She dipped the pen. She drew it along the ruling line of the Vardel coast, and the coast came into being under her hand, cliff by cliff, sound by sound, exactly as she had drawn it eleven times before in pencil and once in proof and now finally in the black ink that would be etched into copper and never, ever changed.

Except that it would change. Coastlines were liars by profession. The sea ate a meter here, surrendered a sandbar there, and any map was true only in the way a portrait is true — an argument made at a particular moment about a face that refused to hold still. Renn had taught her that in her first month, when she was sixteen and still believed maps were facts.

"A map is not the world, girl," he'd said, tapping the table with his pipe, leaving ash in the grain of the wood that was probably still there. "A map is what someone *wants* the world to be. Which is why you must always ask who wanted it." He'd smiled then, the crooked smile, the one that made half his face apologize to the other half. "And why the honest ones among us starve."

He had, in fact, starved. Not literally — there had been food enough at the end, she supposed, though she hadn't been allowed to see him those last weeks, the doctors being firm and Renn being worse — but the atlas had eaten nine years of him whole, and when it was finished eating, there had been very little master left. He died in October with the title page unlettered. Astrid had lettered it herself. *An Atlas of the Northern Provinces*, by Halvard Renn. No mention of apprentices anywhere, which was correct and proper and which had nevertheless annoyed her for four solid months, because if anyone knew how much of that atlas was hers — every third coastline, all of the river soundings, the entire tedious business of the lake district — it was her, and dead men cannot be argued with.

She blew on the wet line. Outside, the wind came off the harbor carrying salt and coal smoke and the particular groaning of ships that had no business being loaded in December but were anyway, grain ships mostly, bound south. Somewhere down the quay a drunk was singing a song about a fisherman's wife, badly, with great conviction.

"Don't encourage him," Astrid advised the window.

* * *

There were three plates left, and she had a system for them, because grief without a system is just drowning with extra steps.

Plate forty-one: the Vardel coast, in progress. Plate fifty-two: the granary districts inland, the flattest, dullest country in the entire atlas, all straight roads and square fields, which Renn had surveyed himself two summers ago and which had put him in such a mood when he returned that he hadn't spoken above a murmur for a fortnight. She had assumed at the time it was the mud. Plate sixty: the index map, the little overview showing where all the others fit, saved for last because it required everything else to exist first.

She worked plate forty-one until her wrist complained, then switched to the fine work — the marginal numerals, the scale bars, the little ticks of latitude — because fine work was restful in the way that counting is restful. Renn had been particular about margins. Obsessive, honestly. Other cartographers left their margins to apprentices or ignored them altogether; Renn checked hers personally, twice, and had once made her redo the numerals along an entire degree band because the sixes leaned too far east.

"They're numerals," she had protested. "They don't lean anywhere. They're numbers."

"Everything leans somewhere," said the memory of Halvard Renn, in the voice he used for things he considered obvious. "Numbers especially."

She caught herself smiling at the empty room and stopped immediately, on principle. Four months of this. Grief, she had discovered, was less like mourning and more like sharing an apartment with someone who had moved out without giving notice — he kept turning up in her own gestures, her posture, her opinions, occupying space he had no right to anymore and paying no rent.

The resentment arrived on schedule, sometime past midnight, as it always did. It came quietly, standing in the corner while she worked, wearing her own voice.

*You inherited an unfinished life,* it observed. *Nine years of his work, dumped in your lap at twenty-five, with his name on it and his debts under it and his enemies — whatever enemies a mapmaker has — waiting politely outside the door.*

"I don't have time for you," she told it. "I have a coast to finish."

*He got to die,* the resentment said. *That's the part nobody mentions. He got to die before the printers, before the reviews, before anyone could tell him the eastern sheets were too crowded or the lake district dull. You get to be the one who's blamed if it's wrong.*

"That's not—" She set the pen down carefully, because throwing it would mean cleaning the ink off the ceiling again. "It isn't about blame."

*No?*

"It's about finishing." She picked the pen back up. "He started it. I'll finish it. That's the whole of the arrangement, and if my name isn't in it, then it isn't, and I'll be annoyed about it privately for the rest of my natural life, which is my privilege as an apprentice, and which you are interrupting."

The resentment didn't leave, exactly. It never left. But it sat down and shut up, which was nearly as good, and Astrid worked on through the small hours, and the Vardel coast hardened into permanence line by line, cliffs that would outlast everyone in Kalvik, rendered by a girl who had never seen them.

* * *

She slept three hours on the cot behind the drying racks and woke at dawn to gray light and the smell of the harbor thawing just enough to be offensive. There was bread from yesterday, cheese going friendly at the edges, coffee stretched with chicory the way Renn had always stretched it — more habit, more rent-free tenancy. She ate standing at the big worktable, looking at plate fifty-two, the granary districts, laid out under weights.

It really was the dullest sheet in the atlas. Even the rivers out there ran apologetically, as if embarrassed by the flatness. And yet Renn had walked it himself, every mile of it, refusing the survey assistants he was entitled to. Two months in the mud, alone, and come back thin and silent and strange.

"He hated the country," she reminded the cheese. "He loved the coast. Why does the atlas have twice the inland sheets the commission asked for?"

The cheese, being cheese, offered nothing.

She spent the morning on corrections to the lake district — someone at the Geographical Society had written a long, pompous letter about the elevation of Lake Imre, and the letter was wrong, because she had taken the soundings herself and the correspondent had almost certainly taken them from a chair — and it was while she was thus pleasantly occupied in being right that the knock came.

Three knocks. Unhurried. The kind of knock that belongs to someone who knows the door will be answered eventually and sees no reason to bruise their knuckles about it.

"We're closed," Astrid called. "Commission's work is at the printer's Friday. Inquiries by post."

The knock came again, patiently, and then a voice — a woman's voice, low and roughened, like paper that had been read aloud too many times: "I'm not here about the commission."

Astrid looked at the door for a moment. Then at plate fifty-two lying under its weights. Then back at the door.

"We have nothing else," she said, which was true in the way that matters least.

"You have the atlas," said the woman at the door. "And I have letters from your dead master. Dated after his burial."

* * *

Later — much later, when she could think in sequences again — Astrid would remember that she had stood up so quickly that her stool went over backward with a crack like a starting pistol, and that she had crossed the workshop without deciding to, and that her hand on the latch was shaking, and that some cold, clear cartographer-part of her mind had noted, even then, the way the lamplight fell through the frosted glass of the door, two shadows where one person should be standing, and thought: *whoever it is, they are not alone, and they know exactly what they've said, and they waited until the work was nearly done to say it.*

But in the moment itself there was only the latch under her hand and the word *letters* ringing in her skull like a struck bell, and the certainty — furious, ridiculous, enormous — that the world was about to change shape on her, and that she of all people should have known better than to trust a map of it.

She opened the door.

On the step stood a woman perhaps fifteen years her senior, travel-worn, gray-eyed, with the look of someone who had slept badly in several provinces. Behind her, half-hidden in the shadow of the gateway, a second figure shifted its weight — tall, coat collar up, face turned away toward the street.

"Well?" said the woman. "May we come in, Miss Voss? It's cold, and what I have to say doesn't improve with weather."

Astrid stepped aside. The two strangers crossed her threshold, bringing the wind with them, and the door swung shut behind them, and the last afternoon of the old world began.
