## 2. Handwriting from the Dead

The bell above the workshop door had not rung in eleven days, and Astrid had counted every one of them.

She knew this because she had taken to keeping a tally in the corner of her drafting slate, the way Master Renn used to tally tide readings — one small stroke per day, five strokes and a diagonal for a full week. Eleven strokes now. Eleven days since anyone had come to the workshop wanting a chart of the Kalvik harbor approaches, or a wedding map inked with roses at the corners, or even directions to the tanner's lane, which was the sort of errand boys ran and old women paid a copper for. Eleven days since anyone had wanted anything from the workshop of Halvard Renn at all.

She was correcting the coastline of the Vastermoor Peninsula when the bell finally rang — a small, tin-thin sound that went through her like a needle through linen.

"We're closed," she said without looking up, because it was easier than admitting they were open to nothing but grief and lamp oil. "Closed until further notice. The atlas will be completed regardless, so if you've come about the atlas, you may wait like everyone else."

There was a pause, the particular pause of someone deciding whether to be offended.

"Everyone else," said a woman's voice, low and unimpressed, "being whom, exactly?"

Astrid set down her pen. It was a fair question. She looked up.

The woman standing in the doorway was perhaps forty, dressed in a gray traveling coat that had been good five winters ago and was now merely honest, with the road's dust on the hem and snow melting off the brim of a shapeless hat. Her face was narrow, wind-burned across the cheekbones, and she carried herself with the flat practicality of someone who had walked a long way on short rations and intended to keep doing so if necessary. Under one arm she held a canvas satchel, gripped not casually but the way one grips a thing that has been gripped for hundreds of miles.

"I'm sorry," Astrid said, and found she meant it only halfway. "We don't get many callers who criticize our clientele."

"You don't get many callers at all, from the look of the windows." The woman stepped inside, stamping snow from her boots with brisk efficiency, and stood a moment letting her eyes adjust to the lamplight. Astrid watched her take in the room: the long drafting tables scarred by forty years of compass points, the drawers of plates and gravers, the smell of ink and beeswax and the particular chalky cold of vellum. Most people looked at the workshop and saw clutter. This woman looked at it the way a carpenter looks at another man's tools — assessing what could be done here, and against whom.

"My name," the woman said, "is Ilse Brenner." She did not offer her hand. "I've come from Tornvik. That's nine days up the coast, four of them walking, because the coastal packet stopped running after the freeze."

"Tornvik," Astrid repeated. It was a relief-district name. It was one of the names that appeared in the shipping lists she'd spent the autumn inking, grain tonnages out and nothing much going in. "That's a long way to come for a map."

"I haven't come for a map." Ilse Brenner crossed the room without being invited, which Astrid would have found outrageous under any other circumstances, and stopped before the drafting table where the atlas lay open under its weights. For eleven days Astrid had worked on the final plates alone — the archipelago sheets, the deepwater soundings Renn had never finished — and she had kept the atlas covered when she slept and uncovered when she worked, the way one keeps vigil over something between a coffin and a cradle.

Ilse Brenner looked at it the way one looks at neither. She looked at it the way one looks at a locked door.

"Master Halvard Renn," she said. "Died the fourteenth of September. Buried the nineteenth, in the churchyard on the hill, with half the town at the graveside and the other half wishing they'd gone. Is that right?"

The words landed in Astrid's chest like dropped stones. "You've read the obituaries."

"I've read more than that." The woman turned. And now something in her face changed — softened was too generous a word; it *loosened*, the way a knot loosens just before it either slips free or pulls tighter. "Apprentice. Four years. Came to him from the inland almshouse school with a hand too shaky for lettering and an eye too good to waste. He wrote to a colleague about you once. 'She measures like she's asking the coast a question and won't leave until it answers.' Does that sound like your master?"

Astrid's throat had gone tight. "Yes."

"Good. Then you'll know I'm not lying when I tell you the rest." Ilse Brenner set her satchel on the drafting table, directly on top of the blotting paper, which offended some deep professional instinct in Astrid and therefore, oddly, steadied her. "But first I want you to do something for me. I want you to look at these letters, and I want you to tell me whose hand they're in. Because I already know. But I need to hear it from someone who knows better than I do."

She opened the satchel and drew out a bundle tied in oilcloth — letters, two dozen of them at least, bound in packets with the worn string of someone who has opened and re-tied them many times. She laid three on the table, untied, facing Astrid.

Astrid did not want to look. Some instinct older than sense told her not to look, the same instinct that makes a person refuse a doctor's diagnosis by simply not attending the appointment. But her hands were already moving, because hands trained to examine fine lines do not ask permission of the heart.

She looked.

The first letter was addressed to a *Fru K. Sandel, Tornvik*, in a slanted, economical script, each letter formed with the compression of a man who resented wasting ink on flourishes. The lowercase *g* with its flattened bowl. The numerals with their peculiar hooked fours, made that way because Renn had broken his right wrist at nineteen and never fully trusted it again. The little habit of crossing his *t*s twice when he was writing quickly, so that they came out looking like small fences.

Astrid had spent four years reading that hand. She had learned letterforms from it before she ever learned them from a copybook. She had inked corrections over it and been scolded for the thickness of her lines. She had, three weeks ago, held a death certificate in that same hand — no, not in that hand; the certificate had been printed, signed by the district physician, and she had thought then how strange it was that no part of his death bore any mark of his own making.

The date on the letter was the second of October.

Five days after the funeral. Five days after Astrid had stood in the churchyard in the rain with her master's grave muddy and unfinished beside her, and had promised the mud she would finish the atlas.

"That's—" Her voice came out wrong, thin as a hairline crack in a plate. She cleared her throat and tried again. "That's impossible. Where did you get these?"

"Read them," said Ilse Brenner.

Astrid read them.

*Fru Sandel* — whoever she was — was being thanked, in the first letter, for provisions received. The thanks were elaborate and slightly odd, gratitude wound around phrases that seemed to be saying one thing and counting another. *The twelve sacks arrived whole and the third of them lighter than marked, which we will discuss when we can speak plainly.* In the margins, small numerals marched in tidy columns: *12/3/7 — 41 — 12/9/2.* Coordinates, Astrid thought automatically, except that coordinates are written in degrees and minutes and these were not those. They were something else. A bookkeeper's shorthand. A cipher.

The second letter, dated the eleventh of October, contained a sentence that Astrid read four times because her mind refused to hold it:

*I know what they will say of me, and it is convenient that they say it. Grief is a kind of darkness, and darkness hides a traveler well. Tell your brother the numbers must go where numbers can be read.*

"He's dead," Astrid said. She heard her own voice as if from across the workshop, across the harbor, across the frozen sea. "I buried him. I chose the plot myself because he once said he wanted to see the masts from wherever he ended up, and there are exactly three places in that churchyard where you can see the masts, and I measured them."

Something flickered across Ilse Brenner's face — not pity, exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a woman who had also once measured a grave's worth in practical terms because practical terms were the only ones that kept her upright.

"I know what you buried," she said quietly. "I'm telling you it wasn't him."

* * *

For a while, neither of them spoke. The stove ticked as it cooled. Outside, a gull complained about the century in general.

Astrid found she had sat down without deciding to, on the low stool at Renn's end of the drafting table, the seat she had never once taken while he lived and had not permitted herself since he died. The letters sat before her like a row of small accusations.

It was absurd. It was cruel. Whoever this woman was — and Astrid's suspicion was climbing back up its ladder rung by rung — she had chosen a wicked thing to lie about, if she was lying. To forge a dead man's hand and dates and private turns of phrase, the *hooked fours*, the double-crossed *t*s, the exact dry humor of *it is convenient that they say it* — that was not the work of a swindler. Swindlers forged signatures. Nobody forged a soul.

And yet.

"If he's alive," Astrid said slowly, "then why—"

"Why let the whole port believe otherwise? Why let *you*?" Ilse pulled the second stool — the apprentice's stool, the wobbly one — over to the table and sat down across from her, elbows on her knees, close enough that Astrid could see the exhaustion underneath the iron in her face. Nine days up the coast, four of them walking. The woman had spent everything she had to get here. "Because someone wants Halvard Renn dead, Miss Voss. Not disgraced. Not fined. Dead. And the men who want it have enough power that dying on paper is the only safety he could buy. He bought it with your grief. I expect he'll answer for that someday, and I hope I'm there to watch. But he's alive, and he sent me, and before you throw me out into the snow, you should understand why."

"Then tell me why."

Ilse Brenner glanced down at the open atlas, at the weights holding its pages, at the archipelago sheet with its patient inked islets.

"Not everything," she said. "Not tonight, and not all of it, because there are things written down that shouldn't exist in writing until the moment they can do the most damage. You'll forgive me if I've come nine hundred versts to be careful. But I'll tell you this much." She drew a breath, and when she spoke again her voice had changed — flattened, stripped of performance, down to the bare plank of it. "In the autumn of ninety-six, the relief grain for the Tornvik districts rotted in its warehouses while the districts starved. Everyone said it was bad luck. Bad weather, bad roads, bad accounting. My brother was a fisherman on the Sandel routes. He didn't die of bad luck. He died of a ledger with honest totals and dishonest contents, and I have spent three years learning to read ledgers the way your master taught you to read coastlines. And your master, Miss Voss, spent the last two years of his supposed life doing the same thing. Recording. Measuring. Putting down what the officials actually did, in a place where nobody would think to censor it, because nobody censors maps."

The word landed like a hook finding flesh.

Astrid looked at the atlas.

Two hundred and six plates. Coastlines surveyed over nineteen years. Soundings, harbors, roads, granaries — *granaries*, she thought suddenly, and her stomach turned over — every symbol placed by Renn's hand or guided by it, every marginal note in his cramped, fenced, hooked-four script. She had spent four months grieving over these pages. She had inked them with tears drying salt-sweet on the vellum. She had thought she was finishing a monument.

She had not known she might be finishing a testimony.

"The margins," Astrid said. Her voice sounded far away again, but steadier now, the voice of a woman looking through instruments rather than at them. "The numerals in the margins of these letters. I've seen that notation before. In the atlas. I assumed they were survey benchmarks." She laughed, and it came out cracked down the middle. "I corrected one last week. I thought he'd misplaced a decimal."

Ilse Brenner leaned forward. For the first time since entering the workshop, she looked afraid — and Astrid realized, with a vertigo that tilted the whole lamplit room, that the fear was not for herself.

"Which page?" Ilse asked softly.

"Does it matter?"

"It matters because if you touched it, and the wrong people compare notes the way you just compared notes, then the safest place in this empire for that atlas stopped being safe about a week ago." Ilse rose, went to the window, and eased back the curtain a finger's width, checking the empty street with the fluency of a woman who had checked many streets. Snow was starting again, fat and slow. "Miss Voss. Astrid. I need you to listen to me very carefully, and I need you to decide something before morning, because I don't think we have longer than that."

"Decide what?"

Ilse turned from the window. The lamp caught her face, hollowed and resolute, a woman carrying her brother's name nine hundred versts through the snow.

"Whether you're going to help me finish what your master started," she said, "or whether you're going to let me take it and pray I'm fast enough. Because they're coming for the atlas either way. The only question left is whose hands it's in when they arrive."

Outside, far down the harbor road, a sleigh bell rang once — some late traveler, probably, some harmless merchant hurrying home through the snow. Probably.

Astrid looked down at the atlas, at the coastline she had corrected, at the numeral she had mistaken for a mistake, and understood that her life, like the century, had eleven days left in it as she had known it — and that whatever came after midnight on the thirty-first, it would not be drawn on any map yet.
