## 3. What the Margins Keep

I did not sleep that night. I want to say it was grief that kept me awake, but grief and I had reached an arrangement by then — it took the hours between midnight and three, and I was permitted the rest. No, what kept me up was Ilse Brenner, sitting in Master Renn's chair with her hands folded like a woman waiting for tea, telling me that my dead master had written her letters dated four months after we had put him in the ground.

There are two kinds of nights in a mapmaker's workshop. There are the ordinary ones, when the lamp gutters and the ink smells sharp and the world outside is just the world. And there are the other kind, when you begin to suspect that the map you have spent years making is not the map at all — that it has been standing in front of you the whole time, patient as a cat, waiting for you to look properly.

I lit every lamp in the workshop. Master Renn would have scolded me for the expense of oil — he once delivered a forty-minute lecture on the moral hazards of wasteful wick-trimming, during which I nearly died of boredom — but he wasn't there to scold me, which was rather the whole problem, or possibly not a problem at all, if Ilse Brenner was to be believed.

The atlas lay open on the drafting table where it had lain for eleven months: *The Comprehensive Atlas of the Baltic Provinces*, forty-three plates complete, the forty-fourth half-inked under my hand. Renn's masterpiece. His tombstone, too, in a way, since the Empire's printing license bore his name and the censors had already been through it twice looking for — well, for whatever censors look for. Sedition, presumably, hidden in a contour line. As far as I knew they had found nothing.

I put on my loupe the way a doctor puts on her stethoscope before delivering bad news.

* * *

The thing you must understand about Master Halvard Renn is that he could not leave anything alone. He was a man who sharpened pencils that were already sharp. He re-glued map corners that were holding perfectly well. Once, I watched him spend an entire afternoon adjusting the kerning of a legend — the *legend*, mind you, six words of text no one would ever read — until the letters sat in perfect harmony, and then he stepped back, sighed with satisfaction, and said, "There. Now it's honest."

"A legend can't be dishonest," I told him, aged seventeen and insufferable.

"Everything on a map can be dishonest," he said. "That's why it must be made honest deliberately. A lie of omission looks exactly like blank paper, Astrid. The only defense is to know every millimeter of what you've drawn."

So I started with the millimeters. I began with plate one — the coastline of the northern archipelago — and worked through them in order, the way he'd taught me to proof any document: slowly, twice, and aloud, because the mouth catches errors the eye forgives.

It took me until the ninth plate to find the first wrong thing, and when I found it, I laughed out loud, alone, at two in the morning, like a madwoman.

There was a sounding marked in the strait between Vardø Island and the mainland: *31 fathoms*. Perfectly ordinary. Except that I had personally verified that sounding from Renn's own field notes two years earlier, and the note said twenty-one. I remembered it because I had teased him about his handwriting — his twos looked like sevens if you weren't careful, and here was the proof that even the great Halvard Renn's sloppy numerals could survive into posterity.

Except. Except that Renn did *not* make sloppy numerals on plates. In the field, yes; on the fair copy, never. He redrew everything. It was practically a religion with him. And yet there it was, a thirty-one where a twenty-one should be, sitting in deep water where no ship would ever trouble to check it.

I went through the remaining plates looking for more soundings that disagreed with the field notes. I found eleven.

Eleven wrong soundings, scattered across forty-three plates like raisins in a pudding, each one in deep water, each one plausible enough that no examiner, no rival cartographer, no censor would ever bother to dispute it. Each one, I realized as I wrote them in a column on scrap paper, converting fathoms into plain numbers:

31, 14, 22, 45, 18...

I stopped writing. My hand had gone cold, and not from the draft under the door.

Because Ilse Brenner's letters — she had left three of them with me, laid out on the workbench like exhibits in a trial — were written in columns of figures between paragraphs of perfectly innocuous small talk about weather and postage rates. Columns of numbers in Renn's neat hand. And the first five numbers in the first column were:

31, 14, 22, 45, 18.

* * *

I sat down on the drafting stool, because my legs had opinions about standing and none of them were favorable.

Here is the strange part, the part I have never quite managed to explain to anyone since: I was not afraid. Not yet. What I felt, in those first minutes, was something so much worse than fear — it was *delight*. Pure, singing, scholarly delight. The delight of a puzzle cracking open under your hands after years of staring at its shell. Eleven soundings, chosen to match a cipher. Coordinates disguised as depths. Hidden in the one place no one would ever check: against reality itself. You could only catch them if you knew the true soundings — if you had read the field notes, if you had *been there*, if you were the apprentice who had spent six summers in a rowboat being seasick while Master Renn shouted measurements at her over the wind.

He hadn't hidden the cipher from strangers. He had hidden it from everyone except me.

"Oh, you impossible old man," I said to the empty workshop, and my voice cracked in the middle of it, and that was when the fear arrived — sliding in under the delight like cold water under a door.

Because if the numbers matched, then Ilse Brenner had told the truth about at least one thing. And if she had told the truth about that, then perhaps she had told the truth about the rest: that the atlas contained more than maps. That men wanted it buried. That Renn —

That Renn might be alive.

I got up and walked around the workshop, touching things the way you touch furniture in a dark house. The smell of linseed oil and iron gall ink. His coat still on its hook, because I couldn't bear to move it and couldn't bear to wear it. The chipped blue mug he drank vinegar-water from, which he swore cured gout and which I swear tasted like punishment.

If he was alive, then he had let me bury him. He had let me stand at the graveside in the rain — it always rains at funerals in Kalvik; even the sky attends out of professional obligation — and he had let me grieve for eleven months while I finished his atlas with my own hands, believing I was carving his epitaph.

And yet some disloyal, practical corner of my mind kept whispering: *if he is alive, then whoever killed him — whoever wanted him dead badly enough to need a death staged — is still out there. And now that someone knows the atlas speaks, you are not a mapmaker anymore, Astrid. You are a witness.*

Witnesses, in my experience of novels and newspaper accounts, had short chapters.

I returned to the table. Fear is no excuse for abandoning a proof; Renn had drilled that into me along with everything else. You follow the evidence where it goes, even if it goes somewhere with teeth.

The soundings gave me the key. The letters gave me the lock. I spread the first letter open — the column of figures ran down the right margin like a ladder — and set the list of corrected soundings beside it. Then I did what any apprentice does with a mystery: I looked for the instruction manual. Renn never built a system without documentation. He labeled his spice jars, for heaven's sake.

I found it in the margins.

Not the margins of the atlas — the margins of his field notebooks, the fat oilcloth volumes stacked on their shelf in chronological order, eleven years of them. On the inside cover of the third volume, in pencil, so faint I had to tilt it into the lamplight, was a single line in his hand:

*Depth is distance from the surface. Remember what we measure.*

Underneath it, a tiny table. Fathom-numbers paired with letters of the alphabet. A child's cipher, really — the kind we used to pass notes in school, except my schoolnotes concerned whether Marta Lindqvist's brother thought I was pretty, whereas Master Renn's concerned, apparently, the fate of provinces.

I worked for two hours. The lamp burned low and I refilled it without noticing I was doing it. Rain started against the north window, then stopped, then started again, undecided. And page by page, letter by letter, the atlas began to confess.

* * *

What emerged was not a message. It was a *ledger*.

Each ciphered sounding resolved to a place — but not a coastal place, not a place any honest atlas would chart. The numbers pointed inland, to coordinates expressed in the only units a marine surveyor could plausibly record: distances converted through depth, bearings disguised as compass corrections in the little tables of magnetic variation printed decoratively at each plate's corner. Magnetic variation! I had inked dozens of those tables thinking they were pure convention, the cartographic equivalent of a doily. Every one of them was slightly, deliberately wrong. Wrong in ways that spelled locations.

I plotted them. That part I could do in my sleep; I have drawn ten thousand coordinates. One by one, points bloomed across a spare outline map of the interior provinces, and I confess my heart lifted as they did — this was the work I loved, the pure pleasure of position, before I understood what the positions meant.

They clustered. That was the first oddity. Soundings cluster along coasts, along shipping lanes, along the places ships go. These clustered along rivers — but not at ports. At towns. Small towns. Towns whose names I knew mostly from famine-year newspaper reports, the ones with black-bordered boxes and phrases like *relief measures underway*.

Beside each point, in the margin of whichever plate concealed it, Renn had placed a numeral — small, unobtrusive, formatted exactly like a plate-reference number. Plate 17 carried the marginal figure 4,000. Plate 22 carried 11,500. I had assumed these were editorial remnants, cross-references he'd neglected to remove.

They were quantities. I understood this the moment I totaled them, because the total — I still remember it, I will always remember it — was 61,240.

Sixty-one thousand two hundred and forty. Tons? Bushels? I genuinely did not know, and for one merciful minute I could pretend it didn't matter.

But the letters mattered, and Ilse Brenner had left all three with me, and the second letter — beneath its tedious paragraphs about the weather in the capital and the rising cost of postage — held a ciphered passage longer than the others. It took me forty minutes to work through it with the fathom-key, and when the last number became the last word, I read it aloud to the empty room, because some sentences need to be said in a human voice or they aren't real:

*"Grain diverted at source. Relief convoys rerouted and resold at Kalvik, Danzig, and points west. Signatures enclosed. The districts starve on schedule while the storehouses fill. If I am silenced, the atlas keeps the account. Tell her to finish it."*

*Tell her to finish it.*

I read that last clause four times. Tell *her*. Not "tell the printer," not "tell the committee," not "tell whom it may concern." Her. Me. Sitting in his chair, wearing his loupe on its ribbon around my neck like a relic, having spent eleven months lovingly completing the most beautiful testimony of corruption the Empire had ever unknowingly commissioned.

The official atlas — the one I had been so proud of, the one I had imagined winning quiet posthumous glory for Master Halvard Renn, Cartographer Royal — was, in fact, a ledger of stolen grain. Every elegant coastline a hiding place. Every magnetic variation a confession. Sixty-one thousand units of food that had existed on government papers and nowhere else, while — I did the arithmetic the way you cannot stop yourself doing it — while people in those river towns had eaten, what? Bark bread, in the bad weeks. The newspapers had been specific about the bark bread.

And the signatures *enclosed* — that meant names. Names of officials. Written somewhere in this atlas, ciphered, deniable, invisible unless you held both the field notes and the key and the patience of a saint.

I blew out two of the lamps. Not for economy — I simply needed less light, needed the room smaller. The atlas sat there glowing faintly in the last flame, forty-three plates of my master's genius, and for the first time I saw it as its enemies would see it if they knew: not a book but a bomb, ticking patiently in plain binding, waiting eleven years for someone to read it correctly.

"They will kill for this," I said aloud, and the room agreed with me by saying nothing.

* * *

Now, I would like to report that I responded to this discovery with courage and clarity. What I actually did was make tea at four in the morning, drink half of it, forget about it, find the cold cup an hour later, and say "oh, honestly" to nobody. Grief and terror and triumph sloshed around in me like bilge water, and somewhere in the middle of it all I heard Master Renn's voice, clear as if he stood behind me:

*"A map is never neutral, Astrid. Whoever draws the map decides what exists."*

He had decided. God help him, he had decided that sixty-one thousand missing tons of grain should exist, permanently, in the only form the Empire couldn't burn: distributed, disguised, scattered across forty-three plates in the custody of a licensed printing house, duplicated in every institutional archive that received a copy. You cannot seize what has already been sent to every provincial library in the realm. The atlas wasn't just testimony. It was a backup. He had hidden the truth in the most public document he could devise, because public documents get copied and private ones get burned.

Which raised the obvious question, the one I had been circling all night like a moth around the lamp:

If Renn had gone to such lengths to make himself unreadable and unkillable — if he had staged a death, vanished, passed his secret through a stranger's hands — then why send that stranger to *me*? Why light a lamp in the one workshop the Empire was certain to watch?

Unless the Empire didn't know yet. Unless the only people who knew what the atlas contained were a dead man, a woman with sad eyes and a dead brother, and a mapmaker's apprentice in Kalvik who had just spent the night teaching herself to read her own inheritance.

A knock at the street door, at half past five in the morning, in the rain.

My heart performed a maneuver I believe is medically described as "attempting to exit via the throat." I stood very still. Honest workshops are not visited before dawn; only dishonest ones fear visitors, and mine had become dishonest overnight.

The knock came again — three raps, pause, two raps. Then, muffled through the wood, a voice I knew:

"Miss Voss? It's Brenner. I watched the street all night. We need to talk before the inspectors' rounds — and you'll want to sit down for what I've brought."

Ilse Brenner, soaked to the bone, held a fourth letter in her hand. This one, she said, had arrived only yesterday. It was dated six days ago. And it was addressed, in Master Halvard Renn's unmistakable hand, to me.
