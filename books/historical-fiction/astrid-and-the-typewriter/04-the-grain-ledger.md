## 4. The Grain Ledger

Ilse Brenner had rehearsed this confession on the coach from Reval, on the ferry across the ice-choked sound, and once, memorably, to a very patient milk cow outside Dorpat who had seemed to understand more than most people. She had planned to begin with the facts, laid out like cutlery: date, place, tonnage, names. Facts were safe. Facts did not weep at the table.

But facts, she discovered, required a voice, and her voice had gone somewhere south of her ribs the moment Astrid Voss had set the atlas down between them like a patient on an operating table.

So instead she began with the cow.

"You'll have to forgive me," Ilse said, accepting the tea Astrid pressed into her hands. "I've been rehearsing this speech for eleven days and I appear to have left it in the coach."

"Rehearsing it to whom?" Astrid asked. There was a wariness in her now that hadn't been there an hour ago — the wariness of a woman who had read letters from her dead master and not yet decided whether to be overjoyed or furious. Ilse recognized it. She had worn the same expression in a mirror for two years.

"A milk cow outside Dorpat," Ilse admitted. "A very sympathetic animal. Nodded at all the right moments."

Something in Astrid's face loosened, a single degree. "Master Renn always said you could tell a good listener by the eyes. Cows ranked above most government officials."

"He sounds like he was a sensible man."

"He was an impossible man," Astrid said, and then her voice caught on the tense, and she looked away at the window, where Kalvik's harbor lay gray and iced under the short afternoon light. "Was. Is. I don't know what verb to use anymore."

*Past tense,* Ilse thought. *That is precisely the country we are standing on the border of.* She set down her tea. The workshop around them smelled of linseed oil and lamp black and cold iron — the smell, she realized with a small internal jolt, of her brother's printing apprenticeship, those last ordinary years before the famine took everything except her anger. Anger kept better than most things. It was the one provision that never spoiled.

"Then let me tell you which verb to use," Ilse said, "and why. And then you can decide whether I'm a madwoman, a fraud, or something worse."

She reached into her coat — slowly; Astrid had flinched when she'd moved quickly earlier, and Ilse noted and filed away that this apprentice had already learned to fear hands — and drew out a folded packet of papers, soft as cloth from much handling. Not Renn's letters this time. Her own. Numbers she had copied out by lamplight in three different towns, from documents she was not supposed to have seen, in a handwriting that was nothing like as beautiful as Halvard Renn's but considerably harder to prosecute.

"The winter of '96," she said. "You'll remember it. They called it the Hunger Winter afterward, though at the time the newspapers called it 'seasonal scarcity,' which is rather like calling a shipwreck 'an unscheduled swim.'"

Astrid didn't smile. Good. Ilse hadn't been trying to make her smile, exactly. She'd been trying to make her breathe.

"I had a brother," Ilse continued. "Matthias. Younger than me by four years, older than me in every way that mattered — he was the serious one, the one who saved his wages, the one Mother said would amount to something. He took a post in the relief districts east of here. Granary clerk. Honest work, honest pay, and Matthias being Matthias, he wrote home every week with the totals in beautiful columns. He loved a well-kept ledger the way your master loved a coastline."

The lamplight hissed. Outside, a gull complained about the century in general.

"For the first months, the columns balanced. Then they stopped balancing. Shipments recorded as delivered that never arrived. Weights that shrank between the rail yard and the storehouse as though the sacks had been sweating. Relief grain — *relief* grain, marked with the imperial stamp, paid for by people like us out of taxes collected from villages that were eating bark — appearing for sale in private markets in Riga and Königsberg at prices no honest merchant could explain." She flattened the papers on the table. "He flagged it. In writing. Three times. He was twenty-six years old and he believed, because he had been raised to believe it, that if you showed the Empire its own arithmetic, the Empire would correct the sum."

"What happened to him?" Astrid asked, though Ilse could see from her face that she had already begun to guess. People who worked with maps always knew how much distance there was between places.

"He was reassigned," Ilse said. "Suddenly. To a district even further east. His letters grew shorter. Then they grew slower. Then, in February of '97, they stopped, and in April a man in a very good coat arrived at my mother's door to inform us that Matthias Brenner had died of fever, that his effects had been inventoried, and that the inventory — which consisted of his coat, his boots, and one notebook — had unfortunately been lost in transit." She heard her own voice go flat and distant, the voice she used for this part, and she was grateful for it. Flat was survivable. "There was no fever in his district that winter. I checked. Fever has a way of appearing in records when it suits the people who keep the records, and disappearing when it doesn't. What his district did have was a granary operating at forty percent of its registered capacity while the registered population starved at one hundred percent."

Silence. Astrid's hands were flat on the table on either side of the atlas, as though steadying it against a wind.

"They starved him," Astrid said quietly. "Your brother found what your master found."

"My brother found the *accounts*. Your master found the *map*." Ilse leaned forward. "Do you understand the difference? A ledger can be burned. An inventory can be lost in transit. A clerk can die of a fever that never existed. But a map—" she touched the atlas's cover, lightly, the way you touch a sleeping animal you don't yet trust, "—a map shows relationships. Routes. Distances. Which granary fed which market. Which rail line carried full sacks east and came back empty. You cannot burn a relationship, Miss Voss. You can only stop it from ever being drawn."

"And he drew it."

"Over four years. He traveled the relief districts under cover of surveying work — his coastal commission was real enough, that's the beauty of it, the Empire paid for its own exposure. And he encoded what he found, because Halvard Renn understood from the beginning exactly what would happen to a plain-language record." She spread her copied pages beside the atlas. "I've spent two years reconstructing pieces of this from the other direction — markets, manifests, the testimony of people too frightened to testify. I could prove theft. I could never prove the *system*. He could. He does. It's in your hands."

Astrid looked down at the atlas for a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — the grief in it had gone from soft to structural, a beam rather than a bruise.

"Show me."

"Gladly. Turn to plate thirty-one."

Astrid turned it: a stretch of inland territory, rivers and roads, executed in Renn's immaculate hand. Beautiful. Unremarkable. Ilse watched her eyes move across it and saw the moment they snagged.

"These coordinates," Astrid said slowly. "They're precise. Too precise. He was never imprecise — but this river bend is drawn a half-degree off where it actually sits. Deliberately. I'd have noticed eventually, correcting against the master plates. It would have driven me half mad wondering how he erred."

"He didn't err."

"No," Astrid said. "He wouldn't. So the error is the message." She pulled the plate closer, and now her fingers were moving with a different quality entirely, the quality Ilse had seen in archivists and pawnbrokers and men counting cards: total attention, appetite married to discipline. "The shifted coordinates mark points inland. Not water. Storehouses?"

"Granaries. Every one of them. And the numerals in the margins—"

"Are in his cipher hand. I know this hand." Astrid's voice went strange. "I've known it since I was fifteen. He used it for his private tide corrections, his jokes, his shopping lists. He taught it to me without ever calling it teaching — he'd leave notes in it and make me decode them for my wages. I thought it was a game." She stopped. Ilse watched the color leave her face and then return, rearranged into something harder. "It wasn't a game. It was apprenticeship. He was building a second copy of whatever this is, and the second copy was *me*."

Ilse sat back, genuinely surprised — and after two years of surprises, genuine surprise was nearly pleasant. "Then he trusted you more than I guessed."

"He trusted me more than he told me." Astrid laughed, once, a sound with no humor in it and considerable pain. "That was very like him. He'd hide the truth inside a lesson and let you find it years later, and then act astonished that you were angry."

"He sounds," Ilse said, "like a man who faked his death."

The words landed in the room and sat there. Astrid stared at her.

"You said it yourself," Ilse pressed on, gently now. "Letters dated after the burial. A drowning with no body recovered — or a body recovered and buried so fast the family couldn't view it. Ask yourself who arranged that burial, and who benefited from it. A man who documents the theft of famine relief by provincial officials, up to and including names that reach the capital, does not simply drown in a harbor. Either the Empire kills him — or he becomes a corpse on paper, and lets them hunt a dead man while the living one carries the evidence somewhere safe."

"And the evidence is here." Astrid's hand rested on the atlas. "In a workshop in Kalvik. With me. A mapmaker's apprentice."

"With you," Ilse agreed, "which means the Empire will arrive here, because they are not stupid, only cruel, and cruelty is often efficient. They will come with a warrant worded politely. They will ask for the atlas for 'archival correction' — that is the phrase, I promise you, I've read it before — and archival correction means a stove." She began folding her papers back into her coat, unhurried, because hurry was contagious and she refused to infect the girl. "When it happens — and it will be soon; they let me reach you only because they followed me — you will have perhaps days. Perhaps less."

"Days to do what?"

Ilse met her eyes. This was the part she had rehearsed to the cow, and the cow had nodded, and she had almost believed herself.

"To decide whether Halvard Renn's death was real or convenient. Because if it was convenient — if he is alive — then somewhere out there he is waiting for this atlas to reach a press that will print what it says. And the only route to that press runs through three checkpoints between here and the capital, past every man the Empire pays to make clerks like your master and brothers like mine die of nonexistent fevers." She rose, and her knees ached, and she was thirty-four years old and tired down to the marrow, and none of it mattered. "I am going to that press, Miss Voss. With or without the atlas, though infinitely better with it. My brother starved in a district whose ledgers balanced beautifully, and I have spent two years learning that the sum will never correct itself unless someone forces the numbers into daylight. I'd hoped you would come with me. I'd hoped you'd carry what he built. But I won't lie to you about the cost, because lying is what the other side does."

Astrid stood too. She was younger than Ilse had first taken her for — mid-twenties at most — and she held herself like someone much older who had practiced it. She looked down at the atlas, at four years of a dead-or-living man's secret testimony drawn in her own beloved teacher's hand, and Ilse watched her do arithmetic of an entirely different kind.

"He made me correct plate thirty-one twice," Astrid said at last. "Twice. He stood behind me and made me redo the river bend until my shifted version matched *his* shifted version exactly, and I thought he was being a tyrant about estuary survey data." She closed the atlas and picked it up and held it against her chest, and her chin came up, and Ilse thought: *there it is. There's the thing the Empire forgot to account for.* "The third time he made me redo it, he said, 'Precision is a kindness to whoever reads you after you're gone.' I thought it was about coastlines."

"It was about testimony."

"It was about testimony," Astrid agreed. "All right, Ilse Brenner. You'd better tell me about these checkpoints."

But before Ilse could answer — before she could unroll the route she'd memorized, the river customs house and the mountain pass and the New Year's gate, the whole long gauntlet of it — there came the sound of boots on the cobbles outside. Several pairs. Uniformed rhythm, unhurried, confident, stopping at the workshop door.

Astrid and Ilse looked at each other across the table where a dead man's secrets lay open to the lamplight.

"Sooner than days, then," Ilse murmured, and reached for the lamp.
