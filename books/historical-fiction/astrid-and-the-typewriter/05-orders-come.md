## 5. Orders Come

The knock came at half past nine in the morning, which was suspicious in itself. Nobody knocked on the workshop door at half past nine on a Tuesday. The fishmonger came at seven. The ink merchant came at noon and stayed too long. The neighbors, who had long ago decided that a house full of maps was a house full of bad luck, did not come at all.

Astrid put down her pen. A drop of sepia fell from the nib onto the blotter and spread into a small continent that no cartographer would ever chart.

"Through the back," said Ilse Brenner, who was already moving. She had been in the workshop four days now, and she had learned the geography of the room faster than any student Astrid had ever taught — which was to say she had learned where the exits were first and everything else second.

"And go where?" Astrid hissed. "It's December. The whole world is out there."

"The whole Empire is at that door. Choose."

But it was too late, because the knock came again, and this time a voice came with it, official and dry as old vellum: "Imperial Archive Inspection. Open by order of the Provincial Registrar."

Ilse stopped halfway to the back window and turned around slowly, and Astrid watched something happen in her face — not fear exactly, but the particular stillness of a woman recalculating every plan she had ever made. It occurred to Astrid, absurdly, that Ilse looked like someone doing long division in her head while the schoolhouse burned down.

"Are they looking for me or for you?" Ilse asked quietly.

"I suspect," Astrid said, "we're about to find out together."

She smoothed her apron, wiped her inky hands on it in the way Master Renn had always forbidden — *an apprentice's hands are instruments, Voss, you don't wipe instruments on your clothing* — and opened the door.

There were three of them. The one in front was round and gray as a filing cabinet, with spectacles polished to such a shine that Astrid could see her own worried face reflected twice over. Behind him stood two soldiers whose coats suggested they had been dressed by an enemy of wool. The gray man held a document folder against his chest the way other men held Bibles or babies.

"Fräulein Voss." He read her name off a card rather than trusting his memory, which told Astrid a great deal about him. "Herr Doktor Kesselring, Office of Archival Correction. These gentlemen are here for your protection."

"My protection," Astrid repeated. "How thoughtful. Is something endangering me?"

"The truth endangers everyone, Fräulein." Kesselring smiled without engaging any part of his face below the eyebrows. "May we come in? We are conducting a survey of cartographic materials in the district. Standard procedure."

*Standard procedure* was a phrase Astrid distrusted on principle. In her experience, nothing standard had ever required two soldiers and the word *protection* used like a knife laid politely on a table. But she stepped aside, because refusing would have been its own kind of confession, and because her legs made the decision before her mind could veto them.

Kesselring entered the workshop the way a magistrate enters a home he intends to describe unkindly later. His eyes went over the flat presses, the drying racks hung with proofs, the great oak table where the atlas lay open under weights — and Astrid felt each glance physically, like fingers pressed against her ribs.

"You are completing the Renn atlas," Kesselring said. Not a question.

"I am finishing what Master Renn began. Yes."

"A great loss." He pronounced the words with the sincerity of a man reading a train schedule. "The Empire mourns its servants. The archive will, of course, require the completed work for preservation. For correction." He paused delicately. "Master Renn's methods were... idiosyncratic. There are those who feel his final atlas requires review before public distribution."

"It hasn't been printed yet," Astrid said. Her voice sounded remarkably steady to her own ears, which felt like a betrayal of her actual condition. "The plates aren't done."

"All the more convenient. You will deliver the complete materials — plates, drafts, field notes, marginalia — to the provincial office within three days. Thursday, let us say. Before the holiday rush." Kesselring produced a receipt book from his coat with the air of a magician producing a dove. "You'll sign for this notice."

Three days. Astrid looked at the atlas on the table, at the weeks of her life lying across it in ink and gold leaf, at the ciphered numerals tucked into margins so fine that only someone holding the letters up beside them would ever see the pattern — and understood with perfect clarity that if that atlas walked into the provincial office, it would never walk out again. It would be *corrected*. It would be burned in some courtyard by men who filed receipts for the matches.

And somewhere, presumably, Halvard Renn — dead these eight months, buried under a stone Astrid had helped carry — was the reason why.

"Thursday," she said.

"Thursday." Kesselring wrote it down. He wrote everything down; it was possible he was more frightened of unwritten things than of anything alive. At the door he paused, and the pause had teeth in it. "The stranger staying with you. She is registered?"

"She is a family friend. From the interior."

"The interior is a large place." He smiled his eyebrow-smile again, tipped his hat with two fingers, and left. The soldiers followed. The door closed. Somewhere down the street, a gull screamed what Astrid considered an extremely appropriate editorial comment.

* * *

For a long moment neither woman moved. Then Ilse crossed the room in four strides and shot the bolt, which was a formality — the bolt was decorative, the door was older than both of them combined — but Astrid appreciated the gesture anyway. Some fears want ceremony.

"Three days," Ilse said.

"He said Thursday."

"They always say Thursday. They think a deadline sounds reasonable if it has a weekday attached." Ilse pulled off her gloves finger by finger, thinking. "Kesselring isn't dangerous. Kesselring is a clerk. The dangerous ones sent him."

"How can you know that?"

"Because clerks give three days. Dangerous men give none." She dropped the gloves on the table and stood over the atlas, and for once she didn't look at the margins or the cipher or the evidence. She looked at it the way Astrid looked at it: as a thing someone had made, line by line, at a standing desk, for years. "They won't preserve it, Astrid. You know that. Whatever 'archival correction' means, it means fire."

"I know what it means." Astrid's hands had begun to shake now that they were allowed to, which struck her as poor timing. She sat down hard on the apprentice stool — her stool, worn to the shape of her by six years — and pressed her palms flat against her knees. "I carried him to the churchyard, Ilse. On my shoulders, practically. And now they want me to hand them the rest of him so they can correct it."

"No," said Ilse.

"No?"

"No. You're not going to hand them anything." She said it the way she seemed to say everything — flat, certain, a woman who had buried a brother and had no patience left for softening language. "Look at what you have. The plates with the granary coordinates. The cipher pages. The letters. Everything they want to erase is sitting on this table in a house they know about, behind a door a clerk has already measured with his eyes. In three days they come back, and if the atlas comes with us instead of to them, they will know exactly what happened and exactly who took it."

"So I should surrender it."

"So you should *appear* to consider it. There's a difference, and the difference is three days." Ilse began to pace, and Astrid recognized the rhythm of it — it was the same walk Renn had paced when a plate wouldn't come right, heel-to-toe along the length of the table, turning at the ends like a compass needle finding north. Grief does funny things; apparently it could make a stranger move like a dead man. "Tell me about the tubes."

"The tubes?"

"Map tubes. You must have dozens. The long tin ones for transport."

"In the back room. Two crates, maybe more. Why?"

Ilse stopped pacing. When she turned around, there was something in her face that Astrid would spend the rest of her life trying to name — the look of a person handing another person a loaded thing and hoping they'd hold it properly.

"Because nobody," Ilse said, "has ever once inspected a tube of blank paper. Censors search documents. Soldiers search bags. But a mapmaker's shipping tubes full of blank stock — that's just weather to them. That's furniture." She tapped the edge of the atlas. "So the question is not whether you keep your master's work. Of course you keep it. The question is what travels and what stays. What do they need to believe they've taken?"

Astrid looked down at the open atlas.

The Baltic coast ran across the double page in a line so fine it might have been drawn with a single hair — Renn's coastlines always looked like that, like the sea itself had signed them. Along the margin, in numerals small enough to be dismissed as survey notes, ran the record that had gotten a man killed. Or not killed. She still couldn't think about that without the floor tilting.

"They need to believe," she said slowly, "that they took everything."

"Yes."

"And what actually leaves this house has to be small enough to hide inside something boring."

"Now you're thinking like a smuggler," said Ilse, with the ghost of approval. "Or a mapmaker. Historically, the professions overlap more than anyone admits."

* * *

The back room smelled of pine resin and linseed oil and the particular dust of paper stored well, which Astrid had loved since she was fourteen years old and had once, in an act of desperation, slept inside a roll of drafting linen to escape the cold. She lit the lamp and dragged the first crate of tubes out from under the shelf, and Ilse stood in the doorway holding the light and making the kind of sound a general makes reviewing troops.

"These are all blank?"

"Blank stock. Proofs that failed. Practice sheets — mine, mostly, from the first years." Astrid pulled out a tube and uncapped it. Inside lay a sheet of paper bearing the faint ghost of a coastline she had ruined at sixteen by breathing at the wrong moment. "Renn never threw anything away. He said waste was a form of lying. Though he also kept a drawer of broken pens for eleven years, so his philosophy had gaps."

"What happens to a failed proof, normally?"

"Nothing. It waits. Sometimes it gets rolled into packing material. Sometimes—" Astrid stopped.

Ilse waited. She was good at waiting; Astrid was learning that about her. "Sometimes?" 

"Sometimes a failed plate gets reworked. You grind the old image down, or you print over it. Or..." Astrid knelt and began pulling tubes, laying them out on the floor like pick-up sticks. "Or you mount the new work on the old sheet. The old image sits underneath forever. Nobody sees it. But it's there."

Silence. Then Ilse said, very softly, "Underneath."

"The cipher pages are thin. The letter sheets are thinner. If we paste them down onto blank stock — rice paste, the kind we use for repairs — and roll them into tubes among forty other tubes of genuinely worthless paper, then even if a tube is opened and unrolled by some bored clerk, he sees a sheet with faint marks on it. Survey numbers. Marginalia. Nothing. The real atlas — the printed plates, the beautiful thing, the thing with Renn's name on the title page — that goes to Kesselring on Thursday, wrapped in brown paper like a good little inheritance."

"And the evidence?"

"Goes wherever we go." Astrid sat back on her heels. Her heart was going like a press at full steam, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "In boring tubes. Inside other boring tubes. Carried by two women nobody looks at twice, because God help the Empire if it ever learns to fear a woman carrying map tubes."

Ilse laughed — a short, surprised sound, the first laugh Astrid had heard from her, rusty as an unused hinge. Then she crouched down beside the crate and got to work.

They worked through the afternoon. It was, Astrid thought later, the strangest day of apprenticeship she had ever served: mounting evidence of imperial crime with the same brush she used for repairing tide charts, while outside the window the fish carts jingled past and the whole city went about its ignorance. The rice paste had to be brushed thin — too thick and the sheets blistered, too thin and they peeled. The letters had to be positioned so their creases fell along the natural curl of the roll. Every numeral that mattered now lived under a skin of blank paper, hidden the way a river hides under ice: not gone. Just waiting for spring.

Twice, Astrid's hands stopped working altogether, and she had to sit with them in her lap until the shaking passed. Both times, Ilse said nothing. Both times, when Astrid picked the brush back up, Ilse handed her the next sheet as though nothing had happened, and somehow that was better than comfort. Comfort would have meant admitting there was something to survive.

By lamplight, near evening, they sealed the last tube. Eleven of them, plus four decoys stuffed with genuine rubbish. Astrid labeled them in her plainest hand: *blank stock, assorted, 40 sheets.* The most dangerous cargo in the province, marked like a shipment of nothing.

"There," she said. Her back ached; her eyes burned; she felt, absurdly, wonderful. "Now they can take the atlas. Let them correct it. The argument's already been copied."

"Don't celebrate yet." Ilse capped the paste jar. "Eleven tubes don't walk to the capital by themselves, and Kesselring's friends will be watching the roads the moment Thursday passes without a delivery confirmation. We need a route that doesn't look like a route."

"The river," Astrid said, and heard herself say it as if someone else were speaking. "Coal barges go downriver twice a week. They carry surveyors' instruments for the channel-dredging office — cases, tripods, drawing kits. I grew up carrying those. Nobody questions a mapmaker with instrument cases. It's like questioning a baker about flour."

Ilse looked at her for a long moment. "And after the river?"

"After the river, there's the mountain pass, and after the pass there's the gate at the printing quarter, and I don't have a plan for either of those yet." Astrid stood, knees cracking, and looked at the eleven tubes lined against the wall like soldiers awaiting orders. Outside, the winter dark was coming down over Kalvik, and somewhere in it Herr Doktor Kesselring was writing things down. Three days. Thursday.

She thought of Renn's voice, patient over the drafting table: *A map is never the territory, Voss. It's an argument about the territory. Make sure yours is worth making.*

"Well then," she said aloud, to the tubes, to the dark, to the dead man whose handwriting had come back from the grave to ruin her quiet life. "We'd better leave tomorrow."

From the doorway, Ilse said, "There's a barge at the coal quay that loads at dawn. The captain owes me a favor."

Astrid looked at her. "What kind of favor?"

"The kind," said Ilse Brenner, pulling on her gloves, "that we will regret asking about later. Pack for cold weather."
