## 6. Three Checkpoints

The map of the overland route to the capital covered half of Astrid's worktable, and by lamplight it looked less like a plan than like a wound — a long red line stitched from Kalvik downriver, over the mountains, and across the plains to Vessengard, where the printing quarter huddled inside the old city gate like a secret kept under a hat.

Ilse Brenner stood over it with her hands clasped behind her back in the manner of a general reviewing troops, which would have been more impressive if she had not been wearing one of Renn's old aprons, found hanging on its hook, because her traveling coat had arrived soaked through and smelled of river tar. On her, the apron said *Halvard Renn, Master Cartographer* in faded embroidery, and she wore it with the defiant dignity of someone daring the universe to comment.

"Three checkpoints," she said. "Say them back to me."

"The river customs house at Brannholm," Astrid said. "The garrison pass at Skarven. And the New Year's gate."

"And why three?"

"Because two would be merciful, and the Empire has never once been accused of that."

Something crossed Ilse's face — the ghost of a laugh, quickly suppressed, as though laughing were a luxury she had pawned years ago and could not yet buy back. "Because three is how you break a courier," she corrected. "The first checkpoint catches the careless. The second catches the clever. The third catches everyone else." She tapped the red line where it crossed the Brannholm customs house. "So we are not going to be careless, or clever. We are going to be boring. Boring people pass checkpoints the way rain passes through a roof — nobody stops a leak they don't notice."

"I've never been described as boring in my life," Astrid said.

"Then this will be your greatest cartographic achievement."

They had been at this for two hours. Outside, Kalvik's harbor bells rang the hour through the fog, and somewhere below the workshop window a fishmonger was arguing with a gull about who owned a herring, an argument the gull appeared to be winning. Inside, the atlas lay closed on the shelf where it had lived for eleven days since the letters arrived — no longer a memorial, Astrid thought, but a fuse.

She still caught herself looking at it sideways. Two weeks ago it had been grief bound in leather. Now it was evidence of famine and theft, a coded ledger of men who had sold relief grain while the relief districts starved, and every time she passed it she felt the same vertiginous lurch as stepping onto ice that turns out to be thinner than it looked.

"You keep staring at it," Ilse observed.

"I keep expecting it to explain itself."

"It explained itself to my brother," Ilse said quietly. "Too late." Then, briskly, before the sentence could settle into either of them: "Brannholm first. Tell me what you know of the customs house."

Astrid pulled a stool over and sat, glad of the practical question. "It sits on a bend where the river narrows. Every barge stops; there's no arguing past it. They board you, they walk the cargo, they check manifests against holds." She traced the quay with her finger. "I know it well. Master Renn surveyed the whole reach six years ago — I held the chain for him. There's a surveyor's office on the upper floor, and the inspectors use their instruments room when barges carry scientific equipment, because scientific equipment frightens them and they'd rather wave it through than ask a question they can't understand the answer to."

"So we carry instruments."

"We carry instruments," Astrid agreed. "A theodolite, a baseline chain, drawing tubes. Nobody searches a woman who knows what a stadia rod is for. They search people who look like smugglers. Smugglers look nervous. Surveyors only look cold."

Ilse considered this. "You're enjoying this."

"I am *terrified*," Astrid said, "and enjoying it is the only thing keeping terror from running the expedition. There's a difference between fear and cowardice, and I intend to keep them separated by sheer professional vanity."

That earned her a real laugh this time — short, rusty, but real — and Astrid found she treasured it disproportionately, the way one treasures the first crack in a frozen river: proof there was water underneath, and that spring existed somewhere in principle.

* * *

They worked late into the night, and the work had a strange rhythm to it, because they were two women building a bridge across a gap so wide it kept making itself known in small ways.

Ilse took her coffee black and bitter enough to strip varnish, drank it standing, and referred to all food as fuel. Astrid took hers with an amount of cream the dairy industry could have built a chapel to, sat down whenever sitting was possible, and believed that meals were among the few honest pleasures life reliably offered. Ilse folded maps into sharp military thirds. Astrid rolled them, because folding a map, she had been taught before she could write her own name, was a violence done to paper that paper remembered. Ilse swore in short, effective bursts, like a carpenter driving nails. Astrid swore rarely, elaborately, and only when a pen nib betrayed her, at which point her language could have peeled paint.

And yet, somewhere past midnight, Astrid realized they had developed a shorthand without noticing. Ilse would say *"the second leg"* and Astrid would already be pulling out the garrison pass surveys. Astrid would mutter *"if the weather holds"* and Ilse would already be recalculating the schedule. It was not friendship. It was something more efficient and less comfortable: two people discovering that their hands fit the same work.

"Skarven Pass," Ilse said, spreading the mountain sheets flat. "Garrison checkpoint at the summit road. This is the bad one."

"Why the bad one?"

"Because Brannholm is bureaucracy and the gate is theater, but Skarven is soldiers. Soldiers get bored, and bored soldiers search things thoroughly just to have something to do. Also—" Ilse's finger stopped on the contour lines crowding the pass "—the weather. In December the road closes without warning. If we're trapped at the garrison inn, we're trapped *with them*. Eating beside them. Sleeping down the corridor from them."

"With our cargo in the next room."

"With our cargo in the next room," Ilse confirmed. "Which is why the cargo travels split. Plates in the instrument tubes. Letters sewn into the coat lining. Nothing in any one bag worth hanging us for alone."

"That's a comforting way to put it."

"I wasn't hired for comfort." Ilse straightened, and for a moment the lamplight caught the hollows of her face, and Astrid saw again the thing she had seen the first night — a woman who had walked out of the relief districts carrying a coffin's worth of grief and converted it, grain by grain, into purpose. "My brother weighed forty-one kilos when they buried him," Ilse said, in the flat voice of someone stating a measurement, because measurements were easier than mourning. "He was nineteen. The granaries along his district were full the entire time. Full, and locked, and sold south. So no — comfort was not in my budget. Passage was. Three checkpoints, and we go through them like water through a roof."

Astrid said nothing for a moment. Grief that large made ordinary sympathy sound like an insult, so instead she did what Renn had taught her: she answered in the language of the table. She pulled the Skarven sheet closer and marked the garrison inn with a small, precise cross.

"Then here's what the map says that the soldiers won't want to hear," she said. "There's a surveyor's track above the pass road — Renn cut it himself, taking elevations. It rejoins the road a mile past the checkpoint. Steep, exposed, miserable in snow. But it means that if the worst happens at the garrison, we aren't only standing in the trap. We have a door."

Ilse looked at the faint dotted line, then at Astrid, with new assessment. "Your master planned routes like this often?"

"He said a map should always contain one path the powerful don't know about." Astrid heard her own voice go quiet. "He said that's what maps are *for*, actually. Everything else is decoration."

* * *

The third checkpoint got its own argument, because the third checkpoint deserved one.

"The New Year's gate is different," Ilse said. She had moved around the table to stand at the capital end of the route, and her shadow fell across Vessengard like an eclipse. "It's not a customs post. It's a cordon. When the century turns, the printing quarter is sealed — imperial order, supposedly against counterfeit festival broadsheets, actually against everything ever printed that made an official uncomfortable. Every bag searched. Every parcel opened. Every person passing through the gate identified and recorded."

"Then we don't take anything through the gate at all," Astrid said slowly, thinking aloud, watching the idea assemble itself out of pieces on the tabletop. "The plates and the letters stop short of the cordon — hidden beforehand, in the quarter itself, before the seal goes up. We walk through the gate carrying nothing but our names and New Year's shopping."

"And if they search anyway?"

"Then they find nothing, because there's nothing to find. You cannot confiscate an empty bag."

Ilse stared at her. "You realize you've just proposed smuggling by *not* smuggling."

"I proposed arriving early," Astrid said. "Which is what surveyors do. It's practically our national character. Nobody suspects a person who arrives early; early arrivals are transparently harmless." She was warming to it now, the plan assembling with the satisfying click of a well-drafted coastline meeting its neighboring sheet exactly. "We come into Vessengard two days ahead of the seal. We place the material with someone inside the quarter — a printer we can trust, or at least a printer we can predict. Then on New Year's Eve we stroll through the gate as two women going to a celebration, and the only thing dangerous about us is our appetites."

"A printer we can trust," Ilse repeated. "In a city where the censors pay by the informer."

"Do you have a better source of printers?"

"No," Ilse admitted. "Only a suspicion that trusting people is how couriers end up as cautionary tales."

"Then we won't trust them," Astrid said. "We'll simply make sure that whatever they know isn't enough to hang anyone. Trust is a luxury. Predictability is a system, and systems survive betrayal." She paused. "Master Renn used to say that too. About bridges, mostly, but I suspect he meant it more widely than he knew."

For a while Ilse was silent, rolling the capital sheets into their tubes with brisk, unloving efficiency — folds, Astrid noted with private satisfaction, avoided entirely. Then she said, without looking up:

"You talk about him in the present tense half the time."

The words landed in the room like a dropped instrument. Astrid's hands went still on the table.

"I know," she said finally. "Eleven days ago I would have said it always. He's the only teacher I've had, and half the time he's still the loudest voice in my head, and the other half I'm furious at him for dying and leaving me an atlas I couldn't finish and debts I didn't make." She looked at the shelf, at the closed covers that had once been only grief. "Now I'm not sure which is worse — that he died for it, or that whoever wrote those letters writes exactly like him."

Ilse set down the tube. Something moved behind her eyes — a flicker, quickly banked, like a curtain twitching shut in a house that was supposed to be empty.

"Cargo," Ilse said at last, gently and firmly at once, "is safer when you don't love it. That goes double for ghosts. Whatever the letters are, whoever wrote them — the job is the same. Three checkpoints. Through like water."

"Through like water," Astrid agreed.

But she noticed, because noticing was her profession, that Ilse Brenner had not denied knowing more about those letters than she had told. And she noticed that Ilse had packed Renn's apron into her own bag rather than leaving it, carefully, as though it mattered — as though it were a keepsake, or a debt, or a disguise.

The lamps burned low. Outside, the fog had taken the harbor entirely, and Kalvik existed only as bells and the smell of salt. On the table the red route ran from grief to the capital, three checkpoints strung along it like knots in a rope, and at the far end of it, in nine days, a new century waited to see whether it would begin honestly or not.

"Sleep," Ilse said, dousing the lamp. "Tomorrow we build the false bottoms."

In the dark, climbing the stairs to the loft where she had slept since she was thirteen years old, Astrid found herself thinking not of checkpoints or cipher keys, but of a rainy afternoon fifteen years gone — of a tall man kneeling at a low table, showing a small girl how to hold a pen, saying words whose weight she was only now beginning to feel.

*A map is never a picture, little one. A map is an argument. Learn to draw, and you'll spend your life deciding who wins.*
