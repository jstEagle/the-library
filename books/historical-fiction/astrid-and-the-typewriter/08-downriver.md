## 8. Downriver

The coal barge *Marta* smelled of tar, wet iron, and a decade of sailors' arguments, and Astrid loved it instantly, the way one loves anything that is not a room in which one has been waiting to be arrested.

"You'll want to keep your hands off the rail," said the captain, whose name was Brakke and whose beard appeared to have been colonized by the same coal dust as the rest of his vessel. "River ice takes fingers off quicker than the garrison takes bribes. And that's saying something, because the garrison takes them very quickly indeed."

"I'll remember," Astrid said.

She had boarded at the coal quay before dawn, wrapped in Ilse's borrowed coat — a man's coat, heavy as grief and twice as warm — carrying a leather satchel of blank map tubes, drafting instruments, and, sewn into the false bottom beneath the protractors, eleven letters in a dead man's hand. The atlas itself was gone ahead of her, packed in a crate of church candles bound for the capital, because no inspector alive would rummage through a shipment that might belong to the Church. That had been Ilse's idea. Ilse, it turned out, had a great many ideas, most of them slightly dishonorable, all of them useful.

The plan was simple enough to fit on the back of a coordinate table: Astrid Voss, apprentice mapmaker, was traveling under surveyor's papers to complete a commission for the Imperial Cartographic Bureau. This was not entirely a lie, which was the best kind of not-lie. She *was* an apprentice mapmaker. She *did* have instruments. The commission was fictional, but then, so was half of every map ever drawn — the neat lines, the confident labels, the cheerful absence of everything the cartographer preferred not to see.

"Surveyor, eh?" Brakke said, squinting at her papers by the light of his lantern. "You're young for it."

"People said that about my master too."

"And where's this famous master?"

Astrid looked out at the river, gray and patient, carrying broken ice toward the sea. "Dead," she said. It was easier every time she said it. That frightened her more than the word itself did.

Brakke grunted, which she would learn was his method of expressing sympathy, doubt, agreement, and indigestion alike, and went forward to shout at his deckhand, a boy of perhaps fourteen named Pijl who moved with the frantic energy of someone perpetually late for something that had already happened.

* * *

The first day was all river and low banks — reeds, willows, the occasional frozen millwheel turning nowhere. Astrid stood at the rail despite the cold, because she had spent seven years drawing coastlines from other men's measurements, and here at last was geography happening directly in front of her face, unmediated, unscaled, gloriously inconvenient to draw.

She took out her notebook anyway.

"What's that?" Pijl asked, appearing at her elbow with the suddenness of weather.

"A bearing on the church spire."

"Why?"

"So that someone who has never seen this river can find it."

Pijl considered this with the gravity of a philosopher. "Seems roundabout."

"It is entirely roundabout. That's what makes it a map." She sketched the bend, marked the depth of the channel from the leads Brakke let her swing, noted the ferry crossing where a woman in a red shawl waved at them as if they were old friends. In the margins, without quite deciding to, she began working the cipher again — the little numerals that lived between Renn's contour lines, granary against granary, tonnage against tonnage, the arithmetic of a famine conducted by daylight.

She stopped when she noticed her hands were shaking. Not from cold. From the peculiar vertigo of holding, in the same pair of hands, a sketch of a church spire and a list of men who should hang.

"You've gone pale," Pijl observed helpfully. "Captain says surveyors go pale from thinking. He says thinking dries a person out like salt fish."

"Your captain is a natural philosopher."

"He says worse things than that about philosophers."

* * *

They made the customs house at dusk on the second day, and Astrid understood immediately why Ilse had called it "the easy one" with the particular flatness of a woman managing expectations.

It crouched at the narrows where the river bent around a hill — a stone building with the Empire's double eagle painted on it by someone who had clearly been paid by the letter, since the bird had acquired over the years a number of additional feathers and what appeared to be a small mustache. A chain stretched across the water. Lanterns burned behind frost-furred windows. Two soldiers waited at the landing stage, and a customs officer stood between them wearing the expression of a man who had hoped to die warm.

"They'll board everyone," Brakke told Astrid, not looking at her, which she appreciated; it was a kindness disguised as indifference, the commonest currency of the river. "Cargo, papers, bags. You just be stone. Stone with good handwriting."

"I'm always stone with good handwriting."

"Mm," said Brakke, and went to throw a line.

The customs officer came up the ladder slowly, as though climbing were a personal affront. He had a ledger under one arm and a candle-soft face and he examined the barge the way a bored man reads a book he has read before. Cargo: coal. Manifest: coal. Crew: Brakke, Pijl, one cat of uncertain allegiance. Passengers: two merchants dozing in the deckhouse, and a surveyor.

"A surveyor," the officer repeated, in the tone of a man who has been told the river contains a mermaid.

"For the Bureau," Astrid said, and held out her papers.

Here is what nobody tells you about fear: it does not feel like running or screaming or any of the dramatic things the penny dreadfuls promise. It feels like standing very still while your own hand, trained for seven years to be steady, holds out a piece of paper, and you watch it hold the paper and think, *look at that, my hand isn't trembling, my hand is braver than I am.*

The officer read. His lips moved. Astrid watched them shape the words *Cartographic Bureau* and felt the strange pride of the apprentice whose master's name still opens doors — doors Renn had, in fairness, never imagined would be opened by a corpse.

"Instruments," the officer said. "Open the case."

And this was the moment Astrid had rehearsed on the quay at Kalvik, in her lodging, in her dreams: she opened the case herself, before his hands could touch it, and became, in the space of four seconds, a different person — not a fugitive but a professional among laymen, mildly impatient, protective of her tools.

"The theodolite stays level," she said, lifting it out with both hands. "If it's dropped, the Bureau replaces it with a new one and bills the delay to your district office. Would you like to hold the plumb line? No? Then I'll just show you the rest."

She laid the instruments out on the deck crate like a surgeon laying out knives: the theodolite in its velvet cradle, the brass plane table, dividers, scale rules, the parallel rule worn bright by Renn's thumb, watercolors in little pans, a bundle of blank map tubes each sealed with the Bureau's wax — forged by Ilse, but forged beautifully, from a genuine impression taken off a discarded requisition, and Astrid had decided not to ask how.

The officer poked at a tube. Astrid let him.

"Maps," he said.

"Blank sheets. For field plotting." She unrolled one an inch, showing clean white paper, and rolled it back before he could notice that the paper was very fine, the kind that takes ink like silk, the kind a person might choose for writing something worth preserving. "The Bureau prefers we draw on their stock. They say our lines are crooked otherwise."

"Do they," said the officer, and for one horrible heartbeat Astrid thought she saw something wake behind his eyes — the small animal alertness of a man who suspects he is being managed. Then a gust came off the river and he shivered comprehensively, from boots to eyebrows, and the animal went back to sleep. "Sign the register."

She signed. *A. Voss, surveyor.* Her own name, her real hand, the letters upright and exact. There was a wildness in that she didn't examine too closely: hiding in plain sight, in the one place no hunter looks, which is inside the truth.

The officer stamped the register, stamped her papers, nodded at the theodolite as one nods at a large dog, and climbed back down the ladder to make some other traveler's evening worse.

* * *

Afterward, Astrid sat in the deckhouse across from the two merchants — wool traders, by the smell of them — and drank tea that tasted faintly of the samovar's previous life, and discovered that joy had arrived without permission and was sitting beside her like a stowaway.

She had done it. First checkpoint. She wanted to laugh, and did, briefly, into her tea, which alarmed the merchants considerably.

Because here was the thing nobody warned you about either: passing was *fun*. The rehearsal, the steadiness, the little theater of the plumb line — she had been *good* at it, better than good, she had enjoyed it, and somewhere underneath the relief a voice was asking, quietly, whether a person who enjoyed deceiving armed officials at dusk on a frozen river was entirely the person she had believed herself to be that morning.

Renn would have known what to call it. He'd had a name for every vice of the cartographer's soul. *The seduction of the clean line,* he used to say, tapping a student's beautiful, wrong coastline. *The prettiest maps are the ones most confidently lying.* Confidence, Astrid thought now, hugging her tea. Confidence was the pretty line today. Confidence had walked past two soldiers whistling, more or less, internally.

"Surveyor, are you?" said one of the merchants, a woman built like a barrel stove with two sharp eyes sunk in it. "Then maybe you can tell me why the government wants new maps of the relief districts. They mapped them two years ago. Nothing's changed there except there's fewer people to count."

The tea went cold in Astrid's mouth.

"Roads change," she said carefully.

"Roads don't," said the woman. "People do. My sister's village lost forty souls to the hunger and the government sent a man to record it as 'outmigration.'" She snorted, a sound like a small avalanche. "Outmigration. To where, I wonder. The graveyard, mostly."

Astrid looked at her — at the chapped hands, the wedding ring worn thin, the eyes that had done their own counting — and felt the letters shift against her hip like a living thing.

"Maybe," Astrid said, "someone will count properly soon."

"Hmph." The woman refilled Astrid's cup unasked, which on the river, Brakke had taught her that morning, was a sacrament. "You've got honest hands. I can always tell. The liars hold their cups like they're being watched."

Astrid held her cup very naturally and did not look at her own hands at all.

* * *

That night the river froze harder and the stars came out with the terrible clarity of winter, and Astrid stood at the rail after everyone slept, doing sums.

One checkpoint passed. Two remained: the mountain garrison pass, where soldiers searched wagons with bayonets and boredom, and the New Year's gate at the capital, where Ilse said the inspections were "thorough in the way that only a place afraid of printers can be thorough." Three days until the century turned. Somewhere ahead, a printing house with a press that could turn Renn's letters into ten thousand copies by morning. Somewhere behind, in Kalvik, inspectors going through her master's drawers with gloves on.

And somewhere — Ilse would not say where, and Astrid had learned not to ask twice — the Empire's hunters, with a description of the stolen atlas and, presumably, of the apprentice who had stolen it.

*We passed because they expected a smuggler,* Astrid thought, watching the ice grind past the hull. *They got a mapmaker instead. But the next man may know what a theodolite is. And the man after that.*

She was not naive. She knew the ease of tonight was borrowed, that luck was a current and currents reverse. But God help her, standing under those enormous winter stars with a dead man's letters against her ribs and a live man's river under her feet, she felt *alive* in a way the workshop had never made her feel — useful, aimed, moving. Grief had sat on her chest for eight months like a lead weight for pressing paper flat. Tonight it lifted, and the lightness was its own kind of danger.

"Don't enjoy it," she told herself out loud, and the words smoked white in the dark. "Enjoying it is how they catch you."

"Talking to yourself," said Pijl, materializing at her elbow with a lantern and no sense of boundaries whatsoever. "Captain says that's the first sign."

"Of what?"

"Being a philosopher." He squinted up at the sky. "That bright one there, the one that doesn't twinkle — is that on your maps?"

"Everything's on the maps eventually."

"Even us? Right now? This boat?"

Astrid considered the river, the barge, the boy, the frozen wheel of the world turning toward midnight and a new century. "Someone will chart this channel," she said. "And in the margin, where the soundings are, there'll be a note. *Barge passed here,* it'll say, in very small letters. *Winter, 1899.*"

Pijl beamed as though he'd been knighted, and went aft with his lantern, leaving Astrid alone with the stars and the small, dangerous warmth in her chest.

Two more checkpoints. One mountain. Four days.

She could almost believe the hard part was over, which was, of course, precisely the moment — as she would learn three days later, sitting across a garrison supper table from a man she used to call friend — when the hard part was only just beginning to look for her.
