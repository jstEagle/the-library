## 9. The Man Who Measures Loyalty

Tomas Reval had a gift that made him valuable to the Empire and insufferable at dinner parties: he could measure anything by eye.

A doorway's width. The tonnage of a crate from the set of a porter's shoulders. The distance between what a man said and what he meant — that was the measurement he was best at, and the one that had gotten him promoted out of the customs house and into the Office of Provincial Integrity before his thirtieth birthday.

"You have an honest face," Inspector-General Kessel had told him, stamping the transfer order with the air of a man disposing of a problem. "People confess to honest faces. It saves paperwork."

Reval had thanked him. He'd been twenty-six and flattered. He was now thirty-one and beginning to suspect he'd been handed a shovel and told it was a telescope.

The atlas file sat on his desk like something dead that refused to stop bleeding. *Atlas of the Baltic Coastlands, Master Halvard Renn, cartographer — deceased.* Forty-one plates. Three years of work by the finest mapmaker on this stretch of coast, commissioned by the Crown, funded by the Crown, and — according to the seizure order Reval now held — stolen from the Renn workshop four days ago along with several blank map tubes and one apprentice.

The apprentice was the part that kept him awake.

*Voss, Astrid. Age 24. Apprentice of record, six years. Presumed carrying the property southward toward Kalvik's river road.*

He knew her handwriting better than his own mother's. He knew she took her tea black enough to stand a spoon in, that she hummed when she inked coastlines and swore in two languages when she didn't, that she could carry a surveyor's tripod through a crowd the way other women carried market baskets. He knew all of this because for three years — before his promotion, before the uniform stopped being a joke between them and started being a wall — they had shared a table at Maren's coffeehouse every Thursday, where she drew maps of places that didn't exist and he pretended not to find them charming.

*Presumed carrying the property southward.*

He read the sentence again. It was the *presumed* that bothered him. In eleven years of service, Reval had learned that the Empire wrote *presumed* when it hadn't bothered to look, and looked only when it had already decided.

"Reval." Kessel stood in the doorway of his office holding a cup of coffee that smelled expensive, which meant someone else had paid for it. "You've read the file."

"Twice."

"And?"

"And I don't understand why the Office of Provincial Integrity is hunting a book of maps." Reval kept his voice level, curious, the voice of a man measuring a room. "Customs thefts go to the harbor office. Missing persons go to the city watch. An atlas goes to... us?"

Kessel smiled the way a door closes. "Because it isn't a book of maps. Not anymore. Renn put things in it that don't belong there. Numbers. Records." He drank. "The old man was a sentimentalist. Sentimentalists write things down, and written things are dangerous things."

"Dangerous to whom?"

"To the Empire, Reval. Which is to say, to everyone." Kessel set down the cup. "You'll take the southern route. River customs house first — they can't pass the water without papers — then the mountain garrison at Vell Pass, then the capital gate before New Year. If the girl is smart, she's already on a barge. If she's loyal, she'll come back on her own and weep about it."

"If she's smart," Reval said, "she knows the river customs house has exactly one inspector on night rotation and he's half-blind in one eye, because I requisitioned the lantern oil budget myself last spring."

Something flickered across Kessel's face — annoyance, quickly filed away under *manage later*. "Then you know where to start looking."

* * *

He started looking, as it happened, at Maren's coffeehouse.

This was not procedure. Procedure said start at the workshop, inventory the remaining instruments, interview the neighbors. Reval did all of that first — he was a careful man; carelessness was how junior inspectors became former inspectors — but procedure did not explain why his feet then carried him four streets east to a coffeehouse where the windows fogged with steam and the owner knew his order without asking.

Maren brought him coffee and a look. "You're wearing the new coat," she said.

"It's the same coat."

"It's got new buttons. Uniform buttons change when men change sides of the desk." She wiped the table he wasn't sitting dirty. "You here about Astrid?"

"I'm not permitted to discuss—"

"She came in Tuesday. Last time before that was three weeks, because you were posted upriver, and she sat in your seat and complained about it." Maren paused, and Reval watched her decide something. "She asked me if I'd heard anything about Halvard Renn's death certificate. Asked who'd signed it. That's a strange thing to ask over coffee, no?"

It was a very strange thing to ask over coffee. Reval turned his cup slowly on its saucer, one full rotation, thinking.

"Did she seem frightened?"

"She seemed like a woman doing sums," Maren said. "And coming out with answers she didn't like."

* * *

That evening Reval did something he had never done in eleven years of service. He opened the file he was assigned to close, and instead of reading forward — toward capture, confiscation, the clean machinery of resolution — he read backward.

Renn's personnel record first. Commissioned by the Crown Survey in '91. Commendations. A note in '96: *cartographer requested relocation of survey route to include inland relief districts; request denied; cartographer persisted; warned.* Another note in '97: *warned again.*

Warned twice and still working on a Crown commission. Reval had seen men dismissed for less. Somebody had wanted Renn exactly where he was, drawing exactly what he was told to draw, right up until the moment Renn died — conveniently, quietly, eight weeks after requesting permission to publish.

Reval pulled the death certificate. Cause: inflammation of the lungs, winter fever. Signed by a Dr. Ehrlich, whose name Reval found in the provincial registry within the hour — registered 1894, no practice address listed since 1896, fees paid annually by a post-office box.

Dead cartographers did not usually require physicians with unlisted addresses.

He sat back in the hard chair and let himself think the thought he'd been keeping in the corner of the room like an unpaid bill:

*Somebody faked this death. And somebody else signed off on the fake so smoothly that either they were fooled — unlikely, given the physician — or they were complicit.*

Which raised the question that mattered, the question that had nothing to do with maps: what did Halvard Renn see, in those relief districts he'd insisted on surveying, that made powerful people prefer him dead?

Reval was a believer in order. He wanted to say that plainly, even to himself, because it was true and because truth was the only instrument he'd ever trusted. Order was the dikes that kept the sea off the fields. Order was queues forming calmly, contracts honored, the weak not trampled merely because they were weak. His father had been a harbor clerk for forty years and had taught him that a ledger balanced was a small act of grace.

But order, Reval understood now, sitting in the lamplight with a dead man's suspiciously tidy file spread before him, had been sold to him in one wrapping and delivered in another. What the Office of Provincial Integrity called order was, more and more, the arrangement by which certain men were never disturbed. The dike was fine. It was the ledgers that were rotting.

And somewhere on a coal barge or a frozen road, an apprentice who hummed while she inked coastlines was carrying whatever it was that rotted them.

* * *

He packed by lamplight, the way his father had taught him: heavy things low, fragile things wrapped, everything measured against everything else until the case closed with a click of satisfied geometry. Surveyor's transit — requisitioned; a man with a transit at a checkpoint looked official and gave officials an excuse to be helpful. Cold-weather kit. The file. His commission papers.

At the bottom of the trunk, wrapped in a shirt he told himself he'd forgotten about, went the little brass compass. Not the regulation one — hers. Astrid had won it off him at cards in '97 and lost it back to him the following spring by the simple expedient of leaving it on the coffeehouse table, and somehow it had migrated into his coat pocket and stayed there through two postings like a splinter he couldn't reach. There was an inscription on the back in Renn's hand: *True north does not negotiate.* He had assumed, for three years, that it was a joke about compasses.

He was no longer sure it was a joke about anything.

* * *

The barge left at dawn, and Kalvik saw him off with its usual ceremony of gray rain. Reval stood at the rail while the harbor slid past — the customs house, the granaries with their imperial eagles stenciled on the doors, fat and sealed, and beside them the grain merchants' private warehouses, also full, also sealed, guarded by private men with public faces.

He measured them by eye, because he couldn't help himself. The imperial granaries had been reported at sixty percent capacity since the famine year. Their rooflines sagged in a way that sixty percent capacity does not cause. Full roofs sag like that. Full, locked, and unopened — while the relief districts upriver had buried their dead in frozen ground that had to be picked open like tin.

He had signed the capacity reports himself. Countersigned, rather. His name, in his small obedient handwriting, beneath numbers someone else had chosen.

The river unwound ahead of him, brown and patient, running south toward the customs house at Torsholm, then the mountains, then the capital gate where the year would turn with bells and fireworks while the presses printed the approved version of everything.

Kessel had said: *if she's loyal, she'll come back on her own.*

Reval had known Astrid Voss for five years, and he would have staked his commission — indeed, suspected he already had — that loyalty, for her, had never once meant obedience. Loyalty, for Astrid, meant finishing what the dead left undone. It meant inking coastlines she'd never seen because someone had to hold the true line. He had loved that about her in the careless way you love a friend's qualities, the way you love weather.

He was beginning to learn what it cost to love weather from inside the storm.

The mate called two bells. Reval went below, laid his commission papers on the narrow bunk, and read his own orders one final time.

*Apprehend bearer. Recover property intact. Property is to be considered highly sensitive. Bearer may be considered expendable.*

The last line was initialed K.

He folded the orders along their creases — once, precisely, the way he did everything — and understood, with the clarity of a man reading a map he has spent his whole life inside of, that the Empire had just asked him to choose between the line he served and the lines he believed in.

Somewhere downriver, past the customs house at Torsholm, past the blind night inspector and the fog on the water, Astrid Voss was making the same journey in the opposite direction of her life. And at some garrison inn above the snowline, if the roads held and the blizzards behaved, the two of them were going to sit at the same table again.

He only hoped, lighting his pipe as the mountains rose dark against the afternoon, that when it happened, he would already know which side of the desk he was standing on.

By the time the peaks came into view through the rigging, jagged white and utterly indifferent, Tomas Reval knew.
