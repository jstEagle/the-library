## 10. Snowbound at the Garrison

The blizzard did not announce itself. It simply arrived, the way bad news arrives — one moment the road ahead was visible, a pale ribbon climbing between black pines, and the next the world had been erased and redrawn in white, without consulting the cartographer.

Astrid found this professionally offensive.

"I could map this," she said to Ilse, who was driving. "I *would* map it, if I could see two feet in front of the horse."

"The horse can see," Ilse said. "The horse is the only one of us earning her keep."

They had left the barge at Torsholm two days earlier, and the journey since had been sledge, then hired cart, then this wobbling excuse for a coach that smelled of fish oil and someone's regrettable cheese. The plates rode in the false bottom of the instrument case beneath Astrid's feet, and every jolt of a wheel went through her spine like a question: *Are you certain? Are you certain?*

She was not certain. She was beginning to suspect that certainty was a luxury sold only to people who had never carried anything that mattered.

The horse — a broad, sensible gray whom Astrid had already privately named Kompas, on account of her unerring instinct for finding the worse of two paths — stopped dead. The wind took the opportunity to shove a wall of snow into the coach's one window, and through the gap Astrid saw, or imagined she saw, a low stone building with a single lit window and smoke bending nearly horizontal from its chimney.

"Garrison inn," Ilse said. "We stop here or we become a newspaper item. TWO WOMEN FOUND FROZEN IN COACH; HORSE AVAILABLE FOR SALE."

"Kompas would fetch a good price," Astrid said. "She has excellent judgment."

"She is standing in a blizzard."

"Everyone has standards."

* * *

The inn was warm in the particular way of mountain places, where the heat comes from an iron stove that has clearly won several arguments with the building around it. The garrison proper stood across the yard, its windows shuttered against the storm, but the inn served soldiers and travelers alike, and the common room was fuller than Astrid liked. Full meant witnesses. Full meant conversation. Full meant sitting still for hours while strangers looked at her face and tried to remember where they'd seen it.

She had learned, on the barge, how to be dull. Dullness was a kind of camouflage, and she was getting rather good at it. She arranged herself by the stove as a woman tired of travel, which required no acting whatsoever, and let Ilse handle the innkeeper — a wide man named Pesk whose mustache entered the room slightly before he did.

"One room," Ilse said. "Two beds if you have them, one if you don't. And whatever is in that pot."

"Mutton," said Pesk, with the reverence other men reserved for poetry.

"We'll take it."

It was while they were eating — and the mutton was, admittedly, worthy of Pesk's tone — that the door opened and the storm reached in with an armful of snow and a tall man in a travel coat.

He stamped, shook himself, surveyed the room with the quick, cataloguing glance of someone accustomed to taking inventory of rooms, and then came to the table nearest the stove, which happened to be theirs.

"Room for one more at the fire?" he asked. "I'll not crowd you."

"There's room," Ilse said, before Astrid could arrange her face into anything suitable.

The man hung his coat, sat, introduced himself as Herr Lindqvist, a surveyor's clerk traveling to the capital on business for his office. He had a long, serious face and eyes that seemed to hold everything at a slight distance, like a man reading a document through glass. He ordered the mutton. He praised the mutton. He asked polite questions and answered none of consequence about himself, and Astrid, watching him fold his napkin into precise thirds, felt the old workshop instinct stir: *this man measures things.*

She had spent six years among people who measured things. It took one to know one, and this one measured everything — the room, the exits, the depth of the snow, and, she was almost sure, the two women sharing his table.

"So," Herr Lindqvist said, warming his hands. "Bound for the capital?"

"For the coast," Ilse said smoothly. "Family business."

"In this season? You're braver than the postal service. They've closed the pass until the wind drops." He nodded toward the window, where the storm hurled itself against the glass like something with an actual grudge. "Could be a day. Could be three."

*Three days.* The words landed in Astrid's stomach like stones dropped down a well. Three days at one table with this watchful stranger, three nights in a building with one door, three mornings of making small talk while the plates sat under her floorboards — no, under her bed, they'd moved them to her room — three days of being *looked at.*

"Then we'll get comfortable," Ilse said, and laughed, and Astrid marveled at it, the ease of the lie delivered with a laugh. She resolved to practice laughing at lies. It seemed a useful skill, though she suspected Renn would have disapproved. Renn had believed maps should tell the truth and people should too, which was perhaps why he was officially dead.

"And you, Fräulein?" Lindqvist turned to her. "What takes you out in the killing season? You have the look of someone with instruments."

Her blood went very still and very cold, which she supposed was ironic given the proximity of the stove.

"My father's trade tools," she said. "He was a draftsman. I'm bringing them to his brother." True, mostly. Every good lie, Renn taught, should be a true thing wearing a different coat. "And what does a surveyor's clerk do, when he isn't rescuing mutton from Pesk?"

Something crossed the man's face — quickly, like a bird over water. "Paperwork," he said. "Mostly paperwork. Maps come to me, reports go out. I spend my life between them, like a ferryman."

"Maps," Astrid said, and heard her own voice go a half-tone too interested, and corrected it. "How modern. Everything's mapped now, isn't it? There can't be much left to draw."

"You'd be surprised." He smiled, and it was a genuine smile, tired at the edges. "Half my work is correcting the other half. A river moves; a village records itself a mile from where it actually stands; some official draws a boundary through a lake because it looked tidier. The Empire believes in its maps more than in its ground. The ground keeps disagreeing."

"The ground usually wins," Astrid agreed.

"It always wins. It just waits." He lifted his cup toward the window, toward the whiteness devouring the road. "This storm will bury the pass by morning. Then the ground will decide when any of us goes anywhere, and there's nothing for it but to be patient and eat Pesk's mutton."

It was, Astrid thought later, the strangest supper of her life. Here was warmth — real warmth, the stove ticking, the tallow candles guttering pleasantly, a witty, courteous stranger telling stories about mislabeled lakes — and every minute of it was suffocating, because under the table her boot rested on the case containing the thing that would hang them all, and across the table sat a man whose questions were too clean, whose answers were too few, and whose eyes kept returning to the instrument case the way a tongue returns to a loose tooth.

Or maybe, she told herself, scraping her bowl, he simply liked maps. Maybe everyone who liked maps wasn't hunting her. Maybe the whole Empire was not, in fact, sitting at her table eating mutton.

But when Pesk showed them up to their room — one bed, as promised, and a mattress thinner than a formal apology — Astrid lay awake in the dark listening to the storm and reconstructing Lindqvist's hands. Long fingers. Ink stains on the right middle finger and thumb. A callus in exactly the place a pen rests.

*A surveyor's clerk,* she thought. *Who writes enough to build a callus like a cathedral.*

Ilse slept beside her like a folded knife. Astrid stared at the ceiling beams and counted the hours until dawn, and somewhere below, the storm buried the road, and buried the road behind them, and made of the little inn a sealed envelope with all of them inside it.

* * *

Morning arrived grey and furious. The drifts had swallowed the yard to the sills of the garrison windows, and Pesk announced over breakfast, with grim satisfaction, that nobody was going anywhere and everybody might as well settle in. The soldiers from across the yard tramped over to escape their own barracks' boredom, and soon the common room was full of wet wool, card games, and a lieutenant who played the concertina badly enough that Astrid suspected sabotage.

Lindqvist sat by the window with paper spread before him, working. Working! In the middle of everything. His pen moved steadily, and every so often he consulted a folded map, and Astrid, pretending to mend a torn strap on the instrument case, watched him sideways and hated how much she wanted to see the map.

Curiosity, Renn used to say, is the cartographer's greatest asset and most reliable cause of death.

"Your strap is winning," Lindqvist observed, without looking up.

"The strap started it."

He laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him — and gestured at the chair opposite. "If your needle needs a rest, I could use an opinion. You said your father was a draftsman. Tell me if this coastline is honest."

And that was how Astrid Voss came to spend the second day of the blizzard discussing hydrography with the man she was increasingly certain was hunting her, across a table, over tea, in full view of eleven soldiers and an innkeeper, while the evidence of state crimes lay wrapped in oilcloth one floor above her head.

It was, she had to admit, better company than she'd had in months. That was the unbearable part.

He knew his work. He spoke about soundings and tide tables with the same love she felt for them, and once, when she corrected his bearing off a headland, he looked at her with such open delight that she felt like a fraud in a way that had nothing to do with her invented uncle.

"You've a professional eye," he said. "Truly professional. Your father trained you well."

"He trained me to argue with maps until they confessed," Astrid said.

"A noble profession." He tapped the chart. "Most men never learn that a map is a claim, not a fact. Whoever draws it decides what exists. Put a road here, and the road will follow within ten years. Leave a district blank, and it may as well not pay taxes." He smiled ruefully. "Forgive me. Clerks shouldn't philosophize. It unsettles the ledgers."

"No," Astrid said quietly. "Keep going."

But he didn't. Something shut behind his eyes, politely, like a curtain drawn by a considerate hand, and he returned to his figures, and Astrid returned to her strap, and the storm screamed at the windows and neither of them said another true thing all afternoon.

That evening the garrison's cook sent over a ham, and Pesk, sensing history, produced bottles of dark plum brandy, and the common room turned festive in the defiant way of people trapped together by weather. The lieutenant's concertina improved with distance and brandy. Soldiers argued cheerfully about whether the capital's New Year fireworks could be seen from the mountains (they could not, and everyone had an aunt who claimed otherwise). Ilse drank two glasses and told a story about a goat and a bishop that made even Pesk wheeze.

Astrid laughed at the goat story. It surprised her — the laugh coming up genuine from underneath all the fear — and she caught Lindqvist watching her when she finished, not suspiciously, but with a strange gentleness, as if he were memorizing something.

"You laugh rarely," he said. "But thoroughly."

"When I laugh often," Astrid said, "it stops meaning anything."

"Hm." He turned his cup slowly on the table. "My mother used to say something like that. About tears, though." A pause. The storm filled it. "Do you ever think, Fräulein, that we'll look back on evenings like this one and wish we'd paid better attention? Before everything changed?"

"I try not to look back," Astrid said. "The roads are bad enough going forward."

He smiled at that, and raised his cup to her, and drank, and Astrid drank, and upstairs the plates waited in the dark like a held breath.

Later, climbing the stairs, Ilse murmured, "You spent all day with him."

"He asked about coastlines."

"Everything asks about coastlines, eventually. What did you ask him?"

"Nothing," Astrid admitted. "I couldn't risk it."

"Mm." Ilse paused at the door of their room. "Well. Whatever he is, tomorrow the storm decides. Pray it decides in our favor."

But when Astrid lay down and pulled the coarse blanket to her chin, she found she wasn't praying about the storm at all. She was thinking about ink stains and careful hands, about a man who said *whoever draws the map decides what exists* — a sentence Renn could have written, a sentence she herself believed down to the marrow — and about the terrible arithmetic of it: that the person hunting them might be the only person in this frozen inn who understood why.

Somewhere below, the wind changed. It came around the mountain's shoulder and hit the eastern wall with a boom like a struck drum, and the candle in their room guttered out, and in the sudden dark Astrid heard Ilse say, very quietly:

"That's not wind."

Astrid listened. Beneath the storm — steady, deliberate, wrong — came the crunch of boots crossing the yard toward the inn.
