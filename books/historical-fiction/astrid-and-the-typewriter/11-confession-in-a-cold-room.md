## 11. Confession in a Cold Room

Tomas Reval had a theory about cold rooms: they told the truth about people.

A warm room lied. It let a man sprawl, loosen his collar, say more than he meant and less than he felt. But a cold room — a room where the water glass wore a skin of ice by morning and your breath hung visible like an accusation — a cold room made everything honest. You could see exactly how much fuel a household could afford by the size of its woodpile. You could see who was expected by counting the places at the table. Cold rooms did not flatter anyone, and Tomas had built his whole career on not wanting to be flattered.

The inn at the mountain garrison pass was called The Iron Stag, which was generous, because the stag over the door had been cast from some cheaper metal that rusted orange at the antlers, so it looked less like iron than like a deer that had wept. It was also, at present, honest down to its bones. The stove in the common room worked. The rooms upstairs did not pretend to. Tomas had been given the corner chamber, and when he set his hand flat against the windowpane it left a print in the frost that stayed there for hours, like a signature on a document no one would read.

He had signed himself in as *Karl Munt, surveyor's assistant*, which had the advantage of being nearly true. He *was* an inspector of the Imperial Archive Corps, junior grade, and he *was* traveling under surveyor's papers, and the distance between those two things was precisely the width of one sealed commission folded in his breast pocket — the commission bearing the signature of General Aldrik Vane, who did not sign things twice and did not explain them once.

*Recover the atlas. Silence follows failure.*

Silence follows failure. Whoever drafted that phrase had never had to sit across a supper table from someone while carrying it.

* * *

The trouble — and Tomas would think about this trouble for the rest of his life, in the way a man thinks about the exact spot where he first stepped onto ice and heard it sing — the trouble began with soup.

The blizzard had closed the pass by mid-afternoon, which meant everyone stuck at The Iron Stag was stuck together, which meant supper was served family-style at two long tables, because the innkeeper, a broad woman named Frau Hess with forearms like dock rope, believed that hungry strangers seated apart grew suspicious, and hungry strangers seated together became, in her words, "merely hungry." Tomas admired this philosophy. He shared a bench with a pair of grain merchants, a sullen customs clerk, an elderly couple who discussed their rheumatism with the devotion other couples reserved for scripture —

— and two women.

One was tall, weathered, with the sun-browned hands of someone who had worked outdoors past the age when respectable women were permitted to do such things. She gave her name as Brenner and her business as textile trade, and she ate the way soldiers eat, efficiently, watching the door without appearing to watch it.

The other—

The other was a slight young woman with ink stains worked so deeply into the creases of her fingers that no amount of travel-scrubbing would lift them. She held her spoon the way Tomas's old drawing master had taught him to hold a pen, and when Frau Hess refilled the bread basket, the young woman caught it against the table's jolt with an automatic little correction of the wrist, precise as an instrument.

Surveyors' apprentices, Tomas thought, do not have hands like that. Mapmakers' apprentices do.

He knew those hands. Not personally — but professionally, catalogued, filed, remembered. *Astrid Voss, apprentice to Halvard Renn, cartographer, deceased.* He'd read her description three times on the coach north: *young woman, early twenties, dark hair, likely traveling with maps or documents, may be accompanied.* The report had left space for a companion and no description for one, which struck Tomas even then as sloppy work by whoever had written it in Kalvik.

She introduced herself as *Lena Holt*, going to her aunt in the capital. Her voice didn't shake. That was the first crack, actually — not in his faith, not yet, but in the evening itself. Because liars' voices shook, everyone knew that; it was in all the manuals. Nobody had written a manual entry for a liar whose voice didn't shake, whose lie sat on the table between the bread and the salt like an ordinary dish, passed without comment.

"So," said one of the grain merchants, warming to the universal subject of weather, "closed in till the roads open, I expect. Two days, maybe three."

"Three," said Frau Hess with the authority of a woman who had buried travelers' schedules before. "And there'll be no grumbling about my rates."

"There's the question," the merchant went on, cheerfully oblivious, "of what they're doing about the relief districts up-valley. Heard at the last station they've rerouted the granary shipments again. Third time since autumn."

"Rerouted," said the customs clerk, into his soup.

"Well, somebody has to account for it all eventually."

"Somebody," agreed the clerk, in the tone of a man agreeing that someday the sun might rise in the west.

Tomas watched the young woman — *Lena Holt, going to her aunt* — very carefully not look up from her soup. And he watched the tall one, Brenner, watch the clerk instead, with an expression Tomas could only call *inventory*. As though the clerk were a thing being measured for a shelf.

He should have said something then. He knew the procedure. A junior inspector traveling under sealed orders was entitled — encouraged — to verify identities at any point of congestion. Blizzard conditions qualified. All he needed was a pretext: papers, please, madam, routine matter, thank you for your cooperation.

Instead Tomas tore his bread and ate his soup and told himself he was being strategic. A checkpoint in a snowbound inn, with witnesses, with nowhere to secure a prisoner, with an unknown quantity of documents possibly already distributed among luggage — no. Better to wait. Better to confirm. Better to be certain before he moved, because certainty was the whole difference between an inspector and a busybody.

This is what he told himself, and it was even partly true.

The rest of the truth was simpler and sat in his chest like a swallowed stone: he had known Astrid Voss once. Not well. A summer, years ago, when Renn's workshop had taken in a delivery of survey paper that Tomas, then a clerical boy in the provincial archive, had been sent to carry. Renn had kept him an hour past necessity, showing a boy with ink-stained cuffs how a coastline drawn at two scales tells two different truths, and the apprentice — a skinny girl of fifteen, all elbows and certainty — had corrected the master twice and been allowed to live. "You'll go far," Renn had told her, dry as dust, "or very specifically sideways." She had laughed. Tomas, hovering by the door with his empty delivery crate, had wanted, with a suddenness that embarrassed him even at fifteen, to be the kind of person a workshop like that would keep.

He had gone instead to the Academy, and then to the Corps, and then to a desk, and then to General Vane's attention, which was not the same as General Vane's favor, whatever the commission in his pocket implied.

*Certainty first,* he told himself again, and helped himself to more bread.

* * *

The child came to him at midnight, in the manner of unwelcome things, all at once.

There was a knock — Frau Hess, lamplight behind her, her face arranged around something unpleasant. "Inspector," she said quietly. She had guessed him inside a day; his boots, she explained, were city boots, and no surveyor's assistant had ever owned boots like that. "There's a thing been found up the valley road. The garrison sent down for you particularly. They said—" she paused, choosing words the way one chooses steps on ice, "—they said it was found along the granary route. And they said you'd want to see it, seeing as you've been asking after the shipment ledgers."

The snow had stopped falling but not stopped existing; it simply stood everywhere, hip-deep, blue in the moonlight, making the world silent in the aggressive way of a room where someone important has just asked a question. Two garrison troopers walked Tomas out with lanterns, and the lantern-light made a small trembling country around each of them and nothing beyond.

The child lay off the road's edge, near the third milestone, in the shallow lee of a stone wall. A boy, perhaps six. Wrapped in a blanket that had once been good wool. Frozen the way only the very poor freeze, which is to say neatly, arranged almost, tucked in upon himself as if he had finally gotten under cover after a long trip.

"He's been there a day or two," said the older trooper, gruff because the alternative was not gruff. "Drifted over. We're to take him to the chapel yard till someone claims him."

"Where did he come from?" Tomas heard himself ask, though he already knew, in the way one knows the end of a familiar sentence.

"Relief districts, most like." The trooper shrugged, and the shrug did the rest. Up-valley, the villages that the famine had emptied into the Empire's care. Up-valley, the granaries. The granaries that the shipments kept not reaching, or reaching and not emptying, or emptying into wagons that rolled somewhere else entirely.

Tomas knelt. He did not know why he knelt; procedure did not require kneeling. He looked at the small frost-stiff face and the good-wool blanket, and he thought: *somebody wrapped him warmly before sending him out. Somebody loved him efficiently.*

Then he took out his notebook — the official one, the one with the Corps stamp embossed on its cover, the one whose every page existed to become part of an argument the Empire was having with itself — and he made himself mark the location.

He was a map man. That was the joke at the root of him, the thing Renn had seen in a boy carrying a paper crate: Tomas Reval loved coordinates. Give him a fixed point and he was happy; give him two and he could find the truth between them. So he marked the milestone, and the child, and then — because his hand did it before his mind authorized it — he marked the granary, four versts up-valley. Three miles of road. A six-year-old does not walk three miles through drift snow unless the nearer houses are empty of anything to beg.

He drew the line in his notebook, child to granary, and looked at it, and understood that he had just drawn an accusation.

Because here was the thing Tomas had been carrying since Kalvik, folded tighter than the commission: the atlas he had been sent to recover was not, according to everything he could pry loose, an atlas. It was testimony wearing the clothes of an atlas. Renn — meticulous, humorless, incorruptible Renn, who had once refused a baron's coat of arms on a municipal chart because the baron hadn't paid the mapping fee and Tomas had found this hilarious and noble in equal parts — Renn had died rather than surrender it. Or rather: Renn had died, and then the Empire had developed a sudden passionate interest in recovering his life's work for "archival correction," and Tomas had accepted the assignment the way one accepts a ladder: without looking down.

Now he knelt in the snow beside a frozen child and looked down.

"Sir?" said the trooper. "You all right, sir?"

"Fine," said Tomas, and stood, and discovered that his knees had gotten colder than the rest of him without reporting it.

* * *

Back at The Iron Stag, the common room had gone down to embers and one stubborn card game. Tomas stood a moment in the doorway, snow melting off him in fat drops, and saw the two women at the stove's far side — Brenner mending a strap with quick fingers, the young one (*Voss, it is Voss, you have known since the soup*) asleep sitting up, chin dropping toward her chest, jerking awake, dropping again, in the rhythm of the utterly exhausted.

They had traveled hard. They were frightened, under the stillness. And they were carrying — if half of what the sealed orders implied was true — the only complete record in existence of how a province full of well-fed officials had converted famine relief into private profit while children walked three miles into the snow and lay down at milestones.

His orders said recover the atlas. His orders said silence follows failure. His orders had been signed by the general whose name, he now realized with a slow cold horror that had nothing to do with the weather, appeared — according to the fragments he'd been permitted to read of the case file — in connection with the *requisition authority* for the up-valley granaries.

He had assumed the file was redacted for secrecy.

He had never once, until tonight, considered that it might be redacted for guilt.

Tomas crossed the room. He told himself: one more test. Certainty. Procedure. If he confirmed nothing tonight, he could report tomorrow that the trail had gone cold in the blizzard; a day lost, honestly reported, forgivably. If he confirmed everything tonight, then he would have to choose, and he was not ready to choose, and he hated himself with a clean bright efficiency for the cowardice even as he committed it.

"Lena," he said gently, and she started awake, and her eyes came up to his with a flash of pure animal alarm before discipline shut it away. "Forgive me. You dropped this." He held out the pencil she hadn't actually dropped — it had rolled off the table; close enough; God forgive him the theater of it — and watched her take it, and watched her fingers check, automatically, the satchel strap looped around her wrist beneath her sleeve.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I'm very tired."

"The roads will open soon," he said. "They say two days."

"Three," said Brenner, without looking up from her mending, in the voice of a woman correcting a coordinate. "Hess says three."

"Three," Tomas agreed. And because he was weak, and tired, and because the child's face was still printed on the back of his eyes, he added, quietly, looking at neither of them: "The up-valley road is drifted shut anyway. Nothing moves that way. Nothing gets found, either, that isn't looking."

The mending needle stopped for exactly one heartbeat, then resumed.

"Good night, ladies," said Tomas Reval, and climbed the stairs to his cold honest room, and sat on the edge of his cold honest bed, and took out his notebook, and looked for a long time at the line he had drawn from the child to the granary.

An inspector's job, he had always believed, was to keep the map true. Every border surveyed, every boundary recorded, every fixed point fixed — because a map that lies is not a mistake, it is a theft; it steals the ground out from under the feet of everyone who has to live on it. Renn had taught him that in a workshop one summer, without ever meaning to teach him anything.

Somewhere below him, two women were sleeping badly with a stolen atlas between them, and the law said arrest them, and the general said silence follows failure, and a boy in a good wool blanket lay in the chapel yard waiting for someone to claim him, and no one would.

Tomas blew out his candle and sat in the dark and did not sleep, and outside the wind got up again, and drove the snow against the pane like something asking to be let in.

In the morning, Frau Hess hammered on his door before dawn, breathless from the stairs. "Inspector! The garrison's opened the pass — early — and there's a detail checking every sled and satchel on the road down." Her eyes were sharp and unreadable. "Your ladies left not ten minutes ago. Hired a sledge. Paid double."

Tomas was booted and coated and moving before she finished the sentence, the commission crackling in his pocket like a live coal, and the whole way to the stables he was aware of two futures pulling at him from either side of the drifts like horses in harness — and he still, God help him, had not chosen which reins to take.
