## 13. What Can Be Carried

They took everything at the pass.

Astrid learned that the word *everything* was heavier than she had imagined. She had carried the atlas through two provinces, had slept with it under her coat against the cold, had learned its weight the way one learns the weight of a sleeping child or a dying fire — intimately, without thinking. And now a garrison clerk with chilblains on his knuckles had stacked it on a table between a confiscated accordion and a crate of undeclared tobacco, and written *1 vol., maps, worn* in a ledger, and that was all it was to him. One volume. Maps. Worn.

She stood in the snow outside the checkpoint house because no one had told her not to, and watched through the frosted window as an officer leafed through Halvard Renn's life's work with the absent attention of a man shuffling receipts. He paused at the harbor charts. He licked his thumb to turn a page. Somewhere inside her something turned over with the page and did not turn back.

Ilse was gone. Not arrested — gone, which was worse, because arrest at least left a shape behind, a name in a ledger, a place to imagine. Ilse had simply walked out the eastern door of the garrison inn while Astrid was being searched and had not walked back in, and the snow was already filling her footprints as though the mountain itself had agreed to forget her.

"They'll want to know who you are," said the soldier walking Astrid down the road, a boy of perhaps nineteen with a rifle he held like a shovel. "But you're nobody, eh? They took your employer's maps for inspection. Happens all the time. Survey documents, restricted zones." He said it in the flat recitation of someone who had been told what story to believe and had decided believing it was easier than the alternative. "You can go."

*I am nobody,* Astrid thought, and found, to her surprise, that it was not entirely untrue, and not entirely unbearable. Nobody could walk away from a checkpoint. Nobody could board the mail coach with her single carpet bag and her frozen hands and sit across from a fat merchant who complained about the price of candles. Nobody could cry, quietly, facing the window, where the frost blurred everything into the white-on-white of an unfinished chart, and be seen by no one.

She cried anyway. It seemed owed.

* * *

The coach reached the capital two days later, or possibly three; the blizzard closed the mountain road and they sat in a coaching inn at Vesterhavn for a long white stretch of time during which Astrid drank more coffee than was good for her and stared at the wall map of the province that hung, crooked, above the stove.

It was the map that did it. Or rather, it was the moment she noticed the map was wrong.

"The coastline there," she said to the innkeeper's wife, before she could stop herself, "is eleven versts too far east. The survey was done in the forties, before the dredging. The harbor silted and shifted the channel nearly a mile south."

"Is that so," said the innkeeper's wife, in the tone of a woman who had survived forty years of guests by agreeing with all of them.

"It is," said Astrid, and then heard herself, and almost laughed — a ragged, inappropriate sound that made the fat merchant look up from his newspaper. Eleven versts. She had never surveyed that coast. She knew it because Renn had redrawn it in 1893 and she had inked the plate, and her hand remembered what her eyes had only glanced at, and apparently none of it had ever left her.

She sat very still for a moment, the way one sits when a floorboard creaks somewhere upstairs in an empty house.

Then she took out her little pocket notebook — the one thing the soldiers hadn't wanted, a girl's book of practice lettering and grocery sums — and wrote at the top of a blank page, in a hand that shook only slightly:

*Grain depot, Tornholm. 59°41′.*

And beneath it, because her memory was already moving, unbidden, like water finding a channel it had cut years ago:

*Third figure in the margin. Seven strokes. Cross-hatch base.*

The cipher. Renn's marginal numerals, the ones that matched the letters Ilse had brought, the ones that turned granary coordinates into a ledger of theft — she had copied them onto plates, corrected them, re-inked them where the copper had worn. She had spent three years handling those numbers the way a laundress handles shirts: thoroughly, repetitively, without ever once asking what was in the pockets.

Except now she found she had washed them so many times she knew every button. Every stroke. The seven-stroke figures that meant tonnage diverted; the cross-hatch that marked a signature verified; the little hooked numeral Renn used only twice, which Ilse's letters suggested meant a name too dangerous to write even in cipher, and which Astrid now understood had appeared beside exactly two coordinates — both military depots, both in districts where the famine had been worst.

Her coffee went cold. She didn't notice. The fat merchant complained about candles again and she asked him, politely, to repeat what he'd said about the price of tallow, and wrote that down too, because her hand needed something ordinary while the rest of her rebuilt the world.

Because here was the arithmetic, and Astrid Voss was good at arithmetic:

They had taken the atlas. Fine. The atlas was copper plates and paper, and paper burns and copper melts, and censors with thumb-licked fingers could do either. But an atlas, she had been taught — she had been taught by the man who wrote the sentence into her bones — *an atlas is an argument*, and arguments live in heads. They had taken the map. They had not taken the mapmaker.

Or rather: they had taken the master and left the apprentice, which was the same mistake made by everyone who had ever underestimated an apprentice, beginning with Master Renn himself, who had once told a visiting academician, in her hearing, that the girl would do for tracing and little else. She had resented that sentence for six years. Now she forgave it. Men say stupid things when they cannot see what they are building. He had built her, plate by plate, line by line, into the one archive the Empire could not raid.

She looked down at her notebook. *59°41′.* Her handwriting was steady now.

"Well," she said aloud, to no one, to the crooked wall map, to the ghost of a dead man who was possibly not dead. "You should have charged me tuition."

The innkeeper's wife, passing with bread, said, "What's that, dear?"

"Nothing," said Astrid. "A lesson I got for free."

* * *

She rented a room that night — a narrow one over a chandler's shop that smelled honestly of wax and dishonestly of the cats that kept the rats honest — and sat at the desk with a single candle and every sheet of paper she owned spread before her like a general's campaign maps, if the general were poor.

Three sheets. She had three sheets of decent paper, half a pencil, and the entire suppressed testimony of the Kalvik grain conspiracy stored in the wet dark of her own memory. This was, she reflected, what came of being a person of principle: other people got the gold, and she got the knowledge, and now she had to make the exchange rate work.

She began.

Not with the cipher — she wasn't fool enough to write the cipher itself where anyone might find it — but with plain lists, drafts, the scaffolding. Depot names. Dates she could reconstruct from Renn's marginal notations about ice on the roads, about the ferry season, about things a mapmaker notes in passing. Quantities, converted from the seven-stroke figures, checked three ways against each other until the numbers agreed, and when they agreed they told the same story the letters had told: that in the winter of '96 and '97, while the relief districts starved, provincial officials had sold relief grain across the border in quantities that would have fed every soul in three parishes, and had signed their thefts into a document disguised as a commercial atlas, because Renn had understood — too late for his own peace, and just in time for his purpose — that the safest place to hide testimony was in plain sight, bound and stamped and catalogued as boring.

By the third hour her candle had burned to a thumb's width and her lists covered both sides of all three sheets, plus the backs of two envelopes and, in extremity, the margin of a chandler's advertisement for whale-oil lamps ("Burns Bright, Priced Fair" — she noted absently that neither claim survived scrutiny, which felt thematically appropriate).

She leaned back. Her neck cracked. Somewhere below, a cat achieved something, and the rats presumably respected it.

It was done. Not the finished testimony — that would take weeks, ink, a press, and a miracle — but the shape of it. The truth, extracted from memory, laid out in her own hand. Not her master's hand. Hers.

And here was the strange part, the part she turned over as she blew out the candle and lay in the narrow bed listening to the chandler's clock insist that time was still happening: it did not feel like grief anymore. Or rather, it felt like grief the way a riverbed feels like rain — it was made of it, shaped by it, but what ran through it now was something with motion in it.

For two years she had thought of herself as a courier of a dead man's work. Finish the plates, guard the atlas, keep the flame — faithful, faithful, faithful, the good apprentice, keeper of another person's testament. Even on the barge, even past the customs house with her heart hammering out a surveyor's rhythm, she had thought of herself as freight. Cargo that could walk. And cargo, she now saw, could be seized at a checkpoint by a man with chilblains and a ledger, reduced to *1 vol., maps, worn*, and stacked between an accordion and some illegal tobacco.

An author could not be.

If they took these papers tomorrow, burned them in the yard, made her watch — she could write them again. And again. As long as she drew breath and could hold a pencil, the atlas existed, in however many copies she had patience and candlelight to produce. They had not confiscated the evidence. They had merely, at considerable expense to the Imperial treasury, produced the first rough draft of it.

She laughed into the dark, briefly, and then let herself feel the other thing underneath the laughter, which was not triumph exactly but something adjacent to it: the vertigo of becoming. For six years she had known precisely what she was — small, useful, second. Now the sentence was unfinished, and the pen was in her hand, and that was terrifying in a way that checkpoints had not managed to be.

*He faked his death to protect me,* she thought. *And I will undo his protection by telling the truth. I'm sorry, Master. But you taught me the crime, not the cowardice.*

Sleep came eventually, dreamless and earned.

* * *

Morning arrived gray and businesslike. Astrid rose, pinned up her hair, counted her money — eleven crowns and a two-kopeck piece, the fortune of a duchess, the fortune of a beggar, depending on the week — and went down to buy bread and ask questions.

The chandler's wife, it emerged, knew everything worth knowing within a half-mile radius and most of what mattered beyond it. Over the course of a shared breakfast and Astrid's strategic second helping of butter, Astrid learned three things.

First: that the newspapers were carrying nothing about any seized atlas, which meant the authorities were keeping it quiet, which meant the atlas was dangerous enough to be embarrassing. Good.

Second: that the free presses in the printing quarter were in their usual New Year's frenzy, running broadsheets and almanacs day and night, and that security at the printing-quarter gate would tighten toward the thirtieth, "because last year somebody printed a satire of the Chief of Police dressed as a goose, and he has no sense of humor and all of December off." Better than good. A deadline. A crowd. Chaos with bells on it.

Third: that a young gentleman had been asking after her.

Astrid's spoon stopped halfway to her mouth. "Asking after me."

"A clerkish fellow, polite, pale. Dark coat. Came yesterday evening and again this morning. Described you — young woman, travel-worn, came off the northern coach alone. Offered the porter a crown to send word when you woke." The chandler's wife examined her with the frank appraisal of a woman who had assessed ten thousand guests and buried at least nine of them in gossip. "I told him we'd rented to nobody. He left a card anyway."

She produced it from her apron like a magician producing a dove. Astrid turned it over.

Printed, in a neat official hand: *T. Reval, Inspector, Second Section.* Nothing else. No message. Just the name, sitting on the card like a stone on fresh snow.

Tomas Reval. Who had shared her bread at her master's table. Who had taught her to skate badly on the Kalvik harbor ice and laughed when she fell and helped her up and fallen himself so she wouldn't be the only one. Who believed — had believed, she reminded herself, watching the card tremble very slightly in her fingers — in order the way priests believe in God: sincerely, and without looking too closely at what the faith was used to justify.

He knew she was in the capital. Which meant either he was hunting her, or — and she held this possibility up to the light and turned it like a suspect coin — he was hunting something else, and she was the closest honest face he could remember.

"He didn't search the house," the chandler's wife observed. "Didn't come in. Just stood there a while, looking up at your window like a man reading a difficult sign."

Astrid folded the card and put it in the pocket where Ilse's letters had ridden, where the atlas's weight had ridden, where everything important apparently ended up sooner or later.

"He's not reading a sign," she said. "He's deciding what he believes. I know the feeling. I decided yesterday."

She finished her tea, thanked the chandler's wife with a lie about calling again soon, and stepped out into the gray morning with three sheets of testimony hidden in her lining, eleven crowns in her boot, four days until the new year, and — for the first time since the pass — a destination.

Somewhere in this city, Tomas Reval was standing under windows, working up his courage or his warrant, and Astrid intended to find out which before he did.

She turned toward the printing quarter and began, briskly, to walk — a nobody, an author, and the only atlas the Empire couldn't confiscate, all three of us, she thought, sharing one pair of boots.
