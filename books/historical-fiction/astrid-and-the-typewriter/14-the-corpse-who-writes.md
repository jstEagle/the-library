## 14. The Corpse Who Writes

The trouble with being dead, Halvard Renn had discovered, was the laundry.

A living man could hand his shirts to a washerwoman and think nothing of it. A dead man had to boil his own collars in a copper pot at midnight, in a rented room above a chandler's shop in a fishing village so small it appeared on exactly one map — his own, the 1887 coastal survey, where he'd marked it *insignificant anchorage; nothing here* and been grateful, seven years later, to have been right.

He wrung out a shirt, watched gray water spiral down the drain, and reflected that he had imagined death would involve less housework.

The village was called Skarva, which meant "the skerry" in the old tongue, which was a joke of some kind, though Renn had never worked out on whom. Forty-one souls, most of them related to each other in ways he'd stopped trying to chart. He was known as Herr Anders, a retired clerk of no consequence who had come for his lungs — this was even true; the sea air helped — and who paid his rent punctually in small, unmemorable banknotes. He had grown a beard. It came in gray and unruly, and made him look like a disappointed goat, but disappointment was an excellent disguise. No one looked twice at a disappointed goat.

What he had not anticipated, in the whole elaborate architecture of his disappearance, was loneliness.

He had planned everything else with a mapmaker's precision: the overturned boat found off the Headland Rocks with his coat in it, the funeral Astrid must have attended (he could not let himself think about her face at the funeral, so of course he thought about it constantly), the slow dispersal of his papers, the false trail south while he came north instead, doubling back through his own survey territory like a fox using its own prints. He had planned the money, the name, the beard, even the cough, which was real and therefore convincing.

He had not planned the evenings. Evenings were when the workshop had come alive — Astrid at the second desk, chewing her pen when she thought he wasn't looking, arguing cheerfully with the coastline of Vastermoor as though the coastline might be argued with. Evenings were ink and lamplight and the particular sound she made — a small *hmph*, triumphant and private — when she got a curve right.

Now evenings were a copper pot, a boiled collar, and a man talking to a cat that did not belong to him and tolerated him only because he was warm.

"You are a poor conversationalist," he told the cat, whose name was Herring and whose loyalty was entirely caloric. "Astrid would have had you drawing isobars by now."

Herring blinked slowly, accepting the insult as tribute.

* * *

The news came the way all news came to Skarva: three days late, salt-stained, and via the fish buyer, Larsen, who came up from the town every other Thursday with provisions and a newspaper that he read aloud in fragments to anyone who would listen, which was everyone, because Skarva's standards for entertainment were low and getting lower.

"The *Kalvik Courier*," Larsen announced, stamping snow off his boots in the chandlery below, and Renn, upstairs, felt the familiar clench behind his ribs and went very still over his ledger.

He had trained himself, these two years, not to flinch at the paper. Most weeks it held nothing — grain prices, a shipping notice, the interminable serial novel about the lighthouse keeper's curse. He had learned to hear Larsen's voice and sort its contents like mail: nothing, nothing, nothing.

But today Larsen's voice had changed. Today it had gone up half a register, the way it did for the serial novel, and the room below went quiet in the way rooms do when something matters.

"*— seized by Imperial inspectors at the Vellund mountain pass — a master atlas of the Kalvik coast, commissioned by none other than the late Halvard Renn — you remember, the mapmaker, drowned these two years back — seized under warrant of archival correction, the officials saying it contains material prejudicial to the security of the province — and get this, get this —*"

Renn's pen snapped in his hand. Ink on his fingers. He did not notice.

"*— the woman carrying it escaped custody. An apprentice, they're saying. A young woman, an apprentice out of Renn's own workshop. She gave them the slip in the snow at Vellund Pass and vanished into the mountains, and there's a warrant out, reward of three hundred marks.*"

Someone whistled. Someone else said something about three hundred marks buying a lot of herring. Laughter.

Renn sat down on the stool by the window because his legs had made an executive decision without consulting him.

She was alive. The word arrived in his chest like a struck bell — *alive, alive, alive* — and for one full minute he permitted himself nothing but that: the pure animal relief of it. The girl was alive. She had walked out of Vellund Pass with soldiers swarming it, in winter, with three hundred marks on her head, and she had *walked out*.

His Astrid. Who once got lost coming home from the market in fog he himself had taught her to navigate.

Then, arriving precisely on schedule, the way a bill arrives after a feast: the shame.

Because she was alive and hunted, and both of those things were his doing — the second more than the first. He had built his survival the way he built anything, carefully, on a firm foundation, and the firm foundation was her danger. Every careful plank of his disappearance required someone left behind to grieve convincingly. He had chosen her, without asking her, to be the load-bearing grief. And now she was carrying the atlas — *his* testimony, *his* evidence, the thing he should have carried and could not, because corpses cannot cross checkpoints — through blizzards and warrants and the full attention of the Empire, while he boiled his collars in Skarva and argued with a cat.

"I am a coward," he informed Herring.

Herring, on the windowsill, declined to dispute the point.

* * *

That night he took out the letters.

They lived under a loose floorboard beneath the bed, wrapped in oilcloth, and they were the reason for everything — the death, the flight, the two gray years. Twenty-two letters in his own hand, copies of the correspondence he had gathered over four years while drawing what he had been paid to draw: granary routes, relief district boundaries, the administrative coastline of the Empire's benevolence. He had gone looking for harbors and found ledgers instead. Grain moving inland in years of famine, moving at night, moving with the signatures of men whose names he then encoded into coordinates that marked no coastline at all.

The atlas was testimony. He had never said so to Astrid in words. But he had taught her that a map is an argument about power, and he had let her ink the plates herself, and somewhere in the back of his mind — he could admit this now, at midnight, to a floorboard — there had been a version of events where she finished it after him, naturally, inevitably, the way apprentices finish masters' work, and the truth went to the printers under her steady hands while he was safely, uselessly dead.

Instead the inspectors had it.

He lay in the dark doing the arithmetic of catastrophe, which was the only kind of arithmetic he'd ever been bad at. They had the plates. Would they read them? The cipher was his own invention — marginal numerals keyed to bearing and distance, a system elegant enough that he'd been quietly proud of it, pride now revealed as yet another entry in the long ledger of his vanity. Given time and lamplight, a clever man could break it. Reval, if they'd assigned Reval —

No. He didn't know if they'd assigned Reval. Two years dead, he knew almost nothing, and the not-knowing was itself a punishment he had designed for himself and served daily.

But he could imagine it plainly enough: some junior inspector, honest in the way the Empire used honest men as tools, sitting over the plates with a lamp. Finding names. Finding, perhaps, the name of the general who signed his commission. And then the machinery doing what the machinery did — the plates burned, the witnesses managed, the apprentice-apprehended-or-dead line item closed out, and the new century arriving clean and blank as censored paper, the famine rewritten as weather.

And Halvard Renn, warm and bearded and *alive*, having arranged it so that the only person who might have stopped all this was a twenty-three-year-old woman with ink-stained fingers and no allies, running through snow for a crime that consisted entirely of being trusted by him.

"Three hundred marks," he said aloud to the ceiling. His voice sounded rusty; he didn't talk much anymore, except to the cat and, increasingly, to Astrid, who was not present, which said something about his condition that he chose not to examine. "Three hundred marks. You couldn't put a price on me when I was alive worth half that."

This was true. The Empire had considered him a modest treasure: a competent craftsman, useful, unremarkable. It had never occurred to anyone until too late that a man whose entire profession was deciding *what gets recorded* might, one day, record something inconvenient.

He turned over. The wind came off the water with that particular January voice, thin and searching, and he thought — as he had thought every night for two years and would think every night for two more, though he didn't know it yet — of the funeral. He had not attended, obviously. He had read about it afterward in a two-week-old paper, a paragraph: *respected master of his craft; survived by his apprentice*. Survived by. As though she were the residue. As though she were what remained after the substance was subtracted.

He had wept then, alone in this room, in a way he hadn't wept at anything since boyhood, and he had told himself it was the price of the plan, and plans have prices, and the price was fair.

It was not fair. It had never been fair. He had simply been the only one with the authority to decide, which is the working definition of unfairness, and he had known it, and done it anyway, because the alternative was dying genuinely, and he had wanted — God help him, at fifty-eight, with his lungs rattling like a badly drawn drawer — he had *wanted to live*.

There it was. Said at last, in the dark, to no one.

* * *

In the morning he made a decision, which was notable mostly because he had spent two years making none.

He would write to her.

Not directly — he was not quite mad, whatever the cat believed. But there was a channel. There had always been a channel, kept open the way you keep a spare key under a stone: old Fenwick at the chandlery owed him a debt from 1891 involving a mis-surveyed harbor and averted bankruptcy, and Fenwick's brother-in-law drove freight to Kalvik twice monthly, and freight drivers ask no questions if the fee asks none for them. A letter, unsigned, in a disguised hand — he had practiced a clerkly script for two years for precisely no purpose he'd admitted to — could reach the capital's printing quarter, to the offices of the *Evening Signal*, that stubborn little paper that had once printed his letter about the Vellund light discrepancy and thereby annoyed a magistrate. If she was alive and free and still fighting, she would think to look there. She was clever. She had been taught by the best, and the best was available to say so because the best was talking to himself in a fishing village.

He got paper. He got the good pen, the one he'd brought from the workshop hidden in a rolled sea chart, the one Astrid called "the bishop" because of its mitre-shaped cap. He sat down at the table by the window, where the morning light fell the way drafting light ought to fall, and he uncapped the ink, and he wrote:

*To the apprentice, from the anchor point.*

Then he stopped.

Four words, and already his eyes were stinging, which was absurd, he was a corpse, corpses are famously composed. Outside, Herring was killing something in the snow with more enthusiasm than skill, and the gulls were filing their usual grievances, and the world went on being ordinary, which was the whole unbearable point of it.

*You passed the first checkpoint,* he wrote. *I knew you would. I taught you to carry instruments as though they grew from your hands, and I see now I also taught you, without meaning to, that a lie can be drawn accurately enough to save a truth. I am sorry for every lesson that costs you. I am sorriest for the one where I taught you what I was worth, which was my life, and let you believe it cost yours.*

He wrote for two hours. He crossed out more than he kept. What remained, when he had finished, was short — half a page — because the truth, he had learned late, compresses better than any lie.

*I am alive. I know what that makes me. Do not forgive me; finish the work. The cipher key you need is not lost — you have carried it since you were nineteen, in the margin habit I beat into you, numerals against bearings, check your own field notebooks of '96. The atlas was always testimony. So are you.*

*Stay dead to me if you must. I will stay dead to the world. One of us should get to live.*

*— H.R.*

He sealed it, disguised the hand on the address with the clerkly script, and sat holding it for a long moment, feeling the weight of paper, which has no weight at all until it does.

Then he opened the floorboard, took out the twenty-two letters, and copied the master cipher key onto a scrap small as a postage stamp, folded it inside the letter, and prepared to send his daughter — for that was the word, wasn't it, the word he'd never used, apprentice, partner, survivor, *girl I made an orphan of grief* — everything he had left.

Which was the truth, and a key, and the news — just beginning to trickle north with the fish buyers, garbled, secondhand, and therefore probably true — that in the capital, a junior inspector named Reval had resigned his commission the same week the atlas was seized, and had last been seen walking into the printing quarter carrying nothing but a satchel and what witnesses described as "the expression of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like."

Renn read that fragment three times. Then he smiled, for the first time in a month, a crooked, goat-like smile under the gray beard.

"Well," he said to Herring, feeding the fire with the crossed-out drafts. "It seems the century intends to end loudly after all."
