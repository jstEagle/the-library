## 15. Reval's Arithmetic

The plates arrived at the garrison office in a crate that smelled of linseed oil and someone else's tobacco, and Tomas Reval signed for them the way he signed for everything — neatly, completely, with a small flourish on the final letter of his name that his instructors at the academy had once called "insufficiently martial."

He had been signing things for eleven years. Requisitions. Patrol reports. Confiscation manifests. He had always believed that a signature was a small act of architecture: you built your integrity one document at a time, and if each one was true, the whole structure would stand.

He carried the crate inside, set it on his desk beside the lamp, and looked at it for a long moment before opening it.

Outside, the blizzard had finally spent itself against the mountains, leaving the garrison pass buried and silent and beautiful, the way disasters often are once they finish. Somewhere below, a cart horse stamped. Somewhere above, ice creaked on the roof like an old man turning over in bed.

Reval pried up the lid.

Inside, packed in straw, lay the copper printing plates seized at the checkpoint two days earlier — the atlas of Master Halvard Renn, dead cartographer, recovered from two women who had, by every report, surrendered it with suspiciously little argument. The senior inspector at the pass had wired the capital for instructions and received back a single line: *Catalogue contents. Forward under seal. No copies.*

No copies. Which meant that whoever opened this crate first would be the only person between these mountains and the capital archives to actually *look* at what the Empire was so eager to bury unread.

Reval told himself he was only doing his duty. Cataloguing required examination. Examination required understanding. Understanding required — well.

He poured himself the dregs of the coffee from the morning pot, cold as a tax collector's heart, and began.

* * *

The first hour was pure pleasure, and he was honest enough with himself to admit it.

These were magnificent plates. Renn had been a master of the intaglio line, and even Reval — whose own mapwork was competent in the way a good clerk's handwriting is competent — could see the difference between competence and this. The coastlines breathed. The hachures marking elevation fell across the mountain slopes like combed hair. There was a plate of the Kalvik harbor rendered at low tide that made him homesick so sharply he had to set it down and look out the window at the snow for a minute.

He had grown up three streets from that harbor. He had learned to swim in water that smelled like this ink probably smelled when it was wet.

"Sentimental," he said aloud, to the empty office, and returned to work.

It was on the ninth plate — the granary districts of the interior provinces — that he noticed the first oddity.

A surveyor's habit dies hard. He found himself checking coordinates the way other men check their watches, automatically, without deciding to. And the coordinates along the southern margin of the granary plate were wrong. Not badly wrong. Not the wrongness of error, which scatters. This wrongness was *consistent*. Every marked point sat precisely forty-one minutes east of where the actual granaries stood — he knew the granary routes; he had walked them last summer on grain-transport inspection, another neat signature in another ledger — and they sat there with the regularity of a code rather than the sloppiness of a mistake.

Forty-one minutes east. Every point. As though the whole coastline of the province had been picked up and shifted, deliberately, the way you'd shift furniture to hide a stain on the floor.

Reval sat back. His coffee had gone from cold to glacial. He drank it anyway.

"All right," he said to the plate, in the tone he used on nervous witnesses. "Let's see what you're hiding."

* * *

It took him until well past midnight, and it took him arithmetic, which was the one subject at the academy he had loved — numbers being the only colleagues who never lied to advance themselves.

The marginal numerals came next. Tiny figures penciled into the plate margins before etching, so faint that a censor skimming for seditious text would glide right past them. They clustered near the shifted coordinates. And when he subtracted — when he took the true position of the Volsk central granary from its marked position, took the marginal numeral nearest it, and laid the two figures side by side —

They matched. Not in value; in *structure*. The displacement in minutes matched the marginal number digit for digit, transposed. It was a key. The shift wasn't concealment. It was *encoding*. Renn hadn't hidden information in the maps. He had made the maps themselves into a ledger, and the margins into its cipher.

Reval worked through the night with the lamp turned low, because a man doing ordinary cataloguing did not need a bright lamp, and a man doing something else needed even less to be seen doing it.

By three in the morning he had decoded four entries completely.

The first was a granary at Volsk, with a figure beside it: 4,100. Units unstated. Given the scale of the district, he assumed sacks.

The second was a transport record — a barge manifest number he recognized, because he had countersigned a copy of it eighteen months ago, another signature, another brick in the structure — paired with a destination that was not the famine district listed on the manifest but a private warehouse concern outside Kalvik, and a second figure: 2,750.

The third entry stopped his pen.

It was a name.

Not encoded, not displaced, not ciphered. Written plainly in the margin in Renn's small, exact hand, as though the man had finally run out of patience with subtlety: *Gov. Aldous Krein — approved diversion, March — sees 30%.*

Krein. Provincial Governor Krein. The man whose portrait hung in the garrison mess hall between the Emperor and the national eagle, whose annual address to the civil service Reval had attended twice, whose voice on the subject of duty and sacrifice was quoted in the academy's entrance examinations.

Reval read the name four times. Each time it declined to become a different name.

His hands were steady. That surprised him. He had expected — what? Shaking. Instead there was a strange, ringing quiet, like the silence after a bell is struck, before the sound decides what it wants to be.

He turned to the fourth entry.

*Gen. M. Ostrava — requisition authority — 12,000 sacks, autumn route — "surplus reclassified."*

General Matthias Ostrava.

Reval set down the pen very carefully, because a part of him understood that if he did not set it down carefully he would throw it, and the plate, and possibly the desk, through the window.

Ostrava had signed his commission. Eleven years ago, a young man with insufficiently martial handwriting had stood in a receiving hall while General Ostrava — then merely Colonel Ostrava, broad-chested and loud-laughing, a man who remembered names — had pinned the insignia on himself and said, "We keep order so the people can keep faith, Reval. Remember which comes first."

He had remembered. He had built his entire life on remembering. Order first, order second, order all the way down, because order was the wall between the Empire and the wolf-dark chaos beyond it, and men like Ostrava were the stones at the top of the wall.

Twelve thousand sacks. The autumn route passed through the relief districts. Reval had been *on* the autumn route. He had inspected the granary at Kessel, where the stores had come up inexplicably short, and written in his report that the discrepancy was "consistent with spoilage," because his commanding officer had suggested the phrasing, and suggesting phrasing was normal, everyone did it, it was how the machinery ran smoothly —

"Spoilage," Reval said to the empty room, and heard, for the first time, exactly what the word sounded like when twelve thousand families were standing in front of an empty granary.

* * *

He did not sleep. He made a pot of coffee instead, real coffee, from his private tin, because it seemed suddenly important not to economize on anything ever again.

Toward dawn he found himself doing something he couldn't quite justify even to himself: he went back through his memory, entry by entry, year by year, running the arithmetic of his own career. The report at Kessel. The transport audit at Drenna where the manifest weights had been "adjusted at source." The winter two years ago when the relief districts' quotas had been quietly reduced and no one asked why, and he hadn't asked why, because asking why was disorderly.

There was a child. He kept coming back to the child. Three days ago, on the road below the garrison, a patrol had found a small body frozen in the ditch by the granary road — a girl, six or seven, no papers, wearing a coat that had been mended so many times it was more patch than wool. She had been walking toward the granary. The granary had been empty since October. Reval had signed the burial register. Entry 447. He had written her name as *unknown*, because no one knew it, and the register required something, and *unknown* was orderly.

She had been walking toward food that powerful men had sold.

He thought about the two women at the checkpoint. The older one, Brenner, sharp-eyed and hard-mouthed, who had given up the plates almost gratefully — too gratefully, he'd thought at the time, filing the observation away as a thread to pull later. And the younger one, the apprentice, Voss, who had stood in the snow with her hands bare and her chin up and watched the soldiers carry away her master's life's work with an expression Reval had spent two days trying not to think about.

It had not been the expression of a criminal caught.

It had been the expression of a person watching something be taken that she had already decided was worth more than the thing itself. He'd seen that look once before, on his grandmother's face, the day the bailiffs took the farm — she'd handed over the deed with her head high because the deed was paper and the land was under her skin and no bailiff could dig it out.

Voss had looked at those plates like they were already under her skin.

"Oh," Reval said quietly, to the gray light coming up behind the mountains. "Oh, you clever, stubborn woman."

If the atlas was a cipher, then the atlas was reproducible. Any mapmaker who had worked on those plates — who had *inked* them, and apprentices inked everything — might carry the whole ledger in her head, coordinate by coordinate, numeral by numeral. Seizing the copper proved nothing. Catching the courier proved less. The Empire had confiscated a printing plate and believed it had confiscated a truth, and truths did not live in copper. Truths lived in stubborn women with chapped hands and in dead cartographers who wrote in margins, and apparently in junior inspectors who had spent eleven years building a house out of signatures and had just watched the foundation crack straight down the middle.

* * *

At seven o'clock, Senior Inspector Grubach knocked, entered without waiting — rank does that — and surveyed the wreckage of Reval's night: the plates in ordered rows, the notes, the cold coffee, Reval himself unshaven and unwashed and looking, he suspected, like a man who had seen a ghost and taken notes on it.

"Well?" Grubach said. "What have we got?"

"A masterpiece," Reval said. And it was true, and it was the safest thing he could say, and he hated how easily it came. "Renn was better than anyone knew. The coastlines alone are worth preserving."

Grubach grunted. "And the rest? Anything subversive?"

"Coordinates," Reval said. "Granaries, transport routes, storage capacities. Dry stuff." Also true. Every word true. He was discovering that a lifetime of precise honesty equipped a man beautifully for precise dishonesty, and the discovery sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. "I'll finish the catalogue today. It goes forward under seal tomorrow, per instructions."

"Good man." Grubach paused at the door, and something flickered across his heavy face — not suspicion; weariness. "You look terrible, Reval. Whatever you caught off those women at the inn, I hope it was worth it."

"It was worth it," Reval said, and meant it in a way Grubach would never guess, and despised himself for the smoothness of it, and filed the feeling away with all the others.

When the door closed, he stood for a long time looking at the fourth plate — the one with Ostrava's name in the margin, plain as a confession, patient as a grave.

Order first. So the people can keep faith.

The girl in the ditch had been keeping faith. She had walked toward the granary in the snow because she believed, the way the Empire had taught her to believe, that the storehouse would hold what it promised. Entry 447. *Unknown.* He knew her name now, or felt he owed it to her to find it, and finding it felt like the first honest piece of work he'd done in eleven years.

Reval took a fresh sheet of paper. Not an official form — a blank sheet, the kind that starts things.

He wrote at the top, in his careful hand with its insufficiently martial flourish: *Questions I am not authorized to ask.* Below it: *1. Where is the apprentice Voss?*

Because she had gone west, toward the capital, toward the printers, toward the New Year's gate — he was nearly certain of it, and certainty was a thing he could no longer afford to file away. Because she was carrying a ledger in her head that his general had sold twelve thousand sacks out of. And because somewhere between the third question and the fourth, Tomas Reval understood that he was no longer deciding whether to hunt her.

He was deciding whether to warn her.
