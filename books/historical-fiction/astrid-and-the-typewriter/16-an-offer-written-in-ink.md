## 16. An Offer Written in Ink

The capital in the last week of December smelled of coal smoke and candied almonds, which Astrid considered a fair summary of the Empire itself: something burning underneath something sweet.

She had been in Vasterholm nine days and had spent them the way a mouse spends time in a house with a cat — moving quietly, learning the rhythms, never once turning on the overhead light. She had taken a room above a milliner's shop on Krondal Street under the name Fru Lindqvist, widow, seamstress, dull as dishwater, please don't look at me. The landlady, a vast and kindly woman named fru Sabel who had opinions about everything except other people's business, brought her bread every morning and gossip every evening, and Astrid paid for both in small coins and smaller smiles.

By day she walked the printing quarter with her hands in her pockets, counting presses. By night she sat at the little table beneath the eaves and wrote out the ledger from memory — page after page, granary by granary, name by name, in the cipher Renn had taught her without ever telling her he was teaching it.

*You learned your letters from coastlines,* he used to say. *A coastline is just a sentence the sea keeps rewriting.*

She missed him the way one misses a limb: constantly, and mostly in the moments when she reached for something and found it gone.

It was on the ninth evening, coming back through the almond-smoke crowd from buying ink, that she saw Tomas Reval standing under the lamp outside the milliner's shop.

Her first thought — absurd, traitorous, human — was *he looks cold.* His coat was good but insufficient for the wind coming off the river, and he had his collar up and his hands jammed in his pockets, and he was doing the thing he had done since they were both fifteen: standing very straight as though straightness could make him invisible. He had never once succeeded at being invisible. He had only ever succeeded at looking like someone trying very hard to deserve whatever happened next.

Her second thought was to run.

She didn't. Running would confirm everything, and besides, she reasoned, with the strange calm of a person whose fear has been burning so long it has burned down to coals, if he had come to arrest her there would be four men behind him and a wagon, and there was only Tomas, alone, under a lamp, looking like a man who had rehearsed a speech forty times on the walk over and remembered none of it.

"Astrid," he said.

"Tomas." She shifted the ink bottle to her left hand. Not, she noted, to throw it. Old habits. New uses. "You've found me. How flattering."

"The widow Lindqvist," he said. "Seamstress."

"Grief changes a person."

"It does," he said, and did not smile, and she remembered abruptly that this was a man who had lost his faith sometime between a frozen child and a general's signature, and that jokes were things you offered people like him the way you offered bread to a drowning swimmer — hopefully, and prepared to lose them.

"You're alone," she said.

"I'm alone."

"Say it plainly, then. Why are you here?"

He looked up at the lamp instead of at her, the way people do when they cannot bear to look at the thing they came to look at. Snow was beginning again, fine and dry, settling on his shoulders like an accusation he'd agreed to carry.

"May I come up?" he asked. "There's something I can't say under a lamp. And I've been standing here for two hours trying to decide whether I could say it anywhere at all."

She let him in because — she told herself — it was better to know where he stood than to wonder. Also because he was shivering, and also because some stubborn part of her, the part that had shared stolen plums with him on the cathedral steps when they were sixteen, wanted very much to be wrong about what she suspected he had come to say.

The room was small and warm and full of paper. He looked at the pages pinned everywhere, at the columns of ciphered numerals in her careful hand, and she watched him understand, watch him do the arithmetic, watch the arithmetic hurt.

"You rewrote it," he said. "All of it. From memory."

"I was apprenticed to a mapmaker," Astrid said. "People think maps are about geography. They're about memory. You draw a coastline four hundred times and you can find it blindfolded." She set the ink down. "Sit, Tomas. You look like a man about to resign his post."

"I have resigned my post," he said, sitting. "Effectively. In spirit. The paperwork will follow." He took off his hat and held it in his lap like a cat that had misbehaved. "Astrid, I need to tell you what I am first, so you know exactly what you're refusing."

"That's honest."

"I'm the inspector who hunted you. I read your file before I ever knew your name was in it. I requisitioned the garrison records. I interviewed the sergeant at the pass who took your plates. I am the reason the atlas is in a sealed drawer in the Ministry archive with a classification number." He recited it flatly, like a man reading charges against himself, which, she supposed, he was. "I signed the search orders for Kalvik. Your landlady's cousin was questioned. I apologize to her, if you ever think it safe to pass along."

"She'll be thrilled to have been questioned. It's the most excitement she's had since the milliner's fire."

"Astrid."

"Yes." All the humor went out of her voice, and she let it go; kindness sometimes required it. "I know. Go on."

He went on. He told her about the seized plates, about sitting up three nights in the ministry reading room with the cipher half-solved, about the moment the numerals resolved into tonnage — twelve thousand sacks diverted from the relief districts while the ledgers recorded *delivered in full* — and about finding names. Not merchant names. Not smuggler names. Names with titles attached. Names whose signatures appeared on commissions, including his own.

He told it badly, actually. He was not a storyteller; he had always been the boy who read the ending first to see if it was safe. But he told it completely, which was rarer and worth more, and when he finished he sat there holding his hat and looking, for the first time since she had known him, like a man with no idea what the rules required of him next.

"There's more," he said. "The part I came to say."

"Then say it."

"They're going to burn the plates." His voice stayed level; his knuckles didn't. "Not file them. Burn them. There's a review scheduled — an 'archival correction,' they're calling it, the same language they used with you in Kalvik — for the second of January. After the holiday, when no one is watching. I've seen the order. I signed the routing slip myself, before I understood what I was routing." He looked up at last, and his eyes were terrible with honesty. "I came to warn you. That's all. That's the whole of it. The evidence you bled for is going into a stove in eleven days, and I couldn't—" He stopped. Started again. "I've spent six weeks deciding whether I serve the Empire or what the Empire says it is. I finished deciding somewhere on Krondal Street, under your lamp, getting snowed on. So. Now you know what I am. A traitor to my own commission, warning the criminal I was sent to find."

"Alleged criminal," Astrid said.

"Astrid."

"Alleged," she insisted, and then, because fairness was a habit she could not break: "All right. Actual. Sit still a moment."

She sat still herself. She looked at him — this man who had measured loyalty all his life in signatures and filings, who had followed every rule until the rules led him past a frozen child and into a drawer full of his superiors' thefts — and she tried to feel what she had expected to feel, which was triumph, or rage, or at least satisfaction.

What she felt instead was tired, and cold on his behalf, and afraid in a new and complicated way.

"Do you understand what you've done?" she asked quietly. "Coming here?"

"Yes."

"If anyone followed you—"

"No one followed me. I checked for six blocks and doubled back twice, which I learned from your file, which is possibly the only useful thing the Empire ever taught me."

"And when they notice the plates are missing from the stove schedule—"

"They'll notice. Eventually. Probably soon." He turned the hat in his hands. "I've thought about it. My career ends either way now — the moment I pulled that routing slip and copied the order, I stopped being recoverable. The only variable left was whether I ended as a man who watched it burn or a man who didn't." A pause. "I'd prefer not to end at all, mind. I've grown attached."

Despite everything — despite the checkpoints and the betrayal at the pass and eleven days of writing dead men's ciphers alone under the eaves — Astrid laughed. It came out cracked and unexpected, and Tomas Reval looked so startled by it that she laughed again.

"I'd prefer you not end either," she said. "For old times' sake. And for newer reasons."

"Astrid." He leaned forward, and now the inspector was gone entirely and it was only Tomas, fifteen years old on the cathedral steps, holding out the plum. "I know what I owe you. I know what my uniform did to you at that pass. I'm not asking you to forgive—"

"No," she agreed. "You're not."

"—but I am asking you to use me. I know the archive rotations. I know which clerks drink and which ones don't and which door has the broken lock everyone pretends not to have noticed. I know how the Ministry thinks, because I was the Ministry, and being thought with is a kind of knowledge you can't get any other way." He set the hat down on the table, decisively, the way he filed things. "Give me a job. Any job. The worst one."

Astrid stood and walked to the window. Below, Krondal Street glittered with early lamps and late shoppers, and somewhere among them, she knew, Ilse Brenner was hiding with a family held hostage by the same men Tomas had just betrayed, and somewhere across the river the presses of the free printers waited for cargo that would get everyone hanged if it moved wrong.

Eleven days. Less, now. Ten and a half.

"You should know," she said without turning around, "that I have spent many hours imagining what I would say to you if I ever saw you again. Most of the versions involved shouting. One involved the ink bottle." She picked it up, weighed it, set it back down. "But none of the versions had you walking into the lion's den and carrying out the fire schedule. So the versions are spoiled, and I'm going to have to write a new one, and I don't yet know how it ends."

"That makes two of us."

"Don't mistake me, Tomas." She turned. "I haven't forgiven you. Forgiveness is a map you draw slowly, and I've only got the coastline so far. But—" she searched for it, found it, offered it like bread to a swimmer, "—I trust the survey. You measure honestly. You always did. It's what you chose to measure that was the problem, and you seem to have fixed that."

He absorbed this the way he absorbed everything: completely, visibly, and without any ability to pretend otherwise. His eyes were bright. He blinked twice and looked at the ceiling and pretended the ceiling was fascinating.

"The worst job," he said, when he could speak evenly again. "Assign it."

Astrid sat down across from him and pulled a fresh sheet toward her, and for a while neither spoke. Then she dipped her pen, and grinned — a real grin, dangerous and bright, the first one in eleven days.

"Well," she said. "How are you at forging a dead man's handwriting? We're going to build the censors their own atlas — and it needs to be beautiful enough to burn."

Tomas looked at the pages on the wall, at the cipher, at the ink bottle that had nearly been thrown at him, and asked the only sensible question left.

"How many days do we have?"

"Ten," said Astrid, "and a half."
