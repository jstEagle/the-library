## 17. The Counterfeit Century

The workshop Reval had found them was a bookbinder's attic above a shop that smelled of glue and dead ambition, rented under the name of a cousin he claimed existed. Astrid did not ask whether the cousin was real. In four days she had learned that Reval lied the way honest men do — badly, apologetically, and only when it mattered.

"Say it again," he said. He sat at the workbench with a sheet of foolscap before him and the expression of a schoolboy facing an examination in a subject he'd been told he invented.

"A map is not a picture of the world." Astrid dipped her pen without looking; her hands had done it ten thousand times. "It is an argument about the world. Whoever draws it decides what matters. Every border is an opinion. Every blank space is a choice."

"And you're going to teach me to make opinions."

"I'm going to teach you to make *bad* ones. Deliberately bad ones. It is much harder than it sounds, so pay attention." She slid the first plate across the bench — a chart of the Vessel coast, beautifully wrong. "This sandbar here has migrated two miles north. This channel has silted shut. A pilot who trusted this map would run aground within the hour."

Reval studied it. His finger hovered over the coast like a man afraid the paper might bite.

"It's gorgeous," he said finally. "That's what troubles me. I've inspected confiscated maps for three years, and if someone handed me this I would call it masterwork and lock it in the evidence vault."

"That," said Astrid, "is the entire plan."

* * *

Reval had arrested smugglers before. He had never *collaborated* with one, and the strange thing — the thing he lay awake turning over at night on the narrow cot beneath the eaves — was how natural it felt. Astrid Voss worked the way he imagined conspirators were supposed to work: quietly, precisely, without wasted motion or wasted words. The Empire's manuals described traitors as feverish creatures, driven by grievance. Nobody in the manuals mentioned that a traitor might simply be a woman who missed her teacher and wanted his last work to mean something.

They fell into shifts. He took the forgery by day — his official credentials let him requisition archival stock from the provincial depot, blank plates and genuine imperial watermarks, which he signed out under the fiction of an ongoing evidence audit. She took the cipher by night, transcribing the true ledger from memory onto thin sheets of rice paper that could be folded to the size of a thumbnail.

On the second evening he watched her write numbers in columns, fast as arithmetic, and asked, "How long have you known it? Truly."

She didn't look up. "Since I was nineteen. Master Renn made me copy every plate of the atlas twice — once for the engraver, once for the archive. He said a cartographer who couldn't reproduce her own work from memory wasn't a cartographer, only a clerk with good lighting." Her pen paused. "I thought it was vanity. It turns out he was writing me into the evidence. I just didn't know the language yet."

"You loved him."

"I resented him terribly," she said. "He left me an unfinished atlas and a workshop full of debts. And yes. Both things can be true at once. Now hold this lamp steady — if the watermark sits crooked the inspectors will notice before they notice anything else. Inspectors always see the frame before the painting."

"That's insulting to inspectors."

"It should flatter them. Frames are important."

He held the lamp. He noticed, and decided not to mention noticing, that she had begun to say *we* instead of *I*, and that she no longer positioned herself between him and the door.

* * *

The decoy atlas grew plate by plate, a masterpiece of careful corruption. They agreed on its doctrine early: the censors must find exactly what they expected to find. Reval knew the search protocols from inside — the way a seized atlas was checked for hidden layers, bleached inks, pinholes. So Astrid built the false atlas to survive inspection brilliantly, its margins dense with harmless trigonometry, while the one thing it lacked was the cipher itself. Let them conclude they had caught everything. Let them file their prize and sleep soundly.

"The trick," Astrid said, burnishing a false lighthouse onto a false cape, "is that it has to be worth seizing. If it looks worthless they'll wonder where the real one went. It has to look like victory."

"Arrogant," Reval said, "but I follow the logic. We're forging a trophy."

"We're forging their own stupidity, which is the easiest thing in the world to forge because they'll supply the original."

He laughed — actually laughed, a rusty sound that seemed to surprise him more than her — and for a moment the attic was only a warm room with two people in it, working late over lamplight while snow came down soft past the windows. Then he remembered the child's body by the granary road, drawn on his own survey maps in his own neat hand, and the laugh died somewhere in his chest. Astrid saw it go. She said nothing. She passed him the burnisher, which was, he was learning, how she said most things.

* * *

By the third day the real cargo was ready, and it looked like nothing at all.

Astrid spread the components across the bench: twelve sheets of rice paper, folded to the size of playing cards, each carrying a portion of the grain ledger in cipher. Beside them, a proof sheet Reval had obtained from a jobbing printer on Candlemaker's Row — a New Year's broadsheet, the kind sold on every street corner in the capital at year's end, full of almanacs and horoscopes and pious verses.

"They check bags at the printing-quarter gate," Reval said. "Every parcel, every portfolio. But nobody in the history of the Empire has ever searched a printer's own delivery of unsold stock." He laid the broadsheet flat and showed her the gutter between pages eight and nine, where the fold left a cavity wide enough for two folded sheets. "Paste it shallowly, wrong-side up, like a misfeed. The pressman finds it tomorrow and complains about the paper merchant. Meanwhile it has walked through the gate in plain sight, twice."

Astrid turned the broadsheet over in her hands. "You've thought about this a great deal for a man who believes in order."

"I believed in order," Reval said. "Order is a map too, apparently. It depends on who drew it." He was quiet a moment. "My commission was signed by General Aldringen. Four days ago I would have shot anyone who said his name belonged in your master's ledger. Now I've read the cipher myself, and I keep thinking about arithmetic. Two thousand sacks diverted at Kalvik. Eleven hundred at Torshavn. You can argue about politics forever, but you cannot argue about arithmetic. It just sits there. Adding."

"That's what Master Renn used to say. Numbers are the only honest witnesses. People lie; numerators don't." She smoothed the rice paper flat. "He also used to say that a mapmaker's job is to put things in their true positions, and let other people decide how to feel about it. I used to think that was cowardice. Now I think it might be the bravest thing there is — to draw the coast exactly as it is and refuse to care whose harbor it ruins."

* * *

They worked through the evening of the twenty-ninth, and somewhere past midnight the conversation drifted, as conversations do in attics, away from the work and into the warmer country around it.

"Tell me something true," Astrid said. Her hands kept folding; her eyes were on the ceiling beams. "Since we're conspirators now. Something no inspector knows."

Reval considered. "I failed my first field inspection. Froze at a checkpoint, let a cart through without searching it, and it turned out to be full of untaxed brandy. My commanding officer wrote in my file: *hesitates.* Sixteen years of service and I'm still that boy at the checkpoint, every time. I hesitate." He turned his cup on the bench. "Your turn."

"I talk to dead people," Astrid said. "Not ghosts. I draft letters to Master Renn in my head when I'm stuck on a plate. *You'd do the shading differently,* I tell him. *Your coastlines were better than mine but I'm faster at rivers.* For two years the only conversations I had worth having were with a man who wasn't listening." She smiled down at the rice paper. "Lately I've stopped. I don't need to ask him anymore. I know what he'd do."

"What would he do?"

"He'd finish it," she said. "Whatever it cost. He'd finish it."

The stove ticked. Snow whispered against the glass. Reval found that he wanted to say something kind and had no protocol for it, so instead he said, "The lighthouse on plate six is two degrees off true."

"I know," said Astrid. "It's bait. The censors will correct it, feel clever, and stop looking. A map should be wrong in exactly one place, so the reader congratulates himself on finding it and trusts all the rest." She glanced at him sideways, and there was, for the first time since the garrison, actual mischief in her face. "Master Renn taught me that. He said the Empire was built the same way. One visible error per province, so everyone feels wise and nobody checks the granaries."

"You're enjoying this," Reval accused.

"Terribly," she admitted. "Is that wicked?"

"It's the first sign I've seen that you expect to survive the week. I find it reassuring."

* * *

On the thirtieth morning they assembled the final pieces. The decoy atlas went into an evidence portfolio with Reval's audit stamps, ready to be "recovered" through channels that would make the seizure look like routine police work rather than a gift. The true letters rode inside the misfed broadsheet, packed in a printer's delivery crate between reams of almanac stock. Four days of work, and it came to one portfolio and one crate, light enough to carry in either hand.

Astrid stood over them a long moment.

"It seems too small," she said. "Two years of grief, one man's murder, a famine, and it fits in a fruit crate."

"Most enormous things do," Reval said. "An empire fits in a filing cabinet, if you shelve it right."

Tomorrow, the thirty-first, they would move separately: Reval to deliver the decoy through his official channels, Astrid with the crate toward the printing quarter, timed to arrive at the gate at dusk, when the year's last traffic jammed the checkpoint and every inspector wanted only to be done and gone to the festivities. After that, the presses. After that, midnight, and whatever century came next.

"One more thing," Reval said as she pulled on her coat. "If Ilse Brenner is still out there — and she is, we both know it — then whoever coerced her knows the route. The capital end of it, at least. If she appears at that gate tomorrow—"

"She won't be carrying a lantern for us," Astrid finished. "I know. I've thought of little else." She looked at the crate, at the small pale corner of rice paper just visible in the broadsheet's fold. "But Tomas — if she does appear, it may not be as a knife. She sold us once with a gun against her family. People who are being held are not the same as people who have chosen."

"People who are being held can still get you hanged."

"Yes," Astrid said. "So can people who have chosen. The difference is only in what you owe them afterward." She lifted the crate, and paused, and frowned at the door.

Down on the street below the bookbinder's shop, someone had begun knocking — not at the shop's front, where customers knocked, but at the side entrance, the unmarked one, four slow knocks and a pause and then four again. A signal. A knock with grammar in it.

Reval's hand went to the drawer where his pistol lay wrapped in an inventory cloth. Astrid set down the crate very carefully, as though it might bruise, and crossed to the window, and eased back the curtain a finger's width.

Standing in the blue snow-light below, hood up, face gaunt and half-frozen, was Ilse Brenner — holding up, in one bare hand, a strip of paper that even at this distance Astrid could see was covered edge to edge in Master Halvard Renn's unmistakable hand.
