## 18. Ilse Returns

Three days after the garrison pass, I still could not sleep in the same room as a closed door.

Reval had found us the lodging — a printer's cousin's attic above a shop that smelled of lead type and lamp oil, two rooms connected by nothing but a ladder and trust. I took the inner room. Reval took the outer one, and the ladder, and the long hours of the night, because he said he no longer slept well anyway and I chose to believe him rather than examine what that confession cost him.

It was on the third night, somewhere past eleven, that I heard the ladder creak.

I was awake, of course. I had been lying on the narrow bed tracing the cipher in my head the way other people count sheep — *grain house at Vellern, forty thousand measures; grain house at Ostmark, sixty-two; signed, countersigned, sealed* — when the creak came, soft and deliberate, the creak of someone who knew which rungs held weight.

Then came a knock at my door. Two knocks. A pause. One more.

I sat up so fast the bedframe groaned.

Only one person in the world knocked like that. Renn had taught it to both of us our first week together — *if you must enter a room unannounced, announce yourself in a way only your own can read* — and Ilse Brenner had laughed at it then, called it sentimental cartography, and used it ever after.

My hands were shaking. That surprised me. I had imagined this moment perhaps forty times since the pass — imagined it with a knife in it, with shouting, with me finally saying all the things that had curdled in my chest while the soldiers carried our plates away in their rough grey arms. In my imaginings I was always magnificent. Cold. Unforgiving. A woman made entirely of righteous edges.

Instead I opened the door and said, stupidly, "You're dripping on the floor."

She was. Snow clung to her shoulders and hair, and her boots left small dark stars on the boards. She looked thinner than I remembered, and older, as though the weeks since the pass had been measured against her differently than against me. Under one arm she carried a wrapped parcel the size of a prayer book.

"Hello, Astrid," she said. Her voice did something complicated in the middle of my name.

"You sold us." The words came out flat and heavy, stones dropped down a well. "You stood at that checkpoint and told them where the plates were hidden, and you watched them take them, and then you walked away."

"Yes," said Ilse. "I did."

"They could have hanged me."

"They would have hanged you," she corrected gently, "and then shot me for good measure, and gone through the atlas at leisure over breakfast. What they actually did was take the plates from a decoy satchel and let two exhausted women stumble downhill believing the worst of each other." She paused. "Although I notice you didn't stumble very far downhill. Or change lodging. Or stop meeting Reval in the same corner café every morning at eight, where any observer with functioning eyes—"

"That's not the point."

"It's a point. It's an adjacent point." She set the parcel down on the table between us with great care, the way Renn set down vellum. "May I sit? I've been standing in alleys for three days and my legs have filed a formal complaint."

I did not answer, which she took — as she took everything — as permission.

* * *

From the outer room came Reval's step on the boards, then his voice through the door, low and wary: "Astrid?"

"It's fine," I called back, and then, because it wasn't fine and lying to Reval had stopped coming easily: "It's Brenner."

Silence. Then, after a moment: "There is tea water. If anyone wants it. If anyone deserves it." Which was, from Tomas Reval, practically a declaration of war on her behalf, and I think we both understood it as such, because Ilse closed her eyes briefly and exhaled.

"Tell me," I said, when we were alone again. "All of it. And don't arrange it nicely. You're good at arranging things nicely."

She flinched. Only slightly. But I had spent six years learning to read people the way I read coastlines — the small irregularities that reveal where the real depth lies — and I saw it land.

"You know I have family in the relief districts," she began. "My sister Marta. Her husband. The boys." Her hands flattened on the table, pressing down as if to keep the words beneath them steady. "After your master's letters started moving, after the atlas disappeared into circulation, they came to Marta's door. Not soldiers. Worse — clerks. Men with lists. They didn't arrest anyone. They simply explained, in the gentlest language, that families in the relief districts are subject to *administrative review*, and that administrative review has many possible outcomes, and that outcomes depend greatly on the conduct of relatives abroad." She looked up. "They gave me a choice at the garrison, Astrid. They always give you a choice. That's how you know it's cruelty and not accident — cruelty is always well-organized."

"So you chose."

"I chose Marta." Simple as that. No ornament, no apology dressed up as explanation. "You'd have chosen her too. Don't tell me you wouldn't, because I watched you carry that atlas past the customs house with your heart going like a rabbit's, and you did it for a dead man's memory. I did what I did for living children who call me Aunt Ilse and think I bring sweets when I visit." Her mouth twisted. "The sweets were a lie too, in the end. Everything was a lie. They made me report everything — the route, the checkpoints, the satchel. And then, do you know what they did?" She laughed, and it was an awful sound. "They kept the file open. 'Thank you, Fräulein Brenner, most helpful, we'll be in touch.' A person with a closed file can run. A person with an open file is a tool, and tools get used until they break."

The rage in my chest — the rage I had fed carefully for three days, three well-tended days of fury, because fury kept grief from getting at it — shifted uncomfortably. Rage wants its enemy tall and simple. Ilse sitting across from me, snow-melt in her hair and shame in her spine, was neither.

"Why come back, then?" I asked. "If the file's open, if they're watching you, walking into this attic is handing them everything on a plate."

"Because they made a mistake." For the first time, something lit in her face — not hope exactly, but the ember that hope is struck from. "They were so pleased with themselves at the pass. They had the plates. They paraded me past them in the evidence room — wanted me to see what my cooperation had bought, wanted the image fixed in my mind so I'd stay compliant. And I saw, Astrid. I saw the ciphered margins." Her hand went to the parcel. "And I saw that one plate was missing."

My breath caught. "The key plate."

"The key plate." She unwrapped the parcel with the reverence of someone handling relics, and there it was: a single copper plate, smaller than the others, etched in Renn's hand with what looked like surveyor's notes but was, as I knew better than anyone now alive except perhaps Reval, the rosette of substitutions that turned granary coordinates into names. "Renn never trusted the whole cipher to one satchel — you told me that yourself, back when I was pretending to be your friend instead of merely being one badly. He split it. The key traveled separately. And when they seized the plates, they seized everything they knew about, and they don't know this exists."

"How did *you* know it existed?"

"Because Renn told me. Six years ago, drunk on plum brandy after the Kalvik exhibition, crying about his late wife, saying, 'Ilse, if anything happens to me, the girl will know what to do with it, but someone has to carry the little one.'" She smiled, and it wavered. "He meant you'd know what to do. He meant I should carry this. I've carried it sewn into my coat lining through two searches and one very invasive customs inspection, and I would like, frankly, to never carry it again."

I reached for the plate and stopped, my hand hovering.

"I need to say something before I touch that," I said.

"Astrid—"

"No. Let me, or I'll lose my nerve, and I've rehearsed this on the ceiling for three nights." My voice shook and I let it. "I hated you. Properly hated you. I built a whole Ilse out of betrayal and put her up on a shelf to be angry at, because being angry at you was easier than being angry at myself for trusting so easily, and easier than being angry at Renn for dying, and easier than being afraid. And now you're sitting here with snow in your hair and a perfectly reasonable reason, and I don't know what to do with the shelf."

Ilse was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "Keep the shelf. Just put something else on it for a while. Anger keeps, like salt meat. It'll still be there if you want it back."

"That is a horrible metaphor and also completely true."

"I'm a courier, not a poet." She slid the plate across the table until it touched my fingertips. "Take it."

* * *

We worked by lamplight, the three of us, once Reval had climbed the ladder — climbing slowly, pointedly unarmed, setting down a tray of tea like a flag of truce. He and Ilse regarded each other across the table with the wary courtesy of two cats discovering they must share a windowsill.

"Inspector," she said.

"Not anymore, technically. Officially suspended pending review, unofficially a traitor to my profession, formally a man who makes excellent tea." He poured three cups. "Brenner."

"You look terrible."

"You should see the other inspectors."

And despite everything — despite the pass, despite the three days of hate — I laughed. It burst out of me sideways, inappropriate and warm, and after a startled second Ilse laughed too, and even Reval allowed himself the small dry smile that was, from him, equivalent to falling off a chair weeping with mirth.

Then we bent over the plate.

With the key laid beside the pages of cipher I had carried in my memory since the garrison, the atlas assembled itself in lamplight. Names emerged from coordinates the way ships emerge from fog — first the outline, then the whole unmistakable shape. Provincial governors. Relief commissioners. A treasury undersecretary whose signature I had seen on Renn's correspondence a hundred times without knowing what it purchased. And at the top of the largest page, the seal that authorized the diversion of famine grain in the winter of '96 — the winter the frozen child had been found near the granary road, the winter that had cracked something open in Tomas Reval that had never quite closed.

Reval copied names with a hand that stayed steady through pure professional discipline. I watched him reach the general's name — the general who had signed his commission — and pause only long enough to dip his pen again.

"Well," he said quietly. "There you are, sir."

"Can they convict?" Ilse asked. "Even with the press? Money buys lawyers the way rivers buy mud."

"With the press and the original plates and a serving officer's testimony—" Reval's mouth quirked. "Former serving officer's. It will be ugly. It will take months. Half of them will bargain down to pensions and disgrace instead of prison, and everyone will call it justice and be half right." He set down his pen. "But their names will be in the light. Once names are in the light, they never fully dry again."

Ilse looked at me across the table, over the copper plate that sat between us like a treaty.

"There's one more thing," she said. "The reason I really came, besides the plate and besides wanting to see your furious face, which was worth the walk." She pulled a folded paper from inside her coat. "The clerk who runs their file office. He owes me — a long story involving his sister and a favor I did her in another life. He can get the file on my family closed. But only if it's requested in writing by an official of the district, dated and sealed, and only before the new year, after which the office reorganizes and everything gets lost forever in the beautiful bureaucracy of 1900."

"Which means," Reval said slowly, "someone has to go into the printing quarter before midnight on New Year's Eve. Where the censors will be searching every bag at every gate. Where they will be looking, specifically, for anyone connected to a certain stolen atlas."

"Which means," Ilse agreed, "that I need to deliver a letter to a bureaucrat through the tightest checkpoint in the Empire on the busiest night of the year. And I can't do it alone, because I'm followed, Astrid. Every step. They let me keep walking precisely so they can see who I walk toward."

The attic went very quiet. Outside, the wind worried at the eaves, and below us the sleeping presses of the printing quarter ticked and settled like large patient animals.

I looked down at the copper plate — at Renn's careful hand, at the cipher I had memorized without meaning to, at the blank sheet of my own future that I had, somewhere along the way, stopped fearing.

"You realize," I said to Ilse, "that if we're caught, the story of how the century ended will be extremely short."

"Two lines," she agreed. "'They were seized at the gate. The end.'"

"And yet."

"And yet," she said, and for the first time that night she smiled properly — the old Ilse smile, crooked and stubborn, the one I had decided I would never forgive and discovered, sitting there in the lamplight, that I already had.

Reval reached for a fresh sheet of paper and uncapped his ink.

"Then we'd better write that letter," he said, "and we'd better write it beautifully."
