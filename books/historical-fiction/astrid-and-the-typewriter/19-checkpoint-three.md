## 19. Checkpoint Three

The capital smelled of tallow and celebration, which is to say it smelled like every city on the edge of a new century: hopeful on the surface, with something older burning underneath.

Astrid Voss walked the last quarter mile to the printing-quarter gate with a leather satchel against her hip and her heart behaving badly about it. Around her, the crowd thickened — families heading toward the riverfront where the midnight fireworks would go up, vendors selling roasted chestnuts and paper horns, children wearing masks of the old year, which was depicted, in Kalvik's fashion, as a thin man with a long beard made of calendar pages.

She had bought one of those calendars-beard masks three days ago as a joke for Reval. He had worn it for exactly four seconds, in the safe house, and then removed it with the gravity of a man removing a medal, and said, "I look like my aunt Greta," and they had both laughed until Ilse told them from the doorway that people who laughed loudly in their trade did not stay in it long.

Now the laughter sat somewhere behind her ribs, folded small, like a map you keep meaning to consult.

* * *

The gate itself was nothing grand — an arch of grey stone spanning the street where the printing houses began, hung tonight with lanterns and a banner reading WELCOME, 1900 in letters that someone had painted slightly crooked. But beneath it stood six inspectors in imperial grey, and behind them two soldiers with carbines, and before them a queue of citizens shuffling forward while their bags were turned out into the cold.

"Every bag," Reval had told her that morning, in the low voice he used when he was delivering bad news gently. "They're not looking for contraband gin, Astrid. They've been told a document is moving tonight. They'll open books. They'll unroll maps. They'll shake printed sheets and listen to them."

"Listen to them?"

"You'd be surprised what a trained ear hears. A sheet carrying a hidden second leaf sounds different when you snap it." He had demonstrated with a broadsheet, snapping it flat, and she had heard it: a faint thickness, a whisper of doubling. "So whatever crosses that gate must be exactly what it appears to be."

"And what I'm carrying must not be."

"That too," he said, and looked at his hands.

* * *

Here was the arithmetic they had arrived at, after four days of the counterfeit century's work:

The censors wanted the atlas. Not the letters alone — the atlas, Master Halvard Renn's great atlas of the Baltic provinces, because the atlas was the cipher and the cipher was the testimony and the testimony was the end of eleven names, some of them very high. The decoy they had built was beautiful and hollow: a fabricated atlas with correct coastlines and innocent margins, aged with tea and handled with dirty hands so it would smell of the road. Reval would ensure it was found, examined, declared authentic enough, and buried in the archive where inconvenient documents went to be forgotten. The real record — the letters, the key, the ledger in Renn's hand — would cross the gate inside the one place no inspector thinks to search: already printed.

Ilse's idea, in the end. Astrid had wanted to memorize everything and carry nothing; Ilse had shaken her head and said, "Memory burns, memory bends, and if they take you and put you in a room with a lamp, memory comes apart at the seams. Paper is stubborn. Paper repeats itself exactly." So the letters had gone to a printer sympathetic to the free press — an old man named Kessel who owed Renn a debt from thirty years back and asked no questions aloud — and had been set in type as a *New Year's Almanac and Broadsheet for the Year 1900*: tide tables, a farmers' almanac of planting dates, market prices for grain by district.

Grain prices by district. That was the joke of it, the fierce private joke that kept Astrid upright in the queue. Every numeral in the tide tables, every price in the columns, was Renn's cipher laid over legitimate print. To any censor, it was the dullest document in the Empire. To anyone holding the key — and five hundred copies would come off Kessel's press at midnight, carried out under aprons and coats and one suspiciously fat loaf of bread — it was the ledger of the guilty, published to the quarter bells.

The broadsheet in her satchel was the proof copy. It was also, folded between its leaves, wrapped in oilcloth thin as onion skin, the original cipher key in Renn's hand — because a broadsheet without its key was only weather.

And the atlas — the true atlas, the plates and pages she had inked through grief, the thing she had crossed a river and a mountain range carrying, the thing Ilse had betrayed her for and been betrayed by — the true atlas was in the satchel too.

Because the plan required it. Because the decoy could not simply appear; it had to be *found*, plausibly, on the person of a courier who had fought and lost, and there was no courier so plausible as the real one, surrendering the real thing.

Reval had explained this to her twice, carefully, the way you explain weather to someone about to sail into it.

"They'll take it from you," he said. "That's the whole design. You give them the genuine article and let it save its life by dying in an archive."

"It's not dying," Astrid had said. "It's being misfiled. There's a difference. My master taught me there's a difference."

But standing in the queue now, four places from the lantern-light and the grey coats, she discovered that knowing a difference and feeling it were cartographies drawn by different hands.

* * *

Ahead of her, an inspector opened a fishmonger's crate, prodded the herring, waved the man through. Behind the fishmonger, a woman with twins argued that baby clothes were not documents. An inspector with a moustache like a drawer handle pulled a book from a student's bag, fanned it, held it to his ear, snapped it shut, and handed it back with a shrug.

Astrid breathed the cold air and did the thing Renn had taught her to do when her hands shook over fine work: she thought about the work instead of the hands.

*A map is an argument about power,* he used to say, tapping the coastline with his pipe stem. *Whoever draws the border decides whose field pays whose tax. Whoever names the bay decides whose language the sailors dream in. Never draw a line you haven't thought about defending.* She had been fourteen and had thought he was talking about lines. He had been talking about everything.

He had begun recording the officials' deeds in the margins of his working maps the way other men leave windows open in summer — casually, habitually, until the habit became a wall of evidence. The granary coordinates disguised as soundings. The diverted tonnages disguised as tidal corrections. *If they ever ask why,* he told her once, unusually sober, *tell them I drew what was there.*

Well. Tonight she would draw what was there too.

"Next."

* * *

The inspector who waved her forward was young, chapped-lipped, bored in the way of men who have been standing since noon. Beside him, though, stood an older one — senior, epauletted, watchful — and Astrid's stomach performed a slow roll, because the older one was holding in his gloved hands a printed notice, and the notice bore a sketch.

The sketch was of her. It was not a good likeness — the chin too soft, the eyes too wide — but likenesses hardly mattered. What mattered was the word beneath it: *WANTED*, and beneath that, *ATLAS OF THE BALTIC PROVINCES — IMPERIAL PROPERTY — REWARD.*

"Bag," said the young inspector.

She gave it to him the way she imagined a woman gives up something she has already grieved: slowly, with clean hands, with a face arranged into tiredness. This part she had rehearsed on the walk. Tiredness was honest. Tiredness explained everything.

He set the satchel on the plank table and opened it.

Out came the atlas first — because she had packed it to come out first, because if the story was that she had failed, the failure should be visible immediately, should break over the inspectors like cold water. The grey covers. The brass corners, scratched from the mountains. The spine she had re-stitched herself at seventeen, badly, and Renn had kept the bad stitching and called it *character*.

The senior inspector's head lifted from his notice like a dog catching scent.

"What's this?"

"An atlas," said Astrid. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her; she had expected it to crack. "Which apparently I shouldn't have."

The young one flipped the cover. Pages of coastlines, soundings, marginalia in faded ink. He turned pages with the respectful boredom of a man handling another man's homework. The senior one stepped closer. He took the notice and held it beside her face, and for one long moment the only sound was the paper horn vendor across the street demonstrating his wares with more enthusiasm than skill.

"You're Voss," the senior inspector said. "The apprentice."

"I was the apprentice," Astrid said. "The master's dead. The work's unfinished. I was bringing it to finish it properly, and now I suppose I'm bringing it to you instead." She let her shoulders drop, just barely. Let the tiredness show at the seams. "Happy new year."

The young inspector almost smiled. The senior one did not. He lifted the atlas from the table himself — with both hands, she noticed, the way one lifts a relic or a weapon — and paged to the middle, to the foldout chart of the relief districts, and his eyes moved along the margins hunting for numerals.

Astrid watched him hunt and felt the strangest sensation, like watching a man read a letter she had written in a language he only half knew. The margins of the true atlas were full — but the ciphered sequences had been worked over, subtly, with Kessel's help and Reval's knowledge of how censors think: altered just enough that a hurried examiner would find the familiar shape of guilt and a thorough examiner would find errors, inconsistencies, a forgery's fingerprints. If he checked it against anything in the next hour, the forgery would hold just long enough. After midnight it wouldn't matter what anyone believed.

But he wasn't checking it against anything now. He was checking it against his own certainty, and certainty is the fastest reader in the Empire.

"This is it," the senior inspector said quietly, to the young one, and something in his voice made Astrid's chest ache in a way she hadn't planned for. He sounded like a man holding the thing he had been promised would make his career. "This is Renn's hand."

"Yes," said Astrid.

It cost her more than the atlas had. Saying *yes* to the theft of it — saying yes as though it were already gone, as though she agreed — was the single hardest syllable of her life, and she had once said goodbye over a grave.

"The rest of the bag," the senior inspector said.

The young one went through it thoroughly. Her spare shirt. Her bread. Her tin of pens. And then the broadsheet — the *New Year's Almanac and Broadsheet for the Year 1900*, proof copy, printer's slug still on the corner — and here Astrid permitted herself the smallest, most dangerous thing she had done all journey: she spoke before he could ask.

"Tide tables and grain prices," she said. "For the districts. My master always said a mapmaker should know what the water and the wheat are doing. It's dull as church. Take it if you want something to fall asleep to."

The young inspector glanced at her. Something almost human passed across his face — the look of a man who has been bored by documents professionally and recognizes a fellow sufferer. He snapped the broadsheet flat, the trained snap, listening for the whisper of doubling.

There was none. Kessel had printed the cipher *into* the page, single-leafed, no hiding at all. Hidden in plain sight means never hidden at all.

"Dull," the young inspector agreed, and folded it, and here — here was the moment Astrid had not been prepared for, the moment no amount of rehearsal covered —

He handed it back.

"Keep it," he said. "New year and all that."

She took the folded broadsheet from his chapped hand and felt the little oilcloth package inside it, light as a moth, heavy as a century, and heard herself say, "Thank you," in a voice that was nearly steady, and turned toward the arch.

"Wait," said the senior inspector.

The crowd noise fell away. The lanterns swung. Astrid stood very still under the crooked banner of WELCOME, 1900, and thought, absurdly, of Renn's bad stitching, and of Ilse somewhere ahead in the crowd with her fifth copy of nothing, and of Reval at the archive steps pretending to be a man doing his job, and of the child's body Reval had once described to her, found near the granary routes, which had cracked his faith like frost cracks stone.

"The reward notice says the courier travels with a woman named Brenner," the senior inspector said. "Dark hair, northern accent, a scar on the left—"

"She left me at the mountain pass," Astrid said, and the truth came out of her like heat from a stove, because it was the truth, all of it, the betrayal and the coercion and the terrible arithmetic Ilse had been forced into, all compressed into one sentence that sounded, to an inspector's ear, exactly like bitterness. "Took the money and vanished. If you find her, tell her the atlas was never worth what she sold."

The senior inspector studied her for a moment longer. Then he tucked the wanted notice away, hugged the atlas to his chest like a rescued child, and jerked his chin toward the arch.

"Go home, apprentice. The century's turning."

* * *

Astrid walked through the gate into the printing quarter, and the sound changed — the hush of the queue giving way to the deep industrial breathing of the presses running behind dark windows, hundreds of impressions an hour, the city's heart pumping ink. Kessel's shop glowed on the corner, and in its doorway stood Ilse, hatless in the cold, watching her come, and behind Ilse, leaning against the doorframe with a calendar-beard mask pushed up on his forehead like a ridiculous crown, was Reval.

"Well?" Ilse said.

Astrid reached into the satchel and drew out the folded broadsheet, and unfolded it, and held it up to the lamplight — tide tables, grain prices, the dullest document in the Empire — and the three of them stood there in the doorway's glow while somewhere across the city a church tower began, softly, to count the last minutes of the old year.

"He gave it back to me," Astrid said. "He said *keep it*. And I—" Her voice did finally crack, right on schedule, four hours late. "I gave them the atlas. I just — handed it to him. He held it like it was treasure."

"It was treasure," Ilse said quietly. "That's why it worked."

"No." Astrid folded the broadsheet along its creases, careful, precise, a mapmaker's hands doing a mapmaker's work. "It *is* treasure. That's the point. Everything it meant is still here." She tapped the paper. "Everything it was — they can file that in a basement and let the damp eat it. My master taught me a map is an argument. Well. In about ten minutes, this argument goes to press, and the Empire doesn't get a rebuttal."

Reval straightened off the doorframe and pulled the mask down over his face, and even through the painted beard of the dying year his eyes were bright and terrified and certain. Inside, they could hear Kessel's press gathering speed, the forme locked, the fifth hundred copies waiting for the stroke of twelve.

"How long till midnight?" Ilse asked.

Astrid looked up. Over the rooftops, above the crooked banner and the lantern-hung gate and the whole grey machinery of the Empire, the first firework of the evening went up and burst — gold, brief, unrepeatable.

"Not long enough," she said, and pushed open the door, and the bells began.
