## 20. Midnight Presses

The printing house on Binder's Lane smelled of ink and hot metal and the particular anxiety of men who had never broken a law before and were enjoying it more than they expected.

Astrid stood at the composing stone with ink to her elbows, watching old Ferenc Kessler set the last lines of the broadsheet by hand. He was seventy if he was a day, half-deaf, and had spent forty years printing customs notices and almanacs and the city's official announcements of grain quotas — the very quotas that were lies. His hands moved now with the tenderness other men reserved for their grandchildren.

"Third line from the bottom," he said, not looking up. "Read it back to me."

"'Fourteen thousand sacks diverted at the Vessel depot, October 1897, by order of—'"

"Louder," said Ferenc, cupping his ear.

"—*by order of Provincial Commissar Aldous Griebe.*"

Ferenc nodded, satisfied, and slid the line into the chase. "Forty years I printed that man's proclamations," he said. "Every festival day, there he'd be on the front page, cutting ribbons, kissing babies. I printed a picture of him kissing a baby once. Ugly baby, as I recall." He tightened the locknut with a wrench older than Astrid. "Now we print what he actually did. There's a retirement gift."

Outside, Kalvik's church bells were gathering themselves for midnight. Through the frost-furred windows Astrid could see the streetlamps, the last revelers hurrying home with their coats pulled up, the river running black beneath the ice-sheened bridges. Somewhere out there was Reval, standing at the checkpoint she was deliberately nowhere near. Somewhere out there was Ilse, who had come back from betrayal carrying the cipher key in the lining of her boot like a splinter working its way to the surface. And somewhere far away, in a room Astrid still could not picture, was Halvard Renn, alive, waiting for midnight the way other men waited for executions.

"You're doing it again," Ferenc said.

"Doing what?"

"Looking through the world instead of at it. Your master does that too." He caught himself, glanced at her. "Did. Does. Whatever the truth is this week."

"The truth is he's alive," Astrid said, and found that saying it aloud still produced the same vertiginous lurch, grief and fury and relief all pressed into one sheet. "And at midnight he stops hiding."

"Then pull the lever when the bells do," said Ferenc. "Nothing waits forever. Not even the dead."

* * *

Tomas Reval had spent eleven years learning to stand at checkpoints in the cold without showing the cold, and tonight every one of those years deserted him at once.

The New Year's gate at the printing quarter was lit like a stage. Lanterns on chains. Inspectors in their best winter uniforms, because the Commissioner believed inspections should look festive. A queue of printers, binders, paper-merchants, and one extremely suspicious quantity of New Year's broadsheets flowing through the gate to be distributed at dawn — the traditional commemorative sheets, everyone agreed, with the traditional verses and the traditional list of the year's officials and their traditional portraits.

Including, in this year's edition, the traditional portrait of General Marshal Edvard Krail, patron and namesake of Tomas Reval's commission.

Reval held the counterfeit atlas under his arm like a sleeping child. It was a good forgery; he'd watched Astrid and Ilse make it over four days in a safehouse that smelled of boiled cabbage, aging the paper with tea, distressing the edges, drawing the false coordinates in Halvard Renn's hand — which Astrid did better than Renn, which was its own small heartbreak for her, he could see. The censors would open it tonight, find the granary coordinates altered just enough to be wrong, and believe they had won. Believing they had won was the whole strategy. Men who believe they've won stop looking.

"Halt." A young inspector, barely fuzzed with authority, stepped into the lamplight. "Papers and cargo."

"Tomas Reval, Imperial Inspectorate, Third Bureau." He showed the warrant card. "I'm carrying the recovered Voss atlas for archival destruction. Sealed orders." He patted his breast pocket. "You're welcome to verify the seal."

The young inspector looked at the atlas, then up at Reval, and something flickered across his face — the particular expression of a junior man meeting a name he'd heard spoken of. *Reval, the one who cracked the garrison affair. Reval, the general's own.*

"They say you caught the smugglers at the mountain pass," the young man said.

"I didn't catch anyone," Reval said. "A woman turned herself in and sold her companion. I filled out the paperwork." He heard how it came out of him, flat and bitter as a stone, and could not call it back. The young inspector blinked. Somewhere behind them the queue shifted and grumbled. The bells of Saint Odile's began their first slow toll.

"That will be all," Reval said quietly, and walked through the gate as the twelfth bell fell and the whole city behind him erupted — whistles, cheers, kisses thrown from balconies, the century dying and the new one arriving with all the noise people make when they are determined to be hopeful.

Inside the archway, an archival clerk took the atlas, weighed it, stamped a form, and carried it away toward the furnace room. Reval stood very still in the crowd of celebrating inspectors and let the thing he had done settle onto him completely: he had just handed the Empire a lie with his own hands, in uniform, under his own name, and there was no version of tomorrow in which that did not end him.

He found, examining himself honestly, that he felt lighter than he had in months.

*An argument about power,* Renn used to say. *Every map is one.* Reval had thought it was mapmaker's vanity. Now, watching the forged atlas disappear toward the furnaces while the real truth rolled past the gate in bundles of holiday paper, he finally understood the lesson, and laughed — once, sharply, alone in the crowd — at how long it had taken him.

* * *

Ilse Brenner had never been good at waiting, which was why God or fate or simple arithmetic had assigned her the job that involved walking.

By half past midnight she was three districts east, hauling a handcart of New Year's broadsheets through streets still loud with celebration, and she had developed a system. She did not thrust the sheets at people. She simply left them where the morning would find them: folded on the milk-cart step, weighted under a bootscraper, tucked into the wire letter-cage of the tenement on Wren Street where — she knew, because she had checked twice — three families lived whose men had worked the relief granaries in '97 and come home thinner than they'd left.

At the corner of the fish market she paused, breath steaming, and looked at the great blank wall of the customs shed, whitewashed every spring against bills and graffiti. Perfectly clean. Perfectly ready.

She pasted a broadsheet up with flour paste, smoothed it out with the flat of her hand, and stepped back.

Her brother's face was not on it. None of the dead were on it, not by name — only numbers, tonnage, dates, signatures, the cold bookkeeping of starvation. *Fourteen thousand sacks diverted at the Vessel depot.* She did the arithmetic she always did, the one no one had ever put on paper: a sack fed a family for a month, roughly; a family was five people, roughly; fourteen thousand sacks was — she lost the count, the way she always did, somewhere in the hundreds of thousands, because the number stopped being arithmetic and became a country.

"Marek," she said softly, to nobody, to the snow, to the whitewashed wall. "Look at this. Look what your stupid sister did."

A drunk staggered around the corner singing, stopped, squinted at the broadsheet, and read it with the laborious concentration of a man reading through beer. His lips moved. He got to the fourth line. He went very quiet.

"Is this true?" he asked her. "It says here the commissar—"

"It says," Ilse agreed. "There are more coming. One on every corner by dawn."

He stared at her. Then he took off his hat, which was such an unexpectedly decent gesture that it nearly undid her, and walked on — and she watched him stop at the next lamppost, take out a stub of pencil, and copy down the commissar's name onto his cuff, so as not to forget it.

That, she thought, was worth everything. That drunk copying a name onto his cuff was worth the pass, the betrayal, the weeks of shame, the look Astrid had given her at the garrison checkpoint — the look Ilse deserved, and had survived, and would carry the rest of her life alongside Marek's memory. Mercy was not the same as forgiveness. Astrid had extended the first and withheld the second, and Ilse had accepted the arrangement without complaint, because it was more than fair.

She took hold of the cart handles. Four hundred sheets left. Dawn was coming, and the presses were still running, and for the first time since Marek died she was not walking away from anything.

* * *

Halvard Renn had spent two years being dead, and he had gotten rather good at it.

Being dead, he had learned, required discipline. You did not visit old haunts. You did not correct people who misspelled your name in obituaries. When a former student greeted you on the street — this had happened exactly once, in a market town four hundred miles from anywhere — you became a slightly deaf old man with a foreign accent and you complained about the price of herrings until the student fled. You learned that a dead man's greatest advantage is that no one looks for him, and his greatest wound is that no one looks for him either.

At nine o'clock that evening, in the rented room above the chandler's shop, he had laid out on the table the document he had written and rewritten for eleven days: his testimony. Thirty-one pages in his own unmistakable hand — the hand that had started all of this, the letters, the danger, the whole avalanche — setting down everything he had seen and measured and ciphered into an atlas. At the bottom of the last page, where a signature belonged, there was a blank space two inches wide.

He had been looking at that space for most of the evening.

Through the window he could hear the bells, and then the cheering, and then — fainter, but unmistakable, rolling up from the direction of Binder's Lane — a sound he knew better than his own heartbeat: a steam press running flat out. He closed his eyes and listened to it the way other men listened to choirs.

*Astrid.* He had watched from a doorway in the capital six days ago, hood up, as she crossed the square with Reval beside her — his Astrid, taller than he remembered, walking like a woman who had decided something. She had passed within fifteen feet of him. She had been carrying, at that moment, everything he had ever taught her, plus several things he hadn't, and he had gripped the doorframe and kept still, because staying dead was the plan, and the plan protected her, and he had repeated that to himself every night for two years like a prayer that had stopped working long ago.

But tonight the presses were running. Tonight the truth was going up on walls by the fistful. And tonight, for the first time, staying dead protected no one. It only protected *him*, and it was beginning to seem to him — an old man, a coward by his own recent accounting, sitting alone above a chandler's shop while the new century rattled its presses — that a man who hid inside his own obituary while his apprentice stood in the light had no right to call what he felt for her love.

The blank space waited.

Renn dipped the pen. His hand, which had drawn coastlines for forty years, did not shake, though he gave it permission to.

*Halvard Renn*, he wrote, in the unmistakable hand, large enough that no censor could pretend it was anyone else. Beneath it: *Alive, and responsible for every word herein. Let them come and find me.*

He blotted it, sat back, and laughed — a rusty, disused sound, like a door opening after years. Then he put on his coat, took his hat, and went down the stairs into the first hour of the year 1900, out into streets where his own testimony was already drying on the walls, and he read it as he walked, over and over, weeping without embarrassment, a dead man strolling through his own resurrection.

* * *

Dawn came up grey and clean over Kalvik, and Astrid climbed the printer's outside stair to the roof with two cups of coffee, one of which she handed to Reval, who had arrived sometime in the small hours looking like a man who had resigned from his own life and slept wonderfully.

Below them, the city was waking to find itself plastered with paper. At the fish market, a crowd had gathered around the customs-shed wall, and someone — Ilse, probably, or the drunk with the pencil — was reading aloud to people who could not read, and each time the reader reached a name, the crowd made a sound Astrid had never heard a crowd make before: not a roar, not a gasp, but a low collective exhale, like a city finding out it had been holding its breath for two years.

"There," Reval said, pointing.

Down on Binder's Lane, moving slowly against the flow of early carts, came an old man in a shabby coat, hat in his hands, stopping at every posted sheet to touch the signature at the bottom of it as if checking it were really there. People were beginning to notice him. People were beginning to compare the signature on the wall to the man standing before it, and Astrid saw the recognition travel down the street like wind through wheat.

Her coffee went cold in her hand. Her legs carried her down the stairs without consulting her.

They met in the middle of the road, carts swerving around them, and neither of them said anything for a moment — the apprentice and the master, the mapmaker and the map — and then Renn opened his mouth to begin, she could see, some prepared speech about forgiveness and danger and why he had done what he'd done, and Astrid, who had rehearsed her own speech for eleven days, abandoned it entirely and said:

"You're late for work. The trials start Thursday, and I need someone who knows the Vessel depot's actual boundaries, because the defense will lie about them."

Renn blinked. Then his face did something complicated and wet and wonderful, and he said, "You've gotten bossy."

"You made me inherit an unfinished atlas. It came with the paperwork."

He laughed, and it cracked in the middle, and he pulled her to him, ink-smelling and thin and alive, and held on while Binder's Lane filled with people reading the truth in the new century's first light. Over his shoulder Astrid saw Ilse at the corner, cart empty, watching, not approaching — and Astrid nodded to her once, a small nod, a down payment on something not yet earned. Ilse nodded back and turned to walk home through the crowd, past the walls full of names.

Above them all, on the printer's roof, Reval drank both cups of coffee and did not mention it, and thought about Thursday, and about the general who had signed his commission, and about how a trial, too, was a kind of map — and wondered, with a sensation between dread and vertigo, whether any of them truly understood yet how much ground it was going to redraw.
