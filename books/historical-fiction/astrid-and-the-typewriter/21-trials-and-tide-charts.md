## 21. Trials and Tide Charts

The courthouse in Kalvik had been built by men who believed that justice required good acoustics and bad heating. Every word spoken in the great hall carried to the last bench, and every person in the last bench was frozen stiff by noon. I sat there through the whole of February — Ilse beside me, her hands wrapped around a stone hot-water bottle she guarded like an heirloom — and listened to an empire take inventory of its own sins.

It should have been satisfying. Everyone told me it would be.

"You'll feel it when they read the verdicts," said the baker's wife, who had taken to leaving us bread we hadn't paid for. "You'll weep."

I did not weep. What I felt instead was something stranger and quieter, like standing on a shoreline at low tide and seeing, for the first time, exactly how much wreckage the water had been hiding all along.

* * *

The trial of Vice-Governor Aldric Mott lasted eleven days. He arrived each morning in a coat worth more than my master's entire workshop had been, and he smiled at the gallery as though we were guests at his dinner party. Mott had been the architect. The broadsheet — Renn's testimony, printed in three thousand copies on New Year's morning — had named him plainly: *the grain diverted at Kalvik, Hesselund, and the river districts passed through offices under Mott's signature.*

His defense was a masterpiece of the bureaucratic art. Yes, his signature appeared. No, he had not read what he signed. A man signing two hundred documents a day could hardly be expected to read them; that was what clerks were for. He produced clerks. The clerks produced other clerks. Somewhere at the bottom of this chain of shrugging men sat a dead junior accountant who could no longer contradict anyone.

"He's going to swim out of it," Ilse whispered on the ninth day, her voice flat as ice on a harbor.

He did not quite swim out of it. The tribunal — three judges, one of whom had the disconcerting habit of falling asleep during witness testimony and waking only for the numbers — found Mott guilty of negligence in office rather than theft of relief stores. Negligence carried four years. Theft of relief stores, given that the stores had been measured in thousands of lives, might have carried the rope.

Four years. For the famine districts' dead, a number the tribunals never settled on but which Renn's ledger put, in its careful ciphered margins, above nine thousand.

I wrote the figure in my notebook and looked at it for a long time. Then I closed the notebook, because there is only so long a person can hold a number like that up to the light.

* * *

Not everyone swam. I want to be fair to the century that had just died and the one taking its place, because fairness was the whole point of what we'd done.

Director Oskar Vell of the Relief Commission was convicted properly — theft, fraud, conspiracy — because he had made the tactical error of keeping his own private ledgers, in plain figures, in a locked drawer, apparently under the impression that locks were arguments. When the verdict came down, Vell fainted, and the baker's wife wept, and Ilse gripped my arm hard enough to bruise.

"Nine thousand," she said, "and they got one honest verdict out of it."

"One and a half," I said. "Mott goes to prison. It isn't much of a prison, but he goes."

"And the general?"

We both looked down the hall to where General Erich Dalm stood at ease in his dress uniform, awaiting his turn before the tribunal. Dalm had signed Reval's commission. Dalm had, per the ledger, authorized the "reallocation schedules" that sent grain to buyers while the relief districts starved. And Dalm had friends — old classmates of the tribunal judges, men whose portraits hung in government corridors, men who came to sit behind him in the gallery in a show of uniformed solidarity that took up three rows.

His trial began in March and ended in nine days.

The tribunal found that General Dalm had exercised "imperfect oversight." He was reprimanded. He was asked to retire early. He left the courthouse through a side door, saluted by two of the guards, and was seen that evening at the Officers' Club, where presumably they toasted his imperfect oversight in decent brandy.

Ilse did not speak for an hour afterward. When she finally did, she said only: "My brother starved forty kilometers from full granaries. Forty. You could walk it in a day."

"I know," I said.

"They gave him a reprimand."

"I know."

"What kind of arithmetic is that?"

It was, I thought, the empire's arithmetic — the same arithmetic Renn had spent his last living years secretly correcting in the margins of an atlas. We had redrawn the map. That was true, and it mattered, and I clung to it the way a drowning sailor clings to something that floats but does not move. Every official in the province now knew that their signatures were legible, that their ledgers could be read aloud in public, that nine thousand people had names. That was not nothing. Some mornings it felt like nearly everything.

Other mornings it felt like a reprimand.

* * *

Tomas Reval testified on a gray Tuesday in late February.

I had not spoken to him since the capital — since the night he came to my borrowed room carrying what he knew the way a man carries a stone in his chest, looking for somewhere to set it down. Since then we had built the counterfeit atlas together over four sleepless days, conspiring over candle stubs and pots of ink like the world's most anxious burglars, and I had learned things about Tomas I'd never known in all the years we'd been friends: that he hummed when he worked, badly and tunelessly; that he apologized to objects when he bumped into them; that his faith in order had not shattered all at once but cracked along fault lines, quietly, the way ice gives way.

Now he stood in the witness box in his inspector's uniform, the last time he would ever wear it, and gave his evidence in a voice that did not shake.

He described the seized plates. He described the cipher and how he had broken enough of it to find the names. He described, in the flat careful tone of a man reading coordinates, how he had located his own commission among the guilty papers — the general's signature, the date, the small institutional courtesies by which one man buys another.

"Inspector Reval," the presiding judge interrupted, "you understand that your testimony implicates officers of your own service."

"I do, sir."

"And that the service takes a dim view of it."

"I have recently become acquainted," said Tomas, "with the service's views on a great many things."

There was a rustle through the gallery — half laughter, half horror. The judge rapped his gavel. Ilse leaned toward me.

"I like him," she said, in the tone of someone admitting to a crime.

"So do I," I said. "It's very inconvenient."

The cross-examination was brief and vicious. A counsel for the defense — one of Mott's men, sleek as a weasel in a good coat — suggested that Inspector Reval had himself conspired with the forgers of the documents, that his decipherment was invention, that a man who betrayed his superiors would say anything.

"A man who betrays his superiors," Tomas replied, "would be well advised to invent something more comfortable than this. I have invented nothing. I have only stopped pretending not to read."

They took his commission that same week. There was no trial for it — just a letter on ministry paper, delivered with bureaucratic courtesy, informing him that his services were no longer required and his pension forfeit, in consideration of conduct unbecoming. Conduct unbecoming. As though truthfulness were a stain on the uniform rather than the only thing that might have redeemed it.

He came to find me the day the letter arrived. I knew before he spoke, because he was wearing civilian clothes for the first time since I'd known him, and they fit him like a costume.

"Well," he said, sitting down across from me at Ilse's kitchen table, "I am officially nobody."

"You look relieved," I said.

"I am appalled by how relieved I am." He turned his hat in his hands — a new hat, bought hastily, slightly wrong. "Eleven years. Do you know what I keep thinking about? The forms. I filled out so many forms, Astrid. Beautifully. Perfectly. I once delayed an investigation three weeks because a form lacked a countersignature, and I was *proud* of that." He shook his head. "And the whole time the countersignatures were the crime. The forms were the weapon. I was guarding the lock on the granary door and calling myself a good man for never once looking inside."

"You looked inside," I said. "Eventually. Most men never do."

"That is the most generous thing anyone has said to me all year, and it is February."

Ilse, stirring something at the stove, said without turning around: "Reval. Would you like supper?"

He blinked. From Ilse this was roughly equivalent to a marriage proposal.

"Yes," he said carefully. "Thank you."

"Good. Because you're useless to Astrid hungry, and she needs useful people. She won't say so herself. She never does."

This was true, and I resented it, and I ate my supper.

* * *

The trials ran on through the thaw. In all, thirty-one officials faced tribunals. Nine were convicted properly. Seven bargained down to negligence, or retirement, or — in one spectacular case — a transfer to a colonial posting, which I gathered was the empire's way of saying *we know, and now go be somebody else's problem.* Four died before judgment, including one who left a confession naming two ministers and was found hanged in a manner the coroner called, with a straight face, self-administered. Eleven were acquitted outright, mostly on grounds of having been extremely far away when the grain disappeared.

So: partial. Sobering arithmetic, if you wanted justice to come out even, and I confess I did want that, some nights, furiously.

But here is what else happened, and I set it down because I lived it and still barely believe it: it happened *in public*. The broadsheet had gone out on New Year's morning and could not be recalled, not by any tribunal, not by any general. The names were read aloud in open court, printed in the papers that dared, argued over in coffeehouses from Kalvik to the capital. Farmers in the relief districts — the ones who had survived — traveled days to sit in those frozen galleries and hear, from official mouths, the acknowledgment that they had been robbed. An old woman from Hesselund stood up during Mott's sentencing, against all procedure, and said in a voice that carried beautifully to the back benches: "My husband ate bark. Say the word 'bark' into your record, Your Honor, next to his signature."

The stenographer, to his eternal credit, wrote it down.

Power, Master Renn used to say, is a map, and whoever draws the map decides where the rivers run. For a hundred years the empire had drawn its own map, and on it the granaries were always full and the rivers always ran smoothly past them. Now there was another map in circulation — Renn's, mine, the stenographer's, the old woman's — and you could not unprint it. People consulted it. People navigated by it. Two of the acquitted officials lost their seats anyway, voted out by constituents who had learned to read the terrain.

Survival was not free. Truth was not free either. Both had cost more than any of us had budgeted. But they were in circulation now, and circulation, I had come to believe, was the closest thing to justice this world offered.

* * *

Renn came to the courthouse on the final day, in the open, alive, accountable — his choice, made at midnight amid ringing bells, and he had not wavered from it since. He testified about the atlas: its making, its purpose, the years of quiet documentation. He answered three days of hostile questions with the maddening patience of a man explaining tide tables to a seagull. When Mott's counsel sneered that a mapmaker was hardly qualified to pass judgment on matters of state, Renn considered this for a long moment.

"Sir," he said, "a mapmaker is precisely qualified. I have spent forty years recording where things truly are as opposed to where powerful men say they are. It is the same profession."

Even the drowsy judge woke up for that one.

Afterward, on the courthouse steps, in the thin March sun, he stood beside me and watched the crowd disperse.

"You're angry," he said. It wasn't a question.

"The general got a reprimand."

"Yes."

"Nine thousand people, Halvard. A reprimand."

"Yes." He put his hand briefly on my shoulder — the first time he had touched me since returning to the ranks of the living. "When I started the ledger, I thought I was building a bomb. Something that would go off once, magnificently, and level the whole rotten structure. It didn't work like that. It works like the tide. It comes back, and comes back, and wears the rock down a little more each year. I'm sorry it isn't lightning, Astrid. But I've seen lightning. Lightning misses."

I looked out at the harbor, where the water was working loose from the ice, dark and patient, moving in its slow enormous way toward everything.

"Tide," I said. "Fine. But I'm keeping score."

"My dear girl," said my master, "so am I. It's what the margins are for."

* * *

That night Ilse, Tomas, and I ate supper in the workshop — my workshop again, swept out, the stove drawing well, the great table cleared except for a single sheet of fresh paper weighted at the corners. They argued cheerfully about politics, which was new; Ilse had opinions about the Ministry of Agriculture that could strip paint. Tomas had begun talking, in a tentative way, about teaching — surveying, navigation, "useful arithmetic for people who intend to check the answers." Ilse declared she would manage his accounts whether he liked it or not, since clearly no one else would.

I barely heard them. I was looking at the blank sheet.

Under it, in the drawers, lay everything we had saved: the letters, the cipher key Ilse had carried back to us at such cost, my memorized plates copied out in my own hand, and the original atlas — surrendered, catalogued, stamped, and eventually returned to me by a clerk with a shrug, as though it were an umbrella someone had left on a train. Renn had looked at it once, all four hundred pages, and then closed it and said, "Well. It was never the atlas. It was what the atlas kept."

But there were other things the margins could keep. Other coastlines. Other numbers that deserved to be written down where the powerful could not decline to read them.

I picked up a pen.

"Astrid," Ilse said, breaking off mid-rant, "what are you doing? It's ten o'clock at night."

"Drawing," I said.

"Drawing what?"

I ruled the first line — clean, certain, north to south — and reached for the tide charts of the famine districts, the ones I'd requested from the harbor office three weeks ago and not yet had the courage to unroll.

"The rivers," I said. "All of them. And the names."

Tomas leaned over to see, and went quiet. Ilse came around the table, and she went quiet too. Outside, the harbor ice groaned and shifted, giving way at last, and somewhere downriver the water began, patiently, to carry whatever we set upon it toward the sea.
