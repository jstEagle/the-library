## 22. A Blank Sheet

The paper arrived on a Tuesday, because in Astrid Voss's experience all important things arrived on Tuesdays — her apprenticeship had begun on one, and so had Master Renn's funeral, which had turned out to be a lie of the most elaborate and infuriating kind.

She had ordered it from the same mill in the north that had supplied the workshop for forty years, and when the crate came she made the delivery boy carry it up three flights of stairs rather than accept his suggestion that "the lady might want it left by the door." The lady did not want it left by the door. The lady wanted it carried as if it were a sleeping child, because that was how Master Renn had always insisted paper be handled, and some instructions outlive their instructors.

The new workshop was smaller than the old one. That was deliberate. The old one had been seized, catalogued, and sold at auction during the trials, and Astrid had attended the auction herself with a flask of coffee and a notebook, recording the sale prices of her own drafting table with the detached precision of a woman surveying enemy coastline. A customs officer from the river checkpoint bought Renn's magnifying lamp for eleven crowns. Astrid had almost told him it was worth more — almost, out of pure professional spite — but he had waved her through that gate two years ago without looking twice at her tubes, and there was a kind of symmetry in letting him overpay.

So: smaller. Two windows instead of four, facing east instead of south, which meant she would lose an hour of good light every afternoon and gain the sunrise over the harbor instead. She considered this a fair trade. She had spent enough of her life working by lamplight on things other people wanted kept dark.

Ilse arrived before the furniture did, which was typical of Ilse, who had never once in their acquaintance arrived anywhere in the correct order.

"You've hung nothing on the walls," Ilse said, standing in the doorway with her arms crossed, surveying the bare room as though it had personally insulted her.

"It's only been mine for six days."

"My brother's house stood empty for two years after they buried him, and you know why." Ilse set down a parcel wrapped in oilcloth. "I brought you bread. It's slightly stale. I was going to eat half of it on the way and then decided you needed the whole lie."

"That's the most honest gift anyone has given me this year."

They had reached a strange and careful peace, the two of them — the sort of peace built not on forgiveness fully given but on the recognition that mercy is a thing you practice daily, like penmanship. Some mornings Astrid woke still furious about the pass, about the snow, about the soldiers' hands on the plates she had bled to carry. And then she would remember the cipher key arriving wrapped in a stocking, and Marta Brenner's letter — *Aunt Ilse came home, Aunt Ilse cries when she thinks no one sees* — and she would grind her ink and get on with it. Trust, she had learned, was less like a plate you dropped and more like a coastline: it eroded, it rebuilt, and the shape was never quite the same twice.

Astrid unwrapped the bread, broke it, and handed half back. "Sit. Before the chairs come, so we can appreciate them properly."

Ilse sat on a crate instead, out of what appeared to be principle.

* * *

By the end of April the workshop had walls again — or at least things leaning against walls. Reval had contributed a shelf unit he'd built himself, which listed noticeably to starboard and which Astrid kept purely as evidence in any future argument about inspectors' competence.

"Your map case is crooked," she told him when he came by that first week, bringing coffee and pretending he hadn't resigned from the Imperial Survey in disgrace.

"The map case is *characterful*."

"The map case requires a shim."

"The map case," said Reval, settling into the only chair with the contentment of a man who had discovered that unemployment suited him like a well-cut coat, "is the last piece of government property I will ever be asked to straighten, and I intend to let it lean for the rest of my natural life."

He looked thinner than he had at the garrison inn, and older, and easier in his skin somehow — as though carrying his resignation publicly had removed a stone he'd swallowed at nineteen. He drank his coffee, complimented the light, and then went quiet in the particular way he went quiet when something was actually on his mind.

"Say it," Astrid said.

"They posted the vacancy yesterday. My old desk. A boy of twenty-three took it." He turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "And I found I didn't mind. I minded for about nine seconds, and then I thought about him sitting there in ten years, deciphering whatever they put under his nose, and I thought — better him at a desk than me behind one, believing it."

"And now?"

"Now," Reval said, "I am told you require someone who can read administrative correspondence, draft petitions, and argue with clerks until they surrender. I am extremely qualified. I have done it to you personally."

It was true. During the trials he had sat beside her through eleven weeks of testimony, translating the Empire's language of evasion into plain speech, and once — memorably — had reduced a provincial treasurer to confessing on the stand simply by asking him, with genuine courtesy, to define the word *surplus*.

"The pay is terrible," Astrid said.

"I've had worse employers."

"The hours are worse."

"Astrid." He set down the cup. "You are offering me work that matters, in a room with good light, run by the only person I have ever failed who was willing to speak to me again. Take the shim away from the argument and let me say yes."

She let him say yes. She did not, however, remove the shim, because the shelf really was crooked, and because she suspected he would find its persistence comforting — proof that some flaws were permitted to remain exactly as they were.

* * *

Renn came on a Thursday, which Astrid noted for the record as the man's ongoing refusal to do anything predictable.

She heard the knock and opened the door expecting the flour delivery, and instead there he was on her stoop: sixty-eight years old, beard gone entirely white, wearing a fisherman's coat two sizes too large and holding a bunch of tulips he had very clearly purchased from the flower cart on the corner and very clearly regretted purchasing approximately four seconds after doing so.

"I was told," he said, "that there is a workshop here requiring the consultation of an experienced mapmaker."

"There is. He's dead, poor man. Terrible loss. Brilliant hand, faked his own death rather than face consequences—"

"Astrid."

"—left his apprentice to carry everything, including several items of extreme personal risk, while he hid in a fishing village eating herring—"

"Herring," Renn said with dignity, "is a noble fish."

She stepped aside and let him in, because she had rehearsed this conversation for two years across a hundred sleepless nights and had discovered, somewhere in the middle of all that rehearsal, that what she actually wanted was not the apology she had scripted for him. It was this: tulips, dignity, herring, him alive and standing awkwardly in her doorway with spring behind him.

He walked the room slowly, the way he used to walk the old workshop, running one finger along the edge of the drafting table without quite touching the surface — an old habit, checking for level by instinct. He stopped at the window where the fresh roll of northern paper leaned against the wall, still wrapped.

"You haven't opened it."

"No."

"Why not?"

Astrid considered the question honestly, because lying to Halvard Renn had never once worked and had twice ended in international incidents.

"Because the last sheet I unrolled," she said, "was for your atlas. And the sheet after that will either be worthy of what it cost, or it won't, and I don't know yet which. Blank paper doesn't frighten me, Master. It's the first mark that does."

Renn was quiet for a long moment. Then he picked up the tulips, looked around for a vessel, found nothing but a rinse jar full of brush water, and put them in it anyway, where they looked absurd and wonderful.

"When I was your age," he said, "I froze for a full year. Couldn't put a line down. Every sheet felt like testimony I wasn't qualified to give." He turned to face her. "Then an old fool taught me that a map is not a promise to be perfect. It's an argument. You make the best one you can, and you let the next mapmaker improve upon it. That's the whole inheritance, Astrid. There's no more to it than that."

"There's also the herring wisdom."

"There is a great deal of herring wisdom, but that comes later, after lunch." He shrugged off the enormous coat. "Now. Show me this famine district map everyone keeps writing to me about, and tell me where you want my corrections, and be advised in advance that I will insist on redrawing at least one river."

"You will not touch the rivers."

"I will improve one river."

"You'll *argue* about one river, and then you'll concede I'm right, as usual."

Renn smiled — the old smile, the one from before, the one she had grieved and gotten back — and pulled a stool up to the table. Outside the window, the harbor light shifted; gulls argued over something; the city went about its business, which these days included reading the news with its spine straightened.

* * *

She cut the wrapping on the first of May, alone, early, before anyone else arrived. This part she wanted by herself.

The paper came off the roll pale as the inside of a shell, and it smelled the way it had always smelled — chalk and rags and cold water — and Astrid stood with her hands flat on it for a long time, letting the silence of the room settle, letting the morning do its slow work.

Then she mixed her ink.

She began, as she had been trained to begin, with the frame — the neat border, the scale bar, the compass rose in the lower corner, its north point drawn finer than the others as tradition demanded. Her hand was steady. Two years ago it would not have been; grief shakes the nib. But grief, she had found, eventually becomes a colleague rather than a captor. You learn to work alongside it. It points out things you'd otherwise miss.

Then the rivers. The Dorna first, wide and unhurried, running down from the hills through the districts whose names the newspapers had learned to print plainly: Kelmark. Ostrev. The low country beyond the weir. She drew them precisely, from the survey data she knew better than her own pulse, and along each bend, in small careful script, she began to write names.

Not the officials'. Those names had their own public accounting now, in trial transcripts and prison records and the broadsheet that fifteen hundred presses between here and the capital had run until the type wore smooth. No — these names belonged to the other ledger. The one no court had jurisdiction over. *Marta Lindqvist, age 6.* *The Osten twins.* *Father Anselm, who gave away his own ration.* One name per bend, per ford, per ferry crossing, so that anyone reading the map would understand: people lived here, at these exact coordinates, and the grain that should have fed them passed within sight of their doors.

It would take her months. Perhaps a year. Families were still writing to her — letters forwarded from the old workshop, from the printers, from Ilse's contacts in the relief districts — sending names, dates, corrections: *not the twelfth, madam, the ninth; he was born in Ostrev, not Kelmark; you have spelled it with two v's.* She answered every one. Accuracy, Renn had drilled into her, was not a virtue of craft. It was a form of respect.

Reval arrived at midmorning with coffee and stood watching over her shoulder for a while, saying nothing, which for Reval constituted eloquence.

"You'll need a second sheet for the low country," he said finally. "There won't be room."

"There will be room."

"Astrid, I've read the district rolls. There are—"

"*Room*," she said, in a voice that closed the discussion the way her master's voice used to close discussions, and Reval nodded and went to argue with the crooked shelf, which was the closest thing the workshop had to a punching bag.

* * *

By June the first sheet hung finished in the window, and people had begun to come to see it — not officials, not reporters, just people. An old woman from Kelmark who stood before it for twenty minutes and then touched the glass where her daughter's name was written, once, gently, and left without a word. A schoolmaster with twelve students who copied the compass rose into their slates. A young girl, maybe fourteen, who returned three Saturdays in a row before working up the courage to knock and ask, red-faced, whether workshops ever took apprentices.

"Do you draw?" Astrid asked her.

"Not well."

"Neither did I, at fourteen. Well — that's not true, I drew beautifully, but I believed I didn't, which amounts to the same problem." Astrid looked at the girl's ink-stained fingers, at the hungry steadiness of her eyes, and recognized the whole species of her. "Come Tuesday. Bring your own hands. We'll supply everything else."

Her name was Petra. She arrived on a Tuesday, naturally, and knocked over the rinse jar within the hour, and Astrid decided before noon that she would keep her forever.

* * *

On midsummer evening, with the light lasting past ten and the workshop windows thrown open to the smell of the harbor, the four of them sat among the map tubes drinking Ilse's terrible dandelion wine: Astrid, Ilse, Reval, Renn, and — because the workshop had accumulated strays the way driftwood accumulates barnacles — Petra, sketching the compass rose for the hundredth time in the corner, getting it nearly right.

"To the century," Ilse said, raising her tin cup, "which we survived."

"To the century," Reval amended, "which we survived *and embarrassed*."

"To the atlas," said Renn quietly, and they all turned to him, waiting for the weighty epilogue, the summing-up, the master's verdict. He took his time with it, turning the cup in his hands.

"When they seized it at the pass," he said, "I received word in my hiding hole and I grieved for it like a limb. Forty years of my life, in a soldier's sack, gone. And then the broadsheets came out, and the trials, and I understood something that took me embarrassingly long to understand." He looked up at Astrid. "They never held my atlas. Not truly. It was already somewhere they couldn't reach. It was in a head it turns out I'd filled years earlier without noticing." He raised the cup to her. "You were never my apprentice, Astrid. You were my second edition. Improved upon. As designed."

Astrid drank, because her throat was too full for anything cleverer.

Later, when the others had gone — Ilse walking Renn home arm in arm, arguing amiably about a river; Reval leaving by way of the shelf, patting it like a horse — Astrid stood alone before the second sheet, still blank on the rollers, waiting for the low country and its thousand names.

She thought about the girl she had been at the first chapter of all this: bent over empty plates in a dead man's workshop, resenting the inheritance of an unfinished life. She wanted to tell that girl something, and after long consideration, this was it:

*The blank page is not the burden. It never was. The burden was believing you had to fill it alone.*

Astrid dipped her pen, set the nib to the corner of the sheet, and drew the first line of the Dorna's western fork — steady, fine, sure of itself — and outside, all across Kalvik, the lamps of the new century came on one by one, like a coastline being charted in real time, by many hands, toward morning.
