## 2. A Promise in Peacetime

The letters arrived on Thursdays, and Mara Winterbourne read them before Edith Calloway did.

Not all of them—never the ones from her solicitor, sealed in double wax and carried by hand—but the rest, yes, steamed open over the kettle in the coach house with the patience of a man who had learned that haste was the only sin invisibility could not forgive. He would lift each envelope with tongs, hold it above the spout until the gum softened and sighed apart, extract the pages, read them twice, fold them back along their original creases, and reseal the flap with a thin stick of glue he kept wrapped in oilcloth among his boot polishes. By half past nine the post tray sat on the hall table looking exactly as it had at half past eight, and by ten Edith Calloway was reading her correspondence in the morning room with the fierce, unguarded attention of a general reviewing dispatches, never once suspecting that her driver knew her war better than she imagined.

He told himself it was duty. A woman in her position—a woman whose name appeared in newspapers beside words like *unwomanly* and *disorderly*, who received threats the way other ladies received calling cards—needed someone watching the gates of paper. And it was true; twice already he had intercepted envelopes with too-heavy postmarks and held them up to the lamp until the shadows of razor blades showed through, and burned them whole in the grate while Edith wondered aloud why the cranks had gone quiet. But duty was only the respectable half of it. The other half was simpler and uglier: he liked knowing things. He liked being, for a few minutes each Thursday morning, the most informed man in the county, though no one would ever know it, least of all him wanting them to.

That was the arrangement of his life. He erased himself from every room he entered. In the house he moved along the edges, close to wainscoting and doorframes, a shape the eye slid over. Servants forgot to announce him; guests mistook him for furniture that could drive. Even his face seemed arranged for forgetting—medium everything, brown hair going grey at the temples in a way that suggested nothing so much as weather. Mara Winterbourne. Not the name he was born with. He had buried the first name so thoroughly that some mornings he needed a moment's digging to remember the shape of the grave.

The kettle murmured. Outside the coach house windows, autumn was finishing its work on the valley—the elms gone to copper, the beeches holding their last gold like coins in a fist—and the river below the slope ran low and glassy between its stone banks, showing the bones of the summer shallows. Two years ago this same week, he had stood on the town side of the bridge with a cracked suitcase and a lie about references, waiting to interview for a position as coachman to a lady he knew only by her newspaper notoriety.

Two years ago this same week, he had met Anton Reval.

* * *

It had rained that day too—he remembered the rain because it had turned the bridge planks dark and made the whole structure sing, a low wooden hum underfoot, as if the crossing were an instrument someone had forgotten how to play. Mara had been walking into town to make his inquiries about the Calloway position, and halfway across he had come upon a man kneeling at the railing with a notebook full of figures, getting soaked, entirely indifferent to it.

The man looked up. He had the particular sunburned steadiness of someone who worked outdoors at things that must not fail, and an accent Mara could not place—not German exactly, not Russian, something worn smoother than either, like a stone from a riverbed.

"You hear it?" the man said, by way of greeting. "The bridge. She is tired."

"I'd assumed it was the wind."

"The wind is only the bow. The bridge is the string." He tapped the railing with two fingers, gently, the way one touches a horse's neck. "I am doing the survey for the new crossing. The iron one, upstream. Anton Reval." He put out a rain-wet hand, and Mara took it, and gave the name he wore then, which was the name he wore now, which was not his name.

"Winterbourne," Reval repeated, and smiled. "A good name for a bridge man. You are an engineer?"

"A coachman. Or soon, if my luck holds."

"Then you understand weight distribution also." He said it without irony, without any smile but the small permanent one, and something in the saying of it—that a driver and a builder might be kin in the mathematics of burden—opened a door in Mara that had been shut a long while. They walked into town together. They ate at the same counter, split the last of the plum cake because neither thought to fight over it, and talked the way men talk when one of them has nothing anyone wants: carelessly, freely, about rivers and roads and the strange loneliness of building things meant to outlast everyone who remembers building them.

Reval was working the border country, he said—bridges mostly, sometimes rail spurs, whatever contracts crossed his path. His own country lay a day's walk east beyond the river, though *country* he pronounced with a small bitter twist, as one says a word that has stopped meaning what it should. There was a woman there. There were letters.

By the third meeting—it had become, without either man announcing it, a habit; every fortnight Reval came through on the survey, and somehow Mara was always on the bridge at the appointed hour, and somehow the plum cake was always available—by the third or fourth meeting, Reval spoke plainly.

"My letters do not arrive," he said. "Or they arrive opened. Or they arrive after questions have been asked of the person who receives them, and she is—" he turned his cup on its saucer, a slow quarter-turn, "—she is not a person who benefits from questions. Her family, also. I need letters to travel that are not letters. Do you understand me? I need them to travel the way you travel, Mr. Winterbourne. Unremarked."

Mara set down his knife. He understood, in that instant, precisely what was being asked and precisely why it had been asked of him and no one else: because he had the face for it, because he was nobody, because a man who has erased himself can carry things across lines that stop everyone else. It flattered some cold starved corner of him even as it alarmed the rest.

"If they search you," Reval said, "they find nothing, because there is nothing. You carry nothing across. You go to the post office in Millbrook—you know it, the one with the green door, the old woman who does not look up—and you post them a village at a time, plain envelopes, mixed in with your own errands. Letters to a dressmaker. Letters to a widow named Halvene. That is all. The addresses change; I will give you the sequence. It looks like nothing because it is nearly nothing. Only—" and here his voice lost its surveyor's evenness for the first time, dropped into something rawer, "—only it is everything to one person, and I cannot do it myself anymore. My papers draw eyes now. Yours do not. Yours are beautiful, Mr. Winterbourne. Blank."

They sat in the lamplight of the inn parlor, rain still worrying the window, and Mara felt the promise assemble itself in him before he consented to it, the way ice forms on water—one crystal, then a sheet, then a weight-bearing whole. He thought of the razored envelopes in Edith Calloway's post. He thought of the grave where his real name lay buried. He thought: *this man trusts me with the only clean thing he has.*

"I'll carry them," he said. "As long as it stays what it is. Paper. If it becomes anything else—guns, names, money—I stop, and you'll get no argument from me."

"It will stay paper," Reval said. "I give you my word, and my word is the only load-bearing thing I have." Then he smiled fully, for the first time, and it changed his whole face, made him suddenly young. "You see? We are both bridge men. You carry people where the road is broken. I only do it slower, with more iron."

They shook on it, and the handshake lasted longer than handshakes do, and neither remarked upon it.

* * *

In the present, Mara folded the last of Thursday's letters along its crease and pressed the flap down with his thumb, and the steam ghosted off the kettle and fogged the little window, and outside the first frost of the season was coming down on the garden like a held breath released.

He had felt it coming all week—in the smell of the air, mineral and metallic, in the way the horses stamped and hunched, in the light itself, which had thinned to something brittle. Frost tonight. Within a month, ice on the troughs. And within six weeks, if the county kept to its schedule, the chain gangs would come to the bridge with their scaffolding and their tar pots and their red lanterns, and the river road would become the only way out of the valley until spring.

He posted the letter tray in the hall, aligned just so, and went out to the yard to breathe the cold.

Edith found him there, or rather passed through him there—she never quite looked at him, which he had come to understand as a form of courtesy, the highest compliment her kind paid to servants—and she stood a moment at the low wall looking down at the river, hands folded in her muff, a small upright figure in charcoal wool against a sky the color of dishwater.

"Mr. Winterbourne," she said. "Thursday, is it? The post came?"

"Just now, ma'am. Nothing requiring you before luncheon."

"And the frost?"

"Tonight, I'd say. I've banked the boiler and blanketed the harness."

She nodded, satisfied—competence was, to Edith Calloway, what beauty was to other women, a thing she registered instantly and never commented on—and then she said, in the offhand voice she used for decisions already made: "The bridge closes the second week of November. Repairs. I've written to the county and been assured it's routine, three months' work at most, but three months is three months. I intend to keep the household running straight through. Guests will want to come early and stay late rather than risk the roads—we'll be fuller than usual, and livelier, and some of it will be tiresome." She turned then, and did look at him, briefly and directly, the way one checks a knot. "You'll manage?"

"I'll manage, ma'am."

"Good." She faced the river again. Down in the valley the water ran quiet over its stones, unhurried, indifferent to the iron crossing that spanned it and the chains that would soon close it. "There's a great deal arriving in this house this winter," she said, to the river or to the frost or possibly to herself. "Some of it invited. Some of it merely inevitable. We shall see which is which."

She went in. Mara stayed at the wall a while longer, hands in his pockets, watching the light die on the water, and let himself think about Anton Reval for the first time in weeks—the fortnightly letters, eleven of them now, still bundled in oilcloth under a floorboard in the coach house, undelivered these four months because the border posts had grown eyes and the green door in Millbrook had grown a new clerk who asked questions, and because Mara had promised himself he would find another way and had not yet found it. Eleven letters lying in the dark like coins in a fist.

The first frost came down with the dusk, silvering the rails, furring the hedgerows, taking the last breath out of the garden. And far down the river road, so distant it might have been a trick of the failing light, Mara saw a carriage turn off the high road onto the valley lane—too early for any guest, too late for any tradesman—carrying toward the house the winter's first arrival, invited or inevitable, he could not yet tell which.
