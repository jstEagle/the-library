## 3. The Bridge Closes

The chains went on a Tuesday, in weather so still that Mara Winterbourne heard them from the coach house, a quarter mile off—iron knocking against iron, each link a small dull bell. He set down the harness he had been mending and stood in the doorway with his head cocked, the way a man listens for rain. Three strikes, then a pause, then the rattle of the padlock being tested. He did not need to go and look. He could see it plainly enough in his mind: the two county men in their gray coats, breath steaming, the chain looped through the iron stanchions at the near end of the span, the notice board nailed up crooked as always—*BRIDGE CLOSED FOR REPAIRS UNTIL SPRING. RIVER ROAD IMPASSABLE. BY ORDER.*

Impassable. The county men loved that word. It was true in the way that most official truths were true—true for carts, true for the mail coach, true for anyone who moved through the world at the speed the world expected of them. Mara had spent eleven years learning what the word did *not* cover. A man on foot, moving at night, with a knowledge of the old drover tracks and the fords that had silted since the mill closed—the river road was impassable to him the way a locked drawer was locked. That is to say, not at all, if you knew where the key lived.

He went back to the harness. There was no use standing in doorways listening to chains. Chains were only weather; you planned around weather.

But he did the arithmetic anyway, because arithmetic was the one comfort he had never learned to do without, and because Edith Calloway would want the numbers by supper, and because a man who could not matter to anyone could at least matter to a ledger.

The house would hold, at full winter strength, nineteen mouths: Edith and her niece, the cook and two kitchen girls, the gardener and his wife, the two housemaids, old Hobbs who saw to the fires, Mara himself, and—this was the part the arithmetic could not settle—the guests. Edith had written to half a dozen people in October inviting them for a fortnight in November. The fortnight had been chosen because the bridge would still be open. The bridge had not kept the appointment.

* * *

He drove into the town that afternoon for the last time before the frost closed the clay cuttings, and the town told him everything he needed to know without his asking. The ironmonger had sold out of lamp oil. The grocer was stacking flour in the window in sacks of a size Mara had never seen him stock—fifty-pound sacks, the kind bought by institutions and the frightened. At the post office, the postmaster's wife was pinning up a notice about the winter mail schedule, and beneath it, in smaller print, a second notice about the Registration of Aliens Act, which Mara read twice while pretending to read the first.

He bought what he could carry without explanation: a spare lantern, a coil of good rope, two blankets of the coarse kind that no one asked about, a tin of matches, a wedge of tallow. He paid in small notes, the way he always did, so that no purchase would be memorable. Then he sat in the coach on the way home and did the sums properly, his gloved fingers moving on his knee like a pianist's.

Coal: forty days' supply in the cellar, sixty if they rationed the drawing rooms, which Edith would not, because Edith Calloway rationed nothing that touched her pride. Flour: enough. Meat: they would live on salt pork and the gardener's pigeons by February and no one would die of it. Oil, candles, tea—the tea would run out first, and the running out of tea would cause more misery in that house than the running out of bread, because the household ran on tea the way the mill wheel had once run on water.

And exits. He counted them the way other men counted their money.

The bridge: chained, watched by no one, but visible from the town road—a man crossing it would be seen by anyone with a window facing the river, and half the town had windows facing the river. The mill door and the mill path: usable, dark, ending at a stretch of bank where the reeds grew tall enough to hide a standing man until the reeds died back in the hard frost, which they would, soon. The drover track north over the shoulder of the hill: nine miles of nothing, arriving at a hamlet with a railway halt that ran one train a day, at dawn, if the snow let it. And the river itself—frozen, in a hard winter, frozen to the thickness of a floor. He had seen it as a boy, in another life, in another country of the mind. Men had walked it. Men had driven carts on it, once, in '95, and the old people still told the story of the year the ice held.

He did not write any of this down. He kept it where he kept everything, in the locked room behind his eyes, and he had learned long ago that the lock on that room was better than any lock the county could forge.

* * *

Edith received the numbers at supper, or rather she received Mara, standing at the sideboard with the ledger open on the tray, while she sat over the tail end of a letter she was not reading. Her niece, Clara, had gone up early with one of her nervous headaches, which were in truth nothing but a young woman's horror of silence, and the dining room was down to the two of them and the tick of the case clock.

"Coal," Edith said, without looking up.

"Forty days. Sixty with rationing."

"We don't ration."

"No, ma'am."

"Then say forty days and let me decide to be reckless." She signed the letter at last, blotted it, and looked at him. She had the kind of eyes that made being looked at feel like an audit, and Mara had long ago stopped flinching from them, which was, he suspected, the only reason she kept him. "Flour."

"Enough."

"Tea."

"Six weeks."

She closed her eyes briefly, as if in prayer, or in the calculation of how much a household's soul was worth per ounce. "Six weeks of tea and forty days of coal. Then the house goes dark and the kettle dies and civilization ends, and all because two men in gray coats couldn't mend a bridge in the autumn like sensible men." She pushed the letter across the table toward him. "This goes to the stationer in the town, who will forward it. I know what you're going to say about the mail."

"The mail goes out twice a week until the cuttings close," Mara said. "After that, once a week, by the north road, if the carrier's horse holds."

"And after the horse doesn't hold?"

"Then the mail walks."

Edith looked at him for a long moment. There was a thing she did sometimes—a small stillness, a settling of the attention, like a hawk deciding whether the movement in the grass was worth the dive—and he had learned to endure it without betraying anything, though it never stopped being like standing in shallow water while something large circled underneath.

"You're the only person in this house," she said, "who thinks about the mail walking."

"Somebody has to, ma'am."

"Somebody has to," she agreed, "and it is never anybody. It is always you. Which is either a great comfort to me or a great warning, and I haven't yet decided which." She rose, gathered her shawl around her with the abruptness of a woman who considered sitting a concession to weakness. "We keep the house open. All winter. Send word to the town that my guests are welcome whenever they can get here, and that once they're here, they're here until the ice goes out, and if any of them find that objectionable they may say so to the river."

"And if the river objects to them, ma'am?"

"Then the river and I will have something in common at last." At the door she paused, her hand on the frame, her back to him. "Mara. The guests. How many do you think will actually come, knowing what they now know about the bridge?"

He considered. He had, in fact, considered nothing else for three days. "People who accepted in October accepted for November," he said. "People who have already made up their minds about a thing don't like to unmake them. They'll come because staying home would mean admitting the bridge beat them."

"Yes," Edith said softly. "That is exactly what I think. Vanity is the most reliable post-horse in England." And she went out, and the clock ticked on, and Mara stood in the dining room doing one last sum that had nothing to do with coal.

* * *

The first of them came on the Thursday, before the frost, when the clay roads were still soft enough to swallow a wheel to the hub. Mara drove to the crossroads to meet the carrier's van, and stood in the gray light with his collar up, watching the shapes resolve out of the mist: trunks, a hatbox, an umbrella wielded like a marshal's baton, and beneath it all the passengers, unfolding themselves with the groans of people who had traveled twelve miles in a dog cart and intended to be compensated for it by history.

There was Mr. Havelock, the magistrate, first, because magistrates are always first; a broad, pink, careful man who shook Mara's hand at the coach step and held it a half second longer than courtesy required, as though weighing the hand for something. There was Mr. Quill, the journalist, thin as a folded newspaper, whose eyes went over the house as he descended—roofline, windows, the coach house, Mara himself—with the quick inventory of a man pricing everything he saw. There was Miss Clara's cousin or friend or whatever the girl was, arriving with more trunks than sense. And there was a fourth, who came last, who came slowly, who stood at the step of the van with his hand on the rail and did not move for the space of three full breaths.

Mara knew him before the mist finished giving him up. He knew the set of the shoulders, the way the man held his head slightly forward, listening, as though the world were a machine whose rhythm he was always checking. Two years since Vienna. Two years, and one promise made on a station platform with the smell of coal smoke and cut stone in the air, and a bundle of letters pressed into his hands with a grip that had said *this matters* more plainly than any words.

Anton Reval looked at him, and looked away, and became a stranger. It was done in an instant, and it was done well—Mara felt a cold professional admiration even as something in his chest went tight and hot. The man gave a name to the maid who came out with the register, a name that was not Reval, and signed the book in a hand that was not his hand, and asked in careful, faintly accented English whether the house had good light for reading, as though light for reading were the whole of what a man needed in the world.

"Mr. Voss," the maid said, turning the book toward Mara for the carrying of the luggage. "From the north. He's staying through, ma'am says."

"Mr. Voss," Mara said, and took the heaviest trunk, and carried it up the stairs past the man himself in the corridor, and neither of them looked at the other, and the not-looking was the loudest thing in the house.

That night, after the lamps were out, Mara lay in his room above the coach house and listened to the frost coming. It came the way it always came, first as a silence so complete the case clock in the main house seemed to tick inside his own skull, then as a fine crystalline creaking in the timbers, then—toward dawn—as the long groan of the river, far off, beginning to think about ice.

He got up and stood at the window. Below him the grounds lay silvered and still, and beyond them the valley, and beyond the valley the dark line of the river, and beyond the river the chained bridge, invisible now, but present, the way a locked door is present in every room of a house.

Nineteen mouths. Forty days of coal. Six weeks of tea. One man in the house under a false name who did not know that Mara knew. One bundle of letters under the floor of the coach house that did not know they had waited two years for nothing. One promise, made in peacetime, by a man who had not known, then, what a winter could close.

The trap had not sprung. The trap had simply, gently, with all the courtesy of the county and the season, finished closing—and Mara Winterbourne stood at the window doing the only arithmetic left to him, the sum of what he would risk, and for whom, and when.

The frost came on. Somewhere down the valley, the river groaned again, longer this time, like a thing turning over in its sleep.
