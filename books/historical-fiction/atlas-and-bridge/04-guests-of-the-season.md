## 4. Guests of the Season

The first sleigh bells of the season came on a Tuesday, thin and metallic, like coins dropped down a well.

Mara heard them from the coach house, where he was greasing the runners of the light sledge, and he stood still with his hands black to the wrist and counted the intervals between bell-strikes and the crunch of runners on gravel. Two horses. Moderate weight. A man driving himself, which meant either pride or poverty, or both. By the time the visitor's knock had traveled through the front door and down the hall to Mrs. Hearn, the housekeeper, Mara already knew roughly what he would find when he went around to unload the luggage: a hard-sided trunk with brass corners gone dull, a leather dispatch case carried by its handle like a man carries a briefcase he never intends to open in company.

He was right. The man who stamped snow from his boots in Edith Calloway's hall was fiftyish, gray at the temples with the particular neatness of a barber who is paid by the hour, and he introduced himself to the empty air in general as Mr. Ambrose Fennick, magistrate of the northern district.

"Stranded, I'm afraid," Fennick said, with the satisfaction of a man who has rehearsed being inconvenienced. "The bridge being what it is. I understood Mrs. Calloway kept her household open through winter."

"Mrs. Calloway keeps her household open always," said Mara, taking the dispatch case, and had the small cold pleasure of watching the magistrate decide, visibly, whether the servant had been witty or merely foreign-seeming.

Edith appeared on the stair with a shawl over one shoulder and no hurry anywhere about her, and whatever she thought of this addition to her winter—Mara could read it in the half-second pause before she smiled—he knew better than to hope it was welcome. She had not wanted guests this season. She had wanted the house quiet, the correspondence steady, the cause moving forward on paper while the world outside lay under snow. But you could not turn away a magistrate without making of him an enemy with a title, and Edith Calloway did not manufacture enemies carelessly. She collected them deliberately or she declined them entirely.

"Mr. Fennick. How dreadful for you." Which meant: how interesting. "You'll want the blue room. It faces east—the ice on the windows makes gardens by February."

She turned to Mara. "Tell Mrs. Hearn."

It was not a message that required delivering; Mrs. Hearn had been standing in the doorway since before the bells. It was a message for the magistrate: this man is mine, and I trust him with my voice. Mara inclined his head and carried the dispatch case upstairs, and on the way he opened neither the case nor his face, and put the room in order—trunk here, washstand there, the single window latched against drafts—and memorized, in passing, the smell of the man's gloves: tobacco, ink, and under it something faintly chemical, like the inside of a photographic shop.

A man who took notes and pictures of things, perhaps. Or a man who liked to be thought capable of it.

The arithmetic in Mara's head quietly added a column.

* * *

They came in a flurry after that, as if the closed bridge had loosened some stopper upstream and the stranded were washing down the river road all at once.

The journalist arrived Thursday—a Mr. Silas Crane of the *Evening Argus*, traveling in a hired carriage with two trunks and a portable typewriter in a case lined with green baize, which Mara carried up to the second-best guest room and which weighed like guilt. Crane was younger than Fennick, softer, with the damp handshake of a man perpetually pleased with his own warmth, and he spent his first quarter hour in the house telling everyone within earshot that he had come north to write a piece on rural winter life, "though of course"—a smile like a drawer sliding open—"if there's anything else up here worth writing about, a good journalist keeps his eyes open."

His eyes were open now, doing their inventory of the hall: the banners rolled and shelved along the west wall, the crates of pamphlets stacked like cordwood, the framed photograph above the mantel of Edith on a platform in the capital, mouth open mid-sentence, ten thousand hats below her.

"Suffragists," Crane said, to no one, delightedly, the way another man might say *shipwreck*.

"Yes," said Mara, hanging the man's coat. "We get a great deal of weather."

The niece arrived Friday, before the roads glazed. Lilian Ashcombe was twenty-three and carried herself the way people carry full cups across crowded rooms—with total, exhausting attention, shoulders up, breath held, waiting to spill. She embraced her aunt at the door for slightly too long, asked twice whether the stairs were safe this winter, and then asked Mara, in a lowered voice, whether the house had ever been broken into, because she had read in the papers about houses being broken into, and her aunt was famous, after all, and famous houses—

"No one has ever broken into this house, miss," Mara said truthfully, because breaking in implied forcing something that was already open.

She laughed at that, a startled, unhappy laugh, and let him take her bag.

And then, on Sunday, when the frost had climbed the windows in ferns half a finger tall and the river down the valley had gone quiet in the way that quiet means *thickening*, the last sleigh came up the drive.

* * *

Mara did not go out for every arrival. He was not the doorman; he was the driver, the reader of letters, the eraser of footprints, and he had long ago taught himself to appear only where appearing was useful. But Sunday afternoon he was in the yard anyway, scraping the harness buckles free of rust with a wire brush, when the sleigh passed him—single horse, rented from the livery in town, driven by a man who sat forward slightly and held the reins with the grip of someone who had learned vehicles as machines rather than animals.

Mara knew the coat before he knew anything else. A dark traveling coat, seven years out of fashion, mended at the left cuff with thread a shade too pale—the mend of a man who owned a needle but no matching thread. He had seen that cuff across a table in a railway station restaurant in peacetime, seen the hands above it folded around a cup of coffee going cold, seen them unfold to write an address on the back of a timetable, in pencil, in a hand that slanted leftward as if leaning out of the wind.

The sleigh stopped. The man stepped down into the yard and looked at the house, and then at the coach house, and then—at Mara.

There is a way of not knowing someone that takes longer than knowing them. Anton Reval performed it beautifully: the polite blankness, the nod one gives a servant whose face is a servant's face, generic, interchangeable, furniture. His eyes passed over Mara the way a lamp passes over a wall. Not one flicker. Two years, and letters in Mara's coat lining, and a promise made over cold coffee in a station that smelled of coal and oranges—and not one flicker.

Good, Mara thought, with his heart going like a bird in a closed room. Good. Better than good. He bent back to his buckles.

The stranger gave his name at the door as Herr Anders, of Stockholm. Mara heard it secondhand, from Mrs. Hearn, who disapproved of foreigners on principle and Swedes vaguely, on suspicion they were not really anything. Herr Anders was an engineer, she reported, surveying work, caught out by the bridge closure, would pay for his keep until spring. He had one valise. He spoke English with an accent that placed itself nowhere, that slid off the map.

"He watches everything," Mrs. Hearn added darkly, meaning it as condemnation, and Mara, drying the supper dishes in his corner of the kitchen, thought: yes. That is exactly what he does. He learned it somewhere it mattered.

* * *

Dinner that first Sunday assembled the whole stranded party at one table, and Mara served it, and serving a table is the finest school of observation ever devised, because no one guards themselves against a man carrying plates.

He watched Fennick place himself at Edith's right with the ease of a man sitting down in his own courtroom, and ask questions that sounded like weather: how large was the household, how far did the estate run, did Mrs. Calloway entertain much in winter, did she correspond widely?—that last slipped in so smoothly between the soup and the fish that only someone who read letters for a living would have caught it. Edith caught it. Mara saw her catch it, saw the fractional brightening, the angler's stillness, and then she answered at length and charmingly about her sister in the south of France and said nothing true at all.

He watched Crane watch Lilian, not with appetite but with professional assessment—the way one watches a nervous person near a nervous animal—and decided the journalist had already marked the niece as the weak board in the fence. He watched Lilian herself push food around her plate and startle when the logs settled in the hearth, and he felt for her something between pity and kinship. Fear of noise. He remembered it from the inside.

And he watched Herr Anders, seated at the far end between Crane and the empty chair kept for form's sake, eat very little, listen very much, and offer, when pressed, a smooth little biography like a stone worn round: engineering work, contracts here and there, the north recommended for its quiet. Crane bit at it, asked about bridges—"they say you people build bridges better than anyone"—and Anders smiled a small, tired smile and said that bridges were mostly a matter of respecting what you were building across. Water is patient, he said. It agrees to carry your weight for exactly as long as it agrees.

"That's almost poetry," said Crane, writing it down.

"It's almost engineering," said Anders, and looked, briefly, directly down the length of the table at Mara—who chose that instant to refill Crane's glass—and then away.

Only once more did the mask slip, and it slipped toward Mara deliberately, which was worse. Passing the bread, Mara found his sleeve caught—not held, barely touched—by Anders' fingertips, a gesture invisible above the tablecloth, and the man murmured, in the accent that belonged to no country: "Forgive me. The salt?"

"The salt is at your elbow, sir," Mara said, in the flat servant's voice he kept oiled for exactly such moments, and moved on, and did not let his hands shake until he was in the pantry with the door shut and the trays safe on the shelf.

So. He knew. Of course he knew; Mara had recognized the coat, and recognition is symmetrical. The question—the whole winter's question, folding itself into the arithmetic now, multiplying columns—was why a man who did not know him would come here, to the one house on the river road most likely to be watched, most likely to be filled with precisely the sort of guests now buttering their bread around the table behind him. And why a man who did know him would pretend, so perfectly, so professionally, not to.

Unless pretending was the skill. Unless the wrong name and the blank eyes and the biography worn smooth were not evasion but craft, and Anton Reval had come to the edge of a closing border the way water comes to a bridge pier: because it was the way across, and water is patient.

* * *

Later, the house banked and sleeping, Mara stood in the coach house with the lantern hooded and took from beneath the false bottom of his trunk the oilcloth packet he had not opened in two years. Nine letters. The string around them still bore the knot he had tied in the railway station while Anton Reval watched with those grey, unhurried eyes and said, *You will not read them. You will not lose them. And if I cannot send word, you will know where they go.*

*I will know,* Mara had said, in peacetime, meaning nothing by it except that he wished to matter to someone, once, even anonymously, even as a hinge and not a hand.

He weighed the packet now in his palm. Above him the frost ticked in the rafters. Down the valley the river lay silent under its thickening lid, patient as its own reputation, agreeing—for now—to carry the world.

In the house, in the blue room facing east, a magistrate slept—or did not—above a dispatch case that smelled of ink and chemicals. At the end of the hall, a journalist's typewriter sat cased like an instrument of surgery. And two doors from the servants' stair, a man named Anders slept with one valise that Mara would have given a great deal to weigh.

Nine letters. One promise. One wrong name under his own roof, registered in his mistress's book in a hand that leaned out of the wind.

Mara put the packet back, unopened, and pinched out the lantern, and stood a moment in the dark doing the new arithmetic—the magistrate, the journalist, the niece, the frozen road, the man who did not know him—and found that the sum no longer balanced, that something had shifted on the far side of the equation, something heavy and patient and approaching, that would not wait for spring.
