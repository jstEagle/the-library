## 5. Enemy by Declaration

The telegram came on a Tuesday, in the blue hour before breakfast, when frost had climbed the inside of the windows and the whole house held its breath against the cold.

Mara saw the boy first—the telegraph office's runner, red-nosed, stamping his boots on the front step while the ice on his cap melted in dark beads onto the stone. Mara was coming across the courtyard with a can of oil for the hinges of the stable doors, and he calculated, as he always calculated, the distance between himself and any piece of paper that mattered. The boy held the envelope out to him. Mara did not take it.

"Mrs. Calloway takes her tea at half past," he said. "Give it to me and I'll see she has it with her toast."

It was a small lie, seamless as all his lies. He would give it to her with her toast. But first it would pass through his hands, and everything that passed through his hands passed through his eyes.

He waited until the boy had gone, until the courtyard was empty except for the horses stamping in their stalls and the crows arguing over something in the bare elms. Then he stood in the lee of the coach house, where no window of the main house could find him, and he turned the envelope over. The wax seal had been pressed carelessly; the flap lifted without violence. It was not quite steaming a letter open when the glue surrenders on its own.

The message was eleven words long, and he read them four times.

*REVAL — SUBJECT OF UNFRIENDLY POWER — STATUS UNDER REVIEW — AWAIT INSTRUCTIONS.*

No sender. No courtesy. The telegraph clerk at the county seat had a heavy hand and the magistrate—Mara knew without being told whose instructions these were—had a heavier one.

*Unfriendly power.* Four months ago Anton Reval had been merely foreign, a curiosity at dinner, a man who could explain the mathematics of suspension bridges to Edith's guests and make even the magistrate nod grudgingly. Three weeks ago the river bridge had closed, chained end to end, and the house had filled with people who could not leave and could not be sent away. Now, by the alchemy of a wire and a winter, Anton had become something else entirely. Not a guest. A category.

Mara folded the telegram along its original creases, pressed the flap down with his thumb until the wax took again, and carried it into the house.

* * *

Edith read it while the maid poured her chocolate, and her face did nothing at all. That was the thing about Edith Calloway that strangers mistook for coldness: she received information the way stone receives rain. What she did with it afterward happened underground, invisibly, over years if necessary.

"So." She set the paper beside her plate, square with the table's edge. "Mr. Prosser has begun his collection."

"He'll want a formal interview," Mara said. "Names. Who vouches for whom."

"He'll want me to withdraw my hospitality from a man under my own roof, in front of my own guests, so that he may report my obedience to his superiors." She broke her toast with precise fingers. "I have been threatened with worse by better men, Winterbourne. Sit down. You make the room nervous, standing like that."

He sat. He always sat where she pointed, in the chair that kept the door in his sightline, because even after two years she had not noticed—or had never remarked—that he chose seats the way soldiers choose walls.

"What will you do?"

"What I do with every obstacle between here and the ballot box. I will make it expensive to oppose me." She sipped her chocolate. "Mr. Reval is my guest until spring or until he gives me reason otherwise. A telegram is not a reason. A telegram is a man in London coughing into a machine." Her eyes came up, grey and level. "You drove him from the station yourself, I believe. When he arrived."

"Yes."

"Do you know him?"

The question hung there, and Mara felt the old reflex rise in him like water finding its level: *deny, deflect, diminish*. *I know what every servant knows, ma'am—he tips well and eats little.*

"No," he said.

Edith looked at him for exactly one second longer than comfort allowed. Then she gathered her papers and rose, and at the door she paused.

"When Prosser asks you questions—and he will ask you questions, Winterbourne, you're the one who sees everything in this house—I want you to remember that you are paid to answer to me and no one else. Whatever else you decide to tell him, decide it first, and tell me after."

She went out. The door closed with the soft click of good joinery, and Mara sat alone with the cooling chocolate and understood that he had just been given both an order and a warning, wrapped in the same silk.

* * *

The magistrate began his files that same afternoon, and he made a small ceremony of it.

Mara carried Mr. Prosser's writing desk up from the morning room to the east bedroom—the magistrate had claimed the room with the best view of the river road, ostensibly for the light, actually for the sightlines—and while he arranged the blotter and inkstand to Prosser's satisfaction, he watched the man open a new ledger, rule its pages with a straightedge, and write at the head of the first sheet, in a hand like fence posts: *HOUSEHOLD OF MRS. E. CALLOWAY — PERSONS PRESENT.*

"You've a memory for faces, I'd imagine," Prosser said pleasantly, not looking up. "A man in your position must."

"A man in my position forgets faces, sir. It's healthier."

Prosser laughed—a short, satisfied bark—and wrote: *Winterbourne, M., chauffeur.* Then, beneath it, in smaller letters that Mara read upside down with the ease of long practice: *observant. careful. worth cultivating.*

"I wonder," Prosser said, laying down the pen and folding his hands, "whether you might drive me into town tomorrow. The roads are treacherous alone this time of year, and I'm told your motor handles them beautifully."

"It does, sir. Nine o'clock suit you?"

"Nine o'clock." Prosser smiled, and his smile had the quality of a door opening onto stairs going down. "There are things a man like you notices, Winterbourne. Comings and goings. Letters. I find that such observations, properly organized, can be worth—" he named a sum that amounted to three months of Mara's wages, said lightly, as if remarking on the weather, "—to the right man."

"I'll keep it in mind, sir."

"Do." Prosser returned to his ledger. *Reval, A., engineer,* Mara read, upside down, as he let himself out. Beneath the name, already, a second line: *see file.*

*See file.* As if a file existed. As if the paper were already breeding paper somewhere in a county office, multiplying in the dark like mould.

That night Mara lay awake in the loft above the coach house listening to the wind work at the roof slates, and he performed the arithmetic he had been avoiding since the telegram arrived.

To acknowledge knowing Anton Reval was to enter the magistrate's ledger himself—not as a line under *household*, but as a thread connecting the name to the name, and threads, once entered into ledgers, could be pulled. To deny knowing him was safer, cleaner, and a lie that would need feeding daily for the rest of a winter spent twenty feet from the man's voice.

And beneath both choices, patient as sediment, lay the older thing. Two years old now. A railway platform in the last warm week of another September, and a tall foreigner with ink-stained fingers pressing a bundle of letters into his hands, saying, *You strike me as a man who does what he says he will do,* and Mara—who had been no one for eleven years by then, who had worn his anonymity so long it had grown through him like a root through a wall—hearing himself answer, *I do.*

Duty, he thought, staring at the rafters, is a knot. You tie it in peacetime when the rope is slack and your hands are easy. And then the weather changes, and someone starts hauling on the other end, and you discover how much of you is caught in it.

* * *

Anton found him, which was itself a kind of answer.

Mara was in the coach house after midnight, running his hands along the cold flanks of the motor as if checking a sick animal, though there was nothing wrong with it that oil and attention would not cure. It was the only room in his life he trusted. Every bolt in it had a purpose; every purpose could be served with his hands. The lamp hung low on its hook, throwing his shadow huge and crooked across the timber wall.

The door opened without a knock. Anton Reval stood in the frame with snow in his pale hair and his coat collar up, and behind him the courtyard lay blue and silent under a sky scoured clean of clouds by the cold.

"I watched your light," Anton said. "I thought—if it was still burning at this hour, you wanted company. If it had gone out, I would have gone back to bed."

"And if I hadn't wanted either?"

"Then you should learn to lock your doors." Anton came in and shut the cold behind him. Up close he smelled of woodsmoke and the particular chill of someone who has walked slowly across a frozen yard, taking his time, being certain. He looked older than his thirty-odd years in the lamplight, or perhaps only more legible—the fine seams around his eyes, the scar through one eyebrow that Mara had never asked about. "Your magistrate made a list today. I am told I am on it."

"You were told."

"The niece talks when she is frightened, and the journalist talks when he is paid, and between them this house has no walls left." Anton lowered himself onto the mounting block, elbows on knees, hands hanging. "So. Before he asks you the question, I am asking it first. Do you know me, Winterbourne?"

Mara set down the rag he had been holding. He wiped his hands slowly, thoroughly, buying the seconds he did not need—he had known this moment would come since September, since the platform, since the word *promise*—but ritual buys time even from certainty.

"I know you," he said.

Anton closed his eyes briefly, and the relief in his face was so naked, so unguarded, that Mara had to look away. This was the danger of men like Anton: they wore their hearts where a working man could see them, and seeing was halfway to carrying.

"Two years," Anton said quietly. "Two years, and you never came."

"I came twice." The words came out harder than he intended. "Twice I stood in the street in Velmory with your letters in my coat, and twice the frontier post had been moved since the last map, and the third time the border itself shut like a door. I wrote to the address on the envelopes. Twice the letters came back. Once they didn't come back at all, and I have spent two years wondering whether that meant they reached her or whether some customs officer is wearing your handwriting as a souvenir."

The silence stretched. Somewhere overhead a mouse moved along a joist, quick and light as a dropped stitch.

"They reached her," Anton said at last. His voice had gone rough. "The first ones. Her sister wrote to me in the spring—through Switzerland, through three pairs of hands—to say that Milena had read them aloud by the stove, all of them, twice through, and laughed at the parts about English cooking." A breath. "Milena. My wife."

Wife. The word landed in Mara's chest and settled there, heavy, displacing something he had not known he was carrying. For two years the letters had lived in a tin box beneath the coach house floor as *the letters*, abstract as scripture. Now they had a woman's name, a stove, laughter.

"She is why you came back," Mara said. "Why now, why here, why under a false name into a country that is about to decide it hates yours."

"Because the border is closing. Properly closing, not the closing of officials' moods—closing in law, with garrisons and timetables. There is a bill before the Reichstag; everyone on my side of the line can feel it the way animals feel weather. In six months there will be no letters across it at all, no wires, no travellers, nothing but guns." Anton looked up, and the lamp flame doubled in his eyes. "I could not spend the rest of my life on the wrong side of a wall from my wife because I was waiting for the paperwork to be polite. So I came to the last open crossing within reach of the rail lines, and I came to you, because you are the only man on this side of it who ever kept his word to me."

"The crossing is chained," Mara said. "The bridge is closed for repairs. There is no way over the river until April except the ferryman, and the ferryman reports to the county, and the county reports to your new friend with the ledger."

"I know what is closed."

"Then you know that what you're asking—"

"I am not asking yet." Anton rose from the block, and standing, he was taller than Mara remembered, thinner, the engineering in him visible now as architecture rather than flesh. "Tonight I am only telling you the truth, because you gave me yours when you said you know me. Milena is on the other side of that river, forty miles as the crow flies, in her mother's house in Ostrow, and every month that passes the wall between us grows another course of bricks. I did not cross an ocean to be stranded in a drawing room discussing votes for women with a magistrate who files me like an invoice." His voice cracked, just barely, on the last word, and he mastered it. "Forgive her house. She is a great woman. But it is not my destination, Winterbourne. It is my waiting room."

Outside, the wind shifted and came around to the north, and with it came a sound that neither of them had heard yet that winter—the river ice, out in the dark, settling its length with a groan like a ship's timber. They both turned toward the sound. Neither spoke.

When Mara finally did speak, he spoke carefully, because he understood he was choosing.

"The magistrate will interview the household. Formally. Under oath, if he can manage it, though he hasn't the authority and will pretend he has. He'll ask each of us whether we knew you before you arrived." Mara met Anton's eyes. "If he asks me, I will say no. I will say I drove you from the station as I drive every guest, that you tipped me a crown and spoke to me of the weather. And then whatever happens afterward—whatever you plan, whenever you plan it—you will tell me first. Not because I own your secret. Because I carried your letters for two years without reading them, and I am owed the ending of the story."

Anton studied him for a long moment, and something moved across his face that was almost pain—the look of a man being offered help he had resolved never to need.

"You never read them," he said. "Not one?"

"Not one."

"Why? They were only letters. No one would have known."

"That," said Mara, turning down the lamp, "is precisely why no one else could carry them."

They stood a moment longer in the dying light, two men bound by paper and promises in a freezing coach house while the river spoke to itself in the dark. Then Anton went out, and Mara heard his footsteps cross the crusted snow—slow, measured, a man rehearsing innocence—and fade toward the house.

Mara knelt, pried up the board, and drew out the tin box. It was heavier than memory. He set it on the mounting block in the dark and rested his hand flat on the cold lid, and through the wall of his chest he could feel his own heart keeping its stubborn, ordinary time.

Eleven words on a wire. A name in a ledger marked *see file*. A wife named Milena laughing by a stove.

The knot was pulling tight, and spring was a rumour, and the river had begun to talk in its sleep.
