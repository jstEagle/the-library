## 6. What Edith Knows

Edith Calloway had been reading her own mail for thirty years, and for the last two of them she had been almost certain that someone else was reading it first.

She did not mind. That was the part no one would believe if she ever confessed it, and so she never confessed it—not to her sister in Bath, not to the treasurer of the movement, not to God, though she supposed God already knew and had likely known longer than she had. She did not mind because the letters arrived each morning sealed and left her desk each afternoon answered, and in between they passed through the hands of a young man who was quiet as furniture and thorough as winter, and competence, in Edith's long experience of the world, was rarer than honesty and considerably more useful.

The steam, at least, he could have been subtler about. There was a particular softness to an envelope that had been opened and resealed—a slackness at the flap, a faint wave in the paper where the fibers had drunk moisture and dried again—and Mara Winterbourne's resealed envelopes waved like flags. He might have used a knife and a thin blade of whalebone, the way she had done in 1889 when she'd wanted to know what her husband's solicitor was saying about her behind the courtesy of his silence. But no. He steamed them, probably over the kettle in the coach house, probably while the horses watched with their liquid, incurious eyes, and then he laid the envelopes under a weight and went about his day, and by the time the post reached her desk every flap sat down neatly, obediently, hiding its small history of deception.

She tested him once, early on. A letter from a woman in Manchester—harmless, but written in a hand Edith had disguised herself, describing a meeting place and a password for the spring campaign. If Mara read the mail, he would know things he had no honest way of knowing. Two weeks later, driving her through the market town, he had taken the long way round without being asked, avoiding the square where constables had taken to loitering near the stationer's shop. When she raised an eyebrow at the detour, he said only, "Roadwork," in that flat, careful voice of his, and looked straight ahead at the gray road, and Edith had laughed inside herself for a week.

So: he read her mail. He knew her secrets, or enough of them. And she kept him anyway, which told anyone paying attention everything they needed to know about Edith Calloway's theology of trust. She trusted patterns, not persons. And Winterbourne's pattern was flawless: he stole nothing, sold nothing, repeated nothing. He simply *knew*, and knowing, arranged the world quietly around her safety like a man straightening a picture frame on his way out of a room.

The only thing she could not determine was what he wanted. Every spy wants something. Money, most of them; a few wanted meaning, and those were the dangerous ones. Winterbourne took his wages without negotiation, spent nothing visible, owned three suits of clothes and a pocket watch that lost four minutes a day and was always corrected. He had no vices she could find and no pleasures either, unless you counted the way he sometimes stood in the stable yard at dusk looking at the hills as if the hills owed him something. A man with no appetite was a man with no handle, and Edith disliked handling people she could not grip.

*He'll do,* she had told herself two winters ago, watching him memorize a guest list in a single pass of his pale eyes. *And when he doesn't do anymore, I'll know before he does.*

That was thirty years of practice talking. Thirty years of reading men's letters and women's faces and the small print of a country that kept assuring her, in its roundabout polite way, that she did not exist except as somebody's wife, somebody's mother, somebody's problem. You learned things, reading other people's correspondence. You learned that everyone lied, mostly by omission, mostly kindly, mostly about love and money—the only two subjects worth lying about. You learned that handwriting sagged when the heart did. You learned that the truth arrived in envelopes even when nobody wanted it, because the truth was patient and postage was cheap.

And lately, in the third week of November, with the bridge chained and the river running high and cold and the house filling up with stranded strangers like a lifeboat taking on water, you learned that the magistrate Mr. Harlbeck wrote to his clerk twice a week, and that his letters came to the house postmarked from the county seat, and that Mara steamed those too—she could tell, she could always tell—and that whatever he found in them made him drive the car an inch more carefully, stand an inch more straightly, disappear an inch more completely into the woodwork of her household.

Edith noticed everything. It was the whole of her method.

* * *

Harlbeck arrived at the breakfast table on a Tuesday wearing the expression of a man who believes patience is a form of authority. He was a broad, pink, careful man, with hands like a butcher who specialized in fine work, and he ate his kippers with a methodical pleasure that Edith found obscene given the company he was keeping.

"The roads," he said, "are going to be a trial this winter."

"Everything is a trial, Mr. Harlbeck. That is rather its nature." She buttered her toast in small, exact strokes. Across the table her niece Clemency was pretending to read a novel while listening so hard her ears practically rang; at the far end the journalist, Mr. Pell, was doing his own imitation of disinterest, which involved writing nothing in his notebook while holding the pen ready, like a man at a starting line.

"Still," Harlbeck said pleasantly, "it gives us all the gift of time. Time to get acquainted." His eyes traveled the room with the unhurried entitlement of a customs officer. They rested, briefly, on the window where beyond the glass the grounds fell away toward the iron-gray river. Then—they were quick, but Edith was quicker—they rested on the doorway where Winterbourne stood with the post tray, silent as a coat on a hook.

"Your driver," Harlbeck said. "Winterbourne. How long has he been with you?"

"Two years." She let the pause sit exactly as long as it needed to. "Why?"

"No reason. Capable-looking fellow. One wonders where capable fellows come from." He smiled at his kippers.

"From nowhere, usually," Edith said. "It's the great advantage of them. Now—Mr. Reval. You've been very attentive to our engineer. I hope you're not making him feel unwelcome. He's come a long way from home."

"Hasn't he just," Harlbeck said, and something moved behind his face like a fish under ice. "Home is rather the question these days, isn't it, Mrs. Calloway? With certain nations falling out of favor. A telegram came through yesterday—you'll have heard, news travels even across a frozen river—that puts his country on the unfriendly list. Officially. There will be more names on it before spring, I shouldn't wonder." He dabbed his mouth. "A man's papers can say one thing and a man's heart another. My job is to take an interest when the two don't match."

"How exhausting for you," Edith said, "spending a whole winter among hearts."

She saw Pell's pen twitch. She saw Clemency's novel lower half an inch. And she saw, in the doorway, the tray in Winterbourne's hands tilt by a degree no instrument could measure and every keeper of secrets could read.

Later, in the hall, she waylaid him beside the hat stand.

"You'll drive me to the village this afternoon," she said. "I have letters for the post. Real ones," she added, watching him, "that I intend to write myself and seal myself, and I'll carry them in my own bag, so you needn't trouble yourself with their contents."

Something crossed his face—relief? disappointment? She had never decided which she preferred to provoke.

"As you wish, ma'am."

"And Winterbourne." She adjusted her gloves, looking anywhere but at him, which was how one addressed such matters. "Mr. Harlbeck is building a file. On Reval, to begin with. Magistrates are like squirrels; they gather for the pleasure of it and forget where they've buried things, but the gathering is real. Whatever you know about our guest—and I suspect you know a good deal more than his name—I advise you to consider whether knowing it is safe, or merely interesting."

He was quiet a moment. The clock on the mantel ticked with the smugness of a well-regulated life.

"And you, Mrs. Calloway?" he said. "Will you consider it?"

"I have considered everything I know for thirty years," Edith said. "It has kept me out of prison, which is more than most knowledge manages." She pulled the door open onto the cold. "Half past three. Wear your muffler."

* * *

The village was nine miles off through bare country, the hedges black and dripping, the fields the color of old dishwater. Edith rode in silence for the first four miles, composing letters in her head, and Winterbourne drove the way he did everything—economically, invisibly, the great machine of the car seeming less driven than persuaded.

At the five-mile mark she spoke, eyes on the hills.

"I was married in 1878," she said. "Did you know that?"

"You'll have mentioned it, ma'am."

"My husband read my letters for eleven years before I caught him at it. He was clumsy about it—flaps glued with too much care, that sort of thing—and when I confronted him he wept and said it was to protect me, which was true, and also because he was frightened of me, which was truer." The car jolted over a rut. "Do you know what I did?"

"Ma'am?"

"I began reading his back. And I was better at it, and I learned more, and within a year I knew his finances, his mistress, and his conscience better than he did. We lived together twenty more years and got along splendidly, once we'd established the terms." She turned to look at him then, directly, letting him have the full weight of the gray eyes that had unmanned bishops and shaken Home Secretaries. "The terms were: we keep each other's secrets, and neither of us pretends otherwise. It was the happiest arrangement either of us ever managed, and I have often thought it a shame the word for it doesn't exist."

Winterbourne's jaw worked once. The road unrolled ahead of them, white at the edges with the first serious frost.

"Mrs. Calloway—"

"Don't," she said, almost gently. "Don't confess and don't deny. Both would be beneath us." She faced forward again. "I am not telling you this because I've caught you. I'm telling you because there's a magistrate in my breakfast room filing away the fact that a foreigner sleeps under my roof, and when men like Harlbeck build files, they eventually need a servant to testify. Servants testify beautifully. Juries adore them. And I want you to understand, before that day comes if it comes, exactly where I will be standing."

He drove for a quarter mile without answering. When he did speak, his voice was as flat and careful as ever, but she heard the seam in it, the hairline fracture where something underneath pressed.

"Why?" he said. "You don't know anything about me. I could be anyone."

"Yes," Edith agreed, watching the hills. "Isn't it restful, finding someone who is genuinely no one? One meets so few of them." She opened her bag and took out one letter—only one, addressed in her own upright hand—and held it out to him. "Post this yourself, at the window, and watch the clerk cancel it. Don't give it to the box."

He took it. Their fingers didn't touch; he was too practiced for that, and so was she.

"Who's it to?" he asked, and then, hearing himself, stopped.

But Edith smiled, small and dry as a pressed flower. "Now you see the difficulty," she said. "Once you start asking, you'll want to know everything, and then you'll be like me, and heaven knows one of us is quite enough."

* * *

That evening the house gathered itself around the fire in the drawing room, as stranded houses do, and Edith conducted her evening like a chess player conducting an endgame she had studied from both sides.

Clemency at the lamp, turning pages without reading them, glancing at Anton Reval whenever she believed no one observed—which was often, poor child, and obviously, which was why Edith had already spoken to Cook about chaperoning the girl's errands. Pell by the fire, performing boredom, harvesting sentences. Harlbeck with his port, spreading his bulk and his patience across the room like weather.

And Reval at the window, ostensibly studying the darkening lawn, actually studying nothing, being a man who had learned in some harder school than Edith's how to make himself a pane of glass. She watched Harlbeck watch him. She watched the magistrate's gaze do its slow arithmetic—the foreign name, the telegram, the wrong-shaped vowels—and file it all away in that pink skull with the tidy efficiency of a man stacking coins.

Reval, to his credit, gave him nothing. But he gave the room nothing either, and innocence is usually louder than that. Innocent men complain about the cold and ask after the shooting and grow tiresome. Guilty men, or hunted ones, become windows.

When the port went round a second time, Edith set down her sewing.

"Mr. Reval," she said, "you've been a fortnight in my house and I still don't know what brought you here in the dead of the season. Bridge engineers are not, in my experience, sentimental travelers."

The room tilted toward him. She felt Pell's pen go ready, felt Harlbeck's attention sharpen into something with edges, felt Clemency hold her breath over her unread page.

"Work, Mrs. Calloway," Reval said, in his accented, careful English. "There is work where there are rivers."

"There are rivers everywhere," Edith said pleasantly. "Including, I'm told, in countries that are presently falling out of favor. How inconvenient for a man to have his rivers taken from him by a telegram."

For one instant—one clean instant before the shutters came down—something moved in Reval's face that was not fear and not guilt but grief, old and specific and unbearably private, and Edith thought: *Ah. Not politics at all, or not only. A woman. It is nearly always a woman or a grave, and frequently both.*

"It is inconvenient," Reval said evenly. "But borders change their minds more often than rivers do. I have learned to wait."

"Well," said Edith, returning to her sewing, "you may wait here as comfortably as anywhere. This house has stood through worse than telegrams."

Harlbeck laughed, a courteous bark, and turned the conversation to drainage rates, and the evening resumed its shape. But as the guests drifted up to bed, Edith remained in her chair by the dying fire, needle idle in her hand, and permitted herself the luxury of thinking three moves ahead.

Here was the board as she read it: a magistrate collecting a case the way a magpie collects silver; a journalist circling for copy; a foreigner with a grieving face and a driver who resealed envelopes; and somewhere in the middle of it all, herself—an old woman with a movement to protect and a house full of witnesses, every one of whom could ruin her with a sentence honestly meant.

Any ordinary woman would have cut the tangled thread. Turned Reval out, dismissed Winterbourne, closed the house, gone to Bath until spring washed the whole business away. Edith had spent thirty years watching ordinary women survive by cutting threads, and she had buried too many of them—buried their courage in drawers and their convictions in obituaries, and attended the funerals of their causes in respectable black.

No. She would not cut the thread. She would follow it.

Because here was the thing she had not said aloud in thirty years, the thing she kept folded beneath the suffrage pamphlets and the accounts and the armor of her formidable name: Edith Calloway had spent her entire life being told that her secrets were crimes—that meetings were conspiracies, that pamphlets were sedition, that a woman organizing other women was a danger to the realm. She knew precisely what it was to have one's ordinary decency reclassified as treason by men with files. And she would be damned, publicly and spectacularly damned, before she handed a stranger's secret to a magistrate merely because the times had dressed it in a uniform.

Besides—and this she allowed herself, alone, as the fire settled into embers—it was not only principle. It was curiosity, the oldest engine she owned. A man who reads her mail. A foreigner who grieves at windows. A promise moving through her house like a current under ice, invisible, insistent, pulling everything slowly toward the river.

She rose, snuffed the candle, and stood a moment in the dark hall, listening to the old house tick and settle around its sleepers. Somewhere above her, Harlbeck was writing in his file. Somewhere across the yard, in the coach house, Winterbourne was doing whatever Winterbourne did with the nights—she had never asked, and had always imagined steam rising off a kettle, and a young man reading other lives by its light because his own was unbearable to sit inside.

Tomorrow, she decided, she would begin leaving certain letters unsealed.

If he was going to read her mail regardless, the least she could do was choose what he found.

She climbed the stairs slowly, one hand on the rail, and did not see—no one saw—the light burning late in the coach house, nor the figure at its window, nor the bundle of letters wrapped in oilcloth that the young man drew from beneath the floorboards and held for a long moment in both hands, as a man holds a thing that is either a duty or a detonator, and cannot yet tell which.
