## 7. Letters Under the Floor

The coach house smelled of leather and cold iron and, beneath both, of the river itself—a damp mineral breath that came up through the floorboards as though the water two hundred yards away were trying to reach him. Mara Winterbourne had learned to read that smell the way other men read weather. When it sharpened, frost was coming. When it went sweet and rotten, the thaw.

Tonight it was sharp. The first hard freeze in a week had made the ground ring like a kettle under his boots, and he had crossed the yard with his collar up and his lantern shuttered to a coin of light, because even after two years of honest work there lived in him the old habit of not being seen going anywhere.

The letters were under the third floorboard from the north wall, beneath the stall where Edith Calloway's carriage mare had once foundered and been put down before Mara's time. He had chosen the spot for its sentiment—no one mourned a dead horse enough to kneel at it—and for its practicality: the board sat over a cavity where the builders had bridged a stone, leaving a hand's width of darkness between floor and earth. He had wrapped the bundle in oilcloth against the damp and tied it with cord cut from a broken rein, so that if anyone ever found it they would think only of tack, of harness, of the ordinary machinery of a working yard.

He knelt now, worked the blade of a chisel into the seam, and lifted.

The board came up with a sound like a knuckle cracking. Cold air rose out of the cavity, carrying the smell of old earth, and Mara reached in and felt for the bundle by touch alone, though he knew exactly where it lay. There is a kind of knowledge that the hand insists on confirming even when the mind is certain—the way a man will touch his pocket twice to feel for a ticket he has already checked.

It was heavier than he remembered. Or perhaps his hands had grown lighter.

* * *

He carried it to the workbench at the far end of the coach house, where the lantern could stand behind the grain-sack curtain and throw no light into the yard. Two years ago, on a night in September that still visited him more often than he liked, he had sworn to a stranger with ink-stained fingers that he would carry these letters across a border and deliver them to a woman whose name Anton Reval would not say aloud, only spell, letter by letter, in the dust of the road with the handle of his whip.

*I cannot take them myself,* Anton had said, in that accented English that turned every *w* into something softer, almost an *o*. *They would open my case at the crossing. They know me there. But you—* he had looked at Mara then with an attention that felt like being handled, like being picked up and weighed— *you are nobody. Forgive me. I mean it as a blessing. Nobody passes.*

Mara had taken the letters because nobody had passed them by, all his life, without taking something. It seemed right that at last somebody should pass through him carrying something worth the trouble.

Then peacetime had ended—not with thunder but with paperwork, the way most things ended—and the border had thickened like ice forming, and the letters had gone under the floorboard instead of over the river, and Mara had told himself every winter since that spring would be different. Spring always told good lies.

Now he set the bundle on the bench and untied the rein-cord with fingers that did not entirely belong to him. Seven envelopes, tied inside the oilcloth in a second wrapping of brown paper. Anton's hand was upright and patient, an engineer's hand; the addresses were written twice, once in Latin characters and once in another script that curled on itself like smoke. The topmost envelope bore a postmark from a town whose name Mara had practiced saying in front of a mirror until he could produce it without stumbling, in case he ever needed to ask the way.

In two years he had never opened them. Not one.

This required explanation, and he had given it often enough to himself at three in the morning: they were not his. Reading them would be theft of a kind no court recognized but his own conscience prosecuted rigorously—he had, after all, spent two years steaming open Edith Calloway's correspondence in this very building and had come to understand precisely what kind of thief that made him. There was a difference, he told himself, between reading letters to guard a woman's cause and reading letters to satisfy a hunger. He believed this distinction the way a man believes in the honesty of his own left hand.

But the magistrate had begun keeping files. The telegram had arrived three days ago—*relations with the government of—* and then the name, struck through in Edith's copy as if even the paper could be made discreet—*now officially unfriendly,* and the word *enemy* had entered the house like a draught, finding every gap. And Anton Reval was in that house, registered under a name that fit him the way a borrowed coat fits, sitting at dinner each evening six places from Mara, never once meeting his eye, performing the stranger so convincingly that Mara had begun to wonder whether the promise itself might have been performed too—whether he had invented, out of loneliness, the whole business of the letters and the woman and the border.

The bundle proved he had not invented it. But proof was not the same as certainty, and certainty was not the same as knowing what the letters said, and not knowing had become, in the past week, a danger. If the magistrate searched—if he came with his courtesy and his notebook and turned over whatever he found—Mara needed to know whether he was guarding seven harmless love letters or seven fuses.

That was the argument he made to himself as he stood the razor-blade over the flame of the lantern and watched it heat. That it was duty. That ignorance, in wartime, was a luxury.

The blade slid under the flap of the first envelope with the small tearing whisper he knew so well from the other side of his trade. Steam would have been gentler; he had no patience for gentleness tonight. The flap tore slightly at the corner. He would seal it again later with flour paste and press it under the flatiron, and Anton would never see, or would see and pretend not to, which amounted to the same arrangement they had maintained at the dinner table for a fortnight.

He unfolded the single sheet and held it to the light.

* * *

It was not a love letter.

Or rather—it was, in the way that a foundation is a house. The writing was addressed to *My dear Mother,* but everything in it bent around a second presence, the way iron filings bend around a magnet. *Anna asks after you in every letter. She is thinner; she laughs less; but she walked to the churchyard with me on Sunday and stood very straight.* And lower: *She keeps your last letter under her pillow. She says the doctor is an optimist, and she has decided to forgive him for it.*

Mara read it twice. The second time he read it slowly, because the word *doctor* had arrived in his chest like a swallowed stone.

The woman was ill. Had been ill, two years ago, when this letter was written—when it was written and sealed and handed across a fence to a passing groom who swore he would carry it, and who had instead buried it under a dead horse's floor while the sender waited across the water for news that never came, because the letters went nowhere, because Mara had kept them like a dragon keeping gold, telling himself that spring—

He set the page face-down on the bench and pressed his palms flat against the wood until the trembling stopped.

There were six more.

* * *

He read them in order, because order mattered now; he had lost two years of order and would not lose the shape of what remained. The lantern burned low and he fed it oil without looking away from the pages. The coach house ticked and settled around him. Somewhere in the walls a mouse ran its nightly circuit, punctual as a clock.

The second letter was mostly about bridges. Anna, it seemed, had married him across a table in a registrar's office with two witnesses borrowed off the street, and had done it while her family looked on in the particular silence that families keep when they believe a mistake is being made. Her father had not spoken to Anton since. The letter explained, gently, patiently, to a mother-in-law he had never met, why her daughter's choice had been reasonable—as if reason were the currency in which such choices were judged—and described, in loving technical detail, the cantilever bridge he was then building across a gorge in the mountains, with figures and load calculations sketched in the margins. Anna had drawn a small flower beside the figures. The flower was better drawn than the bridge.

The third letter mentioned a summons. A ministry, a commission, papers with seals. *They have asked me to design works I will not describe in a letter, and I have asked them to find another engineer, and we are both displeased with each other.* The tone was light. The margins were not: here Anna's flower appeared again, but pressed flat, as if someone had closed the book on it.

The fourth letter was written in a shaking hand, and it was not Anton's.

*My dear mother-in-law whom I have never seen,* it began, and Mara understood that he was holding the voice of a woman he had imagined for two years without once imagining her correctly. She wrote the way some people walk in high wind—leaning into it, deliberate. *I am writing this because Anton's hands are worse than mine and because he will only tell you pleasant things. I am ill. The doctors use long words that mean short things. I am not afraid for myself, but I am afraid he will do something noble and ruinous, and I need you to write to him, to tell him that nobility is cheap and staying alive is expensive and worth every penny.* Then, at the end, smaller: *If these letters reach you, please burn them and tell no one I wrote. I am proud, and pride is the one thing I cannot afford.*

The letter had never reached anyone. It lay in the dark under a floorboard while its author leaned into her wind.

Mara sat with his hands in his lap for a long time. Outside, the frost deepened; the yard gave off its faint creaking, the sound frozen ground makes as it contracts, like a great animal turning in its sleep. He thought about the phrase *noble and ruinous*, and he thought about a man registering under a wrong name at a country house full of strangers, walking toward the one country that wanted him not at all, and he felt the shape of the thing before he could bear to look at it directly.

The fifth letter was dated eleven days after the fourth, in Anton's hand again, and it said: *Anna died on Tuesday morning, quietly, with the window open because she loved the cold. Her father came to the funeral and stood beside me and we did not speak, and it was the truest conversation we have ever had. I am sending you her letter after all, against her wishes, because you should know what she was, and because she was prouder of you than of anything else in her life, and lies of omission are still lies.*

Her letter to her mother was folded inside. Mara read it standing up, though there was nothing in it requiring him to stand except everything. It was four pages long and it forgave everyone in it, including itself.

* * *

One letter remained.

He knew before he broke the seal that this was the one he should have left under the floor. Some part of him—the part that had memorized Edith Calloway's correspondents, the part that could erase himself from any room in under a minute—had known since the third letter, had been reading ahead in the way of a man descending stairs in the dark who feels, three steps early, the absence of the next one.

It was dated fourteen months ago. It began without address, as if meant for no one, or for whoever might one day hold it.

*If you are reading this, I am either dead or beyond reach, and either way the following must be said plainly, because plainness is the only inheritance I have.*

*Two years ago the Ministry of Works requested that I design the fortification of the river narrows at K—, including emplacements sited to fire upon the town itself in the event of insurrection. I refused. I refused a second time in writing. I was then invited to reconsider, in a room with no windows, by men who used my Christian name as though we were friends. I still refused. I resigned my position and left the country within the month. I am told I am now listed as having abandoned state service in time of national preparation, which is their language for desertion, which is their language for treason.*

*I did not build the guns that point at the town. This is the whole of my crime, and I confess it freely: I would do it again, and again, as many times as there are versions of my life in which I am asked. But I know how such confessions travel. I know that to one government this paper is exoneration and to another it is a rope. So I entrust it to no postal system and no pocket but yours, whoever you are, because you promised, and a promise made in peacetime is the only treaty that survives the war.*

Mara read the final paragraph twice. It was short.

*Tell Anna's mother that her daughter did not marry a coward. Tell her I tried to send love and it kept coming back marked undeliverable. Tell her that is not the fault of love.*

The lantern had burned almost to nothing. The oil was low and the wick guttered, and in the shrinking light the page seemed to darken as he watched it, the ink settling into the paper like something sinking into water.

He thought: *this could hang him.* Not figuratively. He sat in a country sliding toward war, in the coach house of a woman whose cause was already a scandal, holding a document in which a citizen of a now-hostile nation confessed—in his own hand, dated, signed—to refusing state service and fleeing prosecution. The magistrate kept files. The niece kept secrets badly. The journalist kept accounts of everything. If this page left the coach house in any pocket but the right one, Anton Reval would not be escorted to the border. He would simply stop arriving anywhere.

And if it stayed under the floor—if Mara chose safety and silence—then a dying woman's last wish and a dead woman's forgiveness and a man's entire defense would rot together in the dark, and Mara Winterbourne would go on being nobody, which was the thing he had once called a blessing and now understood, sitting alone in the cold with seven opened letters, was only solitude wearing a disguise.

He folded the pages along their original creases, returned them to their envelopes, sealed the torn corner with paste and the flatiron, and rewrapped the bundle in its brown paper and oilcloth. His hands were steady. It interested him distantly, the steadiness; it was the steadiness of a man who has stopped deciding and begun merely executing, the decision having been made somewhere below language.

Because there was no choice anymore. That was the burden of knowledge—it converted every question into arithmetic. Anton was in the house under a false name with a confession waiting under a floorboard, the border was closing, the river was beginning to think about ice, and the woman across it—her mother, her family, the addressee of seven letters spanning marriage and illness and death—was waiting for words two years late, some of which could only be delivered by a living man on the far shore.

He knelt, laid the bundle back into the cavity, and was reaching for the board when the lantern finally died and the dark came down like a lid—and in that blackness, from the direction of the yard, came the crunch of a boot on frozen gravel, unhurried, stopping just outside the coach-house door.

Then a knock. Three taps, courteous, evenly spaced—the knock of a man who already knows someone is inside.
