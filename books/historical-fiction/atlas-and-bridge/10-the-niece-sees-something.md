## 10. The Niece Sees Something

The corridor outside the morning room was long and badly lit, which was precisely why I chose it.

I had learned the geography of Ashcombe House the way a thief learns the geometry of a lock—by feel, in the dark, one small pressure at a time. The third-floor passage with its warped boards that announced any footstep. The back stair whose seventh step could be skipped entirely if you knew to skip it. And this corridor on the ground floor, running behind the morning room like a vein behind skin, where a single tall window let in a gray December light so thin it seemed borrowed rather than given. Anyone standing at the window's edge could see down both directions of the hall. Anyone standing flat against the wall between the window and the doorframe could not be seen at all unless someone came around the corner slowly, unhurried, with no expectation of anything but wallpaper.

Clementine Calloway had no expectations at all. That was the problem.

I had been waiting there since half past nine, when the household settled into its winter rhythm: Edith at her desk upstairs composing the week's correspondence, the magistrate Mr. Harrow taking his constitutional turn about the garden as though the frozen beds required supervision, the journalist Pell writing his poison in the library with the door open so everyone might hear his pen scratching like a mouse in the wainscoting. Anton had taken up his usual post near the morning room fire with a book of Edith's he'd pretended to admire—a treatise on canal engineering, dull enough to be invisible—and I had passed him in the doorway with the letter folded inside my glove the way I'd passed a hundred letters in my life, without a word, without a look, a touch of sleeve against sleeve that meant nothing to anyone in the world except the two men who had arranged it.

It should have taken four seconds. It took four seconds. And then Clementine came around the corner with a vase of dried lavender she'd been carrying somewhere for reasons known only to the frightened, and she stopped, and she looked, and I understood in the space of a single heartbeat that everything I had built out of silence was about to be tested by a girl who had never in her life kept a secret larger than a surprise party.

She saw me withdraw my hand from near Anton's coat. She saw the white rectangle of paper disappearing into his breast pocket. She saw Anton go very still—the stillness of a man caught in a searchlight—and she saw me turn and smile at her, because smiling is what you do when you have nothing else left.

"Clementine," I said pleasantly. "Lavender in December."

"It keeps the moths away." Her voice was thin. Her knuckles were white around the vase. "Or—or encourages them. Aunt Edith would know."

Anton said something courteous in his careful English, something about the moths being welcome to the canal treatise, and laughed his small deliberate laugh, and moved past her into the morning room as though the whole exchange were nothing, as though the paper against his heart were a train ticket, a recipe, a note about bridge loads. He was good. He was better than I was. But Clementine was already looking at me, and her eyes had gone wide and wet in that particular way that meant her imagination had outrun the evidence and was now galloping through fields of scandal with no fence anywhere.

"Mr. Winterbourne," she whispered.

"Aunt Edith wants the post bag taken down to the village," I lied smoothly. "There's a letter for Mr.—for our guest. His family writes often. It's a great comfort to a man stranded from home."

"Oh," she said. "Oh. Of course. Family." And then, lower, with the trembling earnestness of a girl who believed herself to be protecting everyone by destroying everything: "Mr. Winterbourne, is something *wrong*? Is something happening in this house?"

"Nothing at all," I said, and held her gaze with the steady blankness I had spent eleven years perfecting, and watched her believe me and disbelieve me in the same instant, and knew which half would win.

* * *

She went first to her aunt, of course. That was the proper order of things; she was a girl raised on the proper order of things. But she got no further than the landing before her courage failed—Edith Calloway mid-morning was a force of weather, and even her niece feared being rained on—and so Clementine stood at the foot of the stairs holding her lavender and her terrible knowledge for the better part of an hour, growing more certain with every minute that certainty was required of her.

I saw it happen. I saw her rehearse. She stood by the hall mirror practicing the words under her breath—I could read her lips in reflection well enough, having spent a lifetime reading lips in reflections—and the words were *aunt, I must tell you something, I'm sure it's nothing,* and each time she said *nothing* her chin lifted a fraction higher, which is the gesture of a person persuading themselves. By noon she had abandoned the direct approach entirely, and I watched her drift instead toward the morning room where Mr. Harrow sat with the newspapers, and I watched her hover, and I watched Harrow look up at her with those pale, patient eyes of his—the eyes of a man who filed things—and say, in his mild way:

"You seem troubled, Miss Calloway. Won't you sit?"

And she sat.

I could not hear them from the corridor. I did not need to. I knew how confessions worked; I had extracted enough of them from other people's correspondence to know their architecture. There is the hesitation, then the disclaimer, then the thing itself delivered in a rush as though speed could make it smaller. Then comes the listener's silence, which is the most powerful instrument in the world, because silence forces the confessor to fill it, and they fill it with more than they meant to give. Clementine gave Harrow more than she meant to give. I could see it in the way she talked with her hands, in the way he leaned forward by degrees, in the way he produced at last, from his breast pocket—not unlike the pocket where Anton's letter now lay—a small black notebook, and licked the tip of his pencil, and wrote.

He wrote for a long time. He asked questions I could not hear. She answered them all.

When she left him she looked lighter and graver at once, the way people do after unburdening, believing themselves to have done right. When Harrow remained, he did not move for several minutes. He simply sat with the notebook open on his knee, looking at the fire, wearing the expression of a man doing arithmetic. Then he closed the notebook, patted his pocket once as though confirming the presence of something valuable, and went to find Edith.

That was when I went to find Anton.

* * *

He was in the stable yard, ostensibly examining the ice on the water trough, which had become his habitual refuge. Foreign engineers may stare at frozen water for as long as they please without arousing comment; it is assumed they are thinking about bridges.

"She talked to Harrow," I said quietly, without greeting. "Twenty minutes. He made notes."

Anton did not startle. I had noticed early that he never startled; whatever he had carried across borders in his life had burned the flinch out of him. He turned his head slightly, watching his own breath climb the cold air.

"What did she see?"

"A letter passing. Nothing more. She doesn't know your name isn't your name, she doesn't know what was in the envelope—but Harrow knows how to ask, and she knows how to answer, and between them they'll build a story neither of them can prove and neither of them will doubt." I pulled my collar up against the wind coming off the river. "The question is what he does with it."

"He does what officials do in my country," Anton said. "He waits. He watches. He counts the days until watching becomes permission." He straightened, and for a moment the engineer surfaced in his face—that habit of measuring things, spans and tolerances, the load a structure will bear before it fails. "Your magistrate is a patient man, Mara. Patient men are worse than angry ones. An angry man makes mistakes tomorrow. A patient man makes none for a year, and then makes only one, and it is sufficient."

"We have time yet. The river road is watched but passable, and the ice—"

"The ice is three weeks from safe. You told me yourself."

"And we may not have three weeks." I heard my own voice go flat and hard, the voice I used for figures and distances. "If he searches—"

"Does he have the authority?" Anton asked. "In this house? Over guests of Mrs. Calloway?"

"He has something better. He has a nervous girl's testimony and a journalist who'd print an accusation before he'd verify one, and Edith has a movement to protect, and Harrow knows—" I stopped myself. I had been about to say *Harrow knows she'll trade us to save the cause,* and I did not believe it, and saying a disbelief aloud is sometimes how it becomes true. "Edith won't allow a search," I said instead.

"No," Anton agreed mildly. "But she cannot prevent one either. Not with soldiers two hours away in the town and a road that belongs to nobody in February." He looked at me then, directly, and there was something in his face I had not seen before—or had seen and refused to name—something that was not fear for himself. "You should take the letters out of wherever they live. Tonight. If he finds them, they are not only my death, they are hers. A woman who harbors—" He used a word in his own language, soft and bitter, and did not translate it. He didn't need to. Every country has the word.

"They're safe."

"Mara." He almost smiled. "You taught me the word for *safe* in English. You said there is no such word, only *safer.*"

I remembered saying it. I had forgotten teaching it. That was the trouble with a life built out of small transactions—you forgot which ones had been received.

"I'll move them tonight," I said.

* * *

But the evening belonged to Edith, and to the machinery she set turning with a single sentence over dinner.

We ate by candlelight because the generator had been temperamental all week, and the dining room at Ashcombe in candlelight was a beautiful lie—six faces gold-edged above the dark, the silverware throwing small flames, the storm windows shuddering faintly whenever the river wind came up the valley. Harrow sat at Edith's right and consumed an excellent saddle of mutton while discussing, of all things, the county's bridge repairs, with the cheerful thoroughness of a man discussing a noose he happened to find convenient.

"Spring, they tell me. Perhaps April." He dabbed his mouth. "An inconvenience for Mrs. Calloway's household, I imagine. All of us sealed in together until the thaw." His pale eyes traveled the table without hurry, touching each of us like a customs officer's hand. "Though I confess I find the company instructive. One learns so much about people when travel is impossible. Everyone's true nature comes out, eventually. Like water finding its level."

"How philosophical, Mr. Harrow," said Pell the journalist, who missed nothing and misread most of it. "One might write a column."

"One might," Harrow agreed pleasantly.

Edith set down her glass with a click no louder than a moth landing, and I, who had spent eleven years learning to hear Edith's silences, heard this one clearly. When she spoke, her voice was warm and ironed flat.

"Since we are discussing the spring, I should tell you all that I intend to put the house in order before it. My correspondence alone has grown shameful—two years of papers in the study, and my niece tells me I hoard letters the way other women hoard buttons." A small laugh around the table; Clementine colored gorgeously, unaware she had just been made an instrument. "So: beginning Saturday, I shall have every room turned out. Guest rooms included—it wouldn't do to have trunks and old linens underfoot when the workmen come. Mrs. Pyle will organize it. I trust no one objects to a little housekeeping."

Nobody objected. Harrow's fork paused perhaps a quarter-second over his plate—a pause no one else at that table could have measured, and I measured it—and then he resumed eating and said that he found thoroughness in housekeeping admirable in a hostess, and that he would be glad to assist in any capacity required.

"You're too kind," said Edith. "Saturday, then. All rooms."

Her eyes crossed mine for less than a second on the word *all,* and there was nothing in them I could read, which from Edith was itself a message.

* * *

I did not sleep. I sat in the coach house among the smells of leather and lamp oil and horse that no longer existed, with the hidden bundle warm inside my shirt against my chest like a second heartbeat, and I thought about Saturday.

A search of the guest rooms would find nothing in Anton's room—he was too careful—but it would find plenty elsewhere, or could, if Harrow searched rather than merely looked. It would find the false panel in my own quarters, if he knew to look for false panels, and magistrates know. And even if nothing was found, the searching itself would be testimony: Harrow would stand in Anton's room moving another man's belongings with his gloved hands, and afterward he would write in the little black book, and the book would grow heavier, and one day in April, when the bridge chains came off and the world reopened, the book would go to somebody in the capital, and Anton would step onto a public road into a country that had declared him unpronounceable, unwanted, *enemy*, and the book would be waiting for him there.

Unless the letters were gone by then. Unless the bundle crossed the river ahead of him, and the confession with it, and the man arrived empty-handed and therefore weightless.

Which meant the crossing could not wait for safe ice. Which meant three weeks had become however many days remained until Saturday, minus one for the move, and the river was still talking to itself under the black surface at night in that low grinding voice it uses when it is deciding whether to hold a man or swallow him.

Through the wall I heard the house settle—Clementine's door closing late, the magistrate's measured tread going his rounds of the corridor as though he owned the geography already, and beneath everything, faint as a pulse, the sound I had come to dread more than footsteps: the ice on the river, shifting, testing its own edges, keeping its own counsel about what it would carry and when.

Somewhere upstairs Edith was awake too. I was sure of it. Two keepers of the same dark, separated by plaster and thirty years of practice, both listening to the same river.

I wrapped the bundle again and put it where no honest search would think to look, and I began, in the cold and the lamplight, to count the days until Saturday—and to understand, with the clarity that comes only at two in the morning, that whatever happened in the guest rooms that day, the real search had already begun, and I had been its object since the moment Clementine picked up her vase of lavender.
