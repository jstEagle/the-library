## 11. Search

The morning of the search came in on a gray wind that rattled the shutters like a creditor at the door.

Mara had been awake since four. He did not need to be told the day's shape; he had felt it assembling for a week, piece by piece—the magistrate's polite questions at breakfast, the journalist's notebook appearing at odd hours like a black cat that had learned to open doors, the niece Clara's flushed face and downcast eyes whenever he entered a room. Innocence, when it turned against you, always wore that particular shade of shame.

He dressed in his driving coat, the plain one, the one that made him furniture. Then he went down through the frozen kitchen, where the cook was already beating life into the stove, and out to the coach house to look one last time at what could not be found.

* * *

The letters were gone. Of course they were gone; he had moved them two nights ago, wrapped in oilcloth, sewn into the lining of the spare carriage cushion that lived under the driver's bench. But habit is a kind of prayer, and he knelt at the loose board anyway, ran his fingers over the empty dark, and felt the strange grief of a thing done well: no evidence of himself anywhere, not so much as a fingerprint of his history on the world.

Except, now, in Anton's eyes. And in Edith's, though he could not yet say how.

He heard the car before he saw it—Mr. Halloran's motor, grinding up the river road with the journalist, Mr. Pike, perched beside the driver like a crow on a plow. The magistrate had telephoned yesterday from the town exchange; even courtesy, in winter, traveled by wire. *A formality, Mrs. Calloway, I assure you. The Alien Act requires certain assurances.* Edith had agreed with a graciousness that should have frightened him more than refusal would have. She never yielded ground she intended to keep.

Mara stood in the coach house doorway and watched the car come, and made himself into the man it needed him to be: Winterbourne, the driver, competent and blank, a man whose interior life was as unexamined as a horse's.

It had worked for eleven years.

He counted the ways it might fail today, and found he could not number them all, which was itself an answer.

* * *

They arrived at ten. Halloran in his good coat, gloved, smelling faintly of church; Pike behind him with his notebook already open, pencil wet, drinking the house in through his eyes—the paneling, the silver, the portraits of Calloways dead and indifferent to posterity.

"Mrs. Calloway." The magistrate bowed over her hand in the front hall, steam rising from everyone's breath as if the house itself were sighing. "I regret the imposition."

"You regret nothing, Mr. Halloran, but you are welcome regardless." Edith stood on the bottom stair like a general reviewing troops who had come, mistakenly, believing this was their parade. She was seventy-one years old and she filled the hall the way a fire fills a grate. "You'll take tea first. My guests are at breakfast, and I don't serve warrant officers on an empty stomach."

"It is not a warrant—" He stopped. Recalculated. "It is a courtesy visitation."

"Then you shall be courteous in the drawing room, after tea." She swept ahead of them, and Mara, holding the door, caught the flicker in Halloran's jaw—the small grind of a man swallowing his rank whole.

Pike lingered at the threshold, looking up at the lintel, the corners, the bell pulls. Writing something. Mara closed the door behind him with the gentlest click, and thought: *you are cataloguing a house you will never understand, and I will bury you in its details.*

That was the trick, he reminded himself, following them in with the tea tray. Not to hide the letters. To drown the searcher in the sheer honest weight of everything else.

* * *

They began upstairs at eleven, once Halloran had extracted himself from an argument about the Franchise Bill that Edith had been winning for forty minutes without once raising her voice. Pike went with them, narrating in his notebook as though dictating a will: *guest room, east wing, first door—occupied by Mr. Greaves, magistrate's colleague—wardrobe searched, valise searched, correspondence examined.* Mara carried the lamp. That was his role; no one questioned it; the invisible man holds the light so that others may see what does not concern him.

Room by room. Drawers opened with rubber-gloved hands. Mattresses pressed, seam by seam, Halloran working with the thoroughness of a man who had done this to better houses than this. Clara's room first—she stood in the corridor clutching a shawl, whispering *there's nothing, there's nothing* until her aunt took her hand and silenced her with a look that said, plainly, *stop confessing to crimes you have not committed.*

Anton's room last of the east wing. Or rather, the room registered to a *Mr. Ansell, civil engineer*, whose papers were impeccable because Mara had written them.

Halloran went through it like a surgeon. He shook out the boots. He held the shirts to the window. He unwound the bandages from a sprained wrist and studied the wrist itself, as though a forearm might declare a nationality. And through all of it Anton sat on the edge of his bed with his hands folded, wearing the mild boredom of a man watching weather.

Only Mara, standing close with the lamp, saw the pulse ticking in his throat like something trying to get out.

"Your papers, Mr. Ansell."

Anton produced them. Swiss registration, letters of reference, a stamped itinerary. Forgeries age strangely—some grow more convincing with handling, some rot—and these had been handled enough to pass. Halloran read each page twice. Pike copied out the name and the dates.

"You are engaged in work on the county bridges," Halloran said.

"I was consulted. Informally. Before the closure."

"And your specialty?"

"Bridges," Anton said, with the smallest pause before the word, and Mara understood that the pause was deliberate—that Anton knew exactly what suspicion sounded like and was feeding it a harmless meal, one grain at a time.

Halloran put the papers down. He looked at Anton the way a man looks at a locked box he has decided not to force today. "Thank you. We'll continue below."

* * *

They came down through the house like a slow flood, and Mara moved ahead of it, and here is what the flood did not know:

At half past six that morning, before the ice on the windows had begun to weep, there had been a knock at the coach house door. Mara had opened it with the letters' hiding place already rehearsed in his mind, three lies loaded—and found Edith Calloway on the step, wrapped in her oldest fur, breath smoking, entirely unsurprised to find him awake.

She had not asked what he kept there. She had said only: "Bring me the bundle you sleep badly about. Seven o'clock. My study. Come by the garden door."

And when he had stood in her study at seven, holding the oilcloth package that contained another man's life—letters, a confession, the whole fragile cargo of a promise—he had watched her take it from him without ceremony, unlock a drawer, and slide it into the very center of her own papers: the suffragist correspondence, the movement's minutes, the membership rolls, the clippings, forty years of paper that no official in the country dared inventory, because to catalogue Edith Calloway's files was to make oneself a character in her memoirs, and every man in office had read enough of her letters to know how she wrote people afterward.

"You'll hide them *with yours*?" he had said, stupidly, because he had prepared for every contingency except being rescued.

"Where else?" She had locked the drawer. "No magistrate alive will go through my movement's papers with a fine-tooth comb, Mr. Winterbourne. They're afraid of what they'll find, and more afraid of my footnotes." A pause; her eyes had gone over him, unhurried, top to toe, the way she read a contract. "Contraband hides best among contraband. You of all people know that."

She had known what he was carrying. She had perhaps known what *he* was, longer than he had known it himself. And she had asked for nothing—which was, he understood even then, the down payment on a debt she intended to collect later, at a rate of her own choosing.

Now the flood reached the ground floor, and Mara held the lamp, and waited to see whether his nerve or hers would crack first.

* * *

The study smelled of ink and cold. Halloran entered it the way men enter rooms where they have been beaten before—Pike at his elbow, pencil poised—and Edith received them in her chair by the window, knitting, of all weapons, knitting, the needles clicking a small metronome of contempt.

"The desk, certainly," Halloran said.

"By all means."

He went through it thoroughly. Bills, household accounts, a manuscript in progress that Pike copied the title of (*On the Citizenship of Women*) with the reverence of a man handling live ammunition. The locked drawer Mara watched from the corner of his eye while keeping his face aimed at the middle distance, and his heart performed no gymnastics at all, because he had discovered sometime around dawn that terror, fully ripened, comes out the other side as stillness.

Halloran tried the drawer. Locked.

"Mrs. Calloway. The key."

"Certainly not."

The needles did not stop.

"Madam, I am empowered—"

"You are empowered to search for documents pertaining to the movements of one Anton Reval, alien, of whom you have no evidence beyond a telegram and a suspicion. What you propose to examine are the private files of a political organization engaged in lawful agitation before Parliament. Open that drawer without my consent, Mr. Halloran, and by Friday every newspaper in London will carry the story—with your name spelled correctly, which Mr. Pike will confirm is the only immortality most officials fear." She smiled at Pike, who colored and did not write anything down. "My lawyer is in town. My typist is faster than yours. Choose."

The clock on the mantel ticked. The wind worried the shutter. Somewhere upstairs Clara dropped something small and porcelain and did not come down to claim it.

And Mara, holding the lamp, watched the magistrate perform the arithmetic that powerful men always perform too late: the cost of the drawer against the cost of retreat. He chose retreat, as she had known he would, as she had built the entire morning to ensure he would—but he chose it with a face like a slammed door, and Mara understood that the price would come later, to someone smaller.

"Then we conclude above stairs," Halloran said. "Mr. Winterbourne. Your quarters."

* * *

His rooms above the coach house. Eleven years of deliberate anonymity, laid open in twenty minutes.

It was almost funny. There was nothing to find because there was almost nothing at all—one trunk of clothing, none of it old enough to remember a previous owner; a Bible, unmarked; shaving things; a copy of the railway timetable annotated with driving routes; wages banked under a name that matched his forged references perfectly, because he had forged nothing so carefully as his own ordinariness.

Halloran went through the trunk garment by garment. Pike recorded: *driver's quarters, sparse, no correspondence of interest.* And Mara stood in the middle of his own erasure and felt—not fear now, but something worse, a vertigo of the spirit—because it was true. It was all true. There was no previous self in the room. If they arrested the emptiness, the emptiness would hang alone.

"You've been with Mrs. Calloway long," Halloran said, not looking up from the folded shirts.

"Eleven years, sir."

"A man remembers his employer's friends."

"A man remembers his employer's enemies better, sir. It's safer."

Pike's pencil paused. Halloran looked up then, and for one naked moment the two of them regarded each other across the little room—the hunter and the thing that would not be caught, each recognizing the other's craft—and Mara thought, with perfect clarity: *he knows. Not what I am. Only that I am something.*

"That will be all," Halloran said.

* * *

They left at half past three. The car ground away down the river road trailing blue smoke, and the house exhaled, and Clara burst into tears in the linen closet and was found there later pretending to look for tablecloths.

Mara went straight to the study. The drawer was unlocked now; Edith sat by the window with her knitting, and beside her on the desk lay the oilcloth bundle, intact, rewrapped with a care that had included pressing one of the letters flat under a book to undo the drawer's crease.

"You didn't read them," he said.

"I didn't need to read them." She snipped a thread with her scissors, a small decisive sound. "I read faces, and yours has been a broadsheet for a fortnight. Sit down, Mr. Winterbourne—or whatever you are called when it doesn't matter."

He sat. Outside the light was failing early, the short winter day folding up like a fan.

"What you did today," he began.

"What I did today was protect my household, which includes a guest whose only crime is his birthplace and a servant whose only crime is loyalty he thinks he's hidden." She set down the knitting and looked at him directly, and her eyes were the pale flint color of the river in January. "Understand me. I hid those letters because it was Tuesday and they needed hiding. I did not join your conspiracy. I do not yet know what your conspiracy costs, or who pays. But I know a magistrate does not bring a journalist to a courtesy search unless he intends to make someone an example, and I intend to choose the example before he does."

"There will be soldiers," Mara said quietly. "When the bridge opens. Perhaps patrols on the road before that."

"Yes."

"And you'll still keep him under your roof."

"He came to my roof in peacetime," Edith said, taking up her needles again, "and peacetime promises are the only ones worth the name. Any fool keeps a promise when keeping it is safe." The needles clicked, twice, three times. "Now. You will tell me what is in those letters that makes you hold the lamp with both hands. All of it. Because next time he searches, Mr. Winterbourne, he will not leave politely—and I do not defend what I do not understand."

Through the window, far off, the frozen river lay white and blind between the bare trees, and somewhere beneath its surface the water was still moving, patient, toward spring.

Mara began to speak.
