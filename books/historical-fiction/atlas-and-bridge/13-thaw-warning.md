## 13. Thaw Warning

It began with the eaves.

Mara noticed it first on a Tuesday morning in late February, standing in the yard with a currycomb in his hand though there had been no horse to groom since November. The eaves of the coach house were dripping—a slow, fat, irregular ticking, like a clock losing time. For eleven weeks that sound had been impossible. For eleven weeks the whole world had been nailed shut with cold, and he had grown so accustomed to the silence of frozen water that the drip struck him as indecent, a door left open in a house where secrets slept.

By noon the south-facing drifts had gone soft at their edges, slumping like tired men. By evening the smell of the river reached the garden—mud and old weeds and moving water—and Mara stood at the window of his room above the stable with his coat still on, feeling the season turn against them like a witness changing her story.

He had learned the river's moods the way he learned everything: by listening when no one watched. All winter it had been theirs. A road of white silence, wide enough to hide a crossing, strong enough to hold a man who weighed less than his fears. Now it was talking again. And a talking river was a river keeping accounts.

At dinner that night, Edith Calloway set down her spoon before the soup had cooled.

"The ice report came with the post," she said, to no one and therefore to everyone. "The correspondent at Harwick says the channel has opened three miles downstream. Three miles." She let the number sit on the table like a stone. "Two weeks ago it was nothing but a crack at the weir."

The magistrate's fork paused midair. The journalist, Mr. Pell, leaned forward with the hunger he never quite managed to disguise as manners. Edith's niece, Clemency, went pale in the way she did now whenever the war or the river or anything larger than a teacup was mentioned—as though all of it were somehow her fault.

"An early thaw," the magistrate said pleasantly. "The farmers will be glad."

"The farmers will be glad," Edith agreed, "and the rest of us will spend March rowing wherever we mean to go." Her eyes crossed the table and did not stop at Anton Reval—she was too disciplined for that—but touched near him, in the region of his silence, the way one checks a lock without appearing to.

Anton ate very little these days. He sat with his shoulders drawn in, the engineer reduced to something folded and waiting, and Mara, serving the fish, counted the days in the hollows under his eyes.

* * *

Afterward, in the coach house, among the smells of leather and liniment and lamp oil, Anton finally broke.

"They are saying a week," he said. He stood by the workbench where the river map lay beneath a spread copy of *The Agricultural Gazette*, its innocent columns of livestock prices concealing penciled contours: sound ice in blue, rotten ice in red, the long diagonal of the ford marked with an X that meant *maybe*. "Perhaps less. The man at Harwick knows ice—he used to run sledges before the railway. If he says the channel is open at three miles, then the current underneath us is already awake. It is eating from below. Do you understand? The ice does not melt first where you can see it. It dies from underneath, in the dark, and stays standing like a wall until the day it isn't."

"I understand ice," Mara said, more sharply than he intended.

Anton looked at him then, and there was something wild in his face that Mara had not seen before—not fear for himself; Mara had understood weeks ago that there was very little of that—but the fear of a man watching a door close on the last room in the world that contained what he loved.

"Then you understand we go soon. Tomorrow. Tomorrow night."

"Tomorrow the ice will hold a hare and nothing else. You want to walk out onto a river that is already drowning itself."

"I want—" Anton stopped. His fists were closed. When he opened them they were shaking, and he seemed surprised by them, as if they belonged to someone else. "Lena's family is across that river, Mara. Her sister writes—I told you what the last letter said. *She asks for you by name.* She is dying by inches in a village I could reach in two hours if the world were not mad enough to draw lines through it." He pressed his palm flat against the workbench, against the map, over the far bank. "I have spent two years being patient. Patience was a virtue when the border was merely closed. Now it is a vice. They will call me up—they have called every man with my name in the district, you saw the notice at the posting house—and if I am taken, I do not get letters delivered. I get buried in some fortification I refused to build, or shot for refusing it twice. There is no version of waiting that ends well. There is only the river, while the river lasts."

"And if the river lasts exactly long enough to kill you?" Mara heard his own voice rise and hated it, this loss of control, this becoming visible. "Listen to me. I have walked that crossing forty times in my head. I know where the current runs fast under the white—the black ice there is thin as window glass and twice as treacherous. In deep cold, it holds. But a warm spell doesn't weaken ice evenly. It rots the middle first, where the current is strongest, and leaves the edges looking honest. You would step onto a mile of solid-seeming white with a grave cut into its heart, and you would find it precisely when you could not turn back."

"Then you go around—"

"There is no around. That is the whole geometry of our lives now. There is the bridge with soldiers coming to it, and there is the ford, and there is spring, which is the same as never."

Anton was quiet a moment. The lamp hissed. Somewhere overhead a beam ticked, expanding in the warmth, and both of them flinched like men hearing rifle fire.

"You could stay," Anton said at last, softly, and the softness was worse than shouting. "You should stay. This is my promise to collect, not yours. You carried my letters because I asked, and God knows I have no right to ask anything further. Stay here. Keep your place. Keep your—invisibility." He said the word carefully; it was the word Mara had used once, drunk on nothing stronger than candlelight and confession, describing the art of himself. "If I go through the ice alone, it is only a fool drowned. If you drown with me, then everything you have hidden yourself for is wasted, and Edith loses the one servant she trusts, and I cannot—" He stopped. Started again. "I have cost you enough."

"That is not yours to decide."

"It is entirely mine to decide! It is my wife, my country, my crime—"

"*Your crime*," Mara said, "is refusing to build a machine for killing people, and the man whose promise you keep invoking gets to say whether he keeps it. You don't get to release me from it to make yourself feel noble. That is not how promises work. They are not favors. They don't expire and they can't be refunded." He pulled the newspaper off the map with a crack of folded pages. "Look at it. Look."

The pencil lines glowed in the lamplight. Blue along the near shore. Red blooming in the middle like a stain spreading through cloth.

"In January," Mara said, quieter now, because the map had made his argument for him, "this was all blue. Every line. We had a month then, and I said wait, and you said the ice was a gift, and it was. Now look at the red. We had a gift and we are down to the ribbon." He looked up. "We wait four days. Four days of hard frost—if the forecast at the livery holds, there is a high pressure coming, real cold, the kind that makes nails snap. Four nights of that and the red shrinks. Then we go together, at dawn, with the light behind us, on ice that has remembered what it is."

"And if the forecast is wrong?"

"Then we go anyway. But we go on ice we chose, not ice that chose for us."

Anton stared at the map for a long time. When he spoke, his voice had gone somewhere flat and terrible.

"You are gambling with my last days with her family. You hear that? Even if I cross, even if I succeed, I cross into a country at the edge of a war, to a dying woman's bedside, and then—what? What is after? There may be no letter back, no message, no proof of anything except that I got there. These four days might be the difference between holding her sister's hand and reading about the funeral in a newspaper." He shook his head slowly. "You talk about ice. I talk about time. They are not the same thing, Mara. Ice forgives. Time does not forgive anyone anything."

"And whose survival is it you're so anxious about?" Mara heard himself say it before he could weigh it, and once said, there was nothing to do but finish. "Because from where I stand, you've been trying to die politely since the day you arrived. You register under a false name, you eat almost nothing, you sit at dinner like a man rehearsing his own eulogy. You want the crossing to be hard. Hard is fine. Hard means it wasn't cowardice. But I have watched men walk into weather hoping it would do what their honor required of them, and I am telling you it doesn't count as sacrifice if what you're really doing is outsourcing the sin."

The silence afterward had weight, and texture, and edges.

Anton's face went through something slow and painful, like ice shifting. "That is not—"

"Isn't it? Tell me I'm wrong. Tell me that if I agreed tonight, tomorrow night, you wouldn't feel a little lighter."

"I would feel," Anton said, very low, "like a man who had finally stopped wasting other people's courage." He turned away, hands braced on the bench, head bowed, and the lamplight put a gold rim on his hair and left his face in shadow. "My wife wrote to me once that I loved causes more than people. I have thought about that sentence every day for two years. She was wrong. But I have done a very thorough job of proving her right."

Mara stood with the map between his hands and felt the quarrel collapse into something worse than anger—the recognition underneath it, the thing they had been circling for weeks. He cared whether this man lived. Not for the promise. Not only for the promise. Somewhere between the candlelit maps and the shared watches of midnight, duty had put out roots, and pulling it free now would tear something neither of them could name and both of them would bleed from.

"We go in four days," Mara said. "Together, or not at all. Those are the terms. I don't deliver half a promise and I don't bury the other half."

"And if the cold doesn't come?"

Mara looked at the map, at the red eating the blue, at the small X that meant *maybe*, and gave the only honest answer he had.

"Then we learn to swim."

* * *

The frost did not come.

The forecast from the livery had spoken of high pressure and hard nights, and instead the wind swung south that very evening and stayed there, warm and wet and smelling of mud, like a traitor's handshake. By Thursday the yard was a mirror of gray water. By Friday, icicles the length of an arm hung from the coach house gutter, thinning hourly, dripping their slow countdown, and Mara stood beneath them in the dark and listened to winter dying audibly, drop by drop, and thought: *four days, and the sky has voted the other way.*

A boy came up from the ferry landing with the evening post—a smug boy on a shaggy pony, riding the river road that would be impassable mud within the week. Among the bills and the parish circulars was a plain envelope, unstamped, delivered by hand. Mara steamed it open in the coach house out of thirty years of habit that was no longer entirely habit, and found a single line in Edith's upright, unhurried hand:

*The Harwick man now says Sunday. Come to me before you do anything foolish.*

Mara read it twice. Then he burned it in the stove, watched the curl of paper go orange and vanish, and stood a moment in the dark with the drips counting down behind him like footsteps approaching.

Sunday. Two days. And the ice, all down the valley, going soft in the dark.

He took down his coat.
