## 14. The Journalist's Price

The thaw came in the second week of February like a rumor nobody wanted to repeat. It lasted only three days—a soft, treacherous warmth that made the eaves drip and the south lawn go gray and sodden—but it was enough. The river, which had been a white road for a month, began to mutter underfoot. Edith Calloway stood at the drawing room window with her hands folded behind her back, watching the far bank as though she could read the ice the way she read correspondence, and said nothing for a long time.

Mara Winterbourne noticed because noticing was his profession. He had spent two years learning the grammar of that house: which doors stuck in damp weather, which guests lied about their appetites, how long Edith's silences ran when they meant something versus when they were merely rest. This silence had weight. He stood at the sideboard with the coffee pot he would not be asked to pour yet, and waited.

"Mr. Halloran wants half an hour with me this afternoon," Edith said at last, without turning. "In the library. Alone."

"Shall I bring tea?"

"He'll want you out of the room, Mara. That's rather the point of asking."

Halloran. Silas Halloran, of the *Argus*, who had arrived in November with a magistrate's letter of introduction and a smile like a borrowed coat. He wrote longhand at breakfast, pages of it, and asked his questions sideways so that they seemed to be about weather or roads or the price of coal, and only later did you find the hook buried in them. Mara had steamed open one of the man's outgoing letters in October—old habit, old sin—and found four pages of careful observation on Edith's household, her correspondence, her visitors, phrased in the flat shorthand of a man building a case file of his own.

"Alone, then," Mara said.

Edith turned from the window. She was sixty-one years old and carried her age the way a ship carries ballast—low, deliberate, useful. "He's going to make me an offer," she said. "Men like Halloran always dress up a transaction as a conversation. I'd like you in the coach house when it happens. Not listening." A pause, dry as paper. "Or not obviously listening. If he leaves by the garden door, I want to know which way he walks. A man's feet tell you what his mouth has decided before his mouth knows it."

"And if he goes toward the town road?"

"Then I've misjudged him," Edith said, "which I do perhaps once a decade, and hate every time."

* * *

The offer, as it happened, took less than half an hour. Mara learned its shape afterward, in pieces—not from eavesdropping on the library itself, which even he would not risk with Halloran, but from the debris the meeting left behind. Edith's voice through two feet of oak door, level throughout, rising once at the end in something that might have been laughter if laughter could cut glass. Then chairs. Then Halloran in the hall, pulling on his gloves finger by finger, taking longer about it than gloves require, giving her one more chance to call him back.

She did not call him back.

At four o'clock Mara was mending a harness strap in the coach house when the library door opened onto the terrace and Halloran came out into the failing light. Mara watched him from the dark of the doorway, a cup of lamp oil warming in his hands. The journalist stood a moment on the frozen gravel, breathing white, and then he did not take the garden path, and he did not take the drive. He went directly across the lawn toward the town road, walking with the unhurried gait of a man who has already sent his telegram and is merely catching up to it.

Mara set down the oil. His hands were steady—he had trained them to be steady even when everything behind them was running—but his heart had begun the cold, quick work of arithmetic. The ice was thinning. The patrols were coming. And now this.

He crossed the yard to the kitchen door and let himself in through the scullery, where Lizzie the maid was scrubbing pots and pretending, as always, not to see him arrive from rooms he should not have been leaving. He took the back stairs. In the upstairs corridor he found Edith standing at the window of her sitting room, still dressed, still composed, looking down at the lawn where the journalist's footprints were already filling with meltwater.

"Well?" she said.

"He took the town road."

"Yes." She said it the way one confirms a diagnosis already suspected. "He offered me the story, Mara. The whole movement—the names, the routes, the money, the women who've trusted me with their livelihoods and in some cases their liberty. Everything in my desk drawers and my ledgers and the heads of forty brave, foolish girls. He'll bury the piece about your Mr. Reval. He'll bury the magistrate's interest, the searches, all of it. In exchange he wants to be first. Exclusive access. 'The inside history of the movement,' he called it, written by him, approved by no one, published when he chooses." Her mouth thinned. "He said it would be a kindness."

"And you told him—"

"I told him that my cause has survived thirty years of men offering to help it for a price, and that it will survive him." She turned from the window. For a moment, just a moment, the composure showed its seams—the fatigue beneath, the plain arithmetic of a woman who had calculated exactly what this refusal might cost and had paid it anyway. "I want you to understand what I've done, Mara. Because you're the one who taught me that nothing in this house is free, least of all information. I have chosen the safety of forty women over the safety of one guest. If Halloran keeps his word—and men like him keep their worst words best—that choice will bring soldiers to my door within the week."

"Anton isn't your responsibility," Mara said quietly. "He's mine."

Edith looked at him for a long moment, and something moved behind her eyes—not surprise, exactly. Recalculation. "No," she said. "The day I let that man sit at my table knowing what his own government would do to him, he became my responsibility. That is what hospitality means when it costs something. Otherwise it's only theater with spoons." She picked up her shawl. "Go and tell him. All of it. He should hear it from us before he hears it from a patrol."

* * *

Anton Reval took the news standing at the window of the blue room, where he had spent most of the winter pretending to read books in languages he claimed were Russian but which Mara, glancing upside-down across the parlor, suspected were Latvian. He listened without interrupting—which was characteristic. Two years earlier, on the ferry dock at Riga, Mara had noticed that quality in him first: a man who absorbed bad information the way stone absorbs rain, showing nothing on the surface while the interior slowly changed.

"So," Anton said at last. His English had improved all winter, losing its hesitations the way his color had returned, degree by degree, in the warmth of the house. "Soldiers at the bridge when it opens. Patrols on the river road. And the ice—" He stopped. Looked at the window as if the river were visible from here, which it was not, which was somehow worse. "The ice will not hold past March. Perhaps not past the end of February now."

"The warm spell—"

"Will return. Warm spells always return. That is what spring is: warm spells returning until the water wins." He turned. In the lamplight his face was very still. "You should not have involved yourself, Mara. I have thought all winter that I should tell you so, and all winter I have been too selfish to say it."

"You didn't involve me. I involved myself. On a ferry dock, in peacetime, over a stack of letters you couldn't post." Mara kept his voice light, because heaviness between the two of them had become, over the winter, a thing neither dared touch directly—too much lived underneath it. "I seem to recall making a speech about it. You laughed at my accent."

"I laugh at many things that frighten me." Anton sat down heavily on the edge of the bed, and for a moment the stillness cracked and Mara saw the exhaustion beneath it, months of it, a man who slept badly and ate politely and jumped, every evening, when the front door slammed. "Listen to me. If Halloran has gone to the magistrate, then there will be men watching the crossings within days. But there is something else, something you cannot know. Before the bridge closed, I received word through the trade agent in town. There is a warrant now. Not for questions—for extradition papers, drawn formally. If they take me, there is no interrogation and release. There is a train east, and after the train—" He spread his hands. A small gesture, final, like a book closing.

Mara stood very still. Outside, somewhere down the corridor, the niece—Clara—was practicing scales on the piano, badly, the sound drifting through the walls like weather. "Then we move sooner," he said.

"Sooner." Anton smiled, and it was a terrible smile, fond and hopeless at once. "On what? The river is rotting. The road will have patrols. The bridge is chained and guarded by ice itself until April."

"There's the mill door, and the north woods, and the shepherd's track above the gorge, and a dozen ways down to the water that no patrol from the town road will think to watch before dawn." Mara heard his own voice going flat and practical, the coachman's voice, the invisible man's voice, and was grateful for it. "I've walked them all winter. Every Sunday. Nobody asks where the coachman walks."

For a moment Anton said nothing. Then: "You have been planning since autumn."

"I plan things. It's what I'm for."

"Mara." Anton rose, and crossed the small room, and took him by both shoulders—an intimacy that had been accumulating between them all winter in half-finished sentences and passed candles and maps drawn in the frost of windowpanes, and which neither of them had ever permitted to become a sentence. "If this goes wrong, it will not go wrong gently. Whatever they do to me, they will do worse to the man they catch helping me. You understand? To them I am a spy, and spies have helpers, and helpers hang too."

"I know what helpers do," Mara said. "I've been one for two years."

* * *

That night the frost came back hard, snapping down over the softened world like a lid. By morning the meltwater in Halloran's footprints had frozen into small gray mirrors along the lawn, and the river—when Mara drove Edith's sleigh down to the landing on the pretext of checking the boat tackle—had refrozen at its edges, but wrongly, the new ice cloudy and thin over black water that breathed beneath it. He crouched at the shelf edge and pressed his palm flat against it and felt, faintly, unmistakably, the current moving underneath. The river had tasted freedom again. It would not forget.

It was on the drive back that he saw the sled tracks.

They came off the town road a half mile below the house—two runners, heavy load, horses shod for ice—and turned up the lane that circled behind Halloran's cottage and rejoined the main road beyond. No destination in themselves. Just a loop. Just a man being shown the ground. Mara pulled the sleigh to a stop and sat with the reins loose in his hands and looked at the tracks for a long minute, reading them the way he read letters: not for what they said but for what they revealed. The loop had paused twice at the top of the rise, where a man with field glasses could see the house, the coach house, the mill door, and—the whole northern sweep of it—the riverbank below the willows, where the crossing would have to happen.

Someone was learning the ground. Which meant someone expected to use it.

By the time they reached the stable yard, word had come up from the village with the fish boy, breathless and pleased to be the carrier of catastrophe: telegraph wires humming all morning, a company of infantry ordered to the bridgehead to secure it for the spring reopening, and—this was the part the boy said twice, savoring it—mounted patrols on the river road beginning Thursday, twice daily, dawn and dusk.

Thursday. Three days.

Mara helped Edith down from the sleigh and held her hand a moment longer than propriety required, which for Edith Calloway was a language all its own. She looked at the sky, at the smoke standing straight up from the chimneys, at the frozen footprints on the lawn, and her face was the face she wore at the head of marches: bright, hard, and entirely without illusion.

"They mean to close every door but the ones they're standing in front of," she said. "Very well. Doors are my profession too, Mara. Find me tonight—we'll want the whole household at dinner Thursday evening, everyone seated, everyone seen, and we will give these gentlemen the longest evening of their lives." Her grip tightened on his arm, brief and fierce. "Whatever you're planning, whatever the two of you have drawn on scraps of paper in the dark—it happens Thursday night. While every eye in this house, including theirs, is fixed on me."

* * *

He found Anton in the coach house at dusk, uninvited, unsurprised—as if some current between them had carried the summons without words. Mara shut the door and lit the lamp and turned it low, and the two of them stood among the harnesses and the sleeping carriages while the frost thickened outside the small windows like a thing growing.

"Thursday night," Mara said. "Dinner at seven. Edith will hold the table as long as it takes—hours, if she must. She intends to be magnificent." He spread the map on the feed chest, the one they had drawn together over six weeks of candle ends: the mill door, the wood path, the shepherd's track, the willows. "We leave at half past seven, while the light's dying and the first patrol is settling in for the night. We're at the willows by ten. We cross at first safe dark."

Anton studied the map. His finger traced the route once, twice, and stopped at the river's line, where the ink showed the faintest tremor—Mara's hand, that night, not entirely steady.

"And the ice," Anton said quietly.

"The ice," Mara agreed, "is God's business, not ours. Ours is the getting there."

Above them, faint through the floorboards, Clara had begun her scales again. Below the willows, under cloud-blackened ice, the river moved in the dark toward the sea. And three days away, down the town road, a journalist was writing his final draft, and a magistrate was sharpening his patience, and soldiers were boarding trains for a bridge that would not open until spring—to make certain, whatever else happened, that no door in that house stayed closed unless they opened it themselves.
