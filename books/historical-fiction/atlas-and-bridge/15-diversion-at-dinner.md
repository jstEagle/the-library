## 15. Diversion at Dinner

The dinner was Edith's instrument, and she tuned it all day.

Mara felt the house change pitch around him from the moment she rang for Mrs. Hobb and announced—clearly, so that the words would travel through the walls to every listening ear in the household—that she intended to give her stranded guests an evening worth remembering before the deep snows came. A dinner. Seven courses if the larder allowed it, five if it did not, and candles on every surface that would hold them.

By ten o'clock the kitchen smelled of browning butter and thyme, and the whole of Calloway House had turned itself inside out like a pocket being emptied for a search. Maids carried silver up from the cellar with the reverence of altar servers. Mr. Pryce, the magistrate, discovered his evening clothes had been pressed without his asking and found nothing to object to in that. Mr. Latch, the journalist, wandered the corridors sniffing for copy and finding only the smell of roast pheasant, which he wrote down anyway in his little book—the word *pheasant*, Mara noted, underlined twice, as though the bird might be a conspiracy.

It was beautiful, what Edith could do with other people's vanity. She had invited them to dinner the way a magician invites a volunteer onto the stage: warmly, publicly, and with no intention whatsoever of letting them see how the trick was done.

Mara spent the morning making the trick possible.

He drove into the village at first light with a list of plausible errands—lamp oil, a repair to the near-side harness, a parcel for the niece, Clemency, who had taken to ordering books she never read as a kind of penance—and he came back with those things and one more besides: a boy's sled, secondhand, its runners filed bright. It went into the coach house beneath sacking, beside a coil of rope and a lantern with a shuttered flame. The mill door key, which hung in the scullery among forty others indistinguishable to anyone but him, now hung there minus one tooth of its shaft, so that it sat loose in its lock and could be lifted silently from either side.

He did none of this hurriedly. Hurry was the enemy; hurry left fingerprints. He moved through the day the way he had learned to move through his whole life—at the speed of furniture, of wallpaper, of things that were noticed only in their absence.

And yet Anton's absence was already beginning to ache in the room where he still was.

Mara found him at noon in the conservatory, ostensibly reading. Frost ferned the glass panes behind him, and the light through them fell broken and white across his hands, and Mara thought—not for the first time, but with new urgency—that here was a man made almost entirely of long straight lines, drawn carefully by someone who had meant him to span something. His fingers rested on the open page without reading it.

"The ice reports," Mara said quietly. "I heard Latch asking after the post. Three days of thaw above freezing. Then cold again tonight."

"Then we go tomorrow night." Anton said it the way engineers say load-bearing facts. Not bravely. Arithmetically.

"Tomorrow night," Mara agreed, and hated how easily the words came out, and hated more the small traitorous voice beneath them that whispered *good*, because some part of him had hoped the weather would refuse.

Anton looked up. He had a way, when he looked at you directly, of making invisibility feel like a coat you'd been wearing so long you'd forgotten you could take it off. "You have done everything," he said. "Every step. I have counted them. Sled, rope, the mill door, the route by the osier beds—"

"Anyone could have done it."

"No." Flatly. "No one else could have done it. That is not the same thing, and you know it is not."

Mara straightened a watering can that did not need straightening. Outside, a jackdaw crossed the white garden like a thrown stone. "Tonight you'll sit at that table," he said, "and you'll eat seven courses and answer Latch's questions about bridges in the abstract, and you will not look at me once. Can you do that?"

"I have been not looking at you for two months," Anton said. "It is the hardest thing I have done since I left home."

Mara left before his face could do anything further to him. There was no time for that. There was dinner to stage and a river to cross and a promise to keep, and feelings were cargo too—heavy, shifting cargo, best stowed deep where they couldn't capsize anything.

* * *

At seven o'clock the dining room burned like a ship at sea.

Edith had commanded the candelabra brought up from the chapel—two dozen flames doubled in the polished mahogany—and the effect was exactly calculated: abundance, confidence, a woman so secure in her own house that she could afford to burn wax like a lord. The magistrate's eyes kept going to the candles instead of to the guests, which was the point. A man cannot watch everyone when he is being shown what to watch.

Edith sat at the head in garnet silk, and Mara, serving, understood—as he ladled soup and poured the hock—that she had dressed for war and chosen beauty as the uniform because it disarmed. Mr. Pryce in his black alpaca kept adjusting his napkin as though it were evidence against him. Latch had positioned himself to her right and was conducting his interrogation disguised as charm.

"And the movement, Mrs. Calloway—you must allow a plain man his curiosity. All these ladies marching, speaking, chaining themselves to railings. To what end? Parliament is not a bridge. It cannot simply be *built*."

"Parliament is worse than a bridge, Mr. Latch," Edith said serenely, "because a bridge carries whoever wishes to cross, whereas Parliament decides who counts as traffic. But we needn't speak of work. Tonight we are merely people trapped together by weather, eating better than we deserve." Her glance swept the table with such frank affection that even the magistrate warmed by a degree. That was the other thing about Edith: she could make captivity feel like hospitality, and hospitality feel like an obligation, and obligations were her native currency.

Clemency sat opposite her aunt, pale as the table linen. She had barely touched her soup. Twice her eyes went to the door, and twice Mara watched the magistrate watch them go there, and he understood that whatever Clemency had given away—the letter passed in the corridor, glimpsed weeks ago, confessed to Pryce in a fit of terrified virtue—was still sitting in her like a swallowed pin.

He served the fish. He refilled glasses. He memorized the room the way he memorized everything: Pryce third from the left, drinking steadily, his file-mindedness loosening into something slower; Latch taking notes under the tablecloth like a schoolboy; the two remaining guests—Mrs. Aldous, a widow of formidable opinions about opera, and old Captain Meade, mostly deaf—performing the useful work of filling silence with noise.

At half past eight, per the plan, Mrs. Hobb would bring up the charlotte and Edith would produce the claret she had been saving for a victory that had not yet occurred, and the dinner would enter its long middle age, and Mara would slip out to "see to the horses," and the mill door would open on the dark, and Anton Reval would walk out of Calloway House and toward the frozen river and out of Mara's keeping forever.

He had known, going in, that the plan would cost this. He had told himself he accepted it. Standing in the corner of the burning room with the hock decanter in his hand, watching Anton laugh politely at something the captain had misheard, Mara admitted to himself that he had accepted nothing. He had only postponed arithmetic.

Anton did not look at him once. It was the hardest thing, he'd said. He did it anyway, flawlessly, and somehow that made it worse.

At twenty past eight, catastrophe arrived wearing an apron.

Mrs. Hobb appeared in the doorway with a face like a dropped plate, crossed to Mara under cover of the sideboard, and murmured: "Cook's burnt the charlotte. Properly burnt—smoke and all. She's near crying. We can put out cheese and fruit but there'll be a gap, and Mrs. Calloway said—"

"A gap won't do. Gaps are remembered." Mara was already moving. "Give me five minutes. Tell her the next course is delayed deliberately—a soufflé story, she'll understand soufflé stories—and keep the wine moving. Wine forgives everything."

He went down the service stair two steps at a time, into the heat and haze of the kitchen, where the charlotte lay smoking under a cloth like a corpse under a sheet, and rolled his sleeves, and did what invisible men do, which is become suddenly, indispensably visible at the exact moment required. He had cooked before. Cooking, like driving, like valeting, like reading other people's letters, belonged to the great family of skills belonging to people who must be competent at everything and matter to no one. In twenty minutes he had a trifle assembled from sponge, preserves, and the last of the cream, and it looked, if one squinted, intentional.

It was while he was carrying it up, both hands full, that he heard the voices in the passage ahead—Pryce and Clemency, stopped by the coat rack, their tones dropped low.

"—only thought you should know I saw the motor car being loaded," Clemency was saying, wobbling between apology and resolve. "Sledge runners, or a sled, or—I don't know what. Under sacking. And the mill door key isn't where it lives. I notice things, sir. I'm sorry to notice things."

There was a silence in which Mara stood absolutely still in the dark of the stairs, the trifle warming his palms, his heart performing some slow enormous labor.

"You're a good girl, Miss Clemency," Pryce said at last, unctuous as oil on water. "Noticing is a virtue. Leave the rest to me."

Footsteps. The dining room door opened and shut, admitting a swell of candlelight and Edith's laughter, and closed again.

Mara came up out of the stairwell with the dessert and a face like a closed shop. He set the trifle down before Edith, and in passing behind her chair he spoke four words pitched precisely for her ear alone:

"Clemency. Magistrate. Sled."

Her fan did not pause. Her smile did not flicker. But he saw her hand settle flat upon the table, the way a hand settles on a map, and he knew she had redrawn the night's campaign in the space of a breath.

She rose, ringing her glass. "Ladies and gentlemen—a toast. To friendship, which winters well." And while every head lifted and every glass rose, Mara slipped from the room.

* * *

The scullery was dark and warm and smelled of boiled linen. Mara took the mutilated key from its hook, went down the passage, and let himself out the mill door into a night hard as hammered tin.

The stars were savage. Cold like this had texture; it took the skin off the inside of your nose. The sled waited under sacking by the wall, rope coiled atop it, the shuttered lantern hanging from his fist. He turned—and Anton was already there, coatless still, breath standing round his head like smoke from a doused fire, having come out the laundry window on the route they'd mapped, because the mill door's opening might be marked.

They did not embrace. They had never embraced. Anton gripped his forearm through the sleeve, engineer-fashion, testing the structure, and said, "The ice will hold me. Say you believe it will hold me."

"I've walked it twice to the far channel," Mara said. "It held me."

"You weigh less than I do."

"I weigh more than you think," Mara said. "I'm carrying things."

For a moment neither of them moved, and the cold pressed in around them like a crowd. Then Anton took the rope's end and began, wordlessly, to help load, and that was its own kind of vow.

They had the sled halfway to the osier beds when the beam of a lantern swung out from the stable yard behind them and caught the snow like a struck match.

Mara pushed Anton down behind the woodstack and turned to meet it, arranging his face into mild, apologetic competence—and it was not the magistrate who came crunching over the crusted snow, but Clemency, in her aunt's borrowed mackintosh, holding a lamp with a hand that shook so badly the light danced.

She saw him. She saw the sled. Her mouth worked.

"I told him," she said. "I already told him—this afternoon—I told him about the sled and the key, and then I couldn't—I sat through the fish course and I thought, they'll arrest him, they'll arrest *you*, Aunt Edith will be in the papers, and it will be because of *me*, because I was frightened, and I have been frightened my whole life and I am so tired—" Her voice cracked like river ice. "So I told him the key was lost. I told him I'd dreamed it, the sled, that I'd been confused, that I'd been reading sensation novels and imagined the whole thing. He believed me. Men always believe a woman is silly when silliness is offered." She laughed, high and awful. "But he'll check the mill door by morning. You have until morning. And everyone at that table will know I lied to a magistrate in his own jurisdiction, and my name will be mud from here to London, and I find—" she wiped her face with the back of her glove, furiously, "—I find I don't care. Is that what it feels like? Not caring?"

Mara looked at this girl he had dismissed for two months as nervous, silly, ornamental—looked at her standing between a magistrate and a fugitive with nothing but a borrowed mackintosh and her own remade conscience—and he could not speak for a moment.

"It's what it feels like," he said. "Exactly that. Go back by the kitchen garden. If anyone asks, you came out because the room was too hot."

"They'll ask," Clemency said. "Let them." She turned, then paused, lamp raised, and looked back at Anton, whom she had betrayed and unbetrayed without ever learning his name. "Good luck, whoever you are," she said. "Deliver your letters."

She went. The lamplight shrank down the garden path and vanished into the house, and somewhere beyond the dark fields the river lay waiting under its groaning skin of ice, three days of thaw already working at it like a rumor.

Anton rose from behind the woodstack. "Until morning," he said quietly.

Mara took up the rope. "Then we don't waste the night," he said, and turned his face toward the river, and began to run.
