## 16. The River Road

The mill door opened onto a wall of falling snow, and for one moment Mara almost laughed. Of all the nights God might have chosen to smother the county in white, He had chosen this one—He who had never once done Mara a kindness that Mara could trace.

"Blizzard," Anton said behind him, and the word carried both relief and dread.

"Blizzard," Mara agreed. "It hides us. It also hides them. And it will fill our tracks faster than we can make them—which is the only mercy in it." He pulled his collar up. "Stay close enough to touch me. If you fall behind by six feet in this, you're gone."

They went out into it.

The snow came down not in flakes but in sheets, in whole torn curtains of it, driven sideways by a wind that had crossed the entire river valley to find them. The world shrank to the circle of Mara's lantern, then to the shape of Anton's shoulder ahead of him when he doused the light, then to nothing at all but sound—the wind's long animal howl, the creak of the mill's abandoned machinery somewhere behind, and beneath everything, so low it might have been imagination, the mutter of the river under its ice.

Mara had planned this road for weeks without letting himself admit he was planning it. He had walked it by daylight with a gamebag and an innocent expression, counting fence posts, noting where the hedge thinned, where a drainage ditch ran invisible beneath the crust, where a copse of dead elms stood close enough together to break the wind. He knew the river road the way he knew the backs of envelopes: thoroughly, secretly, and for purposes no one would approve of.

Now he put that knowledge to work like a craftsman setting his hand to fine joinery.

They went first along the millrace, where the stone embankment held their scent low and the running water killed it altogether. Then—not toward the river, though every nerve in him strained toward it—but away, southeast across the home meadow, toward the shepherd's track that wound up into the hills before doubling back. Behind them, faint even through the storm, a dog began to bark.

* * *

Anton lasted the first hour on stubbornness alone.

Mara watched him the way he watched everything—with the peripheral, ungenerous attention of a man who survived by noticing. And what he noticed frightened him. Anton Reval, who had once stood straight as one of his own bridge piers, was listing to the left like a leaning tower. His breathing had a whistle in it. Twice already he had stumbled on flat ground, and twice he had risen with a speed that was worse than stumbling—it was the desperate agility of a man who knows exactly how much time his body has left and is trying to spend it all at once.

"The sledge," Anton said, when they paused in the lee of the elm copse. It wasn't a question about luggage. It was a man asking whether the thing he was leaning toward still existed.

"Half a mile past the Henderson place. Old man Henderson won't miss it; he hasn't walked past his own gate since November." Mara unwound his scarf and wound it around Anton's neck instead, over his objections, his fingers working the wool with brisk impersonality while something in his chest conducted itself entirely otherwise. "You'll ride it when we get there. That was always the plan."

"That was your plan. I agreed to none of it."

"You agreed to none of it out loud," Mara said. "Which in my experience changes nothing."

Anton made a sound that in a healthier man would have been a laugh. The wind took it away. Above them the sky was a churning absence, neither night nor cloud but simply white-on-black, and the snow came down through their little sheltering dark with the patience of something that intended to go on forever.

"They were at the house within twenty minutes of us leaving," Anton said quietly. "I saw lanterns from the top of the rise."

"I know."

"There were dogs."

"I know that too." Mara checked the sky out of habit, found nothing to check. "Dogs hate this weather worse than men do. They'll lose us at the millrace if they haven't already. What I'm buying now isn't escape. It's hours. Hours are all we need if I spend them correctly."

"And after the sledge?"

"After the sledge, the false trail. After the false trail, the frozen ford at Miller's Bend—no, don't argue, we're not crossing there, we're letting them think we did—and then the last two miles along the reed beds to the crossing." He said *the crossing* and felt the words land in his own stomach like swallowed ice. "You'll be on the other side by dawn."

Anton was silent. In the blue-black half-dark his face was only an arrangement of shadows, but Mara could feel him looking, the way you feel weather change.

"And you," Anton said.

* * *

The borrowed sledge changed everything and solved nothing.

It was a crude thing, Henderson's—a plank platform on iron-shod runners, built for hauling firewood down from the woodlot, left half-buried at the edge of the trees with its rope harness stiff and frozen into a posture of despair. Mara dug it out with bare hands while Anton leaned against a trunk and shook with cold that was no longer ordinary. When Mara touched him, pulling the harness straps straight, he felt the heat coming off the man like a stove door left open. Fever. Of course fever. A week of hiding in a linen closet of a room, eating fear, sleeping badly, and then this.

"You should have told me sooner," Mara said.

"Told you what? That my body has opinions?" Anton lowered himself onto the sledge with a care that admitted everything. "In R—" he caught himself, as he always caught himself before the name of the country that had erased him, "—at home, when the river freezes, we say the ice is making. You watch it for weeks. It talks all night. Cracks like rifle shots, and you learn which cracks mean strength and which mean weakness. My mother could read it. She'd send us out to skate on ice that made the English visitors go pale." His voice was slurring slightly at the edges, the words running together like wet ink. "I keep thinking about that tonight. About how much of my life has been listening to ice."

Mara hauled. The runners hissed over the crusted snow, and the rope bit through his gloves, and he set his shoulders against the weight of the man he was delivering and found, absurdly, that the labor was a kind of happiness. This was the closest he had ever stood to the center of his own life: pulling it forward through the dark, hand over hand.

Behind them, distantly, the dogs sang again—closer than they should have been.

* * *

The false trail took forty minutes and cost more than Mara wanted to pay.

At the fence line above Miller's Bend he stopped the sledge, broke a branch off a dead birch, and dragged it back and forth across the open snow until the ground looked churned and argued-over. Then he walked—deliberately, heavily, printing his boots deep—took off one glove and dropped it by the broken ice at the water's edge where the current steamed faintly in the starless dark, and turned back without looking at the black gap in the river's skin. He did not let himself imagine Anton going through that gap. He had spent his whole life refusing to imagine things; it was his one talent.

Then he led the sledge north along the fence line, on the far side of the hedge where the wind had scoured the snow to bare glassy ice and left no prints at all, until the hedge curved them back west toward the river.

It was there, in the long white silence between the dogs' cries, that Anton began to talk.

Not the way he had talked before—carefully, edited, the confession letter's contents parceled out in safe doses. The fever had taken his editor away. He spoke in his own language first, a low run of syllables Mara didn't know, and then translated himself piece by piece, as if pulling objects out of deep water.

"She was called Livia," he said. "My wife. The letters were never to her—that was the story, even in the letters themselves. Half-truths inside truths, because I knew the mail was read. The letters were to her family. Her mother. Her brother. Telling them she lived. Telling them where she lived." A pause, filled with wind. "Because I could not go myself. Because a man who has refused his country's forts cannot cross back into his country except in handcuffs."

Mara pulled the rope and said nothing, because he understood suddenly that he was being given something and must not drop it.

"In the last letter—the one you read, the one you shouldn't have read and I'm glad you read—I wrote what I am. Not what they say I am. They call me a spy. A traitor. A man who sold the shapes of bridges to enemies." His breath caught; Mara turned and found him half-slumped, eyes bright with fever, mouth still moving. "I refused. That is all I did. They wanted a fortification on the gorge road, guns set into the cliff, and I told them the rock would not hold it, which was true, and they told me to hold my tongue and draw the plans anyway, which I would not do. So I drew nothing. So I left. A bridge engineer whose crime is that he declined to build the opposite of a bridge." A sound came out of him that was nearly laughter and was mostly grief. "They will hang that word *traitor* around any neck it fits. It fits everyone. That is what makes it useful."

The reeds rose around them now, tall and snow-bent, whispering with one vast dry voice, and beyond them Mara could feel the river's presence the way you feel a wall in a dark room.

"Why her family?" Mara asked. "Why risk the border for letters her family could live without?"

"Because her mother believes she is dead," Anton said simply. "And a mother who believes her daughter is dead dies a little every day of it, and I have watched it happen through two winters of silence, and I decided that my safety was worth less than her knowing." He closed his eyes. "You asked me once, at the station in peacetime, why I trusted a stranger with them. I never answered properly. It was because you looked at the bundle and did not ask what was in it. Everyone else asks. You just carried."

Mara stood in the reeds with the rope slack in his hands and let the words arrive in him like weather. *You just carried.* Two years of erasing himself, of reading other people's lives and keeping none of his own, and here was a man telling him that this—this, the carrying itself—had been the whole of what mattered.

"Dogs," he said at last, because the dogs were singing again, and this time there was no mistaking the direction. Behind them. North. Off the false trail or through it—they had brought men who could read snow better than Mara had hoped.

"How far?" Anton's voice was thin.

"To the crossing? Under a mile. To dawn? Three hours too many." Mara took up the rope. "Get on the ice when I tell you and not one step before. And Anton—" He waited until the feverish eyes found him. "Livia's mother will know her daughter lives because of you. Whatever else happens on that river tonight, that part is already finished. Already true. Nothing behind us can unmake it."

For a moment the shadows of Anton's face rearranged themselves into something Mara had never seen on him and could not name, and then he lay back on the sledge, and the runners hissed, and they went on toward the river through the whispering reeds while the lanterns came swaying and golden through the storm behind them, patient as the ice, and closer.

* * *

They reached the bank as the eastern sky showed the first gray suggestion that dawn intended to happen whether or not anyone survived to see it.

The river lay before them vast and pale and wrong. Even Mara could see it now without any special knowledge—the whole surface was dimpled and blistered, ridged in long pressure seams, and from somewhere out in the middle came sounds he had heard only in nightmares: a slow groaning, as of some enormous animal turning over in its sleep, and underneath it a high thin singing, the note the ice makes in thaw, the note old men on the riverbank cross themselves at.

Anton sat up on the sledge and listened to it the way other men listen to music.

"It's making," he whispered. "It's talking all night." He turned to Mara, and his eyes were clear for the first time in hours, burning with fever and something else. "It will hold a walking man. Barely. It will not hold two walking men close together, and it will not hold anyone who stops." He gripped Mara's sleeve with terrible strength. "Listen to me. I go first, alone, fast, the way my mother taught us. You wait five hundred counts and follow or you don't. Those are the terms."

Behind them, down the reed-choked bank, a lantern flared, and a dog's bark cracked the air like the ice's own voice.

Mara looked at the river, and at the far bank where the snow lay smooth and unbroken toward the border, and at the man who had trusted him with a bundle of letters in peacetime because he hadn't asked what was in them. Then he looked back at the lanterns coming up through the storm, and understood—with the strange calm of a man stepping off a ledge—that he had never once in his life been visible until tonight, and that tonight he intended to remain so exactly as long as it suited him.

"Terms accepted," he said, and stripped the harness rope from the sledge, and coiled it in his fist. "But you'll carry this. Fifty feet of rope between us. If the ice takes either of us, it doesn't get to keep him."

Out on the river, the ice groaned, and sang, and held.
