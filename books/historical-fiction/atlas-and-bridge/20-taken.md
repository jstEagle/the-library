## 20. Taken

They took him on the ice, a quarter mile from the far shore, where the river had begun to speak.

Mara heard it before he saw the lanterns—the sound a frozen river makes when it decides to become water again, a deep groaning from beneath the feet, like some enormous animal turning over in its sleep. The cracks ran ahead of him in black seams, spidering, whispering. Behind him, Anton was a small dark figure almost to the opposite bank, and beyond Anton, on the shingle, stood the woman—alive, upright, wrapped in a shawl that caught the last of the moonlight like frost given fabric.

He had done it. That was the thought he carried into custody like a coal cupped in bare hands: *he had done it.* Whatever they took from him now, they could not take the arithmetic of the morning. Anton was across. The letters were delivered. The promise, made two years and a lifetime ago in a railway waiting room that smelled of coal smoke and orange peel, was kept.

"Stop where you are!"

The voice came flat across the ice, stripped by wind of everything but its authority. Mara stopped. It seemed important, somehow, to stop cleanly—to give them nothing fumbling, nothing frightened. He turned slowly, hands away from his body, and counted three lanterns bobbing on the near bank with the patrol, their light smeared and doubled by the river fog rising off the breaking ice.

Two constables came out onto the surface, walking wide apart, distributing their weight the way countrymen do, and Mara understood with distant interest that they were more afraid than he was. Their boots crackled on the rime. One of them kept glancing down between his own feet as if expecting the river to open its eye.

"You," said the nearer one. "You're off Calloway's place. The driver."

"I am."

"Where's the other one?"

Mara looked at him—a long, level, unremarkable look, the look of a man who has spent years perfecting the art of being forgettable—and said, "What other one?"

The constable shifted. Behind them, on the far shore, something bright and small happened: the woman raised her arm, and Anton raised his, and for a moment the two figures made a single shape against the pale sky, joined at a distance by nothing but morning air and a dying river. Then the constable turned to see what Mara was looking at, and Mara let his face go empty, and the moment closed over like water over a dropped stone.

"That's him, isn't it. Reval."

"I couldn't say, sir. I've never seen the gentleman before in my life."

The second constable laughed without humor. "Never seen him. Walked out on the ice at dawn alone, did you? For your health?"

"For the view," Mara said.

The first blow, when it came, was open-handed and almost courteous, a backhand across the mouth meant less to hurt than to introduce. Mara tasted copper and salt and felt his lip split against his teeth. He did not fall. The ice groaned beneath all four of them, a long descending note, and everyone froze—even the law—in the ancient animal deference to a sound like that.

"All right," the constable said quietly. "All right. Hands behind your back."

They bound him and walked him back across the river he had just failed to cross, and Mara kept his eyes forward, because if he looked once more at the far bank he would not be able to keep his face empty, and an empty face was the only luggage he could carry where he was going.

* * *

The magistrate's office in the county town occupied the upper floor of the old customs house, a room built for the inspection of goods and still smelling faintly of tar and hemp rope, as though contraband had soaked into the very timber. They put Mara in a chair by the window, where the grey winter light fell full on his split lip and frost-burned cheeks, and left him there for an hour with nothing but the clock and the smell of tar for company.

It was a technique, and he knew it, and knowing it did not make it less effective. The silence worked on a man like water on a knot. By the time the magistrate came up the stairs—Mara heard the deliberate unhurried weight of him, the pause at the landing, the small ceremony of removing gloves—he had already rehearsed and discarded eleven openings.

Mr. Havelock—Mara knew his name the way he knew every name that crossed Edith Calloway's correspondence—was smaller than his reputation. A neat man, silver-haired, with the patient eyes of someone who had spent thirty years listening to liars and had come to find them restful, like rain on a roof. He hung his coat. He warmed his hands at the stove. He poured two cups of tea from the pot a clerk had left, and set one before his prisoner, and sat down, and drank, and said nothing for another full minute.

Then: "Your name."

"Mara Winterbourne. I drive for Mrs. Calloway."

"Yes." Havelock set down his cup. "You do. Six years, is it? Excellent references. Character witnesses. A man entirely without history before 1907—that's rare, Mr. Winterbourne. Most men have too much history. You have precisely none." He opened a folder on the desk, and Mara watched—noted, catalogued, filed—the thickness of it. Telegrams. A page in a clerk's hand that might be the niece's statement. Another that was certainly the journalist's, written in that self-admiring slant. "Here is what I have, and I'll tell you plainly, because I find plainness loosens people. At dawn today a foreign national crossed the frozen river into territory of a power with which we may shortly be at war. He was assisted. The assistance originated at Mrs. Calloway's house. A bundle of letters in a foreign hand was found—or rather, was *not* found, which is itself instructive—during a search of the premises. And you, the invisible man, the man who reads the post before she does—" Havelock smiled at whatever passed through Mara's face, "—yes, we know about that too; the magistrate keeps files, Mr. Winterbourne, it is the whole of my profession—you were seen leaving the mill door at midnight and returning to the river at dawn. So. Your name."

"Mara Winterbourne."

Havelock nodded, unsurprised, and turned a page. "Then let us speak of consequences instead, since names are shy this morning. The statute covering the harboring and conveyance of an enemy national carries a term of years. That falls on whoever assisted. But conspiracy—" he tapped the folder, gently, like a man tapping a melon for ripeness, "—conspiracy is a wider net. It catches whoever financed the venture. Whoever planned it. Whosever house served as its base of operations." He looked up. "Mrs. Calloway is a woman of national consequence, Mr. Winterbourne. Her cause has powerful friends, and more powerful enemies, and I am, at present, undecided which category I belong to. Your testimony would decide it."

And there it was, laid out on the tar-scented air between them: not justice, arithmetic. The magistrate did not want Anton; Anton was across the river and beyond him forever. He wanted Edith. He wanted the suffragists. He wanted the scandal that would fold a movement into a courtroom and keep it there through the war that was coming, folded and filed and forgotten.

Mara looked at the tea going cold before him, and at the window, where beyond the glass the river was visible, a thin white seam stitched through the brown fields, and he understood that he had been walking toward this chair for six years—perhaps for longer, perhaps since the night in another country when he had buried a name under a railway platform and boarded a train as no one at all.

"Herr Reval acted alone," Mara said. "As far as Mrs. Calloway is concerned, I deceived her thoroughly. She believes I drove to see my sister."

"She'll swear to that, will she?"

"She'll swear to what she believes, which is better. Belief doesn't wobble under oath."

"And the letters?"

"Were mine to carry. A private promise, made years ago, to a man I met before anyone had declared him anything. I broke no law I knew of when I made it. When I kept it, I knew, and I accept the term of years. Alone."

Havelock studied him for a while with those patient eyes, and then he did something unexpected: he laughed, softly, without malice. "Do you know how many men have sat in that chair and offered me themselves? Offered me the whole crime, wrapped in paper, if I'd only leave someone else out of it? Every one of them lying. Every one of them certain I'd never checked." He closed the folder. "But you—I believe you'd take the years. That's the trouble. Belief isn't evidence, and I'm a man who deals in evidence. What I need is the thing underneath. Who are you, Mr. Winterbourne? What am I holding?"

* * *

The cell was cold but not cruelly so, and they left him alone with the evening, and with the river-sound carried faintly even here, thirty miles upstream—the ice was going out all along the valley, the lock-keepers said; it was going out early and all at once, the whole winter letting go in a single night, as if it had only been waiting for permission.

Anton was across. He was across, and the woman was alive, and somewhere on the far shore, in a language Mara did not speak, a family was reading six years of letters by lamplight.

That was the coal in his hands. He cupped it. He breathed on it.

He thought about names. He had worn three in his life, and buried two, and the strange thing—sitting on the plank bed with his split lip throbbing in time with his heart—was how lightly the third sat on him now, how obviously it had always been a coat borrowed against weather rather than a skin. *Mara Winterbourne.* A name chosen in ten minutes for its dullness, its utter refusal to mean anything. He had guarded it the way misers guard coin, and for what? So that no one could find him. So that no one could ever again connect him to—

To the forgery. To the desperate, brilliant, stupid season when he was nineteen and could copy any hand set before him and had discovered that banks, like magistrates, deal in evidence, and evidence can be made. He had gone to prison for eighteen months for it, under his true name, in a jurisdiction that had since changed flags twice. He had come out into a world that had forgotten him, and he had made himself a man whom forgetting suited.

Anonymity. It had been the whole of his estate. The wealth of the invisible man is invisibility itself, and he had hoarded it past reason, past love, past the reach of any hand that had ever reached for him. Edith knew it—Edith, who read her own mail for thirty years and suspected him and said nothing because competence is rare; Edith, who in the end had hidden Anton's letters inside her own contraband, contraband within contraband, without being asked. She had traded on his invisibility for six years and never once asked what it cost him to live inside it.

Now the magistrate wanted to know what he was holding. And the answer, Mara saw with something close to relief, was: *nothing, unless I choose to fill his hands.*

Because Havelock had said it himself, in his neat tar-and-rope office: belief is not evidence. But a name—a true name, freely given, with a record attached in a foreign registry—is evidence of a kind no folder can manufacture. It proved motive. It proved that Mara Winterbourne had reasons of his own, a fugitive's logic, a buried man's hunger to be owed something by the world. It made the story *his*, sealed with the one seal he possessed. And a confession that costs the confessor everything is the only kind magistrates believe.

More than that: it would show Edith's enemies that the case against her rested on the word of a servant with a forged life—and servants with forged lives make poor crown witnesses. Give Havelock the name, and the name would devour the case. His anonymity for her safety. The exchange rate was ruinous and exact.

He heard the boots on the stairs well before midnight—heavy boots, and lighter ones beside them, and the jingle of keys—and he stood up and straightened his collar, because whatever else was taken from him in the next hour, he would not be taken slouched.

The key turned. The lamp in the corridor threw Havelock's shadow long across the cell floor, and behind the magistrate stood a constable with a writing board, and beyond them, faintly, the river went on letting go of its winter, ice cracking and booming in the dark like distant cannon, like a world ending, like a world beginning.

"You've had time to think," Havelock said. He came in and stood, declining the stool the constable offered, as though the room were his drawing-room and courtesy were simply the local custom. "I'll ask once more, and I'll ask it as a favor between professionals. Who are you?"

Mara lifted his head. His lip had scabbed and would scar; he found he didn't mind. Some things deserved a mark.

"My name," he said, and heard his own voice steady in the cold, "is Elias Marchetti. Born in Trieste. Convicted of forgery in 1903, served eighteen months. I changed my name on the day of my release and I have been no one worth finding for six years—which is precisely why Mrs. Calloway hired me, though she doesn't know it, and why everything Herr Reval did passed through my hands and no one else's. Arrest me for what I am. I'll sign anything you put in front of me. But the lady in that house hired a driver, not a conspiracy, and the sooner you write that down, the sooner we both get some sleep."

The silence afterward had texture, like felt. Havelock regarded him for a long moment with an expression that was almost—almost—respect, the way one workman respects another's tools.

"Elias Marchetti," the constable repeated, writing it down, and the name sounded in the cell like a bell rung over deep water, like something surfacing after years, and Mara—Elias—felt it leave him entirely, felt the last door of his old hiding place swing open onto a corridor with no corners in it.

"Trieste," Havelock mused. "That will want verifying. Papers, registries, the Austrian consulate. Weeks, possibly." Something moved behind the patient eyes—not doubt, exactly; recalculation. "You understand that a signed confession naming yourself does not end my interest in that house."

"It ends your evidence," Elias said. "Which is the same thing, in your profession."

Havelock almost smiled. He turned to go, and then paused at the door with his hand on the frame, and the lamplight caught the silver of his hair and the sudden sharpness of his attention.

"One more thing, Mr. Marchetti. A carriage arrived at the county jail twenty minutes ago. Black, closed, four horses, driven like the devil owned them. There is a lady in my outer office who informs my clerk that she represents Mrs. Edith Calloway's interests, that she has brought counsel, and two newspapermen, and a solicitor's letter I am going to enjoy reading very much—" he glanced back, and now he did smile, thinly, the smile of a man who has just seen the size of the wave, "—and that she will wait until morning if she must, but that I should know she is here. Shall I tell her you're awake?"
