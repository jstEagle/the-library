## 21. Edith's Answer

They took my coat when they arrested me. It was not cruelty; the sergeant was almost gentle about it, folding it over his arm as though I were a guest who had stayed too long at someone else's house and could not be blamed for the hour. But without the coat I felt the shape of myself differently—thinner, more legible, a man made of outlines that anyone might trace with a finger.

The cell in the county courthouse smelled of wet stone and lamp oil. There was one window, high and barred, through which I could see a slice of sky going from iron to pearl to the pale gold of late morning, and I understood that they did not intend to question me before noon. Interrogation, like everything else in this country, kept civilized hours.

I sat on the plank bed and rehearsed the thing I had promised myself I would do: give them the name. Not Winterbourne—the other one, the true one, the one I had buried under six winters of snow and honest work. I turned it over in my mouth silently, testing its weight the way a man tests a coin he has carried so long he has forgotten what it bought. *Mara Aldous Fenwick.* Once of Harrowgate. Once guilty of forgery, of flight, of letting another man stand trial for a debt that was mine.

If ruin was coming, it would come to that name and no other. Edith Calloway would be a woman deceived by an excellent servant. Anton Reval would be a foreigner smuggled across ice by a criminal acting alone. The arithmetic was simple. I had spent years learning to erase myself from every room I entered; now, at last, there was a use for it.

The lock turned at half past eleven.

The magistrate came in himself—Hollis, with his grey whiskers and his careful voice, carrying a leather folder the way a clergyman carries a prayer book. He sat opposite me on the one stool, arranged his knees, and regarded me with the weary patience of a man who had done this many times and found it tedious every time but necessary.

"Mara Winterbourne," he said. "Or whoever you are."

"Whoever I am," I agreed.

"You drove Mrs. Calloway for four years. You read her letters—I know you read her letters, don't trouble to deny it, the girl saw you passing correspondence to Reval and the household confirms your habits of discretion." He opened the folder. "You carried an enemy alien across a frozen river in wartime conditions, past a patrol, using methods that suggest either training or long practice at deception. Which is it, Mr. Winterbourne? Or shall I keep saying *mister* until you correct me?"

I looked at my hands. They were still red from the river; two fingernails had gone black where the ice had taken hold of me. I thought of Anton on the far bank, small against the dawn, and the figure that had run to him through the standing reeds—a woman alive after all, her arms around him while the patrol's lanterns bloomed behind us like feverish flowers. I thought of the moment I had turned back, one foot on the near shore again, and seen Anton's face across the widening black water. He had shouted my name. My false name. It had sounded, in his mouth, like the truest thing I owned.

"I'll tell you everything," I said. "On one condition. Nothing touches Mrs. Calloway. She knew nothing. Deceived me as thoroughly as I deceived her—I forged her signature twice, once for feed invoices, once for a railway pass. She is an old woman whose trust I abused."

Hollis smiled faintly, the smile of a man listening to a piece of music he already knew by heart. "And your real name?"

"Fenwick," I said. "Mara Aldous Fenwick. Late of Harrowgate. Wanted since 1908 for forgery and abscondment." Saying it aloud was strange—not painful, exactly, but like stepping out of doors after a long illness. The air was colder than I expected and cleaner too.

He wrote it down. His pen scratched like a mouse in a wall.

"And Reval," he said, still writing. "You knew him before. Letters. A woman across the border. All of it prearranged, I imagine—a spy's network, laid down years ago in peacetime, waiting for the war to ripen it. That is how I will write it, Mr. Fenwick, unless you give me something better."

"That is not—" I began, and stopped, because the door opened behind him.

I have tried, since, to describe to myself the sound of Edith Calloway entering a room she had not been invited into. It was not loudness. It was weather. A pressure change, a shifting of the light. She came in wearing her dark green travelling coat and a hat with no ornament on it whatsoever, followed by two men in city clothes—one elderly and dry as a ledger, one young and quick-eyed—and behind them a woman with a notebook already open, pencil moving before anyone had said a word.

"Mr. Hollis," Edith said, "you will forgive me. Your clerk assured me you were occupied, and I have never let a small obstruction stop me from anything."

"Mrs. Calloway." The magistrate rose, which told me more than his face did. "This is a police matter. I must ask—"

"You must ask nothing yet. Sit down, Mr. Hollis; my legs are seventy-one years old and I intend to use yours instead." She did not sit herself. She stood in the middle of the cell as if it were her own coach house, and looked at me once—only once—a look I am still deciphering. Then she turned back to Hollis and the room rearranged itself around her.

"This," she said, touching the elderly man's sleeve, "is Sir Edmund Grieve, of Grieve, Grieve, and Ashworth, who has forgotten more law than the Home Office remembers. This young man is Mr. Pellow, of the same firm. And this"—a nod toward the woman with the notebook—"is Miss Verity Crane, who writes for the *Clarion*, and who is, at this moment, taking down every word you say, Mr. Hollis, including, I hope, whatever you were about to say about networks and spies."

Hollis's folder closed with a soft, deliberate sound, like a door being shut on something.

"Mrs. Calloway, the man in this cell has confessed to forgery—"

"He has confessed to being *Mara Fenwick*, which I dare say is true, and to forging two signatures, which I doubt, because I have audited my own accounts every quarter for thirty years and my coachman could no more forge my hand than fly to the moon. But we needn't argue it. We needn't argue any of it." She drew a folded paper from her coat. "Because here is what happens instead."

* * *

What happened instead filled three hours and, eventually, most of a week's newspapers.

Edith unfolded her paper and read aloud in the level, unhurried voice she used for reading resolutions at meetings, the voice that made even fury sound like bookkeeping. Anton Reval—she gave his full name, his proper name, and I heard Hollis's pen go still—was not a spy. He was a civil engineer, decorated, published, consulted by governments on both sides of three borders, and in the spring of 1911 he had been offered a commission to design fortifications above a river valley whose towns he had grown up in. He had refused. In writing. The refusal was in the folder now, along with the commission, along with affidavits from two colleagues and a minister's assistant who had since fled to Amsterdam.

"For refusing," Edith said, "he lost his position, his passport was invalidated, and his wife was detained for questioning—repeatedly, over eighteen months, until her health broke and she was moved to her sister's house across the river to be nursed. Her husband crossed that border illegally four times trying to reach her before the crossings closed. Every letter he wrote went through intermediaries. One of those intermediaries," she said, and her eyes touched me again, briefly, like sunlight off water, "was my coachman, who made a promise in peacetime and kept it in war. If that is a crime, Mr. Hollis, then I have misread the scripture, and I attended chapel for fifty years."

"The man is a national of an unfriendly power," Hollis said. "That is not opinion, madam, it is declaration—"

"Declared by whom?" And here Edith's voice changed, just slightly, the way a key changes in music—one note lower, and suddenly the whole melody means something else. "By the same department, Mr. Hollis, that certified the Kesselbach bridge designs as sound? Designs your office accepted in 1909? Designed by Anton Reval?" She let that sit in the cold air of the cell. "The bridge your own county drives across every day of its life was drawn by the hand you are calling an enemy. Half the magistracy of this province has stood on his ironwork and praised its engineering. I have photographs. Sir Edmund has copies. Miss Crane has copies."

Miss Crane's pencil had not stopped moving once.

I watched Hollis understand it. It came over him slowly, like frost forming on a window—not dramatic, not sudden, but total. He was not a stupid man; I had counted him dangerous precisely because he was not stupid. He saw it all in perhaps ten seconds: the headlines (*Bridge Builder Barred, Now Branded*), the questions in the press about why a county that trusted Reval with its river now called him a spy, the awkward fact that the magistrate's own office held files praising the man's work, the photograph that would surely be found of Hollis himself, younger, whiskered, shaking Reval's hand beneath a banner at the opening ceremony. I had not known such a photograph existed. Edith had known. She had been moving chess pieces since November, quietly, while the rest of us thought we were playing the game.

"There is also," she added, almost as an afterthought, "the matter of what Mr. Reval refused to build. Fortifications overlooking civilian towns. I have the refusal letter. Miss Crane will have it printed in full by Thursday. The public, Mr. Hollis, has a curious appetite this year for men who decline to aim guns at children. I cannot explain it. Perhaps the times."

"Mrs. Calloway." Hollis's voice had lost its careful architecture; underneath it he sounded tired, and older than his whiskers suggested. "You are threatening me with the press."

"I am informing you of the weather," said Edith. "The press does what it does. I merely decline to stand out in it unprotected. Now. The charges against this man."

Hollis looked at me. I looked back at him, and I gave him nothing—I had spent four years giving rooms nothing—and perhaps that decided him, or perhaps it was the pencil still whispering in Miss Crane's hands, or the photograph he was already seeing of himself on Reval's ironwork, smiling, shaking the enemy's hand.

"The matter," he said, "will require review. In light of the documentation provided. The foreign aspect especially may fall outside county jurisdiction entirely." Each sentence cost him something; I watched him pay. "The prisoner can be released pending review. On recognizance."

"On recognizance," Edith repeated, as though tasting it. "Accepted. Sir Edmund will wait for the paperwork."

* * *

They walked me out through the courthouse corridor at two in the afternoon, past the notice boards and the smell of floor wax, and the doors opened onto a street rinsed clean by morning rain and shining now with weak sun. Edith waited by the motorcar with her hands folded. Sir Edmund and Mr. Pellow had gone ahead to deal with papers. Miss Crane had gone, I suspected, to make Thursday very difficult for somebody.

Edith opened the car door herself and did not get in.

"Well, Fenwick," she said.

"Ma'am."

"Don't *ma'am* me. Get in. We have forty minutes of road and I intend to spend them deciding what to do with you, and you will speak when spoken to."

I got in. She directed me—of course she did; it was her car, whatever the legal fictions—and I drove us out of town along the river road, the same road I had raced down eleven nights ago with the patrol lanterns swinging behind us, the river visible now between bare trees, running black and broken, the last rotting pans drifting seaward. Spring was doing its slow work. The world smelled of mud and thaw and something green underneath it not yet arrived.

For two miles she said nothing. I felt her looking at the side of my face the way she looked at ledgers.

"Anton telegraphed from the far side," she said at last. "Short message. Two words. *Safe. Owing.*" A pause. "To whom do you suppose he owes something?"

"To the woman he reached, I'd imagine."

"Mm," said Edith, in the tone she used when a witness was lying badly. "His wife is recovering, by the way. Miss Crane confirmed it through her sister-paper across the border. The sister wrote that the letters arrived all winter—that there were twenty-two of them, kept in order, read aloud to her when she couldn't hold them. Twenty-two letters, Mr. Fenwick. You carried every one of them and never knew if she lived to receive the last." She looked out at the river. "I confess I find that harder to punish than anything Hollis had in his folder."

"You're not punishing me?"

"I haven't decided. Don't interrupt." We crossed the little stone culvert where the mill stream ran under the road, and I slowed the car without thinking, and I knew she noticed. "Here is what I know about you, Fenwick. You fled a crime committed when you were nineteen years old—a crime of desperation involving a dead father's debts and a partner who escaped scot-free because he had family to vouch for him. Sir Edmund found the trial records yesterday afternoon; it took him ninety minutes. Six winters hiding, and the law could have found you in ninety minutes at any time. Do you understand what that means?"

I drove for a while before answering. "That I hid from something that wasn't really looking."

"It means you were never invisible, dear boy. You were merely unexamined. There is a difference, and it matters, because I have examined you for four years and I knew within a month that there was a man under the livery. I did not know his name. I did not need to. I needed someone who could read a balance sheet, handle horses on ice, and keep his mouth shut, and I had all three in one coat." She straightened her glove with a small precise tug. "Competence is rare, Fenwick. Loyalty is rarer. I watched you walk onto breaking ice for a promise made in peacetime to a foreigner nobody in this county would spit on. I am seventy-one years old and I have campaigned with women who preached sacrifice from drawing rooms and ran at the first constable's whistle. You are the genuine article and the law calls you a forger. The law is frequently right about facts and wrong about nearly everything else."

"Sir Edmund could get the conviction set aside," I said carefully. "The co-signer, the circumstances—it was a bad trial—"

"Sir Edmund could get you a pardon, a new trade, and a column of apologies in the papers," Edith agreed. "And your name in every broadsheet in the province by Friday. *Ex-convict coachman saves enemy engineer.* They would love it. They would print your face." She turned and looked at me fully then, and there was something in her expression I had never seen there—not command, not calculation, but a kind of fierce, unsentimental tenderness that frightened me more than the patrol had. "Which is why I am offering you the opposite, and you will take it, because it is what you actually want and you are too proud to ask. No pardon. No publicity. Sir Edmund seals the old file—quietly, permanently, it can be done, it costs money and I have money—and Mara Fenwick stays where I found him. Buried. Alive. Available."

"Available," I repeated.

"For work. If you want it. Not mine—" and here she raised a hand before I could speak— "not driving for me. That chapter is finished; you know it and I know it; I will not have the woman who pays your wages also holding your sealed record, that is too much power for anyone to hold over anyone, and I include myself. I have made inquiries. The Halloran school, up past Millbrook, wants a man who can manage their estate office and drive their ambulance wagon—they take girls from the mills, Fenwick, girls who arrive with nothing, and teach them letters and arithmetic and how to refuse a foreman. They want someone discreet. Someone who reads things properly and forgets what doesn't concern him." She faced forward again, smoothing her skirt. "The pay is less. The coat is worse. The work, I am told, matters."

We came around the long bend above the valley, and the Halloran road sign stood at the crossroads, and beyond it, far downstream, I could see the bridge—chained since autumn, its repair scaffolding half-dismantled, county men already swarming over it in the sun like insects wakened early. In three weeks, perhaps four, it would open. The river road would fill with carts and gossip and ordinary life. The house on the hill would empty: the magistrate back to his chambers with his embarrassment folded away in a drawer, the journalist to fresher scandals, the niece—poor Constance, who had covered for us at the dinner table at the cost of her own reputation and stood taller afterward than I had ever seen her—to some city or other and a better class of trouble. Even Edith would go back to trains and meetings and the great slow machinery of her cause.

And I would go, I realized, with a sensation like a knot untying underwater, to a school full of girls who needed letters carried and accounts kept and a man who knew how to be forgotten.

"Yes," I said. "To all of it. The sealing, the school, the worse coat."

"I thought so," said Edith. "You were always a terrible liar about the things that mattered."

We came down through the village and she spoke of practicalities—the file, the references she would write under a name I hadn't chosen yet—and I answered in the right places, and part of me stood slightly apart from it all, watching the river run beside the road, free of its ice at last, carrying everything downstream toward the sea.

It was evening when the post caught us up. Edith's housekeeper brought the tray to the kitchen, where I sat eating my first free meal, and among the bills and the circulars there was one envelope with foreign stamps, creased from a long journey, addressed in a hand I knew—careful, architectural, the hand of a man who drew bridges.

It was not addressed to me. It was not addressed to anyone I could name. The direction line read only: *For the driver — wherever he has gone.*

Edith looked at it, and then at me, and pushed it across the table without a word.

I turned it over twice in my hands. I thought of twenty-two letters kept in order, read aloud to a sick woman; of promises kept past all reason; of a man on the far bank shouting a name that was never mine and meaning me anyway. The kettle ticked on the stove. Somewhere up the hill the house was quiet, and tomorrow the bridge would open.

I slid my thumb under the flap—and stopped, and left it sealed, and put it in my breast pocket over my heart, where I intended, I decided then, to keep it.

Some letters are for delivering. Some, at last, are only for carrying.

I would open it in spring, I told myself. At the school, in the new coat, under whatever name I chose.

I picked it up, and went to pack.
