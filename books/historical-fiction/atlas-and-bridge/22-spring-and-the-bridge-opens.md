## 22. Spring, and the Bridge Opens

The ice went out of the river on a Tuesday, in the night, with a sound like cannon fire that woke the whole house. Mara stood at his window in the grey before dawn and watched the river shrug off its winter coat—great plates of white tilting, grinding, folding under one another like a crowd turning over in its sleep—and he thought: *it kept its promise, and so did I.*

By morning the water ran brown and muscular between the banks, carrying the last of the winter seaward. By noon the county men had come with their keys and their crowbars and struck the chains from the bridge, and the first cart rolled across with a load of seed potatoes, and the driver raised his hat to no one in particular.

The season was over. The house began, gently, to empty itself.

* * *

It emptied the way a stage empties after the last performance—not all at once but in small withdrawals, each guest gathering their coats and grievances and going out into the light.

The magistrate left first, and left quietly, which told Mara everything about how the winter had ended for him. He shook Edith's hand at the door with the expression of a man signing an armistice he had not wanted to sign, and his eyes found Mara's once across the hall—a long, level look, the look of a man who knew precisely what name sat behind those grey eyes now, because Mara had given it to him freely, in a cold room with a stove ticking, and could take nothing back. Then the carriage took him away toward town and his diminished career, and Mara felt the last weight of the interrogation lift off his chest like snow sliding from a bough.

The journalist followed within the week, sulking, his notebook thinner than he'd hoped and his byline unmade. He paused on the front step to deliver a parting shot about *the inevitable march of events*, and Edith said, "Yes, do write that down, it has a certain rhythm," and shut the door on him with a click as precise as a chess move.

Edith's niece Clara lingered longest. She had paid dearly for her winter—the story of what she'd done, covering for two fleeing men at the dinner, had made its rounds through three counties' worth of drawing rooms—but she carried it differently now than she had in January. In January she had trembled. In April she stood in the drive with her trunk strapped behind the trap and her chin up, and when she embraced her aunt she whispered something that made the old woman laugh, and when she turned to Mara she held out her hand as though to an equal.

"You never thanked me," she said.

"I'm thanking you now."

"Too late." But her eyes were warm. "I've decided it was the bravest thing anyone did all winter, and I did it, so you may all be grateful in silence forever." She climbed into the trap, gathered the reins, and drove herself out onto the reopened bridge without looking back—which was, Mara thought, watching the small figure diminish against the bright water, exactly the right way to cross it.

And then there was Anton, who had gone weeks ago, in March, across the border by the ordinary road this time, with papers that were suddenly, astonishingly, correct—papers Edith's lawyers had conjured out of affidavits and outrage and the public embarrassment of a magistrate who could not afford one more scandal. Anton had stood in the hall with his bag in his hand and looked at Mara for a long moment, and whatever passed between them had needed no words, because they had already spent every word they had on the river road, in the dark, with dogs behind them and the confession letter open between them like a wound finally dressed.

He had said only: "You gave me your name to give me my life. I intend to use both well."

Then he was gone, and the house was quiet, and spring came on properly at last.

* * *

The garden woke. The elms along the river road unfurled their small copper leaves. Under the kitchen window the rhubarb pushed up fists of red-green through the black mud, and Mrs. Halloway the cook declared the year officially begun and started talking about asparagus.

Mara stayed on through April without being asked and without asking. He drove Edith to town twice a week for the meetings—her movement had not paused for winter either; if anything the scandal had fed it, the way a flood feeds a valley—and he read her correspondence aloud in the evenings as he always had, and he steamed open nothing, because there was nothing left to hide from her and both of them knew it, and the letters lay open on the table between them like honest weather.

It was on one of those evenings, with the light going amber through the study windows and the smell of cut grass coming in off the lawn, that Edith set down her pen and looked at him over her spectacles.

"I've been thinking about names," she said.

Mara kept reading. "There's a tedious one about drainage on the next page, if you'd rather hear that."

"Mara Winterbourne." She said it slowly, tasting it. "A good alias. Winter-born, born in hiding—I assume you chose it yourself?"

"In a railway station lavatory, with a stolen valise for a desk. I was nineteen and it seemed very clever."

"It is very clever. That's rather the problem." She took off her spectacles and folded them, which she did only when she was about to be serious; he had learned her signals the way a sailor learns clouds. "The magistrate knows your name. The courts know it. It's been printed, in the smaller papers, spelled wrong. You can't wear it again—it fits badly now, like a coat someone else was hanged in."

"That's roughly how it feels."

"So." She leaned back. "I am offering you your old position. Coachman, secretary, reader of correspondence, keeper of my secrets—at wages I should be ashamed to say aloud, except that I'm not ashamed of anything. And you may take it under any name you like." A pause, precise as a placed stone. "Except that I think you're tired of liking names. I think you want one that's true."

The clock on the mantel ticked. Out on the lawn a blackbird was saying something urgent and repetitive about the evening.

"My real name," Mara said, "is the one I traded away. Thomas Ellery Vane. I stole four hundred pounds from a man who deserved it and ran until running became a profession." He had told her all of it in February, kneeling almost, in this same room, while the wind worried the shutters; it came easier now, worn smooth by telling. "I don't want it back. It belongs to a frightened boy who thought money would make him invisible. I was wrong about the money and I'm finished with the invisibility."

"Then we shall choose honestly," Edith said, in the tone she used for motions she intended to carry. "Not an alias. An actual name, chosen the way other people get theirs—deliberately, or by luck, or by love. What was your mother's name?"

He blinked. He had not spoken of her in eleven years. "Margaret. She called me Tam."

"Tam," Edith repeated, trying it against the light. "Tam Vane. No—Vane is a weathervane, it turns with the wind, and you have spent a whole winter refusing to turn." She was quiet a moment, and then something moved behind her eyes, the look she wore when she moved a piece no one else had noticed on the board. "Your father?"

"A bridge carpenter." He heard himself say it and felt the floor tilt gently, the way the ice had tilted under his boots in March. "He built spans on the northern lines. I hadn't—I haven't thought of him in years. He died when I was twelve. Fell."

The blackbird sang. The clock ticked. Edith Calloway, who had read her own mail for thirty years and everyone else's besides, smiled the small dry smile that had frightened magistrates and comforted nieces.

"Well then," she said. "That settles it better than any argument I could make. A bridge-builder's son, who carried a man across a frozen river and delivered the letter himself at the cost of everything he had hidden behind." She picked up her pen. "We shall write to the registrar tomorrow. You will be Tam Bridge. And if anyone asks, you will tell them it is your own name, earned honestly, and let them make of it what they like."

"Tam Bridge," he said. It sounded absurd. It sounded like a joke in a music hall. It sounded, underneath the absurdity, like a key turning in a lock he had believed rusted shut since he was nineteen years old in a railway station with a stolen name.

"Yes," said Edith, returning to her drainage letter. "It will grow on you. Most true things do."

* * *

The letter came the first week of May.

It arrived with the morning post among bills and pamphlets, a foreign envelope, stamps overlapping like scales, addressed in the careful upright hand Mara had last seen mapping a route across the river by candlelight while the household slept. Addressed to *Mr. T. Winterbourne, care of E. Calloway*. Anton had known no other name. Perhaps, Mara thought, holding the envelope in the hall light, Anton had understood perfectly well that no other name existed yet—that the man who had walked back across the breaking ice to draw the patrol away was still, in some essential way, waiting to be named.

Mrs. Halloway brought it out on the salver with the rest. Edith glanced at the handwriting, glanced at Mara's face, and said, "The study is free until luncheon," and returned to her toast.

He did not steam it open. That was the old craft, the coach-house craft, the skill of the invisible man, and he set it aside the way one sets aside a knife no longer needed. He simply broke the seal and unfolded the pages at the study window with the river visible beyond the elms, wide and blue and harmless now, running easy toward the sea.

The letter was long. Anton wrote the way he had spoken on the river road—plainly, with the grammar slightly bent, every word load-bearing.

He wrote that he had crossed in March on the ordinary ferry like an ordinary man, and that the woman—Lena, her name was Lena, and Mara learned it now for the first time, the name that had waited two years inside a bundle under the coach-house floor—had met him at the border station wearing a green coat, and that he had known her from fifty yards and had walked the last forty-nine too fast and stumbled like a boy. He wrote that they had married in the district church with two witnesses and a choir that sang badly, and that he had found work with a firm building a span over the narrows at Vindau, iron this time, not timber, and that he was good at it and the work was honest and no one had asked him to build anything meant for war.

He wrote about the winter. He wrote: *I have tried to explain you to Lena and I cannot, because she asks why a man who owed me one letter would give me his name, his safety, his whole hidden life, and I say because he promised, and she says people do not keep promises like that, and I say this one does, and she looks at me as if I have become a religious man, which perhaps I have.*

And near the end, in a paragraph that seemed to have been written separately, later, the ink a different shade:

*I enclose nothing, because there is nothing I can send that repays it. Only this: you said once, on the river, that you had spent your life erasing yourself from every room. I want you to know that there is one room now—you may picture it, low-ceilinged, with a bad stove and a good view of the water—where your name is spoken every day, and where you are not erased at all. You are carved into the lintel of this house. Come and see it someday. Bring your new name, whatever it turns out to be. — A.R.*

Mara read it twice. Then he folded it along its original creases, put it back in its foreign envelope, and sat for a long time looking at the river.

He had delivered the last letter himself, in February, at great cost—he had walked it up a frozen lane on the far side with patrol lanterns swinging somewhere behind him and handed it through a door to a frightened family, and the door had closed, and he had turned back toward the ice and the law and the end of his hidden life. He had assumed that was the delivery. One letter, one door, one debt discharged.

But here was another letter, and no address to carry it to, and no one in England whose hands it belonged in. It was addressed to a man named Winterbourne who no longer existed, written by a man who lived on the far side of a river Mara could see from this window, and there was no one to deliver it to but himself.

So he delivered it. He walked it upstairs to his room, to the drawer where he kept the few things he owned that were not uniforms and brushes and harness leather—the bundle of Anton's letters, rescued from under the coach-house floor, their wax seals broken, their secrets long since spent—and he laid the new letter on top of the old ones, unopened no longer but undivided, the whole correspondence of a promise kept, and he closed the drawer.

Delivered, he thought. To no one. To the only person it was ever really for.

* * *

On the last Saturday of May, Edith sent the trap into town for the post and Mara drove it himself, because driving was the oldest of his trades and the one he loved without embarrassment. The bridge was busy with spring traffic—carts, cyclists, a gypsy caravan painted yellow, children leaning over the parapet to spit at fish. He slowed at the center of the span, as he always did now, and looked down at the water.

It was high and bright and moving fast, carrying everything seaward: the winter, the ice, the lanterns, the dogs, the cracking plates of terror, all of it gone south and salt. The river kept nothing. That was the difference between a river and a man, he thought, and then corrected himself, flicking the reins: that used to be the difference. He was keeping things now. A name. A letter. A promise, worn smooth as river stone, carried forward.

At the house, Edith was waiting on the steps with the afternoon's correspondence already sorted in her lap, because she could not help herself, and beside her on the sun-warmed stone sat a small official-looking envelope with a county seal.

"The registrar writes," she said, handing it over. "I took the liberty of having them send it here. Open it."

Inside was a single card, formal and brief. *This is to certify that Thomas Ellery Vane, also known as Mara Winterbourne, is entered upon the register as TAM BRIDGE, of this county, the name changed by deed and duly recorded.* Below it, a date. Below that, a stamp, red and ordinary and real.

Mara stood on his own front steps, in his own spring sunlight, holding his own name.

"Well?" said Edith. "How does it fit?"

He turned the card over. On the back, in pencil, in her crisp hand, she had written: *For the record: competent, loyal, impossible to intimidate, reads aloud beautifully. Reference available upon request.—E.C.*

"It fits," he said, and found that his voice needed a moment before it would continue. "It fits like something made to measure."

"Good." She rose, brushed off her skirt, and became in one motion the formidable creature who had faced down magistrates and journalists and half the government. "Because there is a meeting in town on Thursday, and a driver is required, and a reader of correspondence, and a keeper of secrets—though I warn you the pay in secrets has fallen off terribly since the war scare died down. Can you start Thursday, Mr. Bridge?"

Mara—Tam—looked past her, down the long lawn to the elms and the silver gleam of the river beyond, where a man was alive and married and building bridges of his own, where a letter lay in a drawer keeping faith with all the others, where a name waited to be spoken in a low-ceilinged room with a bad stove and a good view of the water.

"Thursday," he said. "And every day after, if you'll have me."

"I'll have you," said Edith Calloway, and went in to her letters, leaving him standing in the sun with his name in his hand—visible at last, even to himself.
