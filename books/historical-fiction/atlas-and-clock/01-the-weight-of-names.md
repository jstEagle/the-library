## 1. The Weight of Names

The snow came in through the broken cloister window all winter, and no one swept it out.

Elodie Oyelaran had stopped noticing it somewhere around the third week of December, the way you stop noticing a wound that has become part of the body. It lay in pale drifts against the chapel's north wall, melting and refreezing into a crust the color of old bone, and the wounded men who could walk sometimes stood over it in their gray hospital shirts and held their hands toward the light without knowing why. Light was free. Everything else in Sainte-Agathe's had been requisitioned, catalogued, or carried off by the army months ago. Even God, Elodie thought some nights, had been seconded to the war office; He answered now only to officers of rank.

The convent sat on a low hill above a town whose name appeared on almost no maps anymore, because maps were intelligence and intelligence was dangerous. It had been built for forty sisters and a school of girls who learned embroidery and scripture. It held now one hundred and ninety beds, most of them improvised from pews and door-laid planks, and it held Sister Superior Gertrude, who believed order was a form of prayer, and Sister Béatrice, who kept the ancient clock in the tower running out of what she admitted was stubbornness rather than faith, and eleven nurses of the Voluntary Aid Detachment who slept four to a cell meant for one novice and woke before dawn to the smell of carbolic and the sound of trucks on the frozen road below.

Elodie had been at Sainte-Agathe's for fourteen months. She knew this because she counted, and because counting was the only luxury the war had left her. She counted beds made, pulses taken, morphine tablets split into halves and quarters with a razor until the tablets wore down to crumbs too small to divide. She counted the dead, though the dead were not supposed to be counted. They were supposed to be processed.

That word—*processed*—had entered her vocabulary in March, delivered by a quartermaster with ink-stained cuffs and a voice like a man reading train times. The fallen were processed. The paperwork moved east in triplicate. The graves received wooden crosses stenciled with numbers, and if a man died without his identity disc, without a letter in his pocket, without a comrade conscious enough to speak for him, then he went into the ground as *Inconnu — Male Inconnu*, unknown male, and the earth kept him the way the army kept everything: anonymously, efficiently, without further correspondence.

The first one she had failed was called nothing at all.

He came up from the clearing station in the last week of March 1917, on a stretcher that smelled of iodine and the sweet rotten reek of gas gangrene. A private, probably—the boots said private, though the boots had been swapped so many times between living men and dying ones that boots proved nothing anymore. His jaw was shattered. He could not have given his name if he had wanted to, and perhaps he had wanted to very much, because for two days and nights his eyes stayed open and fixed on the ceiling above his cot, moving whenever anyone leaned over him, following them, asking something his mouth could no longer ask.

Elodie had held his hand. She had written on the slate at the foot of his bed the words she wrote on every slate: *Rest quietly. You are among friends.* She had moistened his lips with a sponge. When the end came on the third evening, it came without drama, the way it did with the men who had already spent everything; his chest simply declined, politely, twice, three times, and then agreed to stop.

Sister Superior filled out the card. *Unknown Male. Died 27 March 1917. Effects: none.*

But there had been effects. There was always something. In this case a button, brass, worn nearly smooth, pushed deep into the seam of his tunic where a pocket had once been sewn and then taken off—by a tailor, by his mother, by himself with a stolen knife. And under his tongue, where men hid things they could not afford to have found, a scrap of paper folded to the size of a fingernail. Elodie had found it when she prepared the body. It bore five words in a laboring, unpracticed hand:

*tell marie the bread is gone*

She had run down the corridor with it, past the ward, past the dispensary, into Sister Superior's office, holding it out like evidence in a trial. Here, she said. Here is a wife. Here is a name to ask after—Marie, someone's Marie, somewhere in France there is a woman watching her oven go cold. Write it on the card. Send an inquiry. Anything.

Sister Superior had looked at the scrap of paper for a long moment, and Elodie had watched her face do something complicated and then refuse to do it.

"We don't know whose Marie," Sister Superior said. "We don't know his village. We don't know his regiment. The Bureau will not open a file for a sentence about bread."

"Then write it anyway. Write the sentence."

"On what form?"

And that was the whole of it, laid bare between them in the lamplight: on what form. There was no form for a dead man's last errand. The war had forms for everything else—requisitions, burials, ammunition expenditure, the number of lice deloused per company per week—but no form for *tell marie the bread is gone*, and what has no form does not exist, and what does not exist cannot grieve anyone officially.

They buried him on the morning of the twenty-ninth, in the strip of consecrated ground behind the chapel where the army had permitted interments, seven soldiers of the town's garrison firing no volley because ammunition was rationed even for ceremony. The cross read a number. Elodie stood at the fence with the scrap of paper in her glove, and afterward she did not burn it, though regulations—and sense—said she should have. She put it in the back of her paybook, behind her own photograph of a house in Gascony that had likely lost its roof by now.

That night she took a ledger from the dispensary stores—a stock ledger, columns ruled for quantities of quinine and gauze—and she ruled out the printed headings with a straightedge and ink, and across the top of the first page she wrote, in handwriting small enough to deny itself if ever questioned: *Names.*

The first name she wrote was not a name. She wrote the date. Beneath it she wrote: *Private, regiment unknown, jaw shattered by shell fragment. Last words unwritten but he followed us with his eyes for three days, which is a kind of speech. Wife or mother named Marie. Message: tell marie the bread is gone. Buried as Unknown Male, grave marker 4-117.*

She looked at what she had written and understood that she had crossed a line with no going back, and was surprised to find she felt not afraid but steadier, the way a ship feels when its anchor catches.

* * *

By January of the new year the ledger held sixty-one names, and Elodie had developed methods.

She collected them the way other women collected coupons—opportunely, patiently, in the margins of ordinary work. A man brought in semi-conscious from a dressing station would mumble a village, a surname, a girl's name with a diminutive attached, and Elodie would repeat it back to him—*Perrin, yes, Perrin of Montfaucon, your Louise*—and watch the mumbling ease, because being named was medicine, and she administered it as such. Then, later, alone in the linen room with a stub of pencil, she would enter him: wounds, hour, last words if there were any worth keeping, the number of the cross that would pretend he had never had a mother.

Some names she took from the dying themselves, sitting close so their voices reached only her. Some she reconstructed like a seamstress matching pattern pieces: the letter fragments families had sent that arrived after the addressee was dead, addressed to men whose cards said *Unknown*. Some she simply witnessed—a civilian farmhand brought in with a soldier's shrapnel in his shoulder because the front did not distinguish, buried in the soldiers' plot because the town had no means to do otherwise, entered in her ledger under the heading she had begun keeping for them: *Not soldiers. Also forgotten.*

Marguerite Fournier, the youngest of the nurses, nineteen years old and ferocious about it, had caught her once. February, the linen room, Elodie bent over the ledger with the pencil stub in her teeth. Marguerite had stood in the doorway with an armful of sheets and looked at the page for three full seconds, and Elodie had felt the whole edifice of the thing—fourteen months of careful, criminal bookkeeping—balance on the point of collapse.

"You rule a clean column," Marguerite had said at last. "Sister Superior would approve."

"She must never see it."

"No," Marguerite had agreed, dumping the sheets into the press with unnecessary force. "She mustn't." And that was all, then and since—an alliance ratified without oath or discussion, the way real ones are.

There were dangers, and Elodie enumerated them to herself regularly, the way she checked the morphine count. Censorship, first: any writing that left the building passed through military channels, so the ledger could never leave, could never be copied, could never be sent. Discovery, second: possession of unofficial records concerning military personnel was, at minimum, an offense, and in wartime the minimum had a way of becoming the maximum. And third, the strangest danger, the one she could not regulate: that the ledger would become unbearable. That three hundred names would weigh more than she could carry, and she would crack in front of everyone, in a ward, during rounds, weeping out strangers' surnames into the carbolic-smelling dark.

She kept it hidden inside the false back of the linen cupboard, behind the bolts of bandage cloth, wrapped in an operating towel that had been condemned for stains. Every few days she added to it. Every night, without meaning to, she memorized it—lying in her narrow cell, running the names like a rosary until sleep took her mid-decade. *Auban, Marcel. Auban, Émile, no relation that he knew of. Bacou, Henriette—not a soldier at all, a schoolmistress, fever.* She did not examine why she did this. She suspected that some part of her already knew the ledger was not safe, would never be safe, and that the only secure place for three hundred names was inside a single stubborn skull.

* * *

On the last Friday of January it snowed again, the fine dry kind that squeaked underfoot, and the convoys came early.

Elodie heard them from the ward—the downshift of engines on the hill road, the peculiar metallic cough of ambulance springs—and was moving before the sound finished, because fourteen months had taught her body the timetable of catastrophe. Six ambulances, which meant perhaps thirty stretchers, which meant the triage table in the entrance hall would run blood-and-water until midnight, and Sister Béatrice would bring the lamp, and Marguerite would work the door with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, sorting men into the great brutal ledger of *likely* and *unlikely* with nothing but her hands and her eyes.

It went as those nights went. It became, in the way of such nights, timeless—a sequence of stretchers and sponges and a voice, hers, saying the same sentences over and over in the calm register that was itself a form of lying: *You're safe, you're at Sainte-Agathe's, hold still, this will help, you're safe.* Somewhere past two in the morning the flow thinned. Sometime near four, Marguerite fell asleep standing against the doorframe and slid six inches and woke and laughed at herself, badly, and Elodie sent her to bed.

Elodie stayed to finish the notes. This was her ritual: the ward quiet, the lamps turned low, the slate cards updated before dawn brought new orders to overwrite them. She moved down the rows with her pencil, and in the third row from the window she stopped.

A man had died an hour ago—quietly, between two of her rounds, the good death, the one nurses are grateful for. He was a sergeant, they thought, from the disc, though the disc was half-melted by the shell-burst that had done for him and gave up only a fraction of its stamping. He had spoken earlier in the evening, briefly, to no one visible: a woman's name, *Adèle*, and a phrase in Occitan that the man in the next bed had translated unprompted—*he says the vineyard on the south slope should be pruned hard, cut it back hard, she'll understand.* Then he had slept, and then he had died, and the card at his feet awaited its inscription.

*Unknown Male,* it would say. Or nearly unknown—*Sergt., disc partial,* and a grave number.

Elodie stood over him in the lamplight. Outside, the snow kept coming down in the windless, indifferent way it had all winter, filling the cloister, erasing the path between chapel and mortuary, erasing everything it touched with the patience of something that had all the time in the world.

"No," she said aloud, to the empty ward, to the war, to the snow.

She wrote the card what the rules required. Then she went to the linen cupboard, knelt, drew out the ledger from behind its bolts of cloth, and opened it to the first page, where a dead man's last errand about bread lay pressed flat under fourteen months of accumulated names.

By lamplight she entered the sergeant: *South slope, vineyard, prune it hard. Adèle will understand. Occitan speaker, probably Aude or Ariège. Disc melted. Grave to follow.*

Three hundred and eleven names, when the war finally let her stop counting. But on that night there were one hundred and eighty-nine, and she closed the book on them, and wrapped them in the stained towel, and hid them behind the gauze, and stood a moment with her hand flat against the cupboard's wood as if taking leave of a sleeping child.

She did not know—I could not know then, though I know it now, having had long years to learn how stories announce themselves—that the ledger had nine more months to live, and that its ending was already walking toward Sainte-Agathe's through the same snow, patient as everything else that winter. Not an army. Not a shell. Something smaller. Someone small, with silent eyes and pockets full of someone else's money, waiting at the bottom of a hill road for a door to open.
