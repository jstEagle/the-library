## 2. Instructions in Chalk

The knock came at dusk, when the light through the courtyard arch went the color of old bruises and even the shells seemed tired of their work. Elodie heard it from the dispensary, where she was counting gauze that would not last the week, and she knew before she reached the door that something was wrong with it. Soldiers knocked hard, or not at all. Civilians in this town had learned to move like smoke. Nobody knocked twice on a convent door at dusk unless they could not bear to.

She opened it on a woman and a boy.

The woman was young in the way the war had made ordinary—young and aged at once, her coat too big for her, its collar turned up against the sleet that had started an hour ago and was already turning the courtyard stones black. She held the boy's shoulder with one hand. In the other she carried a small canvas bag and a folded note.

"You take children," the woman said.

It was not a question, but Elodie answered it anyway, because the truth was complicated and the cold was coming in over the threshold like water. "We are a hospital for soldiers. There is a children's refuge in—"

"There was." The woman's voice did not rise. That was the worst of it. Everything about her was quiet, pressed flat, a person who had learned that volume cost lives. "There was a refuge in Varenne. There was a house in Varenne. There was a town."

Sleet ticked against the stone. Behind Elodie, down the corridor, someone groaned in his sleep and someone else said *shh*, and the great building settled around them like a ship.

"I can't keep him," the woman said. "You have money there. Enough. Feed him, teach him to lie about his name if you have to—I don't care what you tell him, tell him anything—but do not let the authorities register him. Do you understand? No papers. No lists. Nothing written."

"Madame—"

"No lists." The word cracked then, just once, the only fissure in all that flatness. "Whatever else you do. No lists."

Elodie looked at the boy. He was perhaps six, small for six, wearing boots that had been cut down from someone adult's, stuffed at the toe with what looked like newspaper gone soft and grey. His coat had been a soldier's. His face was fine-boned and utterly still—not calm; still the way a field is still before the guns find the range again. He was looking at Elodie's hands. Not her face. Her hands.

"What is your name?" Elodie asked him, kneeling, because you knelt for children in this work or you did not survive it.

The boy said nothing. He looked at her hands.

"He doesn't speak," the woman said. "He does since—" She stopped. Whatever came after *since* was a country she would not visit aloud. "He understands everything. Don't mistake him. But he won't speak, and it's better if he never starts here. A child who talks remembers things out loud."

Elodie stood. The cold had gotten into her knees and would stay there all night; she already knew it. "Madame, I cannot promise what you're asking. If the civil authority comes—"

"The civil authority is three streets away and burning paper tonight like everyone else." The woman held out the note. "Read it. Then forget me."

The note was written in pencil, in a hand that had been taught copperplate and had abandoned it under pressure: *His name is Henri. He is six years old. Forget his face.*

Below that, nothing. No surname. No mother's name. No parish, no address, no date. Six words of instruction and a name that was barely a name at all—Henri, the name you gave when they demanded one and you wanted to give none.

The coins were wrapped inside it. Elodie weighed them without meaning to—old habit, hospital arithmetic—and understood that it was a great deal of money for a woman in a coat two sizes too large, and that the woman had counted it out knowing exactly how much a child cost per week in a town like this, which meant she had done the sums before, which meant—

"How long have you been carrying him?" Elodie asked quietly.

"Since Tuesday." The woman's eyes went past Elodie's shoulder into the lit corridor, measuring it, finding it wanting, accepting it anyway. "There were four towns between here and Varenne. This is the first door that opened."

*This is the first door that opened.* Elodie would keep that sentence for the rest of her life, though she did not know it yet. It lodged somewhere below the ribs, where the ledger lived, in the small dark archive she had built out of grief and spite and pencil lead.

"Take him inside," she said.

The woman exhaled. It was the only sound she made that was not controlled.

Then she crouched, took the boy's face in both gloved hands, and kissed his forehead, and said something to him so low that even Elodie, standing a foot away, caught only the shape of it—a rhythm, rising and falling, like a line from a song or a prayer or a street name repeated until it becomes a spell. The boy closed his eyes. When the woman stood, she did not look at him again. That, too, was discipline. Elodie recognized it the way one nurse recognizes another's technique across a crowded ward: you did not look back at the ones you left, because looking back was a wound you gave yourself and the leaving had to be clean or it killed you.

"Forget his face," the woman said, for the second time, and turned, and was gone into the sleet before Elodie had finished drawing breath to answer.

The boy watched the gate. Elodie watched the boy. Neither of them moved for a long moment, and the snow thickened, and somewhere east of the town the artillery resumed its low idiot conversation, and then the boy picked up his canvas bag himself—he would not let her carry it, she learned that within ten steps, learned it by reaching for it once and watching him go rigid as wire—and walked into the convent as if he had been expected.

* * *

Sister Superior found them within the hour, because Sister Superior found everything within the hour; Elodie had long suspected the woman smelled paperwork the way sharks smelled blood.

"A child." Marguerite Blaise, twenty-four years old, wimple slightly crooked, standing in the refectory doorway with her sleeves rolled and her forearms still pink from washing, said it the way other people said *a shell landed in the orchard.* "An actual child. At our door."

"A child," Sister Superior agreed, from her seat at the end of the table, where the inventory lay open before her like a confession. She was sixty and built like the door of a strongroom, and she had run this convent through two requisitions and one direct hit on the west range, and she governed it now with the weary precision of a woman balancing a ledger that God Himself audited. "Which is precisely the difficulty. We are chartered as a military hospital. Forty-two beds. Every one of them spoken for. Our supplies are drawn against forty-two souls, no more. A child is not a wound, Mademoiselle Oyelaran. A child is arithmetic."

"Then let me carry his arithmetic," Elodie said. "The mother left money."

"The mother," Sister Superior said, "left money and vanished into a winter night without giving a name, a parish, or a reason, and left instructions that the boy be kept off every list this house maintains. You will forgive me if that does not read as generosity. It reads as trouble wearing charity's coat."

Elodie kept her voice level. She had learned levelness at the same school as the woman at the gate; everyone in this building had. "He is six years old and it is snowing."

"It is snowing on all of France."

"And we have a roof."

"We have a *charter.*" Sister Superior closed the inventory. For a moment she looked less like a strongroom door and more like what she was, a tired woman of sixty whose order had buried eleven of its own sisters since August. "Elodie. Sit down. Listen to me as one practical woman to another. Tomorrow morning I am obliged to report arrivals to the district office—all arrivals, that is the regulation, and the regulation is the only wall between this house and the men with clipboards who decide which hospitals keep their supplies. If I report him, he enters the system. The system will ask who he is. His mother would not tell us, which means whatever she is hiding from, the system is part of it. If I do not report him, and he is discovered—and children are discovered, children cry at the wrong hour, children are seen in courtyards—then this house loses its protections. All of them. For every man in those beds." She spread her hands, palms up, an old gesture of accounting. "Forty-two wounded soldiers against one unregistered child. You tell me the sum."

"That sum has a name," Elodie said, before she could stop herself.

The room went very quiet. Marguerite stopped drying her hands.

"Yes," Sister Superior said softly. "They all do. That is what makes this work unbearable, and why we are careful, and why we will still be careful when you and I are both dead. He stays tonight. It is snowing; I am cruel, not monstrous. But tomorrow morning, Mademoiselle Oyelaran, I want him gone before the district office opens. To the refuge at Saint-Aubin, if it still exists. To any door that opens. There is a cart going at seven." She rose, gathered her inventory, and paused at the doorway beside Marguerite, and for just a moment her gaze moved past them both, down the dark corridor toward the ward where a small figure was presumably being installed among the fevered and the sleeping.

"Forget his face, the note said," she remarked, to no one in particular. "In my experience, people only instruct you to forget what they cannot afford to have remembered."

Then she was gone, and her footsteps faded down the stone, and Marguerite let out a breath that fogged in the unheated refectory.

"She'll do it," Marguerite said. "She'll send him out at seven. She's right, you know. About the arithmetic. About all of it."

"I know she's right."

"But you're not going to put him on the cart."

Elodie looked at her. Marguerite was the youngest of the nursing staff and the quickest to laugh and the first to cry at deaths, and Elodie had privately filed her, months ago, under *kind but not yet serious*—the way one files a medical student who faints at the first amputation. Now Marguerite was looking at her with an expression that belonged to somebody older.

"He doesn't speak," Elodie said. "Did you see? I asked his name and he looked at my hands. He's not mute, Marguerite. Mute children try. He's *silent*. Someone taught him that silence is how you stay alive, and whoever taught him was right, and tomorrow at seven o'clock we are going to put that lesson to use and hand him to strangers with a bag of someone else's money." She heard her own voice drop and flatten and thought, distantly, *that is the woman's voice, the mother's voice, it's already in my mouth.* "No. I need a day. Two days. I need to know what she was running from before I decide what I'm running toward."

"And if knowing takes more than two days?"

*Then the cart leaves empty,* Elodie did not say, because saying it aloud would make it a plan, and plans, in her experience, were the first things the war confiscated.

"Go to bed, Marguerite," she said instead.

* * *

She did not go to bed herself. This was not heroism; it was insomnia with a purpose, and she had cultivated it carefully since October.

At half past midnight the ward was quiet except for the breathing of thirty-eight men—four had died that week, and the beds had not yet refilled; even death was short of staff these days. Elodie moved down the rows with her lamp turned low, checking dressings out of pure habit, and at the far end, in the narrow cot usually reserved for visiting clergy, she found the boy awake.

He was not crying. She had known he would not be crying. He lay with his canvas bag clasped to his chest like a plank in deep water, eyes open, watching the ceiling beam as if memorizing it for later testimony.

She sat down on the floor beside the cot, deliberately below him, deliberately smaller.

"My name is Elodie," she said. "I am going to tell you something true, and you may believe it or not, that is your affair. In this building there is a book. In the book are names. Not official names—the officials have their own books, and theirs are full of lies. Mine are true. When a man dies here and they write *Unknown Male* on his cross, I write his real name in my book, if I can learn it, and sometimes I can't, and then I write what he called his mother, or the name of his dog, or the street he grew up on, because a street is a kind of name. There are almost three hundred names now." She had not told anyone this. Not Marguerite, not the priest, not God, or not in so many words. She heard herself confessing it to a six-year-old stranger and could not stop. "Your mother told me to forget your face. I am not good at forgetting. It is the one thing I refuse to do in this whole war. So here is what we will do instead, you and I. Your name goes in my book. Henri, the note says, and that is all anyone will ever make you say. But I will remember your face, because remembering is my work, and I will remember that you arrived on the fourth of February in the year 1917, in the snow, carrying your own bag."

The boy looked at her for a long time. The ward breathed around them. Somewhere a man murmured a woman's name and subsided.

Then Henri slid out of the cot, keeping hold of his bag, and crouched on the stone floor, and from the bag he took a stub of chalk—the kind children used for hopscotch grids on pavement, the kind that cost nothing and left no ink trail and could be licked off a palm—and he began, silently, deliberately, to draw on the flagstones.

Elodie leaned close. In the low lamplight, in white strokes quick and sure as a surgeon's sutures, the boy drew a street. A crooked street, narrowing to the left, with a church at the top of it and something beside the church—a tower, a tree, a post with a crossbar. Then a second street branching off. A bridge. A bridge with a break in it, drawn twice over, pressed so hard the chalk crumbled.

He had never seen this town. Neither had she. But Elodie Oyelaran sat very still on the cold flags of a requisitioned convent ward at one in the morning, looking at a map drawn by a silent child, and felt the unease that had entered with the knocking at dusk begin, slowly, irrevocably, to harden into something with weight and edges and consequences.

Because she knew that bridge with the break in it.

A dying sergeant had described it to her in November, gasping it between morphine tides, begging her to write it down—the broken bridge, the church with the leaning tower, the street that narrowed to the left—because his whole company came from there, and no one else knew anymore that it had existed at all.

She had written it in her ledger, in her careful hand, three lines down from the top.

She looked at the boy. The boy looked at her hands.

"Tomorrow at seven," Elodie said quietly, "there is a cart."

Henri did not stop drawing. He had begun a third street now, patient and exact, a boy laying out a city no one was allowed to remember, brick by brick, in chalk that would wipe away with one pass of a cloth.

He was building it for her. She understood that with sudden, total certainty, the way one understands a gunshot has found its mark. He could not or would not speak, but he had listened to every word about her book, and this—this erased, perfect, deniable map—was his answer. His side of the ledger.

Elodie fetched paper and pencil from the duty desk and sat back down on the freezing floor to copy it, every line, before dawn could come and the cart with it.
