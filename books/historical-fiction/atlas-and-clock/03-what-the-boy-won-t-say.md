## 3. What the Boy Won't Say

Marguerite had a theory about silence. She'd formed it in her second month at the convent, back when the wounded still frightened her, and she had tested it against every man who came through the ward with his jaw wired shut or his voice box torn by shrapnel or simply some private wound that no stretcher could carry. The theory was this: people did not stop speaking because they had nothing to say. They stopped because they had been given nothing worth answering.

So on Henri's third morning at the convent, she pulled a stool up to the window seat where he sat—knees drawn up, chin resting on them, watching the snow come down over the herb garden—and she brought supplies.

Not questions. Questions were ambushes. She'd learned that with Private Solal, who had wept for an hour the one time she'd asked about his mother and then refused to look at her for two days. No, she brought paper. A whole sheaf of it, salvaged from the dispensary's discarded inventory sheets, blank on one side. And three pencils of different lengths, and a tin of boiled sweets that Sister Béatrice kept for children who came to have teeth pulled.

She set them down beside him without a word and walked away to fold bandages at the far table, close enough to watch, far enough to pretend she wasn't watching.

He looked at the paper for a long time. Ten minutes, maybe longer. The snow kept falling. Somewhere below, one of the orderlies dropped a bedpan and swore with impressive fluency for a man of God's employ.

Then Henri picked up the longest pencil and began to draw.

Marguerite folded bandages. Her hands knew the work so well she could have done it asleep—lengthwise fold, then thirds, tuck the tails—which was fortunate, because her entire mind was on the boy at the window. He drew with a strange, urgent precision, not the looping fantasy scribbles of the other children who'd passed through, the ones who drew suns with faces and dogs with six legs. He drew lines. Straight ones, meeting at corners. Then smaller lines inside them. Windows, she realized. Doors. He was drawing a street.

He worked for nearly an hour. When he finished one sheet he set it carefully aside—he didn't crumple them, didn't tear them, laid each one flat atop the last as if they mattered—and started another. By noon he had five pages. Streets, mostly. One page showed a square with what might have been a fountain, drawn from above, the way you'd see it if you were looking out a window over it. Another showed a row of houses with numbered doors, and he had written numbers beside them: 4, 6, 8. His handwriting was careful and small and far too practiced for a boy of six.

At midday she brought him bread and jam and sat down on the stool again, not too close.

"That one's my favorite," she said, nodding at the fountain square. "The fountain. Is it in a town?"

He took a bite of bread. Chewed. Swallowed. Looked at her with dark, level eyes—the eyes of someone much older, someone doing sums behind them—and said nothing.

"You must be hungry from all that drawing," she tried. "Drawing always makes me hungry."

Nothing. But he ate the crusts, which she counted as a small victory. Children who were shutting out the world entirely left the crusts.

* * *

She reported to Elodie in the corridor outside the ward, keeping her voice low. "He drew all morning. Streets. A whole town, I think, if you put the pages together."

"Did he speak?"

"Not a word. Not to me, not to Sister Odile when she changed his bedding, not even when Corporal Anselme bellowed half the night and woke every patient twice." Marguerite hesitated. "Elodie, he doesn't cry either. I keep waiting. Children cry. It's how they let things out. He just—" She searched for the word. "He watches."

Elodie stood very still in the grey window light. She had been doing a great deal of standing very still since the morning the woman left him; Marguerite had noticed that Elodie carried something new around with her, some weight strapped inside her chest, visible only in the way she held her shoulders.

"Show me the drawings tonight," Elodie said. "After the patients are settled."

That evening, in the narrow room off the ward that served as the nurses' office, Marguerite spread the five sheets across the desk under the lamp. Elodie bent over them, and Marguerite watched her friend's face do something complicated.

"Look." Marguerite pointed. "The fountain square here. And this street connects to it—see the corner house with the awning? And this one runs downhill, there's a slope indicated by these hatch marks. It's the same town drawn from different places. As if he's walking through it in his memory and putting down what he sees."

"He's mapping it," Elodie said quietly.

"Yes, but which town? It's not ours. There's no clock tower like ours, no river. I asked Sister Odile if she recognized it—she grew up south of here—and she said no. I asked one of the convalescent sergeants. He said it looked like somewhere east of the Meuse, but he couldn't say where, and then he got that look soldiers get and went back to his cards." Marguerite spread her hands. "Nobody knows it. He won't tell us its name. He won't tell us anything."

Elodie wasn't listening anymore, not exactly. She had gone very pale, and her finger was moving across the third sheet—the street with the numbered doors—following it up to where it met a small building drawn with more care than the rest. A shopfront. An awning. And painted on the awning, in letters a child would only reproduce exactly, a name.

Marguerite leaned closer. The letters were slightly crooked but legible.

"Elodie? What is it?"

"Where is the ledger?" Elodie said. Her voice was strange—flat, careful, the voice of someone holding a door shut against wind.

"The ledger?" Marguerite glanced instinctively at the floorboard where they both knew it lived, though neither of them ever said so aloud in this room. "Why?"

"I need to check something. Watch the door."

Marguerite watched the door while Elodie knelt, pried up the board, and lifted out the thick school exercise book whose existence Marguerite had known about for months and never once acknowledged, the way you don't acknowledge a candle burning in a window during a war. Elodie flipped through it fast, past pages and pages of names in her small upright hand, until she found what she was looking for, near the beginning, in the first few entries.

She set the ledger open on the desk beside the drawing of the shopfront.

Marguerite read the entry upside down, as she'd taught herself to do long ago, another thing she never acknowledged.

*Delattre, Aurelien, infantryman, age 34. Died of wounds, 14 March. Reported by no unit. Buried under marker "Unknown Male No. 47." Home address per his own account, given in fever: a street he called the Rue des Trois-Frères, in a town he named Varennes-l'Oratoire. Shopkeeper's son. Kept asking whether the awning had been repainted.*

Below it, Elodie had written something else in a different ink, added later: *His town has a fountain in the central square. He described it. He said you could see it from his bedroom window if you leaned out.*

Marguerite felt the floor tilt gently, the way it did after you'd been on your feet fourteen hours. She looked at the drawing of the fountain square, drawn from above, the way you'd see it from a window if you leaned out. She looked at the awning with the crooked letters. Delattre et Fils.

"Henri," she whispered. "What was the woman's name, the one who left him?"

"She didn't give one."

"And the note said—"

"*Forget the face.*" Elodie closed the ledger softly, as though the names inside could be disturbed. "Marguerite. This soldier died in my arms in February. He talked about his son for three days. Henri. Six years old. He made me promise—" She stopped. Started again. "He made me promise somebody would remember the boy existed."

The lamp hissed. Outside, snow was still falling, erasing the garden, the wall, the road, everything.

"And now," Elodie said, "a boy who will not speak has drawn that dead man's town from memory. Door number four, six, eight. The fountain. The awning." She looked up, and her eyes were bright and terrible. "This is the son of a man this war erased so thoroughly that his grave doesn't carry his name. Someone left him at our door with money and instructions to make him disappear. And I want to know, Marguerite—I need to know—who benefits from a child being forgotten."

Marguerite thought of the boy at the window, drawing streets nobody recognized because the only person left who'd recognize them was buried under a stone that said *Unknown*. She thought of his level, patient eyes, doing sums. Counting what?

"We should ask him," she said. "Gently. Together."

"No." Elodie's hand came down flat on the closed ledger. "No questions. Not yet. If someone wanted this child invisible, questions leave marks. Paper leaves marks. You understand? From tonight, nothing about Henri goes in any report, any register, any list. He arrived as a favor to a passing stranger, a transient case, discharged to family. That is all anyone knows."

"You're hiding him."

"I'm remembering him," Elodie said. "There's a difference, and it may cost us both dearly."

* * *

But it was Marguerite who broke first. Two nights later, sitting with Henri while the ward slept and the boiler ticked in the cellar, she abandoned every principle of her theory about silence.

He had drawn two new pages. In the first, the fountain square again—but this time there were figures in it. Soldiers, small and precise, marching past the fountain with rifles shouldered. In the second, the same square, empty. Just the fountain, and the corner house, and the leaning perspective of a child looking down from a high window. At the bottom of the page, pressed so hard the pencil had torn the paper slightly, he had drawn a single small shape: a pair of boots, standing alone in the middle of the square. No person in them. Just boots.

Marguerite looked at those boots for a long time.

"Henri," she said, and her voice came out rougher than she intended. "You don't have to talk. I promise you never have to talk to me. But if you want to—if there's ever a moment where you want one person in the world to know something, and you'd rather it not be written anywhere, not in a ledger, not in a report, nowhere—then you can tell me. Any hour. Day or night. I'm a very good keeper of things."

The boy set down his pencil. He studied her the way he studied everything, completely, without embarrassment. Then, slowly, he reached across and turned the page with the boots face-down, as though closing a drawer.

And he shook his head—not *no*, she understood later, not refusal. More like: *not yet.* Like a man checking a watch and finding the hour wrong.

He picked up his pencil again and began a new map, and Marguerite sat with him in the ticking dark and let the silence stand between them, unbroken, almost comfortable.

It was almost comfortable, right up until the knock came—a hard, official triple on the convent's outer door, at eleven at night, followed by voices in the courtyard and the sound of boots, many boots, and Sister Superior's alarm bell beginning to ring.
