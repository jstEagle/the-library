## 4. The Ledger Grows Heavy

The knock came at half past four in the morning, and it was not the knock of a messenger.

Messengers knocked twice, apologetically, the way people did when they knew their news would hurt you. This was a fist against the oak of the front gate, three blows, then three more, the rhythm of men who had been given permission to break things if nobody opened quickly enough.

Elodie was awake anyway. She had been awake since two, lying in her cell with the ledger beneath her pillow and the wind worrying at the shutters, doing the arithmetic she could not stop doing—how many pages left, how much ink, how many names. She heard Sister Béatrice's bare feet slap down the dormitory stairs. She heard the bolt drawn back. And then she heard boots, many boots, in a building where everyone wore soft soles so as not to wake the dying.

She was out of bed and dressed before her mind finished the sentence it had started.

*Deserters.*

The word arrived whole, the way the worst words do. For weeks the notices on the village board had grown sharper in their language—*all males of fighting age found absent from their units will be treated as*—and for weeks she had known that Saint-Aubin-le-Cloître held more absent men than its rolls admitted. Two convalescents who should have returned to their regiments a month ago and hadn't. The Belgian road-mender sheltering in the tithe barn. Old Tomas, who cooked in the kitchens and had walked away from an Austrian column in 1915 and simply never walked back.

And the ledger itself. Three hundred names in her handwriting, each one an accusation if it were read aloud by the wrong mouth. *Unknown Male, buried 14 March.* *Private Anselme Duquet, executed by his own side, no marker.* Names the army had struck through or never written at all. To a certain kind of officer, a woman who collected such names was not a nurse keeping records. She was evidence.

She took the stairs two at a time.

* * *

The courtyard was full of lantern light and wrong shapes. Six gendarmes in the grey-blue of the interior forces, and behind them two soldiers in proper field uniform with the flat expressions of men detailed to a job they found embarrassing. At their center stood a captain Elodie had never seen before—young through the jaw, older through the eyes, a clipboard held against his chest like a breviary.

"Sister," he said to Mother Superior Cécile, who stood in the doorway with her arms folded into her sleeves and her face arranged into perfect, glacial incomprehension. "By order of the sector commandant. We are conducting an inspection for personnel absent without authority."

"At half past four in the morning," said Mother Superior Cécile.

"The hour is when we were ordered to arrive, Mother." He did not smile. "We will need access to every ward, every outbuilding, every cellar. You will provide a roll of every soul under this roof."

"Every soul," the Mother Superior repeated, as though tasting something spoiled. Behind her, in the shadowed arch of the infirmary door, Elodie saw Marguerite's face appear and vanish, hurrying to rouse the others.

Elodie moved forward before her fear could vote. "Captain, there are forty-one wounded in the wards. Eleven of them are not fit to be moved from their beds, and six of them are not fit to be questioned. If your men wake a man with a chest wound, you may watch him die and explain it to your commandant."

"I have no intention of disturbing the dying, mademoiselle—"

"Sister Oyelaran. Head nurse."

"—Sister Oyelaran. But I have orders, and orders do not distinguish between the dying and the shirking. Your men—" a small, precise correction, "—your patients will remain in their beds. We will simply look at them. A man who belongs in a bed is not afraid of being looked at."

*No,* Elodie thought. *But a man who does not belong in this convent is, and there are four of them, and one is asleep in the linen room because we could not put him anywhere else.*

She curtsied, because curtsying bought seconds, and seconds were going to be the only currency of the next hour. "Of course, Captain. May I accompany you? There are dressings and drains the untrained eye might mistake for—"

"You may." He was already turning. Then he stopped, and looked back at her, and for one long moment his gaze rested on her hands, on the ink stain on her right middle finger that no amount of lye would lift. "You keep the records here?"

"I keep the ward book."

"And other books?"

"We are a convent hospital, Captain. We have many books."

He held her eyes one beat longer than courtesy required, then gestured his men toward the east ward, and Elodie understood that the morning had just acquired a second clock ticking underneath the first.

* * *

The inspection of the wards was tedium braided with terror, which Elodie learned was worse than terror alone. The gendarmes went down the rows of beds with their lanterns, checking faces against nothing—they had no photographs, no lists beyond the sector's own, which told her they were fishing, not hunting—and the wounded watched them go by with the particular stillness soldiers used when they wanted very badly to be furniture. In the third bed of the east row lay Corporal Ferrand, twenty-six years old, febrile, officially returned to his regiment six weeks prior in a clerical error Elodie herself had committed on purpose. His eyes found hers over the shoulder of a gendarme. She gave him the look she gave men before amputation: steady, unhurried, promising nothing except that she would stay in the room.

They passed him by. A man with no legs below the knee made a poor deserter.

It was the cellar that was going to kill them all.

Because the cellar was where the ledger lived—not under her pillow, not tonight; last night she had taken it down to the wine rack behind the stores, wrapped in oilcloth, because she had woken with the shapeless animal certainty that her cell smelled of ink—and because the cellar was also where she had put Anselme Duquet's brother-in-arms, the Belgian road-mender, three days ago when he came to the gate feverish, coughing blood into his fist.

She counted seconds while the captain worked through the upper floors. Every minute spent on the chapel vestry was a minute not spent on the stairs to the cellar. She fed him rooms. She suggested, sweetly, that the tithe barn ought to be searched thoroughly, knowing the barn was empty but knowing also that empty barns take a long time to search properly. Marguerite, catching her eye across the dispensary, read her perfectly and began loudly inventorying bandages, slowing the whole procession by insisting on counting everything twice.

Then Sister Béatrice came down the service stair, white as the bandages, and said only, in the voice nuns used for deaths in the family, "Sister Oyelaran. The boiler."

The boiler. The boiler stoker was old Tomas, and old Tomas had seen the lanterns in the courtyard and done what frightened men did, which was hide, and he had hidden in the cellar.

Elodie walked up the service stairs with her heart beating somewhere in her throat, smiling at the gendarmes as she passed them, and thought with terrible clarity: *I cannot save the ledger and I cannot save Tomas and I cannot save both, and whichever I choose I choose in the next forty steps.*

* * *

She chose neither, at first. She chose the chapel.

It was instinct more than plan—the one room in the convent that even soldiers entered quietly. She crossed it in the dark, past the saints in their niches, to the iron ring set flush in the stone floor before the high altar. The crypt beneath was a twelfth-century bone-house, dry and cold, reached by a ladder and a hatch that the Mother Superior kept locked against curiosity. Elodie had the key on her own chain, because the dead were part of her jurisdiction too; she buried what the war delivered and wrote down who it had been, and the two duties shared a key.

Down the ladder, oilcloth bundle clutched to her chest like an infant. The dark smelled of stone and old wax and, faintly, of the two hundred and nine years of people layered in the niches along the walls. She set the ledger inside the empty niche she had long ago privately assigned to herself—third from the floor, good light in summer—and stood a moment with her hand flat upon it.

Then she went back up, closed the hatch, dragged the kneeling bench over it, and lit two candles, because a chapel in darkness at five in the morning would draw questions, and a chapel with candles would draw none.

Tomas was still in the cellar. That problem remained, breathing and coughing, thirty feet below the floor the gendarmes were now descending toward.

* * *

What saved them, in the end, was not cleverness. It was the specific, sanctified rudeness of Mother Superior Cécile.

When Captain Clipboard reached the foot of the cellar stair with his lantern, he found an eighty-year-old nun standing in front of the wine racks with her arms folded and her voice pitched to carry up through the floorboards like the wrath of God through a cathedral.

"Captain. You will observe the seal on that rack. Conventual wine is exempt from requisition under the provision of—" and here she cited a decree so obscure, so precisely numbered, that Elodie, listening from above with her ear pressed to the cold stone, understood that the Mother Superior had been waiting years for this exact morning. "If your men open that rack, I will require your name, rank, and unit for the report I am obliged to send to the bishop, the prefecture, and the office of the sector intendant. Separately. In triplicate."

There was a silence down there that Elodie felt in her teeth.

The captain came back up the stairs looking like a man who had stepped on a rake in front of witnesses. His men had found the coal store, the preserves, a crate of surgical spirit—which they confiscated, to Marguerite's theatrical outrage—and no one at all. Of Tomas they found nothing, because Tomas, hearing the boots overhead, had done the second thing frightened men do: he had gone deeper, wriggling into the old coal chute that opened onto the garden wall, and by the time the lanterns swept the cellar he was already three fields away, a coughing shadow swallowed by the winter dark.

Elodie stood by the candles in the chapel and listened to the boots recede and did not feel relief. What she felt was thinner and colder, and it took her until the gate slammed to name it.

It wasn't that they had almost found everything. It was that they had come at all. Fishing expeditions became hunts. Hunts became roundups. And somewhere between the courtyard and the cellar stair, the ledger had stopped being a private act of grief and become a thing with weight in the world—a thing that armed men might cross a frozen courtyard at dawn to find. She had written those names believing no one would ever care enough to read them. Now she knew better, and the knowledge changed the ledger's nature entirely, the way a letter changes once you know it may be opened.

Marguerite found her there, standing between the candle flames with her hand still resting on the kneeling bench.

"They're gone," Marguerite whispered.

"They'll be back."

"You knew they'd come?"

"No." Elodie turned to look at her, and saw her own fear answered in the younger woman's face, and hated that she'd put it there. "I hoped we had more time."

Below their feet, in the dark, under two hundred years of the faithful dead, lay a book with three hundred names in it. Above their heads, in the bell tower, the old clock ticked on, indifferent, keeping its own count.

"Help me move the bench," Elodie said. "And fetch the boy."

Marguerite stared. "Henri? Why? He's asleep—"

"Because a child who doesn't speak and doesn't exist is the safest person in this convent, and I want him where the safest place is." She lifted the bench aside and pulled the iron ring, and the hatch breathed cold air up at them. "And because if they come again, Marguerite, I want to know exactly how many people I can fit down a ladder."

They got the ledger first, and then, wrapped in a blanket and still mostly asleep, Henri, who woke fully on the ladder and looked around the crypt with wide, unafraid eyes, as though he recognized the dark. He settled onto the stone beside the niche without a sound. He pointed at the bones in the far wall, then at the ledger in Elodie's hands, then at himself, and raised his eyebrows—a question, asked entirely in gesture: *Am I in the book too?*

"Not yet," Elodie said softly. But she was already thinking about ink.

It was Marguerite, later, standing in the doorway of the chapel with the dawn grey behind her, who said the thing that turned the cold in Elodie's chest into something with edges.

"There was an officer with them. At the gate—he didn't come in with the others. He stayed by the truck, watching the windows." She hesitated. "He asked me whose handwriting was in our ward book. He said—" her brow furrowed, reaching for the exact words, "—he said it was *a very careful hand*, and that careful hands usually had something to be careful about."

The candles guttered in a draft from under the door. Down in the crypt, Henri slept again, curled against the stone like something planted.

"What officer?" Elodie heard herself ask. "What name?"

"He didn't give one," said Marguerite. "But he's coming back tomorrow. Officially, this time. He said he'd like to inspect the *records*."
