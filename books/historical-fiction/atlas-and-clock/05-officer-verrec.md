## 5. Officer Verrec

The truck that brought him up from the coast left Captain Émile Verrec at the convent gate an hour before dusk, and he insisted on walking the last hundred meters unassisted, which cost him more than he let it.

He had been shot through the left shoulder in October — a private's bullet, unglamorous, the sort of wound that happened to men who had spent too long behind desks and too little at the front. The surgeons in the coastal hospital had dug the ball out cleanly enough, but the shoulder had stiffened into a locked drawer, and the medical board had stamped him *convalescent* with the brisk indifference of men stamping anything. Six weeks of rest, they said. Somewhere quiet.

The requisitioned Convent of Sainte-Agathe was quiet, certainly. It sat above the river town like a grey bird with folded wings, its bell tower leaning a degree or two off true, its orchard a stand of black skeletons furred with frost. Verrec stood at the gate for a moment in the failing light and listened. Wind in the orchard. Water under ice somewhere below. And beneath both, faint as a pulse, the front — thirty kilometers east and closing, a sound less like thunder than like a door being opened and closed in a very large house.

He adjusted the strap of his kit on his good arm and went in.

* * *

They gave him a cell on the second floor with a straw mattress, a washstand, and a window that looked east toward the sound he did not care to look at. A novice with chapped hands brought him soup and bread and told him, in the apologetic voice of someone reciting rules she hadn't written, that the infirmary held forty-one patients at present, that Sister Superior Geneviève governed the house, and that meals were taken in the refectory at seven unless one was bedbound.

"And the nursing staff?" Verrec asked pleasantly.

"Four sisters, two lay nurses, and—" the girl counted silently, "—Mademoiselle Oyelaran."

"Oyelaran." He turned the name over. "That's not a local name."

"No, sir. She came two winters ago. From the south." The girl said it the way people say things they have been told and never thought to question, which is to say: the way people say things they have been told precisely so they will not think about them further.

Verrec thanked her, ate his soup, and slept badly, which was usual.

* * *

In the morning he presented himself at the infirmary for his dressing and found it run with the severe competence of a ship. Wards had been carved out of what must have been dormitories and lecture halls; iron cots stood in ranks; the air smelled of carbolic and woodsmoke and, underneath, the stone-cold smell of old churches. He was directed to a chair by the window at the end of the long room, and it was there, with his shirt off and his useless arm propped on a table, that he first saw Mademoiselle Oyelaran at any distance closer than across a courtyard.

She was not beautiful in the way that made men forget their sentences. She was something better suited to her work: exact. Dark-skinned, perhaps thirty, with a long deliberate face and eyes that moved around a room the way a clerk's finger moves down a column of figures — without hurry, without missing anything. Her hands were steady. When she changed a dressing she spoke to the patient the whole time, low and continuous, not comfort exactly but company, a thread of voice the man could hold onto while the world hurt.

Verrec watched her the way he watched everything now, which was professionally.

His own nurse turned out to be the younger of the two lay women — Marguerite, round-faced, twenty at most, hands quick and slightly clumsy with kindness. She unwrapped his shoulder, made the small involuntary face everyone made at the scar, and set to work with iodine.

"You're intelligence," she said. Not accusing. Just filing.

"I was," Verrec said. "At present I'm a man who can't button his own coat."

"Mm." She dabbed. "Sister Superior says you're to rest."

"Sister Superior sounds like a sensible woman."

"She's a terrifying woman," Marguerite said, and smiled despite herself, and he noted that she glanced down the ward as she said it — glanced, briefly, toward Mademoiselle Oyelaran, as if checking whether the older woman had heard her being human.

Across the room, Oyelaran had come to the end of her patient's bandaging and turned to the small desk wedged between two cots — the infirmary's administrative afterthought, stacked with ration forms and admission slips. She drew a ledger toward her and began to write.

Verrec looked away, then looked back. There was nothing remarkable about a nurse writing in a ledger. Hospitals ran on ledgers the way engines ran on coal. But there was something in the way she bent over this one — the particular stillness of her shoulders, the way she angled the book away from the aisle with no apparent deliberation, the way her pen paused before each entry as though she were choosing words rather than copying them.

He filed that too.

* * *

His convalescence gave him a routine within three days, and he was honest enough with himself to admit he built it deliberately. Mornings, his dressing. Then a walk — first only to the end of the corridor, then the cloister, then, by the fifth day, the whole circuit of the grounds, one hand trailing the frozen wall like a man taking a wall's pulse. Afternoons he sat in the refectory or the common room and did what convalescing officers do: read week-old newspapers, drink chicory coffee, lose extravagantly at cards to a sapper lieutenant with half a lung.

And he talked to people. That was the part of the work that never quite left a man. He talked to the sisters about the weather and the state of the roof. He talked to the wounded about their villages, their regiments, their mothers' cooking — and if their answers were sometimes useful later, well, that was the nature of conversation; nobody had ever been interrogated by being listened to kindly.

On the sixth day, Sister Superior Geneviève received him in her office, a cold room smelling of ink and candle wax, and asked him directly what he had been before his wound.

"Records," Verrec said. "Mostly. Personnel records, casualty returns, the great machinery of knowing who exists." He smiled. "A war is fought with shells, Sister, but it is administered with paper. I was paper."

"And now you are resting from paper."

"Now I am resting from paper."

"Good," she said, in a tone suggesting that whatever he had rested from, she intended it to stay rested. She dismissed him with a blessing that felt like a border checkpoint.

It was on the stairs going down that he met Elodie Oyelaran coming up, a lamp in one hand and a bundle of linen under the other arm, and they performed the small sideways dance of two people passing in a narrow stairwell, and she said, "Captain," having clearly already learned his name and rank, and he said, "Mademoiselle," and that was all — except that her eyes went to his sling with a professional assessment so swift and complete that he felt diagnosed rather than observed, and then her eyes went past him, up the stairs, toward the office door, and back down again, and the whole survey took less than a breath.

"Mind the fourth step," she added. "It's loose. We've reported it twice." And she was gone upward, lamplight swinging.

Loose step. Reported twice. Everything in this house, he thought, was reported twice and fixed never. He descended carefully, admiring the economy of the warning: helpfulness offered freely, establishing goodwill, costing nothing, revealing nothing.

He decided he liked her, in the way one likes an opponent worth playing.

* * *

The personnel files surfaced — or rather failed to surface — on the ninth day.

It began, as these things did, with a letter he should probably have ignored: a routine inquiry forwarded from his old section, marked for his attention because he happened to be convalescing within the district and could, if convenient, assist. Three names of men from a local regiment whose files could not be located. Families writing. Standard peacetime-in-wartime muddle.

Except that Verrec had spent four years inside the great machinery of knowing who existed, and he knew the difference between a file lost and a file removed. Lost files scattered randomly. Removed files clustered — and these three clustered around a single incident the previous spring, a field court-martial conducted east of here in the last bad week of the offensive, hushed up even by the standards of hushed-up things. He had seen the edge of it once, in a routing slip, a signature, a word — *desertion* — and had done what sensible men in bureaus did: looked away.

Now the families were writing letters, and the letters had drifted like snow into his convalescence, and looking away had become harder to do at close range.

He mentioned it casually first, the way one introduces a knife to a dinner table — laid beside the plate, not yet picked up. At supper in the refectory, to the room at large: whether the town's records office still functioned, whether the curé kept registers of the men billeted locally, whether the hospital saw many men from the 214th. The sisters answered about registers. The sapper lieutenant answered about the 214th's bad reputation, then looked alarmed at his own mouth and studied his coffee.

Elodie Oyelaran, seated at the far end with a tray she was eating from efficiently and without pleasure, said nothing at all.

That was the answer, of course. Silence of that particular quality — attentive silence, silence with its head slightly tilted — was worth three pages of talk. Verrec kept his gaze moving, rested it nowhere, and changed the subject to the price of eggs.

* * *

He found his real opening two days later, in the infirmary, by accident, or by what he would afterward permit himself to call accident.

Marguerite had finished his dressing early and been called away to a hemorrhage in the far ward, and Verrec, instead of putting his shirt back on, had remained by the window doing his exercises — the slow grinding rotations the physiotherapist had prescribed, jaw set, sweat standing on his forehead, the locked shoulder giving ground grudgingly millimeter by millimeter. It was ugly work and he preferred witnesses absent for it, which was why the room had emptied around him. Except that it hadn't entirely.

Elodie Oyelaran was at the desk ten feet away, and she had been there the whole time, writing.

He realized, mid-rotation, that he had stopped caring who watched — the pain had burned that vanity off — and so he simply continued, breathing in threes, until the exercise released him and he sat back, wrung out, and reached for his shirt.

"You do that wrong," she said.

He looked up. She had not raised her head particularly, but she was watching him now over the top of the ledger, and her voice carried neither sympathy nor criticism, only the flat certainty of expertise.

"Do I?"

"The shoulder wants heat first. Five minutes, hot compress, then the rotation. Cold muscle fights you; warm muscle negotiates." She said *negotiates* the way other people said a diagnosis. "I'll bring one tomorrow. You'll gain ten degrees of arc in a fortnight instead of a month."

"That's generous."

"It's efficient. Men who heal slowly occupy beds." But something in her mouth suggested this was the coin she used where kindness would embarrass them both.

She returned to her writing. Verrec pulled his shirt over his good arm and considered, and decided — against every instinct his training had, instincts which advised patience, patience, patience — to move now, while her guard was set for medicine rather than war.

"May I ask you something professional?" he said. "Since we are colleagues of a kind. You keep records; I kept records."

Her pen completed its stroke and stopped. She did not look up. "Records are the skeleton of a hospital, Captain. Ask."

"A family wrote to my old section about a man of the 214th. Missing since spring. His file cannot be produced — not mislaid, mind, *cannot be produced*, which is a different animal entirely." He kept his voice light, conversational, a man chatting across a ward. "I wondered whether a hospital might see such men pass through. Wounded men whose paperwork arrives damaged, or doesn't arrive. Men who can't be identified."

"We get men without papers," Elodie said. "War takes pockets first."

"And when they cannot speak for themselves? When they die without a name?"

Now she looked up, and her face was perfectly composed, perfectly courteous, and utterly opaque — a shut door painted to resemble an open one. "Then we bury them as best we can, Captain, and pray God keeps better records than we do."

"Of course," Verrec said. "Though I've found God's record-keeping is generally delegated to clerks. Mine, once." He smiled, rueful, self-deprecating, the smile of a tired man embarrassed by his own former bureaucracy. "I confess the missing files trouble me more than they should. Three families writing letters into a hole in the archive. Somebody signed those men away or lost them, and either way somebody knows. It's the knowing that keeps me awake. You'd be surprised how rarely anything keeps me awake."

"Wounds keep our men awake," Elodie said mildly. "I find that sufficient cause."

The rebuff was flawless — gentle, reasonable, impossible to take offense at. He admired it openly, almost audibly, and saw that she registered his admiration and did not like it.

"The compress," she said, rising, gathering a chart. "Tomorrow. Heat first, then rotation. If you're going to occupy a bed in my house, Captain, you'll occupy it efficiently."

"Your house?"

"The sisters' house. My ward." She was already moving down the row of cots, and over her shoulder, without turning, in a voice pitched exactly for him and no one else: "We keep careful records here, Captain Verrec. You needn't worry that anyone is forgotten."

Then she was among the patients, and her voice had become what it became for them — that low continuous thread of company — and Verrec sat by the window with his ruined shoulder throbbing and understood that he had just heard the most elaborate denial of his career.

*Anyone is forgotten.* Not: *we forget no one.* The distinction was exquisite. She had not lied to him. She had simply answered a different, larger question than the one he'd asked — and in doing so had told him everything.

* * *

That evening he took his exercise in the cloister, because the chapel held vespers and the common room held cards and he wanted neither. Snow had begun again, fine and dry, ticking against the arcade roof. He walked the four sides of the square with his collar up, counting laps, letting the cold work on the shoulder where heat could not reach, and it was on the third lap that he noticed the light.

One window on the infirmary's upper floor still glowed — a small yellow rectangle in the dark gable, burning long after the wards had gone dark. He had watched it for several nights now without thinking about it, and tonight he thought about it. The night nurse made rounds with a lantern; she did not sit at a desk. The sisters kept monastic hours and were asleep by nine. Whoever kept that light kept it alone, and late, and seated.

He stopped at the corner of the arcade, snow settling on his shoulders, and looked up at that small stubborn square of light the way a cartographer looks at an unmapped coastline — not approaching, not yet, only noting its position, its bearing, the way it refused to go out.

Three missing files. Three names. One woman three floors up writing by lamplight in a ledger she angled away from the aisle.

Verrec turned up his collar and walked on, unhurried, courteous even to the dark, and began composing in his head the pleasant, harmless questions he would ask her at breakfast.

Heat first, then rotation. Warm muscle negotiates.

So, he suspected, did everything else in this house — given time, and warmth, and a reason to trust. He had a month of convalescence, a letter authorizing assistance with inquiries, and the particular patience of a man who had spent four years learning that wars were not lost at fronts but in the spaces between names.

The light upstairs burned until nearly midnight. He knew because he was awake to see it go out, and because he counted, as he fell asleep, the hours remaining until morning — and, without quite deciding to, the names he would need to ask her about: three.

Though some instinct, old and accurate, whispered that wherever her lamp burned, three would prove to be only where the counting started.
