## 6. Atlas Without Borders

The boy had a system for the silence.

It was not that he couldn't speak. He had checked, in the first days, in the small hours when the ward went quiet and only the wind worried the shutters — he had opened his mouth and tried, and found his voice still there, waiting, rusty as a gate no one used. It worked. That was never the problem.

The problem was that words were doors, and behind every door was the thing.

So he built a country instead.

He built it on the backs of old pharmacy flyers that Marguerite brought him because the paper was thick and took the pencil well. He built it with a pencil stub Sister Béatrice found for him in a drawer of dead things — broken thermometers, a cracked magnifying glass, a rosary missing nine beads. He built it street by street: the rue des Tanneurs where the tannins ran brown in the gutters after rain; the square with the plane trees cut in a ring so the market stalls would fit; the bridge with the iron railings his father called fish bones because you could pretend to be an eel slipping through them if your father lifted you by the arms and swung you out over the water, which he did, every Sunday, until the last Sunday there was.

He drew the streets from above, the way a bird would see them, because from above nothing burned. From above the town was whole forever. He could go down any street he liked and nothing happened at the end of it.

Marguerite sat with him every afternoon when her rounds allowed, which was most afternoons because she traded extra shifts to make them allow it. She didn't ask questions anymore. She had stopped asking around the fourth day, when she understood that questions made him fold in on himself like paper held too near a flame. Instead she narrated. She was very good at narrating.

"That's a wide boulevard," she'd say, tapping the page with one finger. "Somebody important must live on this boulevard."

"The fountain," she'd say another day. "Is there a fountain? There should be a fountain. Every proper town has a fountain and a dog tied to the railing beside it."

He almost smiled at that, the day she said it, because there *was* a dog, a yellow one belonging to the tobacconist, and it had bitten him once and he had cried and his father had bought him a barley sugar on the way home and sworn him to secrecy, which was the first secret Henri had ever kept, and he had kept it beautifully.

He drew the fountain. He did not draw the dog. The dog belonged to the part of the town where his father's name was true, and that part stayed shut.

The nurses thought he was simple, or struck dumb by shock, or maybe half-witted — he heard them whispering at the dispensary door, thinking the ward asleep. *Poor little thing. Doesn't know his own name, probably.* They were wrong, but being wrong about him kept him safe, so he let them be wrong. He let them feed him and wash his collar and call him *petit* in the soft voices women used for broken things.

Only Elodie looked at his maps like they meant something.

She came at night mostly, after the last rounds, when the oil lamps were trimmed low and the ward smelled of carbolic and wet wool. She would sit on the edge of the next bed — not his, never crowding him — and study what he'd drawn that day with her head tilted, and once she said, quietly, as if to herself:

"Arcaded arcade. Two-story houses. That's north-country building. You don't see that south of the Somme."

She hadn't asked him anything. She'd just said it and left it on the blanket between them like a coin she wasn't asking him to pick up.

That was why, on the night it finally broke open, it was Elodie who was there, and not accident but something more like gravity.

* * *

It began with the atlas.

Marguerite found it in a crate of books sent up from some requisitioned school, water-stained and missing its spine, and she put it on his lap without ceremony — *here, petit, since you like maps* — and went back to folding bandages.

He opened it the way other children opened presents.

The paper was heavy and the countries lay flat and colored under his fingers: pink empires, green colonies, blue rivers threading through everything like veins. He had owned a book like this once. His father had owned it, rather, and kept it on the shelf above the stove, and on winter evenings he'd take it down and spread it across the kitchen table and put his big blunt finger on their town, which was too small to have a name printed, only a dot, a crumb of ink.

"Here," his father would say. "You are here. And here—" the finger sliding east across borders drawn in red and black, "—is everyone else. Whole world of them. Millions of towns, millions of dots, every dot full of people who think their dot is the center."

"And is it?" Henri had asked, aged four or five, mouth sticky with supper.

"Course it is," his father said. "Every single one. That's the joke nobody gets."

Henri turned the pages now. He found the sea, which he had seen twice. He found mountains he would never climb and cities whose names were music from a far-off instrument. He traced a river with his fingertip and pretended it was warm.

And then, because the book was honest in the way books are, he came to the page where the war was.

It was printed right there, in colors just as cheerful as the empires': a great grey stain spreading west from the edge of the map, and front lines marked in jagged red like a wound that had been stitched badly and torn again. His finger followed the red line. It crossed the rivers. It crossed the railways. It moved toward the region where the arcaded streets and the two-story houses stood, toward the dot that was a crumb of ink, toward—

His hand stopped working.

The ward went on around him — someone moaning in sleep, Marguerite's scissors snipping, the wind. But inside Henri the map had become the thing, the thing behind all the doors, because maps were how his father had explained the world and maps were also how the world had come to eat them, arriving first as notices nailed to the arcade posts, then as boots, then as a gray morning in the square where the plane trees grew, and a wooden wall that was not for shooting birds.

"Henri."

Elodie's voice. When had she arrived? She was kneeling in front of him now, on the cold stone floor, so that her eyes were level with his. She had taken his frozen hand off the page without pulling at it, gently, the way you unhook a fish.

"You've gone somewhere," she said. Not a question either. She never asked questions. "Where did you go?"

And Henri, six years old and hollowed out by sixty days of holding a door shut with his whole small body, felt the door swing.

"He was there," he said.

His voice sounded strange. Rusty, like the gate. Like someone else using it.

"Who was, mon cœur?"

"My papa." The word came out in pieces. He hadn't said it aloud since the woman left him at the convent door, and saying it now was like setting down a sack of stones he'd forgotten he was carrying until his arms unbent. "In the square. Before the wall."

Elodie did not move. Did not flinch, did not do the face adults did, the soft horror face that made everything worse. She stayed kneeling, absolutely steady, and waited. That steadiness was the only reason the rest of it came out.

"They said he ran away." Henri's eyes were fixed on the atlas, on the red stitched wound. "From the army. They had a word for it. *Déserteur.* Mama said don't ever say it, say he was a mason, say he builds walls, not runs from them. But everybody knew. The tobacconist spat. Madame Fournier took back the eggs we paid for." His breath hitched. "They took him anyway. Even though running away means you're a coward, and my papa wasn't a coward, he carried me through the flood water when the cellar filled, I weighed a hundred pounds, he carried me and laughed—"

The sentence collapsed. Elodie's hand closed over both of his, warm and dry and certain.

"What was his name?" she asked.

Not *what happened*, not *are you sure*, not any of the questions that would have shut the door again. Just that. A name. As if a name were the thing that mattered most in the ruined world, and maybe — Henri understood even then, dimly, in the way children understand the deep currents under adult weather — maybe to this woman it was.

"Aimé Oyelaran," Henri said.

Something crossed Elodie's face then. Fast, gone before he could read it — but not horror, and not pity. Something closer to recognition, the look of someone finding in a dark room the exact object they'd been searching for.

"Aimé," she repeated, tasting it, keeping it. "Aimé Oyelaran."

"He carried me on his shoulders past the fish-bone bridge." Henri was crying now, he realized, without drama, the tears simply present, running steadily the way a tap left on runs. "At the end he wasn't scared. He looked for me in the crowd. He stood on his toes to look over the heads. So I would know he wasn't afraid. He made me watch so I would know."

"I know," Elodie said. "I'm glad you watched. Someone should have."

Nobody had ever said anything like that to him. Everyone else had wanted him not to have seen — hush, petit, don't think of it, forget. Elodie was the first person in the world to say the seeing was right.

"I draw the town from up high," Henri confessed, hiccuping, pointing at the weeks of pharmacy flyers stacked on the chair. "From the sky. Because from the sky nothing's broken."

"No," Elodie agreed softly. "From the sky it's all still there."

"Mama said forget his face. She gave me money. She said forget, and I try, but—" He pressed his fists against his eyes. "If I forget his face then nobody remembers him at all. Because of the word. Nobody writes down deserters. They write down heroes and they write down counts of ammunition and they don't write him anywhere."

The lamp hissed. Down the ward, a man coughed twice and slept on.

Then Elodie did a thing Henri would carry out of that winter and through every winter after, though he would not understand its size until he was grown. She leaned forward and said, in a voice pitched for him alone:

"Then he will be written down. I promise you. Aimé Oyelaran will be written somewhere that doesn't rub out."

* * *

After that night the silence changed shape. It stopped being a wall and became a place he visited sometimes, a quiet room he could enter and leave, instead of a country he was imprisoned in.

He spoke to Marguerite first about ordinary things — that the soup had too much barley, that Sister Béatrice's shoes squeaked on the left foot only, that he was seven in March, nearly. Marguerite received each sentence like treasure washed ashore, and cried once in the linen closet where she thought no one saw, and after that brought him two barley sugars from the village, and he ate one and saved the other and told her about the tobacconist's dog, and laughed, actually laughed, at the memory of the bite and the secret, and she laughed too until she wheezed.

But it was Elodie he showed the real maps to.

Because Elodie had started something he wasn't supposed to know about, except that he did know, because he was small and quiet and invisible, and invisible people see everything.

He saw her at night at the dispensary table with a ledger open under the lamp, writing in that careful upright hand of hers, and he saw that what she wrote was names — long columns of names — and that she wrote them the way monks copied scripture, slowly, as though each one cost something and was worth the cost.

One night, when the ward was asleep and the wind was doing its work on the shutters, Henri slid off his bed and padded across the cold stone in his socks and stood beside her table.

She didn't startle. She just moved the lamp shade a fraction so the light fell on a clean corner of the page, and looked at him.

"I know a name," Henri said.

"So do I," said Elodie. "I know three hundred and ten of them. I'm always short one."

"Aimé Oyelaran," Henri said. "Rue des Tanneurs, behind the fish-bone bridge. Mason. Carried his son through the flood. Wasn't afraid."

Elodie dipped her pen. Henri watched her write his father's name into the world — the *O* round and deliberate, the letters standing straight as soldiers who had chosen their side — and when she finished she turned the ledger toward him and said:

"Read it back to me. Names have to be spoken and written both, or they're only half-saved."

Henri read it aloud. Aimé Oyelaran. Half-saved no longer.

He was climbing back into bed, warm for the first time in weeks despite the cold, when Elodie blew out the lamp — and in the sudden dark he heard her stop moving. Heard her breath catch, small and sharp, the sound of someone who has just remembered something they were safer forgetting.

"Go to sleep, mon cœur," she said, and her voice was perfectly steady, and that was how he knew something had frightened her.

He didn't learn until later what it was. But he heard, that same night, through the wall of the sisters' stairwell where he had gone for water, the low voices in the office — and one of them was new, a man's voice, courteous and unhurried, asking questions in the pleasant tone of a person sharpening a knife while he talks.

*"Tell me, Sister — who keeps such remarkably careful records here?"*

Henri pulled the blanket to his chin and lay in the dark doing what he did best: staying silent, and watching, and keeping count of the doors.
