## 7. The Clock in the Bell Tower

The clock had kept time at Sainte-Agathe for two hundred and eleven years, and Sister Béatrice knew this because she had counted the years herself, one by one, the way other women counted beads. She had wound it every Sunday of her life in the convent—first as a novice on a ladder too tall for her, her hands shaking at the height; later as the keeper of the mechanism, the one the Mother Superior called "our horologist" with a small dry smile, as though it were a joke that Béatrice loved a machine more than most of the living.

It was not a joke. The clock was honest in a way that people had stopped being. It asked nothing but oil and attention, and it gave back the only thing the war had not yet managed to corrupt: the hour. Whatever else happened—whatever the wireless murmured, whatever the trucks carried through the square at night, whatever the hospital wards below swallowed and gave back as silence—the clock said noon at noon. Béatrice had built her faith around that. Not around the Host, exactly, not around the liturgy, though she loved both, but around the plain animal comfort of a thing that did what it promised.

She climbed the tower on the last morning of November with the oil can in one hand and the winding crank over her shoulder like a soldier's rifle, and she knew something was wrong before she reached the mechanism room, because the sound was missing. For two hundred and eleven years the clock had ticked through the tower like a second heartbeat, audible in the stairwell, in the rafters, in the bones of the building. This morning the tower was silent, and silence in the tower was like silence in a chest: not peace. Absence.

The great pendulum hung still. The escapement sat open, the pallet fork resting in the gap between teeth of the escape wheel, and the whole train of gears above it—the going train, the striking train, the wheels her predecessor's predecessor had cut by hand from gunmetal—stood locked and motionless, like a congregation frozen mid-prayer.

Béatrice set down the oil can and did not touch anything. That was the first rule of the mechanism, her rule, taught to every novice who ever climbed these stairs: *when it stops, look before you lay a hand on it.* A stopped clock is a patient. You diagnose before you treat.

She looked.

The cause, when she found it, was almost insultingly small. A wire. Not even a clock part—a length of baling wire, the kind used on the hay carts, the kind that had become the universal currency of the war economy, holding together boots and carts and barricades and, apparently, now the conscience of Sainte-Agathe. Someone had threaded it through the case door and around the pendulum rod, tying the pendulum fast to the frame. A child's knot. A soldier's knot. Done quickly, in the dark, by someone who knew the tower well enough to climb it and not well enough—or not caring enough—to understand what they were stopping.

Béatrice stood in the cold of the mechanism room for a long time, her breath making small ghosts, and listened to the silence the way she imagined a physician listens to a heart that has just stopped on the table.

The tower door at the base was kept locked. She had the only iron key besides the Mother Superior's. Which meant the person who had climbed here in the night had either taken the key from the hook in the porter's lodge, or had come up the outside, over the lead roof, the way the bell-ringers had done during the last visitation of plague, two centuries ago, when the rule had been that the tower be sealed and the bells be rung from the outside gallery.

She untied the wire. It took her some minutes; the knot was wet-frozen and her fingers were seventy years old. She coiled the wire and put it in her apron pocket. Evidence, she thought, and then, wryly, *of what?* Of a stopped clock. The war had made detectives of everyone and given no one anything worth detecting.

She set the pendulum swinging.

The escapement took the first tooth. The escape wheel turned. Above her, gear by gear, the train came awake, and the tick resumed—soft, then insistent, then the full deep heartbeat of it filling the tower—and Béatrice found, to her considerable embarrassment, that her eyes were wet.

She was not a woman who cried at machinery. She was not a woman who cried at much of anything; grief had been wrung out of her in installments over sixty years, the way water is wrung from laundry, until what remained was this dry, efficient, useful person who wound clocks and changed dressings and did not weep. But there was something about the sound. The tick coming back into the tower. It was like watching a man breathe again.

She wound the barrels, checked the hands against her pocket watch—two minutes slow, the pendulum having been held too long—and corrected them, and stood a while longer in the mechanism room with her hand flat against the case, feeling the vibration come up through the wood into her palm like a pulse answering a pulse.

Then she went down to tell the Mother Superior that the clock had been murdered and revived, and that she intended to find out by whom.

* * *

Mother Superior Cécile received the news in her office, which smelled of lamp oil and camphor and the particular staleness of a room whose windows had been painted over with lime-wash for the duration. The blackouts. Even the Mother Superior lived in a brown dusk now, her world reduced to lamplight and memory.

"Wire," Cécile repeated. She was a small woman, sixty-eight, with hands that had run this convent through two epidemics and one requisition and now through a war, and she turned her ring slowly as she listened, an old habit, a rosary without beads. "Through the case door."

"Whoever it was wanted the clock stopped, Mother. Not broken. Stopped. There's a difference. Breaking is anger. This was—" Béatrice searched for it. "Intention. A message, maybe. Or a superstition."

"Or a soldier who wanted to know what silence sounds like."

"Soldiers can't reach the tower. The lodge was locked. I checked with Père Anselme this morning."

"Then a nurse. A nun. A boy." The Mother Superior's eyes went, without her willing it, toward the window and the lime-washed glass beyond which, somewhere down the hill, the hospital wards held their cargo of broken men, and one silent six-year-old who drew maps on the backs of requisition forms. "We have more souls under this roof than at any time in the convent's history, and half of them are not here by choice."

"The clock has been stopped before," Béatrice said. "In '70, during the last war. In the plague years. It's an old instinct. When death comes into a town, people want to stop time. They think if the clock stands still, the moment will stand still, and the moment is survivable, and the next one need never arrive."

"And what do you think, Béatrice? You're the horologist. You tell me what it means when a clock is stopped by hand in the night."

Béatrice took the coil of wire from her apron and laid it on the desk between them, a small gray question mark of metal.

"I think," she said, "that whoever did it was not trying to keep the moment from arriving. I think they were trying to keep a promise to it."

The Mother Superior looked at the wire for a long moment. Then she looked up, and Béatrice saw something in her face she had seen perhaps four times in forty years: the Mother Superior afraid.

"Yesterday," Cécile said quietly, "a dispatch rider stopped at the gate. He would not come in. He stood in the road and drank from his canteen and looked at the hospital windows, and then he rode on toward the coast without delivering anything. I sent Sister Thérèse after him. He told her the line at the river is gone. Not broken—*gone*. That the divisions that held it are names on paper now. He told her the evacuation schedules are being drawn up in the prefecture, and that when they come, they will come in days, not weeks."

"He could be a rumor-monger. Half the riders are."

"He could be. And the other half are the only honest men left in this country, because they have nothing left to lie for." The ring turned, turned. "Béatrice, I want the clock kept running. Whatever happens. Whatever they tell us, whatever falls, whoever climbs those stairs in the night—I want that clock to say noon at noon until the day this convent ceases to exist. Can you promise me that?"

Béatrice thought of the mechanism above them, two hundred and eleven years of gunmetal and patience. She thought of the wire in her pocket. She thought of the strange new arithmetic that had entered all their lives—the counting of beds, of berths, of days.

"I can promise you the clock, Mother," she said. "I can't promise you the tower. Shells don't consult horologists."

"Nothing consults anyone anymore." The Mother Superior rose, and at the window she stood a moment in her brown dusk, a small black figure against a small gray light. "That is the whole of it, isn't it. We used to be a house of God. Then we were a hospital. Now we are a waiting room, and even the waiting has begun to run out."

* * *

The rumor arrived properly by afternoon, the way rumors do—not riding in with dispatch riders but seeping up through the town like groundwater, finding every crack. Béatrice heard it in fragments in the infirmary corridor, in the refectory, at the dispensary window where the village women came for iodine and aspirin and stayed to trade the day's intelligence like currency.

The front at the river was collapsing. The front at the river had already collapsed. The front at the river was holding, but the front behind it had collapsed, which was worse. The English were evacuating from the coast. The English were landing. The Germans were forty kilometers away. Twenty. Sixty. The prefecture had burned its files. The prefecture had burned its files *selectively*, which meant something, everyone agreed, though no one could say what.

By evening the town had the particular quality of a held breath. Béatrice had seen it before, in the cholera summer of her girlhood: the streets too quiet, the shutters closed at hours when they should be open, dogs unattended, and everyone walking a little too fast, as though the day were a room with a low ceiling and they were all tall.

She went up the tower at dusk, out of habit and out of duty, and found the mechanism running clean and true, and stayed longer than she needed to, because the tower was the only place in the convent where the war was not. Up here there was only the tick and the cold and the view: the town below with its thin threads of chimney smoke, the hills to the east going blue and then black, and somewhere beyond those hills, a river, and beyond the river, whatever was coming.

She found she was counting. It was what horologists did; it was what her whole life had trained her for. She counted the days since the last convoy of wounded. She counted the beds she knew were occupied—she had helped change them; she knew the number to within two. She counted the nuns, the nurses, the cooks, the children—one child—and arrived, without wanting to, at the question the whole convent was circling and no one would say aloud:

*When they come with the trucks, how many seats will there be, and who will be counted?*

Because that was the thing about clocks and about wars: both of them reduced everything to numbers. The clock reduced the day to hours, honestly, mercifully. The war reduced people to numbers too, but not honestly—never honestly. It counted the men who could still hold a rifle and let the rest go unnamed. It counted officers before orderlies, the documented before the undocumented, the born-before-the-right-date before the born-after. Somewhere below her feet, in the wards, there were men whose names existed in no file but one—a ledger she had glimpsed once, by accident, in Nurse Oyelaran's hands, and had pretended not to see, because some secrets you protect by not knowing them.

And there was the boy. Henri, they called him. Who had arrived at the door in November with coins and a note and no name that anyone would swear to. Who did not, in the eyes of any ministry or muster roll, exist. Béatrice had lived long enough to know what happened to people who did not exist when the trucks came. The trucks had seats for the counted. The uncounted stayed.

The first stars were out over the eastern hills. From up here, the hills looked peaceful. They always did. That was the great obscenity of geography, Béatrice thought—it looked like nothing from a distance. The river, the line, the collapse: all of it invisible, all of it reducible to a rumor in a town square, while the clock said six o'clock, and said it honestly, and would say it at seven, and at eight, no matter what crossed the hills.

*Time doesn't take sides,* she thought. *That's its one virtue and its one cruelty. It runs for the wounded and the whole, the saved and the abandoned, at the same rate, without mercy or malice, until it doesn't run at all.*

She went down the stairwell in the dark, one hand on the wall, counting the steps as she always did—forty-one—and at the bottom, in the corridor, she met Nurse Oyelaran coming from the wards, a lamp in her hand, her face in its permanent state of controlled exhaustion that Béatrice had learned to read the way she read dials.

"Sister. The Mother Superior is asking for you and me both. Dispensary, in an hour." A pause. The lamp flame bent in a draft from the door. "There's a man from the prefecture. He's been asking about our records."

"Records," Béatrice said. "What records?"

"That's what I intend to find out."

Oyelaran went on toward the wards, and Béatrice stood in the cold corridor a moment, listening. Above her, muffled through stone and timber, the clock struck the hour—seven strokes, unhurried, honest—and the sound went out over the town like a small defiance, like a hand raised against the dark.

Then, beneath the last stroke, far off to the east, so faint it might have been thunder, might have been a door slamming in a distant house, came a sound that Béatrice had not heard since the summer, and had hoped never to hear again this close: artillery. Not near. Not yet. But no longer beyond the hills.

She stood in the corridor and counted the seconds between the flash she could not see and the sound she had. Eleven seconds. She had been a horologist long enough to know what eleven seconds meant.

Three and a half kilometers. The war had crossed the river.
