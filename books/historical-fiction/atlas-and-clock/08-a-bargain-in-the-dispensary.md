## 8. A Bargain in the Dispensary

The dispensary smelled of camphor and old plaster, and in winter it was the only room in the convent that kept its warmth, because the medicine could not be allowed to freeze and so it was allowed everything else. Elodie had come to think of it as the hospital's heart, in the way that a pump is a heart: mechanical, essential, indifferent. She counted out morphine tablets into their brown envelopes and let the work quiet her hands.

Three days since the clock stopped. Two since the artillery had crossed the river. The town had not yet learned what to do with the sound. The wounded asked about it with their eyes when they couldn't with their voices, and she had taken to answering before they asked — *far off, still far off* — whether or not it was true.

The door opened without a knock.

Officer Verrec stood in it with his cap under his arm and the particular courtesy of a man who has already decided to enter and is merely informing you of it. He was walking well now, she noticed. Ten days ago he had come up from the ambulance with a shrapnel graze along his ribs and an air of finding the whole arrangement beneath him but tolerable; now he moved like someone whose body had been returned to him and who intended to use it.

"Sister Oyelaran."

"Nurse. There are no sisters left but the sisters."

"My mistake." He came in anyway. "The Mother Superior said I'd find you here. She said you keep the dispensary, the records, and — I quote her — *everything else that holds still*."

Béatrice would never have said such a thing. Which meant Verrec had said it himself and attributed it, a small theft dressed as charm. Elodie did not look up from her envelopes. "Did you need something for the pain?"

"I needed something for boredom." He settled against the shelf opposite her, unhurried, making himself part of the room's furniture. "I've been reading the convent library. Did you know there are four hundred books and three hundred of them are about saints? The other hundred are agricultural. I've begun to feel I'm being prepared for either canonization or turnip farming."

"Both are quieter than intelligence work."

Something moved behind his eyes at that — not surprise exactly. Confirmation. He knew that she knew what he was, and she had just told him she knew, and neither of them had raised their voice. It was going to be like this, then.

"You write a very careful hand," he said.

"I write a legible one. There's a difference. Careful handwriting is for people hiding something."

"Is it?" He smiled. "I found a requisition ledger in the office. Rations, dressings, splints. Every entry in the same even script, nothing blotted, nothing corrected. I thought: here is a woman who does not make mistakes on paper. And then I thought: no. Here is a woman who makes her mistakes somewhere else."

Elodie finished the envelope she was filling, folded the flap, and set it in the wooden box marked *Ward II* before she answered. Her hands were steady. She made sure of it before he could see.

"What is it you want, Officer Verrec?"

"Names," he said.

The word sat between them like a coin on a table.

"There were eleven men in a transport that passed through here in October," he went on, in the tone of a man discussing freight. "Eleven men entered your registers, and eleven men appear in the divisional returns, and yet when the bureau cross-checked last month, nine families had received no notification of any kind. No wounds report, no transfer notice, nothing. Nine families, Nurse, writing letters to a bureau that has no answer for them because somewhere between this convent and Paris, those men became — administratively speaking — fog."

"The bureau loses things. It's a large bureau."

"It does." He agreed with her easily, which was worse than argument. "Paper burns, clerks die, convoys are shelled. Fog happens. But I've seen your requisitions. You don't produce fog. So either the men's papers never reached Paris—" he paused, delicately, "—or they never left this building at all, except by some route the bureau doesn't have a file for."

The boiler ticked in the wall. Somewhere above them Henri was being taught his letters by Marguerite, and Elodie could hear nothing of it, but she thought of him anyway — the boy who officially did not exist, drawn in chalk on the ward slate as *Henri D.*, the D standing for nothing, invented by Elodie at seven in the morning while Sister Superior watched with her thin mouth.

"Suppose," she said slowly, "that names existed somewhere they shouldn't. What would you want with them?"

"To send them home." He said it simply, and he looked directly at her while he said it, and that was the whole trick of him, she realized: he never flinched at the moment a liar would. Either he was telling the truth or he was the best she had ever seen. "A mother in Roubaix has been waiting since October to learn whether her son is in a hospital or a grave. That is not an abstraction to me. I process notifications. It is most of what my bureau actually does, beneath the romance everyone imagines — we are clerks with rank. We tell women how their sons died. When the paperwork exists."

"And when it doesn't?"

"Then the mothers wait forever. That seems to me a cruelty we could amend."

He let it stand there. Camphor and ticking. Outside, far off, the war cleared its throat once and went quiet again, and both of them glanced toward the window without meaning to, and that small shared reflex — that was a technique too, probably. Making himself human. Making them alike.

"And in exchange for these names," Elodie said, "you offer what? Postage?"

"Protection." The word arrived flat, unornamented, and all the more frightening for it. "There will be more raids. The deserter sweeps are getting closer to the front, which means more thorough, which means less sensible. A sergeant with a list and a bayonet does not distinguish between a man who abandoned his post and a boy with no papers who was left at your door by a woman with money. The next inspection may not be satisfied with the chapel crypt." He saw her face change and had the grace not to smile at it. "Yes. I know about the crypt. I know about quite a lot, Nurse Oyelaran. None of it has gone into a report. Consider what that means."

It meant he had already chosen a side, or wanted her to believe he had. It meant he had walked past danger and pocketed it, and now he was unwrapping it in front of her like a gift, and gifts were never free.

"If I gave you names," she said, "how would I ever know where they went? A name leaves my hands and becomes a telegram, or becomes evidence, or becomes a line in a court-martial file. I can't follow it. I have to take on faith that a stranger in a good coat loved mothers."

"No," he agreed. "You'd have to trust me. That's the entire transaction. I understand why it's a poor bargain." He put his cap back on, unhurried. "So don't decide tonight. Don't decide this week. But consider this: whatever is written down in this building will not survive what's coming. The artillery you hear is not a rumor anymore. When the order comes — and it will come within weeks, I promise you, I have read the maps — there will be trucks, and berths, and lists, and every list will be written by someone. Names will save people or fail them, but either way they will decide. You are holding a great many decisions in whatever drawer you think I don't know about, and decisions decay, Nurse. They rot faster than paper does."

At the door he stopped, one hand on the frame, and turned back with the expression of a man remembering a courtesy.

"For what it's worth — the soldier in October. The first one. The one buried as Unknown Male." Elodie did not move, did not breathe, and he watched her not do it. "His identification disc was logged at the aid station two kilometers east before he was transported. It exists in a regimental box in Amiens. If I were a man who could request that box, and I am, then the man in your — in the convent's ground — could have his name back whenever anyone wished it."

He left the sentence unfinished, open as a door, and went out through it.

Elodie stood among the brown envelopes for a long time. Then she took the lamp down the cold stair to the crypt, because she could not help it, because her body went there the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth. Three hundred and eleven names in the false bottom of the vestment chest, wrapped in oilcloth. She did not open it. She laid her hand flat on the wood instead, feeling the cold come up through her palm, and thought about a mother in Roubaix, whoever she was, setting a place at a table for a son who had become fog.

That was the unbearable genius of what Verrec had done. He had not threatened the ledger. He had held it up beside its own purpose and let them look at each other. She had built the thing out of grief to defeat forgetting, and here was a man offering, politely, at cost, to make forgetting impossible — if she would only hand him the keys.

She climbed back up as the bell rang for evening prayer, and found Marguerite waiting in the corridor with Henri's slate in her hands and a look on her face that Elodie had learned to dread more than shells.

"He drew a new picture," Marguerite whispered. "Elodie. He wrote words under it. His first ones." She turned the slate around.

In a child's careful, terrible capitals: **THEY SHOT HIM IN THE MORNING.**

* * *

Below it, drawn with the same patient finger, a field, a wall, and eleven small strokes like birds flying away from it.

Marguerite's hand was shaking. Elodie's was not, and she understood, dimly, that this was worse — that fear had left her somewhere in the dispensary and been replaced by something colder and more useful, and she did not yet know what that thing would cost.

"Who is *him*, Henri?" Marguerite asked gently, kneeling. "Can you tell us who?"

But Elodie was already looking at the wall. At the number of strokes. Eleven.

The same number of men in the October transport. The same number Verrec had named not one hour ago, in his courteous voice, counting them out for her like coins.

Someone had already connected this child to that wall. Someone had counted before she had.

And tomorrow morning, Officer Verrec had said, smiling his terrible kind smile, he would come back for her answer.
