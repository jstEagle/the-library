## 9. Marguerite's Letter

I have been keeping a secret from Elodie for four months, which is a strange thing to say about the person I tell everything to.

It started in September, when the convoys brought in that terrible batch from the Chemin des Dames — forty-one men, and we lost nine of them before the week was out. I remember the night it began because I remember the smell of that week: carbolic and mud and something underneath both that I have never found a name for and hope I never do. After my shift I couldn't sleep. The dormitory was too warm, too full of other women breathing, and every breath sounded like one of the men on Ward C who had taken to moaning in his sleep, a low animal sound that no amount of morphine seemed to touch.

So I went down to the writing room to compose myself, and there was the post basket, and in the post basket was a letter from the Red Cross information bureau — circular, meant for Sister Superior — listing prisoner-of-war camps in Germany with their postal codes.

I did not take the letter. I want to be clear about that, even if only to myself. I copied the addresses onto the back of my hand like a schoolgirl cheating at catechism, and then I sat down at the writing table with its scratched inkwell and its chair that wobbles on one short leg, and I wrote the first line before I knew I was going to write anything at all.

*Dear Captain Oyelaran. My name is Marguerite Fauvel, and I am the sister of your namesake.*

That is not what I wrote. I am confusing two letters. What I actually wrote was simpler and stranger: *Dear Captain Oyelaran, I hope this finds you alive, because if it does not find you alive it will find no one, and that would be a waste of a stamp.* Humor as a door-knock. It was the only kind of courage I had that night, so I spent it.

His name is Tomas. Tomas Fauvel. He was taken at Verdun in the summer of 1916, and for eight months after that our family heard nothing — not wounded, not missing, not dead, just an absence shaped like my brother that sat at our table and slept in his bed and answered to his name without speaking. Then a postcard came through Geneva, four lines long, censored into gibberish by someone with a heavy hand, saying only that he was alive, interned, and would we please send socks.

My mother wrote him every week after that. My father wrote him once a month, stiff as a sermon. And I wrote him too, but mine were different, because mine were full of things the censor was supposed to read. I told him about the weather and the price of bread and the dog next door having puppies, and threaded between those harmless threads I hid everything I could not say aloud to anyone: that I had left home to nurse in a war hospital because I could not bear to be far from the war while he was inside it; that some nights I hated him a little for being the reason; that I loved him more than I could survive saying.

Four months of letters. He has written back three times. Each reply arrives months late, thinner than it left, the paper gone soft and gray as though the words themselves had been rationed. In his last one he said the camp had typhus in the autumn and that he had been moved to a work detail and that I should not worry, which is how I know to worry.

And here is the secret under the secret, the one I have not even put in a letter: Tomas deserted.

Not the way the posters mean it. Not throwing down his rifle and walking into the dark. He was nineteen, on sentry duty, in February, in a flooded trench up to his knees, and according to the boy who served beside him — the one who came through our town on leave two years ago and stood in our kitchen with his hat in his hands, unable to sit down — Tomas walked the wrong way along the communication trench in a whiteout blizzard and ended up on the wrong side of no man's land, and when they found him the Germans had already picked him up half-frozen, and that was that. Whether he meant to walk anywhere at all, whether he even knew which way was which, nobody can say. The army said it anyway. The army has a word for it, and my parents will not say the word, and so our house has developed a silence around my brother's name like a swollen joint around an old wound.

He exists nowhere now. Struck from his unit's rolls. Absent from the lists. A man who cannot come home because coming home requires paperwork, and his paperwork says a thing my country cannot forgive.

When Elodie showed me her ledger — and she did show me, weeks ago now, in the linen room, opening it the way you'd open a vein — I understood it immediately, the way you understand a language you didn't know you spoke. Names for the erased. She began it for a soldier buried as *Unknown Male*, and she has kept it against every regulation and every sensible instinct, three hundred names in her careful hand, because she cannot bear for men to vanish whole.

And my brother is vanishing whole.

I have been circling this for days. I have started the conversation a dozen times in my head, rehearsed it over the basin, over the bandage table, over the poor unconscious skull of Lieutenant Aubry while changing his dressing. Every rehearsal fails at the same place, because every version requires me to say the word *deserter* to Elodie Oyelaran, whose ledger exists precisely because the army uses that word to unmake men.

Tonight it finally happens, and it happens because of Henri.

The boy has been with us nearly two weeks now and still says almost nothing. He follows Elodie like a small shadow with a pencil, drawing his strange streets on any scrap of paper that survives her attention. Tonight, after supper, I found him in the corridor outside Ward D, sitting cross-legged on the cold tile, drawing on the floor itself with a stub of charcoal he'd scavenged from somewhere. A whole city spreading out around his knees. Rooftops. A river with bridges. A church with a crooked tower, drawn over and over until the charcoal wore down to nothing.

"Who lives here?" I asked him, kneeling.

He looked at me with those enormous grave eyes and pointed at the church tower. Then he pointed at himself. Then he pointed at nothing at all — a blank space between two rooftops — and made a small motion with his hand, like a bird going down.

I held him for a while. He let me, which is more than he lets most people. And holding him, feeling how light he is, how carefully he doesn't cry, I thought: *someone has to write these children down too. Someone has to say out loud where they come from, or the blank space between the rooftops swallows them.*

So when Elodie came down the corridor with her sleeves rolled and her hair escaping its pins the way it always does by evening, I stood up, and I heard myself say, "Can I talk to you? Somewhere with a door?"

She read my face in one glance — she always reads faces well, it's part of what makes her a good nurse and a dangerous person — and said, "Dispensary. Ten minutes."

* * *

The dispensary smells of iodine and camphor and the particular dusty sweetness of the aspirin tin Sister Béatrice guards like a relic. Elodie lights the little spirit lamp under the water kettle because neither of us can talk without tea, and for a minute we just stand there in the small warm circle of its flame, two tired women pretending ceremony.

Then she sits on the dispensary stool across from me, folds her hands, and waits. She is very good at waiting. It is one of the first things I loved about her.

"I get letters," I say. "From Germany. From a prison camp."

Her eyebrows rise, but not in scandal — in interest. That is Elodie all over. Tell her you've committed a crime and she'll ask what happened next.

"Tomas," I say. "My brother. He was taken at Verdun." I have my hands in my lap and I notice I am gripping them hard enough to whiten the knuckles, and I make myself stop. "Elodie, I need to tell you the rest of it before you decide anything, because the rest of it matters."

"The rest of what?"

"What he is. On the records." I take the breath I've been saving for two weeks. "They struck him off as a deserter."

The kettle begins its thin whistle. Neither of us moves toward it.

"He may not even have been one," I rush on, because now that the seal is broken the words come fast and hot. "There was a blizzard. He was nineteen. The boy who saw it happen said the visibility was nothing, absolutely nothing, you couldn't see your own feet — and the trench system there was a maze, Elodie, even the officers got lost in it. But the army needed it to be a choice. So they wrote it down as a choice, and now my brother is alive and in a camp and legally he is no one. No home unit. No discharge possible. No repatriation list will ever carry his name, because officially there is no name to carry. If he dies in that camp, there won't even be a form. He'll just stop appearing."

The whistle climbs to a shriek. Elodie reaches over without looking and moves the kettle off the flame, and the silence that comes down in its place feels like the silence in the wards after a man finally stops fighting the end.

"You want him in the ledger," she says quietly.

"I know what I'm asking. I know it isn't a record of prisoners. I know your book is for—" I stop, restart. "Here's the thing. You showed me the ledger because you trusted me. First page, first name. I've thought about that first name every day since, because I know how he got there — a man buried as Unknown Male, and you couldn't stand it. Well, my brother is being buried right now, Elodie. Slowly. While he breathes. Nobody is shooting at him anymore but the paperwork is finishing what the war started, and there is no one — do you understand? There is no one else on God's earth who will write his name down in a place where it means *he counted*. My mother writes him socks. My father writes him silences. I'm the only one who knows what he actually is, which is my brother, which is enough."

Elodie gets up. For a horrible moment I think she's going to walk out, but instead she takes down two chipped mugs and pours the tea, and hands me the one without the crack in it, and sits back down, and looks at me for a long time.

"Do you know what I did last Tuesday?" she says. "Officer Verrec asked me about my handwriting."

My stomach drops. "What?"

"Casually. Over cards. He'd seen the ward log. He said my hand was unusually regular for a volunteer. He was smiling when he said it." She sips her tea. "I'm telling you that so you understand what adding a name costs now. It isn't just ink anymore. Every name in that book is evidence, and I don't yet know of what, exactly, or for whom. Verrec asks questions the way a customs officer pats your coat."

"Then don't add him," I say, and hear my own voice go flat and hard, a voice I barely recognize. "Burn the page after I've gone. Don't even write him in ink — pencil, in your own private cipher, whatever you need. I'm not asking you to endanger yourself for a stranger, Elodie. I'm asking you for my brother, who is the same thing he was on your first page — a man the reports erased. You started this book because you couldn't watch it happen once. Are you able to watch it happen to mine?"

The lamp gutters. Outside, snow ticks against the shutters, and beneath us, faintly, I can feel the building's old bones settling, stone shifting on stone, the convent murmuring in its sleep.

"Marguerite." She sets her mug down. "Give me the details. Full name, date of birth, unit, date of capture, camp. Everything on his postcards and everything you know besides. If I'm going to keep a man, I'll keep all of him."

It takes me a moment to understand what she has said, because it is not yes and it is not no; it is larger than either. Then my eyes are stinging and I am furious at them and I dig in my uniform pocket for the folded postcards I have carried for three days like contraband, and smooth them out on the dispensary table between us.

*Tomas Aurelien Fauvel,* I say, and she writes it, and watching her careful hand lay my brother down letter by letter into the ledger — into the company of the Unknown Male and all the others she has refused to let vanish — something happens in my chest that I don't have a better word for than *homecoming*. I left our parents' house two years ago. I did not know until this moment, in a requisitioned dispensary that smells of iodine, that I had arrived anywhere since.

"There," Elodie says softly, and blots the page. "Three hundred and eleven names."

And then, before I can answer, before I can even breathe, she goes very still, her pen lifted, staring at the number as if it has spoken to her — and says, "Marguerite. Where is Verrec tonight?"
