## 10. The Evacuation Order

The orders came on a Tuesday, folded inside the usual dispatch of morphine requisitions and burial forms, as if the end of everything could be filed between two signatures.

Elodie found them because she always sorted the mail. It was one of her small usurpations—Sister Béatrice handled the accounts, Sister Superior handled the correspondence with the diocese, but the military post went through Elodie's hands first, and had for two years. She told herself it was efficiency. She knew it was surveillance. A hospital that received official paper was a hospital that could be lied to by official paper, and Elodie had learned never to trust a document she hadn't read before anyone else.

She stood in the draughty corridor outside the administrative office, the packet still sealed, and read the sender's stamp twice. *Direction des Services de Santé — Zone Évacuée.* Then she broke the seal right there, standing up, because her hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking.

*By order of the Regional Command,* the first page began, *all medical facilities within the sector are to be evacuated no later than the 14th of December.*

She did the arithmetic without wanting to. Twelve days. Twelve days of dressings, of fevers breaking or not breaking, of Henri's chalk maps on the scullery wall that someone would have to wash away. Twelve days, and then this place—the convent that had swallowed its bells and its gardens and its name into the war effort—would be given over to whatever came behind the retreating line.

She read on.

*Transport will be provided: one (1) heavy lorry, military registration, departing at dawn on the date specified. Capacity is limited. Priority of evacuation is as follows: commissioned officers; ambulatory wounded fit for transport; medical personnel essential to continued service; civilian dependents only where space permits.*

*Where space permits.*

Elodie read that phrase four times. It had the quality of certain words she'd learned to dread in official language—*expedient*, *necessary*, *regrettable*—words that did their work quietly while everyone pretended they meant nothing. Where space permits. A truck built for twenty men, carrying perhaps thirty if they stacked the stretchers well. And the convent held sixty-one souls that morning. She had counted them herself, the way she counted everything now. Sixty-one, minus three dead since Sunday, plus two wounded brought in overnight from a collapsed signal post.

The numbers didn't care about priority lists. The numbers were going to eat people.

She carried the order to Sister Superior's office the way you carry a live shell—carefully, levelly, without jostling. Sister Superior read it standing at her window, her back to the room, and when she turned around her face had done what it always did: arranged itself into the expression of a woman who had decided, years ago, that God would handle the large questions and she would handle the small ones, and that the trick of survival lay in never letting the two categories touch.

"Twelve days," she said.

"Yes, Mother."

"And one truck."

"One truck."

Sister Superior sat down behind her desk—a real desk, walnut, pre-war, the last piece of furniture in the building that remembered being useful—and pressed her fingertips together. "Then we begin triage now. Not on the fourteenth. Now." She said it without drama, the way she said everything, and that was the worst of it. Elodie had watched this woman ration bandages, ration bread, ration hope, all with the same flat competence, and she had never once seen her flinch. "Draw me a list. Everyone in the building, categorized. Officers first—that's regulation, and I won't have us shot for insubordination in the final week of a war we may yet lose."

"Mother, some of those officers can walk to the next town unaided. Some of my wounded cannot sit up."

"Regulation," Sister Superior repeated, and then, softer, in the voice she used perhaps twice a year: "I know, nurse. I know what it means. Draw the list anyway. And bring it to me before you show it to anyone else, because the moment this building knows there is one truck, we will stop being a hospital and start being a crowd."

Elodie spent that evening in the dispensary with a sheet of paper and a lamp turned low, doing the thing she was best at and hated most: making lists.

It should have been simple. It was simple, on paper, which was the whole obscenity of paper. Column one: officers, five names, four of whom could walk. Column two: stretcher cases, nineteen men, of whom perhaps eleven would survive the journey and perhaps seven would survive the journey *and* the winter after it. Column three: nursing staff, twelve, including Marguerite, who was twenty-three years old and wrote letters to a prisoner camp every week and had once told Elodie, laughing, that she intended to see the sea again before she was thirty. Column four: nuns, nine. Column five, written smaller, as if smallness could hide it: *civilians.* One entry. *Henri, age 6. No papers.*

No papers. She had written it and then stared at it until the ink shone. In the eyes of the regional command, the boy did not exist. He had no regimental number, no identity card, no birth certificate in the file. He was a blank space on a form, and blank spaces were the first thing cut when capacity ran short. She thought of the woman at the door, the coins pressed into her palm, the note in that careful, frightened hand: *forget his face.* Someone had gone to great trouble to make this child invisible. The war had simply agreed.

She heard footsteps in the corridor and covered the list with her forearm out of pure reflex—an instinct so old now it felt like breathing. But it was only Marguerite, carrying a basin of water gone grey with washing, her sleeves rolled, her hair escaping its cap in the way that made the younger sisters cluck and made Elodie, privately, think of spring.

"You've seen it, then." Marguerite set the basin down. Her voice was carefully light. "The order. Sister told the senior staff an hour ago. The whole ward already knows—news travels faster than cholera in this place."

"What have they been saying?"

"That we'll all go together. That there will be more trucks. That the Americans are coming and the war will be over by Christmas and we'll laugh about this." Marguerite smiled the smile that went with it, and then let it fall off her face like something set down. "You don't believe any of it."

"I believe in the truck," Elodie said. "One truck, Marguerite. I've seen the manifest format. I've seen how these things are filled in. Officers get their names printed. Wounded get numbers. Staff get a category. Civilians—" she tapped the covered page under her arm— "civilians get a footnote, if the clerk is feeling generous."

Marguerite was quiet for a moment. Through the wall came the sound of the evening ward: a man coughing, the rattle of a bedpan, someone murmuring a prayer with the particular fluency of a soldier who had taken it back up recently and provisionally.

"Henri," Marguerite said.

"Henri," Elodie agreed.

"He doesn't even know what a berth is. He thinks the trucks bring bread." She picked up her basin again, hugged it against her hip like a shield. "Elodie. If it comes down to it—if it truly comes down to it—you tell me. Not Sister Superior. Not a committee. Me. Promise me that."

And Elodie, who had promised nothing to anyone in four years except the dead, whose entire moral architecture was built on the refusal to promise things she couldn't control, heard herself say: "I promise."

Because it would come down to it. She already knew. You learned to hear it, in a hospital, the way farmers hear weather in their knees. Somewhere in the next twelve days the abstract war—the war of communiqués and casualty figures, of fronts advancing and receding like tides reported by men far from any water—was going to arrive in this corridor and become concrete. It was going to become a specific truck, a specific dawn, a specific number of seats, and specific faces looking at her, waiting for her to do arithmetic with their lives.

She finished the list at midnight. Sixty-one entries reduced to categories. At the bottom, where the form required a signature certifying accuracy, she wrote her name and felt it as a small perjury: accurate to the army, perhaps. To the truth, no. The truth was that every name on that list weighed the same, and the form was built to make them weigh differently.

Before she slept, she took out the ledger—her ledger, the true one, three hundred and eleven names in a careful hand—and added nothing, because there was nothing to add. Instead she read the first page. The first name. The deserter executed in the spring of '17, whose son now slept two wards away with chalk dust on his fingers. *They will erase him twice,* she thought. *Once by rifle, once by footnote.* And she lay in the dark doing the only thing she knew how to do against a war: memorizing.

Twelve days. Outside, snow was beginning again, softening the ruins, filling the craters white and smooth, lying to the landscape about what lay underneath.

In the morning, Verrec would want to see the manifest. She could feel it coming the way you feel a change in pressure before a storm—the courteous knock, the sharp eyes, the questions shaped like kindness. Whatever he wanted from her ledger, the evacuation order had just changed the price of everything.

And somewhere in the town, she was certain, a clerk was already drawing up the berth list—one seat fewer than there needed to be.
