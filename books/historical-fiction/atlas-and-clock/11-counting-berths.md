## 11. Counting Berths

The manifest arrived at noon, carried by a corporal who would not come inside and would not touch anything Sister Béatrice handed him. He left the paper on the gatepost as if it might be contaminated, saluted no one, and was gone before the sound of his engine had finished dying on the road.

Béatrice read it standing in the courtyard, snow gathering on her shoulders, because she had learned in forty years of religious life that some documents are better read in the cold, where the shock has something to do besides making you weep.

It was one page. That was the first obscenity. Twelve days of preparation, ninety-one souls under the convent roof, and the army's answer fit on one page.

*Evacuation priority as follows. Category One: ambulatory wounded officers. Category Two: non-ambulatory wounded, all ranks. Category Three: essential medical personnel, list to be approved by liaison officer. Category Four: civilian dependents, berth availability permitting.*

*Berth availability permitting.*

She counted twice, standing there while the snow thickened and the bell did not ring—the clock had been stopped for three days now, and without it the hours had gone feral, arriving early or late or not at all. Ninety-one souls. Thirty-one berths. She read the arithmetic until it read itself to her, and then she folded the paper into her sleeve and went in to tell Mother Céline that God had sent them a document with a category system in it.

* * *

They met in the chapter room at two o'clock, which by the dead clock might have been any hour at all. Béatrice had asked Elodie Oyelaran to attend, and Mother Céline had raised an eyebrow at it—a nurse, a laywoman, invited into a deliberation of the community—but Béatrice had held her ground. Whatever they decided in that room, Elodie would be the one carrying its consequences in her hands for the rest of her life. It seemed only honest to let her watch the hands that tied them.

Mother Céline sat at the head of the long table with the manifest open before her, and she had arranged her face into the expression she used for bad harvests and worse bishops: patient, immovable, already grieving.

"Read it again," said Sister Marthe, who was old enough to remember the last war and deaf enough to make every meeting loud. "Category Four. What does *permitting* mean, when an officer writes it?"

"It means nothing," said young Sister Constance. She was twenty-three and had not yet learned to soften what she saw. "It means he doesn't have to say no out loud. He writes *permitting* so that when we ask why the child is still here, he can point at the word and shrug."

"The child," said Sister Marthe, and paused, and Béatrice understood that the pause was the whole conversation arriving at once. "Yes. Let us speak of the child, since no one else will. The boy with no papers. The boy whose name nobody knows."

"Henri," Elodie said quietly, from the end of the table.

"He has no name on any document," Marthe said. "You may call him Henri. The manifest cannot." She put one blunt finger on the page. "Look at the categories. Wounded. Personnel. *Dependents.* A dependent must belong to someone, must appear somewhere—in a parish register, a ration book, a gendarme's list of refugees. This boy appears nowhere. Officially—" she spread her hands, and her voice, for all its deafness, was very gentle— "officially, sisters, he does not exist. And the truck cannot carry a person who does not exist. There is no seat for him. There is no *nothing* to put in the seat."

The silence after that was the specific silence of women who have each, separately, already thought of this and were hoping no one would say it.

Béatrice looked at Elodie. The nurse sat very still, her hands flat on the table like someone steadying herself against weather. Her face did the thing it always did—closed slightly, the way a door closes without latching—and Béatrice, who had spent decades reading faces across refectory tables, thought: *she knows something about this boy that the rest of us do not.* She had thought it for weeks. She thought it more strongly now.

"There are ways papers are made," Mother Céline said at last.

"There are ways papers are forged," Constance corrected.

"I said what I said," Mother Céline replied, and did not smile.

Sister Marthe turned toward her with the slow gravity of a ship coming about. "Mother. You are proposing that this community, which has kept the rules of two empires and one republic without breaking stride, now produce false documents for an unknown child."

"I am proposing," Mother Céline said, "that we count our people and then save them, in that order. The paperwork is somebody else's sin. I have enough of my own."

"And if the corporal at the truck counts heads against his list?"

"Then the boy stands behind a tall sister," said Constance, "and we all develop weak eyes."

"You joke," Marthe said.

"I am twenty-three and being trained for a world where trucks decide who lives," Constance said. "Joking is what's left."

* * *

They went around the room after that, because Mother Céline believed in the old forms even when the subject was new, and the forms required each voice.

The wounded came first, obviously; no one argued it, though Constance argued the *order* within it—that a sergeant with a crushed leg outranked a captain with a fever, whatever the manifest's categories implied. Marthe seconded her, which surprised everyone, and then explained that she had buried brothers in the last war and had no patience left for rank among the shivering. The point was noted. It would be overridden. They all knew it would be overridden, and they noted it anyway, because noting it was a kind of testimony.

Then personnel. Here the room grew delicate, because the manifest required a list approved by the liaison officer—Verrec, whose name had begun appearing in their days the way frost appears on glass, silently, everywhere at once. Essential medical personnel. Who was essential? All of them were essential; that was the cruelty of the phrase; it was designed so that whoever composed the list could sleep afterward.

"We are eleven sisters," Marthe said. "Do we evacuate?"

"The convent does not evacuate," Mother Céline said. "Somebody stays with the building. Somebody stays with the men too sick to move. And somebody—" her voice did something almost imperceptible— "somebody should stay who remembers where everything is."

Béatrice heard it and felt the floor tilt slightly, though she did not move. Mother Céline was seventy-one. Mother Céline had not left the convent grounds in nine years. Everyone at the table heard what she had said and no one at the table believed she meant it for herself.

"Mother—" Béatrice began.

"Next item," said Mother Céline.

But the item was not next. The item was the same item, and it had been waiting under everything else all afternoon, and it was this: thirty-one berths, ninety-one souls, and one boy who existed nowhere on earth except inside these walls and inside a ledger Béatrice had seen, once, by accident, in Elodie's careful hand.

* * *

It was Elodie who broke the delicacy, in the end, and she did it the way she seemed to do everything—without raising her voice, without asking permission, as though the thing she said had merely been waiting for someone to stop being polite enough to say it.

"How many wounded can actually travel?" she asked. "Not how many are listed. How many will survive the road?"

Constance blinked. "That isn't—"

"It is entirely the question," Elodie said. "I've been assessing them all week. Fourteen of the Category Two cases cannot survive transport. They'll die on the truck or the day after, in pain, far from here. If we leave them, we leave them to die in a falling town with strangers' shells landing on them. If we take them, we spend berths that could carry children or staff, and they die anyway, just later and elsewhere." She let that sit in the room like cold air. "Someone has made this calculation already. The clerk who wrote this manifest made it. He just didn't have to look at anyone while doing it."

"That is triage," Marthe said. "It is ugly and it is correct."

"It is arithmetic wearing a uniform," Elodie said. "And I am asking whether we do the same arithmetic honestly or let a form do it for us."

For a moment Béatrice saw the younger woman completely—not the quiet nurse with the closed door of a face, but someone with a furnace behind her ribs, banked and burning. She keeps names, Béatrice thought suddenly, remembering the ledger, the columns of careful script. She keeps names because the army loses them. Of course she would be the one to say it.

Mother Céline closed her eyes briefly, a woman accepting a burden she had been expecting. "What do you propose, Nurse?"

"Fourteen berths for the hopeless cases is fourteen murders committed slowly. Take ten of the ones who might live. Use four berths for the staff who'll run whatever hospital receives us—Marguerite first among them, she's the steadiest hands we have." Elodie's voice stayed level; only her hands moved, pressing flat against the wood. "And one berth for a boy who has no papers, whom we will put on that truck if I have to teach every sister in this house to cough at inspection time."

"The officer approves the personnel list," Marthe said carefully. "And the officer, I am told, watches everything."

"Then we pray his eyes are weak," said Constance.

"I've met his eyes," Béatrice said, before she meant to.

Every head turned. She had not intended to speak; she had intended to hold her counsel as she held most things, close and cool. But it was out now, and there was nothing to do but finish it.

"Lieutenant Verrec sees handwriting the way we hear bells. He noticed Elodie's penmanship within a day of arriving. If there is a false name on a list, he will find it, and then the question becomes what he wants in exchange for not finding it." She paused. "Men like that rarely want money. They want acknowledgment. They want to matter to the story."

"What story?" Constance asked.

"Whatever story he intends to tell himself after the war," Béatrice said. "They all have one. He's writing his now, and I suspect he needs witnesses."

The room absorbed that. Outside, the snow had turned to sleet and ticked against the shutters like static, and somewhere below, faintly, they could hear Marguerite singing to the boy while she worked—some nonsense song about a crocodile, delivered with the cheerful determination of a woman refusing to notice she was drowning. The sound rose through the floorboards and made the arithmetic at the table suddenly, unbearably concrete.

"He draws maps," Elodie said abruptly, as if continuing a conversation with herself. "The boy. Streets, towers, a river bend. I know the town they're from. I've known for some time." She did not explain further. She looked at Mother Céline. "His father is in my ledger. First page."

Béatrice watched the Mother Superior take that in—watched her understand, in one long moment, that the quiet nurse had been running her own small country of the remembered for years, right under all their habits.

"Then he is the easiest case in the house," Mother Céline said finally. "A dead man's son. There is no one left to object to his existence. We need only manufacture the mother."

"Sister Marthe," Béatrice said, surprising herself a second time, "has beautiful handwriting."

The old nun turned toward her, and something passed between them—decades of shared winters, shared graves, shared stubbornness. "My eyes are failing," Marthe said.

"My eyes are excellent," Béatrice said. "I'll write. You'll dictate. Between us we'll produce a refugee mother of unimpeachable dullness, and a birth certificate lost in the bombing of the town records, which no one can disprove because the town records are, in fact, bombed."

"That's forgery," Constance said, with unmistakable satisfaction.

"That is mercy with a fountain pen," said Marthe.

* * *

They worked until dark, which no longer announced itself properly since the clock died—it simply accumulated, gray thickening to black, and the nuns lit candles out of sequence and pretended not to notice the disorder. Béatrice wrote in a fair imitation of a provincial registrar's cramped hand while Marthe invented a woman named Odile Ferrand, widowed, of a street in a town whose records were ash, traveling with her son. They gave the fictional Odile a limp, because limps excused slowness and slowness excused everything else. They gave her a ration book number copied from a genuine book belonging to a woman who had died in the spring and would not mind. Béatrice felt each stroke of the pen the way she had once, as a postulant, felt the weight of her vows: as a thing done in front of God, irreversible, binding.

When it was done, the document looked utterly ordinary, which was its genius and its horror. Béatrice held it up to the candlelight and thought: this single sheet of paper is the difference between a boy growing up and a boy disappearing into a ditch with a number instead of a name. Not faith. Not courage. Ink.

"Bureaucracy," she said aloud, to no one in particular, "is the only violence that requires no soldiers."

Elodie, gathering the candle stubs, looked at her. "Then fight it with bureaucracy."

"I intend to. It's the only battlefield where a woman of seventy is in her prime."

But when Elodie had gone—out into the corridor, toward the ward where the boy slept and the wounded breathed and the clock stood dead in its tower—Béatrice remained at the table with the false papers and the true manifest, side by side in the candlelight, and let herself see the whole board.

Thirty-one berths. Even with the arithmetic done honestly—even taking ten wounded who might live instead of fourteen who would not—they were short. Staff, sisters, the boy, Marguerite whom everyone loved and no one had yet dared name aloud in the list. Someone at this table had quietly assumed they themselves would not be on the truck; Béatrice had caught Mother Céline assuming it, and had caught herself assuming it, and suspected Elodie had been assuming it longest of all. That was the trick of the manifest. It did not merely count berths. It made each of them count herself as expendable.

And there was the other matter, the one she had raised and then abandoned: Verrec. The list of personnel required his approval. Tomorrow, or the day after, he would come with his courteous knock and his eyes like an auditor's, and he would look at the new name on the list—Odile Ferrand, limping, fictitious, beloved—and he would know. Perhaps not immediately. But he would know. And whatever he wanted in exchange for his silence, Béatrice feared it would be cheap for him to grant and expensive for them to pay.

She blew out the candles. In the dark, the stopped clock ticked once overhead—a settling of old wood, nothing more, nothing more—and she stood alone in the chapter room holding a lie in one hand and the truth in the other, unable to tell either one apart anymore.

Down the corridor, a child laughed in his sleep.
