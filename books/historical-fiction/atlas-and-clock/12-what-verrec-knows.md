## 12. What Verrec Knows

Verrec had learned to read men by their boots, women by their hands, and lies by the pause that came half a syllable too late. Elodie Oyelaran's pause was barely a pause at all. That was what frightened him. Most people, when he asked after paperwork that did not exist, hesitated long enough to build a lie. She simply went on folding bandages as if the question had been about linen inventory, and only her hands told him anything at all: they never stopped moving. Guilt made people still or frantic. Hers was the frantic kind, disciplined into industry.

He had been watching her for nine days.

Nine days of convalescence in a convent ward that smelled of carbolic and candle wax, nine days of a shrapnel wound in his thigh dressed by two nurses who were far too careful with their words, and one ledger-shaped silence sitting in the middle of everything like an unexploded shell.

He knew there was a ledger because he had been trained to know things, and because Sister Béatrice had mentioned—innocently, over soup—that Nurse Oyelaran kept *notes*. Notes, she had said, for the records. The Mother Superior's mouth had tightened around the word *records* the way a man's mouth tightens around a bone he isn't supposed to want.

Tonight, finally, his leg would bear weight and his conscience would not. He had arranged the meeting the way he arranged everything: politely, deniably, in the dispensary after lights-out, where the bottles stood ranked on their shelves like witnesses who could be trusted to say nothing.

She came at eleven. Of course she came at eleven. A woman with nothing to hide would have come at noon, in daylight, laughing at him.

"Captain." She closed the door behind her without locking it. That interested him too. Locking it would have suggested secrecy; leaving it open to suggestion suggested she wanted a witness within reach. "You said it was urgent."

"It can wait until you're comfortable."

"I'm never comfortable." She took down a bottle of tincture, held it to the lamp, put it back. Not nervous. Calibrating. "You've asked Corporal Anselme about personnel files three times. You've asked me twice. You've spent four afternoons in the records room we keep for the dead. I'd say you're not here for your leg, Captain, but then you weren't here for your leg in the first place, were you? Shrapnel doesn't travel alone through a staff officer's zone unless the staff officer was somewhere he shouldn't have been."

He laughed before he could stop himself—a real laugh, startled out of him like a bird from cover.

"Sister Béatrice says you don't miss much," he said.

"Sister Béatrice flatters me. She misses everything and calls it prayer." Elodie folded her arms. In the lamplight her face was all planes and patience. "Say what you came to say."

So he did. Or he began to, and discovered halfway through that he had rehearsed this speech a hundred times in the dark of the ward, and every rehearsal had been a coward's version.

"The boy," he said. "Henri."

Something moved behind her eyes—the first true thing he'd seen there.

"What about him?"

"His name is Henri Aubertin. His father was Émile Aubertin, corporal, 214th Infantry. Born in Saint-Aubin-le-Brûlé, which I imagine explains the maps the child draws, if anyone here has been paying attention to the maps." He watched her go very carefully still. "Émile Aubertin was executed by firing squad in March of 1916. For desertion."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them the old clock ticked with its dragging, wounded beat.

"He didn't desert," Elodie said.

"No," Verrec agreed. "He didn't."

Here is what Verrec knew, laid out in the order he had assembled it, the way he laid out evidence, the way he laid a table, the way he did everything—with a correctness that had begun to feel like a disease.

In March 1916, the 214th had been fed into the salient at Vieux-Chemin and left there for eleven days while the artillery reorganized itself around somebody's ambition. Eleven days. The survivors came out calling it the meat grinder, and the term was not figurative. Among those eleven days' dead was most of a platoon that had simply ceased to exist in any record, because the regimental clerk had been killed on the second day and his replacement had worked from memory and rumor.

Émile Aubertin had gone out on the ninth night to drag back a lieutenant who was screaming in a shell hole. He brought the man in. Then he went out again, because there had been another voice. He did not come back until dawn, half-frozen, concussed, carrying a map case taken from a German corpse because he thought—concussed men think such things—it might contain something useful.

He was found by a sentry who did not know him, at an hour when everyone was mad with cold, wandering in from no-man's-land carrying enemy documents. The sentence took four hours to convene and eleven minutes to deliver. There was no defense counsel worth the name. There was a medical examination that was skipped, because the doctor was elbow-deep in other men.

And there was the signature. That was the part Verrec carried like a stone in his boot.

Major Raoul Dambach, liaison officer, Third Army intelligence—attached, in that chaotic week, to exactly the sector where the 214th bled. Verrec had found the execution order eighteen months later, in a file he had requested for entirely different reasons, and had read the signature four times before he let himself understand it. Dambach had countersigned. Dambach, whose clerks answered to Verrec. Dambach, who had dined at Verrec's table in Lille not six months ago, telling stories, pouring wine, a man of impeccable manners and immaculate paper.

Dambach had not ordered the execution. The order bore the colonel's name. But Dambach had reviewed it, ratified it, forwarded it—and appended a note, in his tidy hand: *No mitigating circumstances recorded.*

There had been mitigating circumstances. There had been a frozen man trying to carry maps home like a good soldier, and a voice in a shell hole, and eleven minutes.

Verrec had spent two years inside the machinery that produced such papers. He had written summaries, cross-references, annexes. He had never once signed an order like this one—but he had filed orders like this one, hundreds of them, and filed them well, and been commended for the tidiness of his filing.

When the boy had appeared at the convent door in November, faceless, unnamed, Verrec had done what intelligence officers do: he had begun to assemble a picture. It had taken him weeks of letters under cover of routine inquiries. The answer had come last Tuesday, in a courier packet he burned in the ward stove.

The boy at the door was the son of a man whose death paper Verrec's own department had processed, countersigned, archived. The boy was the residue of eleven minutes of his country's arithmetic.

And the nurse across the dispensary shelf had known some portion of this all along, because she wrote names down, in secret, in defiance of every regulation he had ever enforced.

"You knew," he said now. "About the father."

"I knew a name," Elodie said. "First name in my book. É. Aubertin. Executed for desertion, March 1916, according to a chaplain who was ashamed of what he'd witnessed and told me before he died of typhoid. The report said no family could be located." Her chin lifted a fraction. "His family was located. They were hiding."

"Hiding from us," Verrec said. "Yes. From us."

The *us* sat between them, and he watched her decide whether to include him in it. She did not, quite. She waited.

"I need to tell you something," Verrec said, "and I have never said it to anyone, and I am going to say it badly."

"Say it anyway."

"The order that killed his father passed through my department." He heard his own voice do the thing his voice always did—level, pleasant, officerly—and hated it, and could not stop it. "Not my hand. My desk. My section. My signature appears nowhere on it. And none of that matters, Captain Oyelaran, because I was the machine. I was what the machine is made of. I filed the war. I made it neat. And a man died in eleven minutes because nobody in that chain—including the man who trained me, including me—thought to ask whether he was frozen or a traitor."

Elodie said nothing. The lamp flame bent and righted itself.

"There's more," he said. "There's a note on the file. *No mitigating circumstances recorded.* Written by Major Dambach, who is currently in a position to have this hospital left off certain lists. Who is currently, in fact, the reason I can get a berth on that truck."

Now she spoke, and her voice was quiet in the way of a blade going into a sheath. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you keep a ledger of erased names, Nurse, and I keep a ledger of my own, and mine is written in other people's blood, and I would like—" He stopped. Started again. "I would like to be able to write one thing in it that isn't a crime."

They stayed in the dispensary a long time after that, saying less.

He told her the rest in pieces, the way you hand over evidence. That he had requested the Aubertin file originally for bureaucratic reasons—a missing personnel record, flagged during a routine audit, the same audit that had brought him asking questions in her ward. That the flag had led him to the execution order and the execution order to Dambach and Dambach to the boy, and that somewhere along that chain of paper his purpose had changed from investigation to penance without his noticing, the way a man's purpose changes in water: gradually, then all at once, and then he is drowning.

That was the phrase he used, and immediately wished he hadn't, because it was too honest, and honesty from an intelligence officer is a currency that buys suspicion instead of trust.

But she did not look away from him. That was the strange mercy of her. She listened the way she must listen to dying men—not comfortingly, but completely.

"You want to notify the family," she said at last. It wasn't quite a question, and it wasn't quite belief.

"I want to give them the truth their government declined to provide. That Émile Aubertin died going back for a second voice in the dark. That he was concussed and freezing. That the note saying otherwise was a lie written by a man who never saw him." Verrec's hands were flat on the shelf, pressing down as if the wood might shift. "I want the boy to know his father was not a coward. Is that so difficult to believe?"

"It's not difficult to believe." She turned the lamp down a fraction, though no one could see them; perhaps it was only habit, the instinct of a woman used to hiding light. "It's difficult to forgive. Those are different doors, Captain, and you've knocked on both."

"And?"

"And the mother is dead." Elodie said it flatly, having decided, apparently, that he had earned facts and nothing softer. "Pneumonia, two winters ago. The boy lived with an aunt in the village near here until November, when someone brought him to our door with money and instructions to forget his own face. So your notification will have a small audience. One boy, age six, who does not speak."

"He speaks," Verrec said. "He spoke to me yesterday. He asked whether trucks are warm."

She stared at him.

"He asked," Verrec went on, "whether the trucks that take people away are warm, and whether the people come back, and I said yes to the first question and nothing to the second, and he looked at me the way you look at a form that's been filled out wrong. Six years old, and he already audits us." He heard his own voice fray. "Nurse, I have interrogated men twice his size and slept afterward. That child asked me four words about a truck and I haven't slept since."

For the first time in nine days, Elodie Oyelaran looked at him with something other than calculation. It lasted perhaps three seconds. It was the longest kindness anyone had shown him in a year.

"Dambach," she said. "You said he can secure berths."

"He owes me. Or I owe him—we've reached the stage of acquaintance where the distinction no longer matters." Verrec straightened, and his thigh throbbed, and he welcomed the pain the way one welcomes proof of being still alive. "There are twelve days. The truck takes priority cases first, then officers, then whoever the list says. I can put a name on that list. One name, maybe two if I spend all my credit. I came tonight to tell you what I know before I offered, because—" and here was the sentence he had failed to rehearse, the one that kept breaking "—because you should know what kind of man is buying his way onto your list. A man who helped build the thing that killed that boy's father. A man looking for absolution at a discount."

"That's not how absolution works," Elodie said.

"No," he agreed. "I'm given to understand it's ruinously expensive."

She almost smiled. Almost. It was gone before it finished arriving, replaced by the nurse's face, the ledger-keeper's face, the face of a woman already doing arithmetic he couldn't see. And he realized, standing among the ranked bottles in the dark, that he had handed her a weapon and called it a confession. Names on a list. One name, maybe two. And in this convent there was a boy with no papers, a nurse with a brother in a prison camp, and Elodie herself—and only one of them, perhaps two, could be made official.

He watched her understand it. He watched the understanding land on her like weather.

"Twelve days," she said quietly.

"Twelve days," said Verrec. "And Nurse—you should know one thing more."

She waited, hand already on the door.

"Dambach has asked me, twice, whether the boy at this hospital is who I think he is." The lamp guttered, steadied. "I told him no. I lied to a superior officer in writing. Which means that when the twelfth day comes, either the boy leaves on that truck—or I do not survive whatever follows. I thought you should know the price of the ticket before you decide who rides."

Elodie stood very still in the doorway, a black shape against the lesser black of the corridor, and then she said, in a voice that had already left the room:

"Then we had better decide fast. Because the shells came closer tonight, Captain. And I don't think we have twelve days at all."
