## 13. Night of the Long Roll Call

The order arrived at four in the afternoon, carried by a corporal on a motorcycle that coughed itself to death in the courtyard and had to be pushed under the arch by two boys from the transport pool.

*By directive of the Sector Commandant: full accounting of all persons sheltered within the walls of the Convent of Sainte-Agathe, wounded military personnel and civilians alike, to be conducted this night. Names, ranks, regiments where applicable. Inspection officer to arrive at nineteen hundred hours. Any soul not accounted for by name will be treated as unlawful.*

Sister Béatrice brought me the paper while I was changing a dressing on Lieutenant Faure's stump, and I read it twice with my hands still red to the wrist.

"Every soul," she said.

"I heard."

"The Mother Superior wants you in the parlor."

Lieutenant Faure watched me tie off the bandage. He was twenty-three and had one leg and the particular patience of men who have stopped expecting good news. "Roll call," he said. "They did that before Ypres. Everyone in a line, everyone shouting their number. Half the men answering were dead already. The sergeant knew it and made them shout anyway." He looked at the ceiling. "Names are cheaper than men. That's how they think of it."

I washed my hands and went down to the parlor.

* * *

The Mother Superior sat behind the little table where she received the wine merchants and the bishop's clerks, and she had laid out the convent's registers like a hand of losing cards. The admissions book for the hospital ward. The burial register. The ration ledger. Her own list of nuns and postulants.

"They will want names," she said. "Exact names. And we do not have exact names for everyone, do we, Nurse Oyelaran."

It was not quite a question. She had known about my ledger since the spring—known and said nothing, which in the Mother Superior's grammar was a kind of blessing. Or a kind of trap held open, baited, waiting for the mouse to grow careless.

"There are men buried in the yard with no names," I said. "There are men in that ward who gave me names when they were too far gone for anyone to check them. There is a boy in the laundry wing with no papers at all."

"Henri," she said.

"Henri," I agreed, though that was only half his name, the half the woman at the door had given us along with the coins and the instruction to forget his face.

"The inspection officer will be Captain something or other, an adjutant to the Commandant. Not Verrec?"

"Not Verrec." She folded her hands. "Though Verrec will know whatever is said here within a day. He always does."

I thought of him as I had thought of him all week—the convalescing intelligence officer with the courteous voice and the eyes that read my chart entries upside down across the dispensary table, the man who wanted my names and offered protection in exchange, and who had not yet told me what he truly wanted them for. A bargain in the dispensary, struck halfway, hanging between us like a wire.

"What would you have me do?" I asked.

"Do what nurses do." The Mother Superior rose, and her black habit settled around her like a closing door. "Account for everyone. You keep track of people better than any of us, August. That is either your virtue or your crime, and tonight it will be both."

She never called me August. It landed like a hand on the shoulder. Then she left me alone with her losing cards, and I sat for a long moment listening to the wind work at the bell tower, where the old clock had stopped three weeks ago at eleven minutes past four and nobody could reach the mechanism to start it again. Time standing still over a house about to count its souls. Sister Béatrice called it an omen. I called it a clock that needed oiling, but I was glad, in some superstitious cellar of myself, that it had chosen now to stop—as if even time wanted no part of what was coming.

* * *

At six o'clock I went up to the laundry wing.

Henri sat on the stone floor with his chalk stubs arranged around a fresh drawing—a city seen from above, rooftops like scales, a river drawn in a single confident stroke. Six years old and he drew maps the way other children drew monsters. Streets no one recognized. Except I had recognized them, weeks ago, in the margins of a dead soldier's letters: the hometown of Corporal Émile Duret, executed for desertion, buried without ceremony, recorded nowhere on earth except in a ledger hidden under the third floorboard of the linen closet.

His father. Though Henri had never said the word, not once, not in all these weeks of silence broken only by geography.

I crouched beside him. "Henri. Tonight there will be soldiers here. They'll walk through every room with a lantern and a list, and they will ask your name."

His chalk stopped.

"You mustn't be afraid," I said, which was a lie, because he should be afraid, and so should I, and lying to children is a debt you pay later with interest. "But you must listen very carefully. If anyone asks, your name is Henri Marchal. You came from Reims. Your mother is a laundress and she is coming back for you when the roads reopen. Can you say it? Henri Marchal, from Reims."

He looked at me with those grave brown eyes that were too old for his face, eyes that had watched something no child should watch, and he repeated it in his small careful voice: "Henri Marchal. From Reims."

"And your mother is coming back."

"Henri Marchal," he said again, and then, after a silence long enough for me to think he was finished: "Is Reims far?"

"Far enough," I said. "Close enough to walk home someday."

He picked up his chalk and added a street to the map—a narrow one, unnamed—and I understood that he was adding himself somewhere, marking the place where a boy might live. I kissed the top of his head and went down to prepare for the counting.

* * *

At seven the inspection party came through the gate: a captain named Rossignol, dry as a receipt; a clerk with a portable desk strapped to his back like a snail's shell; four soldiers with fixed bayonets who tried to look as if the bayonets were incidental. The chapel candles were lit because electricity in this quarter had been unreliable since October, and by that light the captain's face had the waxy smoothness of a man who has learned to treat human beings as inventory.

"Nurse Oyelaran," he read from his papers. "You are listed as chief of nursing staff."

"Acting chief," I said. "Since Dr. Lemaire was reassigned."

"You will account for every person in this building. Wounded, staff, civilian. Names spoken clearly, entered as given. The clerk will record." He glanced up. "Discrepancies will be investigated."

"Everyone here has a name, Captain."

"Everyone *permitted* here has a name." He let that sit. "Begin."

And this was the thing I had rehearsed all afternoon while my hands worked bandages and my mind walked the wards: the difference between the ledger and the truth. The ledger held three hundred and eleven names, most of them dead, none of them official, all of them erased—dying soldiers buried as *Unknown Male*, deserters shot and struck from regimental rolls, civilians swallowed by the machinery of a war that preferred its casualties anonymous. The ledger could not come out. But the ledger was also the only complete account of what this convent had been, and tonight, when they demanded names, I discovered I could not bring myself to give them less than everything the truth could bear.

So I began with the living.

"Sister Céline Voisin. Sister Béatrice Aubry. Sister Marguerite Gauthier—" Marguerite stood where the staff had been arranged, chin high, and caught my eye with a flicker of something between fear and theater. We had spent the afternoon drilling her brother's story: Pierre Gauthier, prisoner of war, correspondent by letter, alive as far as anyone official needed to know. "Postulant Anne Fourcade. Cook, Madame Roussel. Groundsman, old Tomas Beck, born in Alsace, exempted from conscription by age and bad knees—"

The clerk's pen scratched. Rossignol watched.

"Wounded officers, ward one: Lieutenant Paul Faure, 114th Infantry, amputation, left leg. Captain Arnaud Verrec, Intelligence Section, convalescing—" Verrec stood at the edge of the group in his uniform without insignia of rank, hands clasped behind him like a man attending a concert, and inclined his head slightly when his name was spoken, as if being introduced at a salon. "Lieutenant Marc Ozenne. Sergeant Denis Kaltz..."

Ward two. Ward three. The laundry women. The village girl who scrubbed floors. Each name spoken clearly, each name real, and the rhythm of it began to move through me like something older than paperwork. This was the part I had not anticipated: that saying names aloud, in sequence, in candlelight, in a house of God, would feel like liturgy. Like the litany of saints, that endless procession of holy names recited on the great feast days—except my saints were uncanonized, unwitnessed, some of them damned by the very army whose captain now stood before me with his wax face and his snail-shell clerk.

Then we reached the dead.

"The burial register," Rossignol said.

I opened the Mother Superior's official register, which contained eleven entries, all of them variations on a theme: *Unknown Male, soldier. Unknown Male, civilian. Remains unrecoverable.* Eleven lies by omission, sanctioned and stamped.

"That is the official record," I said.

"I don't want the official record. I want your account. You are acting chief of nursing staff. Every man who died in this hospital passed through your hands. Name them."

The bayonets were incidental. The clerk's pen waited. Somewhere behind me Verrec shifted his weight, the smallest sound, a man leaning forward in his seat.

And I understood, in one cold flash, that this was a test—but not the test I had feared. Not *do you have a hidden ledger*. Rather: *will you perform obedience, or will you perform memory.* Rossignol did not care about the dead. He cared whether I would flinch, whether I would add names to his list that complicated his report, whether the woman before him was a functionary or something less manageable. Give him the safe answer and I was nothing. Give him the true one and I became a person worth remembering, and being remembered by intelligence sections is a dangerous form of survival.

I gave him the true one.

"Corporal Émile Duret, 114th Infantry, died of wounds on the fourth of March. Not unknown. His mother lives on the rue des Tanneurs in Soissons and has received no notification." I heard my own voice steady itself against the tremor underneath. "Private Luc Aubertin, 33rd Artillery, died the eleventh of March, pneumonia. Unknown Male, grave seven—that is Private First Class Jonas Weiss, 201st, died of his burns on the ninth; he told me his name twice because he was afraid no one else would say it. Unknown Male, grave nine—that is a civilian road-mender called only Baptiste, who was brought in with shrapnel wounds from the crossroads mine..."

One after another. The dead of the convent rising in candlelight, each name a small resurrection lasting exactly as long as it took the clerk's pen to scratch it down. Three hundred and eleven names in my keeping, and tonight I gave up thirty-eight of them—not all, never all, but every one that could pass as hospital record, every soldier whose death was honest even if his burial was not. I gave the deserters a wide berth. I gave the civilians their trades. I spoke the names of men whose families would learn tonight's truth never, unless the war ended and someone like me outlived the fear of saying it.

Halfway through, I realized I was not reciting. I was praying. The words had taken on the cadence of the rosary my grandmother wore out decades of Sundays teaching me—name after name after name, decade upon decade, the mystery in each case the same: *that they existed, that they were here, that they mattered.* The candles burned lower. The soldiers with incidental bayonets grew restless and then, oddly, stopped being restless. One of them, a boy from the Ardennes with hay-colored eyelashes, mouthed a name as I said it—his own village name, maybe, or close enough—and looked quickly at the floor.

Rossignol let me finish. I'll grant him that.

"Thirty-eight deaths accounted," the clerk said, totaling. "Against eleven registered burials."

"The register understates," I said. "Bodies came faster than forms."

"So it is everywhere." Rossignol closed his folder. Then, almost as an afterthought, the way a man checks one last pocket: "Civilians. You listed staff and refugees. Children?"

"One child. Henri Marchal, aged six, from Reims. His mother placed him here for safekeeping and was called away by the evacuation of the sector. She intends to return."

"Papers?"

"Lost in the flight from Reims. As most things are lost from Reims these days, Captain."

He looked at me a moment too long. "A child without papers is a child without a legal existence. The directive says every soul accounted for by name."

"He is accounted for. Henri Marchal. You may write it down."

Behind Rossignol, Verrec smiled faintly at the floor tiles, and I could not tell if the smile was admiration or warning or appetite.

"Henri Marchal," the clerk repeated, writing. "Reims. Mother's name?"

The pause lasted one heartbeat. In that heartbeat I saw the woman at the door in the November rain, her coins pressed into my palm, her note folded twice: *forget his face*. I saw the ledger's first entry. I heard a six-year-old voice in the laundry wing say *Henri Marchal, from Reims* like a lesson learned for exactly this examination.

"Léonie Marchal," I said, and gave the dead woman's calmest smile to the living room. "Laundress."

The pen scratched. The captain nodded. The inspection moved upstairs to count beds, and the counting found everything in order, because order was the one thing this house had learned to manufacture out of almost nothing.

* * *

Later—past midnight, the convoy of lanterns gone, the courtyard swept of motorcycle tracks—I stood in the ward doorway and felt the night settle back over the building like snowfall. Marguerite found me there. She carried two cups of the chicory substitute that passes for coffee, handed me one, and leaned against the doorframe shoulder to shoulder with me, looking down the rows of sleeping men.

"You were extraordinary," she whispered. "When you read the graves. I thought the captain would stop you."

"So did I."

"Why didn't he?"

"Because stopping me would have meant the names mattered. And to men like him, they can't matter. It would break something." I drank the bitter chicory. Below us, in the yard, the stopped clock hung in the tower against the stars, its hands still frozen at eleven minutes past four, keeping its own counsel. "Thirty-eight," I said. "Thirty-eight of them exist again, on paper, in a file in some sector office. Someone's clerk will read them."

"And the rest?"

"The rest live where they've always lived."

She was quiet a moment. Then, in the small voice she used for the things that frightened her: "Elodie. Today, while you were with the captain—I was in the village, getting the dressings from the pharmacist. And I saw someone." She turned to look at me, and in the dark her eyes were very wide. "A woman. Standing across from the church, watching the gate of the convent. Thin, with a gray coat, and she kept her head down whenever anyone passed, but Elodie—I'd swear it. I'd swear on anything you like. It was the woman who brought Henri."
