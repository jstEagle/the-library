## 14. The Face She Was Told to Forget

Marguerite had learned to read the village the way other people read weather. A shutter closed meant bad news from the front. Smoke from the bakery chimney before dawn meant a convoy was expected. And when Madame Roux stood in her doorway with her arms folded and her voice pitched low, it meant she had something to sell and wanted to be paid in attention.

"You've got the little one up at the convent," Madame Roux said. "The dark-haired boy."

Marguerite kept her hands busy with the basket of dressings she'd come to collect—gauze, iodine, the small bottle of camphor Sister Béatrice had written on her list. "He's a patient."

"He's nobody's patient. He's got no papers." Madame Roux said it without malice, the way she'd report a broken fence. "My sister-in-law was at the fountain on Thursday. She says a woman was asking after the convent hospital. Asking quiet, the way you do when you don't want the question remembered. Dark coat, head scarf, good boots gone shabby." She paused, savoring the shape of the detail. "Said she was looking for someone who might have come through. Wouldn't say who."

Marguerite closed the clasp on the basket. "People ask after the hospital every day."

"Not like this one didn't." Madame Roux leaned in. "My sister-in-law says she had an accent from the north. And she says—" the old woman dropped her voice further, pleased with herself— "she came back Saturday. Same fountain. Same questions. Only this time she asked for the boy by no name at all. She asked, *has anyone left a child with you.*"

The street seemed to narrow. Marguerite heard the pump handle squeak somewhere behind her, heard a cart, heard her own pulse doing something unhelpful in her ears.

"Thank you," she said, and put a coin in Madame Roux's palm, though the woman hadn't asked for one and would tell everyone she hadn't needed one.

"Coin's not what I want," Madame Roux said, closing her fingers over it anyway. "I want to know if it's true, what they're saying about the front at Rethel."

"It's true enough," Marguerite said, which was a lie, because what they were saying was worse than true, and walked back up the hill faster than dignity allowed.

* * *

She did not go straight to Elodie. This surprised her, once she noticed she'd done it. For two years she had carried everything to Elodie—the fear she couldn't name, the letters she wasn't supposed to write, the brother rotting in a camp she could point to on no map they owned. Elodie was where Marguerite put things down. But this thing felt different. This thing felt like it belonged to Henri first, and telling it wrong might break something that had only just begun to mend.

So she went instead to the ward where he wasn't.

Henri had stopped being a patient weeks ago in every way that mattered. He carried water now. He sat with the dying men who wanted a hand to hold and didn't care whose. He had even, twice, laughed—a sound so unexpected that both times the whole ward had turned toward it like flowers toward an impossible sun. But he still slept in the small linen room off the east corridor, because officially he did not exist and unofficially no one had decided what he did.

Marguerite found him there, sitting cross-legged on the floor with his pencils, doing the thing he did instead of speaking: drawing.

She had gotten better at reading them. The first maps had frightened her—streets drawn from above, rendered with the flat certainty of a bird's memory, corners and courtyards and a church with a squat tower. No one had recognized them. Then Elodie had recognized them, and told Marguerite why, and Marguerite had gone cold for a full day, because the streets belonged to a town four hundred kilometers north, and the man from that town was the first name in a ledger she was never supposed to see and had seen anyway.

This drawing was new. Not a map. A face. Or the start of one—a jawline, an ear, the suggestion of hair cropped close. The pencil moved slowly, the way it did when Henri was drawing something he was afraid of forgetting.

"Henri," she said softly.

He looked up. He had stopped flinching at sudden voices around the same time he'd started laughing; she counted both as victories she'd had almost nothing to do with.

"Can I sit?"

He scooted over, which was answer enough.

For a while she just watched him work. Then, as casually as she could assemble casualness out of a heart hammering like a drummer boy's:

"Has your mother ever been to the fountain in the village? Down by the mairie."

The pencil stopped.

He didn't look at her. He looked at the half-drawn face, and his thumb came up and covered the mouth of it, pressing hard against the paper as if the mouth might otherwise open. Then, very quietly, he shook his head.

"No?" Marguerite said gently. "Or you don't know?"

A pause. Then, in his small careful voice, hoarse from rationing itself: "She doesn't stand where people can see her. Papa said. When he—" The sentence died. He swallowed it whole, the way he swallowed most sentences about his father. "She knows how to wait."

Marguerite's throat hurt. *When he* —when he what? When he was taken. When he was shot. When he became the first line of ink in Elodie's secret book, *Aimé Duval, 34, cooper, executed as a deserter under order of—* and then the blank space where the order's signature should have gone, a blank space Elodie stared at sometimes when she thought no one was watching.

"Henri," she said. "If a woman came here—to the door—and asked for you. Would you want to know?"

His thumb lifted off the paper. The mouth underneath was unfinished, but she could see now it wasn't a stranger's mouth. It was fuller than a child would draw for himself. It was the mouth of the woman in the photograph Elodie had found folded inside the boy's collar six weeks ago, the photograph of three people squinting into northern sunlight, which lived now in the pocket of Elodie's apron like a relic.

"Yes," Henri said. It was the longest word he'd given anyone in days, and it cost him something; she watched it cost him. Then: "But she wouldn't come to the door."

"Why not?"

"Because of me." He said it simply, the way children state unbearable things. "Because of what they'd ask her about Papa."

* * *

Marguerite climbed the stairs to the infirmary office with the basket of dressings she had completely forgotten to deliver and found Elodie exactly where she knew she would be: bent over the admissions register, pen moving in that upright, patient hand of hers, each letter standing like a soldier at inspection.

"Elodie."

"Mm." The pen finished its line. Then Elodie looked up, and whatever she saw in Marguerite's face made her set the pen down entirely. "What is it."

"Madame Roux spoke to me in the village." Marguerite shut the door. It seemed suddenly important that the door be shut. "There was a woman at the fountain. Thursday, and again Saturday. Asking after the convent. Asking whether anyone had left a child here."

Elodie went very still. Marguerite had seen her still before—in raids, in inspections, at the moment Verrec's eyes had first drifted down to her handwriting—but she had never gotten used to it. It was the stillness of a person deciding, very fast, among many dangerous options.

"Describe her," Elodie said.

"Dark coat. Head scarf. Good boots worn down. Northern accent. That's all Madame Roux had."

"That's more than Madame Roux should have had." Elodie rose, crossed to the narrow window, and looked out at the gray afternoon as though the village were laid out in it like a map. "Two visits. That's not grief wandering. Grief wanders once and gives up. Two visits means she's working. Means she's watching the road before she commits to it."

"Then it *is* her." Marguerite's voice cracked on the last word. "You think it's his mother."

"I think," Elodie said slowly, "that a woman left six hundred francs and a note at our door telling us to forget her son's face, and that women who leave money and instructions like that are usually running from something specific, and that specific things have a way of finishing with you whether you run or not." She turned from the window. Her eyes had the shine Marguerite had learned meant fury being converted, molecule by molecule, into resolve. "Yes. I think it's her."

Hope arrived in Marguerite's chest like something detonating quietly. Hope was immediately followed by its twin.

"Elodie—if she's looking, others can be looking. If she found the convent—"

"Verrec doesn't need a fountain and a head scarf," Elodie said. "Verrec has files."

They stood in the silence that followed, the silence that had been living between the three of them—Elodie, Marguerite, the ledger—for weeks now, ever since the officer had arrived with his courteous questions and his convalescent's pale hands and his gaze that landed on things a beat too long.

"Have you told him?" Marguerite asked. "About today. About the woman."

"God, no."

"Will you?"

Elodie didn't answer, and the non-answer was an answer: *it depends on what he becomes.* Marguerite understood. She had spent her life around people—nuns, officers, her own parents—who treated truth like a storehouse to be guarded, dispensing it by the measured sackful. Elodie treated it like water: it went where it had to go and cut its own channels, and woe to the walls in its path.

"There's something else," Marguerite said, and now her own heart was pounding for a second reason, because she had been carrying this for three days and the woman at the fountain had loosened the lid. "I saw him drawing today. Not a map. A face. He covered the mouth when I asked about it." She took a breath. "Elodie, I think he's drawing his father. I think he's afraid he's forgetting him. Six years old—he barely has the face to begin with, and every day it goes a little more—"

"Stop." Elodie said it quietly, but her hand had come up. She pressed her fingers to her eyes for a moment. When she lowered them they were wet, and she let them be wet for exactly as long as it takes to decide not to apologize for it. "All right," she said. "All right. Here's what we do, and here's what we don't do."

"We don't tell Henri yet," she continued, counting the points off like a surgeon counting instruments. "Not until we know it's her and not a story Madame Roux's sister-in-law built out of boredom. We don't change anything he can see—we keep his hours, his meals, his pencils, everything exactly as it is. Children read rooms before they read words."

"And you?" Marguerite asked. "What do *you* do?"

Elodie looked at her, and for a moment something young surfaced in her face, something from before the war, before the ledger, before *Unknown Male* had been carved into a grave marker and ruined her forever.

"I memorize," she said. "It's what I'm good at."

* * *

That night the convent did its usual failing imitation of sleep. Wind worried the shutters. Somewhere below, the boiler ticked and groaned, eating the coal that fed the pages Elodie had not yet begun to feed it. Marguerite lay in her narrow cell listening to all of it and composing, as she always did, the letter she could not send: *Dear Théo. Today I learned your ghost may have a wife still walking the earth. Today I watched a child draw his father from memory like a man copying a document before the archive burns.*

Her brother. Three hundred and eleven names in a book that might get them all shot, and hers would be the three hundred and twelfth if she had her way. She'd asked Elodie for that already. Elodie had said yes the way she said yes to everything that mattered: instantly, and with consequences deferred to a later hour that was always coming.

Toward midnight, Marguerite gave up on sleep and went down to check the ward, and on her way back passed the linen room, and saw through the gap of the door that Henri's lamp was lit.

He was awake. He was sitting up with his knees drawn to his chest, and he was not crying, which somehow was worse—he had the dry, rigid stillness of a child holding a cry in with both hands.

Marguerite went in and sat on the edge of the cot without asking. That was the trick with Henri; you never asked permission for kindness, you simply delivered it, pre-approved.

"Bad dream?" she asked, though she knew it wasn't. Children woke from dreams confused. Henri woke from dreams *certain*.

He nodded anyway, accepting the fiction the way you accept a blanket.

"My brother has nightmares too," Marguerite said. "Théo. He's in a camp in Germany. In my dreams he's always at a table with food he can't eat, and everyone else at the table is asleep." She smoothed the blanket over his knees. "In yours, is it the fountain?"

Henri's head came up sharply.

"I know about the fountain," she said gently. "A woman's been seen there. Asking about you. We don't know for certain it's her, Henri. But we think—Elodie thinks—it might be your mother. Alive. Looking."

The lamp flame bent in a draft. In its light, Marguerite watched six years' worth of hunger cross a small face and fight with six years' worth of armor, and watched the armor lose, and watched him say, in a voice scraped nearly bare:

"She told them to forget my face."

"I know."

"So they don't find Papa's face in mine." His chin trembled once and stilled. "That's why she left me. Not because—" A breath. "Because they shot him for running away, and they were going to ask questions about the family, and Mama hides. She's good at hiding. She taught me to hide. She said, *be forgettable, Henri, forgettable boys grow up.*"

Marguerite gathered him in before she knew she was moving. He came stiffly at first, then all at once, his whole small weight arriving against her chest like something surrendered after a long siege. Over his head she stared at the whitewashed wall and thought about a woman walking through a shattered country alone, leaving her son at the door of strangers with money and a curse-shaped blessing—*forget his face*—and then turning around and coming back anyway, because the past does not stay buried; it walks to fountains in dark coats and asks its terrible questions, and waits to see who answers.

"If she comes," Henri whispered into her shoulder, "don't tell her about Papa. Let her tell me."

"All right."

"And don't let the officer talk to her."

Marguerite closed her eyes. Even six years old, even hollowed out and hiding, the boy had already understood the shape of the danger better than most adults. *Don't let the officer talk to her.* As if Verrec were a kind of weather you sheltered a mother from.

"I won't," she said. "Sleep now."

She stayed until his breathing lengthened, then eased the lamp down to a bead of light and slipped into the corridor—

—and stopped.

At the far end of the passage, outlined against the ward's dim night-lamp, stood Officer Verrec in his gray dressing gown, perfectly still. How long he had been there, how much of the corridor's small traffic he had witnessed, was impossible to say. He raised one hand—not a salute, nothing so formal—an open palm, almost an apology, and inclined his head.

"Bonne nuit, mademoiselle," he said pleasantly, and withdrew into the dark toward his room, his unhurried footsteps sounding, to Marguerite's ear, exactly like a clock that had started again somewhere in the house.
