## 15. Three Names, One Seat

The list was pinned to the refectory door with a bayonet, which told us everything about how the army valued our opinions.

I read it twice in the grey light of the sixth of December, my breath fogging the paper. Forty-two names in a clerk's careful hand. Thirty-six wounded men, stretcher cases all, each with a diagnosis and a transport priority code. Four nuns. Two nurses.

Elodie Oyelaran. Marguerite Sanson.

And no one else.

Henri's name was not on it because Henri had no name to put there. Marguerite's brother was not on it because her brother was four hundred kilometers away behind wire. And between the second nurse and the bottom of the page there was nothing — no line for a boy of six, no line for anyone who had arrived at this convent without papers, without a unit number, without a war to justify him.

"Forty-two," Sister Béatrice said behind me. She had come up silent as she always did, her hands folded into her sleeves. "The truck holds forty-four with the cab."

"The driver," I said. "And the escort."

"Yes." She let the word sit. The clock tower loomed over us both, its face still dead since the night it stopped, its hands frozen at eleven minutes past four like a wound that had scabbed over at the wrong hour. "Then you understand the arithmetic, Nurse Oyelaran."

I understood it. I had been doing it in my head for three days, waking at night with numbers marching through the dark like patrols. One hundred and six souls in the convent. Forty-two berths. The wounded would take theirs by right and by necessity — I would not have traded a single stretcher case for my own seat, and neither would Marguerite, and saying so cost us nothing because it was true. But after the wounded, after the nuns, after the two names the clerk had deigned to write down, there remained the question that had no honest answer.

Henri. Or Marguerite. Or me.

One seat. Not even a seat — a corner of canvas against a tailboard, a space where a person could wedge herself among the litters. But a place on the list, and a list was the only thing between any of us and the sound we could now hear at night, far off to the east, like a door being kicked again and again down a long corridor.

"He'll need papers," Sister Béatrice said. "Whatever you decide."

She left me with the bayonet and the paper and the cold.

* * *

Marguerite found me in the dispensary that afternoon, pretending to count gauze. She closed the door and stood against it, and I saw at once that she had been crying — not recently, but earlier, the kind of crying that leaves a person wrung out and strangely calm, like laundry after frost.

"You've seen the list," she said.

"I've seen the list."

"And?"

"And what do you want me to say, Marguerite?"

"I want you to say it out loud." Her voice didn't waver. That frightened me more than tears would have. "Because I've been saying it to myself for two days and I need to hear if it sounds as monstrous outside my head as it does inside it."

I set down the gauze. There was no point pretending to work; my hands had been shaking since morning anyway, and counting bandages is poor cover for trembling.

"One berth," I said. "Beyond the wounded. Beyond the two of us, if we keep our places, which — "

"Which we keep. We're no use to these men here when they come through. They'll need nurses in the north."

"Which we keep," I agreed, though I confess I said it too quickly, and I think we both heard the speed of it. "So. Henri, or you, or me."

"There," she said softly. "It sounds exactly as bad."

"It isn't a question, really." I made myself look at her. "You know it isn't. He's six years old. He has no one. He has no papers, no town, no name anyone will swear to. If he stays, he doesn't die of shelling, Marguerite — he disappears. He becomes one of the ones nobody counts. I've spent three years writing down the names of people nobody counted. I will not watch it happen to him while I stand in the same room."

"I know." She came away from the door and sat on the dispensary stool, suddenly small, her hands flat on her knees. "I've known since the list went up. God help me, I'd give him the seat myself if there were a way to hand it over like a coat. But Elodie — Étienne thinks I'm coming. He thinks — " She stopped. Pressed her lips together. Started again in a lower voice. "He writes that the thought of me north of the lines is the only thing keeping him from going over the wall of his own head. His words. He's nineteen and he writes like a man of sixty and he is all I have."

"I know."

"You don't apologize for him taking precedence," she said. "Don't you dare soften it. If you try to give me your seat I will never forgive you, and I mean it in the kindest possible way."

For a moment we were both quiet, and the shelling muttered far away like a dog in its sleep.

"So it's Henri," she said. "Except Henri cannot go anywhere, because officially he is a rumor in a coat. Which means someone has to make him real. Quickly."

"And there is exactly one person in this town who can manufacture reality," I said, "and he takes payment in currencies I don't keep."

Marguerite looked at me for a long moment. Then she laughed — a short, broken sound, half horror. "Verrec."

"Verrec."

* * *

He received me in the room the army had requisitioned as his office, which had been the convent's linen room until October and still smelled faintly of starch and camphor beneath the cigarette smoke. He was at the table with dispatch folders squared in front of him like a man playing patience, and when I entered he rose — he always rose — and indicated the chair opposite with a courtesy that had long since stopped feeling like manners and started feeling like a move.

"Nurse Oyelaran. You'll take tea. It's terrible, but it's hot."

"I'll stand."

"As you like." He sat back down and folded his hands. Up close the convalescence sat oddly on him; whatever had bled him in the autumn had healed into something harder than before, and only the slight care with which he moved his left arm betrayed that he was not entirely mended. "You've seen the manifest."

"You know I have. It's pinned to a door I walk past forty times a day."

"Then you know its contents, and I suspect you know its deficiencies." He tapped one folder without opening it. "I also suspect you haven't come to discuss the weather. Ask me, Elodie."

My Christian name in his mouth, mild as milk. I let it pass. There was no longer time to police the small incivilities; the ground was going under all of us together.

"Henri," I said. "The boy. He has no papers. Without papers he doesn't board the truck, and without the truck — " I stopped, because finishing the sentence gave it weight, and I wasn't sure I could carry the weight aloud in front of this man. "You can put a name on a list. You've told me so yourself, in your roundabout way. So tell me the roundabout price."

Verrec regarded me for a moment. Then, deliberately, he rose, crossed to the shuttered window, and opened it a hand's width to check the courtyard — an old intelligence habit, I supposed, checking whether walls had grown ears. When he sat again his voice had changed register, dropped its drawing-room polish by half a tone.

"What I asked you for before was the ledger. Every name. I told you it was for notifications to families, and you didn't believe me, and you were right not to. Partly it was that. Mostly it was the job: names are ammunition, and I am in the business of ammunition." He turned a folder ninety degrees, aligning it with the edge of the table, a tiny violence of precision. "That request is withdrawn. I no longer want your book."

"Then what?"

"A story." He said it lightly, but his eyes didn't. "One story, told once, in the proper quarter, at the proper time. After evacuation. Nothing written, nothing signed. Your word against no one's, because everyone who could contradict it will be gone or dead."

The shelling rolled again, closer, or perhaps only the wind carrying it better. I waited.

"In March of 1916," Verrec said, "a man named Isaac Okafor was court-martialed at a railhead south of here. Desertion in the face of the enemy. The sentence was confirmed at division level and carried out at dawn on the nineteenth. The file is thin. It was always going to be thin, because the trial took forty minutes and the evidence consisted of two statements from sergeants who were, at the relevant time, half a kilometer behind the position the man was accused of abandoning." His voice stayed level, procedural, a man reading an inventory. "There was a letter in the file. A petition for clemency, drafted by his company officer, arguing the man had been sent back along the communication trench for stretcher-bearers and had likely been buried or blown off course. The company officer was overruled. The petition was noted in the margin — *not admitted* — and initialed."

"By whom?"

"By the confirming officer." Verrec looked at his own hands. "Who signed with the authority of my section. My section, Elodie. The initials are mine. Not my signature — my initials, applied by a staff procedure I barely remember endorsing, a stack of confirmations initialled in an evening the way one initials laundry lists. Six of them that night. Five I couldn't tell you anything about. This one, I have since learned everything about." He raised his eyes to mine. "I have spent two months learning everything about it. You know why. You've guessed since the day I noticed your handwriting."

The floor seemed very slightly unsteady, the way it did during distant bombardments, the earth transmitting its arguments upward through stone.

"Henri's father," I said.

"Henri's father." He said it like a verdict on himself. "I can't raise the dead and I can't uninitial a page. What I can do is put a child on a truck. And what I want in exchange is that when this is over — when there are tribunals and inquiries and men like me writing memoirs — you will testify that I interceded for Isaac Okafor. That I tried. That the petition was delayed by others, that I fought for clemency, that I did what a decent man could do." He spread his hands, palms up, the first genuinely naked gesture I had ever seen him make. "A lie, Nurse Oyelaran. I am asking you to carry a lie about me into peacetime, because the truth is that I initialled a death warrant between supper and last post and never even learned the man's name until this autumn, and I find — " his voice caught, recovered, " — I find I cannot bear the truth. Not publicly. Privately I'll carry it fine. I've been carrying it since November. But publicly I need to have tried."

* * *

I walked back across the cloister with the cold getting into my bones and the arithmetic turning over and over like a millwheel.

Because here was the thing I could not say to him in the linen room: I understood. God forgive me, I understood him completely. I had built my whole secret church of names on exactly the same foundation — the terror of being a person through whom other people passed without trace. Verrec wanted absolution by fiction; I had chosen remembrance instead, three hundred and eleven names kept alive against every order and every custom. We were the same sickness with different treatments, and he was offering me a bargain that would bind his lie into my testimony forever, so that the man who erased Isaac Okafor would be remembered as the man who tried to save him.

And the price was nothing. That was the obscene part. He wasn't asking for the ledger. He wasn't asking me to betray a confidence or endanger a soul. He was asking me to spend a truth I barely possessed — I knew only what my ledger knew, a name, a date, *executed as deserter* — to buy a seat for the son of the man whose erasure he had begun.

Marguerite was waiting by the infirmary door, arms crossed against the wind, and beside her stood Henri with his mittens tied together on a string around his neck, drawing shapes in the frost on the stone with one finger. Streets. Always streets. He glanced up as I came near, and then, as he always did, he looked quickly away, as if hoping not to be noticed by whatever came next.

"Well?" Marguerite said.

"The price is a lie," I told her. "His lie, not ours. And the seat is Henri's either way — I'll burn every bridge I have to make it so. But Marguerite—" I crouched down to the boy's height, and he stilled, watching me with those grave, unsurrendered eyes, the exact eyes his father must have had at the railhead in March, waiting for a dawn that forty minutes of paperwork had scheduled. "Henri. Tomorrow I'm going to teach you a new name."

Behind us, across the courtyard, the frozen clock began to ring.

It should not have. Its works were dead; Sister Béatrice had sworn the mechanism was rusted through. But the bell struck once, a single brazen note that rolled over the rooftops and out toward the front, and Henri's hand found mine and gripped it hard, and somewhere beyond the hills the guns fell briefly silent, as if listening.
