## 16. The Price Named Aloud

Verrec had written the offer three times in his head before he spoke it, and each draft had been a lie of a different size.

The first version was honest: *Give me the ledger and I will put your name on the transport list.* He had discarded it within a day. She would refuse, and she would refuse not with anger but with that terrible patience of hers, the way she listened as though she were already writing down what you said. No. The ledger was not for sale because it was not, to her, property. It was something closer to scripture. You did not buy scripture. You could only ask for a blessing.

The second draft was practical: *One berth, no conditions.* But he had learned long ago that gifts without price made people suspicious, and Elodie Oyelaran was the most suspicious woman he had ever tried to deceive, or to save—he still had not settled which verb governed him.

So he arrived at the third draft, the one he carried into the dispensary that evening like a man carrying a loaded weapon he hoped would misfire.

*Tell them I tried.*

The dispensary smelled of carbolic and camphor and, beneath both, the cold stone of a building that had been praying longer than any army had existed. She was there alone, counting gauze into rolls by lamplight, her sleeves rolled to the elbow, her hands moving with the economy of someone who had stopped wasting motion years ago. She did not look up when he came in. She knew his step. That was the trouble with convalescing in the hospital you intended to rob: everyone learned how you walked.

"Sister Béatrice says the truck comes in nine days," she said, still counting. "Nine days, one seat beyond the wounded allocation, and three names that matter. Have you come to tell me the arithmetic has changed?"

"The arithmetic hasn't changed." He lowered himself onto the stool across the table from her, and his side ached where the shrapnel had taught him about humility. "But I've been thinking about what to charge."

Her hands went still. Only for a moment—a half-second's pause in the folding of gauze—and then they resumed, because she would not give him the satisfaction of watching her stop working. He admired that. He admired almost everything about her, which was part of the problem. Admiration was a poor foundation for what he was about to do.

"You said protection," she said. "A name on the list. In exchange for the ledger."

"I lied," Verrec said.

She looked up then.

"Not entirely," he went on, before she could decide what to do with the word. "I would protect this hospital if I could protect anything anymore. I would put the boy's name on that list regardless of what you gave me—there, you have that for free, and I hope it doesn't make it worth less. But the ledger—" He exhaled. "I asked for the ledger because asking for the ledger was a thing I understood. Documents. Names on paper. A man in my profession always believes the answer is in the file. It took me six weeks under your roof to understand that you are not keeping files, mademoiselle. You are keeping faith."

"Flattery is an expensive currency," she said quietly. "What do you want?"

And there it was. The door open, the moment arrived, and every rehearsed sentence in his head dissolved like frost. What came out instead was not the third draft at all. It was the truth, arriving late as usual, dressed badly.

"I want you to say I tried."

The lamp flickered between them. Outside, the winter wind moved through the cloister with the sound of paper being sorted.

"Say it to whom?" she asked.

"To whoever asks afterward. To the inquiry, if there is an inquiry. To the boy, when he is old enough to ask what happened to his father and why." Verrec found that his hands, which were steady enough for map work and code work and the composition of casualty lists, would not stay flat on the table. He folded them. "Henri's father was executed under an order that passed through my section. I told you my unit's signature was on it. I didn't tell you the rest—that I read the file before the signature went on it. That I read it and closed it and sent it forward, because the man who countersigned below me was already dead and the paperwork needed doing and it was easier to do paperwork than to be brave."

Elodie set down the gauze. She folded her hands in her lap, mirroring him, and waited. She was very good at waiting. It occurred to him that she had probably spent her whole life letting terrible things finish speaking.

"I never met him," Verrec said. "I never saw his face until you showed me the boy's. But I read his file twice, after. I know where he was born. I know he wrote letters home in a hand so small the censors flagged it as suspicious. I know he ran because his company had been fed into the same sector four times and he was nineteen and everyone else who was going to run had already run." His voice held. He was grateful for that much. "I could have stopped it. One sentence in a margin. *Recommend commutation pending review.* Six words. I have drafted ten thousand words since and none of them cost me what those six would have."

"And now," Elodie said, and her voice was not cruel, which somehow made it worse, "you want me to write the sentence you didn't."

"No." He shook his head slowly. "That's the thing I've been trying to arrive at all evening, and I keep failing, so let me fail plainly. I don't want you to write anything. I want you to *say* it. Later. Afterward. If anyone ever comes asking—some clerk compiling records, some widow petitioning for a pension, some historian nobody will read—I want there to be one person alive who can stand up and say: Officer Verrec interceded for the condemned man. Officer Verrec attempted to save him." He heard his own voice go thin and strange. "It isn't true. I need you to understand that I know it isn't true. I am not asking you to believe a thing. I am asking you to carry a thing."

The clock in the tower had stopped weeks ago—frozen at some hour that had become simply *the hour*, the convent's private eternity—and its silence filled the pause that followed.

"Why?" she asked. Not suspiciously. Genuinely. As if mercy were a foreign coin she was holding up to the light.

"Because when the war ends—and it is ending, Elodie, you can hear it ending in the way the shells walk eastward—there will be a great settling of accounts, and most of the accounts will be settled by men like me. Men who survived. We will write the reports. We will be reasonable in our memoirs. And somewhere in the middle of all that reasonable paper there will be a line about a deserter executed in the autumn of a bad year, and no one will object, because objection requires a witness." He leaned forward. "You are the only witness. You and three hundred and eleven names in a book you won't show me."

"That isn't the reason either," she said.

"No," he admitted. "It isn't."

He looked at his hands again. This time he let them stay where they were.

"The reason is that I have to live in peacetime," he said. "I have discovered, lying in your ward listening to men die politely, that I cannot do it with this in me. Everyone talks about surviving the war as if survival is a place you arrive. It isn't. It's a country you emigrate to, and they check your papers at the border, and I know exactly what mine say. I want one good document forged. One. A single act of false testimony, entered into the record by the most honest woman I have ever met, saying that once, just once, I tried to stop the machine instead of oiling it." He laughed, briefly, without humor. "Do you see the elegance of it? I'm asking the keeper of true names to keep one false one. For me. For free. Except not for free—the seat on the truck. The seat for the boy, or the nurse, or you. Absolution at market rates."

For a long moment Elodie said nothing. Then she rose, crossed to the window, and stood looking out at the darkened courtyard where snow was beginning again, patient and indifferent.

"You're right that it isn't true," she said at last. "And you're right that I know it. Do you know what I do with untrue names, Officer Verrec? I burn them. Or I refuse to write them at all. Sister Superior wanted Henri gone by morning and officially he does not exist, and I have spent these weeks committing small forgeries on his behalf—false names, invented histories—so I know precisely what a lie costs, because I pay for them nightly in the ledger's shadow. Every false name I give Henri is a theft from the truth. Small. Affordable. But real."

"Then refuse," he said. "Refuse, and take the seat anyway, because you'll take it for the boy and we both know it. I'll write the order tonight. No conditions. I release you from this conversation; consider it unspoken."

"That's the second lie you've told me tonight," she said, turning. "Consider it unspoken? You came down here in the dark to speak it aloud. Confession needs a priest, Officer. You didn't come to bargain. You came to be refused—or forgiven—and you dressed it up as commerce because neither of us is permitted to say the word forgiveness in wartime."

The accuracy of it landed in him like a shell walking toward a trench: close, closer.

"If you testify falsely for me," she went on, "you will carry that too. You think your guilt is heavy now? Wait until you owe your clean conscience to someone else's perjury. I would be handing you absolution minted from counterfeit coin, and you would spend it every day for the rest of your life, and somewhere deep in you, in the place where you knew the execution order was wrong and signed anyway, you would *know*. I don't think you'd survive peace any better with a forged soul than with a guilty one."

Verrec sat very still. The lamp burned between them with its small insistence.

"Then what do you propose?" he asked. "I have one seat and one debt and nine days."

"What I propose," Elodie said, "is that you earn the true sentence instead of buying the false one. There is a woman in the village—Marguerite saw her, near the fountain, asking after a boy with dark hair. Henri's mother is alive, Verrec, and searching, and afraid, and if the town falls the way the front is falling, she will fall inside it too." Her eyes were bright and hard as the coins the boy's mother had left at their door. "Find her. Bring her here. Put *her* name on the list if there must be a list. That is the testimony I will give gladly—not that you saved the father, but that you saved the mother. Not a forgery. A fact."

He stared at her. Of all the outcomes he had war-gamed in his bunk over these long weeks, this had not been among them: that the keeper of erased names would hand him a mission instead of a judgment.

"She may not exist," he said. "Marguerite may have seen a stranger."

"Then you'll know you looked," Elodie said. "And that, Officer Verrec, is the entire difference between us and the paperwork. Not the outcome. The looking."

He rose. His side ached; the whole of him ached, suddenly, in the way of a man who has been holding a wound closed by posture alone. At the door he paused, his hand on the frame, and did not turn around, because he could not have said what he was about to say while facing her.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the six words I should have written. I remember them still. *Recommend commutation pending review.* I've said them to myself every night since autumn. Does that count as trying?"

Behind him, quiet as the falling snow: "Ask me again after you've found her."

He stepped out into the cold corridor, and had gone perhaps twenty paces toward the officers' wing when Sister Béatrice emerged from the shadows of the stairwell, lantern in hand, her face pale as the chalk marks on the requisition lists—and he knew before she spoke that the arithmetic had changed after all.

"Officer Verrec," she said. "The shelling has reached the eastern road. The truck is coming tomorrow."
