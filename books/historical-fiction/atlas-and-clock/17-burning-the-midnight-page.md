## 17. Burning the Midnight Page

The boiler room smelled of coal and wet stone, and it was the only place in the convent where no one asked questions.

Elodie had chosen the hour carefully. Two o'clock in the morning, when even Sister Béatrice's insomnia gave way to sleep, when the wounded in the east ward had settled into the shallow rhythm of men whose bodies were too busy repairing themselves to dream. She came down the service stairs with a candle stub and the ledger wrapped in her apron like a loaf of bread she was smuggling out of a bakery.

The fire was low but alive. Père Anselme's boiler had been fed faithfully through the winter because the alternative was frost-cracked pipes and gangrene in every bed, and so the iron beast breathed in its corner, its grate glowing orange through the slats, patient as an animal waiting to be told what it was for.

She set the ledger on the coal scuttle.

Three hundred and eleven names.

She had counted them twice tonight already, once with her finger and once with her eyes, as if the two methods might disagree. They did not. Three hundred and eleven names written across sixty-two pages in ink that had faded from black to brown over four winters, some entries cramped, some sprawling, one—Augustin Lebeau, gunner, who had survived three years of artillery only to drown in a flooded trench—written so large it spilled into the margin because her hand had been shaking so badly she could not make it small.

She would need to know them all. Not vaguely, not in the way she knew them now, which was the knowledge of a woman who could find any name in the book within thirty seconds. She needed them the way soldiers knew their own numbers. Without looking. In the dark. Under pressure. Because tomorrow night—or the next, or whenever Verrec made his move, or whatever inspection the collapsing army invented next—the ledger could not be found on her person. And if it could not be found on her person, it could not exist at all.

*If it cannot leave,* she thought, *it will live in me.*

She pulled the stool from beside the coal bin, sat, and opened the book across her knees.

* * *

She began the way she taught the new nurses to memorize drug dosages: in groups, with anchors, with rhythm.

The first name was Ismaël Toussaint. Private. Executed for desertion, February 1912, in a courtyard outside a garrison town whose name the military report had also declined to record. Henri's father. The reason for all of this—the ledger, the habit of writing, the stubbornness that had become a life's second vocation. She said his name aloud once, quietly, giving it back to the dark room, then moved to the next.

Marcel Aubry. Then Théodore Bakary, who had been listed as *indigène auxiliaire* in the official roll and nothing else, as if he had been equipment rather than a man who sang while he bandaged his own hands. Then Luc Ferreol, who had died asking whether anyone had written to his mother, and whom Elodie had answered honestly: *I will write to her myself.* She had. Four letters now, under a false signature, describing wounds that healed and victories that meant something, because the truth—that he had bled out in forty minutes with his hand in hers—would have killed the old woman too, and Elodie had decided long ago that mercy and lying were sometimes the same act.

Ten names. She closed her eyes and recited them. Opened the book. Checked. One wrong order—she'd placed Ferreol before Bakary. She corrected herself and started again from Toussaint.

This was how the night would go. She understood that by the third hour. It would not be reading; it would be drilling, the slow brutal pedagogy of repetition, and there was no shortcut because memory did not accept cargo it had not carried itself.

The boiler ticked. Somewhere above her, wind worked at the loose shutter on the refectory window, a sound she had heard every night for a month without ever remembering to fix it. Outside, distantly, the war cleared its throat—a long rolling thud to the northeast, far enough to be weather, near enough to be a reminder.

Twenty names. Thirty.

At forty she discovered something she had not expected: the names came with faces.

Not all of them. Some entries were only names to her, taken down from deserting soldiers passing through or from civilians who whispered information in the dispensary, people she had never seen again. But most of the dead she had nursed herself, and their faces rose unbidden—Ferreol's cracked lips, Bakary's careful smile, the boy Gaspard Oury who was seventeen and lied about it so badly that everyone in the ward knew and no one told. Forty names in and she was crying without noticing, tears running steadily down her chin onto the page, and she let them fall because stopping to wipe them felt like an interruption of work, and this was work. This might be the only work left to her.

*Gaspard Oury.* Seventeen. She said it aloud. *Seventeen.*

By fifty she had developed a method she neither planned nor approved: she stopped trying to remember the names as a list and started telling them as a story. *Toussaint, executed. Aubry, who followed him by eight days, self-inflicted. Bakary, fever. Ferreol, hemorrhage.* A chain of cause and consequence, each name linked to the next by proximity of death or alphabet or simple chronology, and the links held better than the list had. At seventy names she recited the whole chain without error and felt something loosen in her chest—not relief, nothing so kind as relief, but the sensation of a door opening in a house she had thought fully built.

* * *

She was on page twenty-nine when the door opened.

She did not startle—her body had lost the reflex somewhere in 1915, drowned in a shell crater along with everything else soft about her—but her hand moved flat across the open page nonetheless, an instinct older than thought.

Sister Béatrice stood in the doorway in her grey habit, holding her own candle, her face half-lit and entirely unsurprised.

"I heard the stairs," the old nun said. "You went down hours ago."

"I couldn't sleep."

"No," Béatrice agreed. "You couldn't." She crossed the room, set her candle on the shelf above the coal bin, and looked at the ledger with an expression Elodie could not read—which was unusual, because in six years Béatrice had become one of perhaps two people alive whose faces Elodie could read fluently. "How many?"

"Three hundred and eleven."

"How many do you know yet?"

"Fifty-eight." Elodie swallowed. "Fifty-eight perfectly. Perhaps eighty more approximately."

Béatrice nodded slowly, and lowered herself onto the upturned crate she had clearly used for exactly this purpose before, in exactly this room, in some other decade, watching some other woman carry something impossible alone. "Then you have until dawn," she said, "and I have a good memory for lists. My mother kept a dry goods inventory in her head until the day she died. Read them to me. I'll catch your errors."

"Sister—"

"You are going to burn it." It was not a question. "Verrec has been circling you for eleven days like a hawk over a hen run, and the evacuation lists went up yesterday, and you sat through supper doing arithmetic with your eyes. There is only one reason a woman burns a book she has kept secret for four years." Béatrice folded her hands. "Read them to me, Elodie. If it is going to live in you, it should live in more than one place. Memory kept alone is a rumor. Memory kept together is a witness."

Elodie looked at her for a long moment. The candle flames leaned in the draft from the stairwell. The boiler breathed.

"Ismaël Toussaint," she said. "Private. Executed February 1912. Desertion."

"Ismaël Toussaint," Béatrice repeated, in the flat clear voice she used for the Psalms.

They worked.

* * *

Dawn was a grey suggestion at the high vent-window when Elodie reached the last page.

Her voice had gone rough somewhere around name two hundred and forty, and they had taken breaks—Béatrice had produced, from some hidden pocket of her habit, a heel of bread and a twist of salt, and they had eaten in silence like two women sharing a last meal nobody had announced. But the breaks had been short, because both of them understood what the shelling had been telling them all night: the intervals were shrinking. The war was winding itself up for a final gesture, and the town lay in its palm.

"Two hundred and ninety-nine," Elodie read. Her finger rested under the name. "Marguerite Corbin."

Béatrice looked up sharply. "Our Marguerite?"

"She doesn't know it's there. She's never seen the ledger." Elodie hesitated. "It was her brother. Émile Corbin, listed missing at Verdun in '16, officially recorded as *no further information*, which means the army lost him and didn't want the paperwork of admitting it. She asked me, months ago, if a name could be added. So I added hers beneath his. Because if Émile is remembered, she is part of the remembering."

Béatrice was quiet a moment. Then: "Two hundred ninety-nine. Marguerite Corbin." And after a pause: "She'll be on the truck."

"Yes."

"And the boy."

"Henri has a berth. False papers, false name—René Fournier, born Lille. Verrec arranged it." She said the officer's name carefully, neutrally, the way one handles a blade one hasn't decided whether to keep.

"And you."

"I've done my arithmetic," Elodie said. "There is one berth beyond the wounded allocation. One seat for three people who need it. The boy cannot go alone. Marguerite will not abandon him, and I will not ask her to choose between us, so I am removing myself from the count." She turned the final page. "That leaves three hundred and ten names between Marguerite's and mine, and mine isn't written anywhere, and it never was. Let's finish."

"Elodie."

"Sister."

"When this book is ash, and you are standing in a burning town carrying three hundred and twelve names—" Béatrice's voice stayed level, Psalm-flat, but her hands had tightened in her lap, "—you will say them to someone. Do you understand me? Not to yourself. To another living person. A memory hoarded dies with its keeper. A memory given becomes history."

Elodie closed the ledger.

"I'll say them," she said. "Every year. However many of us survive to listen."

"Then we're ready."

"Not quite." Elodie stood, legs stiff, and crossed to the boiler. She opened the grate door fully, and the heat rolled out against her face like a held breath finally released. She fed in a piece of coal, worked the lever until the fire climbed, and waited until the glow filled the firebox—hot enough to take paper quickly, cleanly, without leaving half-burned pages fluttering up the flue for some clerk to retrieve.

She went back to the scuttle. Sixty-two pages. She tore them free in bundles of ten, squared the edges against her knee, and laid the first bundle on top.

"Last chance to know them all first," she said. "Both directions, forward and back. Toussaint to Oyelaran, and Oyelaran to Toussaint."

Béatrice raised an eyebrow. "Recite backward, then. From the end."

Elodie closed her eyes. The exhaustion sat behind them like sand, and beneath it, strangely, a stillness she had not felt since before the war—the stillness of a decision fully made, with nothing left to deliberate. She began at the bottom of the final page and climbed the ledger in reverse, name by name, the dead rising in reverse order like a tide running backward, and Béatrice listened with her eyes shut too, following in the silence, catching her twice—once on a transposed pair of brothers, once on a civilian named Odile Vasseur whom Elodie had nearly skipped—and each time Elodie stopped, corrected, descended again.

She finished at Ismaël Toussaint, in the cold grey light before sunrise, and opened her eyes.

"Forward," Béatrice said.

She recited them all again, forward, without the book, while Béatrice held the ledger open on her lap and turned pages in time like a choir mistress keeping count. Three hundred and eleven names took forty minutes spoken aloud at a funeral pace, and when Elodie reached the end her throat was raw and her cheeks were wet and Sister Béatrice said, simply, "All present," in the voice of a woman closing a register, and Elodie laughed—one short broken sound that was mostly grief wearing laughter's coat—and picked up the first bundle of pages.

* * *

She burned them slowly.

Not out of sentimentality—she had promised herself there would be none of that, and she mostly kept the promise—but out of care. Ten pages at a time, fed flat into the firebox, watched until the corners caught and curled and the ink lifted off the paper in brief ghostly breaths of flame. Names she had written by lamplight while a man died beside her. Names written in the dispensary with one hand because the other was holding a wound closed. The entry for Augustin Lebeau, the drowning, with its shaking oversized letters—she burned that one separately, alone, and watched it go with something that was not quite peace and not quite permission but stood near enough to both that she let it pass.

The smoke rose straight up the flue without color or smell. The convent would be warm today. There was a bleak practical joke in that—the nuns would move through the corridors saying *isn't the heating good this morning* and never know why—and Elodie held the joke in her mind like a coin she would spend later, if later offered anything to spend it on.

Page forty. Page fifty.

Béatrice stood beside her, one hand resting on Elodie's shoulder, and together they watched the last bundle catch. The ledger's spine went in last, leather cover and all, and the fire took it greedily, and for a moment the brass corner-fittings glowed like two small eyes refusing to close, and then they dimmed, and it was over.

Four years of secret keeping. Reduced to warmth.

Elodie stood in front of the open grate and felt for the names inside her the way one probes a sore tooth—expecting absence, finding instead that everything was still there. *Ismaël Toussaint.* First. *Odile Vasseur.* Middle. *Marguerite Corbin.* Near the end, where she belonged. All of it intact. Nothing lost. The book was gone and the book remained, translated at last into the only medium the war had never managed to confiscate.

"It doesn't feel lighter," she said.

"It won't," Béatrice said. "It will feel heavier. That's how you know it worked."

Above them, faint through the floorboards, the convent was waking. Footsteps in the refectory. The clatter of the water pump. And threaded underneath, so distant it might have been imagination, the low continuous mutter of artillery from the northeast—no longer weather, no longer distant at all, closer than yesterday, closer by exactly one day.

Béatrice pinched out both candles. Grey daylight filled the boiler room, and in it Elodie saw the ash floating in the grate like black snow falling upward.

"Twelve days became nine," the nun said quietly. "The Mother Superior had word at matins. The convoy schedule has collapsed—they're moving loading to the day after tomorrow. Dawn."

Elodie's hand closed, though there was nothing left in it to hold.

"Then we load the wounded," she said. "And we put Henri on that truck. And we say three hundred and eleven names at the gate as it leaves, if we have to say them out loud in front of everyone."

"And you?" Béatrice asked. "What will you be doing while the truck pulls away?"

Elodie watched the last of the ash settle.

"What I was trained for," she said. "Staying behind with the ones who can't be moved."

The shells spoke again, nearer now, and this time neither of them pretended it was thunder.
