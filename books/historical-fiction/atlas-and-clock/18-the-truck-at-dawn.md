## 18. The Truck at Dawn

**Elodie**

The shelling started before first light, far off but no longer far enough—a sound like doors slamming in a house two streets away, then one street, then next door. Elodie stood at the ward window with her coat already on and watched the horizon flicker, a gray pulse behind the hills that had been quiet for six years of war and was quiet no longer.

Behind her, the ward was waking. Forty-one wounded men who could walk were to walk; thirty who could not had been judged well enough to be jolted over broken roads in a canvas-backed truck, because there was no other truck and no other choice, and the order signed in Tripoli did not concern itself with fractures.

"Elodie." Sister Béatrice's voice, low, at the door of the ward. "The Mother Superior wants the courtyard cleared by seven. The convoy officer says the schedule has moved up."

"The schedule said eight."

"The schedule," Béatrice said, "is a rumor we wrote down."

Elodie turned from the window. In the corner by the stove, wrapped in a blanket too big for him, Henri was awake—of course he was awake; he had been awake since the first distant slam, sitting perfectly upright with his eyes open, the way children sit when they have learned that panic is for adults and their job is simply to watch. He had his bundle between his knees. He had packed it himself in the night, she had seen him do it through the crack of her office door: the maps, the folded paper soldiers, the button from a coat she had never found the rest of.

"Henri," she said. "Today we go on a truck."

He looked at her. He did not nod. Six months now, and still his silences were more articulate than most people's speech, and what this silence said was: *I have gone on trucks before. Trucks are where things happen to me.*

"You'll be with Marguerite," Elodie said. "The whole way. I promise you."

It was not yet a lie. She made herself believe it was not yet a lie, and went down to the courtyard.

* * *

**Marguerite**

Marguerite had counted the berths eleven times and gotten eleven different answers, which meant the answer was whatever happened at dawn, which meant arithmetic was finished and luck had taken its place.

She carried stretchers. That was the whole of her morning for the first hour: stretcher after stretcher out the cloister door and down the ramp the brothers had built three hundred years ago for wine casks, and every trip across the courtyard she passed the truck—the one truck, its bed already crowded, its driver a boy of maybe nineteen eating a heel of bread with the concentration of a man who did not know when he would eat again—and every trip she counted heads against her list and got a different number.

"Corporal Anselme—he's marked walking but he can't walk," she told Elodie on her fourth pass. "And there's a sergeant in the east ward nobody put on any list at all. And Verrec's orderly came down asking whether officers' baggage counts against the weight allowance, and I nearly hit him with a bedpan."

"Did you?"

"I considered it seriously."

They worked. The sky in the northeast had gone the color of a bruise going yellow, and each thump from beyond the hills came a half-second sooner than the last, the way footsteps come down a staircase. Marguerite had learned to measure war in those half-seconds. When she'd arrived at the convent two years ago the guns had been a weather report from another country. Now they were close enough that the windows ticked in their frames, and she caught herself doing the math without meaning to: *if it takes them a week to cross those hills, we have four days; if it takes them two days—*

"Marguerite." Elodie's hand on her arm. "Henri. Where is Henri?"

"He's in the chapel with Sister Béatrice, where he's supposed to—"

"He was there ten minutes ago. I looked and the pew was empty."

* * *

**Henri**

Henri had gone to the bell tower because the clock had stopped and someone had to fix it.

This was the reasoning he conducted inside himself, wordlessly, in the language of pictures he used instead of words: the picture of the clock face with its hands frozen at twenty past four; the picture of his father's pocket watch, which had also stopped, at ten past nine, and which men in gray coats had taken away along with everything else; the understanding, held somewhere below thinking, that things which stopped needed fixing, and that if the clock could be made to run again then perhaps the guns would go backward down their staircase the way they had climbed it, and the town would unbreak itself, and the order of the world would resume.

He climbed the tower stairs because children were invisible if they moved quickly and kept their eyes down, and he was expert at both.

The stairs were narrow and smelled of pigeon. At the top, under the great slatted louvers, the clock's machinery stood taller than he did—brass wheels and iron weights and a pendulum hanging still as a hanged man. He stood looking up at it, working out where one would even begin, when the world outside went white.

Not loud. White. First. Then the sound arrived through the floor, through the walls, through his teeth, and the tower leaned—not much, only a hand's width, only enough—and dust came down through the louvers in a slow gold curtain, and somewhere below the bells rang once, all together, though no one had pulled anything.

A shell had struck the roof of the nave. Not the tower. Next to the tower. Close enough.

Henri sat down on the stairs and put his arms over his head, because that was what you did, and waited for the second one, and it did not come, and after a while he heard boots on the stairs below him, many boots, taking the steps two at a time.

* * *

**Verrec**

Verrec heard the shell land and felt the building flinch around him like a horse bothered by flies.

He had been in the dispensary, packing his own kit into a single case—he traveled light, always had, a professional habit that had begun as poverty and matured into philosophy—and when the dust settled he was already moving, because ten years of intelligence work had taught his body to move before his mind filed the paperwork. Out the corridor. Into the cloister, where the air was full of plaster smoke and nuns were crossing in every direction like chess pieces under attack, and the counting began in his head automatically: *that shell fell short, forty meters, they're bracketing, the next adjustment walks it either onto us or past us.*

"Where's the child?" he asked the first person he saw, which was Marguerite, white-faced at the top of the ramp with a stretcher she had set down or dropped.

"The chapel—we think—"

"The tower," said a novice, breathless, pointing up. "Someone's in the tower, I heard him on the stairs—"

Verrec took the tower stairs at speed. His leg, which had been shattered at a place whose name he declined to remember, complained, and he overruled it. Halfway up he met the boy coming down, gray with dust, walking with terrible composure, one hand on the wall.

Verrec crouched. It cost him something to crouch. "Are you hurt?"

Henri shook his head. Then, in a voice small and scraped and almost unused: "I was going to fix it."

For a moment Verrec could not speak. Six years of reading men's faces in interrogation rooms and border offices and the corridors of ministries where decisions wore suits, and it was a six-year-old on a stairwell who finally showed him the thing he had been trying to see since he arrived at this convent: the exact shape of what the war does. It stops clocks. It makes children responsible for starting them again.

"So was I," Verrec said. Which was true, though he had not known it until he said it. "Come down with me. It's better fixed together."

He took the boy's hand. The boy let him. And Verrec, descending, understood that he had just done the only completely honest thing he had done in months, and that in an hour he was going to ask this child's protector to help him lie his way into peacetime, and that both of these facts would continue to exist in him at the same time, forever, without resolving.

* * *

**Béatrice**

The Mother Superior was dead.

Not killed—dead the way old women die when the world slams too hard one time too many: Sister Béatrice found her in her study, seated upright in her chair, the evacuation orders still in her lap, her heart having chosen the moment of the shell strike to conclude its long negotiation. Her face was peaceful in a way that seemed almost pointed, as if she had arranged it.

Béatrice stood in the doorway a long moment. Around her the convent screamed and hurried and hauled, and here was this stillness, this final administrative act: the woman who had ruled them for thirty years, signing off at last.

There was no time to grieve properly. Béatrice noted, with the part of her mind that had kept the convent's accounts for two decades, that grief would have to be scheduled for later, itemized, deferred—like so much else. She closed her superior's eyes, straightened the papers in her lap so they looked deliberate, and covered her face with the ledger cloth from the desk drawer, and then she went down to the courtyard to find that the convoy schedule had, as she had privately predicted for weeks, dissolved entirely.

The convoy officer—a captain with mud on his collar and a wireless operator's exhaustion behind his eyes—was shouting at the driver in the flat voice of a man reciting rules to a universe that had stopped listening. The second truck, the one assigned to take the walking wounded at oh-eight-hundred, did not exist anymore; its column had been redirected south to a crossroads that was now under artillery observation. There was one truck. There was one truck and there were seventy-three souls in the convent and the captain kept saying *priority categories* and *weight allowance* and *I have my orders* and each time he said it, another shell landed somewhere beyond the wall, closer, the footsteps on the stairs unmistakable now.

"Sister," the captain said, turning on Béatrice as if the convent's entire catastrophe were somehow clerical, "I need a manifest. Names, ranks, categories. Now."

"You may have your manifest," Béatrice said, "when my dead are prayed over and my living are loaded, and not before, and if you shout at me again, Captain, I will remind you that this house was sheltering the sick when your regiment's grandfather was in short trousers."

Something in the captain's face cracked—relief, she realized later. He wanted, for one moment, to be shouted at by someone who was not the war.

"Yes, Sister," he said. "Take your time."

She had no time. Nobody had time. Time had left the building along with the schedule and the second truck and the Mother Superior, and what remained was work.

* * *

**Elodie**

Elodie found the courtyard transformed into the thing she had dreaded for months: a machine for sorting human beings.

Stretchers in rows. Walking wounded in queues. Nuns moving among them with tags and chalk, marking lapels: *L1, L2, S, C.* The chalk squeaked. Somewhere a man was singing very quietly, a popular song about a girl from the coast, stopping and starting like a music box winding down. Henri sat on the chapel steps between Marguerite and Verrec, dusted white, drinking from a cup Marguerite held for him, and Elodie's whole body unclenched at the sight of him and immediately clenched again, because the sight of him safe was inseparable now from the question underneath every other question, the arithmetic that had been waiting for her all week:

One truck. Seventy-three souls. Priority categories already consuming the space—she could see it happening, the truck filling with men whose tags bore numbers, the categories eating the capacity the way rust eats iron. And after the categories, the remainder. The remainder was the word for her, and Marguerite, and Henri, and the nuns, and every soul in the house who wore a habit or a nurse's apron or nothing at all in the way of papers.

"Elodie." Verrec rose when he saw her, courteous as ever, and there was something new in his courtesy, something almost shy. "The captain will need the ward lists. I've started collating them."

"You've started—" She looked at him. "Why?"

"Because order is the only thing holding this courtyard together," he said, "and because I owe this house a debt I intend to pay in the only currency I have, which is competence." A pause. The guns spoke again, twice, quickly, like a man clearing his throat before difficult news. "And because when we reach the matter of the remaining berths, I want you to know that whatever else I am, I am not bargaining with you today. Today I'm on your side of the table. You may verify that later, at your leisure, with suspicion."

"With suspicion," Elodie agreed. "At length."

He almost smiled. Then his face changed, tracking something over her shoulder, and she turned, and the whole courtyard turned with her, seventy faces tilting upward at once.

The clock tower was smoking. Not burning—smoking, a thin thread rising from where the shell had opened the roof of the nave alongside it, drifting up past the frozen clock face with its hands still at twenty past four, as if the tower were sending up a signal on behalf of stopped time everywhere. And as Elodie watched, the smaller of the two bells, loosened in its yoke by the concussion, swung once, hard, against its rim.

One note rolled out over the town. Broken, off-key, enormous.

The singing man stopped. The chalk stopped squeaking. For the length of that single decaying tone, the whole courtyard—wounded and whole, nurses and nuns, the boy on the steps, the captain with his useless manifest—stood in silence and listened to the clock toll the hour that did not exist.

Then the next shell came down on the far side of the wall, close enough to feel through the ground, and the schedule died completely. The driver was in his cab. The captain was screaming about the bridge road being cut. Marguerite had Henri by the hand and was looking at Elodie across thirty feet of churning courtyard with a question in her face that no longer had the luxury of being unasked—

*Who. Three names. One seat. Now.*

And Elodie, moving toward them through the smoke, reached into her coat pocket and found the ledger there, thin and warm and heavy as a stone, and understood with sudden clarity that before this truck pulled out, she was going to have to decide what a name was worth.
