## 19. The Last Berth

The trucks came before the light did.

They ground up through the frozen mud of the square at half past four in the morning, headlights hooded, engines coughing in the cold like old men trying to remember how to breathe. Elodie heard them from the ward, where she was finishing a bandage change by candlelight because the generator had died two days ago and no one had come to fix it. She tied off the last knot, told Corporal Aumont he would live to complain about the food in the rear echelon, and went to the window.

Three trucks. Not four. The manifest had promised four.

She counted again, as if wishing could add a vehicle. Three. And beyond them, far to the east, the sky had that wrongness she had learned to read the way sailors read weather—a dull orange pulsing under the cloud bank, too steady to be fire, too rhythmic to be dawn. The front was not collapsing anymore. It had collapsed. What was coming toward them now was only the news of it, traveling at the speed of artillery.

Downstairs, the convent had woken all at once. Nuns moving through the corridors with lamps, wounded men being lifted onto stretchers, Sister Béatrice at the great door directing the loading of crates with the calm of a woman who had decided years ago exactly how she intended to die and was pleased to find the occasion arriving on schedule.

Elodie found Marguerite in the refectory, dressing Henri by lamplight. Someone had given him a soldier's coat, cut down, the sleeves rolled three times. He stood very still while she buttoned it, the way he did everything—watching, storing, saying nothing until the words were worth the cost of them.

"Is it time?" Marguerite asked.

"It's time."

"Which truck?"

"The second one. It has the berth list." Elodie took the paper from her apron—the list, the sacred arithmetic of twelve days' argument, reduced now to thirty-one lines in an adjutant's careless hand. She had read it so many times she could recite it backward. Twenty-two for the wounded, numbered by triage priority. Four for the nursing staff: herself, Marguerite, and two others. One for Sister Superior, who would not take it. One for Verrec.

And Henri's name—*Henri Marchal, age six, dependent*—written there by Verrec's intervention, in the space that had been bought with whatever bargain the officer had made with the transport command. A name out of nothing. A boy made official long enough to be saved.

Marguerite lifted him onto the bench and held his face in both hands. "Now you listen to me. You get on the truck. You sit where I tell you. You do not get off until I say, not for anyone, not for any reason."

"I know," Henri said.

"You *know*, but you'll do it anyway?"

He almost smiled. Six years old and he already had the nurse's look, the one that measured how frightened you were and kindly decided not to mention it.

They went out into the dark. The snow had stopped sometime in the night and the air was the kind that hurt—the still, ringing cold that came before a bombardment, though Elodie refused to let herself think in those terms yet. The square was chaos organized by lamplight: stretcher-bearers shouting numbers, a chaplain moving among the waiting wounded with his collar turned against the wind, Sister Béatrice checking each man off her own list because she trusted no list she hadn't written herself.

Elodie walked Henri past the first truck, past the rows of litters lashed down under canvas, and stopped at the tailgate of the second. A sergeant with a clipboard stood there matching faces to lines.

"Marchal," Elodie said. "Dependent. Line twenty-nine."

The sergeant found it, frowned at the boy, frowned at her. "One guardian listed. Nurse Corbet."

"That's correct." She turned. "Marguerite. Take him up."

Marguerite looked at the list in Elodie's hand, then at Elodie, and Elodie watched the understanding arrive in her face the way frost arrives on glass—not all at once but line by line, spreading.

"No," Marguerite said. "No, Elodie. Your berth is—"

"My berth is line thirty. Read it yourself if you like. Lines don't argue."

"You can't—" Marguerite's voice cracked and she dropped it to a fierce whisper, dragging Elodie aside by the sleeve, away from the sergeant, away from Henri's listening eyes. "They need nurses where you're going. Wherever you're going. You can't just give it away, that isn't how—"

"It's exactly how," Elodie said. "It's the only way it works. One of us goes with him or neither of us matters. He knows your hands. He'll sleep if you're there."

"And you'll—what? Stay here? The town is falling, Elodie. You've seen the maps, you've seen—"

"I've seen them." She took Marguerite's hands then, both of them, cold fingers around cold fingers, and made herself smile. "I survived six years of this war by counting things. Beds, pulses, names. Let me count this once without flinching. He goes. You go. That's the sum. Everything else is remainder."

Marguerite was crying now, angrily, the way she did everything—refusing to make it convenient for anyone. "You memorized them all. Tell me you memorized them all."

"Three hundred and eleven. First one Baptiste Oyelaran's boy—no. First one is a private named Anselme Tissot, executed at Varennes, June 1911. Last is a fisherman's daughter called Céleste, drowned off the jetty, no surname ever given. In between, all of them. They live somewhere better than paper now."

"Say one back to me. Prove it."

"Number two hundred and four. Yves Kaddour, stoker, merchant marine, died of pneumonia in the east ward, March of this year. His mother lives in Sète and thinks he was buried under his own stone."

Marguerite laughed and sobbed at once, an ugly wonderful sound. "You madwoman."

"Go," Elodie said. "Before I start being sensible."

She lifted Henri onto the tailgate herself. Inside, under the canvas, the litters were packed tight, and Marguerite climbed up beside a space cleared along the wheel well—small, dark, but a space, with a blanket folded on it. Henri sat down in it as if it were a throne.

He looked up at Elodie. And she saw him understand—he understood everything, these children of the war, they understood more than generals—and he reached into the coat and took out something and pressed it into her hand.

One of his maps. Folded small, soft as cloth from handling. She didn't open it. She knew what would be on it: streets drawn from memory or dream, a river, a bell tower, the small square figure he always put at the center of every map, standing in the middle of the place where he was loved.

"For the road," he said gravely. "So you know the way."

"I know the way," Elodie said, and her voice held, which she counted among the day's miracles. "Look after her. She cries when no one's watching and pretends she doesn't."

"I won't tell."

"That's my brave boy."

Then the canvas came down over them, or she pulled it—she never afterward remembered which happened first—and the sergeant was shouting numbers, and the engine of the second truck turned over and caught, and Elodie stepped back off the tailgate onto the frozen mud of the square.

That was all. No ceremony. No music. She simply stepped back, and the distance between her hand and the canvas was perhaps a hand's width, and then it was ten meters, and then the truck lurched forward into the column, and the war took them out of her reach the way it took everything, without pausing to notice.

* * *

She stood in the square watching the taillights diminish and did not hear Verrec come up beside her until he spoke.

"They've gone ahead to the railhead," he said. "Twelve hours to the coast if the bridges hold."

"If the bridges hold."

"Yes." He was in greatcoat and cap, kit bag at his feet, holding his berth confirmation in one gloved hand. Line thirty-one. The last line. He looked at the paper, then at the remaining truck, where staff officers were arguing over loading order, then—at length, with the particular attention of a man reading something written in a language he'd spent months pretending not to know—at her.

"You gave your seat away," he said.

"The arithmetic favored it."

"The arithmetic rarely favors intelligence officers. We're kept aboard precisely because we don't favor anything." He paused. "There's room on this truck. My line. Thirty-one."

"Take it, then. Go."

"I don't intend to."

She turned to look at him fully for the first time. In the lamplight his face was older than it had been in the dispensary, thinner, the courteous armor of the cat-and-mouse weeks worn through in places. Whatever he had carried since telling her about the execution order—that signature, that unit, that guilt—he was carrying it visibly now, the way soldiers carry wounds they haven't reported.

"Captain," she said carefully, "if this is about absolution, I can't sell it to you. I told you that."

"You did. You were right to." He folded the paper once, twice, precisely, and put it in his pocket. "This isn't absolution. Absolution would be going with them and writing my report and testifying at whatever tribunal they convene when the shooting stops. This is simpler. Somebody has to stay with the ones who don't make the list." He nodded toward the chapel, where Sister Béatrice was now helping load the wounded who would travel lying down, and beyond her, to the wards still full of men too unstable to move, the ones the arithmetic had abandoned entirely. "Thirty-one berths. Two hundred souls in this square. I've spent five years making lists where most people didn't appear. I'd rather spend the last day of the war standing on the correct side of one."

"You'll die here," Elodie said plainly.

"We may all die here. The difference is that I will die having done one thing in this war that I would do again." He picked up his kit bag. Then, quietly, without looking at her: "Tissot. Anselme. Varennes, June 1911. You said it to Corbet just now—I was near enough to hear. You keep them alive by saying them."

"Yes."

"Then say mine, someday, if it comes to that. Auguste Verrec. Not in any ledger. Just somewhere, in your memory, filed under *tried*."

He walked away toward the chapel, coat collar up, not tall and not stooped, just a tired man walking toward the correct side of a list. Elodie watched him go and found, to her surprise, that she was not angry with him anymore. Forgiveness, it turned out, was sometimes just exhaustion wearing its Sunday clothes.

* * *

The third truck left at six, carrying the last of the walking wounded and Sister Superior, who at the final moment pressed her pectoral cross into Elodie's hands and said only, "The crypt. Keep the key. What's hidden there must outlive us all," and was hauled up into the canvas dark and gone.

After that, the square emptied the way a tide empties, leaving behind the things the sea has judged: the unmovable wounded, the few nuns who would not leave their infirmary, a handful of civilians with nowhere the trucks were going, and the dead who had died overnight waiting for evacuation and would now wait for something else.

By eight o'clock the orange pulse under the eastern clouds had become visible flashes. By nine, the sound arrived—distant, arrhythmic, patient. Shells falling on the ridge road. On the railhead, someone said. On the railhead, God help them, where the trucks were headed.

Sister Béatrice appeared at Elodie's elbow with two cups of chicory, and handed her one as though they were simply two women taking a morning break, which, in the ruins of every other category, they were.

"You should have gone," the old nun said. It was not an accusation. Just inventory.

"Yes," Elodie said. "Probably."

"But you didn't."

"No."

Béatrice nodded slowly, sipped her chicory, watched the smoke rising over the ridge. "The clock stopped in November," she said. "Mother Superior called it an omen. She was right, but not the way she thought. It wasn't announcing an ending." She turned her pale eyes on Elodie. "It was announcing you."

"I don't understand."

"You will. Come inside. The shelling will walk this way by noon, and there are eleven men in the east ward who cannot walk at all, and someone must hold their hands and speak to them, and I am an old woman whose voice cracks." She drained her cup. "Tell me some names, nurse. You know three hundred and eleven. I should like to learn them before the end."

Elodie looked east one more time—toward the ridge, toward the railhead, toward twelve hours of bad road and two people asleep under canvas, a boy with a soldier's coat rolled three times at the sleeves and a young woman crying furiously and refusing to admit it—and then she turned her back on the smoke and followed the nun inside.

Behind them, over the rooftops, the bell tower stood against the brightening sky, whole for now, its stopped clock face pale as a moon. The shells were coming closer. She counted them anyway, all the way in, out of habit, out of vow—*Anselme Tissot. Yves Kaddour. Céleste of the jetty.*—and the first of the afternoon's shells fell short, and the tower swayed, and did not fall.

Not yet.
