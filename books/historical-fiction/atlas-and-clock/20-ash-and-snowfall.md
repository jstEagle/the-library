## 20. Ash and Snowfall

The truck's engine faded for a long time after it disappeared, the way a bell keeps ringing after it has been struck. Elodie stood in the road with her hands empty and her coat unbuttoned, listening until the sound dissolved into the wind off the hills, and then she stood there longer, because there was nothing else the road asked of her.

Snow had begun again. Fine, dry snow, the kind that didn't fall so much as hang in the air, undecided. It settled on her shoulders and in the hollow of her collar and she did not brush it away. Somewhere beyond the ridge the shelling had paused—not stopped, paused, like a man drawing breath—and in that pause the whole town seemed to be holding its own breath with it.

"You'll freeze standing there like a fence post," said Sister Béatrice behind her.

"I'm not cold."

"That's how it starts." The old nun came down the convent steps with a lantern in one hand and a blanket over her arm, moving with the unhurried certainty of a woman who had buried three prioresses and outlived two governments. She put the blanket around Elodie's shoulders the way you'd dress a child, without asking permission. "Inside. The chapel roof is half gone but the crypt is dry, and the crypt is where we'll be."

Elodie looked back down the road one last time. The ruts the truck's wheels had cut were already filling with snow.

"He'll be warm," she said. It wasn't a question and it wasn't to Sister Béatrice. It was to the road, or to the dark, or to whoever kept accounts now that her ledger was ash in the boiler.

"He'll be warm," Sister Béatrice agreed. "And you'll be dead of exposure if you don't move. Come."

* * *

The chapel had taken its wound two days before, a shell that had missed the bell tower by thirty feet and taken the roof's eastern slope instead. The nuns had covered the gap with canvas and planks, and the canvas had already torn loose, so that when Elodie stepped inside she stepped into a room where the snow came down gently through the broken vault, drifting past the shattered window tracery, past the headless statue of the convent's founder, and settling in the open pews like a congregation of its own.

The altar was intact. Someone—Sister Béatrice, probably, in the hours Elodie had spent at the truck—had set two candles burning on it, and their light moved on the wet stone walls in a way that made the whole ruined space feel less like a ruin than like a held breath.

"The crypt stairs are behind the sacristy," Sister Béatrice said. "But we won't go down yet. Down is for when they come up the hill. Right now I want to see the road."

They sat in the third pew from the back, the one with the best view through the broken doors, and Elodie realized with a strange, floating clarity that she was being cared for. That after eleven days of deciding who ate, who moved, who hid, who lived, someone had decided that she would sit, and be warm, and wait.

"The others?" she asked.

"Sisters Anne-Cécile and Thérèse went with the wounded, God keep them. Mother Superior refused the berth and then refused it again and finally I told her the convent needed its mother in the valley and she went, weeping, which I have not seen her do in nineteen years." Sister Béatrice's hands were folded in her lap, knotted and calm. "The lay sisters went this morning. The cook. The gardener. You saw the last of them at the truck."

"I saw the last of them."

"And now it's you and me and the mice." She said it without self-pity, almost with satisfaction, as though the two of them had been winnowed down to something essential. "I've bread and a wheel of cheese and a jug of water in the crypt, and a blanket each, and if the fighting comes up the hill we'll be under three feet of stone, which is more than the town can say."

Elodie looked at the candles. "You could have gone too. Verrec would have taken your name."

"Verrec offered me a berth the way he offered you one—meaning to take it back." The old nun's mouth twitched. "I've watched that man for three weeks, child. He is not a bad man. That is the most dangerous kind."

"I know."

"Do you? You gave him absolution at the truck without a word. He stepped back from the step and you stepped forward and neither of you said a thing, and I thought, there—there is a bargain being struck in silence, and only God knows the terms." She turned her head, and the candlelight caught the deep lines around her eyes. "What did he want from you?"

Elodie was quiet for a moment. The snow came down through the broken roof and landed on the pew ahead of them and did not melt.

"He wanted me to say he tried to save a man he didn't save. He wanted to carry the lie into peacetime and have it be true because I'd said it."

"And did you say it?"

"I told him the truth costs nothing to the dead and everything to the living. And then I told him that if he spent the rest of his life acting like the man he wanted me to lie about, then someday the lie would be true, and he could tell it himself." She heard her own words come back to her and was surprised by them. "I think he believed me. I think that's why he gave up his seat. Not for the boy. For the sentence."

Sister Béatrice considered this. "That's the most honest bargain I've ever heard struck in a war," she said finally. "Both parties cheated, both parties paid. God will have to sort it out, and He'll be annoyed."

Despite everything—a shell hole in the roof, the town going dark street by street below them, three hundred and eleven names living in her skull like tenants—Elodie laughed. It came out of her cracked and rusty, a sound she didn't recognize, and Sister Béatrice nodded as though a debt had been paid.

* * *

The shelling resumed at full dark.

It was farther away than it had been that morning—swung south, toward the river crossings—but the ground carried it up the hill in long, patient concussions that Elodie felt in her back teeth and her sternum. Between the impacts there was a new sound she had learned to dread more than the guns themselves: the crackle and rush of burning, off to the east, where the town's oldest quarter lay. The fire was visible from the pew, a dull orange pulse behind the rooftops, and the smoke came up the hill smelling of tar and thatch and, underneath, something Elodie refused to name.

"The granaries," Sister Béatrice said quietly. "And the tanneries. And the row houses on the rue Saint-Léonard, if the wind turns."

"The wind will turn."

"The wind always turns."

They sat with it. Elodie found that she was not afraid, and this frightened her more than fear would have. She catalogued the feeling the way she'd once catalogued symptoms: pulse steady, hands warm, mind clear. Shock, she thought. Or peace. In the field hospitals she had learned that the two were indistinguishable from the outside, and often from the inside too.

"I keep expecting to feel it," she said. "The ledger. I burned it yesterday and I keep reaching for it—my hand goes to my pocket, to the shelf in the dispensary—and it's not there. It's like a tooth gone. The tongue keeps going back."

"Where is it now?"

She touched her own temple. "Here. All of it. I sat with the pages for six hours before I fed them to the boiler. Three hundred and eleven. I can give you the first ten in order and the last ten in order and most of the middle if you give me a minute to find the thread."

"Give me the first."

"Augustin Ferreol. Deserter by sentence, soldier by every other measure. Executed at V—, the eleventh of March. Father of one son." Her voice did not shake. She had practiced this. "His name was the first because his son was the reason for all the rest."

"And the last?"

"Marguerite's brother. Étienne Rousseau. Prisoner at H—, last confirmed alive in September." She paused. "She doesn't know I put him in. She asked me to, and I said I'd consider it, and I put him in that same night. I wanted her to have to ask. I wanted her to know what it costs to ask."

"And does she?"

"She will. When she's older. Or she won't, and she'll be happier, and the name will still be in me either way." Elodie looked at her hands. "That's the strange arithmetic of it, Sister. The ledger is ash, but nothing is lost. Nothing was ever lost. It was only ever moved—from the reports to the pages, from the pages to me. I'm the ledger now. I'm the worst possible ledger. I'm mortal."

Sister Béatrice was silent for a long moment. The firelight from the east pulsed on the wall behind the altar, and the snow came down through the roof, and somewhere in the dark a beam settled with a groan.

"Child," she said at last, "do you know what a rosary is for?"

"To pray."

"To count." The old nun's voice was very even. "Fifty beads, ten and ten and ten, and a mystery at each decade, and you count them because the mind wanders and the hands must hold it to the work. The Church understood something the army never did. Repetition is how the living survive the unbearable. You count. You breathe. You say the names." She turned on the pew to face Elodie fully. "So we will do it properly. You will say them, and I will listen, and when your voice fails I will say them back to you, and we will go through the night like that, and whatever falls on this town will fall on two women who are busy."

Elodie looked at her. "It's not a prayer."

"Everything said aloud in a ruined chapel at night is a prayer. That's what a chapel is *for*."

* * *

They began at the third pew, in the snow, with the candles burning low.

"Augustin Ferreol," Elodie said, and the name went up through the broken roof into the falling dark. "Camille Aubry. Tomas Weller—he was from the east, he said a word for *home* that I never learned to spell, but I remember the sound of it. Henri Weller. His son. No—" she corrected herself, steady, "—the son lives. The son is on a truck going south. Augustin Ferreol's son. Henri Ferreol, if he wants to be. If he wants to be."

"Say the next one," said Sister Béatrice.

"Marguerite Delaunay is not in the ledger. She is alive and she is south and she has Henri's hand in hers, I made her promise, I made them both promise—" She stopped. Breathed. The old nun waited, hands folded, a stone with a candle behind it. "Delphine Marchal. Civilian, forty-one, died of fever in the east ward. Her husband's name was Luc and he was killed in the first year and no report ever joined them. Luc and Delphine Marchal. Joined now. Joined here."

The names came.

They came slowly at first, each one requiring its small excavation—*Piotr Lysenko, laborer, died in the yard, the dogs howled all night; Anselme Guérin, deserter, nineteen, he cried for his mother and I held his hand and I never knew his mother's name, so I give him to her now across the distance, wherever she is, he did not die alone*—and then, as the night deepened and the candles burned to stubs and Sister Béatrice lit two more without breaking the rhythm, the names came faster, easier, a river finding its channel.

The shelling moved and returned. Once a shell fell close enough that the chapel shuddered and a rain of plaster sifted down over them, white dust on black wool, and neither of them stopped. Sister Béatrice's lips moved through the dust, following, and when Elodie's voice cracked on the ninetieth name the old nun took it up without a gap—*"Baptiste Roux,"* she said, as if she had known him all her life—and carried it for twenty names while Elodie drank water and wept without noise, the way nurses weep, and then took the river back.

Somewhere past midnight the fire in the east grew until it lit the whole chapel, and Elodie could see the snow falling through the broken vault in the orange light, thousands of small bright fragments descending into the ruins, ash and snowfall together, indistinguishable, and she thought: *this is what the ledger looks like now. This is what I made of it. It is falling on everything.*

"Say them," Sister Béatrice said, gently, because she had stopped.

"I'm saying them."

"Say the next one."

* * *

Near dawn the guns stopped.

Not paused—stopped, the way a fever breaks, all at once, leaving a silence so total that Elodie could hear the snow landing on the stone. She and Sister Béatrice looked at each other across the dark. They had said two hundred and forty names. Seventy-one remained.

"Is it over?" Elodie whispered.

"Armistices come at dawn," said the nun. "So do bombardments. We'll know by the light."

The light came gray and slow, and with it came a sound neither of them had heard in days: bells. Distant, muffled, from the valley—the town's church of Saint-Sauveur, or what was left of it, ringing out the hour, and then ringing again, and again, not the hour at all but the old alarm-and-jubilation pattern that every child in the region knew.

Sister Béatrice stood. She was ninety if she was a day, and she stood like a woman of sixty, gripping the pew's end.

"That's either surrender or deliverance," she said. "And I have lived long enough to know they often sound the same."

They climbed to the chapel door together. The town below was half-ruined and smoking, the eastern quarter a black skeleton against the gray sky, but the streets were filling—figures, small with distance, coming out of cellars and doorways, standing in the falling snow, looking south. And on the road that climbed the hill toward the convent, a line of them was walking. Slow, ragged, in uniforms of three armies and in no uniform at all, civilians and soldiers mixed together, the walking wounded and the merely walking, coming up out of the fallen zone through the ash and the snowfall.

Elodie's hand went to her pocket, to the ledger that wasn't there.

"Names," she said, her voice hoarse to nothing. "Sister, every one of them will have a name. Every one of them will need it said."

Sister Béatrice put her hand on the girl's shoulder, and together they watched the line come up the hill.

"Then we had better finish the seventy-one first," she said. "We have work, and the dead of this town are about to be very jealous."
