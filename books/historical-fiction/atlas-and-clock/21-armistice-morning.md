## 21. Armistice Morning

Marguerite woke before the bells and lay still in the strange clean bed, listening to a city that was not on fire.

It had been forty-one days since the truck. She had counted them the way she had once counted pulses—mechanically, out of necessity, because counting was the only thing that kept her from imagining every possible version of what might be happening two hundred kilometers behind the lines. Forty-one days of a borrowed room above a bakery that smelled of yeast and coal smoke. Forty-one days of hot water from an actual tap. Forty-one days of Henri sleeping in the next bed with his maps folded under his pillow like talismans.

The bells began. Not the cracked, half-mad bell of the convent—the convent's clock had stopped months ago and its bell rang flat, wounded—but the great bronze bells of the cathedral, all of them at once, rolling across the rooftops in long silver waves.

And beneath the bells, voices.

Marguerite sat up so fast the room tilted. She went to the window with her heart going like a triage monitor and looked down into the street, where people were coming out of doorways in their nightclothes and their good coats both, and someone was shouting it up and down the cobbles: *It's over. It's signed. It's over.*

Armistice.

She did not cheer. That was the first thing she noticed about herself, standing there with her hands on the cold sill while strangers wept and embraced below—that she could not make her mouth do it. Hope had been trained out of her too thoroughly, in too many wards, by too many boys who had heard the word *over* and died anyway within the hour. She stood at the window and felt the news enter her body slowly, like warmth returning to frostbitten fingers, painful in exactly the same way.

*It's over,* she said to herself, testing it. *It's over.*

Downstairs, the bakery was already erupting. Madame Foulon came through the kitchen door flour to the elbows, laughing and crying at once, and grabbed Marguerite by both shoulders as if she'd known her for twenty years instead of six weeks and shook her gently. "It's finished, girl! Eleven o'clock! They signed it at five and it takes effect at eleven and it is *finished!*"

"Where's Henri?" Marguerite asked, because she always asked first about Henri now; it was reflex, like washing hands.

"In the courtyard feeding his birds. Marguerite—" Madame Foulon stopped shaking her but didn't let go. Her face did something complicated. "Your friend. The one who stayed. Today they'll open the roads."

"I know," Marguerite said.

"They're saying survivors are already walking out. Whole columns of them. People who were left behind when the town fell." Madame Foulon's voice dropped, went careful, the way you spoke to families in the corridor. "They're saying some of them made it into the church crypts. Some of them are just—walking out, weeks later, like ghosts."

Marguerite had known this morning was coming for forty-one days. She had rehearsed it in the dark: the telegram she would send, the train she would take, the hill road up to what remained of the convent. But rehearsal, she knew better than anyone, was nothing. You could rehearse a dressing change a hundred times and still your hands would shake the first time the wound was real.

* *

Henri was in the courtyard with the pigeons, which he fed every morning with a ceremony he had never explained and she had never questioned. He was six going on seven now—he had turned seven in this city, and Madame Foulon had made him a cake with a sugar bird on top, and he had eaten one bite and then asked to be excused—and he stood among the pigeons in his too-big coat like a small serious general reviewing troops.

He heard the bells. Everyone in the city heard the bells. But Marguerite watched his face as he listened past them, sorting the sound the way he sorted everything, into things that meant danger and things that didn't.

"It's the armistice," she said quietly, kneeling down to his level. "Do you know what that means?"

He nodded. He had learned, these six weeks, to answer small questions. *Are you hungry? Do you want the blue cup? Is the coat too tight?* Small questions he could manage. Large ones still closed him like a fist around a coin.

"It means the war is stopping," she said. "Today. At eleven o'clock, everything stops."

Henri looked at her. Then he looked at the pigeons, who were unimpressed by history.

"My papa will come back now," he said.

It wasn't a question. It was a fact he had carried since the truck, maybe longer, maybe since the day the woman left him at the convent door—this certainty, buried deep, that the war was a wall between him and his father and that when the wall came down his father would simply walk around it. Marguerite had never corrected it. There are mercies you extend to children and to the dying, and she had decided long ago that this belonged to both categories.

But this morning, with the bells still ringing, the lie had begun to rot, and she could see him seeing it. His face did not crumple. It *set*—and that was worse, that seven-year-old jaw tightening like a man's, like Elodie's jaw actually, though Elodie wasn't his blood, or maybe she was in some way Marguerite couldn't name.

"Henri," she said carefully. "Come inside. I want to show you something."

* *

In the little room under the eaves, Marguerite took her handbag down from the hook and removed the notebook she had kept since the second week—a cheap school exercise book, bought from the stationer's with money Madame Foulon pressed on her—because somewhere on that truck, watching the town shrink behind them, she had understood something that had kept her awake for nights afterward.

Elodie had memorized three hundred and eleven names and burned the ledger, and if Elodie died—and Marguerite refused, absolutely refused, to complete that thought—but *if*, then the names would die with her. All except the ones written elsewhere. And so Marguerite had begun writing them back down, from memory, one each night, in her terrible scrawl, checking herself against the fragments Henri let drop. She had gotten to sixty-three. Sixty-three names recovered out of three hundred and eleven, like pulling survivors from water.

She opened the book to the first page. At the top, in Elodie's handwriting—which Marguerite remembered perfectly, that careful upright script, the way she crossed her t's like fence posts—was copied the opening entry, the one Elodie had shown her once, late, in the dispensary, after the raid, when trust had been handed over like something breakable:

*No. 1 — [name struck through by official record]. Infantry, executed for desertion, [date redacted]. Father of one son. Buried without marker.*

Beneath it, in Marguerite's own hand, she had written: *His son lives. He is called Henri. He draws excellent maps.*

Henri climbed onto the bed beside her and looked at the page. His lips moved slightly; he was reading now, had learned in the convent infirmary from watching Elodie's finger move down columns.

"That's my papa," he said. "Except it isn't. Because it doesn't say his name."

"No," Marguerite said. "Because they took his name away. A very brave woman put him in a book because they tried to pretend he never existed. But even she only ever knew him by number, because his file was destroyed before she met him."

Henri was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the bells had stopped and someone had started an accordion, badly, joyfully.

"He had a name," Henri said finally. "I know it. I've known it the whole time."

"I know, mon cœur."

"You never asked me."

"I was waiting," Marguerite said, "for you to want to say it."

She had waited six weeks. She would have waited sixty years. She watched the boy sit very straight on the bedspread, his small hands flat on his knees the way Sister Béatrice had taught the convalescents to hold them during prayers, and she watched him gather himself, and she understood that for him this was not a sentence. It was a resurrection. Somewhere his father lay in an unmarked grave with no name over it, and here in this sunlit room above a bakery in a safe city, on the morning the war ended, his son could give it back to him.

Henri took a breath.

"Aimé Oyelaran," he said.

The name filled the room. Marguerite felt it land in her chest like a bell struck from inside. *Oyelaran.* So that was where the boy's coloring came from, the deep warm brown of his skin, the particular architecture of his cheekbones—that was his father, and his father's people, wherever they were, whatever coast or country the war had scattered them from. She had wondered. She had never asked. Another mercy, another cowardice; she no longer troubled to tell them apart.

"Aimé Oyelaran," Henri said again, louder, as if to the whole city, as if the name needed volume to travel the distance to wherever graves were.

"Yes," Marguerite whispered.

She uncapped her pen. Her hands were not shaking—she noted this with professional surprise; her hands shook over splinting sometimes, over nothing, and now they held perfectly steady—and she wrote beneath the struck-through line:

*Aimé Oyelaran. Father of Henri Oyelaran. Executed unjustly. Remembered by his son.*

Then, because she was Marguerite and could not help herself, she added: *Who drew maps of home.*

Henri leaned against her shoulder and read it, moving his finger along under the words the way Elodie used to, and neither of them said anything for a while, and the accordion played badly downstairs, and it was the most sacred quarter-hour of Marguerite's life.

When she looked up, Henri was staring at the page with an expression she recognized from the mirror—from the nights she wrote the names, one per night, like penance.

"There are more," he said. "In the book. More papas."

"Yes," Marguerite said. "There are more."

"I can help you remember them."

And that—*that*—was when she finally cried. Not at the bells, not at the armistice, not at the name spoken aloud, but at this: a seven-year-old orphan offering his memory as an archive, offering to carry the dead alongside her. She pulled him in and held him and let the tears come, and Henri permitted it with the grave patience of a child comforting an adult, patting her arm twice, exactly twice, before asking if they could go watch the celebrations.

* *

The city spent the day being loud, and Marguerite moved through it feeling like a ghost attending someone else's party. Strangers kissed her cheeks. Soldiers danced with old women. A man on a café chair read the communique aloud to a crowd that cheered sentences of bureaucratic French as if they were poetry. *Effective at the eleventh hour of the eleventh day.* She drank one glass of wine and found it tasted like the convent's altar wine, which meant her memory was doing something sentimental, and she put the glass down.

At four o'clock, at the telegraph office—which was mobbed, a queue out the door and down the boulevard, everyone in the city trying to reach everyone else—an exhausted clerk climbed onto a desk and shouted for quiet.

"If you are waiting on word from the eastern departments—the fallen zone—the military trains begin running tomorrow. Civilian traffic Thursday. And—" he consulted a paper in his hand, and his voice changed, went rough—"and for those waiting on the missing. They are coming out. On foot, mostly. The army reports columns of civilians and hospital staff walking west along the main road from Saint-Aubin and the surrounding villages. Hundreds already accounted for. They survived in cellars, in churches, in the woods. They are walking out."

The crowd roared. Someone fainted; someone sang. Marguerite stood pinned to the floor of the telegraph office with the roar passing over her like weather, unable to move, unable to breathe properly.

*Hospital staff.*

Saint-Aubin was twelve kilometers from the convent. She knew the geography the way Henri knew maps—it was how they'd passed the evenings, him drawing, her naming, piecing together the country between here and there. Twelve kilometers. Elodie had walked farther than that on injured men's rations. Elodie had done harder things in worse boots.

But Elodie had also stayed behind in a town that fell, in a chapel with a shattered tower, with three hundred and eleven names in her head and winter coming, and for forty-one days there had been no word, no list, no line in any ledger anywhere that said *alive*.

Marguerite pushed out of the office into the cold bright street and stood on the corner with her hand pressed flat over her sternum, and let herself, for the first time in six weeks, imagine it: the doorbell. The knock. A figure on Madame Foulon's step with snow in her hair and nothing but a coat, saying, in that hoarse ruined voice, *I told you I'd come. Did you write them all down?*

She walked home fast. She had a telegram to draft and a train to catch Thursday and a boy to prepare, because if Elodie Oyelaran was walking out of that ruined country along the main road from Saint-Aubin, then Marguerite intended to be standing at the end of that road.

And she was halfway up the bakery stairs, planning it, already packing in her head, when she saw the envelope slid under the apartment door.

Official paper. Military stamp. Addressed to *Mlle Marguerite Vasseur, c/o Foulon*, in handwriting she did not recognize, and her first thought—her absurd, unshakeable first thought—was: *the ledger. Someone knows about the ledger.*

She picked it up. Her hands were shaking now.

Henri was watching from the courtyard door, pigeons gathering at his feet, his face already closing, because children always knew when the mail was bad.

Marguerite opened it standing on the stairs.

It was not from the army. The stamp was military but the signature was not; the page was covered in the sharp, courteous, faintly formal hand she had last seen making polite conversation over the dispensary table while pricing absolution. She read it once. Then she read it again, and sat down slowly on the step, because the world had rearranged itself around a single paragraph and would not stop tilting.

Verrec was alive. Verrec had given up his berth and survived the fall of the town. Verrec was in a hospital in the capital, recovering, writing letters.

And Verrec had seen Elodie.
