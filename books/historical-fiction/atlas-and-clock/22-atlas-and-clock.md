## 22. Atlas and Clock

The train stopped being a train somewhere in the night and became instead a slow, shuddering procession of stops—siding after siding, soldiers waving us through or not, a conductor who had stopped announcing stations and simply opened the door and said *here* or *not here*. I slept in fragments against the window, my coat balled under my cheek, and each time I woke I did what I had done every morning for six weeks: I counted.

Not money. Not miles. Names.

One hundred and eleven by the time we crossed into the occupied-free corridor where the telegraph poles stood straight again. Two hundred and six by the first town with intact glass in its windows. All three hundred and eleven when the conductor touched my shoulder at dawn and said, "Madame. This is yours," and I looked out at a street with plane trees and a bakery already lit and understood that the war had ended somewhere behind me, on some platform I'd slept through, and I had missed it.

I had missed the bells. I had missed the strangers kissing in the streets. I had slept through the whole enormous relief of it, and when I stepped down onto the platform with my one coat and my cardboard case containing nothing but a hairbrush and Marguerite's letters, tied in string, I found I did not mind. Armistice was an event. What I carried was a practice. You cannot celebrate three hundred and eleven people; you can only keep them, the way you keep a fire banked overnight, feeding it small and steady until morning.

The address on the last letter was Rue des Tilleuls, number nine. The city had not been shelled. It had the shocking wholeness of places that had been spared—the shutters painted, the gutters running, children chalk-drawing on paving stones that no boot had ground to powder. I walked slowly. Not from weakness, though there was that too; the cough I'd carried out of the ruined chapel had only lately loosened its grip. I walked slowly because I wanted to arrive as myself and not as the gray, hollowed woman the mirror in the station lavatory had shown me. I washed my face. I pinned my hair. There are small ceremonies left to a person even when everything else has been surrendered, and I performed all of them on the platform like a woman dressing for church.

Rue des Tilleuls ran uphill. Number nine was narrow, brick, with a blue door and a window box where someone had planted bulbs in the autumn and forgotten them, and now, weeks late, the shoots were coming up crooked and determined through the frozen soil. I recognized the handwriting of that neglect immediately. Marguerite planted things and then forgot them and then was astonished when they lived. I had watched her do it with patients for two years. *But he's better,* she would say, wounded, about a man everyone else had given up on. *Look at him.*

I raised my hand to knock, and before my knuckles touched the wood, the door flew open.

He had grown. That was the first thing, absurdly—the thing I noticed, after everything, after the chapel and the ash and the long recitation of names through the nights when the town fell—was that Henri was taller, his wrists shooting out of his sleeves, his jaw losing its last softness toward the boy he would become. He was wearing a sweater too big for him, navy, sleeves rolled four times, and he looked up at me with an expression I had seen once before, on the convent steps, the day a silent woman pressed coins into my hand and told me to forget his face.

He did not ask if it was me. Six-year-olds demand proof; eleven-year-olds decide. He decided. He hit me at the waist hard enough to stagger me back a step on the icy flagstones, arms locked, face buried in my coat, and I held onto him the way you hold onto a railing in high wind, both hands, eyes shut, letting the whole swaying world go on without me for one moment.

"You're late," he said into the wool.

"Six weeks," I agreed. My voice came out wrecked. "I'm sorry. The trains—"

"I know. Marguerite reads me the dispatches. She does the voice." He pulled back and looked at me with a gravity that belonged to no child I had ever nursed, though this child had never really been one. "She does the voice for everything now. Even the bread."

Behind him, on the stairs, Marguerite stood with a dish towel over her shoulder and flour to her elbows, and she was crying the way she always cried—furiously, as if crying were a task she'd been assigned and resented. She didn't come down. She gripped the banister and said, "Well," and then, "Well," again, because Marguerite had never in her life managed a third word at the moments that mattered most, and I climbed past Henri and embraced her among the flour and the dish towel and the smell of something rising in the oven, and we stayed like that while the boy watched us with his father's level gray eyes and pretended, generously, to examine the door hinge.

* * *

The flat was two rooms and a kitchen and a view of rooftops, and it was the richest place I had ever seen. There were books on the windowsill held upright by a brick. There was a photograph, unframed, propped against the sugar tin: Marguerite and Henri on a station platform, squinting, her hand on his shoulder, taken the day they arrived by a stranger whose thumb shadowed the corner. On the wall above the stove, hung on a nail, was the clock.

I stopped when I saw it.

It was the convent clock—or rather, the convent clock's heart, the works themselves, brass and iron, salvaged. I knew before asking. I could see the scorch along the lower plate, the pitting where shrapnel had kissed it the morning the tower took the hit and the whole town heard time stop with a sound like a struck bell dying.

"Béatrice sent it," Marguerite said behind me. "Before—" She stopped. Started again. "There's a letter. I've been keeping it. I couldn't—I kept starting to read it aloud to him and then making up errands instead." She wiped her hands uselessly on the towel. "Elodie. Sit down."

"Sister Béatrice is alive." I said it as fact, because I had counted her among the living every single day since the truck left, and I did not permit myself to count otherwise. "You're going to tell me she's alive."

"She's alive." Marguerite sat across from me, knees nearly touching mine in the little kitchen. "She walked out, Elodie. Third day after the armistice, a Red Cross column found her and four others walking the east road. She's at a convalescent house outside Amiens. Her legs are bad. Her lungs are worse. She writes—" Marguerite laughed, wet and disbelieving, "she writes like she talked. Four pages about the other patients' faults and one line at the end saying she prays for us daily and considers our prayers answered, and would I please teach the boy proper penmanship because his letters are an offense."

Henri, from the doorway, said with dignity: "They are not."

"They are," Marguerite and I said together, and something in the room unlocked, some tension none of us had named, and Henri laughed—a real laugh, startling in its ordinariness—and went to rescue whatever was in the oven, which was, Marguerite admitted, probably beyond rescuing.

I read Béatrice's letter twice. In the margin of the fourth page, in her severe upright hand, she had written: *You told me once the ledger was a sin of pride. I have reconsidered. Some pride is load-bearing.* And beneath that: *Recite them for me when you can. My memory falters at the two hundred mark and I find I cannot bear to lose even one.*

I folded the letter and held it a moment against my sternum, the way you press a hand to a wound that has finally stopped bleeding.

* * *

That evening, after a supper of bread and soup and a cake that had risen on one side only, Marguerite cleared the table and set down paper, ink, and the good pen she'd been saving, and said, "Now."

"Now what?"

"You know what." She wouldn't look at me directly. She concentrated on uncapping the ink with great care. "Three hundred and eleven. You've carried them alone since the boiler. That's finished. We're going to write them down—all of them, tonight, however long it takes—and this time they go in a book that stays above ground, in a drawer, in a home, where the raids can't reach and the officers can't bargain for them and no one ever has to memorize anything again to keep them safe."

"Marguerite—"

"And then," she went on, relentless, "we write letters. One name, one letter, wherever we can find an address. Béatrice will know people. The Red Cross will know people. Some of them are dead—we know that, we'll write anyway, to mothers, to brothers, to anyone the name still belongs to. Henri's father first." She finally looked up. "We promised."

"We promised," I said.

Henri had gone very still by the stove. He came to the table and stood beside my chair, and he did not sit, and he did not speak, and I understood that this was his way of asking—that silence of his, which had once been wounds and was now simply his manner, the way he entered important rooms.

"All right," I said. "From the beginning."

I closed my eyes. The names rose the way they always rose—not as a list but as faces, beds, voices, the cough of one man and the joke another told badly every evening for a week, the deserter shot at dawn whose boots were too large, the woman from the village who'd been buried under her own name at last because I'd written it down. Three hundred and eleven. I began.

"Achard, Luc. Private, second class. Died of fever, March 1917. His mother's name was Séverine. He talked about her plum trees."

Marguerite wrote. Her pen scratched. Henri fetched more paper without being asked.

"Adler, Tomas. Not our army—he'd lost his papers, we never learned his side. Wounds to the leg. Sang hymns in a language nobody knew. He taught it to Marguerite."

"One verse," Marguerite said, writing. "I've forgotten the rest."

"Then we write that down too. What survives survives."

We worked past midnight. The candle burned down and Marguerite replaced it and we worked past that. Somewhere past the hundredth name Henri fell asleep sitting up, his head tipped against my shoulder, and neither of us moved to wake him, and I lowered my voice and Marguerite bent closer to hear, and the pen went on scratching—Aubry, Badeau, Balan, Bastide—each name landing on the page like a stone set back into a wall that had been knocked down.

At name one, the first name, the name that had started everything, I stopped.

I looked at Henri, asleep against me. I thought of a woman at a convent door pressing coins into my hand—coins that were still in my coat pocket; I had never spent them; I never would—and saying *forget his face*, and meaning, I understood now, not *erase him* but *spare him*, spare the boy the knowledge that would follow him like dogs. And I thought of the execution order Verrec had shown me, and Verrec himself stepping back from the truck at dawn, giving up his seat without ceremony, choosing to stay in the falling town with the rest of us rather than ride out on a lie. They had found his body, eventually, in the spring lists. I had added him to the count privately. Three hundred and twelve. He'd have hated that. He'd have insisted on alphabetical placement and correct spelling.

"Delaunay, Aurélien," I said aloud, and Henri stirred, and opened his eyes, and sat up straight, fully awake, as if the name had been a hand on his shoulder.

"That's Papa," he said.

"Yes," I said. "Delaunay, Aurélien. Executed as a deserter, and no such thing. A man who walked home to his son." I watched Marguerite write it, the ink shining wet. "Say the rest of it, Henri. Like we practiced."

He straightened. In the candlelight, with flour still faintly dusting his sleeve and the salvaged clock ticking on the wall behind him—Béatrice had gotten it running; of course she had; that woman could get anything running—he said, clear and unhurried, the way you say a thing you intend never to lose:

"Delaunay, Aurélien. Of Vireloup. Son of Marie Delaunay. Father of Henri. Remembered."

The pen scratched. The clock ticked. Outside, far off, a train whistle sounded, ordinary and safe, carrying somebody home.

* * *

We finished near three in the morning. Three hundred and eleven names filled fourteen pages in Marguerite's careful hand, with my corrections in the margins—dates, regiments, the small human details that were the true cargo: the plum trees, the hymn, the man who'd traded his cigarettes for thread to mend a comrade's coat. Marguerite stacked the pages, squared their edges, and slid them into a wooden box that had once held tea, and put the box in the drawer of the dresser, and put her hand flat on the drawer a moment, the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping child.

"There," she said. "Above ground."

"Above ground," I agreed.

Henri had gone back to sleep properly now, bundled on the daybed, one arm flung out. Marguerite banked the stove. At the window, pulling the curtain, she paused.

"Elodie. Come look."

Below, snow had begun—fine, dry, unhurried, the first snow of the peace, sifting down through the light of the streetlamp over the crooked green shoots pushing up through the window-box soil. Beyond the rooftops the sky was clearing toward a cold bright morning. Somewhere south and west, in a convalescent house outside Amiens, a nun with bad legs was asleep, and tomorrow I would write to her and begin the recitation she'd asked for, two hundred names and then the last hundred and eleven, so that nothing faltered, so that nothing was lost.

"It's coming down like ash," Marguerite said softly. "But it isn't."

"No," I said. "It isn't."

I stood a long time at the glass, my breath fogging it, my coat still on, my case still unpacked by the door. People spoke of homecoming as arrival at a place. But I had left my place; it was rubble and registry errors and a cold grate full of burnt pages. What I had come home to was a narrow brick house with a blue door, a drawer full of names, a boy who had decided I was his, a woman who planted things and refused to give up on them, and a clock on the wall that had survived the fall of a tower and was ticking anyway—ticking, ticking, marking out the hours of the long work ahead: the letters, the searches, the mothers to be found, the graves to be renamed.

Three hundred and eleven people, and room in the box for more. Because the war was over, but forgetting was not; forgetting was a trade that never closed, and someone would always need to stand at its door taking names.

I unpinned my hair. I took off my coat at last, and hung it on the hook by the door, and turned toward the daybed and the candle and the scratch of Marguerite's pen as she began, unprompted, a fresh page headed in her round determined hand: *Letters to Write — No. 1.*

Outside, the snow went on falling, soft as memory, covering the whole quiet city—and inside, the clock ticked on, keeping faith with the morning.
