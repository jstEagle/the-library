## 1. The Weight of Sealed Things

The fog came off the bay before dawn, the way it always did in November, rolling up the harbor streets like something the water had grown tired of holding. Bram Voss moved through it the way he moved through everything: quietly, quickly, and belonging to no one who could see him.

He carried three things, and he had carried them for nine years, since the morning the Meridian Trading Company's agent had taken his mother's mark on an indenture contract and handed him instead to a foreman with a voice like wet gravel. The first was a knife, short-bladed, its handle wrapped in tarred cord, good for cutting rope or seal or—though he had never needed to—anything else. The second was a pearl, small and grey as a raindrop, that had lived against his sternum on a cord of braided hair since before he could remember. His mother had brought it up from the cold dark herself, one of the last she ever found before the company's dredgers stripped the beds and priced her crew out of the water. The third thing was not a thing at all. It was a habit: he counted. Cranes, crates, steps, breaths, coins changing hands in doorways. He kept ledgers in his head the way other men kept grudges, and if you had asked him why, he would have said only that a man who owns nothing learns to hold the world in numbers, because numbers cannot be repossessed.

Nine years old when they took him. Twenty-three now, though the company ledger recorded him as inventory rather than years: *Voss, Bram — courier, first class — value: reliable.* He had read his own entry once, by accident, in the counting-house when a clerk left a book open. Reliable. It was the closest thing to a name the company had ever given him besides the one his mother chose.

The fog thickened near the fish market, where the gulls were already quarreling over the night's offal. He took the harbor stairs two at a time, past the chandlery with its lamps still burning yellow behind salt-frosted glass, past the constable on the corner who nodded to him—not because he knew Bram's name but because he knew the company's satchel, oiled leather, brass-buckled, stamped with the meridian-and-arrow. Everyone in this city knew that stamp. It was on the cranes and the warehouses, on the ferry manifests and the poor-box, on the deed to the constable's own house if he had one. The company owned everything here except the weather, and there were men in the counting-house drawing papers that would give them the weather too, if anyone could figure out how to invoice a cloud.

Bram was trusted for the simplest of reasons: he was owned. A free courier might be bribed, might run, might grow ambitious. An indentured man could be traced, whipped, sold, or voided with a stroke of pen—and so no one bothered to buy him, because the company already held the deed. He was invisible precisely because he was valuable, and valuable precisely because he was invisible. He had understood this arithmetic since he was small enough to be measured against the counting-house doorframe, and he had made his peace with it the way divers make peace with pressure: not by forgetting it is there, but by learning what it costs to live inside it.

His route that morning ran from the counting-house annex to the naval office on the east quay, four sealed orders in the satchel, each wrapped in oilcloth and pressed with wax. He did not read them. That was the first law of his trade, older than any written into his contract: *the courier carries; he does not know.* Knowledge was the one cargo that depreciated the moment you picked it up.

But he counted anyway, because the habit was his own, and by the time he reached the naval office steps he had already noticed that the morning was wrong in small ways.

* * *

It began with the summons. Orders for Bram usually came down through Foreman Gellert, a slab-faced man who smelled of pipe ash and delivered assignments with all the ceremony of handing over a mop. This time a boy in company livery had been waiting at the foot of the annex stairs before the fog lifted, holding a card with Bram's number—the company used numbers for anything it valued—and instructions to bring him directly to Director Corvane's outer office.

Corvane himself received him. That was wrong, too. Bram had seen the director perhaps six times in nine years, always across the width of a hall, always surrounded by the soft rustle of men whose collars had never known rope-burn. Now Corvane stood by the window with his back half-turned, a compact, silvering man with the easy warmth of someone who has never once needed to raise his voice to be obeyed.

"Voss." He said the name as if he were pleased by it. "You'll take these yourself. No intermediate hands."

Four packets lay on the desk, square and heavy, sealed in the company's dark green wax. Corvane watched him lift them, watched the way Bram checked the weight of each without appearing to—a courier's habit, weighing the contents against the promise of the seal—and smiled as if Bram were a dog that had performed some small clever trick.

"You've never lost a delivery," Corvane said. "Nine years. Do you know how rare that is? I tell the board about you. I say, *there is a young man who understands what it means to be given trust.*" He let the word sit in the room. Trust. As if it were a gift rather than a chain with a comfortable lining.

"Thank you, sir," Bram said, which was the correct answer, and the cheap one.

"The armistice delegation arrives within the fortnight. Everything moves before then. Everything." Corvane's eyes were pale and pleasant. "You understand discretion, Voss. You were raised to it."

*I was raised by pearl divers,* Bram thought, and did not say. *I was raised on a jetty by people who held their breath for two minutes at a time to bring up shells worth less than one of your inkstands.* But his face did what it had been trained to do, which was nothing, and he bowed his head over the packets, and carried them out.

* * *

The wrongness announced itself on the third delivery, to the harbormaster's annex on the west quay, and it announced itself quietly, the way dangerous things do.

Bram had handled company seals for nine years. He had carried orders through strikes and storms and one small riot, and part of his head-ledger was given over entirely to wax: the color of it, the depth of the impression, the way the meridian line always crossed the arrow at its exact center because the company's seal-press was machined and never varied. The green wax, dark as bottle glass. The arrow struck true. Every time, every year, like a heartbeat.

This seal was green, and it bore the arrow, and the meridian crossed it true—but the wax itself was wrong. Slightly paler. Slightly brittle at the edge, where a thumb could worry a flake loose, and when Bram turned the packet in the grey light outside the harbormaster's window, he saw that the impression sat a hair too shallow, as if pressed by a hand that had learned the seal rather than owned it.

A copy. A very good copy. Good enough for a harbormaster, good enough for anyone who had never spent nine years carrying the genuine article against his ribs like a second heart.

He delivered it. Of course he delivered it. His hands belonged to the company and they did what they were trained to do, passing the packet across the counter to a clerk who signed the manifest without looking up, and Bram walked out onto the quay with the fog swallowing the cranes behind him and his mind running the count backward at speed.

The fourth packet, bound for the customs shed, bore the same shallow press, the same brittle edge. Two of four. Someone upstairs—or someone with access to upstairs—was routing forged orders under the company's own seal, and using Bram Voss, the most trusted carrier in the port, to deliver them.

* * *

He stopped at midday on the diver's jetty, the old one south of the breakwater, where the blacklisted crews mended nets they weren't supposed to own and the company constables didn't bother to climb down. The tide was out and the pilings wore their skirts of dried weed, and the fog had thinned to a low white ceiling that made the whole harbor feel like a room with the roof pressed down close.

Bram sat on the bollard at the end, where he had sat as a boy watching his mother surface with her catch-bag and her hair slicked black as kelp, and he ate the bread in his pocket and thought.

The arithmetic would not resolve. Forged seals meant forgery, and forgery at that level meant either theft from within or treachery from above, and both possibilities ended in the same place: a courier who had carried them, whose mark was on the manifests, whose face was known at every door from the naval office to the customs shed. If it came to light, the company would not ask who had made the wax. It would ask who had carried it. And there would be a ledger entry somewhere—*Voss, Bram, value: reliable*—that could be closed with a stroke of the pen.

Yet beneath the fear, quieter and more troubling, was something else. A hooked feeling. Because whoever had pressed those seals had done it brilliantly, and brilliance like that required study, access, and nerve—and because in nine years of carrying the company's words, Bram had never once been allowed to know a single one, and some starved part of him wanted, suddenly and shamefully, to know.

He touched the pearl through his shirt. Grey and small and his, the only thing the contracts had never listed.

Behind him, boots on the jetty planks. Not a diver's bare-footed roll; hard heels, deliberate. He turned.

A woman stood at the landward end of the jetty, coat dark with sea-damp, her left sleeve pinned and folded clean at the shoulder. She looked at him the way he imagined surveyors looked at coastlines—measuring, patient, certain the ground would hold still for her. In her right hand she held a folded paper, and she did not come closer, and she did not go away.

"Courier," she said, in an accent that had crossed at least one border to reach this fog. "I need to ask you about a dead man's handwriting."
