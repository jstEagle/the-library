## 2. A Stranger with Dead Man's Ink

The diver's jetty smelled of kelp and tar, of the low tide's sour breath rising from between the pilings where the harbor kept its secrets. Bram Voss came down to it the way he came down to everything — at a courier's pace, neither hurrying nor lingering, his sealed satchel riding against his ribs like a second heart.

He had no business on the jetty that morning. His route ran the counting-house, the customs shed, the harbor master's office, each door a station in the litany he'd recited since he was nine years old: *deliver, do not read; wait, do not ask; be seen, be small.* But Director Corvane had given him a half-hour's idle time between deliveries, and half-hours were dangerous things for a man who had never been taught what to do with freedom, however small its measure. So Bram walked to the water because his mother had walked to the water, because the divers' quarter was stitched into him like a seam, and he stood at the rail where the jetty planks went silver with salt, and he watched the fog peel back from the bay in slow rags.

She was already there when he turned.

A woman sat on an overturned dory near the rail's end, wrapped in a fisherman's oilskin gone stiff with age, her single boot planted on the shingle. Where her left arm should have been there was only a pinned sleeve, folded neat as a ledger page. Her right hand held a letter. She did not look at him the way strangers looked at couriers — appraising the satchel, calculating the theft — but the way his mother's crew had once looked at the surface from below: measuring how far away it was, and whether it could be reached.

"Voss," she said. Not a question.

Bram's hand found the satchel strap. "You have the advantage."

"Ilsa Rehn." She stood, unhurried, joints cracking like ship's rope. "I made charts, once. Before the company decided my arm was cheaper than my wages." She lifted the letter with her one hand, holding it out across the space between them the way you'd hold out bread to something wild. "I need you to read this."

"I don't read other people's letters." The refusal came out smooth, worn smooth by nine years of use. "I carry them."

"This one isn't for carrying. It's for remembering." She didn't lower it. "Look at the hand."

He should have walked. He told himself, even then, that he meant to walk — that his boots were already turning on the wet planks, that the counting-house bell would ring soon and Bram Voss was never late, because a late courier is a questioned courier and a questioned courier is a beaten one. But the fog had thinned enough to show the breakwater, and the light off the water was that particular gray-gold that makes everything look like memory, and the woman waited with a patience that felt less like begging than like arithmetic. She had calculated him. She had gotten the sum right.

He looked at the letter.

The paper was cheap, diver's paper, the kind sold at the chandlery by the bundle for men who couldn't afford ink twice. But the hand on it — the hand was not cheap. It slanted forward with an economy that wasted nothing, the crossbars level as soundings taken with care, the descenders clipped short as if the writer grudged every inch the pen traveled. Bram knew the hand the way he knew the company seal, the way he knew the creak of the third stair in the counting-house: by repetition, by rote, by years of carrying papers written in it without ever being permitted to understand them.

It was Aldous Wren's hand.

"You can't," Ilsa Rehn said quietly, watching his face. "You're about to tell me you can't know whose writing that is. You're indentured. Reading correspondence above your station is theft of company property. They'll have said it so many times you dream it." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. "But you do know. Don't you."

"Aldous Wren is dead," Bram said. His voice stayed level. He was proud of that, later; he was ashamed of it too. "I attended his funeral. There was a coffin. The company paid for flowers."

"The company pays for everything. That's how they own it all."

"The company paid for *chrysanthemums*." The detail escaped him before he could stop it, absurd and specific, and he saw the woman file it away behind her eyes like a clerk entering a figure. Six months ago, rain, the little cemetery on the ridge where the company buried employees who died solvent. Wren's coffin had been carried by four porters who'd never spoken to him. Bram had stood at the back because Wren was the only man in the counting-house who had ever corrected his ledgers gently instead of loudly. The chrysanthemums had been white, going brown at the edges, and someone had wept — not the family, because Wren had none, but one of the filing girls, muffled in wool.

"He drowned," Bram said. "They pulled him out of the harbor by the custom sheds. Everyone knows."

"Everyone knows what they were told." Ilsa folded the letter closed, and the motion had a finality to it, a door shutting. "I'm asking you to commit a small crime, courier. Compare that hand against the archives. One afternoon. You've seen a thousand pages of Wren's writing pass through your satchel — you don't even need the archive, do you? You could compare it against your own memory."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you walk up those stairs and go back to carrying orders you aren't allowed to read, and I'll trouble you no further." She looked past him at the water. "But you haven't refused yet. You're still standing here. Ask yourself why."

The bell rang across the harbor, bronze through fog, and Bram went up the stairs and made his deliveries and did not think about the letter, which is to say he thought of nothing else, and performed so flawlessly around the emptiness that the customs clerk complimented his crispness and Corvane's undersecretary gave him a nod worth more than most men's wages.

* * *

That evening the summons came down the way summons always came, through the pneumatic tube in the wall of the couriers' dormitory, spitting a brass capsule into the tin cup with a sound like a coin dropped in a church box. Bram cracked it with his thumbnail. A slip inside, a time, an address: the harbor workshop, ninth hour, sealed orders to be delivered to the workshop foreman, no receipt required.

No receipt required. He read that twice. In nine years, every delivery had required a receipt, because the Meridian Trading Company trusted nothing, least of all its own paper. A delivery without documentation was a delivery that officially had never happened.

The order itself lay in the capsule beneath the slip, folded twice, sealed with wax. Bram held it to the dormitory's single lamp, and his trained eye did the work before his conscience could object — reading a seal's integrity was not reading a letter; even the instructors had allowed that much. Press-marks, depth of impression, the knife-cut angle of the fold.

The seal was wrong.

Not broken — wrong. The company's seal bore the meridian line through a stylized globe, and the press always bit deep at the globe's north because the die was old and worn unevenly; Bram had noticed the flaw at eleven years old and had never once mentioned it, because noticing things aloud was another species of theft. This seal sat shallow and even, perfect, the meridian crisp as a cut. A forgery, and a good one, pressed from a fresh die by someone with steady hands.

And over the flap, in ink gone brownish where it had dried, a direction note in a slanting, economical hand — the crossbars level as soundings, the descenders clipped short.

Bram set the order down on his knee. The dormitory was quiet except for another courier's snoring and the foghorn out on the point, moaning its one long vowel into the dark. He picked the order up again. He put it down. His hands were steady — they were always steady, that was what made him valuable — but underneath the steadiness something shifted, some great weight resettling in the hold, and the whole deck of his life tilted two degrees, which is nothing, which is everything, which is the angle at which ships begin to take on water.

Dead men did not write delivery notes. Dead men did not forge their employer's seal. Unless the drowning had been arranged like the funeral, like the chrysanthemums, like everything else the company touched — purchased wholesale, executed cheaply, documented in triplicate.

He thought of Wren correcting his figures with a soft *there, lad, the error's always in what you weren't meant to count*, and he thought of Ilsa Rehn's empty sleeve pinned neat as a ledger page, and he thought of the white chrysanthemums going brown in the rain while four strangers lowered an empty box into the ground.

He did not sleep. At the ninth hour he delivered the forged order to the workshop foreman, and watched the man's face go gray as he read it, and heard him say, too casually, *tell Director Corvane the furnace crews start Thursday*, and walked back out into the fog with the lie of the morning sitting in his chest like swallowed ballast.

At the jetty, she was waiting. Of course she was waiting. The tide was low and the divers' quarter stirred behind her, nets hung out, children running the shingle, a whole quarter of people the company had used up and crossed off its books.

"Well?" Ilsa Rehn asked.

Bram stood at the foot of the stairs, a courier still, owned until the company said otherwise, and heard himself say the first sentence of his life that no one had ordered him to say:

"Wren wrote an order yesterday. It's going to burn a building."
