## 3. What the Company Owns

Ilsa Rehn had learned to read coastlines the way priests read scripture: patiently, at dawn, with salt drying white on her sleeves. For eleven years she had drawn the Meridian Trading Company's maps — every shoal, every anchorage, every unnamed cove where a hull could lie quiet against the tide. Her charts went out under the company seal to captains who never asked where the numbers came from, and she had been proud of that once, proud of the clean certainty of ink on vellum, of knowing that a coastline she had measured would carry ships safely home.

She had one arm to hold a pen and had thought, at nineteen, that this was the trade's great mercy. Cartography did not require hauling line or climbing ratlines. It required only eyes, patience, and a hand steady enough to make the world legible. The company had hired her because she was cheap and precise, and it had kept her because her charts saved them insurance money, and for eleven years she had not once asked what the ships carried.

That was the company's genius, she understood now. Not cruelty — cruelty was loud and bred resistance. The company's genius was arrangement. It arranged matters so that no single person ever held enough pieces to see the whole.

Aldous Wren had held the whole.

She had met him in the map room in the autumn of 1916, when he came down from the counting-house to reconcile shipping manifests against her coastal surveys. He was a narrow man with ink-stained cuffs and a habit of humming hymns off-key while he worked, and he treated clerks and cartographers alike as if they were interesting rather than incidental. She had liked him before she trusted him, which was how he preferred it; Wren built trust the way water builds an arch, slowly, invisibly, from pressure applied over years.

It took him fourteen months to show her.

She remembered the evening precisely: rain hammering the skylight, the lamps turned low because coal was rationed now even for the company, Wren laying three documents side by side on her drafting table with the reverence of a man arranging flowers on a grave. A charter manifest. A naval dispatch. A payment record routed through a shell house in a neutral port whose name she had drawn onto charts a hundred times without once thinking about what moved through its harbor.

*The same ship,* Wren said quietly. *Same hull, same voyage, three names in three ledgers. Grain to the north. Munitions schedules to the south. And to both of them, at a markup you would not believe, the answer to a question neither side knew the other had already purchased.*

Armistice intelligence. Terms being floated in back channels, positions weakening, troop movements that existed only as rumors — sold twice, to two enemies, by the men who signed the harbor's leases and paid the constables' wages and owned, as far as Ilsa could determine, everything the fog touched.

*Why show me?* she had asked.

Wren had hummed two bars of some funeral hymn, off-key, and said: *Because you draw coastlines, Ilsa, and I keep accounts, and neither of us has ever once been invited into the room where decisions are made. People like us are trusted precisely because we are not consulted. That is our advantage, and I intend to spend it.*

He spent it. For eighteen months he copied, collated, and hid — building a second set of books in a workshop by the tideline, assembling the case that would hang every director of the Meridian Trading Company. And then one gray morning in spring, Aldous Wren fell off the Customs Stairs into the bay, and the company buried him with a wreath and a eulogy about faithful service, and Ilsa stood at the back of the funeral crowd with her remaining arm pressed hard against her ribs and knew, the way she knew a charted reef lay under calm water, that the drowning had been arranged too.

They took her arm years before that — a cable parted on a survey launch in the northern shallows, and the company's surgeon worked fast and the company's lawyers settled cheap, and afterward the directors' own insurer deducted the cost of the rope from her compensation because *the rope was company property.* She had laughed when they told her. It was that or go mad. The company owned the rope, therefore the rope's failure was somehow hers, therefore the arithmetic of the wound balanced perfectly, and she signed the papers because an indentured woman with one arm and no references had nowhere else to be.

That was the word she kept returning to, in the months after the funeral, walking the harbor with Wren's last letter sewn into her coat lining. *Owned.* Not employed. Not bound, exactly — though the indenture papers hanging over half the city's doorways were ownership in slow motion — but owned in the deeper sense, owned the way weather owns a coastline, shaping it grain by grain until the rock believes the shape of its erosion is the shape of itself.

She saw it everywhere once she looked. The stevedores who borrowed against next quarter's wages at interest the company set. The divers who'd pulled pearls out of the cold water for thirty years and ended their careers owing more than they'd earned, blacklisted the moment their lungs gave out, replaced by boys who didn't yet know what the water took. The constables whose salaries came through a charity the company funded, the magistrates whose chambers the company built, the churchwardens whose roofs the company repaired. There was no outside. There was only the interior of the machine, and the machine's kindnesses were load-bearing, and every debt was a rivet.

Which was why she needed Bram Voss.

* * *

She watched him for three days before approaching him again, because the first approach had failed and she could not afford a second failure.

He ran sealed orders through the fog like a fish through kelp — she'd watched him thread the counting-house courtyard at shift change, when the yard was most crowded, carrying his little oilskin packet against his chest the way other men carried Bibles. Nobody stopped him. Nobody searched him. The gate guards nodded him through with the particular blankness of men who have been told this one is not to be touched, and she understood the mechanism immediately and it chilled her: they let him pass because he was indentured, because an owned thing could be reclaimed, because whatever he carried, the company held the collateral of his whole life behind it.

Trust through expendability. Wren would have admired the elegance of it.

On the third day she followed him along the breakwater road in a thinning fog and watched him stop at the rail where the diver's jetty jutted into gray water, and stand there, not doing anything, just standing, looking down at the pilings where the weed grew thick and black. Grief, she thought. Or memory. His mother had been a diver; Wren had told her that much, told her the boy courier was the one person inside the walls who might be turned, because his loyalties predated his leash.

She came up beside him and did not look at him, the way you approach a gull you don't want to startle.

"You came to the jetty," she said. "After I found you here before. That's twice."

"I work near water. Everyone works near water here."

"Everyone works near water," she agreed. "But you look at it like you're deciding something."

He said nothing. In the fog behind them a crane groaned, swinging its load blind. She watched his hands — a courier's hands, still, economical, one thumb resting inside his belt where a knife would sit if the company allowed him a knife. It probably didn't allow it. He probably carried one anyway.

"I'm going to tell you something," she said, "and then you can walk away, and I won't follow you again. That's the bargain." She turned to face the water, matching his stance, so that for a moment they were two figures cut from the same silhouette. "I mapped your harbor for eleven years. Every channel, every mudflat, every place a body could be sunk and never found. And I can tell you the company doesn't hide its crimes underwater, because it doesn't need to. It files them. It stamps them. It enters them into ledgers in a fair hand and locks them in rooms that are legal to lock, and by the time anyone thinks to look, the crime has become a record and the record has become precedent." She paused. "Aldous Wren kept a second set of books. He's alive. And the company is going to burn every page he saved unless someone carries them out."

"The clerk drowned," Bram said. His voice was flat, careful. "I walked behind his coffin."

"Coffins are cheap. So are funerals. The company bought both." She finally looked at him directly. Up close he was younger than his stillness suggested, twenty perhaps, with the pale indoor skin of a counting-house man and the diver's blood showing somewhere underneath, in the way he stood with his weight always slightly forward, always ready for the deck to move. "You verified the letter I showed you. Don't bother denying it — I saw your face when you recognized the hand. You know dead men don't write letters. Which means you've known for three days that the ground under you is wrong, and instead of reporting me, you came here. To the jetty. Where your mother's people used to land their catch." She let that sit. "You're already halfway out, Voss. You just haven't admitted which half."

The crane swung its load overhead, and something in the boy's face tightened — not fear, she thought. Calculation. He was measuring her the way she had measured coastlines, patient and thorough, and she realized with a small cold shock that she recognized the gesture. It was her own gesture. It was the gesture of someone who had learned early that the world would take things from her unless she knew exactly where everything stood.

"What do you want from me?" he asked.

"Access. Nothing more. You walk through doors I can't. I need to know the workshop's rhythms — when the watch turns, when the tidewater floods the cellar, when Corvane's clerks go home." She kept her voice level, unasking, a gift offered rather than a debt requested. "I'm not asking you to steal anything. Not yet. I'm asking you to notice things you already notice, and tell me."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I'll find another way, and you'll spend the rest of your indenture knowing what you know, delivering sealed orders you're not allowed to read, for men who sold both sides of a war." She shrugged, and the empty sleeve of her coat shifted in the wind. "I've lived with worse knowledge. So have you. The question isn't whether you can carry it. The question is whether you'll carry it alone."

For a long moment he looked past her, out at the water, at the pilings, at whatever the bay held for him — a mother, a crew, a childhood spent holding breath. The fog was lifting off the harbor in ragged sheets, and the far cranes emerged one by one like the masts of a drowned fleet, and Ilsa waited with the patience of a woman who had spent eleven years waiting on weather.

"You said Wren's alive," Bram said at last. "Where?"

And there it was — not yes, not yet, but the question of a man who had begun, however reluctantly, to plan around the possibility of hope. She felt the old wariness loosen one notch, and mistrusted the feeling immediately, because the company taught its creatures to mistake usefulness for affection, and she could not yet tell whether what she felt toward this boy was trust or simply the recognition of another instrument shaped by the same hand.

"Beneath the breakwater," she said. "With friends. Come with me tonight, if you're coming at all, and bring nothing you can't drop in the water."

She turned and started down the jetty steps, and behind her, after exactly long enough to prove it was his own decision, she heard his boots follow.

* * *

The tide was going out and would keep going out for six hours, and by the time they reached the seaward arches where the breakwater's foundations stepped down into the bay, the divers had already lit their lamp behind the rusted sluice grate, and the light came through the ironwork in broken bars, striping the wet stone gold and shadow.

Bram stopped at the mouth of the arch. She watched him take in the scene the way he must have taken in every room of the counting-house: the old woman mending net with fingers gone stiff, the young one boiling tea over a spirit flame, the middle-aged man with burst lungs who breathed in whistles, all of them settled into the damp dark with the ease of people who had made a home of a place designed to exclude them. Owned people, all of them. Blacklisted, indebted, erased from the company's rolls but not from its harbor.

And in the deepest corner, half in shadow, a narrow man with a clerk's stoop sat wrapped in a diver's coat, writing — writing, even now, even here — in a hand Bram Voss would know anywhere.

Ilsa heard the boy's breath catch, once, and then steady itself.

Aldous Wren looked up from his pages, hummed two bars of some off-key hymn, and said, "Ah. Good. I need someone inside the counting-house — and it seems the harbor has finally answered."

Behind Bram, out on the gray water, the fog was closing again, and somewhere beyond it the armistice clock was running.
