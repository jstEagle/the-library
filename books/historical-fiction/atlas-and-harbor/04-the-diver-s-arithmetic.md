## 4. The Diver's Arithmetic

The diver's jetty at low tide smelled of tar and old salt, of ropes gone soft with rot, and beneath all of it — faint as a coin dropped in deep water — of the kelp beds where his mother had once worked her lungs to ribbons. Bram came down the ladder slowly, the way you come down into a memory, one hand on the wet rung and one hand empty, because a courier learned early never to carry company property into places the company did not own.

Which was, he understood now, most of the world. Or what was left of it.

The blacklist had done its work quietly, as the company did everything. No notice posted, no names read aloud in the counting-house. Simply: no contracts offered, no gear issued, no berth on any boat that flew the Meridian colors, which was every boat that mattered. The diving crew his mother had belonged to for nineteen years — the crew that had taught him to hold his breath until his vision flowered with light, that had pulled him from the water more times than he could count — had been erased from the ledgers like a line ruled through in red ink.

They were still there, though. That was the thing about people the company decided not to own anymore. They didn't vanish. They just stopped appearing in the arithmetic.

He found Sefa mending net in the lee of the breakwater, her hands moving with the blind fluency of forty years at the work, and beside her old Pietro, who had been blacklisted longer than anyone and wore it like a rank. They looked up when his boots hit the stones, and neither of them smiled, and neither of them told him to go away.

"You shouldn't come here," Sefa said, in the tone of a woman who had already decided she was glad he had.

"I know."

"Your handler knows you're here?"

"He doesn't watch me at low tide." It wasn't quite a lie. Corvane watched everything, but gently, the way a man pets a dog he intends one day to breed and sell.

Pietro laughed, a sound like gravel poured into a bucket. "Low tide," he said. "That's when they own the least of us. There's a lesson in it."

Sefa put aside the net. Her hands, he saw, were wrapped where the cold water had split the skin — no company gloves now, no salve from the company stores. "You've come about the clerk," she said.

Bram felt the ground shift under him, that same tilting sensation Ilsa Rehn's letter had given him two nights before. "I haven't said—"

"You came down the ladder slow," she said. "You looked at the kelp line first, then the breakwater, then me. You only look at the breakwater like that when you're wondering if it can hide something. Your mother did the same. Sit down, boy. We'll tell you the arithmetic."

* * *

They told it plainly, the way divers tell everything — stripped of ornament, weighted for truth, each fact handed over like something hauled up from depth that might change shape in the air.

Six months ago, on the night Aldous Wren went into the harbor, three men had been working the ebb shallows off Coal Quay, taking whatever the company's boats no longer bothered to claim — stray oysters, dropped tools, the occasional drowned crate. Blacklisted men, all of them, working black water without lamps because lamps cost oil and oil cost money and they had none.

They had seen the boat first. A company launch, running dark, and a figure at the rail who did not fall so much as arrive at the edge and then go over, arms wide, the particular sprawl of a body that has been helped. Then the launch had circled once, slow, the way a man circles to check his work, and gone back toward the counting-house lights.

The divers had gone in after the splash. Not out of heroism, Pietro said. Out of habit. Out of the old, stupid, unpayable instinct that says: someone is under the water, so we go down.

"Half-drowned already," Pietro said. "Lungs full, head cracked open on the pilings, cold as a stone. But breathing. Little bubbles. You learn to spot little bubbles."

They had carried him through the eelgrass to a place the company's charts marked as nothing — a collapsed drainage culvert in the seaward face of the breakwater, dry at half tide, big enough to stand in if you didn't mind stooping. Divers had used it for sixty years. Company surveyors had mapped the harbor four times and never found it, because the company only ever measured what it could tax.

"And he's there still?" Bram asked.

Sefa looked at him a long moment. "You tell me, courier. You carried orders last week with a wrong seal on them. We heard about that too. The harbor talks to us even if the company won't." She picked up her needle again, drove it through the mesh. "Yes. He's there still. Sick, half-crippled from the cold, writing pages upon pages in a hand that shakes worse every week. He says the shaking doesn't matter. He says ink only has to be legible once."

* * *

Bram went out along the breakwater at slack water, when the tide stood between claims and nobody watched the harbor, and found the culvert by feel rather than sight — a lip of stone, a smell of dryness inside wetness, a darkness that opened like a mouth.

Aldous Wren sat against the inner wall with a lantern turned so low it was barely an argument for light, and he was thinner than the man whose funeral Bram had attended, but alive, unmistakably alive, his eyes catching the flame the way a wick does.

"Voss," he said. His voice was ruined, sanded down to gravel. "Sefa said you'd come before the week was out. She said your mother's son would do the arithmetic faster than anyone."

"What arithmetic?"

Wren lifted the ledger page he'd been resting on his knee. "That I owe my life to eleven people who have nothing, and that they keep me anyway. That every day they bring me food they cannot afford and lamp-oil they should be selling, and that no contract obliges them, and no debt binds them, and no ledger anywhere records what they've done." He set the page down carefully, the way you set down something holy or explosive. "The company taught you to measure everything, boy. So measure this. What is the price of a kindness with no entry in the books?"

Bram had no answer. He crouched in the culvert's dark with the sea breathing somewhere behind the stone, and he thought of the counting-house, where every favor was entered and every kindness accrued interest and his whole life had been a column of debits in somebody else's book. He thought of Corvane's warm hand on his shoulder. He thought of his mother, who had died owing the company eleven marks, and how the company had deducted them from her burial.

"Come out of here," Bram said finally. "Both of you. I can move you. I know the routes."

Wren smiled, and it hurt to see, because behind the ruin of his face there was still the quick, dry intelligence of a man who had spent twenty years finding the truths hidden in other men's columns. "Not yet. If I vanish inland, I'm a rumor. If I stay here, I'm evidence — close enough to touch the workshop, close enough to finish. There are records in that building, Voss, that will hang directors. Copies of copies, and originals they think they burned years ago. I need them copied before the armistice delegation arrives, because after the signing there will be a fire, and everyone at the counting-house already knows its date." He tapped the ledger page. "And I need someone who can walk through those doors carrying paper without anyone looking twice. Someone owned. Someone trusted precisely because he is expendable."

The fog was coming in off the water, filling the harbor the way it always did, erasing the cranes and the masts until the city was only a suggestion of itself. Bram sat down on the cold stone across from the dead man who wasn't dead, and understood that he was already past the place where refusing would mean anything.

"Tell me what to copy," he said.

* * *

He stayed until the tide turned. It was the longest he had ever spent anywhere without being paid, summoned, or ordered, and the strangeness of it settled over him slowly, like silt.

Because it wasn't only Wren. Through the evening the others came — Sefa with bread and smoked fish; Pietro's daughter Nell, fourteen and sharp-eyed, who took dictation in a hand neater than any clerk's; two brothers named Hark and Tam who stood watch on the breakwater crown and traded places every hour without being asked; old Marit, who had been blacklisted so long she'd forgotten her contract number, and who boiled seawater for salt because salt was company-stocked and she'd sooner chew stone. Eleven souls, Wren had said. Bram counted them as they came and went, out of habit, out of the ledger habit that lived in his hands, and then caught himself doing it and felt something turn over in his chest.

Nobody recorded their hours. Nobody weighed their contributions or docked their rations or praised them in a voice like being petted. When Nell finished a page, Sefa squeezed her shoulder, and that was the whole wage. When Hark's watch ran long because Tam was sleeping, nobody entered it in a book to be repaid later with interest. They simply covered for each other, the way water fills a shape, because that was what they had decided to be to one another — decided, Bram kept returning to the word, the way you probe a sore tooth. Decided. Chosen. Nothing signed.

His mother had belonged to these people once. He remembered now — remembering the way you remember in a place that gives the memory back — evenings like this one on the jetty, fish shared without counting shares, his small self passed from lap to lap while the crew argued tides and prices. He had thought, at nine, when the company took him, that he was leaving chaos for order. Now, sitting in the dark with the sea talking behind the wall, he wondered if he had simply left a family for a firm, and called the trade freedom because the papers said so.

"You're doing sums," Wren said, without looking up from his writing. "I can hear it. You have a clerk's face, Voss."
