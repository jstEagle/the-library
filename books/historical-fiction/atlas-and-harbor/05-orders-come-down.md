## 5. Orders Come Down

The summons came the way weather comes to this city — without announcement, without mercy, arriving first as a pressure behind the ears.

Bram was in the counting-house annex, returning a satchel of sealed correspondence from the customs pier, when the junior clerk touched his elbow. No name spoken. Just a folded slip of grey paper, and the boy already walking away before Bram's fingers closed on it. *Third floor. Nine o'clock. C.* That was all. It was enough. Everyone in the building knew what *C.* meant, though no one said it above a whisper: Corvane.

He climbed the stairs with his ledger habit ticking underneath his ribs like a second pulse. He had counted, over six years of running sealed things through fog and rain, exactly how many times he had been called to the third floor: eleven. Eleven summonses, eleven commissions that mattered, and after each one something in the harbor had changed — a ship rerouted, a foreman gone, a diver's license revoked. Bram had never known the contents of the orders he carried. He had only ever known their wake.

Tonight would make twelve. He felt it in the way the gas lamps on the landing burned steady instead of guttering; the company had adjusted them, he realized, adjusted the whole building for him, the way one adjusts a room for a guest one intends to keep.

Director Corvane received him standing at the window, hands clasped behind his back, watching the fog come in off the water in long grey shelves. He was a broad, comfortable man, silver at the temples, with the easy warmth of someone who had never once needed to raise his voice because he owned everyone who might hear it. On the table beside him sat a leather folio, and on the folio lay Bram's own knife — the one he kept in his boot, the one he had believed secret for nine years.

"Sit down, Voss," Corvane said pleasantly, without turning. "Or stand. You're more comfortable standing, I've noticed. You sit like a man expecting the chair to be repossessed."

* * *

The commission took four minutes to deliver and would take the rest of Bram's life to carry.

In three days — perhaps two, if the armistice delegation crossed the mountains faster than expected — the Meridian Trading Company intended to be clean. Every document that could not survive the light of an inquiry was to be gathered from the harbor workshop on Salt Row and burned, all of it, the whole paper body of six years' rot: charter amendments, freight manifests, the coded telegraph summaries that had gone out to both fleets through neutral intermediaries. Bram was to escort the burn crew. His routes were trusted; his face was known at every checkpoint between here and the breakwater; and no watchman in the city would think to question a courier carrying orders under the company seal into the workshop at night.

"You won't read anything," Corvane said, sliding the folio across the table. "You never do. It's why I trust you. A curious man is a liability, Voss, but you — you've always understood that some things are heavier than knowing." He smiled, and it reached his eyes, which was the worst part. "There's a bonus structure attached. Your mother's grave has stood unmarked these eight years. Marble travels cheap from the north quarries now. We could put her name in stone by spring."

Bram heard himself accept. His voice came out level, obedient, pitched exactly as it had been trained to be since he was nine years old and a company surgeon had looked into his ears and eyes and pronounced him sound for service. Somewhere beneath the words, the diver's blood in him — his mother's blood, the blood that knew how to read current and pressure and the moment a breath must turn back toward the surface — was screaming a single continuous note. He did not let it reach his face. He had never let anything reach his face.

At the door, Corvane stopped him with a hand on his shoulder, warm and heavy as ballast.

"I've watched you grow up in these corridors," the director said. "Nine years old, soaked to the bone, holding that pearl of yours like it was the last thing floating. I told them then: this one will be worth the investment." He patted Bram's shoulder twice, gently, the way one pats a good dog. "And I was right."

Bram walked down three flights of stairs without feeling his feet touch them. The pearl. He had never told anyone at the company about the pearl. Which meant they had taken inventory of him, once, thoroughly, at the very beginning — weighed and catalogued the single thing of his mother's he possessed — and had simply never mentioned it, all these years, the way one does not mention the collar while the dog is young.

The fog outside had thickened to wool. He walked into it and let it close over him like water.

* * *

The signal was one he had used for years without thinking of it as a signal: a chalk mark on the third bollard of the divers' jetty, left by crews who had been blacklisted but who still came down to watch the water they were forbidden to work. A spiral, drawn small at knee height, meaning *someone below; safe to speak.* Bram added a single horizontal stroke through its center — a modification he invented in the walking, his heart hammering at the transgression of it, at how easy invention was when the stakes finally outweighed the fear.

She found him within the hour, materializing out of the fog near the net-drying racks, her empty sleeve pinned and folded with a precision that spoke of long practice. Ilsa Rehn did not waste movement or words.

"Well?"

"They burn the workshop. Two days, maybe less. I lead the crew in."

"And Wren's originals?"

"In it. All of it, Corvane said." Bram's jaw ached; he realized he had been clenching it since the third floor. "They'll have oil and men with irons. Eight, ten. And me."

Ilsa was quiet for a moment. In the fog her face was half-erased, just the set line of her mouth and the scar through one eyebrow visible, and Bram found he could speak to that partial face more easily than to a whole one.

"Can you delay the crew?" she asked.

"Ten minutes, maybe fifteen, at the checkpoint on Salt Row. The sergeant there takes his tea at half past eleven, and the manifest has to be countersigned twice. If the second signatory is slow—" He stopped. He was doing it again, the ledger habit, mapping the machine's rhythms the way his mother had mapped tides. "I can give you twenty minutes inside, if your people can copy fast in the dark."

"Our people," Ilsa repeated. Not mocking. Testing the weight of the phrase, the way a diver tests a line before trusting it with her life.

"I haven't decided what I am yet," Bram said quietly.

"No one ever does. It gets decided for them, and then they spend years deciding whether to agree." She turned her face toward the black water. "Wren says you'll do the right thing. He says you checked the seal on your very first commission, at nine years old, and said nothing, and remembered the wrongness of it for six years. That's not obedience, Voss. That's evidence being filed."

He hadn't known anyone had seen that. He hadn't known Wren had seen it. Something loosened in his chest and immediately frightened him, because things that loosened could be cut.

"The copies," he said, steadying himself on procedure, on arithmetic. "How far along?"

"Half. Maybe more, if the cellar stays dry." She looked back at him, and even in the fog her gaze had weight. "But originals matter, Bram. Copies can be called forgeries. Copies can be drowned in inquiries. The originals burning is them writing history. We need those pages to survive the fire *and* the fire to happen — both — so the world sees what they tried to destroy."

"That's not copying anymore. That's theater."

"That's justice," Ilsa said. "It has always been theater. The question is only who writes the stage directions." She pulled her coat tighter. "Two nights, then. I'll have the divers ready. Can you get word to me the day of?"

"I can get word to you an hour before," Bram said. "Watch the bollard."

She nodded once and was gone into the fog, soundless, and Bram stood alone at the rail of the jetty listening to the water slap the pilings, thinking: *I have just committed treason.* The thought arrived with astonishing calm, the calm of deep water, where everything sinks eventually and nothing struggles.

* * *

He did not go straight home. He walked the long way, along Salt Row itself, past the workshop.

It crouched at the end of the street like something waiting to die: three storeys of salt-stained brick, windows boarded on the upper floors, the loading doors facing the water so that anything could be brought in or out beyond the sight of the town. Bram had delivered there perhaps forty times. He knew the building the way he knew his own hands — the loose bar on the coal hatch, the drainage culvert that ran from the flooded cellar under the tideline, the stairwell where the lamp always burned a full minute after the watchman's rounds because of a fault no one had bothered to fix. He had thought of this knowledge as inventory. Now he understood it was ammunition, and that he had been stockpiling it all his life without knowing against whom.

A watchman stood at the corner, stamping his feet against the cold. Bram lowered his head and walked past at the pace of a man with somewhere dull to be. The watchman glanced up, recognized the courier's coat, and looked away again. Invisible. Trusted precisely because he was owned. Corvane had said it as praise. Standing now in the shadow across from the building he was commissioned to help destroy, Bram finally heard it correctly: as an epitaph written in advance for the person he had been.

His mother had held her breath for pearls. Four minutes down, working the beds off the point with the other women, coming up with her lips blue and her fists closed tight around whatever the sea had granted. *Everything worth having costs air,* she used to tell him when he was small, sitting on the jetty with the pearl warm in his palm. *The trick is knowing what you'd spend your last breath on.*

He had spent six years spending his breath on sealed orders, on marble gravestones offered by the hand that had buried her, on the warm weight of a director's palm against his shoulder. He stood in the fog and counted it all, the full ledger of his life, and found it overdrawn.

Two days. Twenty minutes inside. Half-copied pages in a flooded cellar, and a dead man alive below the breakwater, and a family forming around him out of blacklist and exile that asked nothing of him except, for the first time in his life, the truth.

Bram turned up his collar and started back along the water. Behind him the workshop sat dark against the darker bay, already in his mind half alight, flames climbing the boarded windows, lighting the harbor like a false dawn.

An hour later, passing the jetty one final time, he saw that the chalk mark on the third bollard had been answered: his spiral with its new stroke, and beneath it, fresh and small, a single word in a hand he knew better than any living person's — a hand that had signed his indenture, his commissions, his mother's burial voucher.

*Aldous* was written there, and then, after a pause marked by the chalk's hesitation, three more letters:

*—wants you.*

Below the breakwater, the dead clerk was asking for him by name.
