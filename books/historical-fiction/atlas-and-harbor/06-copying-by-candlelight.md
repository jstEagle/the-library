## 6. Copying by Candlelight

The cellar breathed like a drowned thing.

Ilsa felt it the moment she descended the iron rungs — the exhale of cold water against stone, the slow tidal pulse beneath the floor grates that told her the sea came this far in twice a day and would not be argued with. The Meridian workshop above them stored rope, tar, chain, and paper; below it, in a chamber no architect had ever intended to flood, the harbor seeped through old mortar and pooled ankle-deep on a slate floor that sloped toward a drain long since rusted shut. Nobody came here. Nobody counted what a cellar held when the ledgers upstairs said the cellar did not exist.

Which was why Wren had chosen it, four months before he died — or did not die — and why they copied his records here now, in the wet dark, with eleven days left until the armistice delegation arrived and the company made certain nothing survived to meet them.

Ilsa had worked by lamplight in forty countries. She had mapped the Snellen Shoals in a storm, inked coastlines for admiralties who paid in blood money, drawn harbor charts so precise that gunboats could thread them blindfolded. Her single hand knew line and contour better than most men's two. But she had never worked like this: pages pegged to a drying frame salvaged from a broken skiff, three candles guttering in jars of sand, the air thick enough to drink, and every stroke of ink fighting the damp that wanted to bloom it into fog.

"You're feathering again," Bram said quietly, from across the upturned cable-spool that served as their table. "Third line."

"I can see that."

"Rice paper takes the damp faster than the stock they use upstairs. If you breathe out when you write—" He demonstrated, angling his face away from the page, holding the breath as the nib traveled. "It keeps the moisture off."

She watched him copy a full ledger column without once lifting his eyes from it, his letters small and even as stitching. Of course he knew. The boy had spent nine years inside the counting-house's paper machine; he knew its papers the way a diver knew tides. He had also, Ilsa noticed, positioned himself between the candles and the cellar's only window — a grate at the waterline, black as a closed eye — so that anyone peering in from the harbor side would see nothing but darkness where a man might stand.

That was the other thing she was learning about Bram Voss. He protected things without announcing it.

* * *

They came at different hours, the divers, slipping down through the coal chute that opened onto the weedy strip behind the workshop. Sela arrived first each night with bread wrapped in oilcloth and news folded into complaints about the blacklist. Old Tammer came after, half-deaf, carrying the ledgers themselves one at a time inside a false-bottomed crab pot — Wren's originals, hauled up from their hiding place below the breakwater two at a week, because more would have meant questions at the fish market. And twice, only twice, Aldous Wren himself had come down on Tammer's arm, gray as paper, his lungs still rattling from six months of salt, to point with a shaking finger at columns only he understood.

"Page forty," he'd rasped the second night, tapping a shipping manifest that looked, to any eye trained in the company's ordinary deceits, unremarkable. "Read the destination codes against the dates. Every cargo marked *salvage* after March was munitions manifests sold twice — once to each fleet. The company brokered the war's weather. They knew when the armistice terms would move before either government did, and they sold the knowing."

"And the proof?" Ilsa had asked.

Wren had smiled then, a terrible thin smile. "Proof is repetition, madam. One lie is a rumor. Two hundred is an inquiry." He coughed into his sleeve. "Copy all of it. Especially the parts that look like nothing. The parts that look like nothing are what will hang them."

So they copied the parts that looked like nothing. Ilsa rendered the codes into a cartographer's cipher of her own devising — dots and bearing-marks that read as coastal survey notes, meaningless to any company man who intercepted them — while Bram transcribed figures and Sela pressed each finished sheet between waxed boards. By the fifth night there were sixty pages drying on the frame like strange pale fish, and Ilsa caught herself, in the candle-gutter between columns, watching Bram's hands and thinking of nothing she could afford to think.

* * *

On the sixth night, the watchman came early.

Isla heard it first as a change in the building's breathing — a door above, the particular drag of a boot with a loose heel — and froze with her nib in the ink. Bram's head came up like a gull scenting weather. He raised one hand, flat: *wait*. His lips moved, counting.

Then, faint through the floorboards, a lantern's rattle, and a voice humming something tuneless and municipal, and the scrape of the top-of-the-stairs bolt being thrown.

"He doesn't come down," Bram breathed. "Not ever. He walks the upper floor, checks the tar stores, and leaves by the yard gate. Eleven minutes, twelve if it's raining."

"It isn't raining," Sela whispered.

"Then eleven."

But the boots stopped at the top of the stairs. Silence. Then the creak of weight on the first iron rung, and another, and Ilsa understood with a cold drop in her stomach that tonight was not tonight's schedule — that the watchman had heard something, or smelled candle-tallow, or simply chosen this night to be diligent, and diligence would kill them all.

The rungs rang under his descending boot. Four. Five. Six.

Bram moved.

He did not panic; that was what she would remember afterward, in the years when she loved him and still did not name it. He pinched out two of the three candles between finger and thumb — quick, silent, practiced — took the third, and stepped into the flooded channel along the east wall where the tide drained through the grates. There, crouching, he held the flame low against the wet slate so that from the stairs it would read as nothing — a workman's forgotten lamp, perhaps, or a reflection off the water.

Then he knocked over the cable spool. Loudly. Deliberately. A crash of wood on stone that rolled through the cellar like cannon fire.

"Hallo?" The watchman's voice, suspicious, close. The lantern light swung down the stairwell. "Who's down there?"

"Bram Voss," Bram called back, in a voice Ilsa had never heard him use — bored, aggrieved, official. "Company courier. Counting-house business. Mind your own floor, Pellham."

A pause. Lantern light probed the dark, found Bram standing alone beside the fallen spool with the lamp at his feet, looking up with the expression of a man interrupted at tedious labor. Behind the drying frame, Ilsa pressed herself into the shadow of the wall with Sela, both of them still as ballast, while the light swept past within an arm's reach of the drying pages — sixty sheets of hanging evidence swaying faintly in the cellar's breath.

"Pellham," Bram went on, before the man could speak again, and here he performed something extraordinary: he yawned. "You threw the bolt on the yard gate Tuesday and Corvane's clerk nearly broke his ankle on the landing. Third incident this month goes in the log. Do you want me to put it in the log, Pellham?"

"I— no. No, sir. Thought I heard rats."

"You heard me knocking over my own work. Go check your tar."

The lantern retreated. The rungs rang upward. The bolt scraped home, and the tuneless humming faded toward the yard gate, and for a long moment nobody in the cellar moved except the papers on their frame, turning gently like moths.

Then Sela began to laugh — helpless, soundless, tears-standing laughter — and Ilsa found herself laughing too, pressing her wrist against her mouth, while Bram relit the candles with steady fingers as though he had done nothing at all.

"How," Ilsa managed, "did you know his name?"

"I know everyone's name." He said it plainly, without pride, and the plainness was worse than boasting. "Pellham drinks. His wife left in the spring. He's frightened of losing the post because the company owns his house, and a frightened man believes whatever an important voice tells him." He looked down at the spool, righting it. "Everyone in that building is owned by something. It makes them legible."

Sela squeezed his shoulder on her way to the drying frame, an easy, sisterly gesture, and Ilsa saw Bram receive it the way a man receives water in a desert — trying not to show how much he needed it. She thought: *he has been legible all his life, and no one has ever read him kindly.* She turned back to her page and wrote the next code-line truer than anything she had ever drawn.

* * *

Near dawn on the eighth night, with ninety-one pages sealed between boards and the tide sighing high against the grates, Wren came down again. He stood a long time over the copies, touching them with one fingertip as if taking their temperature.

"There's a boat," he said at last, to Ilsa though his eyes were on Bram. "Tammer's cousin, a fishing smack out of the outer mole. She can carry the finished pages to the delegation's anchorage before the signing. But she sails when she sails, and the last ledgers won't be copied by then." He coughed, wet and long. "So. Someone must choose what is enough."

Nobody spoke. The candles leaned in their jars.

"We'll send what's ready," Ilsa said. "Two hundred lies hang a man. Ninety might hang a company."

But Bram was staring at the remaining stack — five ledgers, fat with repetition, the very heart of the proof — and Ilsa watched him do arithmetic she recognized, the diver's arithmetic his mother's crew had taught him: what must be risked, what can be carried, what cannot be replaced. She opened her mouth to warn him, though against what she could not have said.

She didn't need to. Above them, muffled by floorboards and tar-barrels, the workshop bell rang the hour — and beneath it, fainter, came a sound neither of them had heard before and both of them would remember: footsteps, many footsteps, and the unmistakable clatter of lanterns being issued in the yard.

Eleven days until the burning, Wren had said. They had copied for eight.

Someone upstairs had decided not to wait.
