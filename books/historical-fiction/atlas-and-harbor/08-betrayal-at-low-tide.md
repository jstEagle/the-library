## 8. Betrayal at Low Tide

Ilsa Rehn had learned to read betrayal the way other people learned to read weather — not by the dramatic signs but by the small ones, the pressure dropping in a room, the way a man stopped meeting your eyes and started meeting everyone's instead.

She noticed it first in Tam Soller's hands.

The diving crew worked in close quarters, and hands told everything. Tam's had always been restless — he was the youngest, twenty-two, with fingers that picked at his cuffs and counted invisible coins when he thought no one watched. But lately those fingers had gone still. Composed. Hands that have made a decision settle like water finding its level, and Ilsa had mapped enough coastlines to know what calm water could hide: reefs, wrecks, whole drowned towns lying quiet under a smooth surface.

He was scrubbing salt off the copying table Thursday night, working slower than he needed to, lingering near the cellar stair where Wren sat transcribing by shielded candlelight.

"You've been to the counting-house," she said quietly, coming up behind him.

Tam flinched so hard the rag dropped from his hand. "I went for mending twine."

"The counting-house doesn't sell twine." She kept her voice level, almost kind. She had learned that from Wren: never corner what you want to live. "Tam. Whatever it is, whatever they're holding over you — tell me before you do something your hands already know about."

For one moment his face opened, and she saw the boy underneath, the one who had been blacklisted at seventeen for surfacing too slow with a company pearl clenched in his teeth — a pearl he'd been trying to return, though no one at the inquiry had believed him. Six years on the blacklist. Six years of company men spitting his name into the harbor mud.

Then the shutters came down, and she knew it was already done.

"It's nothing," he said. "You're tired, Ilsa. We're all tired."

* * *

She should have moved them that night. She replayed that decision for hours afterward, standing in the smoke-stained dark above the tideline, turning it over the way she'd once turned charts — looking for the error in her own soundings. They were three days from finished. Wren's ledgers ran to four hundred pages and the damp ate their ink, curled their paper, made every letter an argument with the cellar air. Three days, she'd calculated. The raid would come at the armistice, she'd assumed, loud and official, part of the general burning.

She had not accounted for a young man's arithmetic. For the way desperation could balance a ledger of its own: one location traded against six years of blacklist, one family sold against the chance of being let back inside the machine. Tam wasn't evil. That was the terrible thing. He was simply owned in a way that ran deeper than indenture papers — owned by want itself, by the dream of being legible again to the company, restored to its books, forgiven.

She understood it better than she wanted to. She had been legible to them once. Cartographer, second class, Ilsa Rehn, her name printed neat as a coastline on every chart she drew. Then Wren had shown her what the margins hid, and she'd chosen illegibility, and she had never once regretted it — until the moment she stood in the dark and realized she'd misjudged the tide.

* * *

It came at low tide, an hour past midnight, when the harbor pulled back from the seawall and left the workshop's flooded cellar accessible only through the boat-slip, and the watch rotations changed, and Bram's forged delivery route put him across the city with a satchel of innocuous freight.

Ilsa was above ground when the bells began — thank God, or whatever kept accounts in this city, because she was above ground, with the half-finished copies packed in oilcloth against her chest where they rode like a second heartbeat. She'd carried them up to dry them. She would remember that afterward: that vanity, that fussiness about damp paper, had saved half the truth.

The rest stayed below with Wren.

She heard the raid before she saw it. Not shouting — the Meridian Trading Company did not shout; it owned constables who whispered. It was the sound of boots moving in unison on wet stone, thirty pairs at least, and the creak of the crane winches being brought around, and then lantern-light blooming in the workshop windows all at once, every floor, top to bottom, the building suddenly lit like a ship at anchor.

She pressed herself into the shadow of the breakwater's landward wall, oilcloth bundle crushed against her ribs, and watched them take him.

They brought Wren up the boat-slip blinking, one shoe missing, his shirtsleeves inkstained to the elbow — a clerk to the last, arrested mid-sentence. A constable held a lantern under his chin, and even at forty yards Ilsa could see him doing it: counting them. Counting the men, noting the sergeant's insignia, memorizing the make of the irons. Six months hiding under a breakwater and he still inventoried everything he saw, because somewhere in that precise clerk's mind, every fact recorded was a fact that might someday be filed against them.

His eyes swept the darkness once — along the wall, across the water, over the exact shadow where she stood — and did not stop. Good, that look said. Go.

Then Director Corvane stepped out of the carriage that had come up silent behind the constables, and the sight of him turned Ilsa cold in a way the night wind hadn't managed. He crossed the stones unhurried, gloves on, and stopped in front of Wren the way a man stops in front of a ledger entry he has finally found after long searching.

"Mr. Wren," Corvane said, and his voice carried across the water, pleasant as a dinner invitation. "We really must reconcile our accounts."

* * *

She ran. There was no heroism in it — she wanted to be honest about that later, when honesty was survivable. She ran the way she'd run from the survey office two years ago, low and fast along the breakwater's blind side, and she did not go back toward the slip, and she hated herself with every stride and kept running, because the copies were beating against her chest and a dead woman's cargo does no one any good.

Half-finished. Two hundred and ten pages of four hundred. The armistice intelligence, the double sales, the convoy schedules sold to both fleets — most of it was in the pages against her heart. But the final section, the one naming names inside the company, the one Wren had saved for last like a man saving the seal on a letter —

Below. With him. In the building they now owned.

She reached the diver's jetty as the sky was going the color of wet ash, and found the crew gathered like survivors of a wreck that hadn't finished happening. Old Maren had Tam by the collar against a piling, and Tam was not resisting, which was somehow worse than if he'd fought.

"She's right," Tam was saying, to no one, to everyone, his voice cracking open. "I only gave them the location. Only the location. They said Wren wouldn't be touched, they said it was just the papers, they said—"

"They said," Maren repeated, and released him the way you release something dead, "exactly what people like them always say. And you signed your name to it anyway."

"I've been blacklisted six years." It came out of him like something breaking. "Six years. You all surface fine and I go down and down and there's nothing down there, don't you understand? Nothing. I wanted my name back."

The jetty went quiet. Even the water seemed to hold itself.

And then boots on the planks behind Ilsa, and Bram Voss came onto the jetty still in his courier's coat, satchel strap across his chest like a bandolier, and she watched him take in the scene — Maren's face, Tam's collapse, the absence of Wren, the ash-colored light — and she watched the understanding arrive in him the way tide arrives, total and unstoppable.

"They used my route," he said.

Not a question. She made herself answer it anyway. "Your delivery schedule. The gaps in it. They've known where you weren't, every night, for weeks. Your competence drew us a map, Bram. They just read it."

He stood very still. She had seen him afraid before, and careful, and calculating. This was none of those. This was a man discovering that the one thing the company had never managed to own — his skill, his precision, the private country of his own capability — had been requisitioned without his signature. His hands, which measured everything, hung at his sides measuring nothing.

"Tam," Bram said quietly, "what else did you give them?"

"Nothing. I swear it on my mother's—"

"What else."

Tam looked up, and whatever he saw in Bram's face made him answer honestly at last. "They asked about you. Whether the courier was one of you. I didn't — Bram, I never confirmed it. But they knew your handwriting was on the freight tags. They showed me a tag."

Bram closed his eyes. When he opened them, Ilsa saw the choice happen — saw flight and rescue stand up inside him and fight, briefly, silently, and saw which one won. She had expected it, and it still hurt to watch, the way true things hurt.

"They'll move him before the signing," Bram said. "Corvane doesn't leave loose entries in his books. If we're going to take him back, it's today, while they're still staging the fire — they'll want him inside it, Ilsa. Clean records. No inquiry, no witness, no grave." His voice didn't shake. That frightened her more than shaking would have. "They're going to burn him alive in the workshop and call it an accident of the armistice."

Above the harbor, the company's cranes stood black against the brightening sky, swinging their loads with the patience of things that had never once needed to hurry.

"We'll need a way in that isn't a door," Ilsa said.

Bram looked at the workshop, at its single lit window, and something old surfaced in his face — the nine-year-old indentured boy who had learned every secret passage in that building because knowing them was the price of surviving them.

"There is one," he said. "And it floods at high tide. We go tonight, or we go never."

Out on the water, the tide had begun to turn.
