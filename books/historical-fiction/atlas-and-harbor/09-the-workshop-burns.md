## 9. The Workshop Burns

They gave Bram a lamp for the job, which was the first insult. He had carried sealed orders through this harbor for nine years without light — he knew its stones by the soles of his feet, knew which cobbles wept grease and which held frost — and now Corvane's men handed him an oil lamp with a brass handle polished by other men's hands and told him to walk ahead of the burn crew like some festival child.

"Keep to the front," said the foreman, a broad man named Hask whose breath smelled of the company's cheap anise liquor. "You know the doorways. We don't want any of us wandering into the tar stores."

The second insult was that they believed him. That after everything — after Ilsa's hand pressed hard against his chest in the dark of the breakwater tunnel only hours ago, after Wren's voice cracked through the cellar saying *go, boy, the pages matter more than the man* — they still looked at Bram Voss and saw the courier. The owned thing. The knife that cuts only where it is sent.

The fog had come in off the bay at dusk, thick as wet wool, and it muffled the harbor bells until they sounded like they were ringing underwater. Nine men walked behind him with cans of naphtha sloshing against their knees. Somewhere out on the black water, a fishing boat was already putting out for the delegation's anchorage, Ilsa aboard, half the copied ledgers wrapped in oilcloth against her one hip. The other half lay hidden in the diver's net-loft. And Aldous Wren — Aldous Wren, who could not run, whose lungs had never recovered from the bay — sat locked in the workshop's record room above the flooded cellar, waiting to burn with the proof of what he'd found.

*The pages matter more than the man.*

Bram had nodded when Wren said it. He had even meant it, in the way you mean things that are too large to hold. But meaning a thing and living with it were different countries, and Bram had spent his whole life learning the routes between them.

The workshop rose out of the fog at the end of the tar quay, three stories of salt-blackened brick, its windows already dark. The company owned it the way it owned everything: absolutely, quietly, with paperwork no one would ever be permitted to read. By morning there would be no paperwork either. By morning there would be a sad story about a lamp knocked over, an unlucky watchman, the terrible cost of war's ending.

Hask checked his pocket watch by lamplight. "Delegation anchors at dawn. We're lit by midnight." He smiled at Bram, companionable, awful. "Your last delivery, eh? Fitting."

Bram said nothing. He put his shoulder to the side door he had opened two hundred times and led them in.

* * *

Inside, the workshop smelled of ink and tallow and the mineral cold rising from the flooded cellar below. The burn crew worked with the efficiency of men who had done this before — Bram wondered, with a sickness that settled low in his stomach, how many other buildings had ended this way, how many other ledgers, how many names. They stacked records in the stairwell where the draft would carry fire upward. They poured naphtha along the corridor seams like men laying a path of silver ribbon.

Bram memorized all of it. Where each can stood. Which stairs were soaked and which were left dry because the men were tired and the fog was cold and midnight felt far away. He counted exits the way his mother had once counted breaths before a dive: without appearing to count at all.

At the door, Hask clapped him on the shoulder. "Wait at the corner till she catches, then report to Director Corvane yourself. He asked for you personal." A wink. "Man thinks well of you, Voss. That's worth more than papers these days."

Bram walked out into the fog. He heard the lock turn behind him — Hask locking the street door out of habit or instruction, and Bram understood, then, in his bones, that the lock was not to keep people out.

He rounded the corner as ordered. He waited exactly long enough for the first match to flare behind the fogged windows, a small orange star floating in the grey.

Then he ran — not away from the building but along its flank, down the tar-slick alley to the water stair, where a rusted iron door hid in the shadow of the seawall. It opened on a passage the couriers called the Cold Vein: a service tunnel cut through the harbor wall generations ago, running beneath the counting-house quarter, surfacing again inside the workshop's cellar. No ledger recorded it. Only the indentured knew it, passed from runner to runner the way divers pass down the locations of breathing holes in a reef. It was the one inheritance the company had given him that the company itself did not own.

He took the steps into the dark three at a time, and the sea came up around his boots, black and thigh-deep at low ebb, and the smoke was already finding its way down through the building's throat above him, tasting the air.

* * *

The record room door was oak, banded with iron, locked with a key that hung now from Hask's belt somewhere in the burning world above. Bram hit it with his shoulder until his vision sparked. Once. Twice. On the third blow something gave — not the lock but the hinge screws, old iron tearing out of rotten wood — and the door sagged inward like a knee buckling, and Aldous Wren was sitting against the far wall with his back straight and his hands folded, as if he had been waiting for a appointment.

"You came back," Wren said. His voice was calm. His eyes were not; they went past Bram to the smoke threading under the ceiling and stayed there.

"I came back."

"The pages—"

"At sea. Ilsa has them. All of them." It was almost true. Enough true.

Wren tried to stand and coughed instead, a deep wet sound that frightened Bram more than the fire had yet managed to. Bram got the clerk's arm over his shoulders — the man weighed nothing, he weighed less than a coil of rope — and turned them toward the corridor, and the corridor was gone. Not gone, but changed: orange light pulsed along its length where the naphtha ribbon burned, and the air had begun to make that sound Bram would hear for the rest of his life, the low animal roar of a building feeding on itself.

"The Vein," Bram said. "We go down. The cellar floods at the far end but it's low tide, there's footing—"

"I can't dive, boy."

"You don't have to dive. You have to walk in water to your chest while I hold you up. That's not diving. That's arithmetic." His mother's phrase, in his mother's voice, out of his own mouth. Wren looked at him — really looked, the way he must once have looked at a suspicious column of figures — and nodded.

They went down the dry stairs, the ones the tired men had spared, Bram counting each step against the memory of every other time he had taken them. At the bottom the heat followed them like a living thing leaning on their backs. The cellar was filling with smoke that moved across the ceiling in slow rivers, dipping lower, searching. The black water of the tunnel mouth gleamed ahead, and beyond it, forty yards of drowned dark, and beyond that the harbor stair and the fog and the open night.

"In," Bram said. "Breathe shallow. Move when I move."

Wren gripped his collar with both hands, a drowning man's hold, and Bram thought absurdly of being nine years old, of his mother's hands adjusting his weight in the shallows, of the first time he had put his face beneath the green water of the bay and seen the pearls lying in their beds like sleeping eyes. *The sea takes nothing you don't give it,* she had told him. *Remember that, and it will never beat you.*

The water took them to the waist, then the chest. It was February-cold, a cold that closed the fist around the heart. Above and behind them the workshop groaned, a long timber complaint, and embers began to drop hissing into the water around them like dying stars. Bram moved by touch and by the count in his head — thirty steps, twenty-five, the ceiling dropping, Wren's coughing wracking them both — and at fifteen steps from the stair his feet found the submerged ledge he had known would be there, the couriers' resting stone, and he hauled Wren onto it and let them both breathe for four seconds exactly.

Then the building screamed.

It was not a word he would ever find better than *scream*. Somewhere above, the main joists surrendered together, and a column of orange light fell down through the heart of the workshop, and the pressure of it came through the tunnel like a hand shoving the sea. Water surged. Bram lost his grip on Wren's collar for one heartbeat, two, found it again by blind luck or by the clerk's own desperate clutching, and dragged him the final yards with his arms burning and his lungs full of smoke-taste, up the harbor stair, out through the rusted door, into the fog.

Behind them, with a sound like the world clearing its throat, the roof fell in.

* * *

They collapsed on the tar quay, and the fog caught fire.

Not truly — but that is what it looked like. The blaze climbed the ruined workshop and lit the low clouds from beneath until the whole sky glowed a bruised amber, and the harbor turned to hammered copper, and every mast, every crane, every gull on every piling stood silhouetted against a false dawn that had come six hours early. Burning pages lifted on the heat and swirled out over the water like a migration of bright birds, settling, hissing, gone. The company's history, becoming weather.

Wren was coughing, laughing, weeping, all three. Bram knelt beside him in the tar-stink and shook and could not stop shaking.

Then a shape came out of the fog at a run — one-armed, oilcloth bundle strapped where the other arm should have been, rowing boat still rocking at the water stair behind her. Ilsa dropped to her knees in front of them both, and her hand found Bram's neck the way you check a pulse and a promise at once.

"They saw the glow from the anchorage road," she said. "I turned the boat around. Tell me you're—"

"He's alive," Bram said. "The copies are safe?"

"At sea within the hour." Her eyes went past him to the burning ruin, to the false dawn eating the harbor, and something moved through her face that was not fear and was not grief. It was the look of a woman watching a debt come due.

Down the quay, torches were gathering — the burn crew, the constables, and among them, unmistakable even at this distance, the neat figure of Director Corvane, turning his head slowly, methodically, along the line of survivors the way a man counts spoons.

His gaze reached Bram and stopped.

Bram rose. His legs held. Behind him the workshop blazed with everything the company had ever tried to bury, and the light of it fell across the water toward the delegation's anchorage like a signal only the honest would read. Corvane began to walk toward him through the copper-lit fog, unhurried, smiling, and Bram Voss — indentured no longer in anything but paper — stood his ground and did not bow.
