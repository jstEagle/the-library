## 1. The Weight of Small Things

Hazel Okafor had known how to pack a life before they had ever needed to.

It was the first thing Master Idris had taught them—not filing, not oiling, not the patient listening of a stethoscope against a watch case—but packing. *Everything you own should fit in one bag,* he'd said, the winter Hazel came to him, ten years old and carrying exactly that: one bag, damp at the corners, smelling of river mud. *Not because you'll need to run. Because knowing what you can carry tells you what you are.*

Hazel had spent seven years learning what they were by weight.

Now, in the back room of the workshop of Master Corvin—Idris's old rival, who had taken Hazel in for reasons no one spoke aloud—they knelt on the floorboards at dawn and laid everything out in rows, the way one would lay out tools before a delicate repair. The light through the single window was the gray of unpolished silver. Somewhere below, the river moved under its winter skin, groaning once, long and low, like a giant turning over in sleep that would not last.

The census wagons were already on the bridge.

Hazel could not see the bridge from this window—it faced the yard, with its rusted vats and Corvin's scrawny dog asleep in a barrel—but they could hear it. Or thought they could. That was the trouble with sound in Bellhaven in winter: everything carried along the ice, every cartwheel and cough, so that a body never quite knew which noises were real and which were only the river talking to itself. But Marisol's cousin had come through the night before with flour and news, and the news was simple enough: two wagons, green canvas, government gray beneath, parked at the far end of Tallow Bridge since dusk. Men sleeping in them. A clerk's desk set up at the bridgehead by lamplight, already taking names.

Taking names. As if names were things that could be picked up like coins from a gutter.

*They can be,* Hazel thought, folding a wool shirt into thirds. *That's rather the whole point.*

* * *

The rows on the floorboards read like an inventory of a very small country.

First row: tools. These were the heaviest and the least negotiable. Hazel's own kit—apprentice grade but honest—was already wrapped in oiled cloth: three screwdrivers ground down to different widths, a pair of tweezers made from a flattened pin, the jeweler's loupe cracked across one lens so the world seen through it wore a hairline scar. Then the things that were not theirs but might as well have been: Idris's old burnisher, lent and never asked for back; a mainspring winder Corvin had thrown at Hazel's head in a moment of temper and later pretended to have given freely; a box of assorted pivots, each smaller than a grain of rice, each worth more by weight than the shirt folded above it.

Second row: papers. One paper, properly. The apprenticeship indenture, four months old, bearing a seal that had cost Corvin's forger cousin two loaves of black bread and a bottle of plum spirit. It named Hazel Blackwood—Blackwood, not Okafor; Blackwood was a Bellhaven name, a name that belonged to no one's memory—and bound *the said apprentice* to *the trade of clockmaking* for a term of years under Master Corvin Aldous, horologist. It was, in the way of such documents, mostly true. Hazel did belong to the trade. There was even a real Aldous on the paper, though the wrong branch of the family; Wren Aldous up at the observatory would no more claim kinship with Corvin than the moon would claim kinship with a puddle.

But it was signed, and sealed, and dated, and when a clerk held it to the light the watermark looked correct. In Bellhaven, that was nearly the same as existing.

Third row: everything else. Two shirts, one sweater, socks darned until they were more thread than sock. A bar of soap. Half a loaf, wrapped in waxed cloth. A stub of pencil and three sheets of cheap paper, blank, because blank pages could become anything—letters, lists, if pressed, a bill of sale for a person who did not exist. A tin of lamp oil. And at the end of the row, alone, wrapped separately in soft flannel as though it were an egg that might hatch:

The watch.

Hazel unwrapped it, because they always did. It was a pocket watch, silver plate gone dull at the edges where their father's thumb had worn it bright and their mother's fingers had let it dim again. It had belonged to both of them, which meant it had belonged to neither, which meant it had been left to Hazel, which meant it had survived when nothing else of theirs had. Its hands stood at eleven minutes past four. They had stood there for nine years. The balance staff was snapped—a clean break, Hazel knew by heart, visible only under the loupe, a fracture in something too small to grieve over properly, which was perhaps why Hazel grieved over it constantly.

Nine years, and Hazel had never repaired it.

Other watches they could fix in their sleep. Escapements, repeaters, a carriage clock with a broken fusee chain that had defeated two journeymen before it came to Hazel's bench. But this watch sat in its flannel like a wound sits in a body, and every year Hazel told themselves: *this winter.* And every winter they took it out, looked at the broken staff, understood precisely what was required—one new staff, turned true, fitted, poised—and put it away again.

Corvin said it was because the parts were scarce. Hazel knew better. Some clocks you keep stopped on purpose, because while they are stopped, the hour they mark is still happening. Eleven minutes past four. Whatever afternoon their parents' time had ended, it could stay ending, gently, forever, inside the case.

Hazel rewrapped the watch and placed it in the satchel first, deepest, against the frame where nothing could press it.

* * *

"You're doing it again."

Master Corvin stood in the doorway of the back room, a candle in one hand though dawn was coming, because Corvin believed daylight was free and therefore worthless. He was a narrow man, all elbows and grievance, with the reddened knuckles of someone who had spent forty years blaming metal for his own impatience. He had been Idris Okafor's rival for thirty years, which meant chiefly that he had hated a better craftsman for thirty years and called it professional standards. When the census had begun asking after Idris's household in the autumn, Corvin had volunteered, loudly and publicly, that he had *no association whatsoever* with the man—and then, in the same breath, quieter, had told Hazel to come sleep in his back room and keep off the streets until things settled.

Hazel had never worked out whether this was kindness or bookkeeping. With Corvin it was usually both.

"Doing what again?" Hazel asked, though they knew.

"Packing like a funeral." Corvin set the candle on the sill and squinted at the neat rows. "Wagons sit on bridges all the time. Government's got wagons the way the river's got water. Doesn't mean it's rising."

"It rose in Kettering," Hazel said. "It rose in Ashford's Landing."

"And I'm sure somebody there packed a bag and felt very wise about it afterward." He sniffed. "If you're going to sit in my shop, you'll work. The Hollis regulator won't clean itself, and your hands are the only ones in this building small enough for the case."

He left. The candle stayed, guttering, and Hazel looked at it and understood it entirely: Corvin's whole heart, expressed as a flame he refused to admit was lighting anything.

Hazel did not unpack. But they did not finish packing either. Instead they sat back on their heels and looked at the rows, and did the arithmetic they had been doing since childhood, the arithmetic no teacher had ever taught because no school in Bellhaven admitted students like Hazel.

A life, weighed. Tools, four pounds. Clothes, three. Bread and soap and oil, two. Papers, nothing—less than nothing, less than the ink on them, because a forged page is a debt you owe forward. The watch, half a pound, and heavier than all the rest together. Total: a satchel a strong child could carry at a run. Nine years of living, and the whole estate came to nine pounds, if the scale was honest.

Most people's lives weighed more, Hazel supposed. Houses. Furniture. Photographs, if you were allowed photographs. Graves, if you were allowed graves.

Unregistered people carried everything. Registered people carried almost nothing, because the city carried it for them—their names in the rolls, their births in the parish books, their deeds and debts and deaths all stacked in the Hall of Records like coins in a vault. That was what registration meant, really. It meant other people kept your weight for you.

Nobody was keeping Hazel's. Nobody had ever kept it. Which was why the bag existed, and why the bag was always ready, and why the sound of wheels on the bridge went through Hazel's chest like a crack through ice—because if the record would not hold you, then your own two shoulders had to.

* * *

Below, in the shop, Hazel worked on the Hollis regulator and tried to be only a clockmaker.

This was the trick, the one survival demanded: to make yourself small enough to pass through a clerk's eye without catching. Hands busy, head down, answers short. When customers came to the counter, Hazel became furniture—an assistant, a shadow, *the boy* or *the girl* depending on what the customer expected and could tolerate, because correcting people was a luxury and Hazel had never once been able to afford it. Corvin called it good sense. What it actually was, Hazel thought, winding the regulator's barrel with slow, even turns, was a kind of escapement. Let the world's attention tick toward you, and release it, tick and release, never letting the gear train wind up so tight that someone looked twice.

The regulator's pendulum swung its slow golden arc. Hazel loved regulators best of all clocks—their honesty. A regulator did not perform. It simply refused to lie.

Through the front window, across the rooftops, Hazel could just see it: the observatory hill, and the tower rising from it, and the dark round bulk of the master clock's face beneath its housing, motionless. Everyone in Bellhaven could see it. The city's official time lived up there—or had lived, until eleven days ago, when the great clock had stopped between one minute and the next, and the bells of the harbor churches, which took their noon from the observatory, had begun drifting apart like quarrelsome brothers.

Officially, the stoppage was under investigation. Unofficially, the coffee houses had decided it was an omen. Everything was an omen lately. The old Margrave was dying or dead—the government said recovering, which everyone understood to mean dying slowly and lying quickly. Across the river and across the country, the men who would come after him were sharpening their historians the way other revolutions sharpened knives, and everyone in Bellhaven, even the ones who could not read, had learned to smell paper drying.

Paper was coming. Paper in enormous quantities. New registries, new censuses, new clean and corrected records in which the past would be tidied like a workshop before an inspection.

And here were the wagons already, on the bridge, taking names ahead of schedule. Getting ready for the new paper by sweeping up whatever the old paper had missed.

Hazel's file scraped a pivot, tiny strokes, breath held. Small enough to pass. Small enough to pass. Tick, and release.

At midmorning the door chimed and Marisol's cousin's boy came in with a delivery of brass stock, and stood at the counter breathing hard, and said, too loud, "They've come off the bridge. The wagons. They're working the Wharf District this morning, house to house. My mam says the clerk's got a list already, printed, and she says—" he glanced at Corvin, lowered his voice—"she says they're not just writing down who's there. They're writing down who *shouldn't* be."

Corvin waved the boy off with a curse about rumor-mongers, but his hands, checking the invoice, had gone very still.

Hazel finished the pivot. Set down the file. Wiped it, wrapped it, and placed it in the oiled roll with the others, because a tool in a drawer was a tool and a tool in a wrap was a decision.

There was a version of this morning in which Hazel finished the Hollis regulator, ate supper, slept, and woke to do it again—that version had lasted seven years and had always, secretly, been rented month to month. Hazel could feel the rent coming due the way one feels weather in a bad tooth.

They climbed back up to the back room and packed the satchel properly at last. Tools against the frame. Clothes rolled tight. Oil, bread, soap, pencil, blank pages. The indenture, in an inner pocket where a hand could find it fast. Last, the watch, flannel-wrapped, eleven minutes past four, still ending.

The bag closed with a click like a well-seated case back. Nine pounds. A whole life, portable, invisible, ready.

Hazel shrugged it on and stood a moment in the gray light, feeling the straps settle, feeling the familiar terrible comfort of it: nothing to lose because nothing owned you, no name on any roll, no grave anywhere holding your people, no address the paper could follow. Free, in the way a dropped coin is free, falling.

Then, below, the shop bell rang—and rang again, impatiently, and Corvin's voice rose in surprise, and then another voice answered it, flat and official and carrying up through the floorboards like cold through stone: a voice reading aloud from a document, dated, sealed, addressed.

"—respecting the master clock of the Bellhaven Observatory, stopped these eleven days—"

Hazel stood very still, nine pounds of life on their back, and listened to official paper climb the stairs toward them.
