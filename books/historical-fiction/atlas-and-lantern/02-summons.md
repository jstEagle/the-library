## 2. Summons

The knock came at the wrong hour—half past nine in the morning, when honest workshops were already deafening and only people with nothing to hide worked in silence.

Hazel froze at the bench, tweezers suspended over the disassembled heart of a mantel clock, and listened the way other people prayed. Below, through the floorboards, came the sound of Master Idris's boots crossing the shop, unhurried, and then the scrape of the bolt. Hazel breathed again. A customer, then. Customers knocked like they were owed something. This one had knocked twice, evenly spaced, like a man reading from a manual.

*City business,* said a voice below, thin and official and terribly pleased with itself. *Clerk of the Second Ward. Is this the establishment of Idris Voss, horologist?*

Hazel set down the tweezers with the exaggerated care of someone who did not trust their own hands. In the mirror of the polished brass plate leaning against the wall—a scrap Idris used for checking escapements—Hazel could see their own face doing what it always did when authority arrived: going smooth, going blank, going nowhere. It was a skill like any other. It had to be practiced.

They had been practicing since they were six years old.

*Second Ward,* Idris called back, *that's us. Come in before you let the heat out.*

Boots on the stairs. Two sets—one heavy and clerical, one that was Idris's familiar uneven tread. And behind them, faintly, a third sound that made Hazel's stomach drop through the floor and into the cellar: the wet, rhythmic slap of a rubber stamp being tested against a palm. Hazel knew that sound. Everyone unregistered knew that sound. It was the sound of a name being decided about.

Hazel stood very still among the hanging pendulums as the door swung open.

The clerk was a narrow man with the colorless competence of boiled linen. His coat was municipal gray, his satchel was municipal gray-brown, and he carried himself with the particular stiffness of someone whose importance existed entirely on paper. Behind him hovered a boy of perhaps twelve, a junior copyist clutching an umbrella though the sky was clear, and behind *them*, visible through the open door at the mouth of Copper Lane, sat something that stopped Hazel's breath for half a second: a wagon.

Not a goods wagon. A census wagon—the long green kind with the slatted sides and the folding steps, parked at the head of Marrow Bridge with two men beside it wearing armbands and expressions of mild, professional boredom. The morning fog thinned around them and closed again. They were checking papers. Even at this distance Hazel could see the ritual of it: hand outstretched, paper surrendered, paper examined, paper returned—or not returned.

*Master Voss.* The clerk produced a leather folio and opened it on Idris's bench without asking permission, which was its own kind of signature. *Corven, Clerk of the Second Ward, Bureau of Enumeration and Residence. You'll forgive the intrusion. I'm making rounds.*

*Rounds,* Idris repeated, in the flat tone he reserved for customers who wanted to haggle over repairs to heirlooms they clearly hated. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with forearms like ship rope and a face that gave away nothing it didn't choose to give away, which was most of why Hazel had survived three years under his roof. He had never once asked Hazel a question he didn't need the answer to.

*The city maintains records of all licensed trades,* Corven said. *Apprenticeships among them. I'm verifying your register.*

*It's by the till.*

The junior copyist scurried to fetch it while Corven's eyes traveled the workshop with the slow, inventorying patience of a man who counted things for a living—not just clocks but coats on pegs, cups on shelves, beds behind curtains. One bed behind the curtain, Hazel noted. Their own pallet up in the loft was already made flat as a shelf, blanket folded to military neatness, because a bed that looked slept-in was a statement and statements got people asked questions. There were three apprentices registered to Idris Voss. Three beds would have been expected. Hazel's sleeping arrangements had been engineered with more care than most of the clocks in the room.

The clerk found what he expected to find, apparently, because his attention moved on to the folio itself.

Hazel went back to the mantel clock. Going back to work was important; work was invisibility, and invisibility was oxygen. But their hands, those hands everyone praised, those fine and famous hands—those hands were shaking slightly, and Hazel watched them shake with a strange detachment, as if from across the room.

The stamp came down twice on the register. Thud. Thud. The sound of names being confirmed. Somewhere in a government office there now existed fresh ink attesting that Idris Voss employed exactly the boys he claimed to employ, no more, no fewer.

*Good. Good.* Corven closed the folio, and Hazel allowed themselves one full breath.

And then the clerk turned and looked directly at them.

It lasted maybe two seconds. Maybe less. It was nothing—a glance, a flicker, the idle sweep of a man cataloguing his surroundings—and yet Hazel felt it land on them like a hand on the shoulder. Because Corven's gaze did not slide past the way strangers' gazes always slid past, trained by years of civic politeness to see apprentices as scenery. It *paused*. It took in the brown skin and the tight dark curls and the mixed face that Bellhaven's better families described, when they described it at all, as *unfortunate features*, and it paused the way a bookkeeper pauses at a column that doesn't sum.

Then Corven smiled—a small, correct smile—and looked away, and asked Idris a question that rearranged Hazel's entire future.

*One more matter. Unrelated.* He drew a second document from the folio, this one on heavier paper, cream-colored, with the observatory's seal at its head. *You'll have heard the tower clock has stopped.*

*Whole city's heard it,* Idris said. *Half my customers mentioned it before they mentioned their watches. That's how you know it's serious.*

*Serious indeed. It's the master clock, Master Voss. The standard. Every regulated timepiece in Bellhaven is set against it—the rail schedules, the tide tables, the court sessions, the factory whistles. It stopped Tuesday night at eleven minutes past four. The Observatory Commission wants it running before the historians arrive, and they want the repair performed by a licensed practitioner, documented and receipted.* He laid the paper on the bench and smoothed it flat with two fingers. *Your name came up.*

*My name.* Idris's voice did not change at all, which was how Hazel knew he was alarmed. *I haven't touched that clock in twenty years. Old Aldous kept it like a widow keeps a grave, and after him—" He stopped. Started again. *After him nobody. The commission's been arguing over the contract for a season.*

*The commission argues; the Bureau acts.* Corven's tone suggested this was an old and tired joke. *I'm authorized to offer the work at schedule rates to a qualified house. Frankly, Master Voss, yours was not the first name considered. But the first house wanted triple rates and the second is under review by the Ward Bureau, so here we are.* He permitted himself the ghost of distaste. *You are, I'm told, the cheapest available option.*

There was a silence in which Hazel could hear everything: the tick of forty clocks disagreeing politely about the hour, the fog dripping off the eaves into Copper Lane, and underneath both, faint as a hairline crack, the sound of a life changing shape.

Because Hazel knew—Irdis knew, the whole workshop knew—that the Bureau did not send clerks out at half past nine in the morning to offer work to the cheapest available option. The Bureau sent clerks because a list existed somewhere, and a name had been moved up it or down it, and moving names was what the Bureau *was*. An official order was a leash with a job attached. Take it, and every morning thereafter you reported somewhere the Bureau could find you, in a tower visible from every street in the city, working on the single most public machine in Bellhaven, with your face and your unfortunate features and your absent paperwork on display to whatever commission, delegation, or census happened to climb the nine hundred steps.

Refuse it, and the refusal went into the folio too. *Declined work offered by the Bureau.* Refusals accumulated. Refusals were how lists got shorter.

*Schedule rates,* Idris said slowly.

*Plus materials. Plus a completion bonus, if it's running within the fortnight.* Corven slid the order another inch across the bench, toward Idris's hands, like a card dealer who already knew the game. *The Bureau would consider prompt acceptance a mark of good standing.*

*Good standing.* Idris picked up the paper. Read it once fast, once slow. Hazel watched his thumb, which pressed hard against the cream stock, whitening at the nail. *I'd need to send someone up today regardless. Assess the damage. My journeyman's laid up with a broken wrist—*

*Was he? How unfortunate,* said Corven, who did not care, and Idris's jaw shifted almost imperceptibly, and Hazel understood in a cold dropping moment that Idris was improvising, that the broken wrist was a story being built in real time, brick by careful brick, for a reason Hazel couldn't yet see.

*—so it'd be my youngest apprentice. Hazel.* Idris said it without looking up. *Good hands. Best I've got, if we're speaking plainly, and you seem like a plain-speaking man. Small enough to get inside the movement housing besides. Aldous always said that clock was built for smaller bodies than ours.*

Corven made a note. Hazel watched the pencil move and felt the world tilt, because the pencil was writing *Hazel*—just the given name, surely, surely Idris hadn't given a surname—and a surname was the one thing Hazel did not have that other people had, the hole in the paper where a person should be. *Okafor* had been buried twice over: once in the flood winter, once in the record books that had never contained it. What remained was *Hazel*, apprentice, unattached, a name light enough to carry and small enough to hide.

But the pencil kept moving. *Age?*

*Sixteen.*

*Registered?*

The word dropped into the workshop like a coin down a well, and every clock in the room seemed to hold its tick to listen.

Idris did not hesitate—he was too good a liar for hesitation—but Hazel saw the effort of it in his shoulders, in the way his hand settled flat on the bench like a man bracing against weather. *Under my bond,* he said. *Workshop-registered. I don't put boys on the city rolls until they're out of indenture; saves them the head tax and saves me the filing. You know how it is, Clerk. Paper breeds.*

*It does,* Corven agreed mildly. *That's rather the point of it.*

He wrote something else—briefly, unhurriedly—and Hazel, watching upside down, caught only the shape of it: a small bracket drawn beside a word, the kind of notation clerks used to mean *see also*. Then Corven blotted the page, folded the order into duplicate, and held out the top copy to Idris with both hands and a bow of precise depth.

*The commission expects assessment by end of day tomorrow and daily progress reports thereafter. Your apprentice can present this at the tower gate. The keeper's household will admit him.* A pause, fractional, deliberate. *Her. Them. Whichever the register says.*

*Register says Hazel,* Idris said evenly.

*Then the register is what I'll write.* Corven gathered his folio, and his junior copied him, and at the door he turned back with the expression of a man remembering a courtesy. *One thing more, purely social. You may have noticed the wagons on Marrow Bridge. Routine verification ahead of the historians' arrival. Nothing for licensed houses to concern themselves with—your register is in order, I've stamped it myself. But the city does ask employers to remind their people to carry their papers. All their papers.* He smiled the small correct smile again, at Hazel this time, directly, unmistakably, and it was somehow worse than the stare had been. *It saves such trouble later. Good morning.*

The door closed. The bolt scraped. The footsteps faded down Copper Lane, and the wagon creaked away over Marrow Bridge, and the fog rolled back into the space where the city's attention had been.

Hazel did not realize they were still holding the tweezers until Idris pried them out of their fingers.

*Sit down,* Idris said, *before you fall down,* and pushed a stool over with his foot, and Hazel sat, and for a long moment neither of them spoke. The forty clocks ticked their disagreement. Upstairs, through the ceiling, the fog-light on the harbor horn groaned once and went quiet.

*He knew,* Hazel said finally. It wasn't what they'd meant to say first. *The bracket. He wrote a bracket next to my name.*

*He wrote a lot of things.* Idris lowered himself onto his own stool with the care of a much older man. He rubbed his face with both hands, and for a moment he looked his age and heavier than his age, and Hazel understood with a lurch that the fear in the room belonged to both of them and had been sitting between them the whole time, unserved, like a third cup of tea. *Listen to me. Listen.* He waited until Hazel's eyes came up. *I could refuse this. Tell Corven my boy's sick, tell him anything. And in a week a different clerk comes with different paper, and asks why Voss's house declines Bureau work, and asks it with the wagons parked outside. Refusing marks you blacker than accepting. That's the design of it. They build the trap so both doors are doors.*

*So I take the trap.*

*You take the work.* Idris's voice was low and rough. *You go up that tower, and you look at their stopped clock, and you fix what's fixable, and you watch everything, and you tell me everything. A stopped master clock isn't maintenance, girl-boy—it's a message. Somebody stopped it. Clocks like that die of neglect slowly, gear by gear, years by years, not all at once at eleven minutes past four on a Tuesday. And whoever stopped it, the Bureau either doesn't know or doesn't mind, and I don't know which frightens me more.* He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment he was not Hazel's master but somebody's—somebody who had learned things in long years of keeping other people's time. *But understand this too, because I'll only say it once. Nine hundred steps up, above every roof in Bellhaven, there's a room with the city's truest clock in it, and the Bureau just handed you a signed paper telling you to walk in and touch it. Men have killed for less than that paper. I've spent three years teaching you to be invisible, and I'm not sorry for one day of it—invisible is alive—but invisible is also small, Hazel, and you were never meant to be small. So yes. Trap and opportunity. Same envelope. That's how this city delivers everything worth having.*

Hazel looked down at the order lying on the bench between them, cream-colored, official, dangerous. At the bottom, beneath the observatory seal, the Bureau's stamp had dried to a dull maroon, and beside it Corven's small bracketed notation sat like a splinter working its way toward the skin.

Present this at the tower gate.

*I'll need the good loupe,* Hazel said at last, and their voice came out steadier than they had any right to expect. *And the long-reach pliers. And I'll want to leave before the bridge gets busy—there were men with armbands on it, and I'd rather not walk past them twice.*

Idris studied them for a moment, and something moved behind his face that might have been pride, or grief, or both wearing each other's coats.

*Pack your satchel, then,* he said. *And Hazel—when you're up there, in all that height and quiet, and you find yourself thinking you've finally gotten free of small rooms and smaller names—* he stood, and began turning off the gas lamps along the bench, one by one, his shadow growing enormous on the wall, *—remember that the higher you stand, the more of the city can see you standing.*

The nine hundred steps began, in a sense, right there: satchel open on the bench, tools chosen one by one, each bright weight settled into its loop. But at the very bottom of the bag, wrapped in flannel at the last moment, Hazel placed the thing that was neither tool nor paper nor name: their mother's watch, stopped these twelve years, ticking nowhere.

Outside, the fog was lifting off the river, and the observatory tower stood above it all with its dead white face, waiting to be read.

* * *

The tower gate opened before Hazel finished knocking.
