## 3. Nine Hundred Steps

The order was folded into Hazel's satchel like something that might bite through the leather.

They read it again at the foot of Observatory Hill, in the gray light that came before the sun decided whether Bellhaven deserved one. *By direction of the Office of Records and Census: the master clock of the Bellhaven Observatory having ceased its function, the apprentice herein named is commanded to present themselves for the purpose of restoration.* The seal at the bottom had been pressed so hard the wax had cracked. Someone had wanted it official. Someone had wanted it very official.

Hazel had never climbed Observatory Hill. Nobody unregistered did, not because there was a law against it—there were few enough laws left to break—but because hills like this belonged to people whose names appeared on things. The road switchbacked up through bare lindens, and from each turn Hazel could see more of the city laid out below: the river running gray-green under its lid of ice, the bridges, the market roofs furred with frost, and there, small as a stitch, the workshop where Master Idris was probably already awake and already annoyed that his cheapest apprentice had been borrowed by the city.

The observatory sat at the top like a stone thought. A squat round base, a tower rising from it, and above that a dome of green copper gone white along the seams with salt and age. The tower's windows were dark. Of course they were dark. The clock had stopped; everyone in Bellhaven knew the clock had stopped, had felt it stop the way you feel a silence when a loud neighbor goes away. Three days now without the noon gun answering the clock's strike, without the harbor bells taking their cue. The city was telling itself time by guesswork and argument.

Nine hundred steps. That number was on the work order too, in a clerk's cramped hand, as though the city counted even the way up to its own sky.

Hazel counted anyway. It was what hands and heads did when left alone together.

The first hundred steps wound inside the base of the tower, a spiral stair worn concave in its centers by two hundred years of feet. Hazel noted that the wear was deepest near the wall—a clockmaker's staircase, then, built for people climbing with tools in their right hands—and filed the observation away the way other people filed away coins. By step three hundred the windows had climbed above the rooftops, and the light came through them in cold blue rectangles that Hazel walked through one after another, a string of doors made of winter. By five hundred their breath was loud in the shaft, and by seven hundred they had stopped counting honestly and started estimating, which any honest craftsman would tell you is where all counting ends.

At eight hundred and forty, or somewhere near it, the stair ended at a door of banded iron with no lock that Hazel could see—only a latch shaped like a lever escapement, a little joke in brass, and beside it a bell pull worn bright.

Hazel pulled. Somewhere above, a bell spoke once, thin and clear, and did not echo, because the tower swallowed echoes the way deep water swallows stones.

Nothing happened for long enough that Hazel began composing the report they would send down the hill: *arrived, knocked, no answer, recommend the city hire someone whose existence it admits to.* Then bolts began to move behind the iron—not one bolt but several, drawn back in sequence with the deliberate music of a mechanism that had been designed to make visitors wait—and the door opened on a chain, six inches of gap, and a pair of gray eyes in it.

"You're late," said the eyes.

"The order said morning."

"The order said morning four days ago. The clock stopped four days ago." The door closed. The bolts ran home again, one, two, three, and Hazel stood on the landing feeling precisely the size the city considered them, which was slightly smaller than nobody. Then the bolts came back, all at once, as if the first opening had merely been a test of patience, and the door swung wide.

"Wren Aldous," she said, as if handing over a document. "Keeper's daughter. You're the clockmaker?"

"Apprentice." Hazel held up the work order. "Hazel."

"Just Hazel?"

"Just Hazel."

Wren Aldous looked at the paper longer than the name. She was perhaps twenty-three, with ink stains on her fingers in the specific pattern of someone who wrote all day and slept badly—stains on the side of the right middle finger, a smudge along the thumb's heel where a page gets braced. Her hair was tied back with what appeared to be a length of clock weight cord. She wore a man's coat two sizes too large and an expression that had been assembled for guarding things.

"Come up, then," she said. "Mind the third landing. The rail's honest but the stair isn't."

* * *

The last sixty steps opened into the clock room, and Hazel stopped walking the way people stop in church.

They had known, in the abstract, what the master clock was. Every apprentice knew: it was the great machine, the mother mechanism, the thing from which all lesser time in Bellhaven descended. But knowing a thing and standing under it were different countries. The movement filled the room the way a heart fills a chest—brass wheels taller than Hazel, steel arbors thick as a man's wrist, four weights hanging in their channels like slow bronze rain that had been falling for two centuries and would fall for two more if given leave. The great dial was above and outside, visible through a glazed aperture where the tower's face would be, and the morning came through it sideways, lighting the dust in the air into a slow golden snowfall that never touched anything.

Everything was clean. That was the second thing Hazel noticed, after awe. In Idris's workshop cleanliness meant fear of the master; here it meant love. The brass gleamed without being garish. The oiled surfaces shone like still water. Somebody had spent a life wiping the prints of their own devotion off this machine so that only the machine would show.

And all of it—every wheel, every weight, every beautiful tooth of every gear—was still.

Not resting. Hazel knew rest in machinery, the loose-shouldered quiet of a thing set down honestly. This was different. This was the stillness of a throat that had been gripped.

"Well?" Wren said. "You're the expert. Tell me it's broken."

Hazel didn't answer yet. They had taken off their coat without deciding to, an old habit—the body going to work before the mind finished being afraid—and they moved along the movement's flank the way you'd approach a hurt animal, hands open, eyes low. The going barrel. The great wheel. The train, wheel by wheel, counting teeth out of pure reflex. Everything in its place, everything correct, everything

There.

At the escapement.

Hazel crouched, brought their eye level with it, and went very still, and after a moment Wren came and crouched too, because she had watched their stillness and understood it was louder than any exclamation.

An escapement, Hazel could have told her, if she'd asked—which she didn't, she just watched—is the whole argument of a clock in one small component. Wheels can store power; springs can hold it; but power doesn't know time. Only the escapement knows time. It is the component that says *no*, over and over, the patient refusal that turns a fall into a measure, letting the wheel escape one tooth at a time, tick, and tock, and tick. Break an escapement and you don't have a stopped clock. You have a stopped idea.

This escapement had not broken. Its pallets were sound, its arbor true, its jeweled bearings clean. But the locking stone—the small block of hardened steel that catches the tooth and holds the whole world between ticks—that stone had been lifted from its seat, turned a quarter-turn, and reseated. To anyone looking, it sat where it had always sat. But it held nothing. The escapement clicked empty, air against air. The weights hung in their channels with two hundred years of descent still coiled in them, waiting for a permission that had been quietly, surgically withdrawn.

Hazel took out their smallest driver, fitted it to the stone's screw, and turned. A quarter-turn back. The stone dropped into its true seat with a sound too small to be called a click—more a decision—and above them, through four floors of accumulated machinery, something vast let out its breath.

The great wheel moved. The train woke wheel by wheel, a shiver running up through brass and steel like spring coming up through ice, and the tick arrived—deep, deliberate, enormous, a sound Hazel felt in their sternum before they heard it in their ears—and then the tock, and the clock of Bellhaven, official keeper of every hour the city owned, resumed its argument with eternity exactly where it had left off.

Hazel sat back on their heels. Their hands were steady. They noticed this with detached interest, the way you notice weather in someone else's country.

"That's it?" Wren said. "That's all it was? One quarter-turn?"

"That's all it takes, if you know what you're doing." Hazel wiped the driver and put it away with exaggerated care. "It wasn't wear. It wasn't fatigue. Nothing failed. Someone turned the locking stone and set it so the damage wouldn't show unless you got down here and looked at the escapement the way I just did." They heard their own voice go flat, the way it did over ruined work. "Your father's clock wasn't broken, Miss Aldous. It was sabotaged."

The word landed in the room and lay there among the clean brass.

"No," Wren said. Not *I don't believe you*. Just *no*, the way you say no to weather, to news, to a thing arriving that you have been expecting in another shape.

"Who has keys to this room?" Hazel asked.

"My father. Me. The two assistants who left when the funding was cut." She stood, and up close her composure showed its stitching: the red around her eyes, the coat sleeves wrung at the cuffs. "The city holds a master key to the outer door. For inspection."

"And the stair door?"

"There's no key. It's mine since he died." She said *died* the way she'd said everything else, flat and forward-facing, a woman carrying a load balanced so carefully that any acknowledgment of its weight would spill it. "Four days ago I heard nothing. That's the thing. Whoever did this was in this room while I slept forty feet below, and I heard nothing, and either they are very good or—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "Why would anyone want the clock stopped?"

Hazel stood too, and looked up through the golden dust at the great wheels turning now overhead, tireless, obedient, and found that the wonder had not left the room—it had only curdled, the way milk curdles, same substance, new nature. Because they knew why, in the place where apprentices who carried forged papers kept such knowledge. Everyone in Bellhaven knew why, if they were honest, and honesty was the one tool nobody stocked.

The clock was official. That was the whole of it. Every ship's log, every debt called due, every curfew, every contract, every court date, every recorded birth and death in Bellhaven drew its authority downward from these turning wheels. Stop the clock and time itself became unofficial—became rumor, became negotiable, became whatever the new men arriving with their ledgers and their soldiers said it had been. You couldn't forge the past while the present was still striking it hour by hour. But stop the present, even for a few days—

"They wanted a gap," Hazel said slowly, thinking it aloud because Wren's eyes demanded thinking aloud. "Days where the official record simply... doesn't exist. No hours struck. Nothing to contradict whatever gets written afterward about what happened during them." They looked at their own hands, small against the enormity of the machine. "My master always said a clock is the most honest thing anyone ever builds. It can't lie about duration. It can only be silenced."

Wren was quiet for the space of eleven ticks. Hazel counted. It was what they did instead of breathing.

"My father used to say something like that," she said finally. "He said the clock was the city's conscience, and consciences get killed before witnesses do." She turned toward the spiral of the stair, and her voice changed registers, dropping into something administrative and armored. "You'll come back tomorrow. And the day after. The escapement needs a full teardown—I want every component checked for a quarter-turn's worth of cleverness, and I don't trust anyone the city sends twice, except that I have no one else, so it will be you."

"I'm an apprentice."

"You fixed in nine minutes what the city's inspectors spent four days failing to notice was even fixable." She had the chain in her hand already, working the door. "Tomorrow. Early. Bring your own oil; the city's is gutter render and lies."

Hazel hesitated on the threshold, the work order crackling faintly in their pocket like a live coal. Going up this hill had been dangerous paper made flesh. Coming back would be worse. Coming back meant becoming a person the census might think to ask about—a clockmaker with no surname on file, climbing daily toward the highest, most visible point in Bellhaven, mending the very officialness that men with wagons on the bridge had come to erase.

But below them, through the tower's windows, the bells of the harbor were taking up the hour, one after another, calling to each other across the frozen city like a family checking voices in the night—and the master clock above Hazel's head was striking, deep and sure, the count that gave them all their cue.

Seven. Eight. Nine.

"Miss Aldous," Hazel said, at the door. "When your father died—who found him?"

The question was none of their business. They knew it was none of their business even as it left their mouth, drawn out by the same instinct that had bent them over the escapement: something seated wrong, a quarter-turn off, invisible until you got low and looked.

Wren Aldous's face did not change. That was how Hazel knew the question had gone all the way down.

"He was found at the bottom of these stairs," she said. "Nine hundred steps. The city called it a fall." She began drawing the bolts home, one, two, three, her voice coming through the closing gap as level as a pendulum. "Come early tomorrow, apprentice. Bring your tools. And bring your own paper—the kind you write on, not the kind you carry. We're going to keep a record of our own."

The door shut. The bolts ran home.

On the landing, in the cold blue light of the eighth-hundredth step, Hazel stood very still and listened to the clock strike ten above them, official and restored, and understood that they had just been hired by something considerably older and more dangerous than the city.
