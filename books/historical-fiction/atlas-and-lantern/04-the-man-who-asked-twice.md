## 4. The Man Who Asked Twice

Silas Crane rose before the gulls did.

He had a theory about that hour—the blue-grey sliver between night and morning when the river fog still owned the streets and the lamps along Cordwainer's Wharf burned down to orange coins in their glass. In his experience, people were most honest at that hour, or least guarded, which amounted to the same thing. The mind that had not yet dressed itself in its daily defenses answered questions the way a sleeper answers: plainly, and without artifice. By noon, everyone lied a little. By evening, they lied well.

So Crane walked early. He carried his ledger under one arm, wrapped in oilcloth against the fog, and a leather folio of forms beneath his coat, and he wore his good grey coat because the census was an office of the state and the state ought to look respectable even at half past five in the morning, knocking on the doors of dyers and rope-walkers and clockmakers' apprentices.

The bridge was already occupied. He could see the census wagons drawn up at the far end, their canvas sides stenciled with the new seal—the crossed quill and key—and the clerks inside them warming their hands over braziers, waiting for the day's lists. Crane did not ride in the wagons. He considered them a mistake of theater. A wagon announced itself; it made people close their shutters and count their children before he ever reached the step. He preferred to arrive alone, polite, with a clipboard and a soft voice, the way weather arrives.

He paused at the center of the bridge and looked down at the ice. It had gone grey-green overnight, thick enough now that boys would be testing it by week's end, and beyond it the observatory stood on its hill like a finger raised in permanent correction. The master clock's face was pale in the fog, and—Crane checked out of habit, as he checked everything—the hands had not moved since the notice came through that it had stopped. Nine days frozen at eleven minutes to four. There was something in that which displeased him, though he could not yet have said what. A stopped official clock was an administrative problem. But it was also, he thought, a kind of statement, and statements interested him more than problems.

He went on into the lower town.

* * *

The first household on his list was Ferro's workshop, metalworkers, seven persons resident according to the tax rolls. Old Ferro himself opened the door with a bar of red iron still in his tongs, took in Crane's coat and clipboard, and said, "Again?"

"Routine verification," Crane said pleasantly. "The new registry requires confirmation of particulars. You'll remember I visited in the autumn."

"I remember you."

"Good. That saves time." Crane opened his ledger to the page marked with a neat paper flag. "Seven persons resident in autumn. Yourself; your wife; your sons Dario and Kep; your daughter Luce; your journeyman Anselm; and a boy listed only as 'shop apprentice.' Is the shop apprentice still with you?"

Ferro's jaw worked. Iron tongs are heavy things, and Crane watched the man decide, visibly, not to do anything foolish with them. "Still with me."

"His name?"

"Ferro. He's my sister's boy."

"Ah." Crane turned back two pages with one finger, unhurried, and read aloud, gently, as if reminding an old man of a pleasant afternoon: "In October you told me the apprentice was named Tammas and came from the salt villages. In November, when I asked your wife, she said he was called Pell, no village given. This morning you say Ferro, your sister's son." He closed the ledger. "You'll appreciate that a registry must choose among these. And whichever we choose, the other two become falsehoods on the record. Falsehoods have consequences now, Master Ferro. They didn't used to. That's the change, and it isn't mine—I only write it down."

The forge ticked behind them. Somewhere upstairs a child coughed.

"He's nobody," Ferro said at last, low. "No papers. His mother died in the flood winter and nobody registered him because—" He stopped.

"Because unregistered children were cheaper than registered ones," Crane said. "Yes. I know how this city has worked. I'm not here to punish you for it, Master Ferro. I'm here to make it impossible to keep working that way, which is different, though I understand it feels worse." He drew a form from his folio and laid it flat on the anvil, weighting the corners with scrap. "Name, birth date so far as anyone knows it, mother's name if known. Fill it in tonight. If the boy is entered now, voluntarily, he becomes a correction of past oversight—not a discovered evasion. There is a difference. It matters. It will matter more in six months."

Ferro looked at the form the way men look at snakes. But he moved the red iron away from the door, which Crane had privately noted as the true answer to the interview.

"Thank you for your candor," Crane said, and touched his hat brim, and wrote three small words in the margin of his own ledger before he left: *cooperative, frightened, honest.*

That was the trade, when it went well. Fear traded for a name. He had made perhaps four hundred such trades since the autumn, walking the lower town in his good grey coat, and he did not think of himself as cruel. Cruelty was inefficient. What he practiced was closer to bookkeeping—a discipline of noticing, of writing things twice and comparing, until the truth of a city lay open on his desk the way the guts of a watch lie open on a jeweler's bench. People resented him, and would resent him more. He had accepted that the way a man accepts the weather. Somebody had to know who actually lived in Bellhaven. For forty years the city had preferred not to know, and the not-knowing had been profitable for some and fatal for others, and the dead had left no records at all, which was the whole trouble.

The new regime wanted the record clean. Clean meant complete, first, and then—Crane had read the directives closely, closer perhaps than the men who drafted them—it meant tidy. Complete and tidy are different virtues. He suspected, in a part of his mind he kept locked and inventoried like everything else, that the second virtue would eventually be asked to eat the first. He did not let this suspicion slow his walking.

* * *

By mid-morning the fog had lifted off the lower town and the sun lay flat and white on the rooftops, and Crane was three households further into his list and one cup of bad coffee deeper into himself. He interviewed the widow Quennel about a lodger who had, by her account, "moved downstream"; he noted that her account had moved the lodger upstream in September and downstream in December, and flagged the page. He interviewed a rope-walk foreman whose answers were identical across three visits to the word—identical, Crane noted, *to the word*, which was its own species of signal, since human memory never repeats itself exactly unless it has been rehearsed. He wrote in the margin: *drilled. By whom?*

It was past noon when he came to the clockmaker's.

Idris Okafor's workshop sat where Cordwainer's Lane bent toward the water, a narrow brick face with a bow window full of watches, each pendulum swinging at its own small disagreement with every other. The brass plate read OKAFOR — CLOCKS & WATCHES, REPAIRED, and beneath it someone had scratched, years ago and shallowly, a second O that had never been sanded out. Crane had noticed the scratch on his first visit and had thought about it more than was probably warranted. Names that leave ghosts of themselves in signage were, in his professional experience, rarely entirely honest signs.

Master Idris received him in the shop proper, among the ticking. He was a broad, stooped man with burn-scarred fingers and the bloodshot patience of someone who spends his life leaning over small failures. On Crane's previous visits he had been courteous in a closed way, answering precisely what was asked, volunteering nothing, which Crane had recorded without resentment: *evasive by profession, not by policy.* A man who repairs clocks for a living understands that every mechanism can be made to say what you want if you know where to press. Crane respected that and distrusted it equally.

"Master Idris. Silas Crane, census office. Routine verification."

"You've verified me twice."

"The registry is new. New things verify often." Crane opened the ledger. "Household of one in September. Yourself. Household of one again in November." He paused, delicately, the way a man pauses before lifting a lid. "And yet in October, in this very shop, in front of two witnesses, you referred to 'my apprentice' regarding a repair on the Harrow Street turret piece. An apprentice does not appear in either census."

The ticking filled the pause. Eleven small clocks disagreed politely with one another, and behind the partition, where the bench work was done, Crane heard—or believed he heard, which in his trade amounted to the same thing—a single held breath.

"Terminology," Idris said. "People say 'my apprentice' for any boy runs errands. I send a lad for parts sometimes. Casual labor. Day work. You don't register a day's wages."

"No," Crane agreed mildly. "One registers a person." He kept his eyes on the ledger, not on Idris's face, because watching the face made people perform and watching the hands made them fidget, and the best information lived somewhere else entirely—in the rhythm of the answers, in what was offered too quickly. "Describe him, then. Your casual lad. For the margin note. Height, coloring, approximate age."

"Why?"

"Because margins are where cities keep their truths, Master Idris. The main text is where they keep their intentions."

He hadn't expected an answer and didn't get one. Idris looked at the middle distance with the expression of a man listening to an escapement he doesn't trust, and said, "I don't recall particulars. Boys blur."

"Boys blur," Crane repeated, agreeably, and wrote it down, and the writing was the point. *Boys blur* was a door, and doors once written stay written.

He asked his remaining questions—fuel stores, lodgers, dependents, all confirmed unchanged—and thanked Idris with genuine courtesy, and stepped out into the cold bright lane. Then he stood a moment under the bow window, ostensibly adjusting his glove, and looked through the glass at the reflection of the shop interior, the trick old shopkeepers never quite learned to defend against. In the reversed room, past Idris's shoulder, the partition door stood open a hand's width, and on the bench beyond it there were two mugs of tea going cold, and a stool pulled up to the vise at working height, and a half-disassembled movement laid out on a cloth with the tools set down in the particular disciplined crescent that only one pair of hands uses and returns to.

One mug could be a customer's. One stool could be anyone's.

But tool discipline like that belonged to a trained hand, and trained hands in Bellhaven were rare enough to count.

* * *

Back at the census office—a requisitioned counting house that still smelled faintly of the wool contracts it used to hold—Crane worked until the lamps needed trimming. The great ledgers lay open around him like a city flattened into columns, and he moved through them with the contentment other men found in strong drink. Cross-referencing was his sacrament. Tax rolls against parish books; parish books against guild registries; guild registries against his own autumn and winter censuses; and everywhere the discrepancies surfacing like fish rising in a pond, each one a person pretending, each pretense a location.

He had a private taxonomy of discrepancies. Some were clerical—transposed digits, dead men still taxed. Some were economic—servants counted as kin, kin counted as servants, depending on which list cost less. Some were griefs: widows who kept reporting husbands alive because a dead husband's ration card fed a hidden son. Crane catalogued those without comment and without mercy, but also without contempt. He had long ago concluded that nearly all lying in Bellhaven was load-bearing. Remove a lie carelessly and the house above it fell on your head.

It was near dusk when he came to the loose sheet in his own field notes—the shop apprentice of Cordwainer's Lane, mentioned once in October, denied twice on paper, present in reflections and tea mugs. He had begun, out of habit, drafting a marginal dossier on the boy, and now he assembled it formally, laying the fragments side by side.

In October, at the Harrow Street turret job, the site carpenter had mentioned "the Okafor boy"—small, dark-skinned, quick with a file. Crane had noted it and thought nothing further; half the apprentices in the lower town were dark-skinned and half of those were unofficial, the city's old arithmetic of who got paper and who got work being what it was.

In November, interviewing the chandler two doors down from Idris's shop, Crane had asked, in passing, whether the clockmaker took apprentices. The chandler had said: "Used to see one come and go. Quiet thing. Went by Hazle or Hazel or some such—never caught a family name. Not that there'd be one to catch." And had laughed at his own joke, and Crane had smiled back, because smiling back was how the next sentence got bought.

Two mentions. Thin. Thin enough that most clerks would have filed it under *unverified rumor* and moved on to solid ground.

But Crane did not deal in solid ground. He dealt in shifts. And now, cross-checking his November notes against the harbor master's flood-year rolls—which he had obtained through channels he did not document—he found the third fragment, and the third fragment stopped his pen.

Among the unclaimed effects from the flood winter, entered and never retrieved, there was a line: *one lady's watch, silver, engraved, recovered near the Lantern Steps; held pending claim.* No claim in nine years. And beside it, in the harbor clerk's cramped hand, a note made years after the fact: *boy came asking after this once, winter following. Told him effects sealed. Gave nothing. Small dark lad, said his name was H.*

Not a name. An initial. A boy who had come for his dead parents' watch and, asked for a name, had offered only the letter it began with—that was not ignorance. That was training. Someone had taught that child, early and thoroughly, that a name given is a name recorded, and a name recorded can be found.

Crane sat back in his chair. The lamp fluttered. Around him the office ticked with the sound of the wall clock Idris Okafor had built forty years ago and the city had never paid fully for, and Crane listened to it the way he imagined the clockmaker listened to everything: for the one tooth in the wheel that wasn't right.

Here was the shape of it, assembling itself in the margins of his mind with the cool pleasure of a completed sum. A clockmaker with no apprentice on any record, who used the word *apprentice* in public and *boys blur* on paper. A workshop with two teacups. A boy seen by neighbors, named partially by a chandler, partially by a carpenter, and once—only once, nine years ago—by himself, as an initial, standing in the harbor office asking after a dead woman's watch.

And one thread more. He pulled it now, slowly, because it was the finest and therefore the most important. The flood winter. The drowned district. The unregistered dead, whom no agency had ever bothered to enumerate because enumeration implied acknowledgment and acknowledgment implied fault. Crane had spent a week in the old municipal scraps on that winter—requisition orders, charity disbursements, a gravedigger's informal tally sold to him for the price of a coat—and in none of it, nowhere, did the name *Okafor* appear attached to the district's dead. Which was strange, because Idris Okafor was fifty-some years in Bellhaven and the only Okafors on any roll at all were himself and, distantly, a brother lost to the river country decades back.

A famous name, absent from a famous disaster. Absence, in Crane's experience, was never empty. It was full of whatever someone had paid to remove.

He dipped his pen. On a fresh sheet, in his small exact hand, he drew a box, and in the box he wrote:

*Okafor (unreg.), given name Hazel or H.—apprentice to I. Okafor, Cordwainer's Lane, unofficial. Age est. 16–18. Origins: flood winter, Lantern Steps district. Parents unrecorded/unregistered—verify. Habits: silent, skilled, careful. Caution: taught to withhold name since childhood—likely coached. Significance: link between I. Okafor and flood-winter irregularities. Priority: high.*

Then, below, because he was honest with himself in ways he permitted no one else to see, he added a final line:

*Note: subject has survived nine years by being unwritten. Handle accordingly. Do not spook.*

He blotted the page, squared it, and placed it in the folio that traveled home with him rather than into the office files, because files were read by superiors and superiors were read by historians, and it was not yet time for this page to exist above his own head. Then he trimmed the lamp and took down the master-list of the lower town and ran his finger down the column of unresolved entries until it stopped, as he knew it would, at the item he had flagged in autumn and never closed.

*Observatory, Mount Aldous. Keeper deceased. Daughter resident. Maintenance staff of record: none. Yet the tower consumes coal, oil, and bread at the rate of a household of four.*

A household of four in a building licensed for one. A stopped official clock. A hidden boy learning a hidden trade in a hidden room. Crane closed the ledger and pressed his palms flat upon its cover, feeling the faint warmth of the day's work rising out of the paper like heat from a beast.

Somewhere up on the hill, behind the pale frozen face of the master clock, his unfinished arithmetic was living and eating and winding springs, and it did not know his name yet. That asymmetry pleased him—it was, in fact, the entire pleasure of his profession—and he intended to enjoy it a few days longer.

Tomorrow, he decided, he would call on the observatory. Officially, this time. There were maintenance logs to request.

He put out the lamp.
