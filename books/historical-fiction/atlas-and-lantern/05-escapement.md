## 5. Escapement

The clock's heart hung open before Hazel like a wound that had been dressed by someone who didn't want it to heal.

They had worked until the tower light turned amber, then grey, then blue-black, and Wren had gone down at some point to argue with Marisol about lamps and came back with one, and a heel of bread, and an oil flask that smelled of nothing like clock oil. Hazel ate without noticing they were eating. That was how Master Idris knew they were working well—when the body forgot itself, he said, the hands remembered everything.

*The hands remember everything.* He'd said it a hundred times over the bench, tapping his own knuckles against the mahogany. Hazel had always taken it as workshop wisdom, the kind of thing old men say to make their trade sound like scripture. Now, alone in the dark under the stopped face of Bellhaven's official time, they understood it differently. The hands remembered what the mind was forbidden to hold.

The escapement lay in pieces on a cloth Hazel had folded into quarters, then eighths, each part aligned like words in a sentence waiting to be read back.

Someone had done this carefully.

That was the thing that would not leave them. A broken clock broke honestly—a mainspring snaps, a pivot wears, a bushing seizes, and the failure has the sloppy generosity of nature. But this. This had been *arranged*. The pallet stones had not cracked; they had been lifted out of their seats with pliers small enough to fit a lady's sewing kit, and set aside, and the retaining screws replaced so that a casual eye would see a mechanism merely worn past its years. The escape wheel's teeth were whole. The arbor was true. Only the small, patient geometry that lets a spring's fury escape one tick at a time had been subtracted, the way you subtract a single word from a contract to change its meaning entirely.

Hazel sat back on their heels and looked up through the skeleton of the great movement, at the dark underside of the dial where the numerals would be, if there were light enough to see them—vast reversed letters, the shadows of hours.

Somebody had wanted this clock to stop being right.

Not broken. Bellhaven could tolerate a broken clock; broken things got repaired or replaced, and either way someone new touched them, someone new looked inside. But a clock that was *wrong*—that kept a time slightly its own, drifting minute by minute into private territory—that was different. A city's official timekeeper was not really about time. Hazel had understood this only dimly before, in the way apprentices understand the economics of their masters: the observatory bell told the ferries when to run, the markets when to open, the trains when to be scheduled, the courts when to swear their oaths. Every stamped document in every clerk's office drew its authority from this descent of gears. If this clock lied, everything calibrated against it lied too, gently, unarguably.

Hazel picked up the first pallet stone. It sat in their palm, a chip of synthetic ruby no bigger than a grain of barley, worth less than a loaf of bread and responsible for all of it.

*Who takes a word out of a sentence?* Idris again, from some evening years ago, holding a pocket watch whose case bore scratches like claw marks. *Only someone who knows exactly what the sentence says.*

Hazel had asked who'd do such a thing. Idris had looked out the workshop window for a long moment, at the census office across the canal, and had said nothing, and had charged the man double for the repair, and Hazel had thought, at twelve, that this was simply justice.

Now they were seventeen, and the sentence in front of them had lost its word, and their job—the job the city had paid three silver for, cheapest available apprentice, paper and all—was to put the word back.

They worked.

It is difficult to explain to anyone who has not done it what it is like to restore an escapement. There is the locking: the exact instant when the wheel tooth catches the pallet face, and you feel it through the tweezers as a tiny refusal, a *no* that lasts a hundredth of a second. There is the draw, the angle that pulls the pallet in rather than letting it fall out, so that gravity and vibration conspire to keep the mechanism honest instead of shaking it apart. And there is the drop—equal on both sides, entry and exit, or the clock will tick unevenly, and an uneven tick is a confession audible to any trained ear in the room.

Hazel adjusted the banking pins by lamplight, breathing shallow, and found the drop was off by nearly nothing, and corrected it, and felt the mechanism come alive under their fingers like a sleeper waking—not all at once but in stages, first a looseness, then a willingness, then something that could only be called attention.

They let the train run down slowly, listening.

*Tick. Tick. Tick.*

Even as a heartbeat. Even as anything.

Hazel sat in the dark and listened to official time resume in the tower above Bellhaven, and felt—for one full minute, which they counted precisely because counting was the only prayer they knew—an enormous and complicated relief, the kind that comes after fear has been sitting on your chest so long you've stopped noticing its weight.

Then they noticed the draft.

* * *

There should not have been a draft. The clock room was sealed—Wren had shown them the shutters herself, dogged down against the weather, because a change of temperature moves brass and steel by fractions of a millimeter and a public timekeeper cannot afford fractions. The air in here was supposed to sit as still as the air in a jar.

But it moved. Coldly. Against the left side of Hazel's neck, below the ear, steady as breath.

They turned the lamp.

Behind the great wheel work, where the case met the wall of the tower, the draft came from a seam. That was all it appeared to be at first—one of the vertical joints between the clock's oak casing and the stone, filled with decades of dust and the grease of age. Hazel ran a thumbnail along it the way they'd run a thumbnail along a caseback to find the hidden lip of a snap bezel, and the joint gave—not outward, as a badly fitted panel gives, but *inward*, with the soft mechanical reluctance of something made to move this way and rarely asked.

A door. A door balanced on a pivot hinge, weighted to close itself, and so precisely fitted that the paint and grime of thirty years had sealed it into invisibility.

Hazel stood very still for a long moment.

Every instinct they owned—the ones built by seventeen years of being unregistered, undocumented, a name spoken only indoors—told them to leave it. To close the seam, finish the job, take the three silver, descend the nine hundred steps, and never mention the draft to anyone, least of all the keeper's intense grey-eyed daughter who guarded this stopped clock like a tomb and looked at Hazel as though she were deciding whether to hand them something heavy.

But the door was already open a hand's width. And behind it, where the tower's solid stone should have continued, there was a smell. Old paper. Candle tallow. The particular dry sweetness of ink long dried in a closed space.

Hazel took the lamp and went through.

* * *

The room was smaller than the clock it hid inside—no more than six feet square, carved out of the tower's thickness directly behind the dial, so that the backs of the numerals formed three of its four walls. In daylight, Hazel thought dimly, the numbers would have thrown their shapes across everything in here. At night, under the lamp, the room was all shelves.

Shelves, and books.

Not books, quite. Ledgers. Dozens of them, bound in whatever binding had been cheap and available—sailcloth, tarred canvas, split leather, boards from packing crates—each spine marked in a small, upright hand: a year. Hazel read the years going down the shelf and felt the floor tilt gently under them, because the earliest went back further than they had been alive, further than the flood, further than the war scare, back to a Bellhaven that existed only in the oldest men's complaints. Forty years of ledgers. Forty years of somebody coming up nine hundred steps—had the stairs even been the same, then?—with a lamp and a pen and whatever the day had given him.

The newest ledger lay open on a lectern bolted to the wall, as though the writer had intended to return.

Hazel did not touch it at first. They stood in the doorway and let the room introduce itself, because that was what you did with any mechanism before you put a tool to it—you observed. You watched for motion. You learned what it wanted.

The pages were ruled in columns, but the columns were not money. Or not only money.

*Born — 14th of the Frost Month, to Nella Voss, laundress, Canal Street. Unregistered. Mother asks it be written anyway.*

*Debt — Tomik Brall, ropewalk, owes the Harbormaster's fund 11 silver, sold downstream to clear it. His wife was not told the destination. She has come twice.*

*Died — the Aldous infant, winter quarter. Registered nowhere. Buried by neighbors above the high-water line.*

Page after page. Year after year. Names in the small upright hand, and beside many of them, in a second hand—smaller, harder, added later—corrections, confirmations, the little marginal notes of a record arguing with itself to stay accurate. Some entries were crossed out and rewritten, which meant the writer had cared about being wrong. Anyone can write a thing down once. Only a keeper rewrites.

Hazel turned page after page, faster now, the way you climb stairs two at a time when you are almost home, though they could not have said home to what.

Births. Debts. Deaths. Marriages the state would not perform, recorded here with witnesses' names. Apprenticeships without papers, attested by the master's mark. People sold downstream, people drowned, people arrived on boats with nothing and were entered anyway, entered *deliberately*, in ink, in a room behind the face of the city's official clock—as though the keeper had decided that if the state would not count these lives, then time itself would have to stand witness. Official time and true time. Hazel heard the phrase arrive in their head fully formed, in Wren's voice, though Wren had not yet said it to them.

And then they reached the last written page, and stopped.

The handwriting was the same small upright hand, but slower here. Tired. The date was the winter of the flood—the winter Hazel's entire life had pivoted around without ever appearing in any record, the winter the river came into the low district and took the houses and the boats and, when it receded, took the count of who had lived there too, because the flooded registry office's books were ruined, and the government declared the loss unrecoverable, and the unrecoverable became the nonexistent.

The entry read:

*Okafor, Hazel. Born in the winter of the flood.*

Nothing else. No hour. No weight. No father's trade, no mother's name, none of the columns the other entries filled. Just the name, and the fact, and beneath it—in that second, smaller hand, pressed hard enough into the paper that Hazel could feel the groove of every letter with a fingertip, the way you read a signature through a sealed letter—

*A. They survived. Both parents. Written so it stays true even if the city forgets.*

Hazel sat down on the floor. Not deliberately; the legs simply ended their arrangement. The lamp rocked and steadied in their grip and threw the numeral-shadows swinging across the shelves, across forty years of names, across the open book with their own name in it, written by a stranger, years ago, before Hazel could write their own name, before Hazel knew it needed writing.

The vertigo came in slow waves. It was not fear, exactly. It was the sensation Hazel had once known watching a master balance a wheel so perfectly that it spun without visible cause, hanging in its own stillness—it was the loss of the fixed point. All their life they had organized the world around a simple load-bearing assumption: *nobody knows*. Nobody knows what you are, nobody has your number, nobody holds your paper. The satchel packed by the door. The forged apprenticeship folded soft from handling. Safety lived in the gap between Hazel and the record, and Hazel had kept that gap swept and polished as lovingly as any pivot.

And the gap had never existed. For their entire life, in a stone room behind the face of the city, there had been a line of ink with their name on it. Someone had climbed nine hundred steps in the winter the river ate the low district, and had written down that a child named Okafor had been born, and had added—pressing hard, as if against argument—that the child survived.

*Written so it stays true even if the city forgets.*

Hazel pressed both palms flat against the cold stone floor and breathed until the waves subsided. Being known, it turned out, felt almost exactly like being hunted. Same quickened pulse, same narrowed vision, same animal conviction that the world had gotten too close. But underneath the sameness there was a difference, and the difference was everything: hunted, you are known by what wants to erase you. Known like this—

Known like this, you are kept.

Footsteps on the stairs. Far below, faint, but climbing. Hazel knew the rhythm already, after barely two days—Wren climbed the way she did everything, evenly, without hurry, as though the steps would still be there if she took them one at a time and she intended to prove it.

Hazel looked at the open ledger, at their own name, and understood with sudden clarity that Wren had known this room existed. Had perhaps been waiting, all this while Hazel cleaned and measured and repaired, for Hazel to find it—or had been afraid they would. The draft, the door, the room behind the numbers: none of it had been hidden from Hazel. It had been hidden from everyone else.

The footsteps rose. Hazel stayed where they were, on the floor of the secret, their name open on the lectern before them, and did not decide whether to hide the discovery or confess it, because some part of them had already decided, days ago, on a bridge crowded with census wagons, when they had chosen to carry the summons instead of burning it.

The stair door opened, and Wren's voice came ahead of her, level and quiet:

"I wondered how long it would take you to notice the draft."
