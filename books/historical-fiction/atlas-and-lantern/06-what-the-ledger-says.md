## 6. What the Ledger Says

*Hazel*

The room behind the clock's face was smaller than the satchel of fear Hazel had carried up nine hundred steps. That was the first thing they noticed—that all the dread of discovery, all the years of folding themselves smaller, had taken up more space inside them than this room did. Four walls of shelved books. A desk with a lamp, its oil still good. And ledgers. Rows and rows of them, bound in the gray leather of official record-keeping, except these were not official. These were the spines the state would never stamp.

Wren stood in the doorway behind them, holding the shuttered lantern, letting Hazel look without explaining. She had that way about her, Hazel was learning—the patience of someone who'd grown up waiting for a clock to be ready to speak.

"Go on," she said quietly. "You've already opened it."

Hazel's fingers hovered over the nearest volume. Twenty years a clockmaker's apprentice, and their hands had never shaken at a piece of work. They shook now.

The ledger opened to a page dated eleven years before Hazel's birth. Not births, not deaths—at least not first. Names, written in a fine, upright hand: *Adeyemi, Sera. Laundress. Unregistered. Husband registered as 'servant, foreign.' Two children, unrecorded.* Below it, in the same hand, a second line, smaller: *Baptized at the river chapel by Father Anselm, who keeps no book of his own. Someone should.*

"Someone should," Hazel read aloud, not meaning to.

"My father's motto," Wren said from the doorway. "He said it the way other men say grace."

* * *

*Wren*

Her father had begun the ledger the winter he took over the observatory, when he was twenty-six and still believed the census was merely inefficient rather than purposeful. He told her the story so many times she could recite it like scripture, which she supposed it was.

A woman had climbed the tower to ask the time—people did, then, when the master clock ran—and she had asked him to write down the hour of her daughter's birth because no clerk in Bellhaven would put a mixed-heritage child on any roll. The girl would grow up unable to inherit, unable to hold license, unable to testify in her own defense. But she would exist somewhere, if a man with ink and authority would bend low enough to do it.

Elias Aldous had bent. Then he had kept bending for thirty-one years, until the bending killed him.

"He didn't think of it as rebellion at first," Wren said now, watching Hazel's hands move across the pages the way they'd moved across the escapement that morning—careful, reverent, reading the mechanism of it. "He thought of it as maintenance. The government keeps one kind of time. Somebody has to keep the other kind, or the city drifts."

Hazel looked up. "Drifts."

"His word. A clock that runs only what its owner wants it to say isn't a clock. It's a lie with hands." She crossed the little room and pulled down a volume from the fourth shelf, thicker than the rest, its corners bumped from handling. "This is the one you want. This covers the flood year."

She saw it happen to Hazel then—the stillness. Not calm. The stillness of an escapement locked, holding all that force in check because letting it go all at once would break something. Hazel set the ledger on the desk as if it were made of ice and opened it to the marker Wren had left there days ago, when she'd guessed who might come climbing her stairs.

*Okafor, Adaeze. Okafor, Chidi.* Their parents' names, written in her father's upright hand, dated the autumn before the winter of the flood. Beneath them: *Married at the river chapel. Witnesses: the Adeyemi household, three souls. Registered nowhere. Recorded here.*

And below that, in fresher ink, added months later: *A child. Born in the winter of the flood. They named her—*

*Hazel.*

*Hazel*

Their own name. Written before they could write it themselves. Written by a man they had never met, in a room they had never known existed, above a clock that had measured out every hour of their life without once being wrong about them.

Hazel had prepared, over the years, for many kinds of discovery. Discovery as arrest. Discovery as the census wagon on the bridge. Discovery meant erasure; they had built their whole posture around that assumption, the way a wall is built against the prevailing wind. No one had ever told them that discovery could mean this: standing in a cold room reading proof that they had been *counted*, faithfully, tenderly, by a stranger, before they were old enough to know they needed counting.

"I don't understand," they said, and their voice came out strange. "Why would he—your father never knew us. My parents were nobody. Laundress and a—" They stopped. Their father's trade was in the ledger too, two lines up. *Chidi Okafor, repairer of small mechanisms, unlicensed.* A repairer of small mechanisms. All those years Hazel had believed the talent came from nowhere, from accident, from the random kindness of gears.

"He knew everyone," Wren said. "That was his work, same as the clock. You keep a mechanism, you have to know every part of it, including the parts the manufacturer pretends aren't there." She touched the page, below the entry. There was more. *The Okafor family lost in the flood ice, the 14th of Deepwinter. The child survived, carried to the east bank by—* the name was smudged, water-damaged or worn— *who would not give hers. Entered in the parish roll as foundling, no name. Entered here as: Okafor, Hazel. Because a name that exists only in an official roll can be taken away by the next clerk. This one is mine to keep.*

Hazel sat down. There was no chair; they sat on the floor, back against the shelf, the open ledger on their knees, and they did not cry, quite, but something in their chest turned over with the slow heavy click of a great wheel taking up slack in a train that had run loose for sixteen years.

"Somebody wrote me down," they said finally.

"Yes."

"Before I could write myself."

"Yes."

"And he kept it." Hazel looked up at Wren, and their eyes were dry and terrible and young. "For sixteen years. He didn't lose it, didn't sell it, didn't trade it for safety. Why?"

*Wren*

Because he promised, she almost said. Because it was Tuesday and he was alive and the ink was on the desk. But the true answer deserved better than the easy one, and Wren had learned from him that answers, like escapements, should release only what they can bear to.

"Come here," she said, and knelt beside Hazel, and turned back through the volume—not to the beginning, but to the pages near it. "Read me a few. Any of them."

Hazel read. *Vasquez, Tomas, debtor, sold downstream to the cane farms, spring of the flood year. His wife remains. She brings bread to the north gate on Sundays and looks at the observatory.* And another: *Ilori, Bess. Mixed registry refused twice by the clerk's office. Third refusal witnessed. Her sons call themselves 'Gray' now, after the clerk who refused her, out of spite. Good boys anyway.* And another, and another, decades of them, the erased and the unsold and the unsoldable, the families too tangled for the census categories, the dead whose graves the city would not mark, the debts that traveled downstream in chains while the debtors' names stayed home.

"He recorded everything the government refused to be burdened by," Wren said. "Births it wouldn't certify. Deaths it wouldn't mourn. Debts it profited from keeping invisible." She closed the volume gently on Hazel's knees. "And every year the book got bigger, and every year he understood better what it was. By the end he knew exactly what it was. He stopped climbing the tower some nights to work on it instead. He said—" and here her voice did something Hazel had not heard in it before, a hairline fracture running through the composure—"he said the clock tells the city what time it is, but the ledger tells the city *what it has done*. One of those is a convenience. The other is a debt. And debts, Hazel, are the one thing Bellhaven has never once paid."

The lamp hissed softly. Somewhere below them the stopped clock hung in its silence like a held breath.

"He died keeping it," Hazel said. Not a question.

"He died refusing to tell where it was." Wren let that sit. "They questioned him for three days. Officially, heart failure. He was sixty-one and healthy as a bridge cable. Afterward they sealed this room behind the clock face, and whoever sabotaged the movement assumed the seal would die with the mechanism. Nobody comes into a dead clock. Nobody counts the parts of a thing the city has stopped believing in." She smiled, and there was no warmth in it. "That was his last design. He made the ledger's lock out of the city's indifference."

*Hazel*

Hazel stayed on the floor a long while, turning pages. The grief came, when it came, not as a wave but as a reading—as though the ledger itself were telling them, entry by entry, what their life had been assembled from. A mother who married at a river chapel with three witnesses. A father who repaired small mechanisms without a license, which was to say: illegally, which was to say: bravely. A birth attended by floodwater and entered into the only record in Bellhaven that told the truth. And a stranger, climbing down from this tower year after year, carrying all of it in gray leather volumes, alone, until it killed him.

*I was never alone,* Hazel thought, and the thought was vertigo. *I was carried. I just wasn't told.*

"I have spent my whole life measuring what I could carry," they said aloud, without deciding to. "One satchel. Everything I am fits in one satchel, and even that's too much if the wrong wagon stops me." They looked around the room, at the shelves, at the hundreds of lives bound and shelved and dusted and kept. "Your father carried all of this. Every day. How?"

"He didn't carry it," Wren said. She took the lantern and held it so its light fell across the whole wall of spines. "He *kept* it. There's a difference. Carrying is what you do when you expect to run. Keeping is what you do when you've decided you belong somewhere." She looked at Hazel steadily. "You climbed nine hundred steps today expecting to fix a clock and leave. You packed accordingly. I watched you do the arithmetic on my stairs—satchel, exits, whether I was a danger. I know that arithmetic. I grew up doing it in reverse."

"In reverse?"

"Wondering who would climb up someday needing what we keep." She set the lantern on the desk. "My father waited fifteen years for your name to walk back up those steps. He died not knowing if it ever would. And here you are, sitting on the floor of his life's work with your bag packed."

Hazel looked at the satchel where they'd dropped it by the hidden door. Tools. A broken watch. A forged apprenticeship paper that had cost half a month's wages and their last shred of ease. The whole inventory of a person designed to leave no wake.

"The census taker is in the city," they said. "Crane. He's been through the workshop district twice. My master says he asks questions twice, compares answers." They heard themselves say it and understood, with a lurch, that they were warning her—her, and Marisol, and whatever else lived under this roof that the wagons might want. "If he finds this room—"

"He'll burn it," Wren said simply. "Or worse. He'll catalog it, and file it, and use it to finish what the flood started. Erasure doesn't need fire, Hazel. It needs paperwork." She rose and offered her hand, and after a moment Hazel took it, and she pulled them up out of the ledger's shadow. "Which is why you're going to help me decide something. Not tonight. Tonight you'll eat—Marisol will insist, and Marisol is not a woman one refuses on an empty stomach. But soon. Because there's a question my father never had to answer, and now it's ours."

"What question?"

Wren looked at the shelves—all those names, all those kept debts—and her jaw set in a way that made her suddenly, unmistakably, her father's daughter.

"Whether a record exists to be hidden," she said, "or to be brought down off this mountain and *read*. He hid it because hiding was possible. I'm not sure it is anymore. The historians are coming when the ice breaks—everyone knows it, whatever they pretend. And when they come, they'll bring new rolls, clean ones, and every name in this room that isn't somewhere in *their* ink by then..." She didn't finish it. She didn't need to. Hazel had spent a lifetime on the far end of that sentence.

They looked down at the open ledger one last time before following her out. Their parents' names. Their own name, in a stranger's careful hand, kept like a promise through sixteen winters.

*Someone should,* the first entry had said. Someone had. Now the someone was them, or could be—but the choosing of it felt, already, like the moment an escapement is set: everything coiled and waiting, and no way to know, until it releases, whether the clock will keep the truth or shatter trying.

Above them, through the clock's silent face, the stars wheeled on, keeping their own indifferent count. Down in the city, on the bridge, a lantern moved slowly from door to door, and stopped, and asked its quiet question twice.

Somewhere in the workshop district, Master Idris was lighting his lamp over an empty bench, and tomorrow Hazel would have to walk back down those nine hundred steps and explain where they had been all day—or explain nothing, which was harder.

Wren banked the lamp and locked the hidden door behind them, and the two of them descended together through the dark of the tower, the ledger's weight distributed between them in more ways than one.
