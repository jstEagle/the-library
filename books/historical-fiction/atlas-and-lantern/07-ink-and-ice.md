## 7. Ink and Ice

Wren Aldous had learned to read her father's silences the way other children learned to read weather. A certain set of his shoulders meant rain coming to the ledger room's stone walls; a particular hum in his throat as he climbed meant good news from the city below, or at least survivable news. And on the last night she ever saw him alive, he had hummed nothing at all—had come up from below with ink on his fingers and a look she could not name then and names perfectly now: the look of a man who has decided what he is willing to die for and is only waiting to see if anyone will make him prove it.

She thought of that look every time she opened the ledger room door.

The room sat behind the clock's face like a heart inside a chest, and it was cold in a way the rest of the tower never managed—not draft-cold but held cold, the cold of stone that had given up on warmth decades ago. Wren lit the lamp she kept there, trimmed its wick, and waited for the light to find the shelves. Nine ledgers stood in a row, spines dated in her father's small square hand, and beside them the loose bundles, the folded affidavits, the scraps of testimony people had pressed into his hands over thirty years like coins into an offering box.

Her father had been keeper of the master clock. That was what his salary said. That was what the city's records said, insofar as the city kept any record of him at all. But Aldous the elder had understood something early—so early that Wren, sorting his papers after his death, could never find the moment of decision, only the accumulated evidence of it—that a clock does not merely tell time. It certifies it. An official timekeeper makes official everything measured against it: the hour a child was born, the hour a debt came due, the hour a man was hanged. Whoever keeps the clock keeps the truth of every timestamp in Bellhaven.

And the government of Bellhaven had been lying about time for a generation.

* * *

Hazel Okafor came up the tower steps at the seventh hour, punctual now in a way they had not been their first day, when they had arrived gray-faced with dread and smelling faintly of lamp oil and fear. Wren heard them before she saw them—the particular rhythm of their climb, quicker on the landings, careful on the worn treads. She had begun, without deciding to, to know that rhythm from all others.

"You're early," she said, not turning from the escapement bench.

"You're talking to yourself again." Hazel hung their satchel on the peg by the door—the peg Wren had installed two days ago without commenting on it, and which Hazel had accepted the same way, both of them pretending this arrangement was temporary even as it grew bones. "I do it too. Marisol says clockmakers talk to themselves because we're the only ones who listen."

"Marisol," Wren said, "has been listening to Piotr argue with Jupiter for twenty years. Her standards for conversation are very low."

That got the almost-smile out of them, the one that came and went like a fish rising. Wren watched them settle at the bench, unroll their tools with those quick precise hands, and felt the thing she had been circling all morning come around to face her. She had rehearsed this. She had rehearsed it three times against the mirror in her father's old quarters and abandoned the rehearsal each time because she sounded like a recruiter, or worse, like her father—which was to say, like someone asking another person to carry something heavy and calling it an honor.

"Hazel. Before we start on the strike train—I want to show you something."

* * *

She took them through the hidden door and did not watch their face while the lamplight found the shelves. She had watched enough faces come into this room. Some went hungry-looking, the way people did at bread after famine. Some went afraid. Her father's face, in her memory of memory, had gone simply steady, the way a man's goes when he picks up a weight he intends to carry the rest of his life.

Hazel went still. Not frozen—Wren knew the difference; frozen people stopped breathing. Hazel breathed, slow and deep, and moved along the shelf with one fingertip hovering above the spines without touching them, the way you'd move past a stranger's belongings, or a shrine.

"This is what my father actually kept," Wren said. "Not the clock. The clock was the door he hid it behind." She drew the fifth ledger down and laid it open on the reading stand. "Births. Deaths. Debts. Sales—and I mean sales of people, Hazel, indentures sold downstream to the rice mills, with dates and prices and the names of buyers, because the city kept no record of any transaction involving an unregistered person. As far as Bellhaven is concerned, none of these people ever existed. No birth entered means no death can be entered. It's very tidy. You can erase anyone by refusing to write them down first."

Hazel's finger had stopped above the sixth spine. The winter of the flood. Their own entry lay a hundred pages into that volume—Wren had shown it to them once, briefly, and had seen what it did: the vertigo, the flinch, the hunger. Being known by a stranger's handwriting was not the same as being safe. It might have been more frightening.

"Why show me the whole room?" Hazel asked quietly. "You showed me my entry. That was kindness. This—" their hand moved at the shelves, at the bundles, at the thirty years of it— "this is something else."

It was. Wren made herself say it plainly.

"When the uprising comes."

"If," Hazel said.

"When," Wren said, because her father had taught her the river, and the river taught one thing above all others: ice that groans is ice that will break. "It will come, Hazel. Everyone in this city can feel it under their feet the way you feel the tower sway in wind. The guilds are done paying. The dockworkers are organized. The garrison hasn't been paid since midwinter and half of them are locals who won't fire on their cousins. When it comes, it will come fast, and whoever wins will arrive with historians."

"Historians," Hazel repeated, in the tone of someone handed a word that doesn't fit.

"That's what they call them. I've read the dispatches from Corvane—the new government publishes proclamations, and my father collected them the way he collected everything else. They speak constantly of historical rectification. Of correcting the administrative record inherited from the old regime. Do you understand what that means? It doesn't mean they'll write down what happened. It means they'll decide what the record is allowed to say, and then the record will say it, and in fifty years it will be the only thing anyone knows." She touched the ledger's page, the ink her father had ground himself because official ink faded and his did not. "A regime that erases people by not writing them down will be replaced by a regime that erases them by writing something else. My father understood that would happen. He used to say: the first lie is silence. The second lie wears a better coat."

Hazel had gone to the window slit, where you could see the river between the rooftops—a white road running south, solid-seeming, beautiful, treacherous. "Your father died keeping this."

"Yes."

"How?"

"The cough took him, officially. But he spent the last winter of his life copying in an unheated cellar because he wouldn't bring these volumes home where a census clerk might see lamplight burning late. He chose the cold over the risk. He caught the fever doing it." Wren heard her own voice go flat and was glad of it; flat was how she could carry it. "He knew the cost and he paid it in installments. That's what I'm asking you to understand. It isn't one grand sacrifice. It's a thousand small cold nights."

"What are you asking me, Wren?" Hazel turned from the window. In the lamplight their face was very young and very old at once, the way it went whenever the ledger was mentioned—as though the room aged them and the world outside de-aged them again, back and forth, until Hazel didn't know which age was real.

* * *

Here is what Wren had decided, in the sleepless arithmetic of the past week, and here is how she had decided to say it:

She needed help. That was true and it was also insufficient. She needed help that would stay—help that understood the work was not copying but custody, that a record only lived while someone tended it the way a clock was tended, wound and checked and corrected against the stars. She could hire a copyist off any street corner for coin. She could not hire what she actually needed, which was a second keeper. And keepers were not hired. Keepers were made, slowly, by shared weight, and the only honest way to begin making one was to tell the whole truth and let the other person choose.

But she was her father's daughter, and her father had asked no one to die for anything. He had asked people only to be recorded. So she dressed the request in the plainest clothing she owned.

"The ice breaks in six weeks," she said. "Maybe five. When it breaks, the river opens, the supply barges come up from the coast, and the historians arrive on them—you don't send an advance party into a city until its roads are open. Between now and then, every page in this room needs to exist somewhere the historians can't reach. Copies. Multiple copies, in different hands so no single script betrays them, hidden in places no one searches." She let that sit, and then she said the part she had rehearsed: "I can't pay much. The observatory's funding comes through the university, and the university answers to the current government, which means I can offer room, board, and Marisol's cooking, and wages of whatever's left after materials. It's apprentice terms. Worse than apprentice terms."

"You're offering me a job," Hazel said slowly.

"I'm offering you a job."

"Copying an illegal ledger. In the tower where the census taker's own office certified the clock you're hiding it behind."

"There's a certain safety in the lamp being brightest," Wren said. "My father called it keeping the fox's den swept."

Hazel looked at the shelf again—at the volumes, at the bundles tied in string, at the whole architecture of one man's stubbornness—and Wren saw them doing sums she couldn't see. Weighing the satchel life against the shelf life. What can be carried versus what can be kept.

"If I say yes," Hazel said, "I'm not an apprentice anymore. Apprentices get dismissed. This is—" they searched for the word and found it with visible reluctance— "complicity."

"It's inheritance," Wren said. "But complicity is what they'd call it, yes."

"And if Crane comes. If anyone comes."

"Then you're an apprentice repairing a clock, and I'm the keeper's daughter showing you her father's papers, which are, I'll remind you, mostly tide observations and grain-price notes. There are forty years of genuinely boring documents in this room and I will bury anything dangerous in boring so deep it would take a month of patient reading to find it." She paused. "Silas Crane doesn't do a month of patient reading. He does interviews. He asks questions and compares answers. He is a hunter of inconsistencies, not a reader of ledgers."

"You've met him."

"Once. He came to certify my father's death registration." Wren let a breath out. "He asked me twice whether my father had kept personal papers. Twice. The same question, phrased differently, separated by ten minutes of talk about the weather. He's not stupid, Hazel. He's worse—he's thorough. Which is why the copies need to exist before the ice breaks and not after. Once the historians arrive, the rules change. Right now we're hiding paper from a clerk. Afterward we'd be hiding history from an army."

Hazel walked the length of the shelf once more, slowly, the lamp shadow moving with them across the spines. Wren did not press. Pressing was for salespeople; she was offering a life's work, and a life's work had to be picked up freely or it would be put down at the first hard season.

"My parents' names are in there," Hazel said at last, not turning around. "Not just mine. Both of them. Where they were born, what my father's trade was, what they owed and to whom, the date they—" the sentence stopped, restarted. "There's a whole page about us. About people the city said never existed. And I've been carrying my whole life in a bag so it could fit through doors ahead of me, and you're telling me there's a room where I was never invisible at all. Just unread."

"Yes," Wren said softly.

Hazel was quiet a long moment. Down in the tower shaft, the stopped clock ticked nothing; the silence of it had become so familiar that Wren sometimes heard it as a sound, a held breath eight stories tall.

"All right," Hazel said, and turned, and their face had settled into something Wren recognized from the mirror on bad mornings and good ones alike—the keeper's look, the weight-accepted look, and it frightened her to see it on someone so young because she knew exactly what it cost. "I'll copy. On conditions."

Wren laughed despite herself. "Conditions."

"First: I choose which copies go in which clock cases. I know cases, I know which shipments get inspected and which don't, and Idris's old clients trust my packing." A pause crossed their face at Idris's name—there always was one now—and moved on. "Second: Marisol and Piotr should know what we're doing. Not everything. Enough that if soldiers come, nobody's surprise gets anyone killed. Surprise is what gets people hanged. Third—" Hazel's voice dropped, and they looked not at Wren but at the sixth ledger, the winter of the flood— "when this is over. However it ends. My parents' page gets copied first, and I keep one copy myself. Not to hide. To keep."

"To keep," Wren agreed, and found her chest tight, and did not examine why.

She pulled fresh paper from the drawer—her father's stock, heavy rag paper bought in reams a decade ago against exactly this need—and set the ink, and showed Hazel where the good lamps were, the ones that burned without flicker so the hand stayed true. And thus it began, without ceremony, the way the most important things begin: two people at a table in a cold room, a book open between them, six weeks on the calendar, and the river below holding its white load in patience, groaning sometimes in the night, growing thinner at the edges, keeping its own count of the days.

They worked until the lamp burned low. Somewhere past midnight Wren looked up from her quill and found Hazel asleep over the fifth volume, cheek on their forearm, ink on their knuckles, breathing slow, and the open page beneath them showing a column of names in her father's hand—people no government had counted, preserved anyway, waiting in the dark like seeds.

Wren trimmed the wick, covered Hazel's shoulders with her own coat, and copied on alone until dawn.

* * *

Three mornings later, the summons came up the tower stairs on the boots of a city messenger who did not wait for a reply. Wren broke the seal standing in the doorway, and read it twice, and felt the temperature of the whole winter change.

The Office of Census and Registration requested—in the language of requests that are orders—an inventory of the observatory's personnel, registered and otherwise, together with the master clock's maintenance logs, to be delivered within seven days to one S. Crane, Assistant Registrar.

Below, in a smaller hand, a line had been added as if as an afterthought: *including any personal papers of the late keeper.*

Wren looked down the nine hundred steps, toward the bridge, toward the offices where that meticulous man sat cross-indexing the city. Then she looked back into the warm-lamped dark behind the clock's face, where Hazel was already reaching for their tools.

"Hazel," she said. "We just lost our seven days."
