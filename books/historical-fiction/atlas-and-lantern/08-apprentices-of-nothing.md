## 8. Apprentices of Nothing

The notice was nailed to the workshop door with four nails, one at each corner, as though the paper itself might try to escape.

Hazel stood in the alley across from it for a long moment before crossing the street, the way one stands at the edge of cold water. The morning fog had burned off into a thin, dishonest sunshine that made everything look cleaner than it was—the cobbles, the shuttered windows of the chandlery next door, the black letters of the seal stamped diagonally across the door's seam.

*BY ORDER OF THE CENSUS COMMISSION OF BELLHAVEN*, the notice read. *PREMISES SEALED PENDING INQUIRY. OCCUPANTS TO PRESENT THEMSELVES FOR QUESTIONING AT THE BRIDGE OFFICE WITHIN THREE DAYS.*

Below that, in smaller type, a line that had been added by hand, the ink still sharp: *Master Idris Voss, proprietor, taken for questioning this date.*

Taken. As though he were an object. A misdelivered parcel. Hazel read the word three times and found it did not soften.

They crossed the street finally, because standing in an alley staring was itself a kind of confession, and pressed their palm flat against the door. The wood was cold. Through it, faintly, they could hear nothing at all—no rasp of file on brass, no muttering, no smell of oil and coal smoke leaking under the sill. The workshop had a smell the way a person has a voice, and its absence was louder than any sound.

Hazel had left that door three days ago carrying everything they owned, and had thought, walking up the hill toward the observatory with the satchel heavy between their shoulders, that they might never see it again—and that this would be a kind of freedom. They had not imagined seeing it again like this: alive behind their back, dead in front of them.

"Move along," said a voice, not unkindly. The fishwife from the corner stall, wrapping her hands in cloth against the chill. She wasn't looking at Hazel when she said it. She was looking at the middle distance, the way people did when they wanted to be seen giving a warning without being seen giving it. "Clerk came round twice already asking after the apprentice. Asking after you."

Hazel's stomach performed a slow, deliberate revolution.

"What did you tell him?"

"That I don't hold with gossip." She snapped her cloth straight. "Third time this month someone's asked me questions, girl—or boy, or whatever you are, no offense meant—I've stopped keeping track. Move along."

Hazel moved along.

* * *

They walked the long way, which was to say they walked without deciding to, feet choosing streets the mind hadn't approved. Down past the tannery row where the smell rose thick enough to hide in. Along the embankment where the river lay under its grey skin of ice, holding its breath, waiting for spring the way everyone in Bellhaven was waiting—for something to break, though nobody agreed on what.

The census wagons were still on the bridge. Hazel had seen them first from the tower window two days ago, two dark boxes of conveyance drawn up at the span's midpoint like growths on the city's throat. Now there were three. Clerks moved between them with ledgers under their arms, stopping pedestrians, and here and there Hazel saw the small theater of it played out again and again: a man doffing his cap, a woman producing papers from her bodice, a boy pointing—pointing—back down the street toward somewhere Hazel couldn't see.

Everyone had a name until someone asked for it twice.

Hazel pulled their collar up and thought about the forged apprenticeship paper riding in the bottom of the satchel, wrapped in oilcloth beside their mother's broken watch. *Idris Voss certifies that the bearer, having served three years at the bench, is competent in the repair of domestic movements.* Three years. It had been five, but five didn't fit the arithmetic of Idris's official story, and Idris had always said the trick to a good forgery wasn't the hand—it was the boringness. Documents got examined when they were interesting. Nobody questioned a paper that promised nothing.

And now Idris himself had been *taken for questioning*, and every boring lie he'd ever told on Hazel's behalf was sitting in that sealed building like a room full of clocks all ticking slightly out of time, waiting for someone to listen closely enough to hear it.

Hazel turned uphill toward the observatory and walked faster.

* * *

Wren Aldous opened the tower door before Hazel finished knocking, which meant she'd been watching from the window, which meant she'd been watching since dawn, which meant she knew.

"They took him," Hazel said. Not a question. News traveled downhill faster than water in this city.

"This morning. Two clerks and a soldier." Wren stepped back to let them in. She had ink on her fingers and shadows under her eyes, and she spoke the way she did everything—flatly, precisely, as if emotion were a gear that meshed badly with the rest of the mechanism and was best kept disengaged. "The ferryman saw it from the water. He came up to tell Marisol."

"The cook."

"You haven't met her yet. You will." Wren closed the door and shot the bolt, a small iron sound that Hazel was beginning to understand was the true bell of the observatory—not the clock, which was silent, but that bolt, drawn every evening, marking the hour when the household became a fortress. "Did they seal the shop?"

"Four nails."

"They're thorough." She said it without admiration, exactly. "Come up. There's bread."

"I'm not hungry."

"You are. You've been walking for an hour and your face is the color of the ledger paper. Come up anyway. Hunger doesn't care what else is happening, and neither should you. That's how they win—people stop eating, start floating, get picked off." She was already climbing. Hazel followed, because there was nowhere else in Bellhaven to follow anyone.

* * *

The kitchen occupied the tower's second landing, a low room built into the curve of the stone, warm from a stove that had clearly been fed with more devotion than economy. And there was Marisol.

She was perhaps fifty, broad as a barrel and just as sturdily built, with grey threading through black hair pinned up with what appeared to be a pencil and a bone knitting needle. She looked up from a pot of something that smelled of onions and paprika and took Hazel in with a single sweep—a head-to-toe inventory conducted so quickly and so completely that Hazel felt catalogued, weighed, and shelved, all before the woman said a word.

"So," Marisol said. "The ghost."

"I'm sorry?"

"The apprentice who doesn't exist. Wren told me about you." She jerked her chin toward a stool. "Sit. Eat. You can apologize to the onions later."

Hazel sat. A bowl arrived in front of them before they had fully committed to the stool, thick with stew and a heel of dark bread balanced on the rim like a raft. They ate, because Marisol was right about hunger—it didn't care—and because refusing food in this kitchen seemed roughly equivalent to refusing gravity.

Old Piotr sat in the corner by the stove's other side, a small man folded into a chair like a compass closed around its needle, his milky eyes aimed at the warmth rather than at anything in the room. He nodded once when Hazel came in, a greeting issued to the general direction of footsteps, and returned to whatever country he visited behind those eyes.

"Idris Voss," Marisol said, while Hazel ate. Not a question either. This household dealt in statements. "You worked for him how long?"

"Five years."

"And in five years he never registered you."

"He registered the work. Not the worker." Hazel turned the spoon over. "He said it was safer for both of us. He said—" They stopped. The sentence *he said he was protecting me* suddenly sounded different in this kitchen, in this tower, with Idris's name on a census notice nailed shut behind four nails.

Marisol watched them arrive at the edge of the thought and let them sit there. "He said a lot of things," she offered at last, mildly. "Men who keep other men's secrets usually say a lot of things. Eat your bread."

* * *

The afternoon went to work, because work was the only language Hazel had for grief and fear both. The escapement waited on the bench in the clock room, its pallets cleaned, its teeth inspected, the sabotage laid out in components like a confession nobody had signed. Hazel bent over it with Wren's father's tools—tools lent, not given, a distinction Wren had made very clear—and tried to lose themselves in the arithmetic of wheels.

It half worked. The hands wanted to move; the mind kept snagging.

Because here was the thing Hazel kept circling and could not land on: the timing. The master clock had been sabotaged—carefully, knowledgeably, by someone who understood escapements—which had produced an official summons, which had put Hazel—an unregistered apprentice whose existence existed only in a forged document and a sealed workshop—in the observatory, in the ledger room, touching the book with their parents' names in it. And now the workshop was sealed and Idris was taken, within days, as though the whole sequence had been gears within gears, one wheel driving the next.

Or as though the sabotage had been bait, and Hazel had walked into it, and the trap was closing at the pace of paperwork.

"You're thinking loudly," Wren said from across the room, where she was cleaning the great dial's glass with vinegar and newspaper. "I can hear it over the escapement."

"Does it strike you as strange? The order things happened?"

Wren set down the glass and considered. "Everything in this city strikes me as strange. Be specific."

"The clock stops. I'm sent for. Within a week my master is arrested and my home is sealed. If someone wanted to find out who I am—really find out—they couldn't have designed a better road. Every step I take to fix the clock walks me further into the record."

Wren was quiet for a moment. Then: "Or," she said, "the census is doing what censuses do—sweeping, indiscriminately, stupidly—and you're reading intention into weather." She resumed polishing. "My father used to say a clockmaker's disease is seeing design everywhere. Sometimes a tooth breaks because metal fatigues, not because God hates you."

"And sometimes someone files the tooth."

"Yes." She didn't look up. "Sometimes someone files the tooth. We'll know which it is when we know who benefits. That's the only test that's ever held."

Hazel bent back to the escapement. But the question stayed in the room with them, patient as dust.

* * *

Night came early in the tower, the way it does in stone buildings that drink the light. Marisol fed them again—Hazel protested, weakly, and was ignored with tremendous skill—and then the bolt was drawn, and the household arranged itself: Piotr to his chair and his private darkness, Marisol below, Wren to the ledger room with a lamp, copying, always copying, her pen scratching away at the hours like a small animal gnawing through a wall.

Which left Hazel the problem of where a person sleeps when they are technically employed and technically homeless.

There was a cot in the clock room, left from some earlier keeper's assistant, canvas over a rope frame, smelling of dust and camphor. Hazel lay down on it fully dressed, satchel beneath their hip with the strap wound twice around their wrist, and stared up into the dark where the great mechanism hung above them, silent, its weights at rest, its pendulum hanging dead as a hanged thing.

Sleep did not come so much as ambush them in patches, and between the patches came the thoughts, arriving in single file like clerks:

*Three days to present myself at the bridge office.* The notice had said occupants. Was an apprentice an occupant? Was a person who legally did not exist an occupant of anything? If they went, they walked into Crane's machinery with a forged paper in their pocket and a name that appeared on no roll anywhere. If they didn't go, the absence itself was a signature, an admission written in the shape of a missing body.

*Idris in a cell somewhere.* Hazel tried to feel the correct things—loyalty, worry, gratitude for five years of bench training and roof and the fiction of a name spoken daily in a safe room—and found instead a colder question moving underneath: *what will he say when they ask him about me?* Idris, who valued his license above almost everything, who had taught Hazel that the first rule of the bench was protect the movement, protect the case, protect yourself, in that order. What did a man like that say, tired and frightened, in a room with two clerks?

Hazel did not want to finish the thought. The thought finished itself anyway.

And underneath even that, quieter, the strangest thought of all, the one that had been humming in them since the ledger room like a struck wire that wouldn't still: *someone wrote my name down.* Before the state, before the census, before Crane and his wagons and his careful lists—somebody had dipped a pen and recorded *Okafor, Hazel, born in the winter of the flood,* had decided, alone, unprompted, unpaid, that a child the government refused to see was worth seeing. Hazel had spent five years being a forgery, a fiction maintained by a bored old man's kindness and a good imitation of official handwriting. And the whole time, up here in the dark above the city, the truth of them had been sitting in a locked room, waiting.

Visibility had a cost. Hazel was learning it tonight, lying on a borrowed cot with their life strapped to their wrist: the cost arrived first, and the reward—if there was one—came later, if ever, and never in the order you'd budgeted for.

Above them, in the black, the dead clock hung like a question the city had stopped asking.

* * *

They woke once, deep in the night, to lamplight moving on the wall.

For one blind second they were five years old again, in the flooded district, watching lanterns come through water. Then the present reassembled itself: stone, canvas, camphor, the tower. Wren stood in the doorway with her lamp, dressed, coat over her arm, a leather folder under it.

"You're awake," she said.

"Barely."

"Good. Barely awake people remember things accurately." She hesitated in the doorway, and Hazel saw, for the first time, that the flat precision of her face had a crack in it—not fear exactly, but the load-bearing kind of tiredness that fear leaves behind when it moves in permanently. "Marisol heard the ferryman's whistle. Boats on the ice below the cliff. Census boats, or something wearing their colors. They're working the riverbank tonight."

Hazel sat up, heart going hard. "Here?"

"Not here. Not yet. The east bank—fishermen's stairs, the unregistered lanes." Wren's jaw tightened. "That's where Marisol's sister lives. Unregistered twenty-two years." She turned to go, then stopped, and said, in a voice carefully stripped of everything: "Get dressed. Bring your tools. Whatever happens tonight, the clock doesn't stop being broken, and neither do we."

She went down the stairs, lamp swinging, and Hazel sat a moment longer in the dark with the strap biting their wrist and the thought arriving cold and complete, the way the worst ones do:

The net wasn't tightening around Hazel anymore.

It had grown big enough to hold everyone they'd met.
