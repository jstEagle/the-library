## 9. The Cook Who Counts

Marisol counted everything. She had to.

Twenty years in the observatory had taught her that survival was arithmetic done quietly: how many loaves the household would eat before they went stale; how many candles the tower could burn at night without a light showing from the road; how many days since the last census wagon had crossed the bridge, and how many more until the next one came sniffing. She counted spoons, she counted steps, she counted the seconds between the master clock's strikes back when it still struck them—and when it stopped, she counted the silence instead, which was heavier and lasted longer.

She counted people too, but differently. People she counted by what they ate.

Wren Aldous ate like her father, hunched over the bowl as if the meal might be confiscated mid-chew, and liked her bread torn, not cut, for reasons Marisol had never asked because some griefs keep their own kitchens. Old Piotr ate whatever he was given and thanked the air above the table, addressing no one, which meant either God or the stars, and with Piotr the two were not always distinguishable. And now there was the Okafor child—no, not a child, an apprentice, though Marisol's eyes, which missed nothing and admitted less, had measured them the first morning: nineteen, maybe twenty, wrists like a wading bird, hands that moved the way hands only move after ten thousand hours of very small work.

The new one ate like someone keeping an account. Every bite weighed against the possibility of the next one. Marisol knew that hunger. It was not the hunger of the stomach. It was the hunger of someone who had learned young that food could stop.

So this morning she made porridge with the good honey—the jar she hid behind the flour, the one she did not put out for just anyone—and she fried the last of the winter apples in butter until the whole kitchen smelled like a house where somebody loved somebody, and she set the table for four instead of three without announcing it, because announcing things was how you got caught.

* * *

The kitchen of Bellhaven Observatory occupied the ground floor's south curve, a broad stone belly beneath the tower, with windows fogged by steam and a door that opened onto the herb garden Wren pretended to tend and Marisol actually did. It was, by design, the warmest room in the building and the one most easily defended: one door, two windows, a cellar with a second exit into the coal chute that Marisol had widened herself, with a chisel, over the course of three patient summers, and told no one about. Not even Wren. Some doors you kept in your own pocket.

She was stirring the apples when Hazel came down the stair that spiraled from the clock room, moving the way the unregistered always moved on their first nights in a strange bed—carefully, as if the floor might report them.

"Sit," Marisol said, without turning. "The bench by the wall. The other one wobbles and I don't apologize twice."

A pause. Then the soft scrape of the apprentice obeying, which pleased her. In twenty years she had learned to sort people fast: the ones who argued with a full plate in front of them were trouble; the ones who sat down were salvageable.

"Eat all of it," she said. "I don't cook portions for guessing."

Hazel picked up the spoon. Marisol listened—not to the silence, but to its texture. Wren had come down earlier, gray-faced, said six words about census wagons and sealed workshops, and gone back up to guard her broken clock like a dog over a grave. So the news was bad. But the apprentice was eating. That was data enough for now.

"You knew my mother," Hazel said finally. Not a question. Wren must have said something—or the ledger had, or the way Wren looked at Hazel had said it, the way recognition leaks out of people through their faces when they aren't guarding it.

"I knew of her." Marisol turned from the stove, wiping her hands on her apron—a real apron, flowered cotton, the single luxury of a woman who owned almost nothing under her own name. "Adaeze Okafor. Came to market on the fish days, sold river herbs and a salve for the hands that every washerwoman in Bellhaven swore by and none of them would pay full price for, because paying a fair price to a woman who doesn't exist feels like theft you can't be charged with." She set the pan down harder than necessary. "I bought from her eleven years. I never once told her I was unregistered too. Isn't that a foolish thing? Two ghosts selling to each other across a market stall, each thinking the other was safe."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because being unseen is a habit before it is a condition." Marisol sat down across the table with her own bowl, though she wouldn't eat much; she never ate much while anyone else was eating. "You learn to walk past your own reflection. You learn that names are doors, and you've been living so long with the door shut that opening it lets in a draft you can't predict." She looked at Hazel straight, which she did rarely; it cost her something, and she let it show, because apprentices needed to see what honesty cost or they'd think it was free. "Your mother opened hers. She used her name at market. Loudly. That took more courage than anything I've done in twenty years, and I include the year I hid in a hay barge during the cholera quarantine."

Hazel's spoon had stopped. Their face did something complicated—grief and pride trying to occupy the same small space.

"She paid for it," Hazel said quietly.

"Yes," Marisol said, because lying to the unregistered about the price of visibility was a cruelty dressed as kindness, and she had sworn off it. "Yes, she did. Eat your apples. They go bitter when cold, like everything else in this city."

* * *

After breakfast—Hazel ate everything, scraped the bowl, and Marisol noted the hollow look receding by a single degree—she put the apprentice to work. Not clockwork. Kitchen work. There was a hierarchy of trust, and it began with onions.

"Slice those," she said. "Thin. If you cry, cry into the pot; we don't waste salt here."

For an hour the only sounds were the knife and the fire and the wind worrying at the tower overhead, and Marisol taught the way her own grandmother had taught, sideways, through tasks, letting the lessons arrive disguised as complaints.

"The baker on Coriander Lane—Ferenc, big man, missing two fingers—he counts his loaves out loud when he closes shop. If you ever need to pass him a message, the count tells you whether the street's clean. Eleven loaves means nothing. Ten loaves means stay away till evening."

Hazel's knife paused a fraction. Listening properly. Good.

"The ferryman, Dossa, works the north stairs, not the main landing. He owes this house more than he likes to remember and less than he pretends. He'll take you across for a look at his boots and no questions. Never use the main landing after dusk—that's where the clerks stand, and clerks are worse than soldiers. Soldiers follow orders. Clerks follow lists, and lists don't get tired."

"And the fishmonger on the east bank?" Hazel asked. Carefully casual. Too carefully.

Marisol smiled at the onions, a thin private thing. "So Wren told you about my sister."

"I didn't ask."

"No. You didn't. Which is why I'll answer." She wiped the blade on her apron and took up the next onion herself. "Yara has kept a fish stall on the east bank for twenty-two years without existing. Her trick is different from mine—I hide in plain employment, she hides in plain noise. A crowd is a kind of record nobody can search. She knows everyone's business within four bridges and forgets none of it, and if you ever need to disappear for a night, her loft sleeps two and smells like mackerel, and you will be grateful for both." The knife came down. "But you do not go to her unless the house burns. Not because she'd turn you away. Because every visit spends her, and I have spent enough of her already."

"How?" Hazel asked. Then, quickly, with the flinch of someone used to being punished for questions: "You don't have to answer."

"It was the winter of the flood," Marisol said. "When the river came up through the lower lanes, the registry office burned—conveniently, some said, though convenient fires are usually just fires—and the lists went with it. For one season, nobody in the flooded district existed officially, and Yara and I and a hundred others thought we'd been freed." She shook her head slowly. "Freed is a word for people with somewhere to be. We weren't freed. We were unmoored. And when the clerks rebuilt the lists, they rebuilt them from testimony—who vouched for whom, who remembered whose face—and Yara stood up in the counting-house and vouched for eleven families by name, including a clockmaker's wife with a baby girl, and the clerks wrote down every name she gave except hers." The knife stopped. Marisol looked at it as though it had betrayed her. "They wrote yours, child. Your mother's. Yours. Yara spent her voice on you before you could walk. That's what I mean by spending."

The kitchen was very quiet. The wind found a gap somewhere high in the tower and made a low sound, like a bottle blown across.

Hazel set down the knife. Their hands were steady—clockmaker's hands, trained against trembling—but their eyes had gone somewhere else entirely, and Marisol let them go, because she remembered going there herself, the country you visit when you learn that strangers once spent themselves on your behalf without ever meeting you.

"You could have left," Hazel said at last. "Before the wagons. Before any of it. Twenty years—you must know ways out."

"Every unregistered person knows ways out. That's half of what we carry." Marisol rose and began banking the fire, needing her hands busy. "There's a barge route downriver that asks no papers. There are monasteries over the border that have forgotten more souls than the state ever counted. I could have gone years ago." She straightened, dusted her palms. "And then who counts the flour? Who notices when Ferenc says ten loaves? Who feeds whoever comes through that door smelling of fear and machine oil?" She nodded upward, toward the tower, the stopped clock, the girl guarding it. "Being invisible is a trade, like any other. You can do it alone, and it eats you. Or you can do it in service of something, and it becomes—" she hunted for the word and refused the pretty ones— "watchkeeping. Someone has to watch the ones who count us. So I count first, and better, and kinder."

* * *

Midday brought Piotr down the stairs, navigating by the banister and the smell, announcing, "Something with apples. And grief. Both, definitely."

"Grief is free, old man. Apples cost money."

"Then I'm rich in one and poor in the other, which balances the books." He found his chair by memory and settled into it like water finding its level. His clouded eyes swung toward Hazel with unsettling accuracy. "Ah. The new hands. I heard you working the escapement last night. Three-quarter beat, slightly heavy on the lift. Whoever trained you made you oil with your left hand first. Unusual. Correct, but unusual."

Hazel stared. "How could you possibly—"

"I hear everything in this tower. Everything. It's the advantage of going blind in a building full of machinery; you become the mechanism." He accepted his bowl from Marisol's hand with a courtly nod toward wherever he believed her face was. "Also, the child breathes like someone counting. Very distinctive."

"I don't breathe like—" Hazel began, then stopped, hearing it, and Marisol laughed despite herself—a short, rusty sound, an engine started after long disuse.

"There," she said. "First rule of this house, and I want it carved somewhere Wren can't pretend not to see it: in this kitchen, we say true things, even useless ones. Especially useless ones. Lies are heavy and I do the lifting." She pointed her ladle at Hazel, then at Piotr, then, for good measure, at the empty chair where Wren should have been sitting. "Out there"—the ladle swept toward the window, the city, the bridge with its wagons—"you lie like your life depends on it, because it does. In here, the truth costs nothing and lies cost everything. That's what makes it a home instead of a hiding place. Understood?"

Hazel nodded. Piotr said, "Understood," and then, wickedly, "The stew needs thyme."

"It has thyme."

"It wants more thyme. I heard it complaining."

* * *

It was late afternoon, the light going copper through the fogged glass, when Hazel finally asked the question Marisol had been waiting for all day. She'd heard it coming the way you hear weather—hours off, inevitable, gathering weight over the water.

"How do you do it?" Hazel was drying bowls, stacking them with a clockmaker's precision, each rim aligned. "Twenty years. How do you live inside a lie that large and not... dissolve?"

Marisol considered giving the easy answer. *Practice. Habit. One day at a time.* All true and all worthless, the coin of consolation. Instead she crossed to the dresser at the wall, reached into her bodice, and drew out the thing she carried next to her heart the way Hazel carried a broken watch: a scrap of paper, soft as cloth with handling, folded eight times.

On it, in faded ink, in a hand that wasn't hers, was written: *Marisol Vey, born spring of the dry year, daughter of Tomas Vey, cooper, and Liana, seamstress.*

"My name," she said. "Written by a priest who married me to a man who died of fever two years later, in a parish register the state burned with everything else. This is the only proof I was ever born. I have carried it for thirty-one years." She smoothed it flat on the table with two fingers, the way you'd calm a bird. "You asked how I don't dissolve. Here is the whole secret, and I'll thank you not to find it disappointing. I decided that I exist, and that the paper is wrong. Every morning. Before the fire, before the bread, before anyone can take anything from me, I decide it again. The state's list says otherwise; the state's list can drown. But a decision made daily for twenty years gets a spine. It stands up on its own eventually." She refolded the paper, returned it to its place over her heart. "Your parents' watch. Do you still have it?"

Hazel's hand went to their satchel—an instinct, Marisol noted, worth remembering: the bag first, always the bag first.

"Then you already understand," Marisol said. "You've been carrying your existence in leather and brass for years. I just carry mine in paper. Same cargo."

"The satchel's lighter," Hazel said, and there was something in their voice that wasn't quite a joke and wasn't quite not.

"It always is. That's how you know it isn't enough."

* * *

Evening came down over Bellhaven the way it did in the cold months—not falling so much as thickening, the dark rising from the river to meet the dark descending from the sky until the city drowned between them. From the kitchen window, past the herb garden's black rows, Marisol could see the bridge: its lamps lit in their proper order, and among them, moving with terrible patience, the shape of a census wagon drawn up at the far abutment, its lanterns burning the specific yellow of official business. Counting. Always counting. Somebody over there had a list, and the list had columns, and one of the columns was for people who answered questions wrong.

She turned from the window and began, deliberately loudly, to make supper.

Hazel looked up from the bench where they'd been cleaning tools. "What—"

"Hush and listen," Marisol said. "Lesson three."

She talked. Not softly. She talked about the price of onions and the laziness of the butcher's boy and the scandal of the alderman's second cousin, filling the kitchen with the ordinary noise of an ordinary woman in an ordinary house, and while she talked she watched Hazel understand it: the performance of existing. How a voice pitched at a certain volume through an open window was a document more convincing than any forged paper. How the safest lie in Bellhaven was supper.

"Wren does this badly," Marisol confided, chopping hard. "She goes silent when afraid, which reads as guilty from the street. Piotr does it beautifully—he's been a harmless blind old man so long he's nearly convinced himself. And me?" She shrugged grandly. "I am the loudest cook in Bellhaven. Everyone knows Marisol. Nobody knows what I am. Noise is a kind of citizenship, child. Learn to hold yours."

Hazel tried it—offered, hesitantly, an opinion about the quality of observatory tea—and Marisol corrected the pitch, the ease, the small laugh at the end that no frightened person makes, and by the third attempt the apprentice almost had it, and something in Marisol's chest, long calcified, shifted half a degree.

That was the trouble with feeding people. You fed them once and then you knew their hunger forever, and knowing was a debt that collected itself.

* * *

Wren came down after dark, hollow-eyed, having heard nothing all day from her father's clock and everything all day from her own head. She ate standing, three bites, then pushed the bowl away.

"Crane," she said.

Marisol's hands went still over the dishwater. "The census taker? The one the market women call—"

"The polite one. Yes." Wren braced both hands on the table. "He's requested the maintenance logs for the master clock. Through the Bureau. Official seal. There's a form." Her mouth twisted around the word *form* as though it were a bone gone bad. "He wants to know who has worked on the clock. When. Under whose authority."

"He'll find an apprentice on the logs who doesn't exist," Marisol said. The dishwater had gone cold. She noticed these things; she noticed everything; noticing was her profession. "Which leads him to a workshop sealed by his own order. Which leads him to a master taken for questioning. He's not searching for a person, Wren. He's assembling a chain, link by link, and every link is someone in this kitchen."

"He's assembling it in triplicate," Piotr said mildly, from his corner. "I heard the courier's boots. Two copies went out tonight. One up the hill to the historians' lodgings."

Silence, then. The wind in the tower. The tickless dark where the clock's heartbeat should have been.

Marisol dried her hands, unhurried, because the first rule of fear was that you never let it choose your pace. She looked at the three of them—her strange, impossible household: a keeper's daughter married to a dead man's mission, a stargazer full of forbidden sky, and now this apprentice with a ghost's name and a forger's hands, standing in her warm kitchen holding a rag like it might defend them.

Twenty years she had hidden alone, and it had worked, and it had eaten her, and she had called the eating watchkeeping. Well. The ledger upstairs had a word for what happened when the erased stood together, even if the state never would.

"All right," she said, and reached for the largest pot. "Then we feed them first. Nobody thinks clearly hungry, and I refuse to let that man make us stupid." She set the pot down with a ring that silenced them all. "Hazel—tomorrow you go to market. Ferenc's stall, Coriander Lane, morning, before the crowds thin. Buy bread. Count the loaves on your way past. If he says ten, you come home the long way, along the river, and you tell me everything."

Hazel frowned. "Everything? About what?"

Marisol smiled, and it was not entirely a kind smile; twenty years underground had left edges on it that no kitchen could smooth.

"About which neighbors are ours," she said. "Because the net is coming down over this city, child, and when it does, the only thing that holds is the counting we do ourselves—and tomorrow, we start counting Crane."
