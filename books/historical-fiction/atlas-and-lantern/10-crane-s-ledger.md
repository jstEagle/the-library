## 10. Crane's Ledger

Silas Crane kept his lists the way other men kept their sins: private, ordered, and reviewed nightly.

His office in the census annex was a narrow room that had once been a linen closet for the customs house, and it still smelled faintly of soap and pressed cloth, which suited him. He had hung no pictures. The single window looked out on the tiled roof of the weighhouse and, beyond it, a grey slice of the river, and he had learned over eleven years of counting people not to look at the river while he worked. Rivers made men think of escape, and escape was a failure of method.

The lamp was trimmed. The ink was fresh. On the desk before him lay three documents, and he regarded them the way a surgeon regards an opened body: with respect, with attention, and without sentiment.

The first was his own census ledger, bound in black calf, its pages ruled in his own hand because he trusted no printer's ruling to be square. Eleven years of door-knocking lived in those pages. Every household in Bellhaven's six wards, every name given to him at every threshold, cross-indexed by street and by date, so that when he returned to a door—and he always returned—he could set the previous answers beside the new ones and see what had shifted. Most people shifted nothing. Most people said *my wife, Agnes, forty-one* this year and *my wife, Agnes, forty-two* the next and were, in every way that mattered to the registry, invisible in their consistency. It was the others he lived for. The man whose brother became a cousin. The lodger who appeared in autumn and denied herself by spring. The widow on Tanner's Row who had three children one visit and two the next, and never an explanation, only a smile arranged like furniture.

People thought lying was a sudden thing, Crane had found. It was not. Lying was erosion. It wore a family's answers thin in predictable places, and if you held the pages up to the light of each other, the thin places showed.

The second document was older: a tax roll from the last full assessment of the old regime, seven years stale now, its entries in a clerk's copperplate that Crane privately considered sloppy—ink too heavy, abbreviations inconsistent—but which he had nonetheless memorized the way a chess player memorizes openings. Tax rolls lied less than censuses. People might hide a grandmother from a census taker, because a grandmother was only a mouth; but they hid nothing from the assessor, because the assessor took money, and Bellhaven would forgive a hidden mother sooner than a hidden coin.

The third document was a single loose sheet, and it was new, and it was the reason the lamp burned late.

Crane dipped his pen, blotted it against the well's lip, and began.

He worked by ward, because working by interest invited enthusiasm, and enthusiasm was the enemy of thoroughness. Ward Three first: the river wards, where the housing leaned and the doors were answered by whoever answered. His own list said forty-one households on Candle Street; the tax roll said forty-four. He noted the three missing, dated them, recalled the smells of each doorway—the pickling brine at number twelve, the wet wool at thirty-one—and moved on. Ward Four: matched, save for a cooperage that had burned and been rebuilt smaller, honest arithmetic. Ward Two, the merchant terraces: everyone accounted for, twice over, the rich so thoroughly registered they seemed to press their names into your palm at the door.

Then Ward One. The hill. The observatory road.

Crane's pen slowed—not hesitated, he would have said later; slowed, the way a man slows to look closely at a fine joint in cabinetry—and he laid the two lists side by side.

The observatory itself was exempt, as institutions were, though he had always found the exemption lazy rather than principled. An institution was only a household with better lawyers. Below it, strung down the hill like beads on a wire, stood nine properties: the glazier, the widowed apothecary, the two rented cottages behind the almonry, and at the bend where the road turned toward the water stairs, the clockmaker's workshop—Idris Vell, master of the trade, assessed on tools, stock, and dwelling at a figure Crane considered flattering to Vell and unkind to the treasury.

Vell was on the tax roll. Vell was on the census. Vell answered his own door, gave his own age without the small swallow most men performed before admitting a number, and had, across four visits in eleven years, never once varied an answer. Crane had written *consistency: exceptional* in the margin after the second visit, meaning it as praise.

It was not Vell who troubled him.

It was the apprenticeship paper.

Crane rose, fetched the second box from the shelf—his correspondence copies, filed by month—and drew out the folder for the spring of the previous year. There it was: the workshop's annual return of household members, the form every licensed tradesman signed attesting to who slept under his roof. Vell's hand, cramped and confident. One journeyman, gone since. One apprentice listed.

*Apprentice: Hazel. Age seventeen. Registered under Article 12, provisional.*

Article 12. Crane had read the apprenticeship statutes more times than most magistrates—it was astonishing how many laws existed solely so that they might be quietly ignored—and Article 12 permitted a master to take an unregistered minor into training pending registration papers *in progress*, for a period not exceeding ninety days.

The return was dated fourteen months ago.

Crane sat back. He did not frown. Frowning wrinkled the record of one's face, and his face was part of his instrument. But somewhere in his chest, in the small cold room where he kept his satisfactions, a match was struck.

He pulled his own census ledger toward him and turned, with two fingers, to the observatory road pages. Four visits. And there—in the second year, in the third, absent in the fourth—his own handwriting, in the column where household members were entered:

*Apprentice (name uncertain — "Hazel"?). Mixed. Not produced.*

Not produced. That phrase did more work in Crane's ledgers than any other three words in the language. When he asked to see a person and the householder produced a sick aunt, a sleeping child, a laborer gone to the wharf—*not produced* went in the margin, and the page was flagged with a small blue cross, and the blue crosses were revisited.

But here was the irregularity, and Crane laid his fingertip flat upon it as though stilling an insect. In the fourth year's entry, the apprentice column was blank. Struck through? No—never struck; he did not strike, he annotated. Blank, with the notation: *Apprentice reported departed. No forwarding. No deregistration filed.*

No deregistration filed. A licensed master could not simply will an apprentice out of existence; the registry required a closing entry, a signature, a destination. Vell had filed nothing. Which meant that in the eyes of the law—which was to say, in the eyes of paper, which was the only country Crane had ever held citizenship in—this *Hazel* had neither arrived nor departed, but had instead hovered, for years, in the amber of an expired provisional clause.

And now—Crane consulted the third sheet, the newest, a workman's chit he had purchased for two coins from a glazier's laborer who owed the census a favor regarding a sister-in-law's status—the observatory's master clock had stopped, and the city works office, needing hands and needing them cheap, had dispatched an apprentice from Vell's shop to repair it.

An apprentice who officially did not exist, sent by official writ, into the official timekeeper of the city.

Crane set down his pen.

For a long moment he looked at the window, at the grey slice of river he did not permit himself, and he allowed the thought to complete itself, because incomplete thoughts were a form of untidiness. Someone at the works office had signed an order naming this apprentice. He would need to see that order. He would need to see the apprentice's file, if a file existed, and if none existed, then the absence itself was the finding. Either the works office had been careless—and carelessness in clerks was a weed that responded to pruning—or someone had wanted this particular nonexistent person inside the observatory, which raised a different and far more nourishing question: why?

He was not a man given to superstition, but he was a man given to pattern, and the pattern before him had the shape of a door left deliberately ajar. You did not leave a door ajar by accident twice.

He began to write.

The request, when finished, occupied a single page, because Crane believed requests should be short and consequences long. Addressed to the observatory's keeper of instruments—Aldous, the records said, though the records also said Aldous had died some years past, and the funding rolls suggested a daughter had inherited the post without ceremony, another small thread to pull another day—it requested, in the courteous and unanswerable grammar of the bureau:

*...copies of all maintenance logs pertaining to the master clock for the preceding twenty-five years, together with a current roster of persons resident or employed at the observatory, for purposes of registry reconciliation.*

Reconciliation. A beautiful word. It asked for nothing, accused no one, and required the recipient either to produce twenty-five years of honest paper or to begin, tonight, deciding what to forge. Either response fed him. Crane had long ago accepted that he would never see the faces of the people he caught; he saw only the wakes they left in the record, and a wake, properly read, told you everything—the stroke rate, the direction, whether the swimmer knew they were seen.

He sanded the page, folded it, and set it beneath the paperknife to await the morning courier.

* * *

He should have stopped there. Eleven years of method said stop there: one inquiry per week, pursued to its root before the next is planted. But the lamp was lit, and the tax roll was open, and Crane had discovered early in his career that his appetite for the work was the one hunger he had never succeeded in putting on a register.

He turned instead to the blue crosses.

There were nine currently flagged in Ward One alone—no, ten; the apothecary's lodger, the one who had been *visiting her aunt* for three consecutive springs, warranted a cross he had not yet inked. He inked it. Then, because the observatory road was warm in his mind, he drew a fresh sheet and began a different kind of list, the kind he showed to no one: not names, but absences.

The observatory, he wrote, employed a cook. He had never interviewed the cook. This was itself remarkable—he had interviewed cooks in every institutional kitchen in Bellhaven, because kitchens were where unregistered persons went to disappear; a kitchen asked no papers of you, only that your hands work and your mouth stay shut. Yet the observatory's returns, year upon year, listed no domestic staff at all. Did the keeper of the city's time eat air? Did she wash her own pots between winding the heavens?

Someone cooked. Someone who was not on any roll.

He wrote it plainly: *Observatory: staff absent from all returns. Probability of concealed persons: high.*

And beneath that, after a moment, in his smallest hand:

*Aldous (elder): recorded deaths in flood winter inconsistent with pension continuance. Investigate.*

He had found that discrepancy months ago and shelved it, because a pension paid to a dead man was theft, and theft was dull. But viewed tonight, beside the nonexistent apprentice and the unlisted cook, it rearranged itself. Theft was dull. Pattern was not. Three irregularities in one building was no longer coincidence; it was architecture. The observatory was not a building with errors in its paperwork. It was a building whose paperwork had been *designed*, the way a clock was designed—with a public face and, somewhere behind it, gearing that did the true work.

Crane sat very still with that thought, and found that it pleased him in a manner he did not examine closely.

Outside, the river fog had come up the hill and pressed against the glass, softening the rooflines of the town he had spent eleven years turning into columns. Somewhere down there, in the dark, the master clock stood stopped, its face blank over the rooftops, and Bellhaven had gone on eating and arguing and lying without official time for a week now, as though the hour were a matter of opinion. He had heard them do it—the ferryman calling half-past-noon by the sun, the market women adjusting by hunger. A city without its clock was a city rehearsing something. He did not know what yet. But he had begun, professionally, to suspect that the stopping and the apprentice and the ajar door were all one motion, one train of wheels engaging tooth by tooth, and that somewhere at the center of it was a room he had not yet been shown.

He blew out the lamp at half past one by his pocket watch—unofficial time, private time, the hypocrisy of every counter of other men's hours—and locked his lists in the cabinet, three turns, key on the chain inside his waistcoat where it lay against his heart like the relic of a religion whose god was the record.

In the morning the request went out with the courier. By afternoon it came back, and the coming back taught him more than the asking.

* * *

The observatory's reply was written in a hand Crane did not know—quick, slanted, intelligent—and it was, on its surface, perfectly compliant. The maintenance logs were enclosed: three slim volumes covering the past decade, faithfully kept, entries in a firm regular script. The current roster accompanied them: Keeper Wren Aldous, one Marisol Vega (cook—so she existed after all, and under her own name too, which was either innocence or extraordinary nerve), one Piotr Brandt (astronomer emeritus), and no apprentices.

No apprentices.

He had asked, in writing, for the roster of persons resident or employed, and the observatory had answered in writing, under a date and a signature, omitting the one person the city works office had officially dispatched to their tower. If the omission was carelessness, it was carelessness of a spectacular kind. If it was anything else, then the girl—the apprentice, *Hazel*, sex unrecorded in every document Crane possessed, which was itself nearly a confession—had been erased from a list within days of his asking, which meant someone at the observatory read his requests and understood them perfectly.

And the logs themselves. He read them twice, then a third time by the window's grey light, and it was on the third reading that he understood he was holding not a defense but a message.

Twenty-five years, he had requested. They had sent ten. The earlier volumes were described, in a single unapologetic sentence, as *lost to damp in the lower archive.* Lost to damp. Twenty-five years of maintenance records for the most important mechanism in the city, and fifteen of them had drowned in a dry hillside building. It was not even a good lie. It was the kind of lie offered not to deceive but to measure—to hold up against his eye, like a lens, and say: *this is how much we do not fear you.*

And the entries themselves were immaculate. Too immaculate. Oilings, cleanings, adjustments, all at proper intervals, all in the same unhurried hand. Crane had read enough ledgers in his life to know the texture of honest bookkeeping: the smudge, the correction, the entry made late and initialed so. These pages had the evenness of a story composed after the fact. Either the keeper kept two sets of books—one grubby and real, one clean for the bureau—or the logs had been rewritten entire within the past week, in which case the rewriting itself was the loudest thing Crane had read all year.

He spread his own census sheets across the desk and worked until dusk, adding, crossing, annotating, and when he was done he had before him a single summary page, and he read it back to himself aloud, quietly, in the empty office, because certain findings deserved to be pronounced at least once:

One apprentice, provisionally registered, lapsed, nonexistent, currently employed atop the observatory hill and absent from every list but his. One cook, self-declared, whose name appeared in no prior record in the city's keeping before four years ago. One dead keeper drawing no pension he could trace—withdrawn, he noticed now, the very season of the flood winter, tidy as a pulled tooth. Fifteen years of vanished logs. One rewritten decade.

And beneath the tower, somewhere, the reason for all of it, stopped clocks being, in Crane's long experience of stopped things, never accidents.

He took out a fresh sheet. He did not write to the observatory again—a second request would tell them only how hot the trail ran, and Crane did not flush birds he intended to net. Instead he addressed himself to the Bureau of Registry, Central, requesting under standing authority the *complete institutional files of the Bellhaven Observatory, including staffing histories prior to the current administration.* Let the archives disgorge what the tower withheld. Paper remembered what people forgot; it was the one article of faith he had never had cause to revise.

He sanded it, folded it, sealed it with his plain grey wax—no crest; crests were vanity—and placed it on the tray beside the first letter's ghost, already answered.

Then he put on his coat, took his hat from its peg, and stood a moment in the doorway looking down through the fog at the town, at the blurred lanterns of the bridge road, at the invisible hill. Nine hundred steps, they said, up the observatory stair. He counted things; it was how he prayed. Soon, he thought, he would climb them himself. There were questions that paper answered, and questions that only faces answered, and the difference between the two was where the guilty always believed themselves safe.

He turned up his collar, descended into the fog, and began, at an unhurried walk, the long approach toward a door on the observatory road—past the glazier's, past the almonry cottages—while above him, blank-faced over the city, the stopped clock waited for whatever he would ask it, and somewhere inside the tower, though he could not have said how he knew, someone had just heard his name spoken for the first time.
