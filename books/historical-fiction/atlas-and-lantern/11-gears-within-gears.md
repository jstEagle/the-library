## 11. Gears Within Gears

The clock's heart came out into Hazel's hands at dawn, when the light through the east windows of the mechanism room was the color of weak tea.

They had been working for six days now, and the tower had begun to feel less like a building and more like a body—one they were learning by touch the way a physician learns a patient. The great going train ran down through four floors of the observatory, weights and wheels and wires, and every part of it had its own temperature, its own smell. The brass near the top smelled of cold. Down in the pit where the pendulum swung, the air was damp and mineral, like the inside of a mouth. Hazel had mapped all of it in their notebook: every wheel counted, every arbor measured, every worn pivot noted in a hand that grew more confident with each page.

But this morning they had come to the escapement again—the part Wren called her father's masterpiece, and the part someone had tried to kill.

The sabotage had been elegant, which was what still troubled Hazel most. A broken tooth on the third wheel could be blamed on age; a snapped suspension spring on accident. But whoever had done this work had understood the mechanism well enough to hurt it precisely, in the two places where damage would propagate invisibly downward until the whole train stopped and no one looking at the face would know why. It had not been vandalism. It had been surgery.

Hazel sat cross-legged on the work platform with the anchor escapement disassembled before them on a cloth, each piece cleaned and laid out in order of removal, the way Master Idris had taught them before Idris was anything to them but a name on an apprenticeship paper that wasn't quite true. *Left to right, in the order you took it apart. A bench in chaos makes a mind in chaos.* The pallets were sound. The escape wheel was sound, its thirty teeth sharp and even under Hazel's loupe. The suspension—replaced, new steel, Hazel's own filing—hung straight and true.

Everything was sound. And yet, yesterday evening, running light without the hands attached, the train had hesitated. Not stopped. Hesitated—a catch, a fraction of a second of resistance, once every sixty beats exactly, so regular that at first Hazel had taken it for the tick itself.

Once per minute. Something geared to the minute wheel.

Hazel found it at mid-morning, and finding it changed everything.

It was hidden behind the minute-wheel bridge, and it was hidden *well*—so well that Hazel had already removed that bridge twice during the restoration and never seen it, because seeing required lying flat against the front plate at an angle no sane person would choose, cheek pressed to cold brass, one eye shut, the loupe wedged sideways. From there, in the slatted dawn light, Hazel saw it: a small cluster of gearing tucked into a recess that had been cut into the plate itself, not added afterward but *designed*, the recess cast into the metal when the plate was poured forty years ago. Four small wheels. A fifth, smaller still, made of steel so fine its teeth looked like the edge of a coin. And driving off the whole assembly, a slender arbor running up through a channel drilled invisibly through the plate toward the front of the movement.

Someone had built a second clock inside the first.

Hazel lay there a long time, breath fogging faintly on the brass, and felt the specific vertigo of a craftsman encountering craft greater than their own. Then they did what any apprentice does in such a moment: they went to find the oldest person available.

* * *

Old Piotr was in the dome, where he spent his mornings in a wicker chair angled at the meridian, "supervising the sky." His cataracts had taken most of the stars from him years ago, but he kept the chair, kept the angle, kept the ritual, and when Hazel climbed the last ladder and announced themselves, he said, without turning, "You've found the back gear."

Hazel stopped on the ladder's top rung. "You know about it."

"I wound it for eleven years." Piotr turned then, his clouded eyes finding Hazel's general direction with unnerving accuracy. "Aldous built it into the works the same year they hung the official dial. He told me: 'The state will keep its time. I'll keep mine. And God help anyone who lets one stop without the other knowing.'" He tapped the chair arm with a knuckle yellowed like old ivory. "The boy who sweeps the floors doesn't ask what's in the walls. But you're not the boy who sweeps the floors anymore, are you."

"Does Wren know?"

"Wren knows her father left something in the clock besides gears. She doesn't know what. He died before he finished teaching me how it worked—I only ever learned my half, the winding and the checking." Piotr's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "You're the first pair of hands since his that's been inside it far enough to meet the rest."

Hazel descended the ladder slowly, carrying this like a full water jar.

Back in the mechanism room, Wren was waiting. She appeared on the ninth day of Hazel's work and had been appearing every day since, ostensibly to bring tea, actually to sit on a stool near the pendulum pit with a book she never read, watching Hazel's hands with an attention that had stopped being about the repair somewhere around day three. Today she put the book down as soon as Hazel came through the door.

"Piotr says you found something."

"Piotr talks too much for a man who guards secrets." Hazel set the loupe down and wiped their hands. "Your father built a second dial. Behind the main movement, driven off the minute wheel through a hidden train. It runs—if it runs—to something at the front of the clock. I haven't traced the arbor yet. It goes up through the plate and disappears."

Wren had gone very still. "Disappear where?"

"That's what we're going to find out."

They traced it together, which took until dusk. The arbor passed upward through a drilled channel in the movement's front plate, then through another channel in the dial plate itself—a channel that ran, impossibly, through the solid brass behind the numeral VII, at a radius and depth that should have been empty. When Hazel held a lamp at an angle and looked along the dial's surface, they saw it: a seam so fine it might have been scratch-brushing, circling a patch of the dial between the seven and the eight, no larger than a saucer, painted over with the same black lacquer as the rest.

A false patch. A door in the face of the official clock.

"I used to dust this dial when I was ten," Wren said quietly. Her voice had an odd flatness, the voice of someone walking on ice and listening for it to speak. "Every week. My father stood below and watched me do it. Every week, he watched me wipe this exact spot and never said a word."

"He was waiting," Hazel said, "for someone who'd take the clock apart far enough to deserve it."

The patch came free that night, though it took two hours and more patience than Hazel knew they possessed. The lacquer had been keyed to release along a scribed line; beneath it, secured by three concealed screws of a size made only by one workshop in Bellhaven—Idris Okafor's workshop, Hazel realized, and filed that fact away where it could cut later—was a second dial.

Small, silvered, perfect. It bore a single hand, geared to the hidden train. Around its rim, instead of numerals, Aldous Aldous had engraved words in a hand so fine Hazel needed the loupe and the lamp held close:

*AS THE OFFICIAL RUNS, SO RUNS THE TRUE.*

And beneath that, smaller still:

*LET THEM STOP ME AND SEE WHAT STOPS WITH ME.*

* * *

They sat with it a long while, the two of them, on the work platform with the lamp between them and the dead clock towering overhead like a whale skeleton in a museum hall.

"It's not a secret clock," Hazel said at last, thinking aloud, because thinking aloud had become something they only did in front of Wren, which was its own kind of discovery. "I thought—it's not a backup. It's not a spare timekeeper hidden away for emergencies. Look at the gearing." They touched the diagram they'd drawn, the little hidden train. "It's driven *off* the official movement. The minute wheel drives it directly. If the official mechanism stops—" their finger completed the line, "—this stops too. Instantly. It can't be wound separately. It has no power of its own. It only exists as long as the official time exists."

"And what does it show?" Wren asked. "What time is it telling?"

"That's just it." Hazel looked up at the great dark face above them, the gilded numerals invisible now, everywhere and nowhere. "It isn't telling a different time. There's no other time. It's telling the *same* time. The exact same time, to the second, geared one-to-one off the official train." They heard their own voice go strange. "It's an argument, Wren. Your father built an argument out of gears."

Wren drew the lamp closer. In its light her face was all planes and shadow, and Hazel thought—not for the first time—that she had her father's habit of listening like the listening itself was labor.

"Explain it to me the way you'd explain it to Marisol," she said. "She always says you clever people talk in circles."

"All right." Hazel folded their legs. "The government looks at that dial up there and says: *that* is the time. Official time. City time. The time your lease is reckoned by, the time your debt accrues by, the time the courts use when they decide what you owe. One time, one authority, one record." They pointed at the hidden dial in its bed of velvet-dark cloth. "Now. Someone stops the official clock—as somebody did, deliberately, six weeks ago. The city has no official time. The courts can't convene, the debts can't be reckoned, the leases can't expire. Time in Bellhaven doesn't pass, officially."

"Which serves whoever wanted it stopped."

"Exactly. But here's the argument." Hazel picked up the hidden dial and held it in both palms, and it was warm from their hands, and heavier than it looked, the way everything true seemed to be in this tower. "This dial proves the stoppage wasn't natural. Because it's geared one-to-one to the official train, it froze at the precise instant the sabotage took hold—and it's engraved with its own meaning. Anyone who opens the clock and finds this knows: the official time and the true time are the *same mechanism*. You cannot break one and preserve the other. You cannot erase the record and keep the authority. Stop the clock and you stop truth with it—visibly, provably, dated to the very second."

Wren was silent. Then: "He wrote it in the ledger too. The night before he died. I never understood the entry. I thought it was a note to himself about repairs." She recited it, slow, from memory, the way one carries a thing carried so long it has worn smooth: "*'The clock is the ledger's twin. What is written must also be geared.'*"

"The ledger," Hazel repeated.

And then it came, all in a rush, the way understanding does—not arriving but *assembling*, click after click after click, a train of thought running down through Hazel's mind and gathering speed: the ledger room behind the clock's face with its shelves of uncounted lives; the entries in Aldous's hand recording births and debts and deaths the government refused to see; *Okafor, Hazel. Born in the winter of the flood*, written down by a stranger decades before Hazel knew they existed; and now this dial, this hidden, dependent, truthful dial geared inseparably to the public face.

"It's the same design," Hazel whispered. "The ledger and the clock. He built them the same way. The official record and the true record—side by side, driven off the same events, the same days, the same people. The government keeps its census; Aldous kept his. And the moment the government's version stops matching—" Hazel's hands shaped it in the air, wheels meshing, "—the discrepancy becomes evidence. You can't fake one without breaking the other. The ledger isn't paper, Wren. It's machinery. It's *part of this clock*. The whole thing—the dial, the ledger, the records, the room—it's one instrument, and its function is to make erasure impossible without leaving proof."

The lamp flame bent in a draft from the stairwell. Somewhere below, the pendulum hung motionless in its pit, holding its breath, holding its single frozen second.

Wren reached across the platform and closed her hand over the hidden dial in Hazel's palms, so that they held it together, her fingers over theirs, the silver rim warming between them.

"He knew," she said, and her voice finally cracked, just once, just slightly, like ice taking weight. "All those years, he knew they'd come for the records eventually. So he didn't hide the truth. He *married* it to the thing they couldn't afford to break. The city needs its official clock. Any regime, any historians, whatever comes after—they need it. And now the clock testifies."

"Then we have a problem," Hazel said softly, "because right now the witness is dead."

They worked out the arithmetic together, there in the lamplight, and the arithmetic was ugly. To restore the official mechanism was straightforward—Hazel could do it in three days, four to be safe. But to restore it *properly*, with the hidden train cleaned and oiled and the secret dial reinstalled and running, meant making the clock whole again in Aldous's full design—which meant the moment the weights dropped and the escapement began to beat, the clock would begin keeping time publicly, visibly, officially. A stopped clock could be ignored. A running clock would have to be *answered*. Whoever had sabotaged it once would know within hours that their surgery had been undone, and would come to see who held the scalpel.

"And there's one more thing," Hazel said. They turned the hidden dial toward the lamp and showed Wren what they had noticed an hour ago and not yet said aloud. At the center of the silvered face, beneath the hand's arbor, Aldous had machined a tiny auxiliary socket—an unused fitting, obviously meant for something not yet installed. Threaded for it. Waiting.

"What fits there?" Wren asked.

"I don't know. Something he meant to add and never did. Or—" Hazel hesitated, then said it, because in this tower they had learned that saying things gave them weight and weight gave them shape: "Or something he intended for whoever came after him. A final component. The missing wheel in the train."

"A message," Wren said.

"A *mechanism*," Hazel corrected, and heard in their own voice the apprenticeship of a lifetime settling into place, the vocation declaring itself. "In this family—in this tower—messages are mechanisms."

Outside, far below, the river muttered under its ice, and the wind coming off it carried the smell of thaw that was weeks early. Wren wrapped the secret dial in flannel and placed it in the drawer of Aldous's old desk, and stood a moment with her hand flat on the wood, and Hazel pretended to be busy with the diagrams so she could have the moment privately.

It was nearly midnight when Hazel packed up the tools and noticed it—the small thing that would not leave them afterward, that followed them down the nine hundred steps and up again the next morning and every morning after.

On Wren's desk, beneath the ledger's open page, lay the census gazetteer that circulated freely through the city offices these days, its lists of irregular persons updated weekly. Someone had been through it with a pencil. Beside one entry—a name, an occupation, an address on the observatory road—a small, neat mark had been drawn, the kind clerks use to flag a record for follow-up.

The name was *Okafor*.

The ink of Hazel's own forged paper had barely dried six months ago, and no clerk alive should have connected that name to this tower. Which meant someone else had written it down first. Someone with access to old records. Someone who knew what the name meant, and had handed it, deliberately or carelessly, into the machinery of the state.

Hazel said nothing that night. But they dreamed of gears, thousands of them, turning in the dark, and among them one small wheel, deliberately flawed, that meshed with everything and ground against everything, and woke with the certain knowledge that the clock was not the only mechanism in Bellhaven that had been designed to leave proof of what it did.

Someone had started the train moving. Now all they had to do was follow it down.
