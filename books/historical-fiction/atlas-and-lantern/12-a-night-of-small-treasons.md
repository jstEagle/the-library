## 12. A Night of Small Treasons

The kitchen of the observatory occupied the tower's second landing, a round room built for storage and repurposed by appetite, and it was Marisol's kingdom in the way that kitchens always become kingdoms when someone feeds people long enough. She had claimed it twenty years ago and no one had thought to contest the claim since. Copper pots hung from hooks her own hands had driven into the stone. Bundles of dried herbs swung from the rafters on strings she retied each autumn. The table—round, scarred, wide enough for six if two of them were willing to sit near the draft from the stairwell—had been carried up in pieces by three generations of keepers who needed somewhere to eat, and it bore the marks of every meal: burns shaped like pot bottoms, a groove worn along one edge where a hundred restless thumbs had worried the wood.

Marisol cooked the way she did everything else: thoroughly, without hurry, and with an attention to detail that could pass for innocence to anyone who didn't know better. Tonight she was making the river fish stew that Bellhaven's poor ate all winter, but she made it her way—with saffron she traded for from a spice barge captain who owed her a debt no census would ever record, and with peppers dried in the observatory's warmest cupboard, and with more garlic than any respectable recipe would permit.

She listened to the tower while she worked. That was the truth of her position, though she'd never have phrased it so grandly. Twenty years of hiding in plain sight taught a person to listen. She heard Wren above her, in the ledger room, moving papers—the particular dry whisper of vellum being shifted, stopped, shifted again. She heard Old Piotr descending from the cupola with his careful half-blind steps, one hand trailing the wall, counting doorways by touch as he always did. And she heard the newest sound, still strange after these weeks, still adjusting itself to the tower's acoustics: Hazel Okafor, down at the clock level, working late among the gears.

The boy—or girl, or whatever Hazel was; Marisol had decided in the first week that it didn't matter and hadn't thought about it since—moved differently now than when they'd first slept in the observatory. Then, Hazel had moved like a person in a house where they might be caught: weight on the outside edges of the feet, doors closed with the slow pressure of fingertips rather than latches. Now there was a confidence coming into those movements. A claim.

Marisol recognized it because she had spent twenty years refusing to let it happen to herself. You did not settle into a hiding place. You kept your body a guest even in the rooms you'd lived in longest, because the day you moved like you belonged was the day you stopped being ready to run.

But watching Hazel, ladling stew into the good bowls now, Marisol felt something twist under her ribs—an old thing, a grief she'd buried so deep she'd mistaken it for character. The child was learning to belong. And belonging, in a city where the wagons crossed the bridge each morning looking for exactly such people, was a kind of wound waiting to be opened.

Well. She had asked them all to dinner, hadn't she? She'd been building toward this for days, turning it over the way she turned a tough cut of meat, looking for the angle that would make it tender. Because Wren had told her what was in the ledger room. Wren had finally told her about the last entry, the one in her father's hand—*Okafor, Hazel. Born in the winter of the flood*—and Marisol had put down her knife and stood very still at her cutting board for a long moment, thinking about what it meant that a stranger had written down a name that the whole machinery of the state was designed to leave unwritten.

Tonight, she decided, they would eat together properly. And tonight, perhaps, they would stop being four people coincidentally sheltering in the same tower.

The bread came out of the oven golden and heavy. She set the stew in the middle of the table on the trivet shaped like a leaping fish that Piotr had cast for her years ago from melted candle stubs and lead scavenged from the roof. She lit the lamps, all three of them, because tonight was not a night for economy.

Then she rang the little bell—actually rang it, the brass bell she used only when the household ate together, which was rare enough that its sound made everyone come quickly, half-afraid something was wrong.

"Kitchen," she called up the stairwell. "All of you. Now, while it's hot."

* * *

Hazel came up first, hands scrubbed pink at the basin, wearing their one clean shirt—the grey linen with the mended collar that they saved for occasions they couldn't define. They smelled of machine oil under the soap, the way they always did now; it had soaked into their skin somewhere below the reach of lye. They took their usual seat, the one facing away from the stairwell windows, and Marisol noted it again without comment: the seat where no one on the street below could see your face through the glass. Some habits she would never ask the child to explain.

Wren came down with ink on her fingers and a distracted look, the ledger-room look, the expression of someone carrying forty years of other people's lives behind her eyes. She'd been copying again—she and Hazel both, nights now, page after page in duplicate, though Marisol knew better than to know too much about where the duplicates went. Knowledge was weight, and everyone in this tower carried enough already.

Piotr came last, of course, because Piotr navigated the world at his own pace and dared anyone to hurry him. He was ancient in the way of trees—not bent, exactly, but weathered into a shape the wind had argued with for decades. His cataracts gave his eyes a pale, moonlike quality, and he found his chair by touching its back first, greeting it almost, before folding himself down into it. He'd dressed for dinner, Marisol noticed with a private warmth: he'd put on the coat with the brass buttons, the one from some earlier life, and he'd combed what remained of his hair.

"Stew," he announced, leaning over the bowl, inhaling. "River pike. Saffron. And—" he paused, nostrils working like a man reading a document "—the small hot peppers from the east cupboard. It is a feast. Someone has died, or someone is in love, or someone is afraid."

"Piotr," Marisol said warningly.

"It is always one of the three," he said serenely, and broke his bread.

They ate. For a while that was all: the clink of spoons, the tearing of bread, the small negotiations of a shared table—Wren pushing the pepper dish toward Hazel, Hazel passing the salt without being asked, Marisol refilling bowls before anyone had to request it. Outside the windows the winter dark lay hard on the city, and far below, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, the river ice ticked and settled against the pilings.

It was Piotr, naturally, who broke the silence. He broke silences the way ice broke under weight—all at once, with no warning anyone could act on.

"You know my name is not Piotr," he said.

Hazel's spoon stopped halfway to their mouth.

Marisol set down her ladle. "Old man—"

"It is a dinner," Piotr said mildly. "You made the feast. I am only supplying the occasion." He turned his pale eyes toward Hazel, or toward the place near Hazel; it was always hard to say precisely whom he looked at. "The boy should know whose house this is. Whose table. If we are going to hang together—and the times suggest we may—I prefer to know the names of my fellow condemned."

"That's cheerful," Wren said.

"I am a cheerful man. Ask the stars." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand, a gesture from some coarser century. "My name," he said, "is Pyotr Vasilievich Zolotaryov. I dropped most of it off the side of a ship forty years ago, coming across, because the harbor police were asking questions about astronomers from countries we were not supposed to have countries anymore. 'Piotr' was what was left. Like a chair missing a leg that still stands, if you lean it right." He smiled, showing yellow teeth. "Zolotaryov means 'goldsmith' in the old language. My grandfather gilded domes. I gilded nothing. I measured stars the government said were not there. Between us, I recommend the stars. They do not arrest you. They only ignore you, magnificently."

Silence held the table for a moment. Hazel was staring at him, and Marisol saw the calculation happening behind the young face—the slow, dangerous arithmetic of realizing that confession was contagious, that a room where one person could say a true name aloud became a room where anyone might be expected to.

Wren spoke next, quietly, taking her turn as though the table were a conversation piece in a game whose rules everyone somehow already knew.

"Wren Aldous," she said. "Except Aldous wasn't my mother's name, and my father never married her, and the registry never recorded either of them properly, because she was a riverwoman and he was a keeper and the clerks of those years preferred tidy paper to true lives. My mother's name was Sabah. Sabah Ren. I don't use it because I never earned it—she died when I was six, and everything I remember of her fits in a thimble. But it's mine. It's in the ledger downstairs, third volume, in my father's hand, because he wrote down everything the city refused to." She looked at her hands, at the ink stains. "I'm named for a bird that lives in chimneys. My father thought it was funny. He had that kind of humor—you only understand it ten years later."

Marisol closed her eyes briefly. *Now*, she thought. *Now it comes around to me.* She had known this moment would arrive the whole evening, had been cooking toward it, and still it landed in her chest like a dropped pan.

"And me," she said, and her voice came out rougher than she intended. She cleared her throat. "Twenty years, I've been Marisol. Cook. Unregistered, which most of you guessed, and quiet about it, which none of you appreciated enough." A weak laugh went around the table and died fast. "My full name is Marisol Inés Cabral. The Inés was my grandmother. The Cabral was a shipping family in the estuary towns, upstream, before the debts took the boats and the boats were the least of what they took." She folded her hands on the table so they wouldn't move without permission. "I came downstream the year of the dock riots. Everyone came downstream that year. Most of them are in the ledger downstairs too, if you look. Volume two. My father's name is there—he sold rope at the Salt Gate—and my mother's, and mine, written in a stranger's careful hand because no clerk in this city would waste ink on us."

She looked up and found Hazel watching her with an intensity that was almost painful, the look of someone seeing their own future worn by another person. Twenty years. Twenty years of feeding people and listening and keeping her body a guest in every room.

"That's why I cook the way I cook," she said simply. "A person who can't have a name learns to make sure everyone eats anyway. It's not the same thing. But it's what there is."

Nobody spoke. Above them, through three floors of stone and timber, the dead master clock held its silence, and below them the river muttered against the ice, and Marisol understood suddenly, fully, what she had suspected for weeks: this tower was the safest room in Bellhaven and also the most exposed—a single lamp in a dark window, visible for miles, full of people the entire apparatus of the state had agreed did not exist.

* * *

Hazel sat with the names stacked inside them like weights in a scale pan—Zolotaryov, Sabah Ren, Marisol Inés Cabral—and felt the table tilt toward them, the way a conversation tilts, the way a mechanism tilts, when the last wheel is waiting to drop into place.

They had known this was coming. From the moment Piotr opened his mouth, Hazel had known, the way you know the escapement is about to catch. And underneath the dread was something else, something that had been growing in them for weeks down among the gears, in the ledger room, in front of that last entry in a dead man's hand: *Okafor, Hazel. Born in the winter of the flood.*

Someone had thought them worth writing down. Before they could write themselves.

"My name," Hazel began, and their voice cracked on the second word, which surprised them—they had rehearsed this in the dark of the cot in the keeper's room, several versions, all fluent. They started again. "My name is Hazel Okafor. Full name—Hazel Adaeze Okafor. The Adaeze was my mother's mother's name. I didn't know how it was spelled until I found it downstairs, in the third volume, page—" they stopped, breathed. They knew the page number. They would always know the page number. "Page sixty-one. Your father wrote it out, Wren. Spelled right. First thing I've ever seen spelled right."

"Hazel," Wren said softly, as if testing whether the name would bear weight.

"The winter of the flood," Hazel went on, because once the escapement catches you let the wheel run. "I was born during it, apparently, in a house in the Weeping District that isn't there anymore. My parents worked the flood year hauling people's things to higher ground, and then they worked the years after that doing everything, any work, and then—" Their hands had gone still on the table, both of them, palms down, pressing against the wood as if the table might otherwise tip. "Then the census came for the unregistered, the first time. Not with wagons then. With lists. And my parents went upstream to the works at Corver's Landing, and the letters stopped, and I was eleven, and Master Idris took me in because my father had repaired clocks for him once and because—" the bitterness came out flat and old "—because an apprentice who doesn't exist asks no wages worth recording."

They had not said the name aloud. They realized it only now, sitting in the lamplight with stew cooling in their bowl. Eleven years of *Hazel*, just Hazel, the syllable worn smooth as a pebble, stripped of everything behind it. Years of correcting no one, of letting documents call them nothing, of measuring the world in what could be carried.

"Okafor," Hazel said.

The word stood in the kitchen. Small. Enormous. It sounded like a struck bell in a room that had waited a long time for the hour.

"Okafor," they said again, steadier, hearing it fit in their own mouth. "It means—it meant something, where the name comes from. My father told me once, before. Something about... the one who goes out and comes back. Or the one who is known at home. I was young. I wish—" They stopped. There was no version of *I wish* that finished well.

Piotr reached across the table and laid his spotted hand over Hazel's wrist, finding it unerringly, the way he found chairs and doorframes—by some navigation finer than sight.

"Known at home," he said. "Good name. Accurate." He nodded toward the ceiling, toward the floors above, the ledger room, the stopped clock. "You are home. You are known. Both witnessed." He looked around the table with his moon-pale eyes. "We are all witnessed. That is what tonight was. Write it in your memory, if nowhere else. Memory is also a ledger, and it cannot be seized."

"It can be lost," Wren said quietly.

"Everything can be lost," Piotr agreed cheerfully. "That is why we eat stew while we can."

Marisol was laughing—wet-eyed, helpless laughter—and getting up to refill the bowls nobody had finished, and Wren was saying something about the third volume, about showing Hazel the entry properly, formally, with witnesses, and Hazel sat in the middle of it all with their own name ringing in their ears like a bell that would not finish dying away.

Later there would be more stew and Piotr telling a story about the comet of his youth that was certainly thirty percent true, and Wren falling asleep upright in her chair with an open notebook sliding off her knee, and Marisol covering her with a blanket without waking her, the practiced tenderness of a woman who had fed strangers into family for twenty years. Later there would be the climb back down to the clock level, and the cot, and the dark, and Hazel lying awake saying the name silently over and over, fitting it to their breathing like a new part into an old movement, checking the fit, checking it again.

But before sleep, near midnight, Hazel climbed the last flights instead—to the clock level, where the great dead mechanism hung in the cold like the skeleton of a whale, and beyond it, through the hidden door, to the ledger room where the lamps never quite went out.

They stood a long time before the shelves. Then they took down the third volume, opened it to page sixty-one, and read their own name by lamplight—read their mother's name, Adaeze; their father's, Emeka; the date, the flood winter, the careful strokes of a keeper who had believed that every person in Bellhaven deserved ink.

And that was when they heard it: footsteps on the tower stair below, many feet, unhurried, and the knock—not loud, not frightened, but official, knuckle-rhythm precise as a metronome—that sounded through the sleeping observatory like the first stroke of a clock that had no business running.
