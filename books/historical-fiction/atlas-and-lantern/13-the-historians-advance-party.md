## 13. The Historians' Advance Party

Silas Crane kept his boots polished for occasions he did not expect and could not have named. It was a habit from his father, who had been a customs man on the lower docks and who had taught him that a man's boots spoke before his credentials did. So when the knock came at the census office at an hour when no honest business was conducted—half past five in the morning, the river fog still thick enough to drink—Crane was already dressed, already booted, already awake in the way of men whose sleep had thinned over the winter into something more like vigilance.

The knock was wrong, first of all. Census clerks knocked twice, softly, apologetic. Soldiers did not knock at all; they entered. This knock was three measured raps, evenly spaced, patient—the knock of a person accustomed to doors opening for them but willing, this once, to observe the formality.

He opened the door onto three figures and a wall of gray coats behind them.

The woman in front was perhaps fifty, dressed in traveling wool of a cut Crane had only seen in imported bolts—plain, expensive, deliberately unadorned in the way that announced itself as a principle rather than a preference. She carried no bag. The two men flanking her carried hers between them anyway, along with their own: leather cases, brass-cornered, the kind made to survive rivers and revolutions.

"Master Silas Crane," the woman said. Not a question. She held a card of stiff gray stock, and on it, in ink so fresh it still shone faintly, a seal Crane did not recognize—a lantern inside a compass rose—and beneath the seal, a title longer than most sentences: *Provisional Directorate of Historical Continuity, Advance Commission on the Administrative Record.*

"You have me at an advantage," Crane said.

"We intend to." She stepped past him into the office without waiting to be invited, and her eyes went around the room the way Crane's own eyes went around a household—cataloguing, weighing, filing. "I am Historian-Commissioner Veyra Adelheid. These are my associates. We are four days ahead of schedule, which you will treat as a state secret until we say otherwise. The main delegation follows when the ice breaks. We are here to prepare."

"Prepare," Crane said.

"For the record," she said, "which is to say, for everything." She removed one glove, finger by finger, and laid it on his desk as if claiming it. "Show me your census."

* * *

Crane had spent thirty years learning what questions to ask, and in the space of an hour Veyra Adelheid taught him that he had been asking children's questions all his life.

She moved through his ledgers the way a surgeon moves through a body—not reading so much as knowing where to cut. She did not linger over names. She went straight to the margins, to the annotations, to the small coded marks Crane himself had devised: the double-stroke for households interviewed twice with discrepancies, the triangle for persons of unverified origin, the quiet circle he used for those who had answered correctly but too quickly, as though rehearsed.

"This notation system is yours," she said. It was not a question either. He was beginning to understand that this woman dealt exclusively in statements wearing question marks, or the reverse.

"It evolved," Crane said carefully. "The old regime's forms were not designed for—" He paused, choosing. "For a city like Bellhaven."

"A city that lies," Adelheid said pleasantly. "Yes. Most cities lie. That is precisely why history requires professionals." She tapped the triangle mark with one bare finger. "How many?"

"How many triangles?"

"How many people in this city whom your census has identified as existing outside the record. Unregistered. Irregular." She said the word *irregular* the way another woman might have said *unclean*, or possibly *interesting*; Crane genuinely could not tell which, and the not-knowing sat in his chest like swallowed river stone. "Give me a number."

Crane gave her a number. It was accurate—he was proud, in some cold compartment of himself, that it was accurate even now, under this particular gaze—and it included, somewhere in its middle ranks, an apprentice clockmaker whose file he had personally built across three interviews and whose pause, on the subject of burial grounds, he had transcribed into his own private notes with a care he had not extended to any other entry in thirty years.

Adelheid wrote nothing down. She simply looked at him for a long moment, and Crane understood that she had memorized the number, and him, and probably the precise temperature of the room.

"And the irregular persons employed by civic institutions?" she asked. "Observatories. Hospitals. The mint."

"The observatory," Crane said, before he could decide whether to say it, "employs a clockmaker who does not officially exist."

Something moved behind the commissioner's eyes—satisfaction, yes, but also something hungrier underneath it. "Good," she said. "You see the shape of the work. Sit down, Master Crane. Let us discuss the ice."

* * *

They sat at his own table, in his own chairs, and drank his own coffee, and by the end of the second cup Crane understood that he had never in his life held power, only proximity to it, and that proximity was about to be tested at a price he could not yet read.

"The uprising," Adelheid said—she said the word *uprising* the way one says *spring*, as a season already counted on the calendar—"will succeed. I do not say this to flatter you or to frighten you; I say it because our planning assumes it and our plans do not fail on assumptions. When the garrison changes hands, when the provisional council convenes in whatever hall they can heat, there will be a period—I want you to think of it as a period, an interval on a timeline—during which the past is unwritten."

"Unwritten," Crane repeated.

"Contested," she amended, with the small patience of a teacher correcting a promising student. "Every new order inherits two things: debts and stories. The debts they must pay or repudiate openly. But the stories—" she wrapped both hands around her cup, and Crane noticed for the first time that her nails were bitten to the quick, the single disorder in an otherwise immaculate person—"the stories can be *set*. A revolution that begins uncertain of its own ancestry ends by eating itself. So we arrive first. We write the ancestry. And the record upon which the new Bellhaven is founded must be clean."

"Clean," Crane said. The word had been used twice before in the orders he'd received by courier over the winter, always in italics of tone, always meaning the same thing.

"Clean," Adelheid agreed. "A census without irregular persons. A registry without anomalies. A city whose records show exactly the population the new state can account for, and no embarrassing remainder." She set down the cup. "There will be historians on the other side who will make careers out of discovering irregularities—unregistered families, erased names, anomalous archives. Every anomaly they find becomes a wound in the legitimacy of the state. We are here to close the wounds before they open."

And there it was, laid out with such reasonableness that Crane found himself nodding along before the horror of it arrived, late, like a guest who had lost his way. He had spent the winter flagging irregular persons, and he had told himself—and it had even been substantially true—that the work was the work, that a census taker counts what exists and notes what doesn't, and what happened downstream of his lists was downstream, and not his. But Adelheid was not describing downstream. She was describing the dam. She was describing the deliberate, documented, historically *certified* erasure of every person in Bellhaven whom the paperwork had failed to love—including, his mind supplied with terrible efficiency, a cook who had fed half the dock workers off no register at all for twenty years, a half-blind stargazer struck from the rolls decades ago, and an apprentice whose name appeared twice on his list with different details, which meant, in the grammar of Crane's own notations, that the boy existed twice nowhere.

"You will deliver the complete census," Adelheid said, "clean, certified under your own seal, before the ice breaks. After the thaw, after the council convenes, the historical record begins—and Master Crane, I want you to grasp this fully—whatever the record contains on the day it begins is all it will ever contain. History does not reopen files. It only cites them."

* * *

They left him at noon with a courier's seal, a deadline, and a case of blank certification forms bearing the lantern-and-compass stamp, and Crane stood alone in his office among his thirty years of meticulous lists and felt the first crack run through him not as doubt but as arithmetic.

He had always believed himself a man of the record. That was the pride of him, hidden under the politeness the way steel is hidden under a clerk's coat: other men guessed, other men remembered fallibly, but Silas Crane *wrote things down*, and what he wrote down was true, and truth arranged in columns was a kind of justice, wasn't it, even cold justice—even when the columns sent debtors downstream and marked mixed-blood households with triangles and put a boy's shifting answers under glass like a moth pinned by someone who found wings beautiful only when they were still.

But Adelheid was not asking him to record. She was asking him to *author*. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole distance between a mirror and a mask, and Crane stood at his window looking down at the frozen river and understood that he was being handed a pen and being told to use it as a knife.

The ice on the Bell stretched shore to shore, white-gray, seamed with pressure ridges where the current beneath worked at it night after night. By day the traffic crossed it—sledges, foot travelers, boys sliding where the ferry slip used to be. The whole city walked on the river as if the river were finished with flowing. Crane knew better. Everyone in Bellhaven knew better. The ice broke every year; the only question ever was the day and the hour, and the old-timers read it in the color of the ice, in the sound of it groaning at night, in the smell of the wind off the water.

Before the ice breaks. That was his deadline. And the ice, this year, was already talking in its sleep.

* * *

He spent the afternoon doing what he always did, because procedure was the only prayer he knew: he cross-referenced.

The clean census required him to certify, under his own seal, that his list matched the tax rolls of the old regime, reconciled and corrected. Reconciliation was his craft. He laid the documents side by side on the long table and began the slow, loving, terrible work—and it was there, an hour in, in the observatory district folio, that the arithmetic turned against him.

The observatory. Keeper Aldous dead these three years, the daughter holding the appointment in some limbo of inherited office. Staff of record: none living registered. Maintenance budget: drawn annually against a signature that had ceased, on paper, to exist. And appended, in his own hand, in his own notation, the entry he had made weeks ago and not yet resolved: *clockmaker present at premises; identity unverified; answers consistent but incomplete; parents unknown; place of burial—*

He had written *pending*.

Pending. Thirty years of columns and he had left a word like *pending* in the middle of a page that a commissioner would soon demand be sealed and certified clean. Pending meant unfinished. Unfinished meant visible. Visible meant that when the historians' full delegation arrived and the record began, the first wound in the new state's legitimacy would be a boy with careful hands and a pause where an answer should have been.

Unless the boy was removed from the page. Or the page was removed from the file. Or—

Or the pending was resolved honestly, which was a thing Crane had watched himself consider for three weeks now with the detached fascination of a man watching his own hand move toward a stove.

He closed the folio. He sat in the fog-colored light of his office and listened to the river mutter under the ice, and he thought about Veyra Adelheid's bitten nails, and what it costs a person to bite her nails down to the quick and still rule a room the way she ruled his. She was afraid of something. He had interrogated enough souls to know the signs. She was afraid of exactly what she had described: the record arguing back. Some ledger, some archive, some stubborn scrap of truth surviving in a cellar or a clock case, standing up on the day history began and saying *no, that is not how it was.*

And if such a thing existed in Bellhaven—and his instinct, thirty years deep, told him it did, told him the observatory was less a tower than a locked box—then everyone connected to it was not merely irregular. They were evidence.

Crane took out a fresh sheet. Under the lantern-and-compass seal, in his smallest, finest, most faithful hand, he began drafting the certification of a clean census for the city of Bellhaven, to be delivered before the ice breaks.

Halfway down the page his pen stopped at the observatory line.

Outside, far off, the river made a sound like a door settling in its frame, and every dog in the dock district began to bark.
