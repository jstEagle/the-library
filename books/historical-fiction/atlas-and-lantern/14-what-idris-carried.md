## 14. What Idris Carried

The message came by way of the ferryman, who came up the observatory hill faster than Hazel had ever seen him climb it, his breath ragged in the cold air, his boots leaving dark crescents in the frost on the stone steps.

"Your master," he said, when he could speak. "The census house turned him loose this morning. He's at my dock. He won't get on the boat. He keeps asking for you."

Hazel put down the polishing cloth. The wheel they had been restoring sat half-finished under the lamp, its teeth catching the light like a row of small promises. Nine days since the workshop had been sealed with wax-stamped paper and the words *BY ORDER OF THE CENSUS*. Nine days of sleeping in the tower room among Wren's father's coats, of eating Marisol's stew at the long table, of learning that a person could be technically employed and technically homeless in the same hour.

Nine days of not knowing what had become of Master Idris Okafor—no relation, whatever the shared name meant, which was nothing, which might have been everything.

"I'll go down," Hazel said.

Wren looked up from the ledger room doorway, where she'd been standing with a page in her hand, copying list number forty-something. "You shouldn't go alone."

"He asked for me."

"That's what worries me." She set down her pen but did not argue further. That was Wren's way: she planted her objections like stakes in a garden and then let you walk between them if you insisted. "Take your coat. And take Marisol's scarf—the gray one. You look like an apprentice who belongs to somebody when you wear it."

Hazel took the scarf without understanding why the instruction mattered, and understood it only later, walking through Bellhaven's streets, past doorways where faces appeared and vanished like fish rising to a surface and sinking again. A person who belonged to somebody was harder to take. The city had taught itself that arithmetic quickly.

* * *

Idris Okafor sat on an overturned crate at the end of the ferryman's dock, his hands flat on his knees, staring at the ice as though it were a mechanism he could not diagnose. The river groaned beneath him—a long, tectonic complaint, the sound of a frozen thing remembering it was water—and each time it groaned, his shoulders climbed toward his ears and stayed there, waiting for the next.

He had been gone eleven days. It had been longer than that, somehow. The man on the crate was smaller than the man Hazel remembered, and grayer, as if the census house had bleached him along with everything else. His right hand trembled in a fine, constant vibration—clockmaker's palsy, or something newer. His coat was the same coat he had worn every day of Hazel's apprenticeship, the brown one with the burn marks on the left sleeve from a soldering accident years before Hazel arrived, and seeing it was the worst part. The coat was familiar. The man inside it was a stranger wearing a beloved house.

"Master," Hazel said.

His head came up slowly, like a heavy gear engaging. When he saw Hazel his face did something complicated—relief and dread arriving together and cancelling neither out.

"You came." His voice was rough, unused. "I told them I wouldn't say anything until—" He stopped. Started again. "I've been sitting here trying to think how to say it, and I can't think how, so I'll just say it however it comes out and you'll have to forgive me or not, whichever you can do."

Hazel stood at the top of the dock steps. They did not come down. They were not sure, afterward, whether that had been instinct or accident.

"The workshop," Idris said. "They sealed it. Did you see? They sealed it while I was still inside. They let me take my coat, that's all. Eleven days of questions." He laughed, a terrible small sound. "Questions about clocks. Questions about you. Mostly about you."

"What did you tell them?"

"That's not where it starts." He looked at his trembling hands. "Where it starts is nine years ago. Before you. Before the flood, even—no. No, during the flood. The winter of the flood."

The river groaned again, longer this time, and somewhere downstream something vast shifted under the ice with a sound like a struck bell.

Hazel knew about the winter of the flood. Everyone in Bellhaven knew about it the way everyone knows about weather that happened before they could remember—it was a shape in other people's stories. The river had risen through the ice and taken the low district whole, and the government had counted the dead for three days and then stopped counting, because counting cost money and the dead cost nothing. Among the uncounted, always, were people who had never been on any list to begin with. People like the Okafors.

"My parents," Hazel said. Not a question. Something in their chest already knew where this was going, some apprentice's instinct for the shape of a mechanism before the case is opened.

"Your parents." Idris closed his eyes. "Amara and Tobias Okafor. Your father brought me a watch to fix, the week before the flood. A good watch, old, English movement, worth more than either of us made in a month. He was proud of it. He sat in my shop and we talked about escapements for an hour, two men who loved the same useless beautiful things, and he never once mentioned that he wasn't registered. Why would he? Why would anyone say that aloud?" His hands turned over, palms up, empty. "But I knew. I'd known for years. The whole street knew. You don't report the unregistered; they're the ones who bring you work nobody else will do, and they pay in what they have, and you look the other way because looking away is cheap and kind."

"And then?"

"And then the census came through the quarter. Not this new one—an older hunt, smaller, quieter, the kind that happens every few years when the tax rolls need padding or someone needs bodies for the downstream contracts. A clerk named Verrek. He came shop to shop with a list of descriptions. Height, coloring, trade, the watch—" Idris's voice caught. "A man who owns a fine old English watch. He didn't know the name. He wanted the name."

The ice groaned. Idris flinched and kept talking, faster now, as if he had opened the case back and everything inside was spilling at once.

"They questioned me twice. The second time they mentioned my lease. My lease, Hazel—my grandfather held that workshop, my father held it, and there was some irregularity in the title, some old debt of my father's that had never been discharged, and Verrek had it all in front of him, and he said, *these things resolve themselves, Master Okafor, depending on how cooperative citizens choose to be.*" He said the clerk's words in the clerk's voice, and then his own voice broke entirely. "It took me one night. One night to decide. I want you to understand that. It wasn't a long torment. I slept on it the way you sleep on buying lumber, and in the morning I walked to the census house and gave them two names and an address and the detail about the watch, and three weeks later the flood came, and whether they'd have taken your parents anyway, whether the flood saved the census men the trouble or covered it—I don't know. I'll never know. I've had nine years of not knowing and it has eaten everything in me that was worth keeping."

Hazel heard all of it. They heard it the way you hear the strike of a clock when you are standing next to the bell—with your whole body, your bones doing the listening.

They had imagined, over nine days of not knowing, every version of what the census house might have done to Idris. Beaten him. Starved him. Turned him against them. But they had not imagined this: that the wound predated the knife. That the man who had taught them to true a wheel, to cut a thread, to listen to an escapement the way you listen to a heartbeat—that man had carried their parents' names to the people who erased them, and had then taken in the orphan those people left behind, and had fed them and taught them and called them *apprentice* with what Hazel had always, until this moment, read as tenderness.

"Why," Hazel said. The word came out flat as sheet brass.

"Because surviving wears the same coat as living." Idris looked up, and his eyes were wet and steady. "Because I told myself I was keeping the workshop, and the workshop is where craft survives, and craft matters, and someday someone would need to learn what I knew—all the sentences a frightened man builds to stand inside while the storm passes. Because I was a coward with excellent reasons. That's the whole confession, child. There's no better version of it. I've been sitting on this dock for two hours trying to find one and there isn't."

* * *

What Hazel felt was not one feeling. That was the discovery that undid them—not rage, or not only rage. Rage was there, yes, hot and clean and simple, wanting to be used. But tangled in it, inseparable, humiliating, was recognition.

Hazel thought of the satchel packed nine days ago, of measuring the world in what could be carried. Of the forged apprenticeship paper folded flat and hidden in the lining. Of every small compromise already made in a short life lived invisibly: the name shortened, the history omitted, the eyes lowered at checkpoints, the lies told fluently and hated afterward. Survival had a shape, and Hazel knew its shape intimately, because Hazel wore it every day.

Idris had simply worn it longer, and heavier, and it had deformed him the way a loaded axle deforms.

That did not excuse him. Hazel held onto that with both hands, the way you hold a tool when the work wants to slip. Understanding was not forgiveness. Pity was not pardon. But fury and pity could sit in the same chest at the same time, apparently, arguing forever, and neither one winning, and the person carrying both just having to keep standing on the dock in the cold with the river shouting underneath.

"You knew me," Hazel said. Their voice sounded far away, like someone else speaking. "You knew whose child I was. You took me in anyway."

"I took you in because—" He stopped. His mouth worked. "There is no honest sentence that begins that way and ends well. I took you in because I owed a debt I couldn't repay to people who couldn't collect it. And because you were brilliant, and because you were alone, and because taking you in felt like building something over the hole I'd made. Repair work. That's all I've ever done with you—repair work on my own crime, using your life as the material." He wiped his face with the back of his shaking hand. "And I'd do the repair again. That's the most shameful part. Even knowing what I know, even sitting here having confessed—if you'd come to me hungry nine years ago I'd have fed you again, and been glad, and still been guilty. Both things are true. Nobody warned me both things could be true."

Down the dock, the ferryman stood very still by his boat, pretending to check a line that needed no checking. Word travels fast in Bellhaven's small trades; Hazel wondered what he saw from this distance. A master and an apprentice. A confession. Two figures on a frozen dock above a groaning river.

"Did they break you to make you tell this?" Hazel asked. "Eleven days. Is this what they wanted?"

"No." Idris almost smiled; it was a ruin of a smile. "They wanted the observatory. They wanted names—the keeper's daughter, the cook, the stargazer, the unregistered apprentice. I gave them nothing. I know how to give nothing; it's the one skill cowardice taught me that turned useful. They released me because a broken man wandering the streets asking for his apprentice makes a better lure than a locked cell. They're following me, Hazel. Or watching. Probably both. I came to warn you and to confess and I'm not sure which order, and it doesn't matter, because I may be the last honest danger you face today."

Hazel went down the steps then. Not to embrace him—they could not; their arms refused the instruction—but to stand close, at the distance of a workbench, close enough to see the burn marks on the sleeve and the tremor in the hand and the place where the gray hair grew thin over the scalp, all the small topography of a man Hazel had loved without knowing they were also learning from a study in damage.

"If you're a lure," Hazel said quietly, "then we shouldn't be seen talking long."

"Hazel—"

"Listen to me." And hearing their own voice take the tone Wren used, planting stakes, Hazel understood something about inheritance that had nothing to do with blood. "You confessed. I heard it. I don't forgive you today, and maybe not tomorrow, and maybe the day the ice breaks I'll feel differently, and maybe never. That's the truth and you'll take it, because you owe me truth. What you don't get to do is decide that confessing finishes it, or that being forgiven is yours to ask for. You get to work instead. Do you hear me? Work. That's the only absolution I know how to grant—there's a bench upstairs with your name on it if you want it, and a household that will feed you and watch you, both at once, forever. Take it or don't."

Idris stared at them. Something moved through his ruined face—shame and relief and a grief so large it looked almost like peace.

"You sound like your mother," he whispered. "She talked like that. Like the world was a mechanism she intended to keep running whether it cooperated or not."

"I wouldn't know," Hazel said, and watched the words land on him like stones, and did not take them back, and hated themselves a little for the satisfaction, and let themselves have it anyway. Fury and pity, tangled. Survival and betrayal, wearing the same brown coat with the burn on the sleeve.

"I'll take the bench," Idris said hoarsely. "Work. Yes. Whatever's hardest, give me that first."

"The hardest job isn't mine to give." Hazel turned toward the hill, toward the observatory, toward the ledger room where decades of stolen lives waited in careful ink. "But there are pages in that book that need copying, and a clock upstairs that needs to run true, and a city full of people who are about to learn whether anyone kept count of them. If you want repair work, Master Okafor—real repair work—we're short of hands and long on hours."

Behind them, faintly, the ferryman called out a warning: two men in census gray at the head of the dock road, walking unhurried, as men do when the net is already cast and there is no need to hurry the catch.

* * *

They went up the hill together through the falling dusk—master and apprentice, betrayer and bereaved, side by side but not touching, two silhouettes against the last red light on the ice—and Hazel spent every step of the climb thinking not about the confession but about the timing.

Crane's men had released Idris at dawn. Idris had spent hours on the dock gathering courage. And the watchers had followed at a distance wide enough that the ferryman had seen them coming and sent word ahead of them up the hill—which meant the net around the observatory was closing on a schedule now, patient and bureaucratic, one question at a time.

There was no more time to copy the ledger slowly, a few pages a night, safe and methodical. The ice would break soon; the historians would arrive; Crane's lists were filling in.

Hazel reached the observatory door, knocked the signal Marisol had taught them—two, pause, three—and found Wren already opening it, already reading their face, already reaching for the lamp.

"We start tonight," Hazel said. "All of it. Every page we can carry."

And below the hill, at the edge of the lamplight, the two men in gray stopped, and watched the observatory windows fill with light, and wrote something down.
