## 15. The Copying Nights

The lamplight in the ledger room was the color of strong tea, and Wren had learned, over the years since her father died, to do everything in it—read, mend, grieve. Now she watched Hazel Okafor learn it too: the way the light pooled in the inkwell, the way it turned the old ledger's pages amber at the edges, the way it made shadows of the letters so that some names seemed to step forward and others to shrink back into the paper.

They had agreed on three copies. One would stay in Bellhaven, broken into sections and hidden where only a clockmaker would think to look. Two would leave the city inside clock cases bound for towns upriver and down, consigned under Marisol's cousin's name to customers who had ordered repairs months ago and would be surprised to receive them early.

"Read first," Wren said. "Then write. Never both at once."

"Why?"

"Because my father said the eye is faster than the hand, and the hand is honest but the eye will flatter you. If you read and write together, you copy what you expected to see. Read it all, then put it down, then write what you remember. Then check."

Hazel looked at her sideways. "That's how you calibrate something."

"That's how you tell the truth about anything," Wren said. "You have to let go of it before you can hold it."

* * *

Hazel's hands were built for this work—that was the strange part. Ten years of filing pallet stones true, of drilling brass no thicker than a fingernail, of holding a graver steady through the long minutes when a slip meant a ruined movement—all of it had trained fingers that could make the same stroke twice, could keep a line level across a page without a rule. But the ledger did not care how fine their hands were. The ledger cared whether they could bear what they were writing.

The first night they worked on the earliest pages, and the earliest pages were mostly debts.

*Aldous, Tomas (keeper), recording: Ferrante, Odile, laundress, owes Dr. Hesse two crowns for the fever of her son, repaid in labor at the observatory, four months, cleared.* Hazel wrote *Ferrante, Odile* and thought of nothing, because there was nothing yet to think with. A name was a name. Ink was ink.

By the second week they had reached the flood years, and the ink stopped being ink.

*Okafor, Adaeze (mother), washerwoman, unregistered, married Okafor, Chidi (father), stevedore, unregistered. Child born winter of the flood. No state record exists. Recorded here so that one exists somewhere.*

Hazel's pen stopped above the page. A drop of ink gathered at the nib, fat and black, trembling.

Wren saw it happen from across the table. She did not say anything. She reached over and moved the inkwell six inches away, out of reach of the falling drop, and then went back to her own copying as if she had done nothing at all. The drop fell on the bare wood instead, and dried there, and is there still, under forty coats of varnish that will never be applied.

"I didn't know they'd be written like that," Hazel said finally. "Like people. Like entries in a shop book."

"Because that's what they are," Wren said. "People who owed each other things. People who paid. He just happened to think that being born was a debt the whole city owed you a record of."

* * *

They developed a rhythm without deciding to. Wren copied the left-hand pages, Hazel the right; they traded at midnight when the lamp needed trimming; whoever was not writing read aloud, softly, the names going into the dark room like coins dropped into a well too deep for sound to come back.

Some nights the names came fast and easy—apprentices taken in, wages owed and forgiven, a stove mended for a widow in exchange for fish. Other nights a single entry took an hour, because the entry was a death and the death had a sentence attached, and the sentence had been written by a man whose handwriting shook:

*Mbaye, Josephine, midwife. Taken by census men in the autumn. Where, unknown. Her hands delivered half the children on the south bank and the state has no idea any of them exist. If you are reading this, you exist. Write yourself down.*

Hazel wrote it twice—once in the copy for the council, once in the copy for the clock case bound for Harwick—and by the second writing their hand had stopped shaking, which frightened them more than the shaking had. They were getting used to it. That was the danger nobody warned you about: not that grief would break you, but that repetition would temper you, the way steel is tempered, until you could carry heat without showing it.

Marisol brought food up at hours that had stopped meaning anything—fried dough at what might have been dawn, cold rice at what might have been dusk. She never stayed. She stood in the doorway with the plate and looked at the two of them bent over the same table, and something in her face was soft and something in it was very old, and she would say only, "Eat. Dead copyists are bad copyists," and go back down the stairs counting them under her breath the way she counted everything.

Old Piotr came once, feeling his way along the wall, and put his ear against the ledger room door like a man listening to a case tick.

"Good," he pronounced. "It's running." And would not explain.

* * *

It was Hazel who solved the hiding problem, because it was a problem of tolerances, and tolerances were their native language.

"You're wrapping pages in cloth," they said, turning a finished bundle over in their hands. "Cloth shifts. Cloth is read by feel through the wood if anyone lifts the case and hears paper whisper. It has to be rigid. Still. Something that doesn't rattle even when the mechanism runs."

"So we glue them to boards—"

"No. Glue ruins them. We build false plates." They were already sketching on the back of a scrap. "Every long-case clock has a bedplate under the movement—brass or iron, thick enough to take machining. I make the plate a hair thinner than spec, mill a recess in the underside, seal the pages flat inside under a sheet of tin, and close it. To any eye, to any scale, to any hand, it's a bedplate. Heavy, ugly, functional. Nobody weighs a bedplate against its invoice twice."

Wren stared at the sketch. "You're putting my father's ledger inside clocks."

"I'm putting it inside the most respectable objects in the world. Nobody opens a clock to check the clock. They open it to check the time." Hazel looked up, and there was something in their face Wren hadn't seen before—not the apprentice's careful blankness, not the fugitive's watchfulness. Craftsmanship, worn openly. "And if the historians seize every clock case leaving this city, they'll find movements. Good ones. Running true. Because I'm not shipping empty cases, Wren. Every one of these clocks has to actually work, in someone's parlor, in another town, keeping actual time. The lie only holds if the truth around it also holds."

"The record rides inside the machine," Wren said slowly, understanding arriving like cold water. "The way my father always said it should."

"He built a clock that argues," Hazel said. "We're just giving it more to say."

* * *

The third case went together on a night when the wind had turned and come off the river smelling of thaw, weeks too early, though nobody in the room knew yet what that smell meant. Hazel fitted the last screw of the bedplate and set the movement on it, and it ran—a low, even heartbeat under their palms, the escapement ticking away the seconds with the indifference of anything true.

Wren had gone quiet across the table. She had a page in front of her that she had not copied. She had been not-copying it for some time.

"What is it?" Hazel asked.

"My mother."

Wren had mentioned her mother exactly once in all the nights, in passing, the way you mention weather. *Aldous, Liana, died of the lung, the year the ice jammed.* Now she pushed the original page across the table, and Hazel saw why it had taken her so long. It was not one entry. It was dozens. Her father had recorded his own wife in his secret book the same way he recorded everyone else—her debts, her fevers, the coat she sewed for the ferryman's daughter, and then, at the end, in the shaking hand:

*Aldous, Liana. Died the fourth of the ice month. The state does not know she existed, so the state cannot mourn her. I am mourning her here, in the only register that accepts her.*

Below that, smaller, almost invisible:

*Note to whoever keeps this book after me: you will be tempted to stop writing people down because it hurts. Write them down anyway. The hurt is the proof it matters.*

Wren's eyes were dry but her jaw was set like someone carrying something heavy down many stairs.

"He wrote me a note," she said. "In advance. Twenty years in advance he sat at this table and wrote instructions to me."

"Yes."

"And I'm following them."

"Yes."

"And it hurts exactly the amount he predicted, which makes me furious, because he was right about everything and he isn't here to be smug about it."

Hazel laughed—one short, startled sound—and then Wren laughed, wetly, and then they were both laughing and neither of them was only laughing, and the lamp burned steadily through all of it, and outside the clock face the night went on not knowing what it was.

* * *

Near the end of the fourth week, they sealed the second case. Hazel screwed down the bedplate, wound the movement, set the pendulum swinging, and wiped their hands on a rag that had once been white.

"Harwick's ferry leaves at noon," they said. "Marisol's cousin takes it to the depot himself. After that it's out of our hands."

Wren nodded and did not move. The room smelled of oil and ink and lamp-black and the ghost of fried dough. Nine hundred steps below, the river muttered against its ice, and the ice answered with sounds like distant doors.

"Hazel."

They looked up.

"When this is done—the copies gone, the council convened, whatever comes—" Wren stopped. Started again, differently, the way she rephrased a question she wasn't sure she wanted answered. "My father spent his whole life keeping a record nobody would thank him for. And I used to think that was a kind of madness. Now I sit at his table with you and I understand it was the opposite. It was the only sanity available." She turned the pen in her fingers. "I don't know how to say what the last month has been. Except that my hands know something my mouth hasn't caught up to."

Hazel set down the rag. Their heart was doing something complicated against their ribs, the way a striking train does just before the hour—everything gathered, nothing released.

"My hands know it too," they said quietly.

Neither of them moved. The lamp fluttered once in a draft from under the door, and steadied. There are sentences that would have been true in that moment and were saved for later, and later would prove they kept.

Wren picked her pen back up. "Page sixty-one," she said, and her voice was almost level. "The Mbaye apprentices. Read them to me."

Hazel read. The nib whispered. Below them the great clock ticked, and inside its ticking, in brass recesses and sealed tin and the patient memory of ink, the erased city of Bellhaven rode toward morning.

They did not hear the footsteps on the tower stair until they were on the last flight, light and unhurried and quite wrong for that hour—polite footsteps, meticulous ones, pausing twice on the way up as if checking something against a list.

Wren blew out the lamp.
