## 16. Crane Calls

The knock came at half past nine, which told Hazel everything before they opened the door.

Marisol had taught them the arithmetic of visitors in their first week at the observatory. Clerks came before eight, when the offices opened and a summons could be delivered with the day's first ink. Soldiers came whenever they pleased, because no one had ever taught them manners or fear. But men like Silas Crane—men who wanted to be welcomed, who needed a door opened for them rather than broken down—came mid-morning, when the household would be awake and settled and soft. Nine-thirty was the hour of a man who hoped you'd offer him tea.

Hazel heard the knock from the clock room, three floors up, where they had spent the dawn re-seating the escapement's pallet stones with a burnisher finer than any the workshop had ever owned. The sound traveled up the tower's stone throat and arrived in their chest before it reached their ears. Below, they heard Marisol's footsteps pause in the kitchen corridor—a hesitation of perhaps two seconds, the length of time it took her to count who was home—and then continue toward the door with the unhurried gait she wore like armor.

By the time Hazel descended to the entry hall, Wren was already there, standing in the shadow of the stair with her arms folded, and Old Piotr had materialized on the landing above with the silent competence of a man who had spent forty years learning how not to be noticed in his own observatory. Nobody said anything. There was nothing to say that hadn't been said over supper, in whispers, since Crane's name had first entered the house like weather.

Marisol opened the door.

"Master Crane." Her voice was warm as fresh bread. "You'll forgive the state of the hall. We don't get many callers."

"I'm sure you don't." Silas Crane stood on the threshold with his hat in one hand and a leather folio tucked under his arm, and he looked, Hazel thought, exactly like what he was: a man built entirely out of patience. He was not large. He was not small. He had the kind of face that a witness would struggle to describe an hour later—the kind of face, Hazel understood with a cold turn in their stomach, that was designed for looking at other people. His eyes were pale gray and very still, and they moved across the entry hall the way Hazel's fingers moved across a movement: reading the wear, noting the repairs, finding where the mechanism had been touched most recently. "I've come about the clock."

"The clock," Marisol repeated pleasantly.

"The master clock. It stopped some weeks ago, I understand. The whole city lost its official time." He smiled—it was a good smile, practiced but not unkindly so, the smile of a man who had learned that courtesy opened more doors than authority. "That's a serious matter, Mrs.—"

"Marisol is fine."

"Mrs. Marisol. A city without its official time is a city that can't certify contracts, can't schedule its courts, can't prove when anything happened. And yet—" he tilted his head, and the folio shifted under his arm, "—the city seems to be muddling through. Markets open. Ferries run. People wake when they always woke. It makes a man wonder what official time was for in the first place."

Behind him, past his shoulder, Hazel could see the bridge, gray in the flat morning light, and the census wagon parked at its near end with its canvas sides buttoned down. Two clerks sat inside it, visible through the gap in the flaps, writing. Always writing.

"You'd best come in," Marisol said, "before you give a civics lecture on my doorstep."

* * *

He took tea. Of course he took tea. He sat in the observatory's lower parlor—the room Wren called the waiting room, because her father had received petitioners there, astronomers and aldermen and once, according to family legend, a man claiming to have caught the moon in a bucket—and he accepted the cup Marisol gave him with both hands, and he complimented the blend, and he asked where she had learned to brew it, and Marisol said *a long way from here* in a voice that closed the subject like a drawer.

Hazel stood by the window with a tray of tools they did not need, holding them the way a soldier holds a rifle at rest. Wren sat opposite Crane with her hands folded in her lap, wearing her father's severity like a borrowed coat. Piotr had not come down. His absence was itself a presence; everyone in the room knew the old man existed officially nowhere, and Crane's eyes had flicked toward the ceiling twice already, cataloguing the sounds of someone moving overhead.

"Now," Crane said, setting down his cup. He opened the folio on his knee. "The master clock. When did it stop?"

"Six weeks ago tomorrow," Wren said. "The eleventh."

He wrote it down. Hazel watched the pen move—small, neat strokes, a hand trained to record without smudging—and felt something twist behind their ribs. It was the same hand, Hazel realized, that had written whatever was in the wagons on the bridge. The same hand that had written *Okafor* twice, with different details, in different columns. A hand that never hurried.

"And do you know why it stopped?"

"Gears wear," Wren said. "Springs fail. It's an old machine. My father maintained it for thirty years, and he's dead, and things fall out of true when the hands that kept them are gone." She delivered it flatly, without grief performed or concealed, and Hazel marveled at it even while fearing for her: how completely she had made herself into a woman whose only crime was inheritance.

"Mm." Crane turned a page. "And the repair. Who is doing the repair?"

"My father's last apprentice finished his term in the spring and went downstream. I hired someone new."

"Someone new." The pen paused. "May I have the name?"

"You may ask for it," Wren said, "and I may tell you that my clockmaker prefers not to appear in government papers. That's common enough among tradesmen. Ask any guild master how many of his journeymen want their names in a census book."

"It is common," Crane agreed mildly. "That's precisely why I keep lists of the exceptions."

The silence that followed had weight. Hazel counted it—one, two, three heartbeats—and used it to move, setting the tool tray down on the sideboard with a craftsman's clatter, making themselves ordinary, making themselves furniture. *Be a thing in the room,* Marisol had whispered to them that morning, pressing a cup of chicory into their hands. *Not a person. Things don't get asked questions.*

"Perhaps I should introduce myself properly," Crane said, and now he looked—not at Wren, not at Marisol—at Hazel. Directly. For the first time since entering the house, those pale eyes settled and stayed. "Silas Crane, Office of Enumeration and Residence. I'm charged with completing the census of Bellhaven before the transition of administration. Which means, among other things, that every person employed in a civic institution such as this observatory ought to appear in my records." He smiled again, and this time the smile had edges. "Your clockmaker, I think, does not."

"They're working," Wren said. "In the tower. They don't come down for visitors."

"No," Crane said softly. "I imagine they don't."

* * *

What happened next, Hazel would spend many nights afterward taking apart and examining, the way one examines a movement that has failed in a way it should not have failed.

They stepped forward. Into the light. Into the center of the parlor, tools in hand, apron dusted with brass filings, and heard themselves say, in a voice steady and pleasant and utterly foreign:

"You needn't talk around me, sir. I'm the clockmaker."

Crane turned. The stillness of him was total, and Hazel understood suddenly that this was the moment he had come for—that the entire visit, the tea, the questions about the clock, had been a mechanism of his own design, and this was the point in the train where the pallet stone catches or slips.

"Ah," he said. "The clockmaker."

"Hazel." Just the name. No surname. The omission sat in the air between them like a coin placed on a table.

"Hazel." He let it rest there, tasting the shape of it, the space where a family name should have followed and didn't. Then he opened the folio again, and produced a sheet, and made a great show of consulting it though Hazel would have bet the master clock itself that he knew every line of it by heart. "Forgive me. It's just that I have a note here—an apprentice of mixed heritage, unregistered, formerly attached to the workshop of one Idris Okafor on Tanner's Row. Age given variously as seventeen and nineteen. Sometimes called Hazell, sometimes Haze, once—I enjoy this one—*the boy who fixes watches*, though I understand the description may not fit." He glanced up, courteous, terrible. "You wouldn't know anything about that person."

The room held its breath. Somewhere above them, faintly, Piotr's chair scraped and went silent.

Hazel felt the lie assemble itself in their mouth the way a gear train assembles under the hands—each tooth finding its seat, each pivot dropping into its jewel, the whole mechanism coming alive with a smoothness that should have taken effort and instead took none at all. This was the shame of it, the thing they would hate in themselves later, in the dark, in the narrow bed Marisol had made up for them: that it was *easy*. That all those years of being nobody, of measuring the world in what could be carried, of answering doors with their body angled to block the view inside—all of it had been training, and the training held.

"Sounds like half the apprentices in this city," Hazel said. "Tanner's Row turns them out by the dozen. Master Okafor wasn't particular about papers if you could file a pivot flat." They shrugged, and the shrug was perfect, artless, worn at the elbows like an old coat. "If your fellow's mixed-heritage and unregistered, he's got plenty of company. You'd know that better than I would—you're the one keeping the lists."

Something moved behind Crane's eyes. Not anger. Hazel had expected anger, or suspicion, or the thin-lipped displeasure of a man contradicted. What moved there was worse: *interest*. The interest of a craftsman encountering unexpected quality in another man's work.

"That's well put," Crane said. "You'd make a fair clerk yourself, I think."

"I'd make a poor one. I like machines that tell the truth."

"Do they?" He rose, cup in hand, and crossed to the window where Hazel stood, and for a moment they were shoulder to shoulder, two people looking out at the bridge and the wagon and the river beyond it, white and groaning under its winter lid. "My trade and yours aren't so different, you know. I take scattered facts—names, dates, addresses, answers that shift between visits—and I build them into a mechanism that shows what a city actually is. Most people lie to me the way clocks lose time: a little, everywhere, until the whole works reads wrong. My job is to find the master error. The one false wheel that throws off every dial downstream." He drank his tea. "When I find it, I correct the record. Do you know what correcting means, in my office?"

"I expect it means erasing."

"It means *certifying*," Crane said gently. "Erasing is what happens afterward, when the historians arrive. I only ever establish what's true. Whether anyone wants it to remain true after me—" he set the empty cup on the sill, "—is above my station."

He gathered his hat and folio. At the door he paused, and turned back, and gave Hazel a small bow, precise as a jeweler's tolerance.

"Thank you for the tea, Mrs. Marisol. Miss Aldous—my condolences on your father, and my compliments on your clockmaker. The city will be glad to have its official time back." His gaze rested on Hazel one final moment, mild and bottomless. "I do hope the work goes quickly. Winter won't hold forever. When the ice breaks, everything that's been frozen in place starts moving again. Records especially. It's remarkable, the things a thaw uncovers."

The door closed behind him. They listened to his boots descend the outside steps, cross the yard, fade toward the bridge.

* * *

For a long moment nobody moved. Then Marisol set down the teapot with a crack of china against tray, and Wren put her face in her hands, and upstairs a door opened and Piotr's voice drifted down the stairwell, dry as old paper: "Well. Is the house still standing?"

"Barely," Marisol called back.

Hazel stood at the window and watched the census wagon pull away across the bridge, and waited to feel relief, and found instead a sickness rising in their throat like floodwater.

Because they had done it. They had lied to the most dangerous man in Bellhaven—to his face, in his own language, in the idiom of mechanisms and tolerances and master errors—and he had *believed* it, or seemed to, or at least had left without them in irons. And the feeling underneath the terror was not triumph. It was the horrible, humming pleasure of a lock yielding to a pick. The seduction of it. All those years of invisibility, and Hazel had told themselves they hated it, had carried their life in a satchel because they were forced to—but standing in that parlor, lying fluently, beautifully, they had felt *competent*. Seen, even, in the only way the world allowed them: as a performance so seamless it erased the performer.

"He'll be back," Wren said quietly. She had lifted her head, and her eyes were red-rimmed but level. "You know that. Men like him don't leave a door they can't open. They mark it and come back with better tools."

"I know."

"He asked you nothing directly. Did you notice? Not one question with your name in it. Everything was *your fellow*, *that person*, *someone new.*" She laughed, short and bitter. "He was building you a door to walk through, and you walked through it, and now he knows the door worked."

Hazel turned from the window. On the sill, where Crane had set it down, the teacup sat with its handle turned outward—exactly, Hazel noted with a lurch, the way a left-handed man replaces a cup after drinking. A small fact. A recorded fact. The man collected them the way the ledger collected lives.

"He asked about the ice," Hazel said. "Twice. When it breaks. Why would a census man care about the river?"

Marisol had gone still by the tray. When she answered, her voice had lost all its warmth, and what remained beneath it was older and harder than anything Hazel had heard from her.

"Because the historians are coming with the thaw, child. Everyone in this city who counts such things knows it. And a man like that doesn't care about the river." She began stacking cups, hard and fast, china ringing like small bells. "He cares about what floats up when it breaks."

That night Hazel lay awake in the narrow bed under the eaves, listening to the wind worry the tower, and did not think of Crane at all—or told themselves they didn't. They thought of their own voice, smooth and easy in the parlor, producing lies the way the escapement produces seconds: evenly, reliably, without visible effort. They thought of how good it had felt.

And far below, through the floor and the stone and the frozen ground, they heard—for the first time that winter, faint as a heartbeat under a pillow—the river begin to groan.
