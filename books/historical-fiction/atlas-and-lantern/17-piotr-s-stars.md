## 17. Piotr's Stars

Old Piotr did not sleep the way other people slept. Other people lay down at night and surrendered; Piotr lay down and continued, in a slower register, like a pendulum swinging through honey. He had done it for forty years—slept in shallow draughts, rising two, three times between dusk and dawn to climb to the roof platform with his brass instruments and his notebook wrapped in oilcloth, because the sky did not keep office hours, and neither did he.

So it was Piotr who heard Hazel come down the tower stairs that morning before the sun had cleared the ice fog, footsteps too careful for someone who belonged there. Guests walked like that. Fugitives walked like that. He put the kettle on the spirit stove in the basement kitchenette—he kept one, against Marisol's standing objection that a kitchen under an observatory was an insult to kitchens—and waited, because he had learned long ago that people climbing toward you or away from you always arrived if you simply let the stairs do their work.

Hazel came in rubbing their hands, breath smoking, and stopped short when they saw him.

"I didn't know anyone was down here."

"Nobody is," Piotr said. "That is rather the point of me." He gestured at the second chair. "Sit. The kettle is small but honest. Like its owner."

Hazel sat. They looked, this morning, the way they had looked since the census man's visit—a person listening for a knock inside every silence. Piotr recognized it. He had worn that face himself for four decades, so comfortably now that he sometimes forgot it was a disguise at all and not simply his face.

"You are up early," Hazel said.

"The stars dismissed me late." He poured the water over the grounds and watched the bloom of it, dark as river mud. "You could not sleep either. Marisol says you pace the gallery at two o'clock. She notices everything. It is her gift and her burden."

"I wasn't—" Hazel began, then abandoned the lie halfway out, which Piotr counted as a good sign. A liar who lied about lying would have finished it. "I keep thinking about the clock. About what's underneath it. The ledger room, the secret dial. I keep thinking: how many more things does this building have under it?"

Piotr smiled into his cup. It was not a kind question, exactly, but it was an honest one, and honesty from the young had a flavor he had come to savor the way other old men savored brandy.

"That," he said, "depends on whether you mean the building or the world."

* * *

He made them finish their coffee first. This was not cruelty; it was ritual, and rituals were load-bearing, like keystones. Then he took up the lamp and led them out of the kitchenette into the basement proper—the part nobody visited, where the foundation stones sweated frost and the air tasted of cold iron and pigeon droppings filtered down from the belfry like slow weather.

There was a door. Of course there was a door; the observatory was a building made almost entirely of doors that pretended to be walls. This one hid behind a rack of disused counterweights, and its lock was older than Wren, older than Wren's father, older than the census bureau itself, though Piotr had replaced its mechanism twice himself, quietly, across the years, the way one replaces the heart valves of an institution one has decided to outlive.

The key hung on a nail behind a loose brick. It had hung there for twenty years, in plain sight of anyone who thought to look behind loose bricks, and no one ever had. People, Piotr had found, saw only what papers told them to see. This was the great flaw of governments and the great mercy of archivists.

"Marisol knows?" Hazel asked.

"Marisol knows there is a door. She has chosen not to know what is behind it. That is her way of keeping it safe—if she does not know it, Crane cannot make her carry it." He turned the key. The lock gave with a sound like a knuckle cracking. "Wren knows. Her father knew. And now you know, which means I have decided something about you, and you were not consulted. Forgive me. At my age one stops asking permission for the necessary things."

"What's down there?"

"Forty years of impossible things," Piotr said, and opened the door.

* * *

The room beyond was long and low, cut into the bedrock the observatory stood on, and it was full—not of furniture, barely of that—but of paper. Shelves of it, hand-built, sagging slightly, each shelf numbered in a cramped hand. Notebooks bound in oilcloth and waxed canvas, hundreds of them. Boxes of glass photographic plates, each wrapped in tissue. Star charts annotated past all legibility, layered on layered, like sediment. And hanging from pegs along one wall, dozens of clock pendulums, each tagged, each labeled with dates and temperatures, retired soldiers of the calibration wars.

Hazel stood in the doorway and did not move. Piotr understood. There was a moment, entering any archive, when the weight of what was recorded pressed back against you like water against a dam, and either you went in or you spent your life outside it. He had watched Wren's father hesitate at his own ledger room the same way.

"What am I looking at?" Hazel said finally, and their voice had gone quiet in the way voices go quiet in churches.

"An argument," Piotr said. "Forty years long. Come. Mind the plates."

He led them down the aisle of shelves, touching spines as he passed, greeting each notebook by its decade the way one greets old acquaintances. "Here. 1831 to 1844. My first instruments, God help me—a quadrant with a crack in the frame and a telescope I bought off a sea captain who swore it had never been dropped, which was a lie on both counts." He pulled a volume and opened it on the reading table beneath the lamp. The pages were dense with figures, columns of numbers marching in disciplined ranks, margins crowded with sketches and annotations in three languages. "And here, later work. Better instruments. Worse government."

Hazel leaned over the page. "These are observations. Positions of stars. Declination, right ascension—I don't know the terms, but I can see the pattern. You measured the sky."

"I measured the sky," Piotr agreed. "Every clear night for forty years. And every measurement I took disagreed with the official almanac."

He said it flatly, without drama, and watched it land. Hazel frowned, the frown of a craftsman encountering a tolerance problem. "Disagreed how?"

"Not wildly. Not enough for a farmer to notice his seasons wandering. But measurably. Systematically. The official ephemeris—the tables the ministry publishes, the ones every clockmaker in Bellhaven calibrates against—they drift. A few seconds a year. More lately. As if the sky itself were being edited by committee." He tapped the page. "But the sky does not take instruction. So either my instruments lie, or the almanac lies. And I tested my instruments against my instruments, and against the transit of known stars, and against the pendulum experiments—which you may see tagged on the wall there, each one dated—and my instruments tell the truth within two-tenths of a second of arc. Which means the almanac is not astronomy at all. It is policy wearing astronomy's coat."

Hazel stared at him. "Why would anyone falsify star tables?"

"Why does anyone falsify anything?" Piotr shrugged. "Because the truth is inconvenient to the schedule. If the official time drifts, and every clock in the city follows the official time, then the city runs on a fiction—and whoever controls the fiction controls the trains, the tides, the market bells, the court schedules. Bellhaven keeps time by decree. It always has. Your master clock upstairs is not merely a machine, child. It is the hinge between what the sky says and what the ministry wishes the sky had said. Wren's father knew it. He geared the secret dial to prove it. And I—" he spread his hands at the shelves, at the forty years of lamplit nights stacked around them in paper and glass— "I supplied the evidence. Slowly. Obsessively. One impossible observation at a time."

"They declared it impossible," Hazel said slowly, remembering. "You said. The state declared your observations impossible."

"They did better than that. They declared *me* impossible." His smile was thin and dry as old varnish. "In 1853, after I published a modest correction—very polite, very mathematical, the kind of thing that should have earned me a medal—the ministry reviewed my credentials and discovered that Piotr Antonovich Reznik, observer, did not exist. No birth record. No citizenship. An asylum seeker's landing paper from thirty years prior, misfiled. A ghost with a telescope." He laughed, softly, at the old absurdity of it. "They could not dispute my data, so they disappeared its author. It was very efficient. They have used the technique ever since. You would know, I think."

Hazel flinched. Piotr had not meant it as a blow, only as a handrail, but blows and handrails feel alike to the grasping.

"So you stayed," Hazel said. "Here. Working for nothing. Watching the sky they said you couldn't see."

"I stayed because the work needed doing and the doing needed a hiding place, and this building has both. The observatory is funded as a curiosity—an ornament, a place for ministers' wives to look at the moon through a clean lens. Nobody audits an ornament." He closed the notebook gently, the way one closes a coffin lid. "Wren's father found me on the roof one night, forty years ago, and instead of reporting the trespasser he asked to see my figures. We argued until dawn. By winter I had keys. By spring, a room. He was the finest man I ever quarreled with."

* * *

Hazel moved among the shelves then, and Piotr watched them do what a clockmaker's hands cannot help doing: reading structure. They touched a box of plates, lifted a pendulum tag, ran a finger along the numbering system, and Piotr saw the understanding arrive in stages, like dawn coming up over the ice—first the shape of the thing, then the intention, then the terrible scale.

"This isn't a hiding place," Hazel said at last. They had gone a little pale. "This is a workshop. The ledger room upstairs, the dial, your observations—you've been building a case. All of you. For decades. A whole apparatus for proving—" They stopped, searching for the word.

"Yes," Piotr said. "Say it."

"For proving that the record is a lie. And keeping the true one until anyone cared to ask."

"Until someone was *able* to ask," Piotr corrected. "That is the difference, and it matters. Wren's father did not build all this hoping for a revolution. He built it hoping for a competent clerk, someday, with authority and a conscience. I built mine hoping for nothing at all. Keeping the record is not a strategy, child. It is a temperament. You do it because the alternative is to vanish twice—once from the world and once from the memory of the world. I refused the second vanishing. That is the entire politics of an old man with a telescope."

Hazel stood very still among the shelves. Then, quietly: "I thought I'd been sheltered here. Taken in. An accident of charity."

"And so you have been. Charity and accident are real. But you have also been *recruited*, which you would have realized sooner if any of us were subtle." Piotr permitted himself a genuine smile. "Consider: a keeper dies, leaving a daughter who guards the clock. A cook hides twenty years in the one building nobody inspects. A ghost astronomer files forty years of forbidden measurements in the basement. And then, into this household, arrives precisely the one apprentice in Bellhaven capable of repairing the sabotaged escapement and reading a maker's hidden intentions in gearwork. Coincidence is a word the young use to avoid feeling chosen. Wren's father chose, before he died, even though he could not have known whom he was choosing. He left the door unlocked for whoever could open it. You opened it."

Hazel looked at their own hands. Piotr watched them do it and thought: yes. There it is. Hands learn faster than hearts.

"My parents," Hazel said. "Their names are in the ledger upstairs. Their watch is in my satchel. Their daughter—" they caught themselves, corrected, "—their child is standing in your basement. And now you're telling me there's a whole lineage under this floor. Keepers. That I'm not the first person the state erased who ended up here."

"You are the newest," Piotr said. "Which is different from the last. That distinction will matter soon."

"How soon?"

Piotr crossed to the far shelf—the current decade, the notebooks still being filled—and drew out the most recent volume, its oilcloth still smelling faintly of lamp oil and night air. He laid it open on the table to the last completed page, and turned it so Hazel could read.

The observations had grown erratic. Not the instruments—the sky. Or rather, the relationship between the sky and the official time had grown worse, faster, in the last months. The drift accelerating. The fiction straining against the fact of it.

"The almanac is being revised again," Piotr said. "Quietly. The new regime's historians will bring new tables when they come—new time for a new order, corrected to suit whatever story they intend to tell. When the historians arrive, every record in this city becomes negotiable except the ones already hidden in rooms like this one." He tapped the page. "The river groans, the ice thins, and the men with new calendars are on their way. I give it weeks. Perhaps less. The sky has been early all winter, and rivers follow skies."

Hazel read the column of figures in silence. Piotr let them. Some understandings should not be hurried.

At last Hazel said, "Then we're not copying the ledger fast enough."

"No," Piotr agreed. "You are not."

He blew out the lamp, locked the impossible room behind its brick and its nail, and climbed the dark stairs beside the newest keeper of Bellhaven's oldest argument—while overhead, faint through the dome, the first stars of evening came out, punctual, indifferent, and true.

* * *

That night the whole household felt it: a change in the wind, a wrongness in the cold. Marisol salted the soup twice without noticing. Old Piotr went up to the roof platform at midnight and stood a long while with his face to the south, where the wind was coming from unseasonably warm, carrying the smell of open water from a river still a hundred miles frozen.

Below him, muffled by stone, the ice groaned again—longer this time, and deeper, like a giant turning in a sleep about to end.

Piotr made his observation, wrote his figure, and noted in the margin, in a hand steady after forty years of nights: *Thaw early. Prepare.*
