## 18. The Bridge at Dusk

Marisol Vane had crossed the Weir Bridge eleven thousand times, or near enough, and she had a system for it. She always walked on the downstream side, where the fishmongers' stalls threw up their brine-and-ice smell and nobody lingered. She always carried something—a basket, a parcel, once a live hen that did more for her anonymity than any disguise could have, because a woman wrestling a hen was a woman with an errand, and errands were invisible. And she never, ever looked at the census clerk's table at the bridge's midpoint unless the looking was part of the shopping.

Twenty years of crossing had taught her that the trick to hiding in plain sight was not stillness. Stillness was what guilty things did. Rabbits froze; women buying herring did not. So she moved through Bellhaven like weather, like smoke from a chimney, like one of those small mercies a city stops noticing because it has always been there: the observatory's cook, broad-hipped and loud-laughed, known by name to half the market and by no record to any of them.

She had a system. She had eleven thousand crossings.

On this evening, the fourth-to-last before she would need to cross carrying copies of a dead man's ledger, she broke every rule at once.

It started with the light. Dusk came down over the river the way flour comes through a sieve—slow, then all at once—and the lamps along the bridge were lit late because the lamplighter had taken sick, and the shadows pooled deep between the brackets. Marisol had meant to go over earlier. She had meant to. But Piotr had wanted his barley soup thick, and Hazel had come in late from the tower with oil-stained fingers and that look they wore when they were thinking about something too large to say, and Wren had asked twice whether there was more of the pickled cabbage, and somehow the hour had gone soft and slipped away from her, the way hours did in that household, and now here she was on the downstream side with an empty basket and the river running black beneath her feet.

She told herself: nothing has changed. The table is the same table. You are the same woman.

The table was not the same table.

There were two clerks now instead of one, and they sat behind a folding screen rather than in the open, and beside them stood a soldier in the gray coat of the provisional garrison—not the old garrison's blue, not yet the historians' black, but that in-between color that meant orders had changed upstream somewhere. And the younger of the two clerks was not writing. He was watching. Not at the paper in front of him, at the faces going by.

Marisol knew him. That was the first thing her body understood, before her mind could arrange any words around it: *I know that face.* Tobin Ferro. Sixteen when he'd been a kitchen boy at the customs house, forever underfoot, forever hungry, and she had fed him—God help her, that was the whole catastrophe of it—she had fed him meat pies on saints' days for two years running, and he had called her *Doña Marisol* with his mouth full, and he had been kind to her in the small ways kindness gets done in kitchens, and now he was sitting behind a screen with a list, and his eyes went up from the crowd the way a man checks a mirror, and they found her.

They found her, and they stopped.

Not recognition—not yet. It was the pause of a man who has seen something familiar in the wrong room. A cousin at a funeral. A neighbor at a wedding across town. His head tilted, very slightly, the way Piotr's dog used to tilt its head at a sound it couldn't place.

Marisol did not freeze. Twenty years had bought her that much at least: her feet kept their market pace, her basket swung on its arm, her chin stayed at its shopping angle. But inside her chest something dropped through floor after floor, like a coin down a well, and at the bottom of the well was a very cold and simple thought: *He knows my cooking. Nobody forgets who fed them.*

She was forty paces from the table. Thirty-five. She began, without deciding to, to assemble a story—the observatory sends me, I am on the maintenance rolls, Wren Aldous will vouch, all of which was true and none of which mattered, because truth was not what the table was for. She had watched clerks long enough to know that. The table was not a place where true things got confirmed. It was a place where names got weighed, and some names tipped the scale into a wagon.

Thirty paces. Ferro stood up.

He didn't stand quickly. That was the worst of it—the courtesy of it, the deliberateness. He stepped out from behind the screen the way a man steps out of a doorway to greet someone he is pleased to see, and the older clerk looked up at the movement, and the soldier shifted his weight off the rail, and the whole apparatus of the bridge—the crowd, the stalls, the dusk itself—seemed to lean toward her by one degree.

*Keep walking,* she told herself, and she did, and she felt her heart going ahead of her down the planks like something loose.

Then the river saved her, or the river's people did.

"OI! Mind your basket, woman!"

The shout came from below and to her left, from the water, where the ferry ran its dusk crossing from the weir stairs to the tannery landing. Old Ansel's ferry—or not old Ansel anymore; Ansel had died two winters back and left the boat to his nephew, a wide-shouldered quiet man named Dov whom Marisol knew chiefly through the observatory's kitchen door, because Dov's wife took day-old bread off her hands and Dov himself, three years ago, had needed a doctor for a daughter's fever and had not asked why the observatory's cook knew a doctor who kept no records and accepted payment in candles. Some debts you pay in bread. Some you pay in silence. The Aldous ledger, she had learned, was only the written half of a much larger book that the poor of Bellhaven had been keeping among themselves for generations.

Dov was standing in the flat boat with a pole, and the ferry was drifting in close to the weir stairs on the city side, ten yards short of the bridge's shadow, and he was shouting again—"Mind your basket!"—and gesturing, wildly, rudely, at her as though she were a stranger who had nearly cost him his catch, and Marisol—who had spent twenty years reading kitchens—read him in half a second.

She turned on him with full theatrical fury. "Mind your own net!"

"Throw it down here, then, if you're so careless! See if I care!" He jabbed the pole at the water by the stairs. "Come collect it yourself, since you're so high and mighty!"

It made no sense, and it made perfect sense, and the crowd around her—fishwives and dockmen and a knot of soldiers off duty—laughed at the quarrel because a quarrel between a cook and a ferryman was the best entertainment the dusk had offered all week. And Ferro, twenty paces behind her now, slowed. Because you cannot politely detain a woman who is publicly, loudly, and with witnesses engaged in a screaming match with a boat.

Marisol stormed to the weir stairs, basket swinging, dignity intact, and went down the wet steps while the crowd chuckled overhead, and Dov hauled the ferry against the stones, and she stepped in, and the boat pushed off into the dark, and the last thing she saw of the bridge was Ferro's face above the rail—uncertain, courteous, troubled—watching her shrink downstream with an empty basket and a heart going like a rabbit's.

Which, she thought, gripping the gunwale with both hands, was exactly what she'd sworn never to be again.

* * *

The river at night smelled of tar and cold stone. Dov poled them out past the weir and let the current take them, and for a while neither of them spoke, because the water carries voices strangely and both of them knew the banks had ears these days.

Then Dov said, not turning his head, "That boy at the table. He worked the customs house."

"He did."

"He looked at you a long time."

"Aye." Marisol let out a breath that shook more than she wanted it to. "Aye, he did."

Dov nodded slowly, the pole dipping and drawing, dipping and drawing. "My wife says the bread's stopped coming from the observatory kitchen. Says you send it by the boy now, round by Tanner's Lane."

"The bread will keep coming," Marisol said. "One way or another, the bread will keep coming."

"That's not what I asked."

"I know it isn't." She watched the lights of Bellhaven slide along her right, the observatory tower rising black against the last orange of the sky, its clock face dark—that stopped face, that blind eye the whole city had gotten used to telling time without. "What you're asking is whether they've marked me. And I can't answer that, Dov. I can only tell you that tonight a boy I fed for two years stood up from a census table because he remembered my face, and I don't know yet whether he wrote it down."

The pole drew, dipped. The tannery landing came up on the left, stinking and dim, and Dov brought the boat alongside it gentle as setting down a sleeping child.

"Ansel ferried people the state said weren't there," Dov said at last. "For thirty years. Never once asked what they were running from or to. He said the river doesn't check papers." He looked at her then, and in the dark his face was just a shape, but his voice was steady as the current. "You fed my girl's doctor in without asking me what I'd done. So get out of my boat, Doña Marisol, and don't use my landing again for a month, and next time you need the river, send word by the bread."

She climbed the ladder onto the tannery dock, and behind her the pole pushed off, and the dark swallowed boat and man together, and Marisol stood a moment in the tannery stink with her empty basket and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that she had just spent the last coin of her invisibility.

Eleven thousand crossings. And the twelfth-thousandth would have a boy named Tobin Ferro sitting behind a screen, tilting his head, remembering pies.

* * *

She came to the observatory the long way—Tanner's Lane, the cooper's yard, the cut-through behind the chandler's that smelled of tallow and hid the sightline from the customs house windows—and she climbed the hill road as the stars came out hard and cold, and by the time she reached the observatory door her knees had opinions and her hands had not stopped shaking since the bridge, though her face, she noted with professional detachment, had not changed once.

That was the thing about twenty years of hiding. Your face learned to work alone.

Inside, the kitchen was warm and the house was loud with the particular noise of people trying to be quiet about important things. Wren was at the long table with papers spread out and her father's magnifying loupe pushed up into her hair. Hazel sat opposite, copying—Marisol could tell by the set of their shoulders, that careful hunch, the fine-work grip they used for other people's lives now instead of watch parts. Old Piotr dozed in the chair by the stove with his milky eyes aimed at the ceiling as if reading a star chart on it.

Wren looked up. Read her in one glance, the way she read instruments.

"What happened," she said. Not a question.

Marisol set the empty basket on the table. It seemed suddenly the most incriminating object in the world, that basket. Eleven thousand times it had made her invisible, and tonight it had nearly hung her.

"Tobin Ferro," she said, and sat down heavily, because standing had become work. "Customs house kitchen boy. Fed him pies for two years. He's on the bridge now, behind a screen, with a soldier. He stood up when he saw me."

The room went still in the way rooms do when everyone in them lives in the same weather. Even Piotr surfaced, turning his head toward her voice.

"Did he follow?" Hazel asked. Their voice was level. Their hands, Marisol noticed, had gone absolutely motionless on the page, pen lifted, a bead of ink trembling at the nib.

"Not tonight. A ferryman shouted me into the river, more or less." She managed half a laugh; it came out thin. "Dov. Ansel's nephew. He owes the observatory more than he owes his own mother, and tonight he paid some of it. But Wren—" and here she turned in her chair, because this was the part that mattered, the part she had turned over and over the whole cold climb up the hill road, "—that's not the point. The point is that it took him four seconds. Four seconds, and a screen, and a list, and a boy I was kind to when he was hungry. Twenty years, Wren. Twenty years I walked past that table with my chin up and my basket swinging, and it held because I was a woman buying herring. And tonight it didn't hold, because they've stopped checking names and started checking faces."

Piotr spoke from the stove, his voice dry as old parchment. "The sky changes before the weather does. Every sailor knows it. You feel the wind come around first, and then—" he waved a vague hand upward, "—then the storm, whether you've rigged for it or not."

"Meaning what, old man?"

"Meaning the wind's come around." He fixed his good eye on her, or on the place near her, the way the half-blind do. "They're not looking for names anymore. Names are easy; names lie. They're looking at faces now. Faces are how you catch the ones whose names never existed at all." He smiled, thin and terrible. "I'd call that progress, if it weren't aimed at us."

Wren had gone very pale, but her hands were steady on her papers, gathering them, squaring them, the reflexes of a woman raised by a man who kept records in a hidden room. "When does Ferro's shift end?"

"How would I know?"

"Because if it ends at nine, he's already written his evening report. If it ends at midnight, he's still sitting there deciding." Wren pulled a blank sheet toward her, then stopped herself, visibly, from writing anything at all—Marisol saw the flinch of it, the keeper's instinct colliding with the keeper's new knowledge that paper was evidence. "Marisol. Think carefully. Before tonight, had anyone at that bridge table ever spoken to you? Asked you anything? Taken your name?"

"No."

"Had you ever given it?"

Marisol opened her mouth. Closed it. Twenty years of discipline, and the discipline held even now, even exhausted, even shaking: she inventoried. Eleven thousand crossings, itemized. Herring, onions, lamp oil, thread, the hen. Conversations about weather, prices, the lamplighter's sickness. Nothing. Not one syllable of her name had ever crossed that table in either direction.

"No," she said slowly. "Never."

"Then he has a memory, not a record. There's a difference, and it might matter." Wren pressed her palms flat on the table. "But it's a difference that shrinks every single day. Crane's men are at the bridge now, at the market gates, at the ferry landings. They're building a book of faces to match the book of names. And Marisol—Hazel—Piotr—" she looked around the table, and her voice, which she kept level with visible effort, cracked anyway on the last word, "—there are four of us in this room and not one of us exists."

Nobody answered. The stove ticked. Somewhere above them the great dead clock hung in its tower like a stopped heart, and below the hill the river ran on in the dark, indifferent, unpapered, carrying its own quiet traffic of debts and boats and names that never touched a page.

It was Hazel who spoke finally, and Marisol heard in their voice something new—not the apprentice's caution, not the orphan's flatness, but the first cold steel of decision being forged.

"Then we stop pretending hiding works," Hazel said. "We finish the copies. All of them, fast, before the ice breaks. And when the council convenes—" they looked at Wren, and Wren looked back, and something passed between them that Marisol, feeding people for twenty years, recognized instantly as the look of two people who have begun to carry the same load, "—we walk in the front door. All of us. Names first."

Marisol laughed then, really laughed, for the first time since the bridge—a short, disbelieving bark. "Front door. After twenty years, the front door."

"You've earned it," Hazel said.

"I've earned a back exit and a hot stove and a boy on a bridge who remembers my pies." She pushed herself up, because the soup would not make itself and fear, whatever else it did, did not excuse a cook from supper. But at the doorway to the kitchen she stopped, one hand on the frame, and looked back at them—at the girl guarding her father's ghost, the stargazer reading storms in ceilings, the orphan with ink on their fingers learning to write other people back into the world.

And beneath the fear, communal now, shared out among the four of them like bread among the hungry, she felt something else settle into place, heavy and warm at once: the knowledge that the hiding was ending whether they chose it or not, and that the only question left—the only one, the whole one—was whether they would be found as fugitives or as witnesses.

She was halfway through chopping onions when the knock came at the observatory's front door—soft, deliberate, three raps—and through it, courteous as a dinner invitation, a voice she had heard only once before, at the bridge table, asking after a missing woman:

"Masters of the observatory. Census business. Might we trouble you for a moment of your time?"

The knife stopped mid-chop. Down the hall, she heard Wren rise, unhurried, to answer it.

And outside, patient as paperwork, Silas Crane waited to be let in.
