## 19. A Question Too Many

Silas Crane had a theory about pauses.

Most people believed that lies lived in words, and so they watched mouths when they wanted to catch a liar. Crane knew better. Words could be rehearsed until they shone; a clever subject could recite a false biography with the flat confidence of a clerk reading a tax roll. But the pause before the first word—there was the ore, unrefined and honest. The body needed a moment to choose between what was true and what was safe, and in that sliver of hesitation, the truth showed its face like a debtor peeking through shutters at the knock of a collector.

He had been collecting pauses for eleven years. He kept them the way other men kept coins: sorted, weighed, catalogued against the standard.

The apprentice from the observatory paused beautifully.

* * *

Crane had requested the third interview be held at the census office on Chandler Street rather than at the observatory, and he had been careful about how he made the request. An order would have required paperwork—a summons entered into the record, a reason stated—and everything entered into the record was something the historians might one day read. So instead he sent a note, handwritten, courteous, almost apologetic: *A few small clarifications remain regarding household enumeration in the Observatory district. Your attendance at your convenience would spare me a second trip up the hill in this wind.*

It was a lie built of true things. There were clarifications. There was wind. And there was a second trip he very much wished to avoid, because the observatory put him at a disadvantage—he had felt it on his single visit, climbing those nine hundred steps into a building that smelled of oil and cold stone and something older than the regime it outlasted. Up there, the clock hung over every conversation like a magistrate. Down here, in a room with one lamp, one table, two chairs, and a window that looked out on nothing but the blank wall of the records annex, the geometry favored him.

He arrived an hour early to arrange the room. He moved the table so that the subject's chair would face the lamp and his own would sit at its edge, half in shadow—not to threaten, never to threaten, but to let the subject's eyes relax. He set out paper, pen, ink, and then, deliberately, a second sheet that appeared to be notes but was in fact yesterday's weather observations copied in his own hand. People's eyes went to papers on tables the way water goes to a low place. It gave them somewhere to look while they decided what to be afraid of.

At four minutes past the appointed hour—the lateness itself informative; the observatory household was disciplined enough to be punctual, which meant the delay was chosen, which meant they were discussing him even now—there came a knock.

"Enter," Crane said, pleasantly, and Hazel Okafor came in out of the grey afternoon with a satchel over one shoulder and the wary stillness of a creature that has learned doors are where the weather gets in.

"Young master Okafor." Crane rose, extended a hand, noted the calluses—file marks at the fingertips, a burn scar at the base of the thumb, the hands of someone who did fine work under pressure. "Thank you for coming down. Sit, please. May I take your coat? No? As you like."

The apprentice sat. Did not remove the satchel. Set it instead against the chair leg, within reach of one foot, the way a soldier rests a hand near a weapon. Crane logged that too, without appearing to log anything, and resumed his own seat, and for a moment the only sound was the annex wind worrying at the window frame and the small tick of the office clock—unofficial time, municipal time, three minutes slow and nobody's authority.

"You've been keeping well?" Crane asked. "The hill is hard on the lungs this season."

"Well enough."

"And the clock? Master Aldous's daughter reports progress, though I confess I couldn't read her letter. She writes the way her father used to talk—as though brevity were a virtue and clarity a tax." He smiled to show it was a joke, and waited to see whether the apprentice would smile back, and measured the delay. Half a heartbeat too long. The apprentice had decided to find it funny rather than finding it funny. Interesting.

"The escapement was the trouble," Hazel said. "It's repaired."

"So I understand. You must know your trade, to bring a monster like that back to life." Crane turned a page of his false notes, unhurried. "Tell me—I like to get these details right for the record—how long have you been apprenticed?"

"Six years."

"With Master Idris Voss, of the workshop on Tannery Row."

"Yes."

"And before that? Apprentices usually begin younger than twelve."

A shrug, smooth as oiled brass. "Household work first. Then the workshop took me."

"Whose household?"

Another page turned. "I don't recall the name. I was young."

*You recall,* Crane thought with something close to pleasure. *You recall it the way I recall my first case number. You simply know better than to give it to me.* He did not press. Pressing was for men who hunted with dogs. Crane hunted with patience; he laid each fact down like a card, face up, and watched which ones the subject refused to look at.

They proceeded through the inventory of ordinary questions. Place of birth—the apprentice said *upriver*, vaguely, and Crane wrote *upriver* in a hand that suggested the answer bored him. Age—given as nineteen, though the hands said older, or perhaps only more used. Trade specialty—escapements, fine work, regulator clocks. Each answer was competent, plausible, and dry as August. And each one matched, almost exactly, the answers given at the first interview, eleven weeks ago, and diverged, in three small places, from the answers given at the second.

Almost exactly. That was the thing that had brought Crane down off his high stool at midnight to reread his own files by candlelight. A careless liar diverged wildly. A terrified liar clung to a script and repeated it word for word, like a prayer. But this apprentice's answers drifted—by degrees, by nuance, the way a story improves slightly in the telling. At the first interview, the apprenticeship began "six years ago, in the spring." At the second, "six years ago." Today: "Six years." The details were being worn smooth, like a coin passed hand to hand, and coins wore smooth because they were handled, and they were handled because they were used, and this story was being *used*, daily, to buy bread and passage and the benefit of the doubt.

Somewhere behind those calm dark eyes was the original mint, and the original die, and the first true striking of the coin. And Crane wanted it the way a miser wants the vault, not the purse.

He was aware—he was always aware—of the argument against himself. The new order wanted a clean census, and a clean census wanted this apprentice either registered or gone, and either outcome served. But Crane had never been a man for outcomes. Outcomes belonged to ministers and generals. Crane belonged to the *record*, to the great patient ledger of who-had-actually-been, and an unregistered apprentice of evident skill, sheltered in a tower full of anomalous documents, guarded by a keeper's daughter who wrote letters like coded dispatches—that was not merely a discrepancy. That was a door, and Crane had built his whole quiet life on the certainty that every door, given enough visits, opens.

"There," he said, setting down his pen with the small final click he reserved for closing scenes. "That's the formality done. The bureau thanks you; I thank you. You've been very patient with an old man's questions."

Something in the apprentice's shoulders eased—one degree, no more. Crane watched it ease and loved it and prepared, with the tenderness of a craftsman touching his finest tool, to undo it.

"One last thing," he said, softly, as though it had only just occurred to him, as though his memory were a drawer he was rummaging in absentmindedly. "Purely for completeness. It will go nowhere unless it's relevant—it hardly ever is." He glanced down at the file, licked his thumb, turned past the forms to a page he had not previously consulted, and frowned at it gently, a man embarrassed by his own thoroughness. "In the earlier records—the old tax rolls, the flood-year registries, the materials we inherited from the previous administration—there are references to a family of that surname. Okafor. Upriver, as you said. Winter of the flood year, which would be—" he let the pause do its own arithmetic "—some years before you were born, or near enough. Parents, presumably. Kin."

He raised his eyes. The lamp sat between them, and he let it.

"It's nothing, I assure you. A clerk's tidiness. We simply prefer not to leave duplicate surnames unexplained in the same district—it makes trouble later, for pensions and inheritances and such." His voice stayed at the level of a man discussing rain. "So. For the record, and then you're free to go."

He folded his hands on the table.

"Where are your parents buried?"

* * *

Later, alone, Crane would reconstruct the silence the way a jeweler examines a stone under lamplight, turning it, looking for the flaw, finding instead—flawlessness.

It was not a long silence. Two seconds, perhaps three. In ordinary conversation it would have passed unnoticed, swallowed by the next sentence. But Crane had spent eleven years training his ear the way Old Piotr of the observatory had supposedly trained his eye on stars: toward the intervals, the darkness between the lights.

The apprentice's face did nothing wrong. That was the marvel of it. No flicker, no telltale flush, no dart of the eyes toward the satchel or the door. The face performed mild incomprehension, the polite blankness of anyone asked an intrusive question by a bureaucrat. *My parents? Why would you ask after—*

But the breath came a half-beat late.

And beneath the table, out of the lamplight, the apprentice's foot settled fully to the floor—the stance a person takes before standing, before running, before fighting—settled and then deliberately, deliberately relaxed again, an entire flight response considered and overruled inside the space of a breath.

And in those two seconds, three seconds, Hazel Okafor chose an answer.

"They died when I was small," the apprentice said. "I don't know where they're buried. There wasn't—there wasn't a burial I attended. The flood year was confused. Records weren't kept."

Every word of it was reasonable. Every word of it was possibly even true. And every word of it was delivered a half-second after a person who thinks in half-seconds should have begun delivering it, because the true answer to Crane's question had not been *I don't know.*

The true answer had been *elsewhere*. Somewhere in the flooded districts, or in no district at all, in a ditch or a common grave or under the ice of the river itself—or in a ledger, in a locked room, nine hundred steps above the city, written in a dead keeper's careful hand—Hazel Okafor knew exactly where their parents were, or exactly where they were not, and the knowledge had risen in them like water finding its level, and they had dammed it, and the dam had cost them that half-second, and Crane had been standing downstream with his cup out for eleven years waiting for precisely this water.

"I'm sorry," Crane said, and meant it, which surprised him a little. "The flood years were cruel to the records as much as to the people. Half the registries of that winter are simply gone—water damage, misplacement, worse. It's why we ask. Not to grieve anyone twice. Only to close the files properly, someday, when it can be done kindly."

"Is that likely?" the apprentice asked. "Being closed kindly?"

It was the sharpest thing said in the room all afternoon, and Crane felt it land below his ribs like a key turning. The question had layers, the way good mechanisms have layers: it was a servant's question—*will your bureaucracy ever release us?*—and it was also, unmistakably, a test of him. *What are you, actually? What is this actually for?* Nobody had asked Crane that in eleven years. He had asked it of himself, some nights, in the voiceless way a man asks it of his ceiling.

"When the historians finish their work," he said, "the record will finally be accurate. That's the whole intention. An accurate record protects everyone in it." He heard the sentence as he said it and understood, distantly, that he was quoting his superiors the way a parrot quotes a bell, and that the apprentice knew it, and that neither of them blinked.

"Then I hope it's accurate soon," said Hazel Okafor, and picked up the satchel, and stood, and offered a hand that was steady as a lathe.

"Thank you for your time, young master Okafor. You'll hear nothing further from us unless something material changes." *And something material changes hourly,* he did not say. "Mind the wind on the bridge."

"Good evening, Mr. Crane."

The door closed. The footsteps descended. Crane sat without moving for the length of ten ticks of the slow municipal clock, listening to the building settle around the absence.

* * *

He worked late. This was unremarkable; Crane always worked late; his colleagues had stopped remarking on it years ago, the way one stops remarking on weather. He lit the second lamp, drew his files toward him, and opened the folder marked **OBSERVATORY DISTRICT — IRREGULARS**, thin as a hymnal, and within it the page marked **OKAFOR, HAZEL — APPRENTICE, UNVERIFIED**.

Three interviews' worth of answers, side by side in his neat columns. Six years ago, in the spring. Six years ago. Six years. Upriver, vaguely. Upright, firmly. Nineteen, perhaps. Nineteen, certainly—except that hands like that took twenty years to build.

And now, appended in fresh ink: *Parents deceased, flood era. Burial site unknown to subject.*

Unknown to subject. He wrote the phrase and then sat looking at it, his pen hovering, because the phrase was a lie, and Crane's pages did not hold lies—not the subjects' lies, anyway; his pages existed precisely to catch lies and pin them like moths, wings spread, labeled. To write *unknown to subject* was to certify the moth as a butterfly. It was forgery of a kind so small it fit inside a single clause, and it was the first forgery Silas Crane had ever committed in eleven years of immaculate service, and he committed it slowly, pressing the nib down with unusual force, as though the ink itself might object.

Why?

He examined the question the way he examined everything: from the outside in. Not mercy—mercy was a word for other men, warmer men, and besides, what he had done was not merciful. A merciful man would have left the page blank. No: Crane had written *unknown to subject* because it was, professionally speaking, *true in the useful direction*. The subject did not volunteer the burial place; therefore the location was unknown *to the record as voluntarily disclosed*. The distinction was exquisite. The distinction was the kind of thing he had mocked, privately, in junior clerks.

He turned instead to the other page, the one the historians' advance party had left with him, the certification schedule. *Complete and clean enumeration to be delivered upon the breaking of the ice.* Clean, they had explained, smiling with their civilized teeth, meant purged of irregular entries—purged of persons whose existence complicated the narrative the new histories intended to tell. And Crane had nodded, because nodding was his profession, and had carried the schedule home, and had lain awake doing arithmetic.

Because here was the arithmetic that would not stay solved: if the historians could erase an Okafor—an entire family, drowned twice, once in water and once in ink—then they could erase a Crane. His father had been a customs assessor under the old administration; his mother had kept a boarding house whose guests included two men later declared inconvenient; his own employment history sat in files compiled by men now called *the previous errors*. The word *clean* did not mean accurate. Crane had spent his life learning what words meant by watching what they did, and *clean*, observed in action, meant *agreed upon by whoever holds the pen*.

He was holding the pen. For now. *For now* was not a tenure; it was a loan, and loans were called in.

Crane looked down at the Okafor page—at the half-second he had caught and pinned and labeled—and understood, with the mild vertigo of a man who has walked out onto ice and heard it speak beneath him, that he had just made a choice far larger than a clause of ink. By recording *unknown to subject* instead of *deceived the examiner*, he had protected the apprentice's secret from his own superiors while preserving it perfectly for himself. He had become, in the smallest possible way, a keeper. He had joined, without ceremony or consent, the long tradition of the observatory tower: men and women who wrote down what the powerful preferred unwritten, and stored it against the day the day changed.

There was a version of him that found this horrifying. He searched for that version, politely, the way he searched a room before an interview, and could not find it anywhere.

He blew out one lamp. In the remaining light he took a fresh sheet and began, in his flawless clerical hand, a private memorandum—undated, unsigned, addressed to no one—which began: *Re: the question of what the record owes the dead.* He got four lines down before he stopped, struck by the sound of his own thought, which was not the sound of a census taker at all.

Outside, the wind came up off the river carrying a smell it did not usually carry in deep winter—the smell of open water far upstream, of thaw moving toward them faster than any schedule allowed—and Crane, who noticed everything, noticed it, and set down his pen, and listened.

Somewhere across the dark city, on a hill, nine hundred steps up, a stopped clock waited to be made to lie or made to speak, and an apprentice with a half-second's worth of secret was deciding, tonight, which way to gear it.

Crane capped his ink. Whatever the apprentice decided, he intended to be there to see it entered—and, he suspected with a sensation like the first hairline tick of settling ice, he was no longer entirely certain whose side of the entry he wished to sign.

He marked the page. He closed the folder. He went home through the warming wind, and did not sleep.
