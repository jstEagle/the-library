## 20. The Broken Watch

The lamp in the ledger room burned low, and Hazel let it.

They had learned, in the weeks since the copying nights began, to work by small light—hands remembering what eyes could not confirm, the way a clockmaker finds a pivot by touch before ever seeing it. Down here behind the face of the master clock, among the shelves of Wren's father's careful hand, the dark was not an enemy. It was a room they could live in.

But tonight Hazel was not working on the ledger copies. Tonight the satchel sat open on the bench, and out of it, wrapped in the flannel that had wrapped it for eleven years, came the watch.

It had belonged to both of them—that was the way Hazel had always thought of it, *both of them*, as if grief required a shared custody even between the dead. Their mother had carried it first; their father had carried it after, and then it had carried nothing at all except its own brokenness, because it had stopped the same winter they did. The winter of the flood. The year Bellhaven decided the Okafors had never existed.

Hazel unwrapped it now with the deliberation of someone opening a grave they intended, this once, to be gentle with.

It was a half-plate lever escapement, good work, older than Hazel by thirty years at least. The case was silver gone soft with handling, dented at the bow where it had been dropped—or thrown, or caught against something in water; Hazel would never know which, and had made a discipline of not imagining it. The dial was enamel, hairline-cracked from the twelve to the three, like a road on a map leading nowhere. The crystal was long gone. The hands were blued steel, one bent.

Eleven years Hazel had carried this thing through every room they'd been thrown out of, every workshop floor they'd slept on, every wagon ride out of a town where questions had started being asked. Eleven years it had sat in pockets and satchels and, for one terrible month in Corvane, buried in a garden under a pear tree while soldiers searched the house above it. And in all that time Hazel had never opened it. Never looked past the dead dial into the movement itself.

There were reasons. Hazel could have listed them the way one lists the parts of a machine: fear of finding it beyond repair. Fear of the grief that lived in fine mechanisms, in the knowledge that their mother's fingers had wound this crown, their father's fingers had held it to his ear in the last hour Hazel ever saw him. A broken thing carried long enough becomes proof of something; a mended thing becomes only a thing. Hazel had not been sure they wanted the proof to end.

And underneath all of it, honest underneath, there was this: Hazel had not believed they deserved to fix it. Repair was a claim. To open the case back was to say *this is mine to mend*, and nothing in Bellhaven's papers said any such thing. Hazel Okafor did not exist. The watch existed. Somewhere in the arithmetic of the census, that meant the watch belonged to no one, and no one could inherit it.

Well. Hazel had spent the last months reading a ledger full of people the government said belonged to no one. They had learned what such arithmetic was worth.

They lit a second lamp anyway. Some work deserved to be seen, even if only by the person doing it.

* * *

The movement came out of the case with the reluctance of anything long unattended. Hazel breathed on it, and dust rose like breath. Under the loupe the brass showed the truth of the years: dried oil gone to amber varnish, rust blooming orange along the barrel teeth, the balance staff snapped clean at the shoulder—the small fatal fracture, the one that had let everything else run down and stop. It had not been drowned, then. It had been dropped, once, hard, and no one with the skill to mend it had been near enough to try.

Or no one who was allowed to be near enough. Hazel set that thought down carefully, the way one sets down a hot iron.

They began.

The work went the way good work does, by dissolving the world. Full plate off; barrel out; the mainspring eased with a let-down key that Master Idris had sold them, years ago, out of the bottom drawer he swore he didn't keep for favorites. Each wheel into its paper envelope, labeled in pencil so small the letters were more intention than mark. Hazel cleaned the plates in spirit, pegged out the jewel holes, watched black grit come away on the pegwood and thought, absurdly, of scrubbing the workshop floor after Idris's arrest, of how grief always seemed to leave the same residue no matter what surface it dried on.

The rust fought them at the barrel. It always does; rust is time refusing to be apologized to. Hazel worked the teeth with an oilstone dust paste and infinite patience, and somewhere in the middle of it—they could not have said when—the shaking in their hands stopped. That was the mercy of craft. You could not carry a satchel full of terror into a train of wheels four millimeters wide. The work would simply not permit it. Whatever else the world took from Hazel Okafor, it could not take the steadiness of a hand trained on pivots finer than a hair.

By the second hour the movement lay clean on the bench, naked and hopeful, every part accounted for. The broken balance staff waited in its envelope like a patient before surgery. Hazel turned it under the loupe, measured it, and reached into the tool roll for the staffs Idris had given them three nights ago—I *dris*, released and ruined and wordless, who had left the roll on Hazel's bench without explanation, the way a man apologizes when he cannot bear to hear his own apology answered.

The new staff seated true. The balance spun free, and its tick ran through the bench and up Hazel's arms like a pulse returning to a limb long asleep.

They assembled the rest in silence: train, escapement, winding works. Oiled each point with one drop, no more. Then the moment that mattered, the one Hazel had circled for eleven years without landing—the case back.

Held to the light, the inner face of the cover showed only old polish and the ghost of thumbprints. But as Hazel tilted it toward the lamp, catching the reflection at a shallow angle the way one catches writing on a sealed letter, lines appeared. Fine lines. Scratched—not engraved with a graver, too hurried for that, but scratched with something sharp and desperate: a pin, perhaps, or the pointed tip of a screwdriver. Letters made by someone with no time and no tools and a message that could not wait for either.

Hazel's heart performed the strange trick it had learned lately, of stopping and racing at once.

They angled the cover again. Read it.

*For Atlas.*

Two words. A name. Not Hazel's name—Hazel had never been called anything but Hazel, had chosen half of it themselves at nine years old when a clerk demanded a first name for a form that was itself a forgery. And yet the letters sat there in the silver with their mother's unmistakable economy, the same hand that had written the labels on her spice jars, the same slanted confidence Hazel saw now every time they looked at their own handwriting in the copied ledger pages.

*For Atlas.* Not a nickname Hazel had ever heard spoken. Not a saint's day name, not a family name from either side of their mixed and unrecorded blood. Something else. Something private. Their mother had scratched it inside the case of the watch she carried against her heart, and she had addressed it—to whom? To the child she expected? To a child she had lost? To—

The room seemed to tilt very slightly, the way the observatory tower tilted in high wind, everything solid revealing itself as suspended.

Hazel set the cover down. Picked it up. Read it again. *For Atlas.*

And then memory, which had been standing politely at the door all this time, came in.

Their mother singing, once, in the flooded district house, over the noise of rain: a nonsense rhyme about carrying the sky, *my little atlas, holding up the heavens, my lantern walking ahead of me in the dark.* Hazel had been four, maybe five. They had thought it was about the stars Piotr loved, or the lamps on the bridge. Children take metaphors literally and lose them the same way. But their mother had sung it the way you sing a name. The way you sing to one person in a room full of people.

She had named them Atlas. Or meant to. Or had named them twice—once aloud for the clerks, *Hazel,* pliant and forgettable, and once in silver, where the census takers could never scratch their way in: the true name, the carried name, the name that said *you are strong enough to hold up whatever comes.*

Hazel did not remember deciding to weep. They were simply aware, some minutes later, of tears falling on the bench between the wheel envelopes, and of making no sound, because the ledger room had taught them that particular discipline. The grief did not come alone. It came the way the tide comes, with everything it has collected: the pear tree in Corvane; the clerk demanding a first name; the forged apprenticeship paper with its careful false seal; the line in Aldous's ledger—*Okafor, Hazel. Born in the winter of the flood*—a stranger's kindness writing them into existence. All of it gathered and rearranged around these two scratched words like sediment around a dropped stone.

They had spent their whole life measuring the world in what could be carried. And all that time they had been carrying, unknowing, the one measurement that mattered: *Atlas.* Held up. Meant to hold.

* * *

Hazel wiped their face with the heel of their hand, the practical gesture Marisol used, and got back to work, because the work was there and the work did not care about revelations. Dial on. Hands straightened—one gentle coax of the pliers, no marks, their mother would not have wanted marks. Case screwed down with the tension of a promise.

Then the winding.

The crown turned stiffly, then willingly, then with the light ratchet-click of consent. Hazel counted the turns the way Idris had taught them: enough for the day, never enough to strain the spring. Eight turns. Nine. Ten. On the eleventh the balance woke, hesitated like a swimmer at a cold edge, and committed—and the watch began, in Hazel's palm, to tick.

A small sound. Six beats to the second, slightly fast, the kind of fault a good clockmaker would correct and a grieving one would forgive forever. Hazel pressed it to their ear the way their father must have, the way anyone does, the universal gesture of listening for a heartbeat that isn't yours.

It kept ticking.

*You're eleven years late,* Hazel told it silently. *We all are.*

But that wasn't right either. The watch hadn't been late. It had been waiting, the way the ledger had waited, the way the master clock waited overhead with its sabotaged heart and its secret dial geared to truths no one official had authorized. Everything true in Bellhaven had simply stopped moving until someone came with the hands and the nerve to start it again. Hazel looked down at the watch, and understood—as clearly as if the mechanism itself had explained it, wheel by wheel—that this was what the copying nights meant, what the whole observatory meant. Not rescue. Winding. Someone had to be the power source. Someone had to turn the crown.

Hazel set the watch on the bench, face up, running, between the lamp and the open ledger, and let it tick while they thought.

Their mother had hidden a name inside the one object sturdy enough to survive her. What else had been hidden? What names had the flood taken that the river still held? Tomorrow—no, tomorrow Crane might come again, tomorrow the historians' advance party might arrive with their clean ledgers and their civilized voices—but there would be evenings. There would be lamplight. There was a district downstream where the water had receded and the neighbors remembered, and a ledger entry that pointed to it like a compass needle. If their parents had left a name in silver, perhaps they had left other things elsewhere: a marker, a witness, a photograph, a grave with flowers tended by strangers. The record argued back. Hazel was beginning to believe it always could, if you found where it was written.

They closed the ledger softly, banked the lamp to its last blue tongue, and climbed the nine hundred steps with the watch in their waistcoat pocket, ticking against their ribs like a second, stubborn heart. At the top, the tower was cold and enormous and familiar now; the stopped hands of the great dial hung against the stars at ten minutes to midnight, frozen mid-argument. Hazel stood beneath them a while, listening to the small mechanism live against the vast dead one, and felt the grief finish its slow transmutation into something harder and more useful. Not peace. Resolve. The difference, Hazel thought, was like the difference between a spring at rest and a spring wound. Same steel. Entirely different intentions.

Below in the kitchen, faint up the stairwell, Marisol's voice and Wren's, low, laughing at something; Piotr's chair creaking; the ordinary music of a household built out of people who officially did not exist. Hazel put their hand over the pocket where the watch ticked and almost went down to them.

Almost.

Because at the landings below, boots were on the stairs—two pairs, unhurried, official—and a voice Hazel had learned to dread for its courtesy was saying, pleasantly, the way one discusses weather:

"Then perhaps the keeper's daughter can explain why your maintenance logs list an apprentice the city has no record of."

Silence from below. Then Wren's voice, level as a balance wheel: "Come to my father's study. I'll show you whatever you like."

Hazel pressed back into the shadow of the clock case, the watch suddenly loud as a hammer in their palm, and understood that the hours of quiet mending were over. The name their mother had hidden in silver was safe. Everything else was about to be asked for outright—and Wren was walking toward the study with two census men and whatever bargain the new regime had sent her, and there was nothing Hazel could do but listen to her footsteps fade and pray she knew, as Hazel was only now learning, exactly what to conceal, and what it would cost.
