## 21. Terms of the Regime

They came on a Tuesday, which Wren would think about afterward for a long time. Not with soldiers, not at dawn, not in any of the shapes the fear had taught her to expect—but in the mild middle of a gray afternoon, three of them in good coats, stamping the snow off their boots on the observatory steps like ordinary visitors come to look at the stars.

The tall one was called Magister Corvane. She learned that from the letter he presented, and from the way he said his own name, as though it were an introduction he had rehearsed until the rehearsal had worn smooth and natural. Behind him stood a woman with ink-stained cuffs who did not give her name at all, and a younger man who carried a leather case the way Hazel carried tools—close to the body, protective of its corners. Historians, the letter said. Advance delegation of the Provisional Commission on Records and Continuity. Wren read *continuity* twice before she understood it was meant to be reassuring.

"Keeper Aldous," Corvane said, and bowed—not deeply, but correctly, the bow of a man who had studied bows. "We've heard so much about this place. Your father's work especially. It would be our great privilege to see the collection."

*Collection.* Wren felt the word go into her like a splinter, small and clean, and she smiled the way her father had taught her to smile at officials: politely, and with nothing behind it.

"We keep clocks," she said. "And weather records, and star charts, and one very bad taxidermied owl. If any of that is a collection, you're welcome to it."

Corvane laughed. It was a good laugh, warm at the edges, and that was the worst thing about him.

* * *

She took them up, because refusing would have been its own confession, and because part of her—the part that had spent twenty years alone in a tower guarding her father's secrets—wanted to know what they already knew. That was the arithmetic of the new city: every visit was a measurement, and the only defense was to measure back.

Corvane climbed the nine hundred steps without complaint. He stopped at the third landing to admire the stonework, at the fifth to catch his breath and remark that the view must be magnificent, at the seventh to ask how old the tower was, and whether the foundation had ever been surveyed. The woman with the ink-stained cuffs asked nothing. She looked at everything—the joints in the masonry, the wear on the handrail, the width of the doorways—and Wren understood that she was doing the survey Corvane had mentioned, measuring the tower for purposes Wren could not yet name.

At the clock room, the master clock hung silent in its case, still dead since the sabotage, since Hazel's repairs, since the second sabotage and the strange flaw Idris had left in it like a signature written in pain. Wren had grown used to the silence of it the way one grows used to a missing tooth—the tongue returning again and again to the gap. Corvane stood before the stopped dial with his hands clasped behind his back and made a sound of genuine sorrow.

"Stopped," he said. "How long?"

"A generation," Wren said. "More."

"And yet the city kept its own time anyway." He turned to her, and his eyes were kind, and that too was the worst thing about him. "Every hearth, every market bell, every factory whistle running on private time. Do you know what that means, Keeper Aldous? It means Bellhaven has been living on memory. On habit. On ten thousand small agreements nobody wrote down." He shook his head slowly, as if at a sadness too large for one man. "A regime cannot be built on that. A regime is built on records. On a single time everyone can trust, because everyone can verify it."

"Then we should fix the clock," Wren said. "It wants an escapement and a week of honest work. My apprentice could do it."

"Your apprentice." The woman spoke for the first time. Her voice was dry as a pen nib. "Okafor."

The name landed in the room and lay there. Wren kept her face still by thinking about the gear train, about tooth counts, about anything mechanical and indifferent.

"The apprentice," she said, "who was officially sent by the city clerk to repair the official clock. You can read the order yourself. It has a seal on it."

"I'm sure it does." The woman opened her leather case, unhurried, and drew out a folder, and did not open it, only held it, the way one holds a card one does not need to play yet. "Seals are easy. Continuity is hard."

* * *

They brought out the tea things in the keeper's parlor, because Marisol insisted, and because Marisol's insistence was one of the load-bearing walls of Wren's life now. The cook poured for the historians herself, standing straight-backed in her good apron, an unregistered woman serving tea to the men whose job was to make her never have existed, and if her hand trembled no one saw it, and Wren loved her so much it hurt somewhere under the ribs.

Corvane sat with his cup balanced precisely on his knee and explained the future to them.

He was good at it. That was what Wren kept circling back to, in the days after. He was genuinely, catastrophically good at it. He spoke of the new commission the way her father had spoken of the solar system—with the calm of a man describing something that was already true and merely waiting to be observed. There would be archives, he said. Unified ones. The chaos of scattered parish books, guild rolls, private ledgers—all of it consolidated, verified, corrected. Bellhaven's history, properly written at last, instead of accumulating in drawers like unsorted correspondence.

"History is a kindness," he said. "That's what people don't understand. An unrecorded life is a life that can be done to, without appeal. We intend to do nothing to anyone. We intend only to write down what is true."

"What is true," Wren repeated.

"Verifiable," he amended smoothly, and smiled, acknowledging the correction as though she had scored a point, and Wren felt the trap close another notch, because he treated even her suspicion as participation.

Then he set down his cup, and folded his hands, and came to it.

"The commission is aware that your father maintained certain records outside his official duties. Personal notations. Irregular entries concerning persons of, ah, ambiguous documentation." He said it gently, the way a physician says *growth*. "These anomalous records represent a real problem for continuity, Keeper Aldous. Not a legal problem—we are not the census bureau, we are scholars—but a scholarly one. Unverified claims. Names without provenance. Sentiment, frankly, masquerading as data." He spread his hands apologetically. "If such a book were to circulate—if it fell into the wrong hands during the transition—it could do enormous damage. To those very people it claims to protect, most of all. Their claims would be tainted forever. Dismissed as forgery, sentiment, sedition. Whereas—" and here he leaned forward, and his voice dropped into the register of confidence, of conspiracy shared between reasonable people, "—whereas surrendered to the commission, catalogued and assessed by professionals, they could be given proper weight. Where the evidence supports them. In due course. With review."

"And the observatory?" Wren asked, because he had left a silence shaped like that question and she was tired of waiting for him to fill it himself.

"The observatory," Corvane said, "is the finest scientific institution in this city, and the commission sees no reason why its funding should become collateral damage in an argument about paperwork. Keep the records flowing—the meteorological series is invaluable, the transit observations irreplaceable—and let us take the anomalous material off your hands. You'll be relieved of it. Frankly, Keeper Aldous, you'll be free of it. You've been guarding your father's grief for twenty years. Let the professionals carry it now."

Twenty years, Wren thought. So they knew that too. They knew the shape of her whole life, and they had dressed their appetite in sympathy for it, and God help her, some exhausted corner of her soul sat up like a dog hearing its name.

* * *

She nearly said yes.

She would spend a long time afterward sitting with that fact, turning it over in the dark the way one turns a stone found in a pocket. She nearly said yes, and not because she was a coward, and not because she doubted her father—not even, in the end, because of the funding, though the funding was real and she thought of the roof, of the south shutter that let snow into Piotr's observing room, of Hazel sleeping on a pallet in the clock room because there was nowhere else. She nearly said yes because he was kind to her. Because he was the first person in twenty years to look at the weight she carried and say, aloud, *that looks heavy; you shouldn't have to hold it alone,* and mean it, or mean something enough like meaning it that her body couldn't tell the difference.

That was the civilized face of it. Torture would have been simpler. Torture would have told her exactly what she was defending. But Corvane offered her rest, and respect, and a professional vocabulary for surrender, and for three full seconds—she counted them later, lying awake—her mouth was actually forming the word.

What stopped her was not courage. It was grammar.

*Surrendered to the commission.* Passive voice. *Given proper weight. Where the evidence supports them. In due course.* Every promise he made was built so that no one had ever promised anything. She had spent her life around mechanisms, and she knew a slip-gear when she heard one—a mechanism designed to engage only when it suited the maker, and to disengage under load. His sentences were full of them. Where the evidence supports them—who decided what supported? Due course—due to whom, coursing toward what? He had offered her nothing at all, wrapped so beautifully that unwrapping it felt like rudeness.

And underneath the grammar, one other thing, small and cold as a bearing: *sentiment masquerading as data.*

Her father's handwriting. Forty years of it. The births he had stood witness to, the debts he had recorded so the interest could not be invented twice, the names of the sold and the drowned and the simply uncounted—including, on the last page in his living hand, *Okafor, Hazel. Born in the winter of the flood.* Corvane called that sentiment. Which meant Corvane had read it, or knew of it, which meant somewhere in the commission's folders there was already a description of the ledger, which meant the folder the ink-cuffed woman held open on her knees was not empty.

Wren set down her cup. Her hand did not shake, and she noted that with a distant, craftswoman's satisfaction, the way she'd note a pivot seating true.

"Magister Corvane," she said, "my father's papers are my father's papers. I won't surrender another man's life's work over tea. But I'll tell you what I'll do." And here she did the bravest thing she had ever done, which was to sound reasonable. "I will inventory them. Properly. Catalogue, condition reports, provenance notes—everything you'd want, done to archival standard. It will take time. The collection has been uncatalogued for twenty years; it wants weeks, not hours. When it's done, I'll submit it to the commission myself, and you may assess whatever I submit."

Corvane regarded her for a long moment. She watched him decide whether she was stupid, or lying, or simply a scholar after all, and she could not tell which conclusion he reached, and that frightened her more than anything else in the afternoon.

"An inventory," he said at last, pleasantly. "In exchange for time."

"In exchange for a proper catalogue," Wren said. "You're historians. Tell me you'd rather have it done well than done fast."

Something moved behind his eyes—appetite, maybe, or admiration, or the patience of a man who has learned that most doors open themselves if you stop leaning on them. "Two weeks," he said. "When the ice breaks, the commission convenes formally, and the record of this city begins its new volume. I would like your inventory to be its first acquisition. Consider it, Keeper Aldous—an archive that opens with the observatory's name in it. Or consider what the alternative looks like, if the anomalous material surfaces some other way, in some other hands, and the commission is forced to treat this institution as a source rather than a partner." He rose, and bowed his correct bow, and his kindness settled back over him like a coat. "Two weeks. I'll send the woman—Frau Vess—for the inventory. She has a good hand."

Frau Vess closed her case without opening it. The young man with the tool-carry shoulders glanced once, quickly, at Wren—something almost human in it, an apology or a warning, gone before she could read which—and then they were stamping the snow off their boots on the steps again, going away into the gray, polite as churchwardens.

* * *

Marisol cleared the cups in silence and washed them in silence, and Wren stood in the doorway of the kitchen watching her hands move in the water, and neither of them spoke until Marisol said, without turning around:

"You didn't tell them about the copying."

"No."

"You didn't tell them there's nothing left in the ledger room but dust and a false bottom."

"No."

Marisol set the last cup in the rack and dried her hands, slowly, and turned around, and her face was the face of a woman who had hidden in plain sight for twenty years and knew precisely what plain sight cost. "Then you bought two weeks with a lie you can't renew," she said, not unkindly. "Child, when Frau Vess comes for that inventory, there had better be an inventory. Or better than an inventory."

"There will be something," Wren said, and heard how thin it sounded, and hated it.

She went up. Nine hundred steps, taken fast, because her legs needed the burning and the tower was where her thoughts had always come apart into sense. At the top, the clock room was cold and smelled of oil and iron, and Hazel was half inside the movement, sleeves rolled, a lens strapped over one eye, working at the second sabotage's wound with the tiny deliberate motions of someone coaxing a confession out of steel.

Wren stood in the doorway and watched them work, and Corvane's voice kept playing in her head—*you've been guarding your father's grief for twenty years, let the professionals carry it*—and beneath it, quieter, the traitor's whisper: *it would be lighter. It would be so much lighter.*

"Hazel," she said.

Hazel backed out of the mechanism, blinking, and pushed the lens up onto their forehead, leaving a red ring around one eye, and looked at her, and waited. They were good at waiting. They had been invisible for so long that waiting was the first language they'd ever learned fluently.

"They know about the ledger," Wren said. "They came today. Historians—the regime's advance party. They offered me a deal: the records for the funding. Two weeks to produce an inventory that doesn't exist, or they come back as something other than guests." She heard her own voice go flat and fast, the voice of a report rather than a person, and made herself stop, and start again. "I nearly said yes. I want you to know that. I sat there with their tea in my hands and I nearly gave them everything my father bled for, because a man was kind to me about being tired."

Hazel slid off the work stool and came toward her across the cold floor, and for a moment Wren thought—she didn't know what she thought. But Hazel only stopped at arm's length and said, quietly:

"You said no."

"I stalled."

"You said no long enough to walk back up nine hundred steps and warn me." Hazel's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "My mother used to say a stall is just a no wearing better clothes. Two weeks is a no, Wren. It's just a no that buys us time." Then, before Wren could answer, before either of them could examine how close they were standing or how little either of them wanted to move: "Where's the true ledger now?"

"Gone. Scattered. Half of it is in clock cases on wagons headed for Thornmill and Ashford by now. Half of the copies are in the false bottom of the case Piotr uses for his bad star charts. The rest—" Wren exhaled. "The rest is wherever we decide to put it next. Why?"

Hazel reached into the breast of their shirt and drew out a folded paper, soft with handling, and unfolded it on the bench: a page copied from the ledger in Hazel's careful, cramped hand. Wren recognized it. The flooded district. An entry from the winter of the flood, a household listed with the notation her father always used for the disappeared: *witnesses remain.*

"There's a name here," Hazel said, "a street, and a note in your father's hand that says someone who remembers is still alive. The ice breaks early this year—I can feel it in the water smell, Marisol says the same. If the council convenes when it breaks, then whatever we're going to have as proof, as witnesses, as more than ink—there's a window, and it's closing." They looked up at her, and their eyes had the fixed brightness she had learned to read as fear moving fast enough to look like purpose. "I'm going down to the flood district tomorrow. Before Crane comes back. Before the ice goes."

Wren looked at the copied page, at her father's steady script, at the notation that meant *the paper is gone but the people are not*, and she thought of Corvane's kind voice saying *sentiment*, and she picked up her coat.

"Then I'm coming with you," she said. "I've guarded a book for twenty years. It's time I met what's in it."
