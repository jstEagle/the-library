## 22. What the River Keeps

The ledger said *Lantern Row*, and the city had spent eleven years insisting there was no such street.

Hazel found it anyway, the way water finds a channel—by following what would not be denied. The map in the observatory's atlas room showed a blank stretch of riverbank below the tannery district, labeled only *Former Allotments, Reclaimed*. But Wren's father's hand, cramped and patient in the ledger's ninth volume, had written: *Okafor, Emeka — boatwright, Lantern Row, third house past the willow stake. Wife: Sefu-born, name given as Adaeze. Lost in the flood of the high thaw, winter of the child's sixth year. House gone. Neighbors scattered or drowned. Recorded against all instruction.*

*Against all instruction.* Hazel had read that phrase a dozen times now, turning it over like a worn coin. It was the closest thing to pride the old keeper had allowed himself.

So Hazel went down through the tannery district on a grey morning that smelled of wet lime and old smoke, satchel light for once—only tools, the mended watch, a heel of bread—and let their feet do the remembering their mind could not. They had been six when the river came up. Six years old, carried out on a door with the hinges still attached, and everything after that was the workshop, the back bench, the rule of *don't ask, don't be asked*. Eleven years of not asking had built up inside Hazel like scale inside a pipe, and now, walking down toward the water, they felt it begin to dissolve, flake by flake, into something that hurt more cleanly.

The flood line was still visible if you knew how to read it, and Hazel did; reading marks was half their trade. The tannery wall wore a tide of darker brick two meters up, and above it, paler brick, and the boundary between them ran crooked as handwriting down the whole street. Below that line, everything was newer. Rebuilt cheap. Above it, the old city kept its soot and its cornices and its memory.

Lantern Row began where the willow stake had been—there was no willow anymore, only a stump in an iron collar, but the stump was enough—and continued along a lane so narrow the eaves nearly shook hands overhead. Third house past. Hazel counted without meaning to. One: a chandler's, shuttered. Two: a boarding house with washing strung across its face like rigging. Three—

Three was a ruin, or had been, and had then been rebuilt around its own ruin, the way bodies rebuild around wounds. A low stone ground floor, older than anything else on the street, surmounted by two sagging stories of timber added by someone who built the way poor people always build: hopefully, and out of whatever was at hand. A brass knocker shaped like a fish. Smoke from the chimney.

Someone lived there. Someone had always lived there, probably, while the government's maps wrote *Reclaimed* across it and the census walked on past. Hazel stood in the lane with their heart going hard and shallow and understood, suddenly, exactly what kind of place this row had been—the kind of place where people who were not on any list had made lists of their own, in doorways, in debts, in who borrowed salt from whom. The kind of place Wren's father had known to come looking.

Hazel knocked on the fish.

* * *

The woman who opened the door was small and brown and folded like a deck chair, with eyes gone pale as fogged glass but still, unmistakably, working. She looked at Hazel for a long moment. Then she looked past Hazel, down the lane, both ways, the way people look when being seen is a habit they've never managed to break.

"You have his shoulders," she said. "Emeka's. He carried them high, here—" she touched her own neck "—like he was always about to argue with the sky. Come in before the lane sees you."

Inside was dim and warm and smelled of river, tea, and lamp oil. Every surface held something mended. Hazel noticed it the way they noticed everything now: the chair legs scarred where joints had been reglued, a kettle with a patched seam, a window glazed with panes of three different ages. This was a household that repaired instead of replaced, and something in Hazel's chest unclenched a degree.

"I'm called Mother Ansel," the woman said, pouring tea without asking. "Was called that before your time and I'll be called it after, likely. Sit. You'll be the Okafor child."

Not a question. Hazel set down the cup too quickly. "How—"

"Sit," she said again, gently, and Hazel sat.

"Old Aldous came down here," Mother Ansel said, "the keeper, the winter after the water. Big man, careful boots, ink on every finger. He sat where you're sitting and he wrote down everything we told him, and when I asked him why bother, seeing as nobody official wanted us written anywhere, he said—" her voice changed, took on another man's cadence, and Hazel understood she had carried this sentence for eleven years the way Hazel had carried the watch "—he said, *'What the state won't hold, somebody must.'*" She sipped. "He wrote your mother's name as best we could give it to him. Adaeze. He asked me three times to spell it. He was not going to get it wrong."

"He spelled it right," Hazel said. Their voice came out strange, flattened. "In the ledger. It's right."

Mother Ansel nodded, unsurprised, unsurprising. So Wren had told her—or the ledger itself had traveled, word moving down the rows the way water moved, finding every low place. "And you've come back to the row after all these years. Workshop boy. They say you fixed the observatory clock." Her clouded eyes were fixed on Hazel with terrible accuracy. "They say a lot of things lately. That's new, saying things."

"I came because I didn't know this existed until last month," Hazel said. "I came because a book told me my parents had a house on a street that isn't on any map, and I needed to find out if the book was lying."

"And is it?"

Hazel looked around the room—at the mended kettle, at the fish-shaped shadow of the knocker visible through the window's middle pane, at the flood line running down the lane outside like a sentence underlined in brick.

"No," they said. "It undersold it."

* * *

Mother Ansel talked for two hours, and Hazel let the tea go cold in their hands and listened the way they worked a frozen movement—slowly, with even pressure, no forcing.

Emeka Okafor had built boats. Not the deepwater kind—shallow-draft skiffs and lighters, the workboats of the row, and he'd built them in the yard behind the third house, and the whole street had been either his customers or his debtors or his family, categories which on Lantern Row overlapped almost entirely. He had a laugh you could hear across the water. He argued with the sky, Mother Ansel said again, delighted by her own phrase. When the rains came late he'd stand in the yard and shout at the clouds like a man haggling.

Adaeze had kept accounts—not the money kind, or not only. She kept the other ledger, the human one: who owed whom a kindness, whose child needed shoes, which widow on the row could be trusted with a secret and which couldn't. People brought their disputes to her the way they brought their hulls to him. "She settled more fights than the watch ever did," Mother Ansel said. "Fair, and she made you *feel* it was fair, which is harder."

And the child—Hazel could not make themselves ask, and did not have to, because Mother Ansel simply turned toward them and said, "You sat on my counter right there, that spot, and ate dried apples and named every tool in your father's rack before you were five. *Adze. Auger. Gouge.* Your mother said you'd be a scholar. Your father said you'd be a better boatwright than him, which was his way of saying he loved you, because he never once said it plain. He'd have said it eventually. Men like him get there. The water just didn't give him the time."

The ice inside Hazel cracked further. It was not grief, exactly, or not only—it was the vertigo of the ledger room repeated at full scale, being *known*, being remembered by people who owed nothing to the record and had kept one anyway, in mouths and counters and dried-apple afternoons. The government's papers said Hazel Okafor had never existed. But existence, it turned out, had witnesses.

"The night of the water," Hazel said. It wasn't quite a question. They had never known, and had trained themselves never to want to know, and now the wanting was enormous.

Mother Ansel was quiet a moment. Outside, the river made its long grey sound, patient as ever, holding everything it held.

"High thaw," she said finally. "Ice jammed at the bend, water backing up behind it all day, and the criers came through too late—they always came through the row too late, we weren't on the official route, see, the route followed the registered streets. Your father got four households onto his lighter before it went over. Four. The Delacroixs, the two Voss brothers, Widow Neme and her girls. He went back for the Oyelowos." She looked at her hands. "Your mother wouldn't get in the first trip. She said she'd go when the child went. And the child—" the fogged eyes came up to Hazel's face "—the child was already on the door, downstream, pulled out by the ferryman Kessel at the coal wharf. So she stayed for a child who was already safe, because nobody could tell her so in time. That's the whole of it. There's no version where anyone failed you, understand? I've had eleven years to check the arithmetic. The water was just faster than the news."

Hazel set the cold cup down very carefully, because their hands wanted to do something else entirely, and this woman deserved better than a smashed cup.

"The ferryman," they managed. "Kessel."

"Dead now, peace on him. His sister runs the ferry." Mother Ansel rose, joints crackling like green wood, and went to a dresser in the corner—a great dark thing with drawers that clearly opened easier than they looked. From behind a stack of linens she brought out a flat packet wrapped in oilcloth, tied with string, handled like the relic it evidently was.

"I kept one thing," she said. "Out of the whole house. Your father's yard flooded to the eaves; there was nothing left to keep. But this was pinned inside my own shutters since before—I used to keep the row's notices pinned there, and your mother gave me this the spring before, some festival, maypole or boat-blessing, I don't recall which. I forgot I had it until the keeper came asking, and then I was glad, and then I was afraid to have it, and then the years went by." She pressed the packet into Hazel's hands. "It should go where it's going now. Whatever that is."

Hazel unwrapped it with clockmaker's fingers.

The photograph was small, the size of a playing card, silvered and foxed at the edges, and it took a moment to resolve in the dim room—and then it resolved all at once and stopped Hazel's breath.

A boatyard. Spring light, judging by the leaves. A broad-chested man with high-held shoulders stood grinning beside a half-finished skiff, one hand resting on the gunwale as though introducing it. Beside him a slim woman with a headwrap and an expression of amused skepticism—as though the photographer had made a claim she was prepared to negotiate—held on her hip a round-faced child of perhaps four, who was staring directly into the camera with an intensity that had not diminished in eleven years. It stared out of the photograph now. It stared at Hazel.

Hazel had no memory of their mother's face. That was the fact they had lived around for eleven years like furniture in the dark, bruising a hip on it nightly. And here it was. Amused. Skeptical. Negotiating. Here were the shoulders they apparently carried high. Here was a child who did not know yet what the river was going to take, staring at the future with everything still ahead of it, and the future had arrived, and it was standing in Mother Ansel's kitchen holding the photograph in hands built for fine work.

"They're real," Hazel said. It was all there was. "The papers said—I mean, there were never papers. There was never anything. But they're real. They were real."

"That's what the keeper wrote in his book," Mother Ansel said softly. "I watched him write it. *Real.* Well—he wrote *recorded*, but it meant the same."

Hazel laughed, or sobbed, or performed some operation indistinguishable from both, and Mother Ansel tactfully went and put more water in the teapot and let them have their minute at the window, where the light was better, where the faces were clearer, where the flood line on the brick across the lane ran steady under the grey sky like a signature.

When Hazel turned back, they said, "Come to the observatory."

Mother Ansel paused, teapot in hand.

"There's a ledger there," Hazel said. "Their names are in it. My names are in it. And soon—" they thought of Crane's lists, of the historians' advance party, of the copying nights and the plan taking shape upstairs in the tower like a movement slowly coming into beat "—soon there will be a record that counts, an official one, one that can't be erased again. And when it is, I'm going to enter every name from Lantern Row that the ledger holds. And I would rather enter yours alive and present, sitting where your counter used to be, than secondhand."

The old woman was silent for a long moment. Then she said, "You argue with the sky too. I can hear it." She set down the pot. "I'm eighty-one years old, boatwright's-boy. I don't climb hills."

"There's a ferryman," Hazel said, "who owes the observatory."

That earned a real smile, sharp and young under the age. "Then I'll come by water," she said. "Like everything else worth bringing upriver."

* * *

Hazel left as the afternoon tilted, the photograph wrapped again in its oilcloth but carried now not in the bottom of the satchel, beneath everything, the way the watch had ridden for eleven years—but in the inner pocket, against the chest, where documents went. On the doorstep Mother Ansel caught their sleeve.

"One more thing, since you're collecting the past today. Your father had a brother. Didn't drown—went upstream before the bad years, before the registrations started, looking for work in the yards at Marrow's Bend. Emeka sent letters; the row passed them hand to hand till the addresses stopped working. Last anyone here heard, the brother was alive, and had a son of his own." Her grip tightened. "You have cousins, child, if the water spared them. Somebody ought to know that. Names don't end unless we let them."

Hazel walked back up through the tannery district with the flood line at shoulder height and rising behind them, and the city looked different—thinner, less solid, its official streets laid like paper over deep water. Everything Bellhaven pretended to be was a thin crust. Underneath: rows and ledgers and photographs in oilcloth, witnesses and willows and brothers upstream, a whole submerged city keeping its own true level, waiting for the thaw.

At the foot of the bridge, where the census wagons had stood on the morning Hazel packed their satchel, a new notice had been pasted to the post, fresh paste still gleaming. Hazel stopped. It was headed with the historians' seal—the double column, the closed book—and the text beneath was brief:

*By order of the Advance Commission on Historical Continuity: all persons possessing records, writings, or testimonies pertaining to persons absent from the General Register shall surrender such materials at designated stations within six days of publication, for assessment and lawful disposition.*

*Lawful disposition.* Hazel read it twice, feeling the photograph warm against their chest like a second heartbeat. Six days. The ice was due to break in eight.

They turned toward the hill and the tower, and climbed.
