## 24. The Plan of Gears

The plan began, as most things in the observatory did, with a gear that would not sit still.

Hazel had laid it on the long table in the keeper's quarters—a brass wheel the size of a dinner plate, lifted from the master clock's striking train, its teeth worn to a soft shine by a century of counting hours. Around it, as the evening deepened and Marisol set out bread and cold tea without being asked, the others gathered the way iron filings gather around a magnet: Wren first, arms folded; then Old Piotr, feeling his way along the wall with his white cane; then Marisol herself, wiping her hands on her apron though they were already clean.

"The clock is repaired," Hazel said. "Or nearly. The escapement is true, the second dial runs, the strike train will sound when I let it." They turned the gear with one finger, and it spun with a whisper, balanced, obedient. "Which means we have a machine that can tell the whole city what time it is. The question is what time we tell them."

Wren pulled a chair around and sat backward on it, chin on her arms. "Say the rest."

"The uprising is coming. Everyone in this room knows it—the dockworkers have stopped pretending otherwise, and the soldiers on the bridge have started checking each other's faces instead of ours. When it's over, whoever wins will call a provisional council. And the first thing any council does—" Hazel tapped the gear, and it stilled— "is decide what happened. They'll write it down. Officially. With seals."

"And the historians," Piotr said into the quiet. His blind eyes were aimed at the ceiling, where the shadows of the tower timbers crossed like clock hands. "The ones who arrived early. They are not waiting for victory to start their version."

"No," Wren said. "They're not."

"Then here is what I keep failing to say plainly, so I will say it badly." Piotr lowered his face toward the table, toward the warmth of the candle and the circle of hands. "A record that stays in this tower protects no one. A truth locked in a basement is only a rumor with better handwriting. It must be *entered*. Into whatever they build next. On the first day. Before the ink of the new regime dries around the old lies."

Marisol sat down slowly, her chair groaning. "You want to hand it to them. The ledger. To the victors. Voluntarily."

"I want to *witness* it," Hazel said. "There's a difference. A document delivered is evidence. A person standing in front of the council, opening the book, saying the names aloud—that's testimony. It can't be filed away. It has to be answered."

The candle fluttered. Somewhere above them, through three floors of stone and timber, the stopped official dial hung in the dark like a coin at the bottom of a well, and beneath it, geared to it, the secret dial that told the truer hour.

Wren looked at the gear for a long moment. Then she said, "Then we do it properly. We plan it like my father planned the mechanism. Every wheel cut before it's needed. Nothing left to hope."

* * *

They worked until the second bell past midnight, and the table filled.

Hazel drew first—not maps but the tower itself, from the ledger room to the lantern gallery, nine hundred steps rendered in quick confident strokes. "The clock goes public before the council convenes. That's the order of operations. If the master clock starts striking while the city is still deciding who won, then true time is already running when they sit down. They can't certify an official noon that contradicts a bell every citizen just heard."

"They could stop it again," Marisol said. "Someone stopped it once."

"Let them try while half of Bellhaven is listening for it." Hazel's voice was flat and certain, and hearing it come out of their own mouth frightened them a little. "But you're right. Which brings us to the escapement. If it fails again—if anyone touches it again—we need it to fail *loudly*, and we need to know who. I'm cutting a witness into the works. A flaw that only shows if a hand other than mine disturbs the pallets. A clockmaker's signature in reverse."

Piotr smiled without teeth. "A trap made of precision."

"A trap made of honesty. Everything in that machine should be exactly what it claims to be. Anything that isn't will announce itself."

Wren was already writing. She wrote fast, in her father's cramped upright hand, lists that grew legs as she spoke them: "Duplicates. We finish the copying—all of it—within four nights. Pages go into clock cases bound for Ashford Landing, Corvey Mill, and the ferry towns. Marisol knows the shippers who don't open crates. One copy stays in the tower, but not in the ledger room—Piotr's archive, behind the star charts, where Crane's men already searched once and found nothing because they didn't know what they were looking at."

"They will search better the second time," Piorr said mildly.

"Then the true ledger never sleeps in the same place twice. It moves between the clock case in the west alcove, the false bottom of Marisol's flour bin, and a sealed oil chamber in the turret clock I've never shown anyone." She looked up. "And on the day the council convenes, it doesn't hide at all. It walks down nine hundred steps in plain daylight, carried by—"

She stopped. The room stopped with her. Everyone had known this part was coming; everyone had been quietly arranging, in the private ledgers of their own hearts, reasons why it shouldn't be them.

"Hazel carries it," Marisol said.

"It should be me," Wren began. "It's my father's—"

"It's your father's, and that's exactly why they'll take it from you." Marisol's voice held no apology, only the terrible practicality of a woman who had survived twenty years of hiding by seeing rooms clearly. "If the historians get one chance to seize that book back, they'll take the daughter of the man who wrote it. You'd be arrested before you reached the bridge and they'd call it lawful custody of stolen records. But an apprentice. A clockmaker sent to repair the clock, carrying tools." She nodded at Hazel. "Nobody stops a tradesperson with a heavy bag. I've walked past more soldiers than I can count carrying things heavier than books."

Hazel felt the weight of the satchel before they touched it—it hung on its peg by the door, empty these weeks, or supposed to be. "I'll carry it," they said. "And I'll testify. My name is in that book twice—once in his hand, once in mine, soon. No one else in Bellhaven can say that."

"Once," Piotr corrected gently, "you'll say it aloud. That's the third time, and the one that counts."

* * *

They assigned the roles the way a watchmaker assigns trains: strike, going, and escapement, each dependent on each, the whole thing dead if one fails.

Wren would hold the observatory. Someone had to keep the tower—and if soldiers came during the council session, someone had to be upstairs with the duplicates and the knowledge of where everything slept, speaking in her father's calm bureaucratic voice until the words ran out. She accepted this with a tightness around her jaw that everyone saw and nobody mentioned.

Marisol owned the river. The ferryman Tomas owed her more than she ever said aloud; he would be at the ice road's edge from dawn on the chosen day, boat warm, current read, ready to move a person or a book or both across water that might be breaking up. "If the ice goes early," she said, "the roads die and the river becomes the only street in Bellhaven. So the river is mine."

Piotr claimed the sky. He would watch the weather, the smoke columns, the behavior of gulls—which, he informed them, left the flats twelve hours before the ice shifted—and give the household its calendar. "Revolutions have schedules written in wind," he said. "I have spent forty years learning to read instruments the state says don't exist. This one reads itself."

"And Idris?" Hazel asked, finally. The name came out carefully, the way you lift a cracked plate.

The room went still. Idris had been at the observatory five days now, sleeping in the storeroom below the clock room, working under Hazel's direction in silence, his master's tools laid out each morning with the exactness of a penance. He ate what he was given. He had not asked forgiveness again since the first night.

"He knows the census office," Wren said. "Its rhythms, its clerks, its cowardices. When Crane moves—and he'll move before the council sits—Idris may hear it first."

"You trust him?" Marisol asked, and the question wasn't hostile. It was genuinely open, which was worse.

Hazel turned the brass gear on the table, slowly, watching the teeth catch candlelight. "I trust that he betrayed my parents to survive, and that it has been eating him alive for fifteen years, and that a man being eaten alive makes a very reliable alarm." They looked up. "I don't forgive him. Forgiveness is a debt and I'm not paying it yet. But I'll use him. He'd want to be used. It's the only coin he has left and he knows it."

Piotr nodded once, slow as a pendulum. "That," he said, "is how records work too. You don't have to love what's written down. You only have to enter it honestly."

* * *

Near dawn, when the plan covered three sheets of paper and the tea had gone cold twice, Marisol brought up eggs and black bread and they ate standing, the way exhausted conspirators do, and the talk loosened into smaller, braver things.

"Do you know what I'll put in the ledger," Marisol said suddenly, "when there are new pages? My mother's name. Anaya Ferro. She died unregistered in the fever year, and there isn't a single piece of paper in Bellhaven that says she lived. Twenty years I've kept myself off every list, and I find I mostly did it so that someday I could put us both on one."

"Anaya Ferro," Piotr repeated solemnly, tasting it. "A good name. It scans well. It would strike nicely on a bell."

Wren laughed—a short, surprised sound—and then covered her mouth, and then laughed again, and for a moment the tower held nothing but five tired people laughing at a joke about typography at four in the morning while a revolution assembled itself somewhere beyond the bridges. Hazel found their own mouth hurting and realized they were smiling.

It was Wren who brought them back. She flattened the last sheet against the table and read the schedule aloud, her voice steadying with each line:

"Nightly copying until done—four nights, rotating rooms so lamplight comes from a different window each time. Piotr watches wind and ice and gives us the word. When the fighting starts, nobody goes to it; we stay with the machines. The moment word comes that a council has convened—Tomas handles word on the river, Idris from the town side—Hazel takes the true ledger, in the open, down the steps, across by whatever route Piotr calls, and stands before those men and women and opens the book. The clock strikes the hour while they listen. And the names go into the record before anyone can decide the record is finished."

She set down the page. "Questions."

"One," said Hazel. "What do we do if it all happens too fast? If the council sits before the copies are safe?"

No one answered at once. Outside, far downstream, came a sound so low it was felt more than heard—a long groan from the direction of the river, the ice shifting in its sleep, dreaming of spring.

"Then," Marisol said at last, quietly, "we do the brave version instead of the clever one."

Piotr rose, felt his way to the window, and stood with his face to the east, where the sky was turning the grey of unpolished steel. "Wind's shifted," he said. "Warm tonight. From the south."

Hazel joined him at the glass. Below, Bellhaven lay under its last honest darkness, chimneys and rooftops and the great pale scar of the frozen river, and somewhere among those roofs, census wagons and historians' couriers and frightened clerks were all sleeping inside the same closing hour. Above the tower the stars were going out one by one, the way they always did, punctual as anything.

"Warm wind," Hazel murmured. "How many days?"

Piotr tilted his head, listening to something far away—the river, perhaps, or forty years of archived mornings.

"Fewer than four," he said.
