## 25. Sabotage

Hazel heard it before they understood it.

They were three floors down in the ledger room, copying the winter pages of the flood year, when the sound came through the tower's bones—a wrongness in the clock above them, a metallic shriek that ended in a clatter like a handful of coins dropped down a well. The lamplight shivered. Wren's pen stopped mid-stroke and left a blot like a small black planet on the page.

"That wasn't the chime," she said.

"No," Hazel said. "That was the escapement."

They took the stairs two at a time, nine hundred steps compressed into a single breathless climb, and when Hazel pushed open the door to the clock chamber the first thing they saw was the pendulum—still. Not slowing. Still. The great rod hung at its resting arc, motionless as a hanged man, and above it the master clock's hands stood at eleven minutes past four in the morning, frozen at the moment of their own death.

The second thing Hazel saw was the escapement itself, or what remained of it. Someone had been inside the cage of brass and steel that regulated the whole city's official time. The pallet stones—rubies, old ones, irreplaceable—had been pried from their settings and were gone. The escape wheel had been forced backward until three of its teeth snapped, and the broken pieces lay scattered across the floorplate like the wreckage of something small and silver that had fallen from a great height. And the mainspring barrel had been released without restraint; the coiled power of eight days' winding had torn through the train in one furious instant, scarring every wheel it touched.

This was not neglect. This was not age. Hazel had spent six weeks learning this machine the way you learn a face, and what they were looking at was a face they knew with its features deliberately rearranged.

Wren stood in the doorway behind them, very quiet. Marisol, who had come up with the lamp, made a small sound low in her throat, the sound of a woman seeing a wound she recognized.

"Crane," Wren said.

"Maybe," Hazel said, and knelt.

* * *

The morning came up grey through the observatory's east windows while Hazel worked among the wreckage, cataloguing. That was the training speaking—the part of them that Master Idris had built, the part that said *when the world breaks, describe the break*. So they described.

The pallet stones had been removed with a tool, not fingers. A watchmaker's screwdriver, narrow blade, judging by the marks on the setting screws—and there was only one width of screwdriver in Bellhaven that fit those screws cleanly, because the screws themselves had been cut to an eccentric standard by a keeper who had distrusted standardization on principle. Wren's father's own specifications. Whoever had done this had used tools cut to Aldous patterns.

The escape wheel teeth had not been snapped by force alone. Hazel held one fragment to the lamp and saw the fine score line running across the base of each broken tooth, a hairline groove machined into the metal before the break. Stress lines. Placed lines. Someone had pre-cut the wheel so that a modest blow would shatter it along predictable paths—which meant preparation, which meant time inside the clock chamber, hours of it, unobserved.

And then, near dawn, in the bottom of the movement frame where no hand would go except for cleaning or repair, Hazel found the third thing.

It was a flaw. There was no other honest word for it. A small steel block, no bigger than a thumbnail, wedged between the fourth wheel's arbor and the frame plate, jamming the train. It had contributed nothing to the destruction—indeed, it had been installed *after* the destruction, its surfaces still bright, unmarked by the violence around it. It served no mechanical purpose whatsoever except to prevent the train from turning even if someone repaired everything else.

But it was not merely a block. Hazel lifted it free with their tweezers and turned it under the light, and their stomach turned over slowly, like a wheel coming to rest.

The block had been filed. Deliberately, precisely, with a jeweler's file, into a shape—and the shape was a notch pattern Hazel had known since childhood, since the first year in Idris's workshop, since the day a broad-shouldered man with flour-white scars on his knuckles had put a scrap of failed brass in Hazel's small hands and said: *I mark my rejects so no one sells them as true work. Three cuts, deep-short-long. My father's mark, and his father's before him.*

Three cuts. Deep-short-long.

Idris's mark.

* * *

Hazel sat on the floorplate for a long time with the marked block in their palm, letting the cold of the brass soak upward into their hand, and thought about coats.

Survival and betrayal could wear the same coat—Idris had taught them that already, in the terrible interview after his release, when he had wept and confessed and begged and Hazel had given him neither forgiveness nor refusal but only silence, which is heavier than either. Idris had sold Hazel's parents to the census years ago to keep his workshop. He had been broken out of prison hollow-eyed and trembling, and he had come to the observatory once, hat in hand, and Old Piotr had fed him at the kitchen table because Piotr held the opinion that hunger made every other sin worse, and after that Idris had gone back to his sealed workshop and his empty nights, and Hazel had not seen him since.

So the mark in Hazel's palm said: *Idris was here.*

And the broken rubies and the shattered wheel and the stopped heart of Bellhaven's official time said: *Idris destroyed this.*

Unless.

Hazel got up. Their knees complained; they ignored them. They carried the block to the window where the grey light was strongest and looked again, really looked, the way they would look at any piece brought into the workshop for assessment, because sentiment ruins more repairs than carelessness does.

The filing was fine work. Finer than fine—it was the work of a man whose hands shook now, whose eyes had gone uncertain since whatever had been done to him in the census building, and yet every cut was clean, controlled, the kind of control that comes not from steady hands but from slow ones. A trembling craftsman who refuses to hurry. Hazel knew exactly one person who worked that way anymore.

And the block itself—why block the train at all? If Crane wanted the clock dead, the missing pallets and the shattered wheel accomplished that completely. The block was redundant. Unless it was not a block at all unless it was a message addressed to the only person in Bellhaven who would ever crawl into the movement's guts and remove it by hand. Unless it was not sabotage at all unless it was a letter written in the one language its author and its reader shared, the language of flaws, of deliberate imperfections, of *I mark my rejects.*

Deep-short-long. Hazel turned the phrase over. Their master had another saying too, older, from the first year, when Hazel had asked why he kept a cracked lens on his bench as a paperweight instead of throwing it away.

*A flaw in the right place,* Idris had said, *is information. Only fools grind them out.*

Hazel went back to the movement and looked at where the block had been seated. Not randomly placed—it had been wedged against the fourth wheel's arbor at a specific orientation, and the arbor, Hazel noticed now, bore a fresh scratch, a thin bright line spiraling around the polished steel like a thread. Not damage. A pointer. Hazel followed the line of the scratch with their eye, around and down, to the fourth wheel itself, and there, on the wheel's under-face where it would be invisible from every angle except the one a repairer's eye takes when lying beneath the movement with a lamp, was a second mark.

Not Idris's maker's mark. Numbers.

A date, cut small and precise: the seventeenth of the ice month. Three days hence. And beside the date, a word abbreviated in the old workshop shorthand every apprentice in Bellhaven had been beaten into learning: *BRDG* — bridge. And beneath that, a final character that stopped Hazel's breath: a single stroke, slanted, the shorthand sign for *under*.

The seventeenth. The bridge. Underneath.

"He didn't do it," Hazel said aloud, to the empty chamber, to the hanging pendulum, to nobody. "He did it, but he didn't *do* it."

* * *

They gathered the household in the kitchen at mid-morning, because a secret that stays in one skull becomes a stone, and Hazel had learned enough in these weeks to know what needed weighing. Wren sat with her arms crossed and her jaw set. Marisol kept her hands busy out of twenty years of habit—kneading dough that did not need kneading—while Piotr sat nearest the stove with his clouded eyes aimed at the warmth and his ears doing all his seeing.

"The clock is dead," Hazel said. "Killed, not died. And Idris's mark is all over it."

Wren's arms uncrossed slowly. "Your master."

"My master." The words tasted strange—half claim, half accusation. "Pallet stones taken. Escape wheel pre-cut and snapped. And a marker block hidden in the train with his cuts on it."

"So he betrayed you twice," Wren said flatly. "First your parents, now the clock. Once a man has sold one family, why stop?"

"Because the mark isn't the whole message." Hazel set the steel block on the table, and the kitchen went quiet around it, the way kitchens do around evidence. "There's more. He left me a trail, buried in the movement. A date, three days out. The bridge. And the word *under*."

Marisol's floured hands went still.

"Crane's men took Idris," Hazel said. "Or summoned him, or threatened him—I don't know how yet, and I'll know before the week's out. But here's what I think happened. They put a file and a hammer in his hands and told him to kill the clock, because a clockmaker's work passes inspection where a soldier's crowbar wouldn't. And he did it. Look—" Hazel spread the broken wheel fragments on the table, the scored teeth, the evidence of a job thoroughly done. "He destroyed it properly. No half-measures they could catch. And then, in the wreckage, where only a clockmaker would ever look, he built the one flaw he could choose the place of. A flaw in the right place is information."

"A meeting," Piotr said quietly. It was the most anyone had heard him say in days. "Under the bridge, on the seventeenth. Your master is telling you where he can be found—or where something can be found." His blind gaze swung toward Hazel with uncanny accuracy. "Or where he will be made to stand while they finish what he started. A message like that runs both ways, child. It may be a rescue. It may be bait. The man who wrote it may not even know which he intends."

"I know," Hazel said.

"You'll go anyway," Wren said. It wasn't a question.

"I'll go anyway." Hazel looked down at the block, at the three cuts, deep-short-long, worn by four generations of Blackwood-adjacent hands—no, Okafor hands now, claimed hands, chosen hands. "He sold my parents to keep his shop. I've had six weeks to hate him for it and I have, every day, and it hasn't gotten smaller. But he also taught me everything I know about reading a mechanism, and then he walked into a room full of men who wanted this clock dead, and he used everything he taught me to leave a note in the corpse." Hazel's voice caught on something they refused to name. "Betrayal and rescue in the same wound. That's Idris. I don't have to forgive him to follow the trail."

Marisol began shaping the dough at last, quick and decisive, as if a decision had been made somewhere in the flour. "Then you don't go alone, and you don't go at dusk like a thief. The ferryman owes us. And underneath the bridge—" She glanced at Piotr, and something passed between the old ones, some memory of years when meetings under bridges had been common currency. "Underneath the bridge is where the current runs fastest and the ice is thinnest. Whatever he's leaving you there, he chose a place where followers make noise."

"Seventeenth," Wren said. She pulled the copy-book toward her and wrote the date at the top of a fresh page, her handwriting sharp as a graver's cut. "We have three days to finish the winter pages, hide the last duplicates, and put the clock's heart back together enough that the city doesn't notice its pulse is gone before we're ready for it to stop mattering."

"The clock comes first," Hazel said. "A dead clock tells Crane we're vulnerable. A running clock tells him nothing, because he already knows we're dangerous—he just can't prove how. I can restore the train in two days if I cannibalize the regulator for the pallet settings. The rubies are gone, but—" Hazel hesitated, then reached into their shirt and drew out the broken watch, their parents' watch, dead these many years, warm from resting against their sternum. "His pallet stones are close enough in cut. It's what it was for. Keeping time."

Nobody spoke while Hazel set the watch on the table beside Idris's marked block, the two objects side by side like opposing testimony.

"It's the right trade," Piotr said gently, to the room, or perhaps to Hazel. "The dead lend their tools to the living. That's the oldest arrangement there is."

* * *

Hazel worked through the afternoon and into the dark, alone in the clock chamber by choice, fitting the watch's tiny ruby pallets into the great escapement's settings with shims of their own cutting, re-truing the escape wheel tooth by tooth from spare stock, coaxing the scarred train back toward rotation. It was the finest work of their life and there was no one to see it, which felt correct; some repairs are arguments conducted privately between a maker and the made.

Around midnight they stopped, wiped their hands, and allowed themselves the thought they had been refusing all day.

Idris had stood in this room. Idris, coerced, terrified, half-broken, had climbed the nine hundred steps in custody or in secret, had knelt where Hazel knelt, had opened the same cage of brass—and in the middle of his destruction he had thought, *the apprentice will look under the fourth wheel.* In the worst hour of his life, betting everything on being read correctly by the person he had wronged most. That was not absolution. Hazel held onto that. That was not forgiveness, and they were not required to feel anything clean about it.

But it meant the old man had chosen a side, in the only language he had never once lied in.

Hazel set the last shim, closed the movement, and wound the barrel—gently, a quarter turn, listening. The train breathed. The escapement ticked once, twice, caught, released, caught. Above them the great hands trembled and advanced a single minute, and somewhere below, all across the sleeping city of Bellhaven, official time resumed without anyone knowing it had ever paused.

Hazel sat back on their heels in the lamplight, exhausted, hands bleeding in two small places, and watched the pendulum gather its rhythm like a man remembering how to walk.

Two days. Then the bridge, and whatever waited underneath—rescue, bait, or the old man himself standing in the shadow of the pilings with his master's tools and his unspeakable debts.

The clock struck one, and Hazel counted every stroke.
