## 26. Idris's Last Repair

The knock came at the fourth hour after midnight, and Idris Voss knew before he opened the door that it would be Hazel. He had known it, in fact, since the moment his hands had finished their work in the observatory tower two nights past—known it the way a clockmaker knows when a train has been set and there is nothing left to do but wait for the hour to arrive. Certain things, once wound, cannot be unwound. He had spent seventy years learning that truth in brass and steel, and still it had taken him a lifetime to understand that it applied to men as well as to movements.

He opened the door without lighting the lamp first, because he did not want to see his own face reflected in the glass while he said what he had come to say.

"You shouldn't be out," he said. "The wagons are on the bridge again."

"So are you," Hazel said. "And you knocked."

* * *

He let them in. The workshop—the workshop that had been sealed with census wax and then unsealed with census wax, which told its own story about how the wind was blowing—was cold. He had not lit the forge since they took him. There seemed no honest way to heat iron anymore without wondering whose name might be hammered into it.

Hazel stood just inside the door with their satchel against their chest the way soldiers hold their packs in boats, and Idris saw, for the first time in all the years of the boy's—of the apprentice's—employment, how thin the line had always been between this child and the street. He had told himself for nine years that taking Hazel in was charity. He had told himself it was pragmatism, cheap labor, good sense. He had told himself many things at fair market value.

"Sit," he said.

"I'd rather stand."

"Yes." He almost smiled. "You would."

He went to the long bench at the back of the shop, the one under the window where the light came best, and knelt—with difficulty; his knees had been complaining since the cell—to the cabinet beneath it. The bottom drawer. Behind the drawer. His fingers found the panel by touch even in the dark, because his hands remembered what his mind kept trying to forget: that he had built this hiding place not for tools but for shame, and shame weighs nothing and takes up every inch of room you have.

He lifted out the roll of oiled leather and carried it to the bench, and unrolled it under the moon through the window, and heard Hazel's breath change.

Every master clockmaker owns one set of tools he does not use. They are not display pieces; they are not heirlooms in the decorative sense. They are the tools he was judged by—his examination pieces, his journeyman works, the files he ground himself at nineteen from blank steel because his own master told him a file bought ready-made carries another man's errors in its teeth. Idris's set lay open now on the bench: eleven pieces, each wrapped in its own pocket of oilcloth, each polished by four decades of palms. The fine screwdriver with the handle worn to an oval that fit no hand but his. The jeweler's pliers with jaws so fine they could lift a pivot the width of an eyelash. The little hammer with the head of hardened whalebone that struck softer than any metal and never marked.

"These were my master's before they were mine," Idris said. "He gave them to me the day I passed, and he said—" His voice caught on something. He pushed through it. "He said, *These are for making things true. Anything else you do with them is theft.*"

Hazel had not moved. In the moonlight their face was very still, the stillness of someone conserving themselves for what was coming, and Idris understood that they already knew—not the details, but the shape of it. Apprentices know. That was the whole horror of it. He had been lying to this person across a workbench for nine years, shoulder to shoulder, passing tweezers back and forth like a shared hand, and the whole time some part of Hazel had known there was a room in him kept locked.

"Say it," Idris said. "Whatever you came to say."

"You sabotaged the escapement."

Not a question. But it deserved an answer made of more than a nod, so he gave the answer entire, standing at his own bench like a man at a dock waiting for a ship he did not want to board.

"The first night," he said, "two days before the clock stopped. A man came to the shop. Not Crane—Crane is a clerk, a careful animal; he sends others. This was one of the others. He sat where you're sitting and he ate one of Marisol's almond biscuits that somebody had brought from the market, and he asked me did I know the apprentice at the observatory. And I said I knew of them. And he said, there's going to be a new time in Bellhaven, Master Voss, and clocks keep whatever time they're told, and your name is on a list of men who have been generous before. And then he put a paper on my bench." Idris's hands had begun to move without him, the old unconscious choreography of explanation—he picked up the whalebone hammer, set it down, picked up nothing at all. "It was a copy of a deposition. Twenty-two years old. My signature at the bottom."

Hazel said nothing. The silence had a texture to it, a weight per square inch, like the pressure inside a mainspring barrel just before it lets go.

"You know what it was," Idris said. "You'll have worked it out. It was the names. Your parents' names. Where they lodged, where they worked, what they were to each other. I gave those names to the census office twenty-two years ago, in the winter after the flood, in exchange for—" He laughed, one dry note with nothing in it. "In exchange for nothing, as it turns out. In exchange for being left alone, which lasted exactly as long as it suited them, which is the only warranty the government ever honors. I told myself I did it to keep the workshop. To keep *this*—" he gestured at the benches, the tools, the hanging weights, all of it suddenly obscene—"so that there would be somewhere in Bellhaven where a certain kind of child could learn a trade. I told myself the names were already known. Everyone's names were known, that winter. What difference could one more list make?"

"And now?" Hazel said quietly.

"Now I know what difference. Now I've read a ledger." He closed his eyes. "There was a girl in the flood records. Okafor, infant, survived, lodged with the mother's cousin on Tanner's Row. My handwriting, Hazel. I didn't just sell them the names. I wrote them out fair, in my best round hand, the way I write invoice headings. I made them *legible*. I prepared your family for filing."

The words ran out of him the way air runs out of a bellows with the valve jammed, and when he was empty he sat down heavily on his stool, an old man at a bench in the dark, and told the rest.

"So the man with the biscuits says, stop the clock. Not break it—stop it. Make it look like age, like neglect, like the city's official time simply died of natural causes, because the historians coming after the ice breaks want it dead of natural causes. A murdered clock raises questions. A deceased one gets a respectful funeral and a new movement installed by the regime's own makers, and every bell in Bellhaven tells the new time from then on." He looked at his hands. "I refused. I want that understood, though I have no right to be understood. I said no. And he smiled—the worst thing, the smile—and said, then we start with the boy from Tanner's Row, and we don't stop there, we go down the whole ledger, name by name, and we can do it slow or we can do it fast, and which way depends entirely on the cooperativeness of Master Voss."

"That's why the flaw," Hazel said.

"That's why the flaw." He nodded, grateful and ashamed of the gratitude. "They watched me do it. Stood behind me at the clock while my hands did what they were told. But they're not clockmakers. They watched the hands; they couldn't read them. So I did what was ordered—pallets shortened, oil fouled, the count wheel pinned wrong—and then, in the doing, I did one thing more. One thing only a maker would find, because only a maker would ever get that deep into the escapement and live there long enough to notice. The recoil pallet on the exit side—I cut it with a deliberate eccentricity. A wrongness with a shape to it. Any competent repairman fixes it and moves on. But someone who has been taught properly, someone who looks twice at everything because they've had to—that person sees it isn't wear. Wear is chaos. This was *authored*. And the shape of it—a pallet cut short on one flank, long on the other—is the oldest warning sign in the trade. It means: *check the train behind me. Something upstream is false.*"

"You left a message in the mechanism," Hazel said. "For whoever repaired the clock."

"For you." His voice broke on it, finally, and he let it. "I couldn't say it. I couldn't write it—paper is their country, paper is where they win. But steel, Hazel—steel I've spoken since I was nine years old. Steel doesn't lie about who cut it. So I put the confession where only an honest pair of hands would ever find it, and then I went home and waited two days for you to climb nine hundred steps and either hang me with it or understand it."

* * *

The fire had gone out entirely in the little stove, and neither of them moved to light it. Somewhere outside, the river was speaking in its new loosened voice, the ice beginning to mutter in its sleep.

"Why tell me now?" Hazel asked. "Crane's men will know you talked. Whatever protection the threat bought—"

"It bought nothing. It was never meant to buy anything. Men like that pay in advance and collect forever." Idris reached into his coat and brought out a folded paper, laid it on the bench beside the tools. "My deposition. The original. The one with my real signature, twenty-two years ago. The other was a copy, but copies convince juries, and this one convinces historians. When the council sits, you may need a traitor who testifies to the trade. Apparently we are common enough." He paused. "I signed it before I understood what I was signing. That is not an excuse. It is a measurement. I offer it to you the way I offer everything tonight—at full disclosed weight, take it or leave it."

Hazel looked at the paper for a long moment. Then at the tools.

"I can't give you absolution," Hazel said at last, and Idris flinched, because it was the sentence he had been dreading and the only one he had earned. "I can't forgive you for my parents. That's not mine to give, and they're not here to give it. The people who could forgive you are in the ledger, and ledgers record, they don't pardon."

"No," Idris said. "No, they don't."

"What I can do is take the tools." Hazel's voice was steady, and their hands, when they reached out, were steadier. "Because that's not forgiveness, that's arithmetic. You owe a debt to people who can't collect. So it transfers. That's how debts work—you taught me that too, though I don't think you meant to. The tools pass to me, and whatever they make goes toward what you spent them on. Not for you. Through you. Do you understand the difference?"

Idris understood. It was the cruelest possible mercy, and the only honest currency on the table: not a clean slate but a working one, an indenture with no term, redemption priced not in sorrow—which was cheap, which everyone had in stock—but in service, rendered forward, forever unreceipted.

"Yes," he said. "That I understand. That I can do."

"There's one more thing." Hazel rolled the tools closed, carefully, each piece in its pocket, the leather creaking like a ship. "The flaw you left. I found it. And you were right that it pointed upstream—but it pointed further than you intended, or maybe you did intend it, I can't tell anymore. The train behind the escapement isn't just false where you touched it. There's a second dial behind the face, geared to the main works. Wren's father built it. True time and official time, running together." Hazel shouldered the satchel, and the weight of it seemed different now, settled. "The historians think they killed the city's clock. They haven't understood yet that they walked into the one building in Bellhaven where the machinery keeps receipts."

Outside, down toward the bridge, a whistle sounded—one long note, then two short. The signal the household had agreed on. Idris's blood went cold at it; Hazel only turned their head, listening, and their face in the moonlight was not afraid.

"That's Marisol," Hazel said. "Boats on the water. The ferryman's moving early." They looked back at Idris, and for the first time in nine years, looked at him the way an equal looks at a colleague across a bench. "Get your coat, Master Voss. If you're done repairing his work, the clock needs a real clockmaker—and the ice is going out sooner than anyone planned."
