## 28. Crane Comes at Night

Silas Crane had always believed that a man's character revealed itself in the order of his papers, and so he had spent the afternoon before the raid reading other people's handwriting until his eyes ached. Tax rolls from the old regime, water-stained and inconsistent. His own census lists, forty pages of them now, cross-referenced in three colors of ink. And at the bottom of the stack, sealed with wax he was not technically authorized to break, the maintenance logs of the Bellhaven Observatory master clock.

The logs were a confession written by an institution. Forty years of entries in the same careful hand—*escapement adjusted, dial cleaned, weights rewound*—and then, eleven years ago, the hand changed. The entries grew shorter, then sporadic, then stopped altogether, three years back, on almost the same day that Aldous the keeper had died. And then nothing. A great official clock, the timepiece by which every court clerk and shipping manifest in the city set its days, left to run down like a forgotten kitchen timer.

Except it hadn't run down. It had been *stopped*. He had verified this twice, through two different clerks who owed him small favors: the mechanism showed tool marks consistent with deliberate interference, and someone had recently begun repairs. Someone young, unregistered, whose name appeared on his list twice with details that did not agree.

Crane laid his pen down and looked out the window of his office at the census bureau, where the river lay below in its winter armor of ice, pale and vast under a moon three days from full. The historians had given him until the ice broke. The ice would break early—he had consulted the ferryman's boy, who knew the river better than any almanac—which meant he had perhaps four nights.

Four nights to deliver a clean census. Four nights to make the city agree with its paperwork.

He thought, as he often did late at night, about his father, who had kept accounts for a tannery and had once shown Silas how a ledger could be made to say whatever its keeper wished, provided the keeper was patient. *Numbers are obedient,* his father had said. *It's people who argue.* Crane had built his whole life on the opposite principle: that people were obedient too, if you asked the questions carefully enough, and wrote down the answers, and asked again later to see whether they shifted.

Most people shifted. That was the pity of it. Memory was soft; paper was not. He had built his career in the gap between the two.

But the Okafor child had not shifted where it mattered. Three interviews now, and the story of the parents held perfectly—the flood winter, the drowned district, the dates—while everything around it flexed like a witness coached in truth rather than lies. And today, when Crane had asked where the parents were buried, the pause had been long enough to measure. Not the pause of someone who didn't know. The pause of someone deciding how much of the truth he could afford.

He had marked the page. He had requested the soldiers. And now, folding the maintenance logs back into their envelope, he admitted to himself the thing he had been circling for days: that this raid was not only procedure. The historians wanted the observatory's anomalous records surrendered as a demonstration, a lesson in the new arithmetic—one archive burned or bent so all the others understood their place. Crane wanted something smaller and stranger. He wanted to see the room where a man had written down thirty years of people the state insisted did not exist, including, apparently, an apprentice clockmaker born in the winter of the flood.

He wanted to know whether the record would argue back.

He put on his coat. Outside, four soldiers waited with lanterns shuttered dark, and the night smelled of thaw—the first breath of it, mud and iron underneath the frost—and Crane thought, with a sensation he declined to name, that the river sounded different tonight. Looser. As though something beneath the ice had turned over in its sleep.

* * *

They went up through the sleeping city in file, boots muffled with sacking, because the historians had been explicit: no spectacle, no crowd. Irregular persons were to be taken quietly and processed quietly, and anomalous records were to be removed before morning so that no one could stand in a public square and claim the state had anything to fear from paper. Fear of paper. Crane had written the phrase down once in his private notebook because it struck him as the truest thing anyone in the new regime had said aloud.

The observatory stood against the stars like a drawn pin. At its base, the door was supposed to be locked; it was not, and Crane noted this with professional dissatisfaction—a household that expected visitors, or wanted none to be able to prove they'd come. Inside, the stairwell smelled of onions and lamp oil and, faintly, of ink. A good smell. An honest smell. He hated how much he liked it.

The soldiers moved through the ground floor room by room. Kitchen, empty, embers still breathing in the stove. Pantry, stocked for six, not three—an inventory problem Crane logged mentally. The old stargazer's chamber under the eaves: empty cot, charts pinned everywhere, telescopes wrapped in oilcloth as if for a journey. The cook's narrow room behind the kitchen: bed made with military precision, a single wooden saint on the sill, gone.

"Empty," reported the sergeant, and there was disappointment in it, the animal letdown of men who had hoped at least for scuffling.

"Not empty," Crane said. "Evacuated. Which is worse." An empty building was nothing. An emptied building was a decision, and decisions had authors, and authors left things behind on purpose. He had raided enough households to know the difference between flight and strategy, and this—these neat beds, this banked fire, this unlocked door—was strategy.

"Sir," said one of the soldiers from the foot of the tower stair. "There's someone up there. Sitting. Waiting for us, near as I can tell."

Of course there was. Crane climbed the nine hundred steps at a clerk's pace, refusing to arrive winded, and came out into the clock room with its great pale drum of machinery standing silent in the lamplight, hands frozen at some dead hour, and found the keeper's daughter seated on a stool beside the mechanism as though she had been there all her life and intended to remain past the end of it.

She had a book open on her knee. She closed it—not hurriedly, not guiltily—with the composure of a woman setting a boundary rather than hiding evidence. Her hair was bound back. Her sleeves were rolled as if for work. She looked, Crane thought, exactly like what she was: the last person in the building, positioned precisely where she was most needed.

"Miss Aldous."

"Mr. Crane." She did not rise. "You'll want the ledger room. It's behind the dial. I'll save you the trouble of finding the latch—it sticks in cold weather." She stood, unhurried, and led him past the frozen escapement to the hidden door he had deduced but never seen, and pressed a spot in the casework, and the panel swung open on a small stone room lined with shelves.

Shelves of ledgers. Dozens of them. Spines labeled in faded ink by decade, by district, by category of person the government had preferred not to count. Crane stepped inside and felt the particular cold thrill he got only in archives—the sense of standing inside another man's discipline—and then, a heartbeat later, the colder thrill beneath it.

The spines were blank.

Not faded. Blank. He drew the first volume off the shelf and opened it to its middle and found page after page of ruled lines with nothing upon them, and the second volume, and the third. Empty. Every one. Ruled and bound and ready for writing, decades' worth, and not one word in any of them.

"You'll notice they're unused," Wren Aldous said from the doorway, and her voice was level as a spirit gauge. "My father bought blanks in bulk. It was cheaper. The ones you want—" she paused, delicately, the way a fencer offers an opening, "—were never kept here."

Crane set the empty ledger down with care, because his hands wanted otherwise. "Where are they, Miss Aldous?"

"Gone."

"When?"

"Before you decided to come. Before, I suspect, you finished your third interview of my apprentice." She folded her arms. "You've been asking one question per visit, Mr. Crane, very patiently, and each question has told me exactly which page you'd ask for next. My father taught me clocks. Clocks teach you this much: anyone who can read a mechanism can read a man. You are a very legible man."

The sergeant shifted in the doorway, uncertain whether this counted as resistance. Crane ignored him. He walked the little room slowly, touching shelf edges, checking the backs of drawers he knew would be empty, finding instead a fine gray dust of graphite and erasure, and the faint smell of fresh copies—ink and warm paper—though the copies themselves were nowhere. Under the last shelf, caught in a corner, one scrap: a corner torn from a page, carrying half a name in a hand he recognized from the maintenance logs, *...ren, b. —*, and nothing else. He put it in his coat pocket. Fragments were his trade. He had built careers on less.

"Your apprentice," he said, turning. "Hazel Okafor. Born in the winter of the flood, according to both your father's habit and the child's own account. Where is Hazel now?"

"I don't discuss my household with strangers."

"I'm not a stranger. I'm the census." He came closer, stopping at the threshold, giving her the distance that courtesy required and control required more. "Miss Aldous, let me describe your situation plainly, because I respect you too much to pretend otherwise. In three days, possibly fewer, the historians will convene across the river, and whatever exists tonight will either be delivered to them or destroyed by them, and they do not distinguish, in my experience, between the record that defies them and the keeper who kept it. You can be the woman who cooperated, or the woman who was found holding what they wanted. There is no third column on my form."

"There's always a third column," Wren said. "You just haven't ruled it yet."

For a moment—brief, unwelcome—Crane felt something adjacent to admiration. Then he did his job.

"The observatory is seized," he said formally, for the sergeant's benefit and the record, producing his warrant. "By order of the provisional census authority, the premises, mechanisms, and all documents pertaining to irregular persons are placed under seal pending inventory. Miss Aldous, you are detained as keeper of anomalous records pending questioning. Sergeant—"

"Sir." The soldier came to attention.

"—she goes gently. She is not to be handled roughly, not to be shouted at, and not to be separated from her own coat. Write that down. I want it in your report that I said it."

Something crossed Wren Aldous's face then, quickly, that Crane might have mistaken for relief if he had been a fool, and he was not a fool, and so he understood it instead as confirmation: she had planned for this. She had emptied her house, armed her door, blanked her shelves, and sat down on a stool to wait for him, and being arrested was the outcome she had budgeted for. Which meant the true ledger—the real one, the thirty years of names, the page with *Okafor, Hazel, born in the winter of the flood*—was already beyond his reach, moving through the city in the hands of someone he had interviewed three times and still could not make sit still on paper.

Which meant, further, that everything he did tonight was happening inside someone else's design, and Silas Crane, who had spent his life making other people legible, was playing a role in a plot whose author had read him like a maintenance log.

The soldiers bound her wrists with cord, gently, as instructed, and she submitted with the serenity of a woman keeping an appointment. At the doorway of the clock room she paused, looked back at the great stopped drum of the mechanism, and said—not to Crane, quite, but to the room:

"Wound it before you go, if you know how. It doesn't like sitting still."

Then she went down the nine hundred steps between two soldiers with her spine straight as a pendulum rod, and Crane stood alone in the clock room with his warrant and his scrap of torn page and the silence of a dead clock, and listened.

Below the tower, far off, muffled by walls and distance but unmistakable, the river groaned—a long, deep, structural sound, the sound of a thing deciding. The ice was going early. Sooner than his informants said, sooner than the almanacs said, sooner than anyone's schedule but one.

And somewhere between here and the council hall across the water, in the dark, a courier with a clockmaker's hands and a forged apprenticeship paper was already crossing the city with thirty years of names in a satchel built for carrying a life.

Crane took out his notebook, and by the light of the soldier's lantern, standing in the room where official time had been murdered, he began—for reasons he examined later and did not resolve—to write down everything he had seen, in full, in his own precise hand, leaving nothing out.

The ice cracked again. Louder. Closer.
