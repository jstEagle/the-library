## 29. Wren's Bargain

The soldiers came up the hill at the third hour past midnight, and Wren heard them before she saw them—the particular rhythm of boots that did not care who slept. Nine hundred steps below, the city lay dark under a sky scoured clean by wind, and the river ice groaned once, long and low, like something turning over in its sleep.

She had expected this night for eleven days. Ever since her father died, she had rehearsed it in the small hours: not the fear of it, but the choreography. Where Hazel would stand. Which satchel would go down which stairwell. What she would say to Crane when he came through the door with his notebook and his terrible courtesy.

What she had not rehearsed was how quiet the observatory would feel once it was emptied of everything true.

* * *

They had four hours' warning, in the end—not from any spy, but from Marisol's ferryman, who sent his boy running up the hill with a basket of late pears and a note folded inside the napkin like a confession: *Wagons at the bridge. Soldiers. They ask for the observatory.*

Marisol read it twice and set it flat on the kitchen table, weighting one corner with the salt dish, as if it might blow away into some other night where it said something kinder.

"They'll want the ledger room," Wren said.

"They'll find it," said Old Piotr, who was feeling his way along the wall toward his chair by the fire, his milky eyes aimed somewhere over all their heads. "That's the trouble with hiding things in plain sight. When they come for them, they come straight."

"Then we let them find it," Wren said, and heard her own voice steady in a way that surprised her. Her father had used that voice. She had inherited it along with everything else. "We let them find exactly what we want them to find."

Hazel stood at the edge of the lamplight with their satchel already half-packed—they had never fully unpacked since the first night, Wren had noticed; the bag lived by their hip the way other people's hands lived in their pockets—and their face had gone to that still place she had come to know, the clockmaker's face, where the fear went in and the arithmetic came out.

"The true ledger is three hundred pages," Hazel said. "It doesn't fit in a satchel."

"It fits in a coat," Wren said. "Yours, specifically. I've been thinking about your coat for a week."

* * *

The work took two hours, and it was the strangest work of Wren's life, because it was undoing. For eleven nights she and Hazel had copied—page after page, her father's cramped hand translated into Hazel's precise one, ink drying by the fire while Marisol kept them fed and Piotr sat up with them listening, listening, as though the names themselves made a sound only he could hear. Now they reversed it. The ledger room behind the clock face, which her father had filled over forty years with the evidence of uncounted lives, was stripped to what a searching eye would expect: shelves of maintenance logs, star charts, the official correspondence of an obedient institution—and, on the reading desk in the place of honor, wrapped in oilcloth against damp, a ledger.

It was real leather. It was real paper. It even held real names—forty of them, copied fair and complete, births and debts and deaths in her father's format, in her father's abbreviations, with his little marginal crosses marking people sold downstream. Forty names out of more than two thousand.

"That will hang him," Hazel said quietly, meaning Crane. "Forty names proves the room was real. Proves there were more."

"There were always more," Wren said. "That's the whole argument of my father's life." She smoothed the oilcloth over the false ledger one last time. "He'll believe it because he wants to believe it. A man who has spent months hunting a book does not want to be told the book is smaller than his ambition."

"And you?" Hazel asked. It was the question underneath everything they'd said all night, and now it surfaced, wearing the polite clothes of logistics. "You're staying."

"I'm the keeper," Wren said. "Keeper of anomalous records. They'll have a title for me by morning; the historians love titles." She made herself smile. "I'm also, tonight, the only person in this house whose arrest means nothing. Think it through. You're the apprentice who officially does not exist—if they take you, no one files a protest, because there's no record of you to protest with. Marisol has been invisible for twenty years; if they take her, twenty years of careful nothing comes apart in an interrogation room. But I'm Aldous's daughter. I hold the observatory charter. I am *legible*, Hazel. I'm legible all the way down. They can't make me disappear. They can only make me inconvenient."

"And inconvenient people—" Hazel began.

"—get rooms with barred windows and eventually get traded back," she finished. "Historians negotiate. That's what historians *are*. My father used to say the census counts bodies, but the archive counts arguments. Tonight we're giving them an argument."

Piotr spoke from his chair, without turning his head. "In my country, when the surveyors came, my mother gave them the false well and drank from the true one herself until they left." He smiled at the fire. "I never understood it until this year."

* * *

They went down separately. Hazel first, with the true ledger sewn into the lining of their coat in flat packets between canvas, so that the silhouette stayed a clockmaker's silhouette—a person shaped like tools and spare parts, walking down nine hundred steps with a lantern lit, unhurried, visible, invisible. Wren watched from the tower door until the small light reached the bottom and turned east, toward the river road, toward the ferryman's landing, toward the council hall where, if the warm wind held and the ice broke early, the victors would convene within days.

Then she came down herself, and made tea, and waited.

Marisol fussed uselessly around the kitchen, putting bread where bread did not belong, straightening things that were already straight. "You could come with us," she said, for the fourth time. "Down the coal stair. There's time—"

"There isn't, and it wouldn't help. If the keeper flees, they burn the hill looking, and they find the coal stair, and then they find whatever else we've hidden in this city." Wren turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "My father stayed in this room during the flood year because someone had to be able to say afterward what the water did. This is the same night, Marisol. Different water."

At half past four, the knocking came.

* * *

Silas Crane entered the observatory the way he entered everything: politely, thoroughly, taking off his mud-flecked boots at the door without being asked, which somehow was worse than if he'd tramped filth through the hall. Two soldiers stayed outside; two followed him in and stood with the uncomfortable stillness of men who had been told they were escorting a clerk and suspected they were attending something else.

"Miss Aldous." He inclined his head. Behind the lamplight his eyes moved over the hall—the cold hearth, the four teacups (a mistake, Marisol had realized too late, and Wren had watched her realize it), the astronomical charts pinned above Piotr's empty chair. "Forgive the hour. And forgive, I hope you'll accept, the irregularity of my commission. I'm instructed to secure certain materials in advance of the historians' arrival."

"You're instructed to seize my father's records," Wren said. "You may as well say it plainly. It's late, Mr. Crane, and I've found that plainness costs less at this hour."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth—not quite amusement. Respect, maybe. Or its counterfeit. "There exists," he said, opening his notebook, "credible indication that this institution maintained records beyond its mandate. Registries of persons. Entries concerning individuals absent from the lawful census." His pen hovered. "Is that indication correct?"

"It's correct," Wren said, and watched his pen move, and understood with a strange floating clarity that she was now doing the thing her whole life had been rehearsal for: standing in a room with a man who wrote things down, feeding the page on purpose. "Come. I'll show you the room myself. It's behind the clock face. My father built the door so well that the last two keepers of this observatory nearly lost it."

Nine hundred steps. Crane climbed them all without complaint, and Wren noted—he thought she didn't see, but she had been raised to notice men noticing—that he counted landings, counted doors, counted the worn places in the treads the way she might count teeth in a gear train. A man building a map of the tower in his head. Perhaps, she thought, for the last time, for someone else's benefit. Perhaps for his own escape route. The thought arrived uninvited and she put it away.

At the top, in the drum room beneath the great dial, she showed him the hidden catch—her father's door, her father's hinge—and stood aside as the soldiers pried it wide with their bayonets because Crane, meticulous even here, declined to touch the mechanism himself.

The lamp inside found the shelves. The logs. The desk. The oilcloth bundle.

Crane lifted the ledger as though lifting something that might still be warm. He opened it at random, then again, then a third time, and Wren stood in the doorway of her father's life's work and watched a professional read a forgery, and knew—in the way she knew a escapement was sick before it stopped—that he was going to believe it, and hated how much of her relief depended on that knowledge, and hated, underneath that, the small voice asking whether he *should*.

"Two thousand entries," Crane murmured. "Give or take."

"Give or take," Wren agreed, which was true, in its way.

He closed the book. He looked at her for a long moment, and the lamplight did something complicated with his face, and then he said, in a tone stripped suddenly of all its clerical varnish: "Where is the apprentice?"

"Asleep," Wren said. "In the city. They keep lodgings near the workshop of Master Idris Voss. Ask anyone. Ask your own lists—you have them listed twice, don't you? With different details?" She saw the flinch, feather-light, and pressed it. "Your lists are wrong, Mr. Crane. Your lists have been wrong from the beginning, because the truth about this city was never in them. Take the book. It's the truest thing your office has touched in forty years."

He held her gaze a moment longer. Then, quietly, folding the forgery into its oilcloth again: "Miss Aldous, I am required to take the keeper of anomalous records into custody pending the historians' review."

"I know," said Wren. "I've been keeping the room tidy for it."

* * *

They let her pack a bag—searched it first, of course, with the thoroughness she had counted on, finding nothing but clothing, her mother's ring, and a pencil. At the door of the observatory hall she stopped and asked Crane for one minute, and he granted it with a nod, and she used it standing in the middle of the room her father had filled with the sound of other people's lives, saying goodbye to chairs.

Marisol gripped her hands hard enough to hurt, and pressed something small and wrapped in cloth into her palm—a heel of bread, and inside the bread, Wren felt without unwrapping it, a thin folded square of paper. Marisol's own name, and date, and birth village, written in a cook's blunt hand. *So you have somebody with you,* Marisol whispered. *Somebody registered.*

Old Piotr kissed her forehead and said, in his own language, a sentence she didn't know but understood completely anyway.

And Hazel—Hazel was not there. Hazel was, by every account that mattered, asleep in lodgings near a sealed workshop across the city, carrying two thousand lives sewn into the lining of a coat, walking toward a frozen river that was already beginning to mutter in its sleep.

Wren let the soldiers take her arm at the door. Down the hill, in the grey before dawn, the wagons waited, and the ice on the river below gave another long groan, longer than before, and Crane walked beside her cart holding her father's forged ledger against his chest like a man carrying either evidence or doubt.

"You should know," he said, as the wheels began to turn, and his voice was low enough that only she heard it, "that I count everything. Doors, steps, entries, lies. Forty names in that book. In my experience, Miss Aldous, collections that matter are never complete. They're only ever surrendered early."

"Then you already know what I traded you," Wren said.

"Yes," said Silas Crane, and looked out at the whitening river. "The question that will occupy me, I think, for the rest of this ride—is what you're carrying away."

Behind them, up the dark hill, the master clock stood silent and stopped, waiting for hands it trusted; and ahead, down on the ice road, small in the first grey light, a figure with a clockmaker's satchel was already halfway across the river.
