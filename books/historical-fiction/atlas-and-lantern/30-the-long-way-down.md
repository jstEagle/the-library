## 30. The Long Way Down

The ledger weighed more than a clock.

Hazel had carried clocks before—carriage clocks in their brass shells, a regulator movement cradled against their chest like a sleeping child—and they had always known the weight of a thing by what it was for. But the ledger, wrapped in oilcloth and bound against their back with the satchel's strap crossed over one shoulder, had no weight that hands could measure. It weighed forty years. It weighed every name Wren's father had written in his careful, upright hand: the births no clerk recorded, the debts sold downstream, the dead buried under wrong names or none at all. It weighed *Okafor, Hazel. Born in the winter of the flood.*

It weighed Wren, standing at the top of the tower with her chin lifted and her father's keys in her fist, saying, *Go down. Don't look back at me. If you look back I'll lose my nerve, and I've never once lost my nerve.*

Hazel did not look back. It was the hardest thing they had ever done, harder than the ice road would be, harder than the nine hundred steps, because every step down was a step away from her and she was walking toward Crane and his soldiers and the room where she meant to stand in front of them like a wall made of one small stubborn woman.

*Count the steps,* Wren had said. *You counted them going up. Count them going down. A clockmaker counts. That's how you keep from running.*

So Hazel counted.

Nine hundred. Eight hundred ninety-nine. The spiral stairs were cut into the tower's stone heart, lit only by the arrow-slit windows that came every few turns, and through each slit Hazel could see the river—the whole gray breadth of it, locked under its winter lid, except that it wasn't locked anymore. The warm wind had come in the night like a thief with kind intentions, and now the ice wore a skin of meltwater that shone in the early light like spilled mercury. Out past the pilings, where the current ran hardest beneath the surface, the ice had begun to speak. Not crack—not yet—but speak: long groaning syllables that rose up through the stone of the tower itself, through Hazel's boots, through the bones of their feet, as if the river were reciting something it had been holding in its chest all winter.

Eight hundred fifty. Seven hundred eighty.

The ledger shifted against their spine with each turn of the stair. Hazel had rewrapped it three times before leaving, testing the knots the way Marisol taught them—*a knot should hold like a promise, not like a hope*—and still their hands wanted to keep checking. Under the oilcloth, wrapped separately in waxed paper, rode the true pages they had copied on the lamplit nights, and inside those pages, folded flat, the photograph of their parents that the old woman in the flooded district had pressed into Hazel's palm. And inside the ledger's own binding, sewn into the spine where only a bookbinder or a clockmaker would think to look, the page that mattered most: the original record of the Okafors, in Aldous's hand, with the dates and the debt and the small marginal note—*watch repaired gratis; good people; the state is wrong about them*—that Hazel had read so many times they could trace the letters with their eyes closed.

Seven hundred. Six hundred forty.

At six hundred twelve the stair passed the level of the clock room, and Hazel heard it—the great silent weight of the stopped mechanism overhead, the pendulum hanging like a held breath. They had wound nothing. That was the plan's first tooth: the master clock would stay stopped until it could be started in public, in front of the council and the city both, so that no one could claim afterward that time in Bellhaven had ever quietly resumed on the old regime's terms. True time and official time, geared together, or neither. Wren had said it like an equation. Hazel carried it like a vow.

Five hundred. Four hundred thirty.

Through a window slit on the four-hundredth turn, Hazel saw them: two figures on the observatory road below, moving fast, coats dark against the snowmelt mud. Soldiers' stride. One of them raised an arm and pointed—at the tower, at the road, at the ferry landing where the ice road began. Hazel's stomach dropped the way a weight drops when a tooth slips in the escapement—that sick free instant before the catch catches again.

They did not stop counting. Three hundred ninety-nine. Three hundred ninety-eight. But their legs understood now what their mind had only planned: this was not a walk. This was a race between their feet and other people's, run across a river that was deciding, hour by hour, whether to hold or to swallow.

Two hundred. One hundred ten.

At the bottom, the door. Old Piotr stood outside it in the blue shadow of the tower, half-blind eyes turned toward the river he could not properly see but somehow never missed, and when Hazel burst out he caught their arm with a grip far stronger than his years advertised.

"The ferryman's waiting at the third piling," Piotr said. "Not the landing—the landing's watched. He'll take you out along the shore ice to the willow bend, then cross where the current keeps it thick. He says the ice there is honest." A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "He says ice is like people. You can tell the liars by the sound."

"Marisol—"

"Gone an hour ago, the long way, with the duplicates. She carries her own life; you carry yours." He pushed Hazel gently toward the shore. "And I carry mine right here. Someone must be standing visibly at the front door looking harmless and confused. It is my great talent. Go."

Hazel went.

* * *

The shore ice took their weight with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and then held.

Hazel had grown up beside this river without ever once being allowed onto it—unregistered children did not skate, did not fish from the ice, did not exist loudly enough to be seen falling through it—and so the first twenty steps were pure terror, each footfall a question the ice answered grudgingly. Then the ferryman's voice came out of the willow haze ahead: "Left of the pressure ridge! Where the snow lies thin, the ice lies thin—walk on the white!"

Tomas. That was his name—Marisol had said it often enough, the ferryman who owed the observatory since the night on the bridge, the man whose sister's name lived in the ledger's middle pages among the debtors sold downstream. He was a broad, weathered man in a coat the color of wet rope, and he did not greet Hazel or ask after Wren. He simply looked once at the bundle on their back, nodded as if confirming a cargo manifest, and turned upstream.

"Keep pace with me and step where I step," he said. "The river's talking this morning. When it talks, you listen with your feet."

They went along the shore where the ice lay over shallows, thick and sure, the willows whipping in the warm wind, and the whole time the sounds came out of the channel behind them and to their left—the deep-water voice of the river, groaning and sighing, and now and then a sharper report, a *crack* that leapt away across the plain like a whip being tested. Each crack, Tomas paused, head cocked, reading it.

"That one's far," he'd say. Or: "That one's the ridge settling. Fine." Once, closer: "That one we remember." And he altered their line inward, toward the bank, without hurry, as though adjusting a mechanism by a single tooth.

Hazel found, to their astonishment, that the counting had not stopped. It had simply changed units. Steps became cracks; cracks became breaths. *A clockmaker counts,* Wren had said, and here was the truth under it: counting was not a trick for calm. Counting was how you stayed in the present tense when the present tense was trying to kill you. Every number was a tooth in the escapement, catching the fall, releasing it, catching it again.

Halfway to the willow bend, Tomas stopped so abruptly Hazel walked into his back.

Ahead, maybe three hundred yards downstream, two figures had come out onto the ice from the town landing. They moved badly—soldiers, unlearned in ice, walking like men on a ship's deck—and one of them shouted something the wind shredded. But the direction of the shout, and the pointing arm, needed no words.

"They saw us leave the tower," Hazel breathed.

"They saw someone," Tomas said. "On this river, that's enough." He stood a moment, listening, head tilted, and Hazel realized he was doing what Piotr did with stars and Hazel did with gears: reading the instrument. The river was an instrument. It had a mechanism, currents and depths and thicknesses, and it was even now changing its own settings.

"We can't outrun them to the bridge road," Tomas said. "But we don't need to." He turned and looked at Hazel, and for the first time his face showed something besides competence—a fierce, almost tender resolve. "There's a place where the current comes close under the ice, near the old pier pilings. It sings this time of year. Anyone who doesn't know it walks over it and goes through. We go around it, wide, and let them find it their own way."

"You want to lead them onto the river's trap."

"I want," Tomas said, "to introduce them to my sister's keeper." He touched the wind, as if hailing it. "Come. Wide around. And whatever you hear behind us—do not turn around. Turning around on ice is how the river collects its toll."

They went wide. Behind them, fainter now, the soldiers' voices carried across the flat white, calling to each other, correcting course, following the smooth line that any sensible man would follow, the straight line, the short line, the line that led over the place where the current sang.

Hazel heard the ice open.

It was not like the other cracks. Those had been percussive, sharp, finished. This was a long tearing note, rising, and then a second note underneath it, deeper, and then a sound like the whole river inhaling. Hazel's body obeyed the old workshop reflex—they flinched, braced, turned their shoulders away—and Tomas's hand closed hard on their arm.

"Don't look."

But the shouting told the story anyway. Two voices, then one, then a splashing chaos of both, and then—one voice only, screaming the other's name, screaming for rope, for help, for the shore. The scream did not last long. The river took it the way it took everything, without malice and without mercy, and afterward there was only the wind and the groaning and the small human voice, farther off now, retreating toward land, sobbing or calling or both.

Hazel stood shaking on the honest ice, the ledger heavy against their back, and discovered they could not decide what they felt. Horror, yes. Relief, terrible and immediate. And under both, a cold clear thought that felt like it belonged to someone older: *the river has been keeping records too.* Everything the census burned, everything the historians would rewrite, the river kept. It had taken the debtors' barges downstream. It had taken Hazel's parents in the flood winter. And today it had taken a soldier of the clean census, and given back one witness, weeping, who would carry the story whether he wanted to or not.

"Move," Tomas said softly. "Grief later. Distance now."

* * *

They came off the ice at the willow bend on the far shore, where the ground rose into a tangle of bare trees and frozen reeds, and from there Tomas led them a mile inland along a drainage path only he seemed to know, hard-going, the meltwater soaking Hazel's boots, the ledger's straps cutting into their shoulder until the pain became part of the rhythm, one more count in the sequence. Twice they heard hoofbeats on some distant road and flattened in the reeds, Hazel's cheek against frozen mud, breathing through their mouth, feeling the ledger's corners press into their spine like fingers checking that it was still there.

Once, lying in the reeds, Hazel thought of Wren standing in the ledger room with her father's keys, arranging herself to be found, and their chest hurt so sharply they had to press a fist against it. *Don't look back.* They hadn't. They were carrying her forward instead, which was the only way she had allowed herself to be carried.

Near midday the drainage path joined a cart track, and the cart track joined the world: a hamlet, smoke from chimneys, a dog barking. People stepped out of doorways to watch them pass, and Hazel braced for the old reflex—the shrinking, the becoming-no-one—but Tomas walked straight-backed down the lane and called greetings by name, and one by one the doors gave up their watchers, and an old man pressed a heel of bread into Hazel's hand without stopping, and a woman at a well said, "That's the clockmaker, then? The one from the tower?"—not a question really, more a confirmation, the way you confirm a fact already agreed upon.

Word had gone ahead. Marisol's web, spun neighbor to neighbor all winter, which neighbors could be trusted and which could only be fed false comfort. Hazel walked through it feeling, for the first time in their life, *expected.*

By late afternoon they could see the provisional council's hall on its low hill above the ferry town—a squat stone customhouse requisitioned overnight, flags of no design yet flying from its eaves, carts and armed men and ordinary people milling at its doors in the disorder of a government still inventing itself. The ice on the river here was black and rotten at the edges, and the air smelled of water. Spring was coming up the valley like an army.

At the foot of the hill, Tomas stopped.

"I go back," he said. "Wren's bargain buys you the road, not me the seat at the table. And there's a soldier on our side of the river who owes the river a drowning, and I mean to be sure he tells the tale where it does harm—to Crane." He gripped Hazel's shoulder, hard, once. "You're the cargo and the courier both, clockmaker. Walk in like you belong, because you do. And when they ask your name—" his eyes went to the bundle on Hazel's back, and something in his face broke open, just for a moment, grief and gratitude in the same expression— "say it loud. My sister waited forty years in those pages. Don't whisper her out."

Then he was gone, back down toward the ice road, a broad figure in a rope-colored coat, and Hazel stood alone at the bottom of the hill with the ledger on their back and the council hall above them, its doors open, its windows full of silhouettes arguing the shape of a new country.

Hazel checked the straps one last time. Knots like promises. They thought of the nine hundred steps, counted and survived. They thought of the ice, which had spoken and been answered. They thought of their mother's engraving inside the watch case, the name they had never known was theirs, and they put their hand flat against the oilcloth over the ledger's spine, the way you steady a pendulum before you set it swinging.

Then they climbed the hill, and pushed open the doors of the provisional council, and said, in a voice that cracked on the first word and steadied on the rest:

"My name is Hazel Okafor, and I have brought you the record of everyone your papers say does not exist."

The hall went quiet. At the far end, behind a trestle table strewn with maps, a woman in a historian's gray coat rose slowly to her feet—and beside her, flanked by two of his own soldiers, his face drained of all its careful color, stood Silas Crane.
