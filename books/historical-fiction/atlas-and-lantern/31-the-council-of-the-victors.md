## 31. The Council of the Victors

The council hall had been a customs house in the old regime's days, and it still smelled like it—tar, wet rope, and the ghost of stamped paper. Now the counting desks had been shoved against the walls and long tables dragged to the center, and men and women who three weeks ago had been printers, ferrymen, and one very loud fishwife sat behind them with the careful, terrified posture of people holding something that could still be taken away.

Hazel stood at the foot of the room with the ledger in both arms.

They had dressed in their good coat, which was to say their only coat, brushed clean of tower dust. The satchel had come too, because Hazel could not remember how to walk into a room without it, but it hung empty-feeling on their shoulder, the tools left behind at the observatory, and the absence of that familiar weight made them feel like a clock with the pendulum removed. All the works present. Nothing to keep them steady.

The ledger weighed eleven pounds. Hazel knew because they had weighed it on Marisol's flour scale the night before, twice, the way you check a thing when you are afraid it will dissolve.

"Hazel Okafor." The woman at the center table—Councilor Adeyemi, who had run the ferry before the uprising and now ran, apparently, everything—consulted a paper. "You're listed as witness for the morning session. You have the floor until the bell."

*Listed.* Hazel almost smiled at that. Somewhere in this building there was a page with their name on it, written by a clerk who had never asked whether the name was permitted. It was the first time in their life the word had appeared on official paper, and it had happened by accident, the way most true things did.

"I have a record," Hazel said. Their voice came out lower than they meant it to, and they made themselves lift it. "A record the government never kept. Births. Deaths. Debts. People who were sold downstream. People who died in the flood and were never counted. I'm going to read you some of the names, and then I'm going to tell you what the master clock at the observatory says about them, and then you can decide what kind of city you're building."

A murmur went through the benches. At the side of the room, apart from the council, sat the historians—five of them in gray traveling coats, arrived ahead of the ice with their soldiers and their catalog cases, the advance party of a memory not yet written. Their leader was a thin man named Voss with ink-stained fingers and a face like a closed ledger himself. He had watched Hazel enter the hall with the expression of a man appraising a shipment.

Councilor Adeyemi nodded. "Begin."

Hazel set the ledger on the table before them. The leather cover was cracked at the corners, dark with forty years of the tower's lamp smoke, and the brass clasp Wren's father had fitted to it was tarnished green. It looked, Hazel thought, like exactly what it was: a thing made by hand, kept by hand, carried down nine hundred steps by hand at four in the morning while the river groaned like a living animal underneath.

They opened it to the first page.

"Keeper's note," Hazel read, "in the hand of Tomas Aldous, first of January, forty-one years past: *This book is not against the government. It is against the government's forgetting. The state counts what it can tax. I count what it cannot afford to lose.*"

* * *

They had rehearsed this on the tower stairs, Wren counting the steps aloud behind them like a metronome, until Hazel could read the hard pages without their voice breaking. It had seemed like enough. It was not enough. Nothing was enough for the moment when Hazel turned to the entry dated the winter of the flood and read, in a stranger's careful hand:

"Okafor, Adaeze. Born in the spring of the bridge works, daughter of the clockmaker Idris's neighbor, registered to no one because the registrar's office refused the marriage. Okafor, Chidi. Her husband. Boatman. Died in the flood, age thirty-one, unrecorded. Adaeze died the same winter. Their child survived. *Okafor, Hazel. Born in the winter of the flood.*"

Hazel stopped. They had not stopped in rehearsal. On the stairs, with Wren's voice counting, they had read it clean as a gear train. But here the words came out of their mouth and did not seem to end—they hung in the cold air of the customs hall, and Hazel heard their own name spoken aloud in a public room for the first time since they were six years old, and it was being spoken as a fact. A fact with a date. A fact with witnesses.

The hall was very quiet. Somewhere a soldier shifted his boots.

"I am that child," Hazel said. "I was never entered in any census. I have no paper but a forged apprenticeship, which I burned last night. Everything I am, the government of this city does not know. And yet here it is." They put their hand flat on the page. "Written down before I could write. By a man I never met, who thought I was worth ink."

* * *

The historians let Hazel read for nearly an hour before they moved.

It was well done, Hazel had to admit that. Voss did not interrupt, did not sneer. He waited until Hazel had read the debts—the boatmen bound to the granary owners at rates no free man could work off, page after page of them, each with a date and a name and, in Aldous's hand, small marginal notes: *sold downstream, March; sold downstream, April*—and then he rose, unhurried, and addressed the council as if Hazel were furniture.

"Councilors. What you are hearing is a private document of unknown provenance, kept in secret, by a man now deceased, in circumstances we cannot verify. It is touching. It may even be substantially accurate. But a record's value is in its *verification*—its chain of custody, its corroboration by the civil rolls, its freedom from the passions of those who benefit from it." He turned, and his eyes were not unkind, which was somehow worse. "This young person has an obvious interest in the outcome. The document cannot be authenticated. And I would remind the council that the transition requires continuity. A city that repudiates its own records repudiates its own debts, its own property lines, its own treaties. We are here to build the new memory *on* the old one, not in place of it."

"Continuity," said a voice from the benches. Old Piotr had come down from the tower for this, half-blind, holding Marisol's arm, and his voice carried the way a man's voice carries when he has spent forty years calling numbers across a dark room to no one. "You mean the granary debts. You mean the property of the men who ran the census wagons."

"I mean," Voss said, with great patience, "that a record which exists only because one man wrote it down is a memoir, not an archive."

"Then let it be tested," Hazel said.

They had not planned to say it. It came up out of them the way the first strike of a wound clock comes—built into the mechanism, waiting only for the release. Hazel heard their own voice go steady, go *clockmaker's* steady, the voice they used for naming a flaw to a client who did not want to hear it.

"You say it can't be authenticated. I say it authenticates itself, if you know how to read it. Master Aldous was not only a keeper. He was a horologist. He built the master clock's second dial—the one your maintenance logs call an anomaly and never once inspected. The official mechanism and the true mechanism are geared together. The clock cannot run true unless the hidden dial runs, and the hidden dial cannot run unless the official one does. He made the city's official time *depend* on the record of the people the city erased." Hazel turned the ledger around and lifted it so the council could see the flyleaf, where Aldous had drawn the gear train in his own hand, dated and signed. "And the ledger's dates are keyed to the clock. Every entry is timed to the strike. You can take this book to any horologist in Bellhaven—take it to Idris, if he's fit to hold a tool—and the strike intervals will match the maintenance logs of the master clock for forty years, entry for entry. Nobody could forge that. Not without rebuilding the clock. Not without being the clock."

Silence. Then the fishwife-councilor, whose name was Bren and who had a voice like a dropped anchor, said: "Is that true? Can it be checked?"

"It can be checked," Hazel said. "That's what a record *is*. It's a thing that can be checked. That's the whole difference between a record and a rumor. The historians' memory can't be checked, because it isn't written yet. This one has been waiting forty years for someone to look."

* * *

The council wavered. Hazel watched it waver, the way you watch a balance wheel hesitate between beats, and understood that they were watching the actual mechanism of the new city—nothing more exalted than that, just a wheel deciding which way to fall.

Councilor Adeyemi turned the ledger's flyleaf toward the man beside her, a former schoolteacher who kept touching his collar as though it still had a census clerk's pin in it. The schoolteacher read the gear diagram for a long time. Voss's colleagues passed him notes. At the side of the room, the soldiers had not moved, but Hazel had learned in the tower to read mechanisms by their sound, and the sound of the hall had changed—the murmur was no longer *what is this*, it was *what if this is true*, and that was a different machine entirely.

"The document raises questions of debt," said the schoolteacher at last, carefully. "Questions of restitution. Questions we cannot settle this morning."

"Questions," said Bren, "that your clean census would have settled by leaving them out."

"We haven't certified any census yet," Adeyemi said, and something in the room went very still, because everyone in it knew what that sentence was holding open. "Councilor Voss. Your delegation requested certification of the civil rolls before the ice breaks. This witness has put forward a document that contradicts those rolls on—" she looked down at the ledger, at the pages Hazel had read, at the pages Hazel had not had time to read— "on what looks like several hundred persons. I can't certify a record and its refutation in the same hour."

"Then don't certify either," Voss said smoothly. "Defer. Convene a commission. In the spring—"

"The ice breaks in three days," said Piotr, from the benches, in the tone of a man reporting a star. "I've watched this river forty years. The wind came around south last night. You'll have your spring when the river says so, and not a day before, and neither will your commission."

Voss's composure did not crack, but Hazel, watching, saw his hands go quiet on the table—the stillness of a man recalculating. And Hazel understood, with a horologist's cold clarity, what was actually happening in this room: it was not an argument about truth. It was an argument about *time*. The historians needed the old record sealed before the ice broke, because after the ice broke the city would fill with boats and witnesses and the victors' own hungry ranks, and sealed records are easy to keep and hard to open. The ledger had come down nine hundred steps and across the cracking river for exactly the same reason. Both sides were racing a thaw.

"There is one more thing," Hazel said.

Adeyemi looked up.

"The census that was being compiled against us—the clean census, the one with the irregular persons struck from it. It exists. It was nearly finished. The man who built it is Silas Crane, and he is in this city, and I will tell you something about him that I never thought I'd say." Hazel's mouth was dry. "He is the only person alive who has read both records. His and ours. He has spent months verifying us without knowing it—every name he hunted, every answer that shifted between visits, he checked and rechecked until he knew the unregistered people of this city better than they know themselves. If you want this ledger tested, councilors, you could not buy a better examiner. He is meticulous. He is honest in his method, if not in his purpose. And three days ago he asked me where my parents were buried, and wrote down my answer, and my answer was the truth, and I think—I think it cost him something to write it down."

"Hazel." Wren's voice, low, from the end of the bench where the observatory household sat. Not a warning. A question: *are you sure?*

Hazel was not sure. That was the truth of it. They were offering the wolf as a witness because the wolf was the only one who had counted the sheep honestly, and it might destroy them all, and it was still, Hazel believed, the only way to make the record argue back with a voice even the historians would have to answer. A record verified only by its friends was a memoir. Voss had said so. Fine. Then let it be verified by its enemy.

"Crane's certification is not yet signed," Hazel said to the council. "He was ordered to deliver a clean census before the ice breaks. He hasn't. I'm told he sat up all last night with the Okafor page in front of him. I don't know what he'll do. Nobody knows what he'll do. But I know that he knows the difference between a record and a forgery, because he built one, and a man who builds a forgery knows exactly what weight a true thing has." Hazel closed the ledger. The sound of it, the soft clap of forty years of names, went through the hall like a small bell. "Send for him. Make him certify one page—mine—in front of you all. If he certifies the census instead, then you'll know what the historians' memory is worth. If he certifies the ledger—"

Hazel stopped. The next words were the ones they had carried down the tower stairs and across the ice road, through the dark, with the river groaning under them, and saying them aloud in this room felt like winding a spring past its safe limit.

"—then the record wins. Not me. Not the observatory. The record. Because a city that can be made to check its own books is a city that can be kept. And keeping is the whole trade. That's what a clock is. That's what a ledger is. That's what I am, now, if you'll have me."

* * *

The council did not decide in the hall. Hazel had not expected them to. Adeyemi said the matter would be taken up at the afternoon session, and the bell rang, and the benches emptied like a tide going out, and the historians gathered their catalog cases and withdrew in their gray coats, Voss last, pausing beside Hazel's table just long enough to look down at the ledger with something that was not quite contempt and not quite hunger.

"Chain of custody," he said quietly. "It will come to that, in the end. Everything does. Who held it, and what they held it *for*."

"Forty years of dead keepers," Hazel said, "and one apprentice. Hold it for the names. That's the whole chain."

Voss looked at Hazel for a moment longer, and then, to Hazel's astonishment, inclined his head a fraction—the acknowledgment of one professional to another—and was gone.

Marisol found Hazel's elbow. Wren stood close, her father's ledger between them, her knuckles white on the bench edge. "You did it," she said. "You made them stop."

"No," Hazel said. Their legs had begun, now that it was over, to inform them what they had asked of them all morning; they sat down heavily on the bench. "I made them *waver*. That's not the same thing. A wheel that wavers can still fall either way." They looked up at Wren, and past her at the tall windows, where the light had come up thin and strange and warm, a south wind's light, the light of a thaw. "It falls when Crane certifies. That's the escapement of the whole thing. One man, one page, one signature. And we've just spent the morning telling the whole council to send for him."

Wren was quiet a moment. "You trust him?"

"No," Hazel said. "I trust what he is. There's a difference. A balance wheel doesn't have to love the spring to keep the time."

Outside, faint under the council bell, under the noise of the city waking, they could both hear it: the river, groaning, cracking, counting down.
