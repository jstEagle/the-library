## 34. Names on the Record

The provisional council's hall smelled of wet plaster and lamp oil, of men who had slept in their coats for a week, of ink—so much ink, as though the whole building were a wound that had been dressed in it. Hazel stood at the edge of the chamber with the ledger against their chest, both arms wrapped around it the way one holds a sleeping child in a crowd, and waited to be told what to do.

No one told them. That was the first surprise. For three days the hall had been a churn of delegates and clerks and soldiers with red armbands, men and women arguing over the shape of a city that no longer had a government, and every one of them, in the end, had looked at Hazel Okafor and stepped aside.

Wren walked beside them, her wrists still ringed with the pale marks of the census manacles, her chin high in a way Hazel had learned to read as fear worn like armor. Marisol had come down from the observatory with bread wrapped in a cloth, because Marisol's answer to history was always bread. Old Piotr had insisted on coming, though the walk had cost him; he leaned on Hazel's shoulder now, his clouded eye aimed somewhere past the ceiling, past the roof, toward whatever stars were waiting above the morning's smoke.

"The table," Wren said quietly. "There. By the window. They've left it for you."

They had. A plain table of scarred oak, a chair, a lamp already trimmed and lit, a pot of ink and three pens laid out like surgical instruments. Someone—Wren would not say who, but Hazel suspected a clerk with tired eyes who had read too many pages of Crane's confiscated census—had understood that what was about to happen in this corner of the hall was not decoration. It was infrastructure.

Hazel set the ledger down on the oak.

The sound it made was small. A book, meeting wood. But Hazel felt it travel up through their palms the way they felt the strike of the master clock through the tower's stones, and they saw Piotr feel it too, the old man's head tilting like a bird's.

"Open it anywhere," Hazel said, mostly to themselves. "It falls open to the flood winter. It's been opened there so many times."

They opened it there.

The pages were the color of weak tea. Aldous's hand marched down them in a script so even it might have been ruled by machine: *Born, the 14th of the flood winter, to Adaeze Okafor and Tomas Okafor, in the drowned district, in the house of the ferryman's sister. A girl, they said. Or a boy. The midwife would not say, and the mother laughed and said, then write it true: a child.* And beneath, in the same patient hand: *Registered nowhere. Counted here.*

Hazel had read that entry a hundred times since the night they'd found the ledger room. They had read it by lamplight, by candlelight, by the grey light that came through the clock's dial before dawn. They had read it aloud once, alone, and had to sit down afterward on the keeper's stool with their head between their knees. But they had never read it in a room where it *mattered*—where the words could do something besides keep a dead man's kindness alive.

Now the room was full of witnesses.

* * *

The council had convened at the ninth hour, and it was the eleventh now, and the argument over the historians' proposed census had been going for most of it. Hazel heard it the way one hears weather through a wall—rises and falls, a voice like oiled hinges (the historians' chief, a man named Verel whose smile Hazel had already learned to distrust on sight), the flat, stubborn voice of the ferryman's delegate, the sharp interruptions of a woman from the dyers' guild who had lost two sons to the old regime's presses and trusted nothing printed by anyone.

The historians had a proposal, and it was a reasonable one, and that was the horror of it. The city's records were in disarray. The old regime's rolls were corrupted by a decade of falsification. Therefore, Verel argued, a new census must be compiled—quickly, cleanly, by professionals—and the anomalous documents circulating in the city (he did not say *ledger*; he said *anomalous documents* the way a man says *vermin*) should be surrendered for expert evaluation before anything was entered into the permanent record.

Evaluation. Hazel turned the word over. It meant a locked room. It meant pages that went in and never came out. It meant Wren's father's forty years of ink dissolving into some archive where the inconvenient entries could be weighed against the convenient ones and found wanting.

The council wavered. Hazel could see it wavering, the way one sees a scale tremble before it tips. The historians were funded. The historians were credentialed. The historians had already begun calling the uprising *the Restoration*, as though something were being restored to the people who had never had it taken from them.

"Delegate," the chairwoman said—she was the dyer, her name was Corvane, and her voice had a rasp like a file on iron—"you've asked this council to accept a record no one has seen. You've asked them to accept a book kept in secret by a dead man. Why should the provisional government of Bellhaven write its first act in a stranger's hand?"

The hall went quiet in the particular way halls go quiet when a question is honest.

Hazel stood up.

It was not a decision, quite. It was more like the escapement releasing—something held, held, held, and then let go at the exact instant the mechanism required it. Hazel's knees were unsteady. Their hands were not. Their hands, built for fine work, built for balance wheels and pallet stones and the thousandth of an inch, were perfectly steady as they lifted the ledger from the table and carried it to the front of the chamber, and Wren fell into step beside them, and Piotr's hand found Hazel's elbow, and the three of them crossed the floor of the victors' council like people crossing ice that they had tested and trusted.

"Because," Hazel said, and their voice came out lower than they intended, so they said it again, louder, into the hush. "Because I am the stranger. And it is my hand. And I am going to write in it in front of you, now, so that you can watch what a person looks like when they decide to exist."

* * *

They had rehearsed none of it. There had been no time, and besides, Wren had said the night before, turning her cup in her fingers in Marisol's kitchen, you can't rehearse a testimony. You can only refuse to lie in it.

Hazel set the ledger on the council's table, beside the historians' neat stacks of proposals, and opened it to the flood winter, and read the entry aloud.

*Okafor, Hazel. Born in the winter of the flood.*

"Adaeze Okafor," Hazel said. "My mother. She came upriver on the barges in the year of the toll riots, unregistered, because the toll registers did not take river people. She worked the dye vats for three years and her hands were blue at the knuckles until the day she died. Tomas Okafor. My father. He was a clockmaker's journeyman—no, that's not true, that's the softened version. He was a clockmaker. Idris Vane took his best work and put his own name under it, and my father let him, because a name on a workbench was worth more than a name on a register, in those years. They drowned in the flood winter. The city's record of them is this: nothing. The city's record of me is this: nothing. I have been nothing for nineteen years, and I am very tired of it, and I have brought the book that says otherwise."

Verel's voice, silk over gravel: "A book kept in secret, by an unknown hand, cannot be verified. Any forger could—"

"Then verify it," Hazel said. "That is what I am asking. Not that you believe me. That you *check*. Send for the midwife's daughter, who lives on Tanner's Row and remembers her mother speaking of the birth. Send for the ferryman, whose sister's house they drowned in front of. Send for the parish sexton who was paid to bury two unregistered bodies in the paupers' strip and kept his own list, because sextons keep lists, it is the one disease all keepers share. Every name in this book has a witness. That is what a ledger *is*. It is not paper. It is a chain of witnesses, bound in leather."

Hazel turned pages. The hall had gone very still. Even Corvane's file-voice had stopped.

"Here. Marisol Ferreira, cook, born in the salt quarter, erased from the rolls in the year of the bread levy because her mother could not pay and the clerk sold the family's registration to a man who wanted the name for his own son. She has fed this city for twenty years from a kitchen it officially does not know exists. Here—Piotr Brandt, astronomer, whose observations this state declared impossible because they contradicted the almanac the state sold. Forty years of sky, written down every night by a man the sky never once contradicted. Here—" and here Hazel's voice did something they had not planned, something that cracked and then steadied, like a bridge taking weight—"here is Idris Vane, master clockmaker, who sold my parents' names to the census to keep his workshop, and who has spent every night since the ice broke copying pages in a hand that shakes, because he says a debt you don't write down is a debt you get to keep lying about. He is in this book. I put him in it myself. A ledger that only holds the people you love is not a ledger. It's a diary."

Someone in the gallery laughed—a short, startled sound, quickly swallowed—and Hazel saw Corvane almost smile.

"And here," Hazel said, "is Wren Aldous, who was arrested as the keeper of anomalous records, which is the finest charge I have ever heard spoken aloud, and who spent four days in a census cell rather than tell a man named Crane where the true pages were. And here—" Hazel's finger came to rest on the entry they had read a hundred times, and they found, to their surprise, that their eyes were burning and their voice was level, "is me."

* * *

They had not meant to write yet. The plan—such as the plan had been, assembled in fragments over the copying nights—was testimony first, entry after, if the council permitted. But Corvane looked at Hazel across the table for a long moment, this rasping woman with ink stains to her elbows and dead sons, and said, "The book is here. The witnesses can be summoned. Until they come, the record is a claim. So make the claim, clockmaker. Write it. We will watch."

A clerk brought the table. Another brought the ink. The historians' delegation murmured among themselves, and Verel's face wore the expression of a man watching his argument being dismantled with its own tools.

Hazel uncapped the ink and chose the middle pen—the one with the good nib, the one Wren had cut for them from a raven's quill the second week of the copying nights, saying, *your hand is too fine for a bought pen; you press like you're afraid the page will argue back.*

The new pages were at the back of the ledger, past Aldous's last entry, past the blank years. They were the same paper—Wren had found the old stock in her father's chest, heavy and cream-colored, milled in a paper house that no longer existed, which meant the pages themselves were witnesses of a kind—and Hazel had ruled them the night before by lamplight, using the same spacing Aldous had used, measuring it with the dividers from their satchel because some inheritances are taken with instruments.

They wrote the Okafor names first.

*Adaeze Okafor. Born on the river, year of the toll riots. Died in the flood winter, in the drowned district, in the house of the ferryman's sister. A dyer. Her hands were blue at the knuckles.*

*Tomas Okafor. Born in the low town, son of a river mother and a low-town father, registered as neither. A clockmaker. His work keeps time in three towers under other men's names, and in this book under his own.*

Hazel's pen did not shake. Hazel noticed this the way one notices a held breath—not the holding, only the release. Around them the hall was silent except for the scratch of the nib, and Hazel understood, with a clarity that felt almost cold, that this was the sound the whole uprising had been making for a week without knowing it: a nib on paper, a name going down where it could be found.

*Marisol Ferreira,* they wrote. *Cook. Keeper of the observatory kitchen. Twenty years unregistered, twenty years feeding the registered. Counted here.*

*Piotr Brandt. Astronomer. His observations were declared impossible; the sky has been consulted and declines to agree. Counted here.*

*Idris Vane. Master clockmaker. He sold two names to save his own, and he has paid for them with forty nights of copying, and the debt is entered here so that no one—himself least of all—may pretend it was never owed. Counted here. Judged here. Kept here.*

Then Hazel turned to the next line, and the pen stopped.

Because the next line was their own.

It should have been easy. It was the easiest entry in the book—a name, a birth, a parentage, all of it already written once in a dead man's hand. All Hazel had to do was copy it. But their hand hovered over the cream-colored page, and the hall waited, and Hazel understood suddenly why Aldous's script had been so even, why the old keeper had ruled his lines and spaced his entries and never hurried: because the person being written down could not afford a trembling line. Because a record kept for the erased must be *legible to the erased*, must say, in its very hand, *you are worth care*.

Hazel thought of their mother's laugh, which they did not remember but which the midwife's daughter had described to them on Tanner's Row three days ago—*she laughed like the flood was a joke she intended to win*—and Hazel pressed the nib to the page.

*Okafor, Hazel. Born in the winter of the flood, in the drowned district, in the house of the ferryman's sister. Child of Adaeze and Tomas Okafor. Apprentice clockmaker. Keeper of this ledger, by inheritance and by choice. Registered nowhere, until this line. Counted here.*

They signed it. Their own name, in their own hand: the letters they had practiced in secret as a child on the backs of spoiled work orders, the letters they had written on forged papers with their heart hammering, the letters that had never once, in nineteen years, been written anywhere that a fire or a clerk or a historian could not simply erase.

The nib lifted. The ink gleamed, wet and black and utterly irreversible.

* * *

What happened next happened quickly, and Hazel would spend years remembering it in fragments.

Corvane read the entries aloud, all of them, her file-voice giving each name the flat dignity of a verdict. The ferryman's delegate stood and said, "I can witness the flood winter. My sister's house. I was eleven." A woman from the gallery—Hazel did not know her, would learn later she was a laundress from the salt quarter—stood and said, "I can witness Marisol Ferreira. She fed my children through the bread levy." And then, like ice going out, witness after witness, names rising from the benches to answer the names on the page, until Corvane raised her hand and said, "Enough. The record stands pending verification, and the verification will begin today, and the historians' proposed census is tabled until this ledger has been checked entry by entry."

Verel rose, smooth as oil. "Delegate Corvane. The council cannot found a state on a single book. Books burn. Books are lost. What happens to your new record if the keeper—"

"The keeper," Hazel said, "is standing in front of you. And there are four copies of this book in clock cases in four towns upriver, and I will tell you where none of them are, and I will wind the clock that keeps this city's time tomorrow morning, and if you want the record destroyed, Delegate Verel, you will have to do it the old way—by making so many people liars that the lie becomes the truth. And I think you'll find, if you try, that this city has just stopped agreeing to that."

The hall did not cheer. Hazel had expected—feared—cheering. Instead something quieter happened, something that stayed with them longer: the delegates simply began to write. Corvane dictated; clerks bent; the ledger's names went into the council's minutes, and then into a second copy, and then a third, and Hazel watched their parents' names travel outward across the room in a dozen different hands, multiplying, becoming impossible to gather up again.

"There," Wren said softly at Hazel's shoulder. Her voice was rough. "Now it's not a secret. Now it's just true."

Hazel looked down at the ledger, open on the council's table, surrounded by the machinery of a government being born. The ink on their own name had dried to the color of old iron.

* * *

They walked home in the late afternoon, the four of them, through streets that smelled of thaw. The ice on the river was going dark and rotten at the edges; the ferrymen had begun measuring it with poles and shaking their heads. Word had run ahead of them, as word does, and people came out of doorways to look—not at Hazel, Hazel noticed, but at the ledger under their arm, the way sailors look at a lighthouse they'd only ever heard described.

At the foot of the observatory hill, Idris Vane was waiting. He stood with his cap in his hands, and when he saw the ledger he did not come closer; he only bowed his head, the bow of a man before an altar he no longer felt entitled to approach.

"Is it done?" he asked.

"It's done. You're in it. All of it."

He nodded, twice, and his eyes were wet, and he said, "Thank you," and Hazel found that they could not say *you're welcome*, and did not try, and Idris nodded again as though that silence were the answer he'd expected and deserved, and stepped aside to let them pass.

They climbed the nine hundred steps. Marisol went ahead to put the kettle on; Piotr stopped on the two-hundredth step to catch his breath and point out, through the tower's arrow-slit, the first star of the evening, which was not actually a star but the planet he had spent forty years defending, and they all stood and looked at it anyway.

The ledger room, when they reached it, had been cleaned. The soldiers had taken the visible shelves, the decoy volumes, the furniture. What remained was the room itself—the hidden door, the narrow window, the desk with its worn hollow where a keeper's elbow had rested for forty years—and the new pages Wren and Hazel had ruled, waiting in their stack, blank as fresh snow.

Hazel set the ledger down on the desk and opened it to the last written line. Their own name. Their own hand.

Wren stood in the doorway, watching. She had not spoken since the hill. Now she came forward and put her finger on the page—not on the name, but on the space beneath it, the empty ruled line waiting for whatever came next.

"Tomorrow," she said, "when you wind the clock in front of the whole city, this book should be there. On a stand. Open. Let them see that the time and the names come from the same room."

Hazel looked at the line beneath their own. Blank. Ruled. Patient.

"And after tomorrow?" Hazel asked. "When the historians are gone and the council moves on and the city finds new things to argue about? Who keeps it then?"

Wren smiled—the small, crooked smile that Hazel had first seen on the nine-hundredth step, guarding a stopped clock like a tomb, and had been learning ever since.

"Who do you think?" she said. "You ruled the pages, Hazel. You cut the pen. You wrote your own name in front of the victors of a revolution." She tapped the empty line once, lightly, like a clockmaker tapping a case to hear if the movement was sound. "The only question left is what you write next—and whether you'll let me help."

Below them, faint through the tower's stones, the river groaned—the long, deep sound of ice remembering it was water. Tomorrow the clock would be wound. Tomorrow the city would count the first strike.

But tonight there was ink still wet on the page, and a blank line waiting, and Hazel Okafor picked up the raven's quill and dipped it, and began.
