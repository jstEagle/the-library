## 35. Winding the Clock

Hazel woke before dawn to the sound of Marisol singing.

She sang badly, on purpose and without apology—a low, wandering tune with no fixed notes, the kind of song that existed only to fill a kitchen while bread went into the oven. Hazel lay for a moment in the narrow cot beneath the clock room, listening to it come up through the floorboards along with the smell of woodsmoke and warm flour, and thought: *this is what a house sounds like from the inside.* For nineteen years they had heard houses from the outside. The distinction mattered more than any gear train they had ever studied.

The cot was still a cot. The satchel still hung by the door. But something had shifted in the architecture of the world overnight, the way ice shifts before it moves—nothing visible, everything changed.

They dressed in the dark, out of old habit, then laughed at themselves and lit the lamp. There was no need to dress in the dark anymore. That was the whole point of yesterday. That was what thirty-four names entered into a ledger in front of a provisional council actually *meant*, underneath the politics and the historians' sour faces: that a person could strike a light at four in the morning without calculating who would see it.

* * *

By five, the tower was full of people, which was itself a small miracle of scheduling, because half of them were not supposed to be seen together and all of them had spent years being invisible in separate directions.

Wren had claimed the clock room since midnight. She sat cross-legged on the floor amid an arrangement of tools, oil pots, and candle stubs that Hazel recognized, with a jolt, as their own organizational system—the one they used at the bench, transferred wholesale to another person's hands. She had not slept, or if she had, it was the way sentries sleep, with her back against the case of the master clock and one hand resting on its wooden flank like a rider's hand on a horse's neck.

"It's ready," she said, before Hazel was fully through the door. "It's been ready since two. I checked the escapement nine times."

"You checked it eleven. I heard you come up after I'd gone to bed."

"Twelve," Wren said. "You fell asleep during ten."

Hazel knelt beside the open case. The movement gleamed in the lamplight—brass brought back from years of tarnish, every pivot polished, every tooth of every wheel inspected and counted. Nine hundred steps below them, Bellhaven slept under a sky going grey at the edges, and above them, motionless for a generation, the hands stood at eleven minutes to four, where they had stopped the night the sabotage began. Official time in Bellhaven had ended at eleven minutes to four on some ordinary evening years ago, and no one had corrected it because correcting it would have required admitting what had stopped it.

"Marisol wants to know when you're eating," came a voice from the stair, and Old Piotr descended into the light, one hand on the rail, his clouded eyes aimed a degree to the left of everything he looked at, which somehow never prevented him from looking directly at anyone. He wore his good coat, the one with the brass buttons he polished only for occasions the state recognized, and then unpolished again out of spite. Today they shone.

"After," Hazel said.

"After is a country you keep promising to emigrate to," Piotr said. "Marisol says now. Marisol does not say things twice, which you would know if you had lived here twenty years instead of three weeks." He lowered himself onto the work stool with the care of a man negotiating with his own knees. "Besides. You cannot wind a thing like this on an empty stomach. Ask me how I know."

"How do you know?"

"I calibrated this clock to six transit stars over forty years, boy, and I did half of them hungry." He held out a hand, palm up. "Bread."

Hazel fetched the basket Marisol had sent up—dark rolls, hard cheese, a jar of plum preserve with a scrap of paper taped to the lid reading *EAT ALL OF IT OR DON'T COME DOWN*. They ate sitting on the floor of the clock room, shoulder to shoulder around the base of the greatest machine in the city, like children picnicking inside a cathedral organ. Somewhere below, the stairs creaked with early arrivals: ferryman Toma, who had carried a ledger across cracking ice; two of the apprentice girls from the riverside district, unregistered until last week, who had appointed themselves honor guards; a nervous delegate from the provisional council's clerical office, who kept apologizing for existing and whom Wren had stationed by the door with instructions to write down *everything*.

"The historians are already in the square," the delegate reported, in the tone of a man delivering bad news about weather. "The ones who stayed after the withdrawal. They've brought instruments. They intend to measure the clock's rate against their own standard."

"Good," said Piotr.

"They'll dispute it if it disagrees with—"

"Good," Piotr said again, with more relish. He patted the clock case the way one pats a fighting dog. "Let them bring their little standard. This movement has kept true time through sabotage, neglect, a revolution, and my cooking. Their standard has kept nothing but appointments."

* * *

The sun came up clear and cold, the kind of cold that meant the end of winter rather than the middle of it—the light had a different angle now, a promise in it. From the belfry rail, Hazel looked down at the square and felt their stomach perform a slow, deliberate revolution of its own.

There were more people than there should have been. More than the council had expected; more than the criers had promised; more, perhaps, than the city itself knew it contained. They filled the square and spilled down the bridge road and clustered on the frozen wharves, and among them Hazel could pick out, if they looked, the shapes of lives that had lived bent double for years and were standing up straight today to see what straightness felt like. The washerwomen from the flooded district. The old woman who had given them the photograph, wrapped in three shawls, holding someone's arm. Faces turned upward toward the dial, waiting for a clock most of them had never once heard strike, because it had gone silent before most of them were born.

"They've come for the names," Wren said beside them. She had come up quietly; she moved quietly even in her own tower. "Not the clock. The clock is just the bell over the door."

"Some of them came for the clock."

"Some of them came for the warmth and the pickpockets' parade. It doesn't matter why they came." She was quiet a moment. Below, the crowd made the sound crowds make when they're not yet a crowd—hundreds of small conversations, a sea sorting itself. "My father used to say the city only knows what it can count. He meant it bitterly, mostly. But look at them, Hazel. They've come to be counted. On purpose. Nobody's making them."

Down in the square, a line of soldiers held a lane to the observatory steps—not the old regime's soldiers; the colors were new and the discipline was improvised—and through that lane, flanked by clerks carrying cases of instruments, came the historians. Hazel watched them set up their reference chronometer on a folding table with a glass cover, watched them level it, fuss over it, treat it with the tenderness one shows to an argument one expects to win.

"Let them measure," Hazel said, and was surprised to find their voice steady. "It'll agree. That's the whole point of it. True time and official time, geared together. My father's design—" They stopped. "Wren's father's design. It only works if both of them are honest. If official time drifts from true time, the second dial shows it. It's a confession built into the gearing."

"And if the officials aren't honest?"

"Then the clock testifies against them," Hazel said. "Every day. Twice a day, if anyone checks."

Wren smiled—not her usual sharp-edged smile but something older and softer, an heirloom expression, probably her father's. "He would have liked you," she said. "He'd have pretended not to. He'd have found fault with your filing technique for six months. But he'd have liked you."

* * *

At midmorning, the household gathered in the clock room for the last inspection, and it was there, with everything finally done, that Hazel understood—or let themselves understand—what the day was actually asking of them.

The winding crank stood ready in its socket. The weights hung at the top of their shafts, full of stored distance, waiting to fall. Everything else—the ledger downstairs in its new case, the council delegates in the square, the historians with their instruments, the crowd, the thawing river, the whole improbable machinery of a city deciding what to remember—everything depended on a person turning a handle.

That was all. That was everything. A clock is a promise that someone will keep showing up.

"I can't do it alone," Hazel said.

Wren frowned. "It takes two to wind it anyway. The great wheel and the strike barrel—"

"No. I mean—" Hazel looked around the room. At Piotr on his stool, hands folded on his cane. At Marisol, who had come up despite her declared policy regarding towers and heights and stood gripping the doorframe with white knuckles because some things outrank fear. At Toma the ferryman, cap in his hands, awkward indoors. At the two apprentice girls, who had crept in behind everyone and stood pressed together like one small brave animal. "I don't want to do it the old way. One keeper, hidden, keeping the truth in a locked room for people who might never find it. I want—" They heard their own voice drop, go careful, the way it did when they were cutting a very fine thread. "I want it done in the open. All of us. Each person turns it a little. So it isn't mine, or Wren's. So it's *kept*, plural. Like the ledger."

Silence, except for the tick of Wren's pocket watch, which she had taken to carrying set to the secret dial's time, in permanent argument with the official one.

"That's not how clocks are wound," Piotr said at last.

"No," Hazel agreed. "It's how they're going to be wound from now on."

Piotr considered this for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, the way judges nod. "Your father," he said, "wound it alone every Sunday for thirty years and told no one, not even the stars. I always thought it was devotion." His clouded eyes found Hazel's face, or near enough. "Now I think it was loneliness wearing devotion's coat. Yes. All of us. Even the girl by the door."

The delegate by the door started, dropped his pen, picked it up, and wrote something down very fast.

* * *

They came down the nine hundred steps in procession, and the crowd saw them coming, and the sound that rose up the tower's stone throat was unlike anything Hazel had ever been inside of—not a roar, not yet, but a gathering, a tide turning, hundreds of voices arriving at the same expectation from different directions.

The doors had been opened wide. Someone—the council, or the crowd itself—had cleared the steps. Hazel walked out into the white morning light carrying the winding key, which was longer than their forearm and heavier than their satchel had ever been, and Marisol walked beside them with her chin up and her fear worn openly, like a brooch, because she had decided that hiding it on this day of all days would be a kind of lie. And behind them came Piotr leaning on Toma's arm, and Wren carrying her father's maintenance log open to the last page he ever wrote, and the apprentices, and the clerk with his pen, and the whole strange family of the observatory, exposed on the steps in daylight, each one a name the census had tried to erase.

The square went quiet the way snowfall is quiet—all at once, and full of pressure.

Hazel mounted the platform that had been built against the tower's base, beneath the great dial, and turned to face the city. Their mouth was dry. Nineteen years of practice at being unseen had taught them nothing at all about speaking to four thousand people. They had planned a speech. It left them entirely.

So they said the true thing, which was shorter.

"This clock stopped because someone wanted your time to stop counting," they said. The words came out thin in the enormous air, and they lifted their voice the way one lifts a weight, badly at first. "For a generation, Bellhaven ran on time nobody owned. Births happened that weren't written. Debts were collected that were never owed. People lived here"—their voice caught, and they let it—"people lived here and died here and the record says the ground was empty. Yesterday the ledger went into the council's archive with every name we could restore. Today the clock goes back into the city's keeping. Both of them run the same way. Not on one person's secret. On everyone's hands."

They held up the key.

"We're going to wind it together. All of us. And then it will strike, and when it strikes, that's the true time, checked against the sky, geared to the official dial so that the two can never quietly disagree again. If they ever do disagree, the clock will say so. Publicly. Twice a day." They paused, and found Marisol's eyes, and Wren's, and added, because it was true and because the moment demanded truth over eloquence: "Keeping time is keeping faith. That's all it ever was. Come put your hands on it."

* * *

They wound it together.

Piotr first, though it cost him—he insisted on standing unaided at the crank, and turned it through four full revolutions while his breath smoked in the cold and the ratchet clicked like slow applause, and when he stepped back he was laughing at nothing, at everything, wiping his eyes with the heel of his hand. Then Marisol, who had sworn for twenty years that she would never touch the mechanism that could betray her, and gripped the key like a rope thrown to a drowning woman, and turned it, and did not let go when she finished until Wren gently unfolded her fingers. Then Toma, then the apprentices, then the clerk, then—when word traveled down the steps and the crowd surged forward and the soldiers, after one alarmed glance at the delegates, simply stepped aside—then *everyone*. Dockhands and washerwomen and historians, to their lasting astonishment, invited forward by Wren herself with a gesture that dared them to refuse. The line lasted over an hour. The weights sank inch by inch down their shafts, gathering the fallen distance of a generation, and Hazel stood at the movement's heart the whole time, setting each arbor, engaging each pawl, feeling through the brass the accumulating pull of four thousand small acts of ownership.

The historians' chronometer, when they consulted it, agreed with the secret dial to within two seconds. Hazel watched the senior historian write the figure down, watch his hand hesitate, watch him write it again, firmer. Some records argue back even when you don't want them to.

At noon exactly by the sun—Piotr called the transit from the belfry, voice cracking with age and triumph, and Hazel made the final adjustment, the smallest they had ever made, less than the width of a hair—the strike train released.

The first blow of the bell rolled out over the square like something geological, like the river ice letting go all at once, and the crowd counted.

*"One."*

The sound went out over the rooftops, down the bridge road, across the water, into the flooded district where a photograph of two dead people hung on a wall of a house that had never officially existed, and the crowd counted.

*"Two."*

Hazel stood with their hand flat on the vibrating case, feeling the strikes travel up through their arm's bones into their chest, and Wren's hand found theirs on the wood, and Marisol was crying without any attempt to hide it, and somewhere below a child's voice, clear and off by one—

*"Three."*

—was learning, right now, in real time, what noon sounded like; would know it forever; would expect it, demand it, miss it if it ever went silent again. That was the whole mechanism, Hazel thought. That was the escapement of the whole idea. Not brass. Expectation. People who have heard the bell count the hours, and people who count the hours notice when they stop, and people who notice are already halfway to asking why, and asking why is the beginning of every ledger ever kept.

*"Four."*

The official dial and the secret dial above it turned together, minute hand aligned with minute hand, true time and declared time geared into honesty, and the crowd counted on, louder, strangers counting in each other's faces, and Hazel Okafor—who had once measured the world in what could be carried—stood in the open at the center of the ringing air and measured it, just for a moment, in what could not be taken away.

*"Five."*

And it was then, in the space between the fifth stroke and the sixth, with the whole city's breath drawn for the counting, that Hazel saw, at the far edge of the square, apart from the crowd, a familiar figure standing utterly still—one man, hat in his hands, head bowed slightly toward the bell as though it were speaking to him alone—and recognized, with a feeling too complicated for any single name, Silas Crane, holding a folder, watching the record being kept.

*"Six."*

Crane looked up. Across four hundred people, his eyes found Hazel's.

And he began, slowly, deliberately, to walk toward the platform, the folder extended ahead of him like something he could no longer bear to hold.
