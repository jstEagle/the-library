## 36. What the Satchel Holds Now

The satchel had a smell, and Hazel had never noticed it until it was empty.

Leather, mostly—old leather gone soft as chamois where the strap crossed the buckle—and beneath that, faintly, the ghost of machine oil and lamp soot and the particular metallic sweetness of brass filings that had worked themselves into the seams over years. It was the smell of a life packed thin enough to run with. Hazel lifted it from the floor by the door of their small room above the workshop and turned it in the morning light coming through the frost-furred window, and for the first time since the winter of the flood, the bag weighed nothing at all.

They set it on the bed and sat down beside it.

It was a strange thing, unpacking when there was nowhere to flee. The habit of packing was itself a kind of knowledge, learned early and rehearsed until it lived in the hands rather than the head: which items went against the back panel where they'd ride softest, which wrapped in cloth to keep from ringing, which could be sacrificed if running meant dropping the whole bundle and trusting the body alone. Hazel knew the weight of everything they owned the way other apprentices knew their multiplication tables. The satchel at full load came to just under nine pounds—lighter than the smallest striking weight of the master clock upstairs, heavier than anything should have to be when its contents were a whole self.

And now it was empty, and the room was theirs, and the door did not need to be memorized.

Hazel unbuckled the flap anyway—an old motion, the tongue of leather sliding free of the worn slot—and looked inside.

* * *

The tools came out first, because tools always came out first; even unpacked, order mattered, and the order was the order of use.

Twelve pieces, counting the loupe in its little hinged tin. Six files graduated from coarse to fine, their wooden handles darkened by two generations of grip before ever reaching Hazel's palm. The pliers with jaws so fine they could lift a click-spring without marring its blue. The staking set, wrapped in oilcloth, each punch nested in its drilled hole like an insect in a specimen case. And the screwdrivers—four of them, ground down by hand to tips finer than a needle's eye—each one wound at the handle with a thread of colored silk so they could be told apart by feel in the dark.

Hazel laid them on the bench by the window, one by one, and found their hands doing something they hadn't asked them to do: arranging. Not stacked for storage, not rolled into the tool-bag that could be snatched mid-stride, but *set out*—files in their size order along the back rail, screwdrivers standing tip-up in the jar of sand Marisol had given them the first week, pliers closed but within easy reach of the chair. An arrangement for working. An arrangement that assumed a tomorrow with work in it.

They stood back and looked at it and felt something turn over quietly in their chest.

For eleven years the tools had lived folded, wrapped, ready. Ready was a word Hazel had thought meant competence. They understood now that it had meant fear wearing competence's coat—that every night spent rewrapping a staking punch in its oilcloth had been a small prayer to the god of departure: *let me be quick enough, let me be light enough, let me leave nothing behind that proves me.* A clockmaker's apprentice learns that a mechanism is only as good as its weakest spring, and Hazel had lived as a mechanism built entirely around one function: release. Escapement of the self. Tick, and then, if necessary, run.

On the bench, in the honest morning light, the tools looked almost startled to be lying open. As if they too had expected to spend their lives half-packed.

Hazel picked up the finest screwdriver—the one wound in green silk, the one Idris had made before Hazel was born, the one returned to them in a broken man's hands not three weeks past—and set it in the jar with the others. It stood among its brothers as though it had always been there. Perhaps, in some way that didn't fit inside an official record, it had.

* * *

The watch came next.

Hazel took it from the satchel's inner pocket—the pocket sewn against the back panel, the one closest to the spine when the strap went over the shoulder, because if you were going to save one thing off a burning bridge you had already decided what it was. Their parents' watch. The broken watch that had kept its brokenness for eleven years like a held breath, until Hazel had sat up one long night in the observatory with Wren asleep across the ledger table and repaired it, plate by plate, jewel by jewel, back to life.

It ran now. That still astonished them. They would take it out and hold it to their ear the way other people listened for rain, and there it was: the small dry heartbeat of it, tick-tick-tick, indifferent and faithful, keeping the same time as the great clock on the hill because Hazel had regulated it so. Official time and true time, geared together in a thing the size of a plum.

They carried it to the mantel.

The room above the workshop had come with the appointment—*Clockmaker Second Class, Observatory of Bellhaven*, the letterhead real this time, the seal genuine, Hazel's name entered in triplicate in records that would survive any historian's pen—and the mantel was a plain shelf of river-cut oak above the small iron stove, and nothing had ever stood on it. Hazel had watched it for weeks the way they watched all empty surfaces: assessing it for what could be grabbed and gone with. Now they set the watch upright against the chimney brick, case open, face outward, dial catching the light.

A thing on a mantel is a declaration. Hazel saw that plainly. A watch in a pocket says *I am careful.* A watch in a satchel says *I am ready to go.* A watch on a mantel says *I live here, and here is where my time is kept.*

Inside the case back, where only a maker or a lover would look, their mother's engraving waited as it always waited: *A.O. — for Atlas, who carries us.* Atlas. Their middle name, given in the winter of the flood by a woman who died before she could explain it, recovered from forty-year-old brass by lamplight while the city outside tore itself into a new shape. Hazel touched the engraving once, lightly, with one fingertip—a clockmaker's touch, the touch that moves a balance wheel a half-degree and no more.

"Mum," they said aloud, to the empty room, because saying things aloud was another skill they were practicing lately, "I'm putting you somewhere visible."

The watch ticked on the mantel. The stove ticked as the iron warmed. Two clocks in conversation, and neither of them hiding.

* * *

The forged paper was last, and Hazel held it a long time.

It was a single sheet, folded into quarters until the creases had gone gray and furred, and it had cost Master Idris three evenings of careful penmanship and a wax seal borrowed from a client's correspondence. *Let it be known that Hazel Okafor, of lawful age, is bound apprentice to Idris Voss, master clockmaker of the Guild of Bellhaven...* Every word of it invented. Every stroke of it criminal. It had been, for eleven years, the thinnest membrane between Hazel and the wagons—a lie written in a frightened man's best hand, and Hazel had carried it the way soldiers carry ammunition, checking its position by feel a dozen times a day without looking.

There had been seasons when Hazel hated the paper. There had been seasons—it shamed them now, but shame was a thing they were learning to set on the bench alongside everything else—when the paper had seemed proof that Idris had cared, in his flinching, calculating way, and Hazel had loved it for that. Both things true. Survival and betrayal wearing the same coat; Hazel had learned that lesson twice over now, once as the one hidden and once as the one deciding whom to trust with the hiding.

They unfolded it on the bench and read it once more, top to bottom. The ink had faded to the brown of old rust. In the lower corner, in pencil so faint it was nearly imaginary, Idris had written a date—the day he'd forged it—and beside the date, a single word: *sorry*. Hazel had never noticed it before. Eleven years in the same fold of leather and they had never once looked at that corner, because looking too closely at your own forgery felt like looking too closely at a bridge you had to cross.

*Sorry*, in pencil. Weighed against eleven years of terror, it was nothing. Weighed against the fact that a man with no reason to risk himself had sat up three nights with a borrowed seal to make an unregistered orphan exist on paper—paper being the only country where such a person could legally breathe—it was almost something. Hazel filed it under *unresolved*, where they kept Idris entire, and carried the sheet to the stove.

The fire had come up nicely. Hazel opened the grate.

They did not burn it quickly. That surprised them; they had imagined crumpling it, tossing it, done. But their hands, trained to unhurried precision, refused to hurry, and so Hazel fed one corner to the flames and watched the fire eat upward line by line—*lawful age*, gone; *bound apprentice*, gone; the false guild stamp curling black and lifting off the page like a moth taking flight—and thought about what burning meant.

Not erasure. That was the historians' trade, and Crane's, and every clerk who ever ruled a line through a name because the name was inconvenient: erasure pretended the thing had never been. Burning the forgery was the opposite. It was certification. *This document is hereby retired, its work complete, its subject no longer in need of it.* Somewhere in the provisional council's archive, in a hand the clerks were still learning to call beautiful, Hazel Okafor existed in original ink, witnessed, entered, irreversible. The paper in their fingers was scaffolding around a finished building. It could come down.

The last corner caught. Hazel watched the word *sorry* flare and vanish—I'll keep that part anyway, they thought, I don't need the pencil to hold it—and closed the grate.

Smoke that smelled of nothing but old paper rose up the flue. Hazel stood with their palms open at their sides, empty for perhaps the first time in their adult life, and waited to feel lighter, and instead felt—strange. Unbalanced. A mechanism stripped of its mainspring doesn't feel free; it feels slack, until it learns what it wants to be wound *for*.

That would come. Everything worth building took fitting, and re-fitting.

* * *

In the afternoon they went through the satchel itself, because an empty bag tells you truths a full one keeps.

They turned it out completely over the bed and inventoried the residue of a lifetime of readiness: brass filings worked into the seams, a single green silk thread gone from a screwdriver's handle, a pressed flower they had no memory of picking—small and white, from the flooded district maybe, from some errand run in the years before memory began—a bent pin, half a biscuit gone hard as pumice, and in the very bottom, flattened by years of weight, a scrap of paper in a child's handwriting: *if lost return to the clock shop by the bridge.*

Hazel sat with that a while.

They had written it, they supposed, at seven, at eight—some early year when the strategy of invisibility was still forming and some part of them had rebelled against it in the only language a child has, which is the wish to be found. *If lost, return.* Return where? To a shop that had sealed its doors against them, whose master had sold their parents' names? To a bridge that had carried census wagons both directions? And yet. The child had been right in a way the child couldn't have known. Hazel had gotten lost, thoroughly lost, in the ordinary way of the erased—lost to the record, lost to kinship, lost even to themselves—and they had been returned, by nine hundred steps and a stopped clock and a dead keeper's stubborn kindness, to exactly the place the note specified: the clock shop by the bridge. Or near enough. The observatory was the clock shop the city had elevated, and Hazel had come home to it the way water comes home to a level.

They smoothed the scrap flat and put it in their breast pocket, not the satchel. Some documents you carry on your person.

Then they cleaned the bag properly—brushed the seams, oiled the leather until it drank the oil and glowed, stitched the fraying strap-anchor with waxed thread in small even stitches, a repair done slowly and well because the bag deserved honesty about its age—and set it down by the door.

Empty.

They looked at it sitting there, and understood that they were not done with it and never would be, and that this was all right. The world had not grown safe. The historians still negotiated in the council hall; Crane's fate was still unwritten; the river had broken its ice early this year and nobody yet knew what an early thaw argued about the summer to come. There might be running again someday. But the bag by the door meant something different now, and Hazel wanted the difference to be legible even to themselves, so they did a small ceremonial thing: they put into the satchel, deliberately, knowingly, three items only.

The loupe, because seeing clearly was portable.

The green-silk screwdriver, because some repairs could not wait for home.

And a blank notebook, bought that morning from the stationer on Cordwainer Street with their first proper wages—blank, unlined, waiting—for reasons Hazel could not have fully explained except that an empty book and an empty bag felt like cousins, and they wanted to find out what happened when you let both stay open.

*If lost, return,* said the note in their breast pocket. *Return* was no longer a direction on a map. It was a practice. You returned to the bench each morning. You returned the names, one by one, to the pages that would hold them. You returned yourself, daily, to the decision to be seen.

* * *

Evening brought Marisol up the stairs with a covered dish and the news, delivered in her sideways fashion, that Idris had asked—twice, through the journeyman who now ran his old workshop—whether he might come by. Whether there was any work. Any work at all. He'd said, reportedly, *even sweeping.*

Marisol set the dish on the table and studied Hazel with the flat frankness she reserved for questions she wasn't going to ask directly. "You don't have to say yes."

"I know."

"You don't owe him a broom."

"I know that too." Hazel lifted the lid—braised river fish, onions, the good dark bread—and found their appetite arriving the way courage arrived: after the decision, not before. "But he was a clockmaker before he was a coward, and he's a better one of the first than anyone else in this city. And the ledger needs a second pair of trained hands if anything ever happens to mine. And—" they paused, turning the truth over like a movement under the loupe—"and I want to see what he does with it. With being pardoned but not forgiven. Whether a man can learn a trade he failed at for thirty years."

Marisol snorted, which from her was agreement. "Sweeping," she muttered, at the door. "We'll see how he holds a broom." Then, softer, with one hand resting a moment on the doorframe of the room where Hazel was visibly, permanently, recordedly alive: "Supper's at seven. Your clock strikes it. Don't be late to your own time."

Hazel ate slowly, watching the light die over the rooftops toward the river, listening to the deep voice of the observatory carry across the city: seven strokes, unhurried, exact, each one falling on Bellhaven like a stone into still water. From the mantel the watch answered at the end of the seventh beat, its small tick slipping into phase with the great mechanism on the hill, official time and true time and one repaired heart of brass all keeping faith together.

Afterward Hazel washed the dish, trimmed the lamp, and sat down at the bench—not to repair anything, simply to sit where the tools lay open, in the room where their name hung framed above the door in letters anyone could read. Below, by the entrance, the satchel stood empty against the wall, holding its three chosen things: a lens, a screwdriver, a blank book.

Ready, Hazel thought, testing the old word in its new meaning. Not ready to run. Ready to answer.

Down in the workshop, the night bell rang, and with it came a knocking at the street door—heavy, hesitant, unmistakably the knock of a man who had been practicing what he would say the whole walk up the hill—and Hazel Okafor rose, lit a second lamp against the dark, and went down to open it.
