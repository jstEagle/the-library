## 37. Idris at the Bench

The bench was too low for him now, or he was too tall for it—either way, Idris Voss's knees complained from the first hour of the morning until Marisol chased him out at dusk. He had built that bench thirty-one years ago, in a different shop, under a different name on the signboard, and he had assumed—foolishly, he could see that now—that a man who made a thing got to keep the sitting of it.

It was Hazel's bench now. Hazel's shop, technically: the observatory's ground floor had been given over to the clockworks annex, and the provisional council had stamped papers with real seals saying so. Idris worked at the second bench, the one against the north window where the light came in cold and honest and showed every flaw in a file stroke. He was grateful for that light. He did not deserve the warmer one.

* * *

He had been at the observatory nine weeks when Wren finally put the ledger in front of him.

Not the original—the original lived upstairs behind three locks and whatever new arrangement Hazel had engineered into the door, because Hazel engineered everything now, quietly, the way water finds the low places in stone. This was the fair copy, the one they let scholars touch with clean hands. Wren set it on his bench without ceremony, the way you'd set down a delivery of brass stock.

"You should read it," she said. "All of it."

"I helped bury it," Idris said. "That's not the same as not knowing what's in it."

"It isn't," Wren agreed. She had her father's way of agreeing with you in a tone that meant the opposite. "But you buried it for them, not against them. There's a difference in the weight. You should find out how much."

She left it there. That was Wren's method entirely: she left things where your conscience would have to walk around them.

Idris looked at the ledger for a long time after she went. It was a thick book, rebound in gray leather, its corners guarded with tin. He knew the hand inside before he opened it—he'd have known Aldous's script anywhere, that upright patient lettering, like a man writing for someone who would read it a hundred years on. They had been friendly once. Not friends. Friendly the way two men are friendly when one keeps the city's time and the other sells the city its springs and escapements, and both know which of them the city would miss more.

He opened it to no particular page. That was cowardice dressed as chance, but he took his openings where he could.

*Aldous, Tomas. Born in the spring of the weir collapse. Father: keeper of this record.*

He turned pages. The entries ran like a river under ice—you couldn't see them all, but you could feel the current. Births. Deaths. Debts forgiven, debts sold downstream, debts inherited by children too young to sign their own names. Whole families the census had never heard of, recorded here in ink that had outlasted the government that refused them.

And then, near the middle of the book, in a section Aldous had titled *Sold Downstream — Accounts Unresolved*, Idris found the page he had been walking around for forty years without ever letting himself look at directly.

*Okafor, Serafina. Okafor, Daniel.* Their trade listed. Their street listed. The flood year noted in the margin, and beside it, in Aldous's smaller hand: *Names given to census office by workshop license holder, I.V., in exchange for continued licensure. Recorded here so the transaction itself is not erased.*

Aldous had known. Had known and written it down—not to accuse, exactly, though it accused; not to forgive, exactly, though somewhere in that patient upright script there was something that might have been forgiveness if Idris squinted hard enough at it. Aldous had written it down because it was true, and because a record that flinched was no record at all.

Idris closed the book. His hands were steady, which seemed like a betrayal of its own.

* * *

"Hazel tells me you've been reading," Marisol said that evening, setting a bowl in front of him at the long table. The observatory kitchen smelled of onions and river fish and twenty years of a woman cooking where officially no woman was. Even now, even pardoned, even entered into the restored ledger in her own strong hand, Marisol moved through rooms like someone keeping an exit in mind. Some habits outlive their reasons.

"Some of it," Idris said.

"The Okafor page?"

He looked up. Her face gave nothing away, which from Marisol was itself a message: she was asking as the household's gatekeeper, not as his friend. Fair enough. He hadn't earned the other question yet.

"Yes."

"Then you know what you're eating dinner next to." She sat down across from him. "Hazel knows too. Has since chapter and verse. And Hazel still signed the paper that brought you here as journeyman. So the question isn't whether you're allowed at this table. The question is whether you can stand being somewhere you're allowed."

Idris ate his fish. It was very good. Everything Marisol made was very good, and it had taken him six weeks to stop apologizing while he ate it.

"I stood worse tables," he said. "For worse reasons."

"I know," she said. "I've seen the worse reasons. They wear your coat."

* * *

His days settled into a rhythm that felt suspiciously like peace, and he distrusted it accordingly.

Mornings, he worked stock: cutting wheels, pivoting arbors, the honest repetitive labor of preparation. Hazel had assigned him this work without comment, and Idris understood the assignment completely—it was apprentice work, journeyman work, the work he'd have given a promising fourteen-year-old once upon a time, back when he still believed a man's reputation was a thing he owned rather than a thing other people kept for him. The irony was precise. Hazel had learned precision from somebody. Just not from him.

Afternoons, when the light held, he sat with the fair copy of the ledger and read it the way he'd been told to: all of it. He kept a notebook of his own now, copying out names, dates, trades—a concordance, Hazel called it, so that any entry could be cross-referenced to the duplicates hidden in clock cases across four provinces. It was clerical work. It was also, he understood, penance structured as usefulness, and he suspected Hazel had designed it that way, gear by gear, the way you'd design an escapement to convert falling weight into steady motion. Take the guilt. Let it turn something.

Some evenings Hazel worked at the far bench and asked him questions.

Not accusations. Questions about mechanisms, mostly—though lately the questions had drifted toward history, toward people, toward the territory Idris had spent decades making uninhabitable for himself.

"Master Aldous ordered his mainsprings from you," Hazel said one evening, not looking up from a wheel they were crossing. "Before my time."

"From 940 onward, yes."

"Why you?"

Idris considered lying. He'd been considering it for weeks, actually, holding the lie ready the way you hold a spare part—just in case. But the whole architecture of this arrangement, the whole mad machinery of the observatory household, ran on the assumption that he wouldn't.

"Because I didn't ask what they were for," he said. "Other suppliers asked. Aldous wanted a man who delivered good springs and kept his mouth shut about the rest."

"And you kept it shut."

"For eleven years." He turned the arbor in his fingers. "I told myself it was discretion. A craftsman doesn't gossip about his clients. Then the census offered me a choice between my license and strangers, and I found the same silence fit that too. Silence is very flexible, Hazel. It fits whatever shape they need."

Hazel's file moved steadily across the wheel teeth. *Tick,* said the file. *Tick.* When they finally spoke, it was in the level voice they used for testimony, for the council, for the record.

"Aldous wrote down what you did. In the ledger. So it couldn't be smoothed over later."

"I know. I've read it."

"He also wrote that you were the only supplier whose springs never failed." The file stopped. Hazel looked up then, and their eyes were their mother's eyes, which was a cruelty Idris had never gotten used to. "Both things are in the record. That's how the record works. You don't get to be only one thing in it."

"No," Idris said. "You don't."

"That's not a punishment," Hazel said. "I used to think it was. It's just what staying costs."

* * *

Winter deepened. The anniversary of the ice-break came and went, marked by a civic holiday nobody quite knew how to celebrate yet, and the observatory marked it instead the way it marked everything: by winding the master clock in public, by opening the ledger to new entries, by feeding anyone who climbed the hill. Idris stood at the edge of the crowd during the winding, hat in his hands, and watched Hazel's small sure fingers set the great winding key, and thought: *I taught half this city's clockmakers badly and one of them well, and the one I taught well isn't mine.*

The thought arrived without bitterness, which surprised him. Grief, he was learning, came in more varieties than the catalogs admitted. There was the grief of loss and the grief of confession and the grief—which nobody warned you about—of watching something be repaired correctly without you. It was a lonely grief. It was also, he suspected, the only kind that cleaned rather than corroded.

In February the first shipment of duplicate-ledger clock cases came back from the eastern towns with receipts, and Hazel spent two evenings verifying seals, and on the third evening called Idris to the main bench.

"We need the Aldous archive catalogued," Hazel said. "Piotr's observation books, the maintenance logs, the correspondence. Forty years of it, and most of it unindexed. The historians' delegation arrives again in spring—they'll want to see order, not heroics. Order is harder to argue with."

"You want me to do it."

"I want the person who knew Aldous best to do it. You bought his springs for eleven years. You visited the tower. You argued with him about temperature compensation twice a year." Hazel slid a box of correspondence across the bench. "You're the only living person who can read his shorthand marginalia. That's not forgiveness, Master Voss. It's logistics."

"Understood," Idris said. And then, because honesty had become a discipline he practiced daily, like scales on an instrument: "Thank you for saying the second part out loud. People keep forgiving me sideways. It's harder to bank."

Something moved through Hazel's face—there and gone, like a flaw in otherwise clear glass. "Wren says the same thing," they said. "About her father. That everyone offers absolution like it's a gift, when usually it's just the forgiver trying to put the weight down." They returned to their wheel. "Keep the weight, Master Voss. It balances better than you'd think."

* * *

He found the first letter in March, and it rearranged him.

It was filed—badly, in Piotr's blind-man's filing system—among calibration notes, dated the winter of the flood. Aldous's hand, cramped with cold or haste:

*V. came today. Asked again about the Okafors' registration status. Told him nothing. He pays well for silences but I am not in that market. Note for the record: if anything happens to the tower, the ledger's location dies with me unless the girl learns the door. Must teach the girl the door.*

*V.* Silas Crane. Of course. Even Crane's name was abbreviated in Aldous's shorthand, as if the man himself were too tedious for full spelling. Idris sat with the letter a long time. He had always assumed—he had needed to assume—that Aldous never knew the full shape of what Idris had done, that the ledger entry was assembled afterward from census gossip. But no. Aldous had known in the winter of the flood itself. Had watched Idris come and go. Had refused Crane the same silence Idris had sold him, and then had died—had been killed—with the record intact.

There was no entry in any ledger for what that did to a man's chest.

He copied the letter into his notebook anyway. Letter by letter, date by date, he built the concordance, and the concordance became a chronology, and the chronology became something none of them had quite possessed before: the full account of forty years of refusal, written in the margins of invoices and calibration logs by people who had believed, each of them alone in their own era, that paper could be made to hold.

* * *

On a raw evening in late March, with the river muttering about spring again beneath the hill, Hazel stayed late at the main bench and called him over—not to fetch or hold, but to sit. They set a movement between them on a leather pad. Small, old, silver-cased. Idris recognized it before he recognized why: the watch. The parents' watch. The one Hazel had carried out of a burned district in a satchel when the satchel was the whole world.

"It stops every fourth day," Hazel said. "I've rebuilt it twice. It runs beautifully and then it stops, always at the same interval, and I cannot find the fault because there is no fault. Which means the fault is in my assumptions. So." They slid it across. "Journeyman's eyes. Fresh ones. Tell me what you see."

Idris picked it up. The case was warm from Hazel's bench hours. He opened the back and there was the engraving he'd heard whispered about in the household like scripture—the mother's inscription, the name Hazel hadn't known was theirs—and below it, inside the movement itself, something else. Something he doubted anyone but a clockmaker would ever notice.

A repair. An old one, done in the flood winter, by hands that had been shaking. A tiny spring had been replaced with a substitute—not a proper part; a cut-down piece of something else, a hairpin or a brooch wire, filed and fitted by someone working by candlelight with no right tools and no permission and no time. The substitute was perfect. It was also, he saw now, four ten-thousandths proud at its anchor point, and every fourth day the accumulated error kissed the balance cock and stopped the heart.

"She repaired it herself," Idris said quietly. "Your mother. During the flood, probably, when there was no one to bring it to. Look at the anchor—she peened it with the back of a snips or something close to it. No clockmaker does that. Someone with no bench does it."

Hazel leaned in. For a long moment neither of them spoke, and the tower overhead ticked its enormous slow tick, the sound the whole city now set its mornings by.

"She fixed it," Hazel said at last, "well enough to run for twenty years."

"Well enough to run for nineteen years and three hundred sixty-one," Idris corrected gently. "Which is better than most of us manage." He set the watch down between them. "I can dress the anchor properly. Ten minutes' work. It'll never stop again."

Hazel was quiet. Then: "No. Leave it."

Idris looked at them.

"If I fix it, it becomes my repair." Hazel picked the watch up, cradled it, listening to it tick as if hearing it for the first time. "Her repair runs every fourth day and reminds me. Yours would run forever and remind me of nothing." They met his eyes. "You understand? Some things aren't waiting to be fixed. They're waiting to be *read*. That's the whole ledger, Master Voss. That's the whole argument. We don't restore the past so it stops hurting. We keep it running so it keeps telling the truth."

Idris nodded, and could not speak, and did not trust what his face was doing, and so looked down at the bench instead—at the fine bright scatter of steel and brass, at the notebook full of other people's courage, at his own hands, spotted and scarred and steadier than they deserved to be.

"Incoming," Old Piotr announced from the doorway, in the ringing voice he used for announcements and little else, "rider on the ferry road. Green sash. Council rider, coming up the hill fast."

Marisol appeared behind him, wiping her hands. Wren's footsteps were already on the stairs, quick and even. Through the north window Idris could see the lantern bobbing up the switchback road, and beyond it the dark river, and beyond that the eastern sky where the historians' delegation would appear within the month with their clean documents and their patient erasures.

Hazel slipped the watch into their pocket and stood, already moving, already the keeper of everything.

"Green sash means official correspondence," Wren said from the stair door, slightly breathless, a sealed packet in her hand, the council's wax broken open. She read it once, and Idris watched her face go carefully, professionally still—the stillness of a woman deciding how much to tell the room, and in what order.

"They've requested the complete archive," she said. "By name. Every observation log, every maintenance record, every ledger—" her eyes lifted, and found Idris first, of all people, "—*and the concordance*, 'as compiled by Journeyman Voss.' Signed by the delegation's chief historian." She paused. "Countersigned by Silas Crane. Newly appointed. Clerk of the provisional archive."
