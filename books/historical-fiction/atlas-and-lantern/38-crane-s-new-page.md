## 38. Crane's New Page

The letter of dismissal came on paper so fine it was nearly translucent, and Silas Crane read it four times before the words would hold their shapes.

*Your services are no longer required by the Provisional Administration of Bellhaven. All census records in your custody are to be surrendered to the Archive Division by the end of the month. You are thanked for your diligence.*

Thanked for his diligence. He set the letter on his kitchen table, aligned its edges with the table's edge, and sat looking at it while the morning light moved across the room the way it always did—slowly, predictably, without any regard for what had changed.

Twenty-two years in the counting service. Fourteen under the old regime, eight under the council that had followed, and then the revolution, which had not been called a revolution until it was over and could be named safely. Twenty-two years of household rolls and tax ledgers and the small, patient work of knowing who lived where. He had been good at it. That was the word people used, sometimes with admiration, more often not: *good*. Silas Crane was good at finding the seams in a story, the place where a wife's surname shifted spelling between visits, the lodger who appeared on one roll and vanished from the next, the apprentice at the observatory who existed in no register at all and yet stood before him with steady hands and a lie assembled so carefully it deserved, he had thought at the time, a kind of professional respect.

He had asked one question too many. That was how he thought of it now, in the nights, when the questions came back to him the way they did—not the ones he had asked others, but the ones that had begun asking themselves.

*Where are your parents buried?*

He had marked the page. He had done everything the instructions required. And then the historians had arrived with their clean census, and he had watched them take his meticulous lists and feed them into a machine of forgetting, and somewhere between the certification and the burning—he never learned whether they burned them; the historians were not forthcoming about methods—Silas Crane had understood that a record is only as honest as the hand that keeps it, and that his hands had not been honest, only precise.

Precision without honesty. He had built a career on the distinction without ever noticing there was one.

* * *

He applied to the council's archive in the second week of spring, when the river had settled back into its banks and the city smelled of mud and rope and the particular green sweetness of things beginning again.

The archive occupied the old customs house, three floors of shelving built into what had once been warehouses for tobacco and indigo, and when Crane walked in with his application folded in his breast pocket, he was struck—as he had not expected to be—by the smell. Paper and ink and lamp oil and dust, and beneath it all something like stone kept dry. It was the smell of every counting house he had ever worked in, and yet it came to him differently now, the way a word read in a new context comes out meaning something else entirely.

The clerk at the intake desk was a young man with ink-stained cuffs and the harried air of someone doing three jobs badly. He read Crane's application twice, looked up, and said, "You're the census man."

"I was the census taker."

"You're the one who certified the ledger." The young man's voice had shifted—not warmer exactly, but attentive, the way one attends to a curiosity. "At the council hearings. The Okafor testimony."

Crane had known this moment would come, in some form, wherever he sought work in Bellhaven. The hearing transcripts had been posted publicly—itself an innovation he was still adjusting to—and his name appeared in them eleven times. He had counted.

"I certified the ledger's authenticity," Crane said. "Yes."

"They say you destroyed your own census to do it." The young man leaned forward. "Is that true?"

"It is not," Crane said. "I declined to certify my own census, on the grounds that it contained falsifications I had been instructed to make. The records themselves were surrendered intact, as required. What the Archive Division chose to do with them afterward is recorded in their own accession logs."

He heard himself delivering this answer—in full, precisely, with dates implied if not stated—and recognized the old habit rising in him like water finding its level: the compulsion to be exact, even about his own disgrace. Especially about his own disgrace. If he was to be remembered, let him be remembered correctly.

The young man made a note. "There's a position," he said slowly. "Clerk, third class. Accessions and cross-reference. It pays poorly and the hours are worse and most of the work is copying entries that other people copied badly the first time. Nobody qualified wants it."

"Nobody qualified wants it," Crane repeated, "because it is tedious, exacting, and thankless."

"Yes."

"Then I am well suited to it."

* * *

They assigned him to the observatory annex on his fourth day.

He should have anticipated it—the archive had been consolidating the recovered collections since winter, and the observatory's papers were the largest single acquisition—but knowing a thing in advance is different from walking through a door and finding it waiting. The annex was a long room on the archive's second floor, given over to crates and document cases stamped with the observatory's seal: a tower beneath a star. And at the desk at the far end, cataloguing with a pen that scratched like a small animal in undergrowth, sat Wren Aldous.

She looked up when he entered, and Crane watched her face do something complicated and quick—surprise, then calculation, then a settling into watchfulness that he recognized because he had spent a lifetime producing it in others.

"Mr. Crane," she said.

"Miss Aldous." He stopped an appropriate distance from her desk—the distance of courtesy, which he had always measured carefully and which meant, he understood now, something different than he had once believed. "I've been assigned as clerk to this collection. Third class. I'll be working under your direction, if acceptable."

"Is it acceptable?" she asked. "That's the question, isn't it? Whether I find it acceptable."

"Yes."

She set down her pen. She was thinner than she had been in the winter; he knew why, having read the arrest records he had helped create, and having sat through the weeks of her custody in the public gallery of the hearings because he could not make himself stay away and could not make himself speak. Her eyes were the same, though. That unblinking steadiness of a woman raised guarding a tomb.

"My father died keeping records the government wanted erased," she said. "You spent last winter hunting the people those records protected. Now the council gives you a desk in the room where his life's work is being catalogued, and asks me to direct you." She picked up a document case and weighed it in her hands, as if measuring what it might cost to throw. "Do you understand how that reads, from where I'm sitting?"

"It reads," Crane said, "like either an error or a test. I have not yet determined which."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not a smile. The ancestor of one, perhaps, several generations removed.

"And which do you think it is?"

"I think," Crane said, "that the new administration does not fully trust me, and has assigned me here because you are the person in Bellhaven least likely to extend me any benefit of the doubt. If I am faithful to the work, you will report it faithfully. If I am not—" He paused. "If I am not, I imagine the report will be prompt and thorough. They are right about you in this. I have been on the receiving end of your thoroughness before, though at the time I mistook it for evasion."

"You mean the lies," Wren said flatly. "Okafor's lies. The apprentice who didn't exist."

"The apprentice who existed very much," Crane said, "and was better at not existing than anyone I had ever interviewed. Yes. I remember."

He remembered more than he intended to say. He remembered standing in the observatory's lamplight while Hazel Okafor lied to him with those careful, craftsman's hands folded still, and thinking: *this person has rehearsed invisibility the way I have rehearsed questions.* And he remembered, later, after everything, lying awake and understanding that he had admired it, and that the admiration had frightened him more than the defiance ever had.

Wren studied him for a long moment. Then she slid a crate across the desk.

"Birth registers," she said. "My father's. Every entry needs transcribing into the master index—name, date, parentage, witness. The handwriting deteriorates after the flood year; he wrote half of it at night, by feel. If you can't read an entry, you mark it uncertain and bring it to me. You don't guess. Is that understood?"

"Uncertainty noted, never resolved by inference," Crane said. "Understood."

"One more thing." She drew out a single page and laid it face up between them. It was a copy of the ledger's first leaf, the one bearing the keeper's declaration, written in Edmund Aldous's upright hand: *Let it be kept that none may be unwritten.* "That's the standard here," she said. "Not speed. Not completeness, even. Him. Whatever you were before, whatever you counted and for whom—in this room you work for that sentence. If you can't, tell me now and I'll send you back downstairs."

Crane looked at the sentence for longer than reading required. His father's voice, dead thirty years, had always said that a list was a form of power and power was a form of safety, and Silas had believed it the way one believes weather—simply, continuously, without examination. *Let it be kept that none may be unwritten.* It was the same sentence inverted. Same grammar. Same verbs. Everything else turned inside out like a glove.

"I can," he said.

* * *

The work was everything the intake clerk had promised and less, in the way that hard simple labor is always less than dread. Crane transcribed. He cross-referenced. He learned the quirks of Edmund Aldous's hand—the way the descenders lengthened in cold years, the shorthand marks for debt that appeared in the margins like weeds between rows of names—and he found, within a week, that he had begun to know this dead man. Not his face, which no portrait preserved, but his habits. His patience. His refusal to round a number or soften a fact. Aldous had recorded debts owed by men who later became magistrates, and recorded them anyway. He had recorded children born to women with no husbands and no papers, and given the fathers' names when the mothers gave them, and left the space blank—honestly, visibly blank—when they did not. He had written, beside one entry, *mother asks that the child be forgotten; I decline,* and Crane sat with that line for the better part of an hour, his pen idle, feeling the ground shift somewhere beneath his profession's foundations.

A record-keeper's job, he had always believed, was to know. Aldous's job had been to keep. The difference, Crane was coming to see, was the difference between holding and holding safe.

On the ninth day he found the flood-year pages, and Hazel Okafor's name.

*Okafor, Hazel. Born in the winter of the flood.* Witnessed. Dated. In Edmund Aldous's careful hand, entered the way every child in the city ought to have been entered, in the book that should have needed no courage to write in.

Crane transcribed the entry into the index. Name, date, parentage, witness. His pen did not shake. But he sat afterward with both hands flat on the desk, breathing, aware that he was feeling something he had no procedural vocabulary for. He had put this name on a list once. Twice. Three times. He had asked where the parents were buried, and the pause had answered him, and he had marked the page, and the marking of that page had traveled—through the clean census, through the historians' orders, through soldiers' boots on observatory stairs—to a night when Wren Aldous had traded her freedom for another person's escape. Every consequence had followed from his question with mechanical precision. Escapement, catch, release. He understood mechanisms now; he had spent a season studying them whether he meant to or not.

And yet the same name, in the same hand that his own inquiries had endangered, sat here in the archive being catalogued, protected, entered into a permanent record that his certification had defended at the cost of his career. The wheel came around. He did not understand the mechanism. He only knew he was somewhere inside it.

* * *

"You mark your uncertainties honestly," Wren said, three weeks in. "Piotr says you've alphabetized his observation cards without being asked, and Marisol says—" She stopped.

"Marisol says what?"

"That you waited for her to serve you before you touched anything on the tray. She counts things like that. She's kept count for twenty years." Wren closed the index volume. "You're doing the work properly, Mr. Crane. I want that on the record, since I intend to complain about everything else."

"What else is there to complain about?"

"That it's you doing it." She said it without heat, the way one states a measurement. "Every day you sit at that desk and transcribe my father's book, and every day I have to decide again whether a man can become a different instrument or whether he's only been restrung. I don't have an answer. Neither does Okafor. We've discussed it."

Crane absorbed this. The honesty of it was almost physically uncomfortable—no one in the old service had ever told him plainly what they thought of him; they had simply complied, or evaded, and he had catalogued the difference.

"For what it is worth," he said, "I ask myself the same question nightly, and arrive each morning at the same provisional finding: that I do not know, and will therefore proceed carefully."

"And if proceeding carefully isn't enough? If the work requires more than care?"

"Then I hope," Crane said, "that someone will tell me plainly, as you just have."

Wren regarded him across the desk, and for a moment something passed between them that Crane could only describe, in his private internal ledger, as recognition: two people who had both mistaken precision for virtue, meeting on the far side of the error.

"There's a request come down from the council," she said, changing course. "They want a consolidated index of every unregistered birth my father recorded—cross-checked against the provisional citizenship rolls, flagged for follow-up interviews. Someone will have to contact those families. Some of them won't want to be found. Some of them were found already, by you, and will not receive a knock on the door from a familiar face with any kindness." She slid the order toward him. "I can assign it to the junior clerks downstairs. They'd mangle it. Or I can assign it to you, and spend every day of the work watching you."

Crane looked at the order. Names of the unfound. Doors that would open or not open. Questions that would be answered or refused—and this time, for the first time in his working life, questions whose answers would be kept, not taken. Given, or withheld, by the people he asked. He found that the prospect did not frighten him. It humbled him, which was a different organ altogether.

"Assign it to me," he said.

"You understand I'll be watching."

"Miss Aldous," Crane said, taking up the order and squaring its edges against the desk, "watching is the one thing I have never minded."

That earned him, at last and briefly, the thing that was not quite a smile.

* * *

He began the following week, and the work undid him slowly, in the manner of all honest work.

He knocked on doors. He waited on stoops. He was refused nine times out of twenty, and accepted the refusals without argument, entering each one in his log—*declined to be indexed; request withdrawn*—and discovering, entry by entry, that a refusal honorably received was itself a kind of record, a testimony that the door belonged to the person behind it. Some of them recognized him. One woman spat. A boatwright who had hidden in his own cellar for six years listened to Crane's whole rehearsed speech and then said, quietly, "You asked my brother where his daughter was buried. I read the transcripts." And then, after a silence that Crane did not fill, because he had finally learned that some pauses were not answers to be marked but wounds to be left alone: "Come back Thursday. Bring the book. My mother remembers the midwife's name."

Thursday, Crane brought the book. The mother remembered the midwife. The midwife, located through the ledger, remembered two more families. The record, handled gently, argued for itself.

In the evenings he walked home along the river past the observatory hill, and some nights the clock was striking as he passed, and he would stop and count the strokes with the crowd of whoever else had paused on the road—shopkeepers, dockhands, children—and feel among them the peculiar democracy of people checking themselves against a shared truth. Official time and true time, geared together. He had spent a winter trying to make those two things diverge, and had succeeded, and the success had been the worst failure of his life.

On an evening in late spring, the ice long gone and the river running high and bright, he reached the bottom of the observatory path and found Hazel Okafor waiting for him there, satchel over one shoulder, tools catching the last light.

"Mr. Crane."

"Apprentice Okafor." The title was official now—journeyman, actually, as of the anniversary; Crane read the council bulletins the way he read everything, completely. "You wanted something."

"To walk partway with you, if you don't mind. Wren sent a message about the follow-up interviews; there's a family on Tanner's Row that won't open for anyone but a clockmaker, and I'm to go Thursday." Hazel fell into step beside him, and for a dozen paces neither spoke. Then: "You certified the ledger. Everyone says you destroyed yourself doing it. Wren says that's sentimental and the truth is more complicated. Which is it?"

Crane considered the question with the seriousness it deserved, weighing each clause.

"The truth," he said, "is that I asked one question too many, and the answer unmade the questioner. What I did at the hearings was not destruction. It was correction. I had entered a false figure into the record, and a record-keeper's only remaining duty, once that is discovered, is to correct it—at whatever price the correction carries." He glanced sideways. "You of all people should appreciate the principle. It is the escapement, applied to conscience. A mechanism that releases exactly what must be released, no more, no less, so that the wheel may advance."

Hazel stopped walking. For a moment their face did something Crane had never seen it do in all his interviews—something unguarded, young, startled into openness.

"That's the strangest apology I've ever received," Hazel said.

"It was not an apology."

"No," Hazel agreed. "But it was the thing underneath one." They resumed walking, and the evening took them both forward along the darkening river, past the lamps coming on one by one, each flame a small witnessed keeping of the dark. At the corner where their ways divided, Hazel paused. "Thursday, then. Tanner's Row. Knock loudly. They listen for confident hands."

"I will."

"And Mr. Crane—" Hazel turned back, and the last light was behind them, so that Crane could not read the expression, only hear the voice, level and deliberate as a striking clock. "Next month the archive opens the public reading room. The first thing on display will be my parents' page. I'm asked to stand beside it. I'd rather have you standing on the other side."

Crane stood very still. Somewhere behind him the river ran, carrying winter out to sea, and ahead the lamps went on lighting themselves, patient as entries in a book.

"Why?" he asked—which was, he realized, the first question in months he had asked without already knowing the shape of its answer.

"Because the record should show both of us there," Hazel said. "What was hunted, and what hunted it. Kept side by side. Let it be kept that none may be unwritten—even the ones who have to learn, late, how to write."

They went on up the hill toward the observatory, and Silas Crane stood a moment longer in the lamplight, then took out his notebook, opened it to a fresh page, and wrote the appointment down: *Thursday. Tanner's Row. Confident hands.* Beneath it, after a moment, in smaller script, the second engagement—the reading room, the display case, the page in his own hand that had once condemned and now, corrected, would testify.

Two entries. Both binding. He squared the notebook's edges against his palm and walked home through the city that was learning, slowly, to keep itself.
