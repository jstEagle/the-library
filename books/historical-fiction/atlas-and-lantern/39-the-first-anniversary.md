## 39. The First Anniversary

*Wren*

The ice began to speak at dawn, exactly as her father had promised it would, every year of Wren's childhood, in the same low groan that traveled up through the observatory's foundations and set the pendulum weights humming in their cases. She stood at the north window with a cup of Marisol's chicory going cold in her hand and watched the river shrug off its white skin, and she thought: *on schedule*. After everything—the raids, the arrests, the historians with their civilized voices and their erasing pens—the river had kept its appointment. There was a lesson in that, she supposed, about what endured.

Behind her, the tower was already awake. Down on the second landing, the morning class was assembling: six apprentices this term, ages eleven to nineteen, every one of them unregistered until the day they arrived, every one of them entered into the new ledger within their first hour under her roof. She could hear Hazel's voice running beneath the clatter of them like a bass string—steady, amused, occasionally sharp when someone reached for a mainspring without eye protection. A year ago Hazel had climbed these nine hundred steps with a satchel full of everything they owned and a face like a person reading their own warrant. Now they moved through the workshop as though the place had been built around them, which, Wren thought, was not far from the truth.

She dressed, pinned her hair the way her father had liked it—out of her eyes, no nonsense—and went down to open the day's page in the ledger.

The book had outgrown its first binding. That had happened in autumn, when the entries from the old regime's hidden years finally met the flood of new names and the spine simply gave up with a crack that had made the whole room go silent, as if the building itself had gasped. The council's archivists—real ones now, appointed rather than imposed—had rebacked it in oxblood leather at no charge, and the clerk they'd assigned to assist had turned out to be Silas Crane, of all people, meticulous and quiet and unfailingly polite, who copied names in a hand so perfect it looked printed and never once, in four months, asked a question he didn't need to ask. Wren still watched him when he worked. She suspected she always would. But she had stopped flinching at the sound of his pen.

Today's page lay open under the lamp, and she read back over the week's entries the way other people said prayers. *Ash, Tomas, apprenticed at eleven, mother living, ferry district.* *Vael, Suri, apprenticed at sixteen, no kin listed, entered at own insistence.* Page after page, month after month, the ledger growing fat with people who had been told for their whole lives that they were weightless.

Her father had started this book to keep the government from succeeding at forgetting. She kept it now because forgetting, she had learned, was not only something governments did. It was something people did to themselves, quietly, to survive. Every name entered here was a small act of refusal, and refusals, repeated daily, became a practice, and practices became an institution, and institutions—well. Her father had died believing one man and one book could hold back a regime's historians. He had been almost right. It had taken two more people and a cook.

The clock above her struck seven, true time and official time together now, one strike, indivisible, and she heard the apprentices below cheer the way they did every morning, because children would make a festival of anything, and Wren found she did not mind at all.

* * *

*Hazel*

The watch sat on the mantel of the workroom where Hazel had placed it a year ago and never moved it: their mother's watch, the case back opened toward the ceiling so the light caught the engraving inside. *For the one who keeps us. Amara Okafor.* They had carried that watch closed for fifteen years, through two workshops and a hundred hideouts of the heart, and had needed a full night of lamplight and a jeweler's loupe to discover that their own name—or the shape of the life that would become theirs—had been written inside it all along. Some mornings they still stood before it like a person checking that a door they'd dreamed was real.

"Hazel." Little Suri Vael was tugging their sleeve, holding up a balance spring she'd blued herself, her whole face lit like a struck match. "Is it right? Look at the color. Is it right?"

Hazel looked. The spring gleamed a deep, even cornflower, the exact blue that meant heat applied patiently and withdrawn at the perfect moment. It was better than right. It was the work of someone who had decided, at sixteen, that she existed and intended to leave evidence.

"It's better than my first attempt," Hazel said, which was true, "and my third," which was also true, "and if you can cut the pivot to match we'll put it in the tower regulator and your name goes in the maintenance log."

Suri went very still, the way they all did, the first few times, when the record touched them personally. Then she nodded once, hard, and took up her graver, and Hazel stepped back to let the work happen.

That was most of teaching, Hazel had discovered. Not the demonstrating—though they demonstrated until their voice went hoarse—but the standing back. Making room at the bench the way Master Idris had once, in his better years, made room for a mixed-blood orphan nobody official would sign papers for. Idris himself was downstairs now, at the repair bench by the window where the light was best, doing slow, immaculate work on townspeople's watches, pardoned but never quite forgiven, calling Hazel *Master Okafor* in a voice that wanted forgiveness in it every time. Hazel had stopped correcting him months ago. Not because it was absolution. Because it was accurate, and accuracy, they had learned from a ledger, could carry more weight than mercy.

Through the window, past the benches and the bent heads, the river was coming apart in long grinding shelves, the ice groaning, splitting, sliding over itself with a sound like a giant turning in sleep. The whole class kept drifting to the glass to look, and Hazel let them. Some things you were required to witness. The year before, on the day the ice broke early, Hazel had run across it with forty pounds of other people's lives in a satchel while soldiers' boots rang on the bridge behind them. Today the same river was only weather. Today there was nowhere to run, and nothing to carry, and the strangeness of that still hadn't worn off.

Marisol appeared at the stairhead with a tray of rolls and the particular expression she wore when she was delivering food and intelligence in equal measure. "Ferryman's boy says the road will take wheels by noon," she announced. "Council session's confirmed for tomorrow, ten o'clock, and Crane sent word—" she paused, delicately, "—he says the archive has received a petition he thinks we should see. From downstream. Another city wants to start a ledger."

The workroom went quiet. Six apprentice faces turned toward Hazel, waiting, and Hazel understood—with a vertigo gentler than the old kind but related to it—that they were waiting not for permission but for meaning. For someone to tell them what they were part of.

"Then tomorrow we vote on it," Hazel said. "Tonight we eat."

* * *

*Wren*

They ate upstairs, in the round room under the clock, the way they ate every anniversary eve: the long table her father had built for instruments, pressed into service for people; Marisol's braided fish and the good wine Piotr hoarded against occasions; Old Piotr himself at the head, nearly blind now but still able to find any dish on a table by ear and smell, telling the story of the great calibration of '41 for perhaps the ninth time, improving it with each telling.

Wren looked down the table and counted her family the way her father had once counted stars. Marisol, twenty years invisible, now legally resident, still cooking for twelve though the household was six, because, she said, hunger had a longer memory than law. Piotr, whose impossible star-charts hung framed in the council hall now, and who pretended not to be proud, and failed. Idris, invited every year, attending every year, sitting at the far end every year, at the boundary of the light. And Hazel beside her, laughing at something Suri had said, the lamplight catching the edge of their jaw, and Wren felt the thing she had been feeling all year rise in her chest again and press against the place where she kept it.

The copying nights. That was when it had started, though neither of them had named it then. Months of them bent over the same lamp, shoulders touching, passing the true ledger between them page by page while the tower ticked around them like a vast patient heart. She had learned Hazel's silences the way she'd learned her father's mechanisms—by feel, in the dark, without instruction. She knew now which silence meant *concentrating*, which meant *grieving*, and which—the rarest, the one she lived for—meant *happy and trying not to show it*. She had catalogued them all and said nothing, because there had been a revolution to survive and a book to save, and because some part of her had assumed that whatever this was, it was load-bearing, and you do not tamper with load-bearing structures.

But the ice was breaking. The year had come all the way around. And Wren Aldous had inherited from her father a religion of records, and she was tired of keeping the truest one locked in her chest.

After the plates were cleared, she did what her family did with important things. She got the ledger.

* * *

*Hazel*

The room understood at once—six apprentices, Marisol, Piotr, Idris at his far end—as Wren set the great oxblood book on the table and opened it to the current page. This was the ritual now, the anniversary entry, witnessed by the whole house. Last year Hazel had written their own name in their own hand before the provisional council with their pulse hammering in their ears. This year the pen was just a pen, and the room was warm, and that difference was the whole victory, contained in a nib touching paper.

Wren entered the date, the ice-break, the council session pending. Then, instead of closing the book, she turned to a fresh leaf, and dipped the pen, and held it a moment without writing.

"I have an entry," she said, "that isn't for the census."

Hazel's heart performed a maneuver no escapement could have regulated.

Wren wrote, in her small exact hand, speaking as she wrote the way her father must have done through all those hidden years: "*Aldous, Wren, keeper, records that on the twelfth of Thaw-month, in the year after the Restoration, she enters into this book the only fact she has ever been afraid to write down.*" The pen paused. The fire popped. Somewhere below them the master clock turned its enormous patient gears, true time and official time geared together, asking nothing.

Then Wren looked up—not at the room, at Hazel—and finished aloud: "I love you. I have since the copying nights. I'm entering it on the record, because this book is where true things go so they can't be taken back."

The apprentices made a sound like a kettle coming to boil. Marisol said, "Finally," with the satisfaction of a woman who had been cooking for two people in love for eight months and running out of patience. Even Idris smiled, at the end of the table, in the half-dark.

And Hazel—Hazel, who had spent a childhood learning to measure the world in what could be carried, who had lied brilliantly to Silas Crane and hated it, who had testified before victors with a steadier voice than they'd known they owned—found that the only response available was the honest one. They laughed, wet-eyed, and reached across the ledger, and took Wren's ink-stained hand.

"Give me the pen," they said. "If it goes in the book, it goes in both our hands."

They wrote beneath her line, in their careful clockmaker's script: "*Okafor, Hazel, clockmaker, records the same fact, from the earlier date, and confirms the entry as accurate.*"

"*From the earlier date,*" Wren repeated, delighted. "You're dating my love confession in a municipal register."

"Everything in this book should be verifiable," Hazel said, "or it isn't worth the ink."

Piotr raised his glass to the room he couldn't quite see. "To the record," he said.

"To the record," said everyone, and the toast ran down the table to Idris at the end, who lifted his cup last and drank with his eyes closed, and Hazel, watching him, felt the old tangled knot of fury and pity turn one final degree toward something like peace—not settled, maybe never settled, but at rest, wound down, the way a mechanism rests when its work is done for the day.

Outside the window the river heaved and split and shed its winter entirely, ice grinding down toward the sea, and nobody in the room stopped talking to listen, because it was only weather now, on schedule, right on time.

* * *

*Wren*

Later, when the apprentices had been sent yawning to their bunks and Marisol had shooed the last of them up the stairs, Wren and Hazel stood together at the north window, the ledger closed between them on the sill like a sleeping animal, her shoulder against Hazel's shoulder, both of them watching the last floes ride the black water out of the city.

"Crane's petition," Hazel said quietly. "Another city. Do you believe it?"

"I believe Crane believes it. Which is nearly the same thing, these days." Wren turned the thought over. "But he enclosed a second letter. He does that—slips things in that aren't quite part of the official correspondence." She drew the folded paper from her vest and handed it over. "I've been waiting for the right moment. It seemed wrong to open it during the toast."

Hazel unfolded it in the window's moonlight. Wren watched their eyes move, once, twice, and saw the color change in their face—not fear, exactly. Recognition.

"What is it?"

"The petition isn't from downstream," Hazel said slowly. "It's dated three days ago, and it's countersigned by the historians' delegation. They didn't retreat last year. They regrouped." They looked up, and outside the ice boomed against the pilings like a bell. "They're requesting the ledger, Wren. For the national archive. Officially. With funding attached. And a condition—" Hazel's voice went flat and precise, the voice Hazel used for hard truths finely machined. "*All future entries subject to review.*

"They want to put it in a case," they said. "Glass, seals, a curator. Safe forever. Readable by appointment." A pause, the clock ticking through it. "They want to stop it, the way you stop a clock. Under glass it's a relic. On this table, with the pen in reach, it's alive."

Wren looked at the book on the sill—her father's book, her book, theirs now—and heard her father's voice saying, years ago, over a smaller mechanism: *a clock that isn't running isn't preserved, it's embalmed.* Below them the tower ticked on, nine hundred steps of stone and steel keeping faith with the sky, and tomorrow the council would convene at ten, and somewhere in the city a printer was already setting type, and the question that had kept her family for two generations had just changed its shape again.

"We'll vote tonight," she said, "before the session. All of us. The whole household." She picked up the ledger, and it was heavier than it had been that morning, or her arms were weaker, or love had gotten into it somehow. "Because if they want to put our book under glass, Hazel, then tomorrow we're going to need to teach the council the difference between a record and a reliquary."

Down in the dark, the master clock gathered its weights, and struck.
