## 1. What the Forms Leave Out

The river had a voice that winter, and it was not the voice of shells. It spoke under the ice all night, a low grinding syllable, like a man turning over in his sleep, and Juniper Thornbury learned to count its mutterings the way other nurses counted the pulses of the wounded. The dressing station stood forty paces from the bank, a converted granary with a stone floor that drank the cold and gave it back, and every morning she woke to the same arithmetic: how many men had come in overnight, how many had gone out, and how many would be written into the record as if they had never existed at all.

It was the third category that kept her awake.

The casualty forms came down from division twice a week, carbon-copied, initialed, correct in every particular except the truth. They listed the men who had died wearing regimental numbers. Everything else — the orderlies who went out under fire to drag in boys the stretcher-bearers wouldn't reach, the local women who carried water to positions no official map admitted existed, the deserters shot at the crossroads by men whose names their own officers never entered anywhere — fell through the paper like water through a net. The forms were designed, she came to understand, not to record the war but to record only that portion of it which could be survived by the men signing them.

She began with Pieter.

He had been an orderly for eleven months, a flat-faced Flemish boy of nineteen who could carry a stretcher all day on rations that would have felled a mule, who whistled tunelessly while he worked because silence made him think of the shelling. On the fourth of December he went out past the wire to fetch a captain of the 112th who was still breathing with most of his chest gone, and a machine gun opened on him from the mill ruins, and he did not come back until dark, and when he came back there was nothing left to write a regimental number on.

The form said: *one civilian male, unidentified, buried vicinity of station.* No name. No age. Not even the courtesy of *killed*, which was a word with weight behind it. *Unidentified.* As if eleven months of carrying other men's blood off the field had been the work of a fog.

Juniper copied his name onto the flyleaf of her ledger that night by candlelight — a plain shopkeeper's ledger, bought in better days for the tracking of flour and thread, its ruled lines now repurposed — and beneath it she wrote what she knew. *Pieter Maes. Nineteen years. Orderly, this station since January. Killed by machine-gun fire from the mill, 4 December, going out for Capt. Aldous of the 112th, who also died.* Then, because the forms had taught her what omission looked like and she wanted hers to look like nothing of the kind, she added: *Whistled while he worked.*

* * *

Marta found the page four days later.

There was no way to hide anything from Marta Kessel. She had hands like a laundress and eyes like a customs officer, and she could read a chart upside down across a ward faster than most doctors could read it right side up. Juniper looked up from changing a dressing and saw her standing at the foot of the cot where the ledger lay open in Juniper's kit, and saw her read three lines, and saw her face go through a whole weather system in the space of a breath.

"You should not have that," Marta said.

"It's a ledger."

"It is evidence." Marta closed the book and set her hand flat upon it, as if pressing something down that might otherwise rise. "Paper is dangerous, Juniper. You are writing down things that men with signatures have decided did not happen."

"They happened."

"Yes." Marta said it without heat, the way one confirms that winter happens, that shelling happens. "And when the thaw comes and this side changes hands or doesn't, someone will collect the papers of this station, and they will find a list of names that contradicts forty signed reports, and they will ask who kept it. And you will say *I did*, because you are honest, which is the most dangerous thing a person can be out here."

Juniper finished tying the bandage, smoothed the tail flat against the man's shoulder — a private, sleeping, lucky for now — and wiped her hands. "Then I'll keep it badly," she said. "In bad handwriting. In code."

"In what code?"

"The code of writing the truth plainly. Nobody checks for that. Nobody believes it."

Marta almost smiled. Almost. There was something underneath her disapproval that Juniper couldn't name yet, some seam of feeling held very carefully shut, like a wound packed but not sutured. "You think the men who write the reports are liars," Marta said. "Some of them are. Some of them are afraid, which produces the same result but forgives differently. And some of them sign what they are told to sign, and hate themselves in comfortable rooms afterward." She picked up the ledger, weighed it in her broad hands as though pricing it. "One day an officer will lie in your ward with a wound you have to decide how to treat, and you will know his name from these pages. Think about that before the book grows any thicker."

She set it down and went back to work, and Juniper told herself the warning was only caution, and knew even then that it was memory wearing the mask of caution.

That night she added two more names. Annette Vos, a woman of the village who had boiled water and bound wounds in her kitchen until a shell found the kitchen on the ninth; the report called her *collateral damage to a forward installation*, which was a phrase with no face in it, so Juniper gave it a face: *Annette Vos, fifty-one, baked bread for the ward every Thursday, refused payment, hummed hymns off-key.* And below her, a name Juniper hesitated over longer than any: Corporal Dane Harrow, who had walked away from the line on the eleventh and been brought back at dusk and shot at dawn under an order nobody would initial. The forms gave him half a line: *executed, disciplinary, see annex.* There was no annex. There was never an annex.

*Dane Harrow,* she wrote. *Twenty-three. Afraid, and honest about it, which the army cannot forgive. Shot 11 December. He asked me to write to his mother and I did not know what to tell her that was true, so I wrote nothing. I am writing it now.*

Her candle guttered. Under the floor the river ground its teeth, patient, keeping its own ledger of everything it swallowed.

* * *

The days shortened toward the solstice and the ledger thickened. It changed character as it grew, Juniper noticed — stopped being a project and became a companion, then a conscience, then something closer to a passenger she could not put down without it screaming. She wrote in it during the hour after the evening rounds when the ward slept and the orderlies smoked by the stove. She wrote in fragments, in pencil when candles were short. She developed rules without deciding to: dates always, ages when known, one detail of the living person for every fact of the death. A voice. A habit. A joke. Something that would let a mother, a wife, a sister recognize the man and not merely the corpse.

Word travels strangely in a dressing station, by glances and half-heard sentences and the angle of a lamp. Within a week, men were finding her. An orderly lingered at the end of a shift and said, quietly, that his cousin had gone missing at the weir crossing in October and appeared on no list anywhere, and could she — and Juniper could, and did. A sergeant described his brother, last seen being carried toward the second aid post before it was overrun. She took them all down. The ledger stopped being her testimony and started being everyone's, which made it heavier and made it necessary, which are the same property in different light.

And it made it dangerous in exactly the way Marta had said. Because now it wasn't only her neck. Now there were men in the ward who knew the book existed, and knowledge spreads outward from a dressing station like dye in water, and somewhere upstream a staff officer's clerk would hear it eventually, and clerks talk to officers, and officers burn things.

She caught herself hiding it then — inside a folded blanket, under the false bottom of her kit — and hated the hiding and did it anyway. That was the shape the thing had taken. Comfort and threat in one binding, indistinguishable, the way morphine was comfort and threat. You did not stop needing it because needing it might kill you.

On the nineteenth of December, the shelling moved closer to the river, walking down the far slope in slow deliberate strides, and the wounded came in all night, and by dawn the granary smelled the way it always did and the forms arrived as they always did, and the tally on them was eleven men, and Juniper counted thirteen dead with her own hands between midnight and six.

Two names the forms left out.

She wrote them standing up, at the washstand, the ledger propped against the basin, ink from the communal bottle she was not supposed to touch after hours. She was on the second name when she felt the ward go quiet behind her — that specific hospital quiet, attentive, the quiet of people watching — and turned.

Captain Ellery Voss stood in the doorway, supported between two stretcher-bearers, pale as the ice on the river. His coat was stiff with old blood and new. He was looking not at her face but at the ledger against the basin, and she knew the cover of that book from reports she had handled for months, because his signature sat at the bottom of the retreat dispatches, the neat, upright hand that had erased forty men from a single October week — and now the hand that had signed those omissions was bleeding in her doorway, and his eyes said he knew exactly what she was holding.

"Nurse," he said. His voice was steady the way cracked things are steady right up until they fail. "I'm told you keep excellent records."
