## 2. The Officer Who Counted Twice

The shell that brought him in came at dusk, when the light through the tent canvas turned the color of old bone. I heard the stretcher party before I saw them — the particular rhythm of men carrying a load they expect to die, slower than urgency, careful as thieves. Then the flaps opened and the cold came in with them, and Captain Ellery Voss was laid on the table nearest the stove as if someone had finally decided to stop pretending he wasn't going to be trouble.

I knew his face before I knew his name. That is an honest thing to admit, though honesty was becoming a habit I couldn't afford. His photograph had been in the divisional gazette eight months ago — a citation for the holding action at the quarry, a lean jaw and eyes that looked at the camera the way men look at weather they intend to walk into anyway. The face in the flesh was thinner. The eyes were worse.

"Fragment, left shoulder," the senior bearer said. "Walked half a mile on it, then didn't."

"We walked everybody half a mile and then some," Voss said. His voice was dry gravel, unhurried, the voice of a man who had decided pain was a clerical matter to be dealt with in due course. "Some of us further than others."

Marta cut away his tunic while I prepared the line of morphine. The wound was ugly but not mortal — a red star of shrapnel above the joint, meat bruised and torn but the architecture beneath intact. He would keep the arm. He would keep everything, which was more than could be said for what he'd carried out of wherever he'd come from, because his pockets, when Marta went through them for the intake form, produced a map case, a waterlogged notebook, and a casualty report folded so many times it had gone soft as cloth.

I saw the report before she set it on the shelf with his effects. I saw the signature.

*E. Voss, Capt.*

The same hand that had signed the return from the Kell retreat in October. The same slanted capitals, the same impatient crossbar through the *V*. I had spent three nights of my life with that signature, comparing it against itself, telling myself I was imagining the resemblance between one officer's penmanship and another's, because the alternative was that the man who wrote forty names out of existence was still alive, still signing things, still walking around in the world with both his arms attached.

Now here he was on my table, and his arm was attached, and I was the one holding the needle.

"Count backward from ten," I told him.

"I'd rather count forward." He watched my hands work. "Ten's depressing. Everything after it is less."

I gave him the morphine anyway. Not enough to put him under — the wound needed cleaning and the cleaning needed him awake and braced — but enough to soften the edges of the world. He bore the probing without sound for a long time, and then, when I lifted the second fragment free and he had to breathe somewhere other than through his teeth, he said, conversationally, as if continuing a discussion we'd been having for years:

"You're the one who writes the extra list."

My hands did not stop. I have been proud, all my life, of my hands' refusal to betray me. They kept working — swab, forceps, the small bright dish where the fragments rang like coins — while the rest of me went very cold and very still.

"I don't know what you mean."

"The orderlies. The deserters shot at the crossroads in November. The two women from Pell who were bringing eggs to the forward posts and caught the barrage on the embankment." He recited it flatly, like a man reading a laundry list, and each item landed in me like a stone dropped down a well. He had done his own counting. He knew exactly what the forms left out, because he had been the one holding the eraser. "Sister Thornbury. I've seen you at the burial details with your little book. You think nobody notices a woman writing in a war. Everybody notices. It's just that nobody cares until it matters."

"And does it matter?" I asked. "To you?"

He was quiet while I irrigated the wound. The stove ticked. Somewhere beyond the canvas a transport ground past, wheels complaining, and a man shouted something about chains. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a world that had agreed on its own version of events.

"It mattered in October," he said at last.

"Forty men," I said. I hadn't meant to say it aloud. I had meant to keep it, the way I kept everything, in the ledger's safe dark. But the morphine had loosened something in the room besides him. "The withdrawal from the Kell crossings. Forty-one, actually, if you count Lieutenant Ames, whom your report lists as captured when the field burying parties logged him as killed the same week. Your report has them straggled. Dispersed. Unaccounted for — which is not a category, Captain, it is an absence wearing a uniform. Forty men went into that retreat and your paper says they mostly came out, scattered across units, reassigned, absorbed. I have written to four of those units. None of them ever received a Captain Voss's forty ghosts."

The fragment dish sat between us with its little metal harvest. He looked at it rather than at me.

"You've done your homework," he said.

"I do everyone's homework. That's rather the point of me."

For the first time he turned his head and looked at me directly, and I understood that whatever the morphine had softened, it had not touched this. His eyes were entirely sober and entirely exhausted, the eyes of a man who had been carrying a weight so long his body had grown around it.

"Sister." He said it quietly, pitched under the sounds of the ward, under Marta's movement at the far table, under the wind working at the guy-lines. "Burn nothing."

Not *I can explain.* Not *you're mistaken.* Not even *it wasn't like that.* Just: burn nothing. Three words, and the way he said them made clear they cost him more than the shrapnel had.

"Why?" I kept my voice level. "If there's nothing to find."

"Because paper is patient." His gaze went to the stove — the iron door, the orange seam of light beneath it — and then came back to me. "Because you're keeping a record, and records get audited, and audits get people moved to posts where the winter is longer and the mail doesn't come. Because whatever you think you know about October, you know it from the outside, and the outside is wrong in ways that will get you hurt if you write them down carelessly." A breath. "And because some of those forty names are only alive on paper anywhere at all because of how the report was written. Tear it up and you don't correct history. You just kill them twice."

"That," I said, "is the most elaborate defense of a lie I have ever heard."

"Yes," he said. "It is. I rehearsed it for two months. It's better than the true one."

"And the true one is?"

He closed his mouth. The line of it went hard, and I watched a decision happen behind his face — or rather watched a decision fail to happen, watched the man arrive at the door of confession and stand there with his hand on the latch and then, slowly, step back. What he said instead was:

"Clean it again tomorrow. It'll fester otherwise."

"That's not an answer, Captain."

"No," he agreed. "It's a wound. I'm told you're good with those."

* * *

He slept badly, which surprised me; I had expected the sleep of the justified or at least the sedated. Around two in the morning I found him awake, propped on his good arm, watching the ward by stove-light. The wounded breathed in their rows. Frost ferned the inside of the canvas. I was doing what I always did after hours — the ledger open on my knee in the circle of a shielded lamp, adding the day's uncounted: a private of the 118th dead of his wounds whose paperwork still said *recovering*; a Belgian boy of perhaps sixteen who had drifted into the ward, worked as an unpaid orderly for a month, and been taken by pneumonia no form would ever mention because he had never officially existed.

I felt Voss watching the pencil move. I did not look up.

"My brother kept accounts," he said, into the quiet. "Grain merchant, up north. He used to say every ledger balances eventually — you just don't get to choose the currency." A pause. "He'd have liked you. He'd have told you to run."

"From the ledger or from you?"

"From me, probably. I'm the part that compounds."

I closed the book. In the dimness his face was all shadow and suggestion, and I found — this is the part I have never managed to write cleanly, in any draft, in any decade — that I did not hate him. Hatred requires a certain simplicity and he had none. He had signed the lie and he knew what the lie cost and he had walked half a mile on an open shoulder to bring the evidence of it, waterlogged and creased soft, directly into the hands of the one nurse in the sector mad enough to be keeping the opposing account. Men do not carry their guilt that far by accident.

"Why did you really come here?" I asked. "There are hospitals closer to the railhead. There are hospitals with heat that works."

"There are hospitals," he said, "where nobody reads."

* * *

Morning brought the routine cruelty of rounds, and with it a development I did not expect: Voss asked for work. Said lying flat would drive him mad, said his right arm worked fine, and commandeered a stool at the admission desk updating the ward's card index with a diligence that bordered on penance. He wrote steadily for two hours, and I watched him do the thing I could not decide whether to admire or dread: he handled the names. All of them. He spoke each one under his breath as he filed it, a syllable or two of restitution per card, as if he could pay down the October debt at a penny a name.

At noon he called me over, ostensibly about a misspelled regimental number. His voice stayed at desk-height, pitched for me alone.

"When the commission comes through after the thaw — and it will come, they're assembling one already, historians and staff colonels, men who will decide what October *means* — they'll want tidy papers. Tidy papers travel well. Messy ones drown." His finger rested, lightly, on the folded casualty report Marta had shelved with his effects. He did not pick it up. "Everything you need is in that report and in what's missing from it. The margins. Read the margins against the dates. And when you understand it — burn nothing. Promise me that first. Promise before you know why."

"Captain—"

"Promise," he said, "or I'll take this shoulder to another hospital and you'll spend the rest of the war wondering."

I looked at him for a long moment. Outside, the river lay grey and solid under its skin of ice, holding its breath, waiting for spring the way we all were. I thought of Marta's warning — *paper is dangerous, Juniper, paper gets people shot* — and of the forty-one names sleeping in my pages, and of the strange, unbearable fact that the man who erased them was sitting at my desk saying their numbers under his breath like a rosary.

"Burn nothing," I said. "I promise. Now tell me why."

But the answer never came that day, because the answer arrived instead by courier at fourteen hundred hours — an envelope bearing command's seal, addressed to the Chief Medical Officer, marked *URGENT — SEASONAL*, and the ward went to its feet around me as matron tore it open, and the first word I saw over her shoulder, in the bureaucratic calm I would come to dread more than any barrage, was *evacuation*.
