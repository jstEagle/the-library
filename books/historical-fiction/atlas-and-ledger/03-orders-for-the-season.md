## 3. Orders for the Season

The orders arrived on a Tuesday, in the same canvas satchel that brought the rations and the ammunition returns, as though the evacuation of forty-one souls were a matter of inventory equal to tinned beef.

I was changing a dressing when the corporal came through with it, boots crunching the frozen sawdust in the doorway. He read the dispatch to Matron in the passage, loud enough that we all heard it through the canvas walls: *Evacuation of forward medical assets to commence upon first hard frost. River crossing to close at such time. All documentation to be reconciled prior to transfer.*

*Reconciled.* I had learned by then what that word meant. It did not mean counted. It meant made agreeable.

Marta looked up from the basin she was filling and caught my eye across the ward. Neither of us said anything. You learn that much, in a year at the river — how to hold a conversation entirely in the space between your faces, where no one else is listening.

The frost came within the week. It came in the night, silently, the way everything important comes, and by morning the mud outside the dressing station had gone grey and hard, printed with boot-marks like fossilized speech. The men complained of their teeth chattering while being operated on, and we ran the stove day and night until the canvas smelled of coal and iodine and the sweetish rot beneath both.

And then Major Hessler's clerk appeared with a wooden crate and instructions.

"Paperwork," he said, setting the crate on my table beside a tray of forceps. He was a young man, pink-cheeked, apologetic in the way of someone who has decided his job is not his fault and has never once examined that decision. "All ward records to be reviewed for accuracy before transfer. Anything redundant, duplicative, or outside authorized formats goes into disposal."

"Disposal," I repeated.

"The brazier." He indicated it with his chin, as though pointing out a lavatory. "Matron has the schedule. Casualty lists reconciled against regimental returns. Nothing to cross over but what matches."

Nothing to cross over but what matches.

He left the crate and went, and I stood looking at it for some time. In it were my own forms — the ones I filed properly, the official ones, the lies written in my own hand because the truth would not fit in the boxes provided. Beside them lay the other papers, the ones I had not been meant to keep: the orderlies' names, the deserters shot behind the orchard wall whose bodies were logged as *missing*, the two local women who had carried water to our wounded for three days and been killed by the same shell that spared the tent, entered nowhere at all because they had never been on anyone's strength.

And underneath all of it, wrapped in a spare shirt at the bottom of my footlocker, the ledger itself. Sixty-one names now. I had stopped counting the nights I woke to add one.

* * *

That evening Marta found me at the wash-tent, wringing out bandages in water so cold my hands had gone past pain into something abstract.

"They'll burn it," she said. No preamble. We had moved beyond preambles months ago.

"They'll burn the paperwork. They don't know about the ledger."

"They'll search the footlockers. They always do before transfer. You know that."

I did know that. The searches were ritual — brisk, performative, aimed at liquor and letters containing coordinates. A plain exercise book might pass unnoticed among dressings. But it might not. And if it did not, I could not finish the arithmetic of what followed, because I had never let myself do it.

"So I make copies," I said.

Marta set down the cloth she'd been folding. "Copies."

"If there are three of a thing, it takes more than one fire to end it." I hadn't planned the sentence; it came up from somewhere below thinking, some sediment of my grandmother's voice, who had buried her church silver in the garden twice in her lifetime and dug it up twice more. "If the ledger goes into a brazier, or into a river, or into a clerk's pocket, the names still exist somewhere else. That's all I want. For them to exist somewhere else."

"And where does the somewhere else live? On you? You'll be searched. On me? Same search." She shook her head slowly. "Juniper. Paper is dangerous. I told you in October and I'm telling you again with less time left to say it."

"You told me. And I've kept writing anyway. So help me do this better instead of telling me to stop."

She looked at me a long moment across the steam rising off the washtub. Behind her, through the tent flap, I could see the river — flat, steel-coloured, already wearing its first skin of ice along the reeds. Somewhere past it, the bridge. Our single thread to the rest of the world, scheduled to be cut at an hour some major would decide over coffee.

"All right," she said finally. "But not here, and not tonight, and not stupidly. If we're going to do it, we do it like scrubbing a floor. Boring. Thorough. Nobody notices a clean floor."

It was, I think, the most practical act of love I have ever witnessed, agreeing to that.

* * *

We worked over four nights.

The method was Marta's, and it was beautiful precisely because it was dull. The ward received a new consignment of bandage wrappers that week — heavy paper envelopes, machine-stamped, sealed, indistinguishable one from another. Nobody counts bandage wrappers. Nobody has ever counted bandage wrappers in the history of armies. So each evening after lights-out, by candle stub inside the double thickness of the linen store, I copied the ledger's entries onto sheets torn from a duplicate order book — thin paper, half-size, made to be carbon-copied and forgotten. Name. Unit if known. Date. Manner of death or disappearance. Where the body lay, if I knew, which I often did; I had made a private geography of that ground, the way other women made scrapbooks.

Then each sheet went into a wrapper. Then the wrappers went back into the consignment crates, mixed among hundreds of their identical sisters, to be shipped out with the regular stores ahead of the evacuation — ordinary supplies, bound for the base hospital, unremarkable, unsuspicious, carrying sixty-one dead men and women folded into paper like seeds.

"The wrappers get opened eventually," Marta pointed out the second night, holding a sheet up to the candle to check my handwriting against the original. "Someone unpacks these."

"Yes. Someone unfolds a wrapper, finds a name, and either throws it away or doesn't. It's a chance."

"It's a thin chance."

"Every chance at this table is thin." I took the sheet from her, compared it, nodded. "The ledger is the certain copy. This is the scattering. If the certain copy survives, fine. If not—" I blew out more breath than the sentence required. "Then perhaps one wrapper, opened by one bored quartermaster two hundred miles from here, is the whole of the monument."

Marta was quiet awhile. The candle guttered; outside, wind moved along the guy-ropes with a low note like a wet finger on glass.

"My brother's name isn't anywhere," she said at last, not looking at me. "You know that. Not on a form, not on a stone. He was struck from the rolls for the crime of walking away from a position that had already killed everyone around him, and the record of him ends with a word I can't even say anymore." She folded my copied sheet with the exactness she used on dressings. "So when you ask me whether a name on loose paper matters — don't ask me. You already know what I'll tell you."

"I wasn't asking."

"No," she said. "You weren't."

On the third night Captain Voss nearly caught us.

He was walking again by then, unevenly, the wound in his thigh having decided against killing him. He came to the linen store door well after midnight — I heard his stick on the frozen ground too late to douse the candle entirely — and stood in the entrance with the light striping his face, looking at the order-book open on the table, at my pen still moving, at Marta's hands full of wrappers.

For a moment none of us breathed.

Then he said, quietly, "The third drawer of the records crate sticks. Lift it toward you as you pull." And he turned and walked back across the frost, his shadow long and crooked in the moonlight, without asking what we were copying or why.

I sat very still afterward, my heart going hard against my ribs.

"He signed those reports," Marta whispered. "Forty men, Juniper. His signature erased forty men, and he knows it, and now he knows about this."

"Yes," I said.

"Aren't you afraid of him?"

I thought about it honestly, the way I tried to think about everything at that table. "I'm afraid of what he wants from me," I said. "Which is worse than being afraid of what he might do."

* * *

The fourth night we finished. Sixty-one names, three times over: once in the ledger, once in eleven wrappers bound for the base hospital, once in a packet Marta sewed into the lining of her own coat with surgical thread and a surgeon's patience.

I closed the last wrapper, stamped it with the ward's mark, and stacked it in the crate with the others, and felt nothing whatever that I had expected to feel. No relief. No triumph. Only the sensation of having swallowed something cold that would sit in me, unmelted, for a very long time.

Because that morning the notice had gone up on the ward board, typed, official, trimmed square:

*Frost confirmed. Bridge crossing will CLOSE upon receipt of further orders, anticipated within ten days. All personnel and material not across the river by closure date remain the responsibility of forward command until spring.*

Until spring. Read plainly, it meant: whoever is on the wrong side when the span closes winters where they are, with what they have, under whatever shell-fire the thaw brings. The army phrased things gently. It did not phrase them falsely.

Ten days. Perhaps fewer; the further orders might come tomorrow. And the crates of bandage wrappers were not scheduled to ship for six.

I stood at the ward board in the grey light reading the notice a third time, and beside me Matron sighed and said what everyone was saying, that it would be fine, that there was ample time, that the engineers had promised the bridge sound until the appointed hour.

Across the yard, coming up the path from the river, walked Captain Voss — faster than his wound should have allowed, leaning on his stick, his face the colour of the ice.

"Miss Thornbury," he said, reaching me, breathing hard. "You need to hear this before it comes through channels. The engineers inspected the piers this morning." He lowered his voice. "There's damage from the October shelling nobody reported. The bridge is going to close early."
