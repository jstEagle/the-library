## 4. Frost on the Wire

Marta had learned to read hands before she learned to read faces. Faces lied at the front; hands couldn't afford to. Juniper Thornbury's hands, that third week of November, were telling Marta everything her mouth would not.

She watched them now across the dressing station — quick, sure hands wrapping a corporal's forearm, and then, when they thought no one was looking, drifting to the pocket of Juniper's apron where the small black ledger lived. A touch. A press of fingers, like checking a pulse.

Marta knew what lived in that pocket. She'd known for a month.

"You count them," Marta said, later, in the lean-to where they boiled bandages over a spirit stove. Steam between them. Frost furring the canvas seams like white mold. "The ones the forms leave off."

Juniper's hands went still above the kettle. That was answer enough.

"I don't know what you mean," Juniper said.

"Orderlies. The two local girls who carried water up the ridge and never came back down. The deserter from the Silesian regiment we buried by the alders without a tag." Marta kept her eyes on the water. "You think I haven't noticed you writing at night? You turn the page the same way every time. Like a woman saying grace."

The stove hissed. Somewhere out on the river the ice boomed — a long, tectonic sound, the water shifting under its lid. Everyone flinched at it the first week. By November they barely heard it.

Juniper sat down slowly on the packing crate they used for a stool. She looked older in the lean-to's bad light, and younger too, somehow — the fatigue stripping everything but the stubbornness underneath.

"If you're going to tell me paper is dangerous," she said, "get in line. You've told me twice already."

"I'm going to tell you something worse than dangerous." Marta wrung out a bandage, hung it. Her own hands doing their honest work while her mouth did the hard thing. "I kept a list once. My own."

Juniper's head came up.

"Spring of '15. A clearing hospital outside Verdun, worse than this one if you can believe it." The words came out flat, rehearsed by years of silence. "I wrote down every man who died who wasn't in the returns. There were so many. Men who arrived with no papers, men the system lost, men whose deaths would have embarrassed someone with rank. I wrote names, units when I could get them, wounds, dates. Forty-one names by summer."

"And?"

"And I gave it to a chaplain I trusted. He was going to take it to the diocesan office, get it into church records at least. Something. Anywhere." She heard her own voice go very steady, which was how she knew she was close to not being steady at all. "The convoy he traveled in was hit outside Bar-le-Duc. Everything burned. My list, his letters, him. All of it gone in ten minutes of German artillery."

* * *

Juniper didn't say *I'm sorry*. Marta was grateful for that. Sorry was a coin everyone spent and nobody wanted.

Instead Juniper reached into her apron pocket and set the ledger on the crate between them. Opened it. Turned pages with those careful grace-saying hands until she reached the newest entries, and Marta read them by stove-light:

*Kowalski, Stefan, stretcher-bearer, 3rd Field Amb. Died 9 Nov. Shell gas. Buried near the pump house. No service.*

*Maria Vasilievna. Anna Vasilievna. Sisters, water carriers, age unknown. Missing 14 Oct after the ridge barrage. Local. No unit to report them to. No one to write to.*

*Private Emil Brandt, deserted 2 Oct, shot as example near the crossroads — no. He wasn't shot. He was wounded, then left. Left is the word. Wounded, then left, 2 Oct.*

There was a blot there, as if the pen had been held too long against the page.

"You crossed out *shot*," Marta said.

"I wrote it wrong the first time. Or I wrote it true and softened it the second." Juniper closed the ledger but didn't put it away. "I don't know which frightens me more."

"My brother is on somebody's list like this," Marta said. "Not yours. Yours only goes back to October. Tomas died in March of last year, at a place the war has already forgotten the name of." She fed another bandage to the boiler, watched it sink. "The official return says he fell *with distinction*. Fourteen words about a battle. His name isn't among them. The division that abandoned his position filed a report saying the position was never held."

Juniper went white. Not dramatically — just the blood leaving, quietly, like troops withdrawing.

"They said never held."

"Said it in writing. Commendations for the men who pulled back. Tomas and thirty others stayed because the order to withdraw came late, or came never — depends which rumor you believe — and the report needed the retreat to look like a maneuver. So the dead became a fiction." Marta shrugged, and the shrug cost her more than shouting would have. "I was nursing in Rouen by then. I got his kit in the post four months later. Someone else's paybook inside. The clerk couldn't even keep the effects straight, because Tomas was never really dead, you see. Dead men require paperwork, and Tomas had been erased before he stopped breathing."

The ice boomed again, closer, or maybe just louder in the silence.

"So when I saw you writing at night," Marta said, "I knew exactly what you were. I've been you. And I know how you end: with ash and a convoy outside Bar-le-Duc."

"Then why are you telling me instead of reporting me?"

"Because I'm done losing lists." Marta turned from the boiler and looked at her straight on. Juniper Thornbury had a spine like a tent pole — you could see it holding her up even sitting down. "One copy dies when one truck burns. One copy dies when one matron gets curious. If you're going to do this mad thing, you'll do it properly. Duplicates. Separated. Carried out of here by more than one pair of hands."

"You're volunteering."

"I'm insisting. There's a difference and the difference is that I don't care whether you agree." But something in Marta's chest cracked open as she said it, some door she'd kept shut since the spring of '15, and through it came an ache so clean it was almost relief. "Tomas gets in your book or I'll write him in myself."

"He's already in," Juniper said quietly. "Page twelve. I interviewed a gunner in October who served near his sector. I've been carrying your brother since before you caught me at it."

Marta had not wept since the kit came in the post. She did not weep now. But it was close, for a moment — close as the wire to the ward, close as the frost to the window glass.

* * *

They worked through the evening, copying.

It was Juniper's method and it was clever, Marta gave her that: pages copied onto thin tissue stock, folded into squares, and slipped inside rolled bandage wrappers in the supply crates. Paper hidden inside paper. A commission searching for documents would find dressings, and dressings were sacred; no officer wanted to be the man who unrolled gauze while wounded men waited. They worked by shielded candle, Juniper writing, Marta reading the source aloud in a low voice, name after name after name, a litany, a rosary of the erased.

Forty-three entries. Then forty-four. Marta's voice held.

At half past eleven the raid came.

No warning worth the word — a flare, then the stutter of machine guns along the wire, then shells walking in from the east with the casual confidence of a visitor who knows the house. The ward tent took shrapnel across its northern corner. The lights failed. In the dark there was only the order to move the wounded to the dugouts, and the wounded crying out, and the smell of earth and blood and cordite, and Marta hauling a stretcher with her arms screaming while somewhere behind her Juniper shouted a name — her own name, *Marta*, *the ledger*, *where are you* — and Marta shouted back that it was in the crates, the crates were in the store lean-to, and then a shell landed close enough that God himself ducked, and the world was noise.

By two in the morning it was over. The raiders had probed the wire and withdrawn; the colonel's staff called it a reconnaissance in force, which meant they still didn't know either. The ward reassembled itself in the wreckage the way it always did, orderlies sweeping glass, wounded counted, the dead — three of them — laid out under blankets to wait for forms that would say nothing true.

Marta found Juniper kneeling at the opened supply crates, going through bandage wrappers with hands that shook for the first time since Marta had known her.

"Count them," Juniper said hoarsely. "Please. I can't — my eyes won't —"

Marta counted. Folded squares of tissue, each one a life, each wrapper a grave marker made of linen. Thirty-one. Thirty-two. Thirty-three.

Thirty-three.

"There should be thirty-six," Juniper whispered. "We finished the Kowalski entry and both sisters. Three pages tonight."

They searched. They searched the crates, the floor sweepings, the burnt corner of the ward tent where the wind had thrown things, the mud outside, the stretcher straps, the pockets of the three dead men — Marta doing that last part herself so Juniper wouldn't have to. Nothing. One page, gone. Three names dissolved into a November night: Kowalski, and Maria Vasilievna, and Anna Vasilievna, the sisters who had carried water up a ridge and vanished, erased now twice over.

"It could be anywhere," Juniper said. "It could be under a stretcher. It could have blown into the wire."

"It could be in someone's pocket," Marta said, because someone had to say it. "A looter. A raider. Some orderly who picked it up thinking it was nothing and will use it to wipe his boots."

Juniper sat back on her heels in the wrecked light of the store lean-to, and Marta watched her decide something — watched it happen the way you watch ice decide to crack.

"Then we move faster," Juniper said. "Before anything else walks away. Before the evacuation orders come and they start simplifying paperwork — and they will come, Marta, you mark me. Command never leaves a paper trail pointing backward." She looked up, and her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely level. "How much do you trust the chaplain at the crossing station?"

"Not at all," Marta said. "Which is why I'm asking what he carries."

Outside, far off toward the bridge, searchlights swept the clouds, and the river boomed again beneath its thinning ice — longer this time, deeper, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.
