## 5. The Bridge Closes

The shell took the bridge at four minutes past two in the afternoon, and by half past we all knew the season was over.

I heard it before I understood it — a sound like a door slamming in a very large house — and then the concussion came through the ground and up through my boots and rattled the instruments on the shelf. Marta grabbed the table. A bowl of forceps slid and she caught it against her hip without looking, the way you catch things in a ward, your body trained past your mind.

"Span," someone said from the doorway. "They've hit the span."

We went out into the cold because everyone always goes out to look, that's the truth about people no one puts in the reports. The bridge stood at the bottom of the slope like something drawn by a child who had run out of ink. The middle third of it sagged, timbers splintered upward, and through the gap you could see the river — grey, thickening, edged already with ice plates along the shallows.

"The engineers said first frost closes it," Marta said beside me. She meant the official closing. The paperwork closing. The crossing would be condemned long before anyone risked timber under load again.

"That wasn't first frost," I said. "That was arithmetic."

She looked at me then, and I watched her do the same sum I was doing. Wounded who couldn't walk: eleven. Walking wounded: perhaps twenty, if walking meant what we generously allowed it to mean. Staff: three of us now, with Pell gone back over the water a week ago and not replaced. And one bridge that no longer reached.

"We're on the wrong side," Marta said quietly.

"We've been on the wrong side since spring. Now it's just official."

* * *

Voss heard it too. He was propped in the corner cot nearest the stove, the one we'd given him less out of rank than out of the unspoken agreement that he required watching. His wound was doing well, which is an ugly thing to think about a man whose health improves your problem rather than solves it, but there it was. The ball had gone through his shoulder in September. By November he could sit up for hours. He used the hours to watch us work, and to watch the river, and to say nothing at length, which is a skill some officers perfect.

Now he said, "Nurse Thornbury."

I crossed to him. Up close he had the smell of men who have been in a bed too long — carbolic and wool and underneath it something like paper left in damp.

"You heard?" I said.

"I heard." His eyes went past me to the window, to the broken line downstream. "How long does the ice take?"

Marta, folding lint three cots away, went very still. Not dramatically. She simply stopped moving her hands, which from Marta was the loudest thing she could do.

"Cold nights this week," I said carefully. "The shallows are holding already. The main channel, another fortnight if the weather holds. Less if it turns."

"And if it doesn't hold?"

"Then nobody crosses."

He nodded slowly, as if I'd confirmed something he'd been carrying around since morning. Then he did an extraordinary thing. He looked at Marta, deliberately, and said, "Sister, would you check the stove."

"The stove is fine, Captain."

"I'd be grateful anyway."

She looked at me. I gave her the smallest nod, though God knows why I trusted him, except that I had read his signature forty times by then, in reports that subtracted men from the world the way a clerk subtracts column from column, and I had begun to believe that a man who carried that signature in his kit everywhere he went — and he did; I'd seen the folder — was a man carrying something toward a destination.

Marta withdrew to the far end of the ward. Voss waited until the stove door clanked shut on her pretense.

"There were eighty-one of us on the ninth of August," he said. "When the retreat started. You've seen the returns."

"Forty-one," I said. "The report lists forty-one effectives withdrawn."

"Yes." He said it without flinching, which I think cost him more than flinching would have. "Forty names never existed. Not killed — killed goes in the book, killed has a form. These men were never born, so far as the army is concerned. Conscripts from a sector command that didn't want its losses counted in a month when losses were being audited." His good hand lay open on the blanket, palm up, as though offering me something invisible. "I didn't compile the list. I signed the return. My signature makes it true."

"Why are you telling me this now?"

"Because the bridge closed today, and yesterday I still believed I'd be evacuated, court-martialed quietly somewhere safe, and buried in the machinery before anything I said mattered." He turned his head and looked at the window, at the sagging span. "The bridge closing is the first honest thing that's happened to me in months. It means I'm not going anywhere unless I choose the manner of it."

"You could die on this side," I said. "You know that. If they come across the ice before we do—"

"If they come across the ice," he said, "they will find a dressing station full of wounded men and three nurses, and none of that will interest them. What interests them—" he touched the folder beneath his pillow with one finger, not taking it out, just marking it, the way you'd mark a page "—is paper. And I intend that when the paper moves, it moves across the river in daylight, in the open, with my name attached to every word. No more omissions. Starting with mine."

I stood there with my pulse in my throat. Somewhere behind me Marta had stopped pretending about the stove; I could feel her listening the way you feel weather change.

"You understand what you're saying," I said. "Testimony isn't protection. It's a target."

"It's a ledger entry," Voss said. "It's the only kind I can still make."

* * *

That evening the cold came down like a verdict. The thermometer on the porch post — we kept our own records too, everything unofficial in this place — fell from minus six to minus fourteen between supper and midnight, and the wind found every board in the building and interrogated them one by one.

Marta and I sat at the table with the lamp turned low and the duplicate pages spread between us, and I will tell you what claustrophobia actually is, because it is not small rooms. Claustrophobia is a large room that has become an island. It is knowing that the world continues on the far bank — the commission assembling its historians, the archivists sharpening their appetite for verified accounts — while yours contracts to the radius of a lamp flame.

"They'll send boats before the ice takes," Marta said. "They have to. Wounded priority."

"They'll send boats for officers," I said. "The rest go over the ice or wait for spring, and spring means the commission arrives on our bank with its forms."

"So we don't wait for spring."

"No," I said. "We don't wait for spring."

She turned a page toward her, one of the duplicates, wrapped in its bandage roll. Her brother had died in an omission once — she'd told me that much, in October, in the dark, in the voice you use for things you've decided only to say once — and I watched her look at these names, strangers' names, orderlies and deserters and the women from the village who'd carried water under fire and vanished from every record, and I watched her decide they were all her brother.

"The channel ice won't hold a stretcher party for weeks," she said. "Maybe not at all, this early."

"Then we go light. Split the loads. The worst cases stay warm until the last possible night."

"And Voss?"

"Voss walks," I said. "And carries."

* * *

Near dawn the shelling started again, distant and unhurried, working over something downriver, and Voss called weakly from his cot and I went to him thinking the noise had hurt him and instead he was sitting fully upright, grey-faced but upright, folder on his knees.

"Nurse Thornbury," he said. "How thick does the ice need to be? For a man on foot. In numbers. Real numbers, not comfort."

"Six inches for a file of men, spaced. Ten for a stretcher with bearers. That's the engineers' figure, and engineers are optimists about their own materials and pessimists about everyone else's."

"How many nights to ten inches, at minus fifteen?"

I did the sum. We both did. The shelling downriver punctuated it like a clock.

"Four," I said. "Five to be sure. And Captain — the ice doesn't care about being sure."

"No," he said. "Nothing out here does. That's rather the point." He laid his hand flat on the folder. "Five nights. Tell Sister Brandt. We go on the fifth, fog or moonlight, whatever the sky gives us. And Nurse—" he caught my sleeve as I turned, gently, the first time he had ever touched me "—when we reach the far side, whatever happens to me, the pages go to the archive. Not to an officer. Not to a committee. To the archivist himself, by hand. Promise me the routing."

"I promise the routing," I said.

He let go of my sleeve and lay back, and outside the shells walked their slow way along the river, patient as winter, as though the guns knew what we had just decided and were content to wait for the ice with us.
