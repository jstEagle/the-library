## 6. The Commission Arrives

They came with the thaw's first false morning, when the river still held but the air had gone soft enough to make you distrust it. Juniper heard them before she saw them — engines, two of them, laboring up the far bank road where no supply convoy had run in three weeks. She was folding bandages at the ward window, and she watched a grey staff car and an open lorry crest the rise trailing a smell of petrol that reached even across the water.

Marta appeared at her shoulder. "Commission," Marta said. She'd seen their kind before, in another sector, another year. "Historical. They'll have brass buttons under the greatcoats. Men who write books."

"Books are good," Juniper said. "Books keep things."

Marta looked at her the way she looked at a wound that was worse than the dressing admitted. "Their kind of books keep what they're told to keep."

The commission crossed at midday in a pontoon boat because the bridge — closed, holed, its middle span sagging like a broken wrist — would not bear even their weight of ceremony. There were four of them: two archivists with leather cases chained to their wrists, a photographer whose camera on its tripod made the wounded flinch as if it were a new species of gun, and a major named Aldous Renwick, who introduced himself to Juniper with a handshake that lasted precisely as long as propriety required.

"You kept the station running through the winter," he said. It wasn't a question. He'd read something. He had the particular smoothness of a man who has read everything about you before you've been asked anything. "Remarkable. The army will want your account."

"My account is in the ward book," Juniper said. "Admissions, discharges, deaths. All of it."

"All of it," Renwick repeated pleasantly, and smiled, and Juniper felt the smile go through her like cold water finding a seam.

* * *

They set up in the ruined customs house on the far bank, which meant the interviews happened over there, which meant the wounded who could walk were ferried across to give their testimony and ferried back, and those who couldn't were interviewed where they lay. The archivists worked in pairs, one asking, one writing, and everything went into the record in a hand so beautiful it looked like forgiveness.

Juniper watched from the doorway of Ward B while a private from the 112th told his story for the third time, polishing it with each telling, adding a bayonet charge he had almost certainly slept through. The archivist wrote it all down without correction. Verified, Juniper thought. That's what verified means here. It means written down by someone whose handwriting is better than yours.

Captain Voss sat propped against the wall of the far cot, his wound dressed, his face greyer than his bandages. He had refused the ferry twice. When the archivists came to him he gave his name, rank, unit, and said: "I was at the retreat from the crossing in October. I can account for the men of my company."

One of the archivists — the younger one, a soft-bodied man with ink-stained fingers — looked up with genuine interest. "You were at Harrow Bend?"

"I was."

"Then you'll want to see this." He turned a ledger toward Voss with the tenderness of a priest displaying a relic. "The official after-action summary. Your name appears in the commendations."

Voss read it. Juniper, pretending to check a dressing two cots down, watched his face do very little, which was how she knew it was doing a great deal. Forty men. She had counted them herself, in October, in the mud below the embankment, and written every name in her own ledger while the official report recorded a fighting withdrawal with minimal losses. Forty men who had been left on the wrong side of a collapsed pontoon bridge, some alive, calling out across the water until the shelling stopped them.

"The numbers seem low," Voss said quietly.

"The figures were reconciled against muster rolls," the archivist said, with the serenity of a man reciting scripture. "Discrepancies were resolved."

"Resolved," Voss said. "Yes."

He caught Juniper's eye across the ward. He didn't say anything. He never said anything outright; that was the arrangement between them, unspoken since the night she'd recognized his signature on the report and he'd recognized that she recognized it. *Burn nothing*, he'd begged her, meaning the ledger, meaning himself, meaning perhaps both.

* * *

Renwick came to the station at dusk, alone, which was itself a message. Officers did not come alone to places like this unless they wanted something off the record, and everything about Renwick was a man who understood records better than most priests understood God.

He stood at the window a moment, looking at the river going dark. "It will break within the week," he said. "The ice. You understand you're on the wrong side of it."

"I'm aware."

"The ferry runs tomorrow. After that, nothing crosses until the water drops in April. Perhaps May." He turned. He had a kind face, Juniper thought, the way a well-bound book has a fine spine; the contents were another matter. "Sister Thornbury. I've read your ward book."

"Then you know more than your archivists bother to ask."

"I know you count things." He said it without accusation, almost with admiration. "Orderlies. Local women. Men not on any muster roll. I imagine there are others." He let the silence sit. "I'm not here to take it from you. I'm here to offer you a place in the record."

Juniper set down the bandage she was rolling. "Go on."

"When we cross back, there will be room in the car. Yours, if you wish it. Safe passage, priority transfer, a posting away from this bank before the ice goes. And your ward book enters the archive entire — every admission, every death, under your own name as contributing witness." He smiled again, that cold-water smile. "That's more than most nurses get in a lifetime. A named contribution to history."

"And in exchange."

"In exchange, the record confines itself to what you witnessed directly. Wounds treated. Deaths certified. The retreat in October falls outside your direct observation — you were at the forward station that night, weren't you? Two miles east?" He shrugged gently. "Secondhand accounts muddy an archive, Sister. We've found they invite dispute, and disputed records serve no one. Not the families. Not the dead."

"Not the dead," Juniper said.

"No one serves the dead," Renwick agreed. "We serve the living, and the living need a clean history. Forty men or thirty-nine or none — the war doesn't change. But a nurse who smuggled accusations into an official archive, that changes things. That gets pages sealed. Whole volumes. You wouldn't be protecting those men. You'd be burying them deeper than I ever could."

It was a good argument. That was the obscenity of it. He'd built it well, load-bearing in every joint, and standing there in the lamplight with the river muttering under its ice, Juniper felt the whole weight of it settle on her: safe passage, a named page, her work preserved in vellum and buckram, against a word like *secondhand* that could dissolve forty lives into fog.

She thought of Marta's brother, though Marta hadn't told her yet — she only knew there was a brother, the way you know a house has a locked room. She thought of Voss reading his own name among the commendations, his face doing nothing at all.

"You'd want my silence," she said. "Not just my omission. You'd want me to stand at your table and certify the record as complete."

"I'd want you to sign what you saw," Renwick said. "Nothing more honest than that."

"The retreat isn't secondhand to me." Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "I pulled six of those men out of the water below the embankment at dawn. Three died on my table. I wrote their names myself. If that's secondhand, Major, then your archive is a rumor."

Something moved behind Renwick's eyes — recalibration, quick and professional. "Six men," he said softly. "Out of forty."

"Forty-one," Juniper said, and stopped, because she had said too much, and knew it, and saw him know it. The official figure was nothing; the unofficial figure was nothing; only her ledger held the true arithmetic, and now she had confirmed to the enemy of the truth that such an arithmetic existed.

Renwick nodded slowly, as if she'd passed an examination. "Tomorrow the ferry leaves at ten. My offer stands until it casts off." He put on his cap. At the door he paused. "For what it's worth, Sister — I hope you take it. The river won't negotiate, and neither, after tonight, shall I."

* * *

He was gone and the ward was dark except for the stove, and Juniper sat with her hands flat on the ward book as if holding down a lid. Marta came and stood beside her, saying nothing, which was Marta's fluency.

"He offered me the archive," Juniper said. "My names, printed, permanent. For the ones he wants erased."

"And you told him about the six."

"I told him about the forty-one."

Marta was quiet a long moment. Then: "Then he knows the ledger exists, and roughly how big it is, and that you'll die on this side of the ice rather than burn it." She said it without drama, an inventory. "Good. Now he'll search us properly."

Outside, the wind had turned, coming up the valley from the south, warm and wrong for February, and beneath the floorboards, faint but unmistakable, the river began to speak — a long groan, then a crack like a rifle shot, then silence, then another crack, closer to the near shore.

Juniper looked at the dark window, at the reflection of the lamp and her own face ghosted over the moving black water beyond.

Ten o'clock, Renwick had said. The ferry leaves at ten.

And the ice, she thought, may not wait for either of us.
