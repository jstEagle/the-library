## 7. Names in the Bandages

Marta heard the lorries before she saw them — engines labouring up the frozen track from the south, two of them, maybe three, with the particular careful slowness of men who did not want to be surprised by what they found. She was folding bandages at the window and her hands stopped without her permission.

The ward behind her was quiet in the way it had been quiet for three days: forty wounded men breathing in uneven rhythm, one stove ticking, and Juniper at the far table with her head bent over a dead soldier's kit, sewing.

"Marta." Juniper didn't look up. "You've gone still."

"Lorries." Marta kept her voice level, the way she'd learned to keep it over four years of carrying things to men who would die of them anyway. "From the commission, I think. Renwick said they'd come."

Juniper's needle paused mid-stitch. The grey blanket over Private Aldous Reeve — dead six days, awaiting transport whenever transport decided to come — rose and fell with nothing beneath it, and under that blanket, if anyone looked closely, which no one should, there were pages where a body ought to be.

"How much longer?" Marta asked.

"Twenty minutes. The canvas takes the thread badly." Juniper bent again. "Go and meet them before they meet us."

Marta went out into the white morning, pulling her coat closed as she walked. The air smelled of thaw — mud and iron and something green that had no business existing in February. Up the valley the river ran black in its channel of ice, groaning at intervals like an animal in a trap. She had grown up on a river. She knew that sound. It meant the water underneath had woken and wanted out.

The first lorry crested the rise. Three vehicles, olive-grey, unmarked except for the stencilled crest of the Historical Commission — a laurel around a quill, which had always struck Marta as the funniest joke the war had made. A captain climbed down from the lead cab, then a civilian in a good wool coat, then four enlisted men with blank faces and empty satchels.

Satchels for carrying documents out, Marta thought. Not for bringing anything in.

"Good morning." The civilian spoke French-accented English and smiled the way officials smiled when they already knew your answers. "I am Monsieur Delacour, of the Commission for the Preservation of Campaign Records. You are the matron?"

"Sister Brandt." *German name, let them chew on it.* "Acting matron. Our matron was evacuated before the bridge closed."

"Ah, yes, the bridge." He said it lightly, but his eyes went past her to the ward, counting windows, counting exits. "An unfortunate complication. We are here on a matter of some importance to history, Sister. The official record of this sector must be consolidated before the thaw. There are forms missing. Reports that were never filed or were filed incorrectly." He produced a paper from his coat. "We are authorised to collect all campaign documentation held at this station. All of it. And to search for — forgive the phrase — unauthorized documents."

Unauthorized. Marta felt the word go through her like a splinter entering skin: not deep, but finding the nerve at once.

"Of course," she said. "Our records are in order."

"I'm delighted to hear it," said Delacour, who plainly believed nothing of the kind.

* * *

They worked methodically, which was the worst of it. The enlisted men went through the records cabinet page by page while Delacour interviewed her at the table, unhurried, courteous, asking each question twice in slightly different words. Marta answered everything she could honestly and everything else with the flat competence of a woman too busy for nonsense. She had been doing paperwork under shellfire since 1915; she knew how boring she could make herself seem.

"The casualty returns for October," Delacour said. "The retreat across the northern ford. I show a dressing station receiving ninety-one wounded on the twelfth and reporting sixty-one transferred."

Marta counted linen in her lap and did not let her hands pause. "Transferred, died, and miscounted," she said. "You've never run a station under fire, monsieur. Men arrive without papers. Men leave without them. On the twelfth of October we were shelled twice during transfer and half my clerks were bleeding."

"So the discrepancy is ordinary confusion."

"War is ordinary confusion, monsieur. Everything else is what people write afterwards."

He smiled at that, thinly, and wrote it down, and she understood with a cold slide in her stomach that she had just said something quotable, and that he might be one of those rare dangerous men who collected the wrong sentences.

Across the ward, Captain Voss lay propped against his pillows watching the search with the stillness of a man reading a room for exits. His wound was healing crooked and he favoured his left side when he moved, but his eyes moved fine. When the enlisted men reached the shelf where the ward's transfer folders sat in their neat false simplicity — Juniper's three days of work, every number reconciled, every omission made to look like clerical sloppiness rather than design — Voss coughed, once, wetly.

"You there," he called, in the cracked parade voice of an officer. "The third folder. It's mislabelled, I signed it myself in a hurry, the dates inside are wrong. If you're consolidating records you'll want it flagged, not filed."

The soldier hesitated. Delacour came over. For a moment the folder hung in the balance of it, and Marta folded the same bandage twice and thought: *he is drawing their eyes to the lie we built so they won't look for the truth underneath, and if they open that folder and read carefully, it hangs all three of us.*

Delacour flipped through it, frowning, then handed it back. "Flagged," he said. "Thank you, Captain. May I ask how you came by your wound?"

"A retreat," said Voss pleasantly. "The one everyone keeps asking about."

The silence lasted two seconds. Then Delacour smiled and moved on, and Marta exhaled through her nose, slowly, and hated him for making her grateful.

* * *

At noon the search reached the dead.

It was routine — effects inventoried, personal papers confiscated for "consolidation" — and Marta walked beside the corporal as he worked down the row of men waiting for transport, because that was where she had to be. At Aldous Reeve's trolley she stopped, lifted the blanket at the corner, and let her face do what faces did.

"He's been dead six days," she said quietly. "Gangrene, then pneumonia. I wrote his mother myself. Do you need me to unpack him? Because I will, if the record requires it, but I'd rather not, and neither would you."

The corporal looked at the body — at the stiff yellowed feet, the smell that six days made honest — and shook his head. "Just the tags and papers, Sister."

She gave him the tags. She gave him the letter from the mother, unsent, which was real, which she had written. She gave him Reeve's pay book and a photograph of a girl outside a brick house, and the corporal entered it all in his inventory and moved on, and beneath the blanket, sewn into the canvas of the burial wrapper with sutures Juniper had learned on living flesh, lay eighty-one names, two duplicate sets of casualty figures, and the truth about the northern ford.

Marta pulled the blanket straight, tucked it under Reeve's shoulders the way she tucked it under every man, alive or otherwise, and stood a moment with her hand flat on the canvas.

Her brother had been an omission once. Erich, twenty-two, listed as *missing, presumed captured*, in a report his own captain signed with a comfortable signature on a comfortable day. There had been no capture. There had been a field, and an accounting that didn't balance, and a family told a soft word instead of a hard fact. She had been nineteen. She had kept a list afterwards, in the back of her prayer book, of every name she heard spoken in wards and canteens that the paperwork didn't hold — and when the prayer book wore out she had burned the pages herself, alone, terrified, at a stove exactly like the one now ticking in this ward, and she had never forgiven the burning, and she had never stopped hearing the names.

Not this time. This time the names would cross the river if she had to carry them in her teeth.

* * *

She found Voss awake after the lorries left, staring at the ceiling.

"You lied well," she said, sitting on the stool by his bed.

"I lied adequately. They'll check the dates eventually." He turned his head. "Which means whatever your plan is, it happens soon. Before they come back, before the thaw, before Renwick's ferry."

"There's a plan."

"There's always a plan." He looked at his own hands on the blanket — clean hands, officer's hands, the hands that had signed. "Sister Brandt. Marta. I need to tell you and Thornbury something, and you're going to want to argue, so let me finish first."

She waited.

"When we cross — if we cross — the ledger isn't enough. Names prove men existed. They don't prove who erased them." He said it flatly, without self-pity, a man reciting inventory. "Delacour's commission wants a clean history because clean histories protect the men who ordered the ford abandoned with the rearguard still on the wrong side. I know because I wrote the report that buried it, and I know who dictated it, and I can prove the dictation, because he sent me written instructions afterward to confirm the figures. I kept the instructions. Sentimental, I suppose. Or I always knew I'd want them someday."

"Voss—"

"They offered me silence for passage. I know they did. They'll offer again." His voice stayed level, but his knuckles had whitened on the blanket. "If I present testimony, they arrest me. Not maybe. Certainly. A serving officer contradicting the record in front of a victors' commission — that's not whistleblowing, that's mutiny with paperwork. I'll spend the rest of the war in a cell, if I get a rest of the war."

"Then don't—" Marta stopped. She had been about to say *don't do it*, and she heard, in her own mouth, the exact voice that had once told a nineteen-year-old girl to burn her pages and stay safe, and she could not finish the sentence.

"Don't what?" Voss asked softly.

"Don't let them talk you out of it at the last minute," Marta said. "That's all. That's the only thing I have to say."

He nodded slowly, and something went out of his face that had been in it for days — not fear exactly, but the anticipation of fear, packed and ready. In its place came a kind of settled exhaustion that looked almost like peace.

"Tell Thornbury I'll sign tonight. Full confession, dated, witnessed. She'll want it in her own hand alongside the original." He closed his eyes. "And Marta — the man in the burial wrapper. Tell her I'm sorry he has to travel like that. And tell her the ice won't wait for ten o'clock. I grew up on a river too."

* * *

Evening came early, the way it did, and the ward filled with lamplight and the small sounds of men being made comfortable for night. Marta carried the supper tray down the aisle past Reeve's trolley, past the flagged folder in the cabinet, past the place where eighty-one names slept in a dead soldier's seams.

Then she stopped, halfway down the ward, because from outside — faint, rising up through the floorboards, through the stone, through the soles of her shoes — came the river again. Louder now. Closer. Not a groan this time but a long tearing crack that ran the length of the valley, followed by a boom like distant artillery, followed by shouting from the sentry post by the bank.

A runner burst through the door, snowmelt streaming off him, hardly pausing to salute.

"Ferry's cancelled! Ice is moving at the narrows — whole span of it, breaking up from the southern bend! Command says the crossing is closed until—" he caught himself, and Marta watched the sentence die in his face and be replaced by the true one. "Until it freezes again. If it freezes again."

Behind her, somewhere down the ward, she heard Voss say, very quietly, to no one: "Tonight, then. Or never."

Marta set down the tray. Her hands, she noticed, were perfectly steady.
