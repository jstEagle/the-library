## 8. Over the Ice

The fog came up from the river like something breathing.

Juniper had watched for it three nights running, standing at the wire with her breath freezing in her collar, and on the third night it rose — a grey wall thick enough to hide a cathedral, let alone three figures and two stretchers. She went back inside the dressing station and touched Marta's shoulder in the dark.

"Now," she said.

Marta was already awake. She had been awake for days, Juniper suspected. There was a particular stillness to people carrying too much, the way a full cup is held steadier than an empty one.

Voss got himself upright without help, which cost him more than he let show. The wound in his side had closed to an angry seam, but the fever came and went, and his hands trembled in a way that had nothing to do with cold. He pulled his greatcoat on over the bandages and said nothing, which had become his whole character since the bridge closed. Confession had emptied something out of him. What remained was quiet and stubborn and, Juniper thought, not entirely certain he deserved to survive the night.

He would find out on the ice.

They wrapped the wounded in blankets and then in sacking, anything to muffle sound. Private Ansel, nineteen and half-dead of his belly wound, they lashed to a stretcher because he could not walk and could not be left — not because the commission on the far bank wanted him, but because Juniper did. That distinction had come to matter more than any order she had ever received. The second stretcher carried Corporal Reyes, unconscious, his leg splinted with slats from a crate.

The ledger went into four pieces. Marta carried half the duplicate pages sewn into the lining of her coat — she had done the sewing herself, muttering the whole time about needles meant for flesh — and Voss took the rest against his chest, strapped where the wound was, so that if anyone searched him they would find the bandage first. Juniper carried the original. It rode under her sweater, bound flat against her ribs, and when she breathed deeply she could feel its corners like the edge of a second conscience.

They went out through the latrine trench, past the wire where the frost hung in white beads, and down the bank to the ice.

The river had frozen in layers this year, freeze and thaw and freeze again, so that the surface was a history of itself: clear bands and cloudy ones, seams of old open water sealed shut like healed wounds. Juniper had crossed rivers before. She knew what the books said about spreading your weight, about spacing yourselves, about listening. The books did not say what she actually felt, which was that the ice was a held breath, and every step was a small argument with it.

The fog swallowed them at ten paces.

* * *

They had agreed on a route in the afternoon light: straight out from the willow snag, angling downstream toward the far bend where the channel ran shallow over gravel and the ice, Marta judged, would be thickest. Marta had grown up on a river in the east; she read ice the way Juniper read forms, scanning it for what was written underneath. She went first, testing each stride with a long iron bar she'd taken from the ruined pontoon pile. *Tap. Tap. Step.* Her voice came back through the fog in a low counting rhythm, almost a song.

Twenty paces out, the river began to speak.

It was not cracking, exactly. It was groaning — long, tectonic sounds that rolled beneath their feet and off across the water, the sound of a thing enormous adjusting its weight in its sleep. Juniper felt it in her knees. Ansel stirred on the stretcher and moaned, and she stopped, and Voss behind her stopped, and for a moment the four of them stood pinned in the fog like figures in a photograph, waiting to see whether the world would permit them to continue.

It permitted them. They continued.

Juniper counted steps to keep from thinking. At forty she lost count and started again. The stretcher poles cut into her palms. Ansel had stopped moaning, which was worse than the moaning; she made herself believe he was sleeping. Ahead, Marta's tapping went on, steady as a pulse, and Juniper fixed on it the way you fix on a lamp in weather.

Then the pulse stopped.

"Marta?"

"Here." A pause. "Ice is thinning. Feel it going soft. Bear left — no—" The word broke off. When Marta spoke again her voice had changed, flattened into the tone Juniper had heard her use over men who were dying: total attention, no room left in it for comfort. "There's open water ahead. Black. I can see it moving."

"How wide?" Voss asked.

"Ten meters. Maybe twelve. The fog's off it here — warm water steaming." Another pause. "We can go around upstream or—"

The groan came again, longer this time, and under it a new sound: a thin, high singing, the noise ice makes in the last hour before it fails. Somewhere out in the white, the river shifted its shoulders.

"We go around," Juniper said.

"It'll take time we may not have. The thaw—" Marta began.

And then, out of the fog behind them, from the near bank, came a sound that decided everything. A shell, falling somewhere behind the dressing station they had just left. The flash lit the fog orange, a brief stained-glass glow, and the boom rolled across the river and the ice answered it with a crack that ran past them like a whip.

"They're shelling the ward," Voss said quietly.

"There's no one in the ward," Juniper said.

"There soon will be. And the bridge span—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to. If the far pier took a hit, the crossing they'd planned around the bend might land them in open water anyway, and the fog would lift eventually, and the commission's boats were coming with the thaw regardless. Every hour belonged to someone else now.

Marta's voice came back through the white. "Around, then. Single file. Juniper first with the stretcher, I'll break trail."

"You've been breaking trail all night."

"And my brother drowned because somebody else went first," Marta said. "Move."

* * *

They went around the open lead by walking a wide arc upstream, and the arc nearly killed them twice. Once the ice flexed under the stretcher and water welled up through a seam and ran black across the surface in a slow fan, and they stood motionless until it stilled. Once Voss went down — not through, just down, his bad leg folding — and Juniper heard him swear in a language she didn't know, low and continuous, while Marta came back for him and hauled him up by the coat with a strength that had nothing to do with her size.

At the head of the lead, where the water narrowed to a channel maybe six feet across, the ice was thinner but bridged, snow-crusted, untested. Marta tested it. Tap, tap. It rang dull.

"It'll hold a person walking soft," she said. "Not a stretcher. Not at once. We go one by one, and the stretchers go around the long way — there's thicker ice thirty meters up, I can feel it."

"I'll take Ansel around," Voss said. "You three go across and I'll rejoin—"

"You can barely stand."

"I can walk thirty meters flat," Voss said, and there was something in his voice Juniper hadn't heard from him before — not guilt, not apology. Command. Whatever else the war had burned out of Ellery Voss, it had left this ember. "Reyes goes across with Juniper. I'll meet you at the willow line on the far side. If I don't—" He looked at her, and finished it anyway. "Burn nothing."

He took the stretcher and went, angling away into the fog, and Juniper watched him until the white closed over him and hated herself a little for how much she wanted him to make it, and why.

Marta crossed first with the iron bar. Then Reyes, dragged on the second stretcher by Juniper and one of the orderlies' abandoned sledge-runners Marta had rigged. Then Marta came back for the final trip — half the duplicates still against her body, the pages she'd sewn in with her own hands.

That was when the singing started again, everywhere at once.

The ice began to fail in the middle distances, out where the fog hid specifics. Cracks ran under their feet like sentences being spoken too fast to follow. Marta didn't hesitate; that was the terrible thing, she simply turned and went back out onto the river alone, toward the black water, because somewhere out in that direction Ansel's stretcher had hit thin ice — they heard it, the splintering cough of it, Voss shouting something lost in fog — and she moved the way she had moved through four years of wards, toward the worst sound in the room.

Juniper never saw the moment the ice opened. She saw only the aftermath: the hole in the world, black and steaming, and Marta beside it, and the stretcher sliding, and Marta's hands — those hands that had sewn names into linings and closed the wounds of strangers — pushing the stretcher up onto solid ice, giving it the last of her weight, her balance, her everything.

Then the river took her the way it takes things: without malice, without ceremony.

Juniper was moving before she knew it, flat on her belly, arm out. She caught Marta's wrist. The cold coming off that hole was like a door opening onto nothing. Marta's other hand found the ice edge and her fingers — gloved, thank God, gloved — scrabbled for grip, and for one full second Juniper believed.

"The pages," Marta said. Her face was very calm. Water was already filling her mouth on the second word. "Juniper. The pages. Say them."

"No. No, pull—"

"*Say them.*"

And because it was the last order Marta Brandt would ever give, Juniper said them — names, dozens of names, the orderlies and deserters and local women, the forty men of the retreat, her voice cracking across the ice while her grip failed and the river pulled, and Marta smiled once, briefly, the way she smiled when a man she'd given up for lost sat up on the table alive.

Then the ice edge broke under Juniper's elbows, and the river was stronger than grief, and Marta was gone. The coat lining with its sewn pages went with her, ink dissolving even now into all that dark water, half the record returning to the thing that had made the omissions necessary in the first place.

Voss found Juniper on her hands and knees on the far bank, sobbing without sound, the original ledger still strapped against her ribs. Behind them, across the river, the fog was beginning to lift, and through the thinning white she could see lanterns moving on the opposite shore — orderly, official, unhurried.

The historical commission had arrived early.

"Can you stand?" Voss asked.

Juniper wiped her face with a frozen hand and got to her feet. Half the names were at the bottom of the river. The other half were still hers to carry.

"Then walk," she said. "We're late for the historians."
