## 9. The Historians' Table

They received us in a requisitioned schoolhouse two hundred yards from the water, in a room that still smelled of chalk despite the maps nailed over the blackboards. The commission had hung bunting. Someone had decided that history required bunting.

I stood at the long table with the ledger wrapped in Marta's spare apron against my chest, and I waited while three men finished a conversation about luncheon.

The one in the middle — Colonel Ashe, by his collar, though he'd introduced himself only as chairman of the Sub-Commission on River Crossing Operations — had the particular smoothness of a man who had spent the war nowhere near anything that could bleed. To his left sat a captain with a typewriter and the anxious posture of someone who would rather be anywhere else. To his right, apart from the others at his own smaller table, sat the archivist.

He was younger than I expected. Thin, ink-stained cuffs, spectacles mended with wire. He had before him a stack of folders tied in government string, and he was not looking at the colonel or the bunting or the window. He was looking at me, or rather at the shape of the parcel under my arm, with an expression I recognized because I had worn it for months over my own ledger: the look of a man who suspects paper is lying to him and cannot prove it.

"Miss Thornbury." Ashe consulted a card. "Matron of the Number Seven dressing station, correct. The commission appreciates your service. We're collecting verified accounts for the official history of the winter operations. If you'll describe conditions—"

"I have conditions," I said. "Written down. Forty-one pages of them."

The captain with the typewriter looked up. Ashe did not.

"The commission works from verified testimony," he said, "cross-referenced against unit records. Personal diaries are valuable color, of course, but—"

"These aren't diaries." I unwrapped the ledger and set it on the table, and the sound it made — wet pages, swollen binding, the river still in it — was louder than I expected. "These are names. Two hundred and six of them. Orderlies, deserters shot without trial, local women who carried ammunition until the shells found them. Every man the casualty returns marked *missing* who was never missing at all. They were erased, Colonel, and I wrote them back."

The room went very quiet. Outside, a supply wagon ground past; the ice on the river boomed once, deep down, like something turning over in its sleep.

Ashe picked up the ledger the way one handles evidence in a case one has already lost. He turned perhaps four pages. His face performed nothing at all, which told me everything: he had known what was in reports like Voss's. He had probably countersigned them.

"This material," he said mildly, "reflects on the conduct of officers during active operations. Unverified reflections. You understand that we cannot simply—"

"He verified it." I took the second bundle from my coat — oilcloth, bandage-wrapped, dry as prayer. "Captain Ellery Voss. He signed the false return after the retreat. Forty men struck from the record so the division's accounting would balance. This morning he signed this."

Voss sat in the corridor outside, under guard — technically his own escort, practically a prisoner waiting to be made one formally. He had written the confession in my hand's stead because mine shook too badly, and he had signed it with the same neat signature I had first seen on the erasure itself. There was a symmetry there I did not enjoy.

Ashe read the confession all the way through. When he finished, he set it down and aligned its edges with the table's edge, a small precise violence of a gesture.

"Captain Voss," he said, "is a wounded officer in shock, whose statement will naturally be reviewed by the appropriate tribunal before any—" He stopped. Recalibrated. Smiled at me with real warmth, which was worse than the alternative. "Miss Thornbury. You've been through a great deal. The crossing alone — we heard the ice went out under you. The commission is prepared to see you properly commended. Rest, a posting well behind the lines, whatever assistance—"

"You're offering me a trade," I said.

"I'm offering you recognition."

"For my silence."

"For your *perspective*," he said gently. "The history must be usable, Miss Thornbury. A nation cannot fight wars if its histories read like indictments. Those men you mourn — the record can honor their service without the... complications."

"The complications are the service," I said. "That's the whole of it. That's every word in that book."

* * *

It was the archivist who broke the silence, and he broke it not with argument but with procedure, which I came to understand was his native language.

"Colonel." He rose, folder in hand. "Regulation Nine of the commission's charter requires that all documentary materials recovered from operational zones be entered into the provisional record pending review. Entered. Not adjudicated. Not weighed. Entered." His voice was quiet and completely steady. "If we begin sorting documents by how convenient they are, we are not historians. We are clerks of the victors, and the record will be worth exactly what that's worth."

"Mr. Hale," Ashe said, and his warmth evaporated entirely, "your mandate is to preserve, not to editorialize."

"Precisely," said Hale. "Preserve."

They looked at each other across the bunting. I understood then that I had crossed the river twice — once over the ice, and once here, into a different country where men fought with folders and charters, and the casualties were true things buried in filing cabinets. Hale was fighting the same war I had fought in the dressing station, page by page, except his trenches were regulations.

Ashe turned to me last, and I watched him decide how much of a scene he could afford in front of the stenographer.

"Consider what you're doing," he said. "You'll make yourself unemployable to every bureau that matters. The men named in that book have families who believe tidy lies. You'll hand grief a shovel and tell it to dig up its dead."

"Some of those families," I said, "wrote to me. Care of the station. Asking. For years they'll ask, and the returns will say *missing*, and missing is a wound that never closes because nothing is buried in it." I put my hand flat on the ledger. "You may keep your commendation, Colonel. I kept the names. That was always the exchange."

Silence again. Then Ashe gathered his gloves.

"Minute it," he said coldly to the stenographer, "as received and referred for review." And to me: "History is longer than you think, Miss Thornbury. So is memory, among men like myself."

He left. His footsteps went down the corridor past Voss without pausing, which frightened me more than any threat.

* * *

Hale opened a fresh folio and began, and I stood beside him and watched.

He worked the way Marta used to work a ward — no wasted motion, everything touched once and properly. He entered the ledger as Document 44-C, Provisional Series. He entered Voss's confession as supporting deposition. And then he began the thing I had crossed a river for, the thing Marta had died in the black water holding half of: he copied the names into the register itself, in his small even hand, one by one.

Alois Brandt, orderly. Vera Sostek, carrier. Private R. Emmen, shot at the wire, listed as deserter, was not.

Over the officer's objection. Over the colonel's cold little minute. Into the record.

He reached the middle of the second hundred and stopped. His pen hovered.

"These pages," he said quietly. "There's tearing here. Water damage. Were there more?"

"Half went into the river," I said. "With a nurse named Marta Reiner. She was carrying duplicates." My voice held, mostly. "She kept a list once herself. Her brother is somewhere in the omissions these reports made. She's not in anyone's ledger yet."

Hale wrote: *Marta Reiner, nurse. Died in the crossing, date as above.* Then he looked up at me over the wire-mended spectacles. "We should note her name was preserved by testimony. Yours."

"Note that half of hers drowned," I said. "The record should know what it doesn't hold."

He noted it. I watched him do it, and felt the strange arithmetic of the morning settle: two hundred and six names safe, forty restored by confession, one added for the woman who taught me that paper is dangerous and worth it anyway. And beneath it all, the shadow I couldn't write away — the names no one survived to speak, the pages in the river, the men whose erasure had been so complete that even I never learned they existed. Vindication, it turned out, was just another form of inventory. You counted what you had and knew the count was short.

Hale tied the folio with government string and set it with the others, and it looked very small on the shelf. One ledger among folders. Paper against paper.

"Will they bury it?" I asked. "Referred for review — I know what that means."

"It means it exists now," Hale said. "Ink doesn't refer itself for review, Miss Thornbury. It's in the registry, and registries get consulted — in ten years, in fifty, by some clerk or scholar or widow who pulls the folder and reads. All I can promise is that the door stays findable." He hesitated. "There will be a hearing regarding Captain Voss. Within the week. The tribunal will want you as witness."

Down the corridor, through the frosted glass of the door, I could see two military policemen taking up positions outside the room where Voss waited, and I understood that Ashe's footsteps had not passed him by at all — they had gone to make a telephone call.

"They won't stop at Voss," I said.

"No," Hale agreed, tying his cuffs. "They'll want to know who helped him sign, and who carried the book, and whether the matron intends to keep testifying." He picked up his pen, then set it down again, and for a moment he was young and tired and human. "For what it's worth — thank you for getting it across the ice."

"Marta got it across the ice," I said. "I just carried what she couldn't."

He inclined his head, and returned to his register, and I went down the corridor toward the frosted glass and the armed men and Ellery Voss, who had traded his career for forty names and was now, I very much feared, about to learn what the victors do with witnesses — because the summons already coming down the hall in the hands of a sweating lieutenant bore three signatures, and one of them was Colonel Ashe's after all.
