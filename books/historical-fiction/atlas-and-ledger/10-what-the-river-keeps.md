## 10. What the River Keeps

The archive smelled of cold paper and floor wax, the way all such places smell — as if the past had been scrubbed and pressed and hung on rails for inspection. Juniper stood at the entrance of the reading room for a long moment before she went in, her coat still buttoned against the March wind, because some part of her had expected to be stopped at the door. Turned away. Told that the ledger was a matter of closed record now, that the war was forty years settled, that old women with grievances should write letters like everyone else.

No one stopped her. A young man at the desk asked for her reader's card, stamped it without looking up, and pointed her toward the third bay from the window.

She found it where the catalogue said it would be: *Thornbury, J., Ward Ledger, 1917–1918. Access restricted; handle with gloves.*

They had given it a number. That was the thing that caught in her throat first, before she'd even touched it — not the ledger itself but the small typed label pasted inside the front cover, a call number, a shelf mark, a place in an order of things. Forty years ago she had sewn those pages into a dead soldier's kit bag while a commission officer's boots rang through the ward, and now the pages lived in a grey archival box with acid-free tissue between them, catalogued by someone who had never heard the river groan.

She signed the request slip. She waited. She put on the white cotton gloves they gave her, which made her hands look like a surgeon's again, and when the box came she lifted the ledger out with both arms, the way you lift something that might still be breathing.

It was smaller than she remembered. That was the second thing. In 1917 it had been a stone in her pocket, a weight she carried across the ice in her coat, pressing against her ribs like a second heart. Now it sat in her gloved palms light as a bird's skeleton, its boards scuffed, its spine cracked, the water stain along one edge — Marta's river, the river's signature — gone brown and soft as old tea.

*Names,* she thought. *All those names, and it weighs nothing at all.*

She opened it.

Her own handwriting looked back at her, younger and more hurried than she felt. The first entries were neat enough — name, rank or none, date, cause where known, unit where known, and then the column she had ruled herself, the one no form had ever asked for: *Who else will say this?* Some entries said *Marta.* Some said *Voss.* Most said nothing, because there was no one left to say them, and that blank space had been the loneliest writing she had ever done.

She turned the pages slowly. Orderlies who died carrying stretchers and appeared in no report. The deserter shot at the wire whose body the morning tally recorded as *enemy, one.* The local women — she could still see their faces, the miller's daughters, the schoolteacher who bandaged men in her kitchen until the shell came — entered under *civilian losses, estimated.* Estimated. As if a person could be approximated.

The reading room was quiet around her. A graduate student two tables over worked through a stack of regimental histories with the patient boredom of the young. Outside the tall windows, the city went about its business, trams ringing, umbrellas bobbing. Nobody here knew what these pages had cost. Nobody here knew about the fog, or the sound the ice made — a low singing, almost musical, right before it let go of you.

She reached the entry she had come to find.

*Marta Reznik, ward nurse. Carried a stretcher case across the ice, 14 February 1918. Went down at the third crack. Held the stretcher above the water until the last. Half the duplicate pages lost with her.*

Juniper had written it kneeling on the far bank, soaked to the waist, Voss's coat around her shoulders doing nothing against the cold. Her hand had shaken so badly the ink had feathered. She had written Marta's name the way you set a bone — quickly, firmly, knowing it would hurt.

And there beside it, in the margin, the small annotation in another hand, pencil, official: *Duplicate pages not recovered. See Commission Report, vol. IV.*

See Commission Report. As if the report were the original and Marta the footnote.

Juniper closed her eyes.

* * *

They had let her read for two hours before the archivist came to find her.

She recognized him at once, though he must have been thirty then and was past seventy now — Havel, the reluctant man behind the desk on the day she'd laid the ledger down among the commission's tidy folders and said, *This goes in, or nothing I know goes in.* He had hesitated. An officer had objected, loudly, citing procedure, citing verification, citing the impossibility of admitting uncorroborated material into the historical record. And Havel — thin, tired, a man who had spent his whole career watching what power did to paper — had picked up his pen anyway and begun entering names.

He stood at the end of her table now, older, bent, his hands folded, and he looked at the ledger in front of her the way a priest looks at a relic he is not entirely sure is genuine and is entirely sure matters.

"Frau Thornbury," he said. "I hoped you would come before—" He didn't finish it. Before the building was renovated. Before the collection was reorganized. Before one of us dies, either of us, and the chance goes out of the world.

"Herr Havel." She gestured to the chair opposite. "Sit. My legs are eighty-two years old and they've earned a rest, but yours haven't."

He smiled and sat. Up close she could see how carefully he had dressed, collar pressed, tie knotted high — a man who had put on his best to meet a book.

"I wanted to tell you," he said, "what happened after. You never asked, all these years. You sent your letters, your corrections, your objections to volume four, and then you stopped, and I always wondered if you'd made peace with it or simply run out of strength."

"Both," Juniper said. "In alternating weeks." She turned the ledger gently toward him. "Tell me what happened after."

He told her. The names she'd brought across the ice — the ones that survived, the ones Voss's confession corroborated — had been entered into the record in the spring of 1919, over objection, in an appendix nobody read. Then, twenty years later, a younger historian working through the appendix had noticed the discrepancies with the official narrative and followed them, and followed them, until the retreat that had officially never happened was a retreat with a chapter, and the forty men Ellery Voss had counted twice had their count restored. Voss himself had served two years for the false report — he'd insisted on testifying, knowing what it would cost, and paid it standing up — and had lived afterward outside Graz, keeping bees, answering every letter from every family who wrote to ask about a father, a brother, a son. He'd died in 1954. Juniper knew that part already. She had gone to the funeral. There had been bees in the clover by the churchyard wall, and she had thought: *he counted twice, and the second count saved him.*

"And the ledger?" she asked.

Havel touched the box lid with one finger, lightly, the way you touch a sleeping animal. "It sat in the appendix files for years. Uncatalogued. Then the reorganization of '31, and a clerk found it, and by then the history had changed enough that it wasn't dangerous anymore — only true. So we gave it a number." He paused. "You're looking at me as though that's a small thing. It isn't. A number means it can be found. A number means a girl in fifty years, a student who never heard of any of you, can pull this box and read the name of a woman the reports called *estimated civilian losses.*"

"The reports also called her nothing at all," Juniper said. "For forty years."

"Yes." He didn't flinch from it, and she was grateful. "The record is imperfect. It was imperfect when you wrote it, and it's imperfect now, and it will be imperfect when everyone in this room is dust. We keep what survives. That's all keeping has ever been."

Juniper looked down at the open page, at Marta's name in her own shaking ink, at the pencil note beside it. *Duplicate pages not recovered.* Not recovered. As if they were somewhere, waiting. As if the river might one day give them back.

"Marta kept a list too," she said. "Before mine. She told me on the night of the raid — she'd lost a brother to an omission, years before, and she'd started writing names in the flyleaf of her anatomy text until an officer found it and burned it in front of her. Everything she knew about omission, she knew from the inside." Juniper's voice held steady. She had practiced this, in the train, in the hotel room, in the years. "Her name isn't in the official record except as a line in my ledger. No service noted. No grave located. The river keeps her, and the river doesn't annotate."

Havel was quiet for a moment. Then he took off his glasses, polished them on his handkerchief with the deliberation of a man buying time, and put them back on.

"What would you have the record say?"

"That she existed. That she chose. That she went down holding the stretcher above the water — that detail matters, Herr Havel, don't ask me why, but it does, it's the whole of her, she held it up until the last — and that half my duplicate pages are in the mud with her, and whatever names were on them, she drowned carrying other people's truth. That's not a correction. That's just a sentence. But it should be *in* something. Somewhere it can't burn."

Havel nodded slowly. Then he rose, went to the desk, and returned with a pencil — not a pen, she noticed, and understood: pencil meant provisional, pencil meant honest, pencil meant *we do not pretend to permanence we haven't earned.*

"In the margin," he said, and slid the pencil across the table to her. "Your ledger. Your margin. I'll enter it properly in the finding aid tomorrow, cross-referenced, so no clerk loses it. But the first writing should be yours."

Juniper took the pencil. Her hand, which had shaken on the far bank at twenty-four, was steady now at eighty-two, and there was a bitter joke in that she almost made aloud and didn't.

She turned to Marta's entry. Below it, in the margin, in small clear letters, she wrote:

*Marta Reznik kept her own list before mine, and it was burned. She carried mine across the ice and kept it above the water to the last. Whatever the river holds of hers, hold this too: she was not estimated. She was exact.*

She sat back. The words looked small on the page, four lines in a margin, next to forty years of silence. It was not enough. Nothing would be enough; she had known that since the night the ice sang. But it was *in* something now, cross-referenced, numbered, findable, and a girl in fifty years would run her finger down this page and stop at the name and maybe — maybe — carry it a little further into the future, the way Marta had carried the stretcher, the way the ledger had crossed the river, the way everything true had ever traveled: hand to hand, at cost, in the dark.

Havel read it without touching the page. When he looked up, his eyes were wet, and he made no apology for it.

"It will hold," he said.

"So will I," Juniper said, "for another hour or two. Then I'd like tea, if this establishment permits tea near the collections."

"It does not," Havel said, "which is why I know a place two streets over where they've poured the same pot since 1936." He began, carefully, to close the box — and then stopped, his hand flat on the lid.

"One more thing," he said. "You should know before you go. Last month a farmer's son brought us papers found in the riverbank below the old crossing — the spring floods shift the silt, apparently, and the river gives things back. Boots. Buckles. And a bundle of pages, water-stained, fused together. Handwriting on the visible corner. Numbers. Names, possibly." He met her eyes. "We haven't separated them yet. It takes time, and care, and someone who knew the original hand."

Juniper looked at the ledger in its box, at the brown stain along its edge, at the margin where Marta's name now lived in her own steady script.

"Then you'll want me back," she said.
