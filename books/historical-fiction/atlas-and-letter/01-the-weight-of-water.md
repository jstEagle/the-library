## 1. The Weight of Water

The first crate of the morning weighed as much as a drowned man.

Silas knew this because she had carried a drowned man once, when she was nine years old and the men on the dock were too busy shouting to do it themselves. Her father had come up from his last dive of the day with Anselmo Cruz in his arms instead of shell, both of them streaming, and the crowd had parted like water around a prow, and no one had moved to help because no one wanted to touch what the sea had finished with. So she had put her shoulder under Anselmo's arm, small as she was, and walked him up the planks while her father held the other side, and she had learned two things that day: that dead weight is heavier than living weight, and that grief is mostly a problem of logistics.

The pearl crates were only dead weight. She could manage them.

She counted her breaths going up the gangplank — four in through the nose, four out through the mouth, the way her father had taught her before he ever taught her letters. *A diver who wastes breath drowns,* he used to say, crouched beside her on the sand with his face half silvered by the light off the water. *A diver who hoards it drowns too. You give the sea exactly what it asks for, no more, no less.* He had been talking about air. She had made it into a whole life.

Four in. Four out. The crate rode her shoulder like a sleeping animal. Nineteen years old, five feet and a handspan, and she could carry eighty pounds of oyster shell up a wet plank without spilling a drop of herself onto the boards. Not because she was strong — though eight years of this had knotted rope into her shoulders — but because she never fought the weight. She let it settle where it wanted and simply became the thing that carried it.

"Marchetti!" The foreman's voice cracked across the yard like a whip over oxen. "You're dawdling."

She wasn't dawdling. She was four crates ahead of Pietro, who was twice her size and complained about his back every hour on the hour. But arguing cost breath, and breath was currency, so she said nothing, turned, and went back down for the fifth.

The Verrone Trading Company yard smelled of brine and tar and money. Money had a smell here; Silas had learned that early. It was the sharp vinegar reek of fresh-painted signboards, the pipe tobacco of the gentlemen who came down from the counting house in their gray coats to watch the shells come in, the ink-and-iron tang of the weighmaster's office where every crate was recorded and every record was law. The harbor itself smelled of fish and salt and the honest rot of things the sea gave up, but the company grounds smelled of paper. Paper was what turned pearls into profit, and profit into power, and power into the gray coats and the brass buttons and the tall windows that looked down on people like her the way a man looks at weather.

She set the fifth crate on the scale. The weighmaster, a fat man named Odell with ink stains up both wrists like old bruises, ran his thumb along the manifest.

"Marchetti. Lot forty-one. Sixty-two pounds." He wrote it in the book without looking at her. They never looked at her. That was the arrangement, unspoken but iron-clad: she did not look at them, they did not see her, and everyone got through the day.

"Sixty-two," she confirmed. Her voice came out low and even, a servant's voice, a voice with all the corners sanded off it.

"Next."

* * *

By midmorning the sun had burned the mist off the harbor and the yard had filled with its daily theater. Divers came in off the luggers with their catch, brown and dripping, their hands nicked white from prying shell. Buyers strolled the rows with jeweler's loupes screwed into their eye sockets, holding nacre up to the light like priests examining relics. Somewhere near the gate a cart horse was screaming about something, and somewhere near the water a woman was selling fried dough, and the smell of it followed Silas around the yard like a dog hoping for scraps.

Her stomach answered it. She told her stomach to wait. Lunch was at noon, and lunch was bread and cheese and, if Brigid was feeling generous, a spoonful of pickle relish that she pretended was an accident. Brigid's accidents were the closest thing to love the trading house served on a regular schedule.

Four in. Four out. Crate six. Crate seven.

It was on crate eight, coming up the plank with the shell biting through her sacking shoulder-pad, that she saw the gentleman in the gray coat stop at the foot of the ramp and turn to watch her pass.

She knew better than to meet his eyes. She kept her gaze fixed on the middle distance, on the grain of the plank three feet ahead, and catalogued him the way she catalogued everything — sideways, cheaply, without spending attention she couldn't afford. Gray coat, good wool. Brass chain across the waistcoat. Fifty-ish, heavy through the middle, hands soft. One of the counting-house men, then, not a buyer. But he stood wrong for a clerk. Clerks hunched. This man stood like the building belonged to him personally, like the harbor was a rug laid out for his convenience.

Director Verrone himself. She had seen him maybe six times in eight years, always at a distance, always surrounded. Today he was alone except for a secretary trailing three steps behind with a leather folio, and today, for reasons she could not fathom, he was watching the pearl crates come up the ramp, and his eyes passed over her the way a man's eyes pass over furniture — and then came back.

"Girl," he said.

Silas stopped. She did not set the crate down — you didn't set a company crate on a public plank without leave — but she shifted it higher on her shoulder and inclined her head. "Sir."

"What lot is that?"

"Forty-one, sir. Weighed already." Sixty-two pounds. She had the number ready the way other girls kept songs ready.

Verrone nodded slowly. He had a face built for banknotes, broad and smooth and reassuring, with small eyes that did none of the reassuring. "Good shell this season?"

"The divers say the beds are deep this year, sir. Deeper means fewer, but finer."

Something flickered behind the small eyes — surprise, maybe, that the furniture could talk. "Your father was a diver," he said. It wasn't a question.

"Yes, sir."

"And now you carry what he would have brought up."

The sentence sat there between them, dressed in pleasant tones, and underneath it Silas could feel the shape of the thing it actually was: *your father is dead and his death bought us your back.* Eight years ago, and the company still owned the arithmetic of it. Every crate she carried was a payment on a debt that had been calculated the week her father drowned — the boat lease, the gear, the advance against the season's take, all of it called due at once because a dead diver couldn't argue terms. Eleven years old, and the company had offered her mother a choice: the workhouse for the girl, or the girl's labor against the debt. Her mother had wept and signed. Her mother was three years gone now too, and the signature lived on, and Silas carried crates.

"Yes, sir," she said.

"Hm." Verrone adjusted his cuff. "Diligence is a virtue, Marchetti. The company remembers diligent hands." He said it the way a man drops a coin in a beggar's cup — not enough to change anything, just enough to make himself feel the virtue of it. Then he walked on toward the weighing office, secretary scurrying, and Silas stood on the plank with sixty-two pounds on her shoulder and counted four breaths in, four out, until her heart agreed to be reasonable again.

*The company remembers diligent hands.* She turned the phrase over as she walked the crate to the sorting shed. It sounded almost like kindness. It was actually a receipt. Everything the company said to its indentured was a receipt — proof of transaction, proof that nothing given was given free. Diligence would be remembered the way a loan was remembered: precisely, coldly, and with interest.

In the sorting shed, the openers worked in rows, knives flashing, and the smell hit her the way it always did — sweet rot and iodine, the smell of the sea's jewelry being pried loose. She logged her crate with the shed mistress, signed the chit with her neat, small, school-learned hand — the company taught its indentured children their letters, because an illiterate worker couldn't be trusted with a manifest — and went back down the plank for crate nine.

This was the whole of it. This was the day, and the year, and the shape of her life: plank, crate, scale, chit, plank. Four in, four out. She had made her peace with it the way you make peace with tide — not by loving it, but by learning its schedule so well that it could never surprise you. Surprise was the enemy. Surprise was how men drowned. Her father had taught her that too, though he hadn't meant it the way she'd taken it.

* * *

Noon came, and with it Brigid's bread.

The dormitory mess hall occupied the ground floor of the trading house on Rope Street, a long room with scarred tables and windows set high enough that you could see sky but nothing worth reporting. Forty-odd indentured children and young workers ate there in shifts, and Brigid O'Rahilly presided over the kitchen at the back like a general whose war had been lost decades ago but who intended to feed her troops properly regardless.

"You're thin," Brigid announced, which was what she always announced, and slid Silas's bowl across the counter with a second piece of cheese hidden under the bread where the matron wouldn't see it. "Eat. You've been hauling since dawn."

"I always haul since dawn."

"And you're always thin. Eat."

Silas ate. Around her the hall roared with the particular noise of young people who had twelve minutes of freedom and intended to spend all of them talking. Pietro was holding forth about a fight at the Anchor Tavern. Little Ada, ten years old and all elbows, was reciting multiplication tables to anyone who'd listen because she'd decided that being fastest at figures was her way out. Fenwick sat at the end table with his lame leg stretched into the aisle where everyone had learned to step over it, doing sums in the margins of yesterday's newspaper, and when Silas glanced over he caught her eye and touched his pencil to his forehead in a little salute. Fenwick saluted everyone. It was his way of insisting on dignity in a place designed to grind it off.

She ate her hidden cheese and watched the room and felt the thing she always felt at noon, the thing she trusted least: warmth. Belonging. The dangerous idea that this — the noise, the bread, the stepping-over-Fenwick's-leg — was a family, purchased at the price of obedience and renewable only at the company's pleasure. She knew better. She'd buried a father and a mother, and she knew that families you could lose to a signature weren't families, they were tenancies. And still the warmth came, every noon, punctual as the bell. She had stopped fighting it. She just refused to build anything on it.

"Marchetti." The matron's voice cut the room's noise cleanly. "Weighmaster wants you after shift. Discrepancy on lot thirty-eight."

A cold thread went through her, quick and thin. "Yes, ma'am."

Discrepancy. The word had teeth. A discrepancy on a lot she'd carried meant either that someone above her had made an error and needed a neck below them to account for it, or that someone thought *she* had. Either way, the procedure was the same: stand straight, speak softly, admit nothing that wasn't proven, and count your breaths.

Pietro leaned over as the matron moved on. "Thirty-eight? That was Odell's count Tuesday. Fat sod can't read his own—"

"Pietro." She said it quietly, and he shut up, because even Pietro understood that criticizing the weighmaster within these walls was a luxury priced beyond any of them.

But the cold thread stayed with her through the afternoon, through crates eleven through nineteen, through the slow gold slide of the light across the yard. Not fear, exactly. She was too tired for fear most days. More like the feeling of a door standing open somewhere in a house you thought you knew — a draft you couldn't trace.

* * *

The weighmaster's office at dusk was a small kingdom of paper. Ledgers lined the walls in ranks, spines dated, decades of the company's memory bound in calfskin. Odell sat behind his desk with a lamp pulled close, and on the desk, open between them, lay Ledger Seven — the current season's intake book — and beneath his thick finger, lot thirty-eight.

"Tuesday's count," Odell said. "Read it back to me."

Silas read it. "Lot thirty-eight, received from lugger *Cormorant*, sixty-one pounds, opened yield eleven pearls, graded three-A, sold to Halloway & Sons at—" She stopped. The number on the line was not the number she remembered from Tuesday, when she'd stood in this same room and watched him write it. "Sold at four crowns the pearl."

Odell's eyes came up. For the first time in eight years, the weighmaster looked directly at her, and she understood immediately that this was the point of the exercise — that whatever happened next required a witness, and witnesses were chosen for their invisibility.

"That's not what I wrote Tuesday," she said carefully. "It was three crowns, ten. I remember because Pietro joked about it. He said Halloway was robbed."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, the evening bell rang across the water, and gulls rose off the warehouse roofs in a ragged gray sheet.

"Ledgers get corrected," Odell said.

"Not after signing. We both signed."

"Ledgers," Odell repeated, with the patience of a man explaining tides to a child, "*get corrected.*" He closed the book. His hand stayed flat on the cover, and Silas looked at that hand — soft, ink-stained, entirely steady — and understood that she was standing inside something much larger than a pricing error. Men like Odell did not correct numbers for small reasons. Small errors were left alone, fossilized in the record, because small errors cost nothing. Numbers got changed when the truth in them had become expensive to somebody.

"Yes, sir," she said.

"You'll note in your own book that lot thirty-eight was recounted and amended per weighmaster's instruction. Standard form. Nothing more."

"Yes, sir."

He dismissed her with a flick of two fingers, and she went, walking evenly, breathing evenly, four in and four out, past the ranks of calfskin ledgers with their patient spines. At the door she paused — barely, half a heartbeat, nothing a watching man would mark — and glanced back at the shelf behind Odell's head. Ledger Nine sat there between Eight and Ten, its spine newer than its neighbors', its brass clasp gleaming.

She had never once seen Ledger Nine opened.

Outside, the harbor had gone violet and the lamplighters were working Rope Street, and the trading house windows glowed the color of weak tea. Silas crossed the yard slowly, her body aching in its usual honest way, her mind moving along a track it did not usually permit itself: *what does a company have to hide, and how big does a thing have to be before a man changes a signed page in front of a witness?*

She climbed the dormitory steps. Behind her, far down at the harbor mouth, a ship's lantern swung at anchor where no ship had anchored at dusk — a dark hull riding low, lights doused except the one, waiting on the tide.

She stood a moment in the cold, counting breaths, and told herself it was nothing. Cargo comes and goes. Ships anchor when they anchor.

But her father had taught her to read water, and water had taught her this: when the surface looks calm and the current pulls anyway, something big is moving underneath.

She went inside, and made her chit neat, and said nothing to anyone — silence and busy hands, survival's oldest recipe. Above her, in the clerks' quarters, she could hear Fenwick's uneven tread crossing his room, and Brigid banging pans below, and the ordinary music of the only home she had.

Three days later, everything would begin with a knock after midnight. But that night she only slept, lightly, the way divers sleep — one ear open for the sea.
