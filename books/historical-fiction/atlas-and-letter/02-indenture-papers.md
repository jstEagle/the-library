## 2. Indenture Papers

The paper arrived on a Tuesday, the way ruin always did in Port Amalia — on schedule, stamped, and requiring two signatures.

Silas saw it first as a color. The clerk's boy came down the warehouse row carrying a folder the red of dried blood, and she watched it move from hand to hand like something contagious, and then it was in front of her, and Mr. Quill — the junior factor with the damp handshake and the ring of keys that sang when he walked — was saying her name.

"Marchetti. Girl. Pay attention."

She paid attention. She had been taught to pay attention by a man who could read a current from the shiver of kelp fifty feet down.

"Your indenture papers," Quill said, as if announcing weather. "Renewal of terms. Standard language. You'll sign at the mark and again at the bottom."

She looked at the folder and did not see the folder. She saw another folder, eight years ago, another red, another table — and the sound of rain on the roof of the Verrone Trading Company's counting house, which even now she could not hear without tasting salt.

"Sign where?" she said, because that was all that was required, and silence plus busy hands did not include refusing paper.

Quill laid a finger on two X's. She signed. Her handwriting was very good. Nobody ever remarked on this, but it was true: eight years of ledgers had given her a hand like an engraver's, small and exact and legible to God. She signed *Silas Marchetti* — her father's surname kept, her mother's name given, a combination nobody in the port's records had ever questioned and so neither would she — and Quill blotted it, and took the folder away, and that was that. Another seven years. She was nineteen; she would be free at twenty-six, if the sea and the company and her own lungs held out.

Free. The word sat in her chest like a pearl too large for its socket.

* * *

Here is what the papers said, though Silas knew them by heart before she could read them, because Brigid had read them aloud to her once, slowly, in the dormitory kitchen with a candle between them, and had wept at the fourth clause and sworn Silas to secrecy about the weeping:

*Whereas one Matteo Marchetti, pearl diver, late of the Verrone Trading Company fleet, perished in the Company's service upon the twenty-ninth day of September in the year 1903, leaving debts outstanding to the sum of forty-one pounds, twelve shillings; and whereas the said Marchetti departed this life leaving issue one daughter, Silas, aged eleven years; be it therefore agreed that the said debt shall stand secured against the labor of the said child for a term of fifteen years or until such time as the principal and lawful interest be discharged...*

Fifteen years for forty-one pounds. She had done the arithmetic herself, later, at eleven, with a stolen pencil stub: it came to less than three pounds a year for a human life. Even at eleven she understood that the arithmetic was not meant to balance. It was meant only to be written down, so that afterward everyone could point at it and say *the paper says.*

Her father had owed money to the company the way every diver owed money to the company — boots, knife, the suit he never wore because he dove skin like the old men of his village, rope and goggles and the medicine for his ears. Debt was the water they swam in; you didn't fight water, you learned its weight. And when the *Corvina*'s crew came back without him — line cut clean, they said, a knife above the anchor, though no one could say whose — the debt did not drown with him. Debts floated. That was what made them useful to men who collected them.

She remembered the counting house. Everyone remembered their counting house; it was a genre of memory in Port Amalia, shared like weather. Rain on the glass. A lamp turned low to save oil. The factor — not Quill, a man before Quill, with a face like a folded napkin — reading the figures aloud while she stood on a stool so she could see the ledger, because he had said, in a tone of great fairness, *the child should understand the terms.* Across the desk a wall of pigeonholes rose into shadow, each hole stuffed with papers, and she had thought, with the terrible practicality of eleven, that the company must be made entirely of paper, that somewhere inside it there was nothing at all except documents wearing a suit.

There had been talk of the workhouse. There was always talk of the workhouse; it hung over orphaned children in the port like gulls over fish guts. *Saint Brendan's Receiving Home,* which everyone called the Sinks, because children went in and something smaller and deader came out. The factor pronounced the words — *in default of surety, the child to be remanded* — and dipped his pen, and Silas, who had not cried at the funeral because crying felt like opening a door she'd never get shut, felt the door begin to give.

And that was when the clerk spoke up.

* * *

Tomas Reyes was then — let her count — perhaps fifty. He sat at the third desk from the window, where clerks with weak lungs were stationed because the draft came off the harbor, and he had the gray, rumpled, overlooked look of a man who had spent thirty years being walked past. She had seen him before, dimly, in the way you see furniture. He handled manifests. His handwriting was famous among the other clerks for being beautiful, which was a strange kind of fame, and useless, and theirs.

"The girl has surety," he said.

The factor's pen stopped. "The debt stands at—"

"I know what the debt stands at." Reyes rose, unhurried, joints cracking like ship's rope, and came around the desk. From his waistcoat pocket he drew a purse — a real purse, leather gone soft with use, and the softness told her more than any figure could: that the money in it had been carried a long time and counted often. "Forty-one pounds, twelve. I'll stand surety. Amend the instrument. Indent the labor to me as guarantor and assign my interest to the company, standard terms."

"You can't afford—" began the factor, and stopped, recalculating. Men like him could smell what a man had saved the way sharks smelled blood. "Standard terms," he repeated, already writing, already turning the thing into paper, which was how he made everything safe.

Silas stood on her stool and stared at Reyes. He did not look at her. He watched the pen, following each word as it went down the way a man watches a surgeon's knife near his own flesh, and when the factor finished and held out the blotter, Reyes signed his name in that famous hand — she caught only a glimpse of it, loops like waves breaking — and put the purse back in his pocket, and said, in her direction but not quite to her, "Well. Go home, then. You live on Rope Street, don't you? Go home."

She went home. It was only years later, lying awake in the dormitory with the arithmetic gnawing her, that she understood what *home* had cost him: that the soft purse was a lifetime of unspent things — new coats, drink, marriage, passage anywhere — converted in one afternoon into a child he had never spoken to before. Forty-one pounds, twelve. She had tried once to imagine what else it might have bought and found she could not make the sum come out to anything better than what it had bought. That frightened her worse than the workhouse had.

* * *

In the present, the warehouse bell rang the shift change, and Silas took up her crates.

Eight years, and she had never once asked him why. This was not cowardice, or not only cowardice. It was policy — hers, learned early: gratitude with roots that deep is better left unexamined, because examination turns up rocks, and under rocks are the things that make you choose, and choosing is dangerous in a house where obedience is the rent. She thanked him instead in the only currency the company respected: competence. She kept her ledgers immaculate. When Reyes's manifests crossed her station — and they crossed it often; he still sat at the third desk from the window, grayer now, still beautiful-handed — she checked them twice and flagged nothing and asked nothing, and once, catching a transposed tonnage figure before it reached the harbor master's book, she corrected it silently and left no note of the correction, and saw him notice, weeks after, some flicker at the third desk, a look passed over her head like weather. Neither of them ever mentioned it. That was the whole architecture of their acquaintance: things noticed, never named.

He limped slightly, these days. He drank tea from a chipped cup he would not replace. On feast days he gave coins to the harbor children with a furtiveness that suggested he expected to be arrested for generosity. The younger clerks called him Old Reyes behind his back and thought she didn't hear. She heard everything. Hearing everything and saying nothing was, by now, less a skill than an organ — something she'd grown, like the callus on her palm from the crate ropes, like the diver's patience in her chest that let her go long minutes between breaths.

Because there was another thing the papers had done, besides binding her. They had put her inside the walls of the Verrone Trading Company — into its ledgers, its freight rooms, its rhythms of tide and cargo — and a girl who lived inside a machine for eight years learns its sounds. She knew which factors skimmed. She knew which ships ran dark-lanterned through the outer roads on nights the watch rolls showed full crews ashore. She knew that Ledger Nine lived in the locked cabinet in the senior counting room and was taken out less often than ledgers a tenth its size. None of this was knowledge she wanted. Knowledge in Port Amalia was ballast: useful when trimmed right, fatal when it shifted.

She thought about her father a great deal this week, the renewal having stirred him up out of the sediment where she kept him. Not his face — faces fade first, and she had been angry at herself about that for years and had finally forgiven it, on the theory that he would forgive it too — but his instructions. *Count your breaths going down. Four counts in, six counts out, and never race the bottom.* He had taught her to read water the way other fathers taught scripture: the sea as a text with moral content, currents as intentions, weather as temper. *The water doesn't lie,* he used to say, wringing out his hair on the *Corvina*'s boards, silver in the dawn. *Men lie. Paper lies. Water just is.* And then he'd laugh at himself, because he was a man who worked for a company and signed its papers twice a year and knew exactly what he was.

Paper lies. She had signed a fresh lie that morning, or at least a fresh copy of the original one — the fiction that forty-one pounds could be worth a life, that a debt survived the debtor, that a signature freely given by an eleven-year-old standing on a stool was anything but a robbery conducted at reading pace.

But Reyes had signed too. Reyes, whose paper was the only paper in her whole history that had ever told the truth about anything: that she was worth saving.

* * *

That evening the dormitory smelled of barley and onions, and Brigid's voice came up the stairwell ahead of her body, scolding someone for something, and Fenwick's lamplight showed gold under his door where he was doing accounts he'd never finish because he did everyone's but his own. Silas ate at the end of the bench, as usual, and listened, as usual, and the ordinary music of Rope Street played over her head — Brigid threatening murder by wooden spoon, the little ones squabbling over a torn primer, someone's cough going around the room like a collection plate — and beneath the music ran her own quiet accounting.

Seven more years. Or: seven more years of this warmth, purchased at the rate of three pounds annually, guaranteed by a soft-pursed clerk at the third desk from the window who had bought her without ever once asking her to be grateful out loud, which was somehow the deepest hook of all. Gratitude demanded loudly can be resented. Gratitude that asks nothing sits in you like a stone in a shoe, working deeper with every step, until you have built your whole walk around it.

She washed her bowl. She mended a crate strap that didn't need mending, to have hands occupied while her mind moved. And she was crossing the yard in the last gray light, empty bowl in hand, when she saw Tomas Reyes himself at the servants' gate — which was wrong, immediately and completely wrong, because clerks of thirty years' standing did not come to the servants' gate, and because he was looking over his shoulder as he walked, and because his coat, in the dying light, had the stiff wrong hang of a garment worn over other garments, packed for flight.

He saw her seeing him. For one heartbeat neither of them moved, and the harbor sounded far away, and she thought absurdly of her father's voice: *when the surface looks calm and the current pulls anyway.*

Reyes pressed one finger to his lips — the oldest gesture in the world, older than paper, older than lies — and shook his head almost imperceptibly, and was gone into the lane.

Silas stood in the yard a long moment, the bowl cold in her hands, the evening going dark around her like water closing over her head.

Something big was moving underneath.
