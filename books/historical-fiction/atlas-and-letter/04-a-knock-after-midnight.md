## 4. A Knock After Midnight

The knock came at the hour when even the rats were honest.

Silas heard it first as a dream — three soft raps, spaced like a heartbeat that had forgotten its rhythm — and lay still in her narrow cot in the loft above the counting room, listening to the house settle around her. The Verrone Trading Company dormitory slept in a particular way: Brigid snoring below like a kettle left on the stove, Fenwick's cane tapping the floorboards whenever he turned over, the younger children breathing in the ragged chorus of the growing. She had learned to read those sounds the way her father had learned to read the sea. Anything wrong in them woke her at once.

This was not one of those sounds. This was something else entirely.

Three raps. Then silence, stretched thin as gutted line.

She sat up. The loft was black except for the seam of moonlight beneath the door, and in that seam a shadow moved — brief, quick, gone. Someone had been standing there. Someone had knocked and then walked away without waiting for an answer.

In eight years of indenture Silas Marchetti had answered every bell, every summons, every knock that belonged to her. No knock belonged to her at this hour. A knock after midnight at the servants' door of Rope Street was either trouble or death, and neither of them waited politely afterward.

She dressed in the dark by feel, as her father had taught her — *the sea doesn't wait for light, little fish* — pulling on her stockings, her skirt, the shawl she kept folded at the foot of the cot against the cold that came off the harbor in all seasons. She took the candle but did not light it. Her feet knew the stairs. Eleven steps down, turn at the newel post with the crack shaped like a bird's wing, eleven more to the passage that ran behind the kitchen to the servants' entrance.

The house held its breath around her. Even Brigid's snoring had paused, as if the cook too had heard something in her sleep and decided it was nothing worth dying over.

At the servants' door Silas stopped with her hand on the bolt and made herself count, the way she counted everything now — breaths, crates, coins, days. Her father had taught her that too, though he'd taught it underwater. *When you're frightened, count. Fear wants you to breathe fast and rise too quickly, and rising too quickly is what kills.* Four counts in. Six counts out. The bolt was cold under her fingers, iron furred with salt-rust.

She opened the door.

For a long moment there was nothing — just the alley, silvered and empty, the smell of low tide and ash from the boil house two streets over, a cat watching her from atop the wall with the flat yellow insolence of cats. Then the cat moved aside, the way animals do when they've been guarding something, and Silas looked down.

A bundle lay on the step. Wicker basket, handle tied down with string. A blanket folded over the top of it, wool, good quality, dyed the deep blue that sailors paid extra for because the color held against salt. And pinned to the blanket, catching the moon, a scrap of paper no bigger than her palm.

Silas stood very still. She had grown up on stories of what got left on doorsteps in port cities — the workhouse mothers told them to the children like ghost stories, and later the older indentured girls had told them again, differently, with different endings. Babies left at doors did not become wards of trading houses. Trading companies had ledgers, and ledgers had no column for foundlings.

The bundle made a sound.

Not a cry. Smaller than a cry — a kind of hiccoughing sigh, the sound a small animal makes in sleep, and Silas felt it go through her body like current through water, some old unmarked channel opening that she had never once used in nineteen years. She knelt. Her knees hit the wet stone and she didn't notice.

She pulled back the edge of the blanket.

A face looked up at her. An infant — weeks old, perhaps two months, impossible to say — with a dusting of black hair and cheeks round as moored buoys and eyes that were open. Open and fixed on Silas with the terrible gravity of the very young, who do not yet know enough to look away. One fist had worked free of the swaddling and lay open against the blanket, palm up, fingers curled loosely inward, offering nothing, asking everything.

*Forget this face,* said the paper pinned above her head.

But it was the handwriting that stopped Silas's heart in her chest and held it stopped.

She knew that hand. She had known it since she was eleven years old, since the worst day of her life, since the day her father's debt came due along with his body, both dragged up from the same gray water. She had seen it on receipts and chits and one letter — one single letter, kept hidden in the lining of her one good shoe until the shoe fell apart, and then copied out from memory into the back of her ledger where no clerk would ever think to look.

Tomas Reyes wrote his letters tall and narrow, leaning slightly rightward, like men walking into wind. He crossed his t's low and close, impatient strokes. His capital T began with a hook, always, like a wave about to break.

The note on the blanket said: *Forget this face.*

And it was written exactly so.

* * *

She took the basket inside. She did not remember deciding to; her hands simply did it, the way her hands did everything now, working ahead of her mind while her mind caught up slowly behind. She set the basket on the kitchen table, lit the lamp, turned the wick down low so the glow wouldn't show under the door of the passage, and stood looking at the child the way she would look at a crate whose manifest did not match its contents.

Unregistered cargo. Undocumented. Left at her door at the hour when discovery meant ruin for everyone in the building, because Inspector Hale or Director Verrone or any of the forty pairs of company eyes that moved through Rope Street daily would ask one question — *whose?* — and Silas had no answer that did not end in the workhouse for the baby and the constabulary for herself.

The infant watched her. It did not cry. That frightened Silas more than crying would have. Crying demanded things; silence asked nothing and accepted whatever came.

"You should cry," Silas whispered. "Someone will hear you and come, and then it won't be my problem."

The child hiccupped again, that small sighing sound, and waved its open fist at the lamplight.

Silas unpinned the note. There was more writing on the back — she'd seen the shadow of ink through the paper when it hung against the moon. She told herself she was only reading it to know how much danger she was in. That was the sensible reason. That was the reason a careful woman would give.

She read it twice.

*She is not mine, but she is yours now if you are brave. I cannot explain and you would not believe me if I could. Do not go to the constabulary. Do not go to the church. Trust no one who works for the Company — which means, I am sorry to say, trust almost no one. Feed her. Keep her warm. Forget this face, forget me, and live.*

There was no signature. There didn't need to be. The signature was the whole architecture of the man — the hooked capitals walking into wind, the cramped economy of someone who had spent thirty years fitting words into ledger margins. Tomas Reyes. The clerk who had come to the counting room the day after her father drowned, when the company men were talking in their dry voices about debt and obligation and the workhouse, and had laid a fold of banknotes on the table without ceremony, saying only that Marchetti had been owed wages the books had failed to record, and that errors of that kind reflected poorly on the house. He had looked at Silas — eleven years old, hollow-eyed, holding her father's diving helmet under her arm because she couldn't bear to let it out of her sight — and he had said, quietly, so none of the others heard: *Your father kept secrets for me once. Consider this the interest.*

She had never understood it. She had never asked. In eight years she had thanked him with lowered eyes whenever their paths crossed in the corridors of the house, and he had nodded back the way one nods to a stranger, and that was all. Some debts you don't discuss. Her father had taught her that before he taught her anything else.

And now here was Tomas Reyes's handwriting, pinned to a sleeping child on her doorstep, telling her to be brave.

"Brave," Silas said aloud, and the word sounded strange in the kitchen, too large for the room. The lamp guttered. Outside, far off toward the harbor, a ship's bell rang twice — the middle watch changing, the night dividing itself into halves the way it always did, indifferent to what lay on tables in dark kitchens.

She thought of the whispers that had been moving through the clerks' room for the past week, the talk that stopped when she entered with her duster and started again behind her back. *Reyes has fled. Reyes is named. Treason, they're saying — sold the port charts to a foreign navy, charts of the minefields and the naval anchorage, and the Crown wants him hanging from the customs house gantry by Friday.* Fenwick had gone gray when he'd heard it, grayer than his usual gray, and had rebuked the younger clerks for gossiping, and had then sat at his desk copying figures and getting them wrong, which Fenwick never did.

Tomas Reyes, traitor. The man who had paid to keep an orphaned girl out of the workhouse, a traitor. The man her father had trusted with his life and his silence, a traitor.

And that same man had carried a baby through the dark streets of the port at night, past the constables and the company watchers, up Rope Street to the one door he apparently believed would open.

"Why mine?" she asked the note. The note said nothing further. The baby yawned — an enormous, whole-bodied yawn that seemed to take in the entire kitchen — and closed its eyes, and in closing them became suddenly, unbearably precious, the way anything becomes precious the moment it can be lost.

Silas stood at the table for a long time. The middle-watch bell faded. Somewhere above her a floorboard creaked and she froze, one hand on the basket's rim, but it was only the house doing its nightly settling, timber contracting against the cold.

Here was the arithmetic, laid out clean as a ledger page. Turn the child over to the authorities at dawn: safe, correct, and fatal — to the baby, certainly to the workhouse and probably to worse, because a foundling connected in any way to a fugitive traitor was a loose thread, and the Crown pulled loose threads until the whole garment came apart. Say nothing: risk everything, every day, indefinitely, in a house that was searched twice a year by men who counted the spoons. Give the child away, pass her along to some fisherman's wife, some widow — disappear her, as the note instructed. *Forget this face.*

That last was the sensible course. That was survival, and survival was the religion Silas had practiced for eight years with perfect devotion: keep your head down, your ledger neat, your hands busy, your mouth shut. She had been so good at it. She had built her whole self inside it, like a barnacle builds inside its shell.

She looked at the small face, slack now with sleep, the dark hair fine as smoke against the blue wool.

Her father had once told her about the pearl divers of the old camps, the ones who worked the deep beds before the companies came with their air hoses and their quotas. They believed every diver carried a second breath, held in reserve somewhere below the ribs, kept for the moment of drowning — one breath that was yours alone, that no company owned, that you spent only once. Her father had said it laughing, but he had also said it true.

Silas reached down and touched the baby's open fist with one finger. Small fingers closed around it, grip astonishingly strong, the grip of something determined to hold on.

"There," she said, very quietly, to the sleeping child, to the kitchen, to the note, to the man somewhere out in the dark running from the rope that was already being braided for his neck. "There, then."

It was not yes. But it was not no, and she knew, with the certainty of a woman who had spent her life reading small signs — weather, water, the faces of men across ledger desks — that it was going to be yes.

She took the note to the stove. The coals in the range were banked for the night but still alive, breathing orange in their bed of ash, and she fed the paper to them corner-first and watched it curl. The ink flared greenish where the words *brave* and *trust* burned, and then the whole of it was light, and then it wasn't, and Tomas Reyes's handwriting was a curl of ash that broke apart when she poked it with the poker and scattered among the coals like black snow.

*Forget this face.*

She went back to the table. The baby slept on, undisturbed by the small arson conducted on its behalf, one fist still open against the blanket.

Silas fetched the second-best laundry basket from the scullery and lined it with a flour sack and a folded apron, and transferred the child, swaddling and all, taking care with the head the way she'd seen mothers do on the ferry, the way she somehow already knew to do, which alarmed her a little. She tucked the blue blanket in tight. She set the basket on the floor beside her cot's ladder where it would be shadow from the doorway, and then, after a moment's consideration, moved it up onto the cot itself, wedged between the wall and her own body. If anyone came, they would have to come past her.

Then she climbed up, and lay down, and did not sleep.

She listened to the baby breathe — that quick, shallow, astonishing rhythm, twice the speed of a grown person's, as if children lived faster because they had less time. She matched her own breath to it without meaning to. Count in for hers, count out for hers. Her father's voice in her memory, patient, half-muffled by water and the helmet's brass: *Fear wants you to breathe fast, little fish. Don't give it what it wants.*

Outside, the tide was turning. She could hear it in the gurgle of the culvert under Rope Street, the water reversing itself, beginning its slow withdrawal. By dawn the mudflats would lie bare and gleaming, exposing everything the harbor had swallowed overnight — bottles, bones, the occasional secret the city preferred submerged.

Somewhere out in that dark, Tomas Reyes was running, and had left the most dangerous thing in the world on a servants' step in a wicker basket.

Somewhere above the counting room, in the directors' wing, Director Verrone slept his polished sleep, and in the constabulary barracks Inspector Hale sharpened whatever it was hunters sharpened, and in the dormitory Brigid snored on like a kettle that would never quite boil.

Silas lay in the dark with her arm curved around a stranger's child and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like falling, that the life she had so carefully built — the neat ledgers, the busy hands, the silence — had developed a crack in it, and that nothing would ever fit back through that crack the same way again.

The baby stirred, sighed, and settled deeper against her side, trusting her absolutely, having known her for one hour.

"Don't," Silas whispered into the darkness. "Don't trust me yet. I haven't decided anything."

But her hand had already begun, absently, helplessly, to rock.

Below the window, far down Rope Street, boots rang on cobbles — a patrol, unhurried, passing. Silas held her breath and counted, four in, six out, the way her father taught her, and the boots faded, and the house breathed on, and the night ran out toward morning like water through an open hand.
