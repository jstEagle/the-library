## 6. Ledgers and Lies

Fenwick had learned to hear everything while appearing to hear nothing. It was a skill the lameness had taught him, in the way that afflictions teach their own peculiar curriculum. While the other clerks ran errands and climbed ladders and carried crates, Fenwick sat at his desk by the east window with his crooked leg propped on a stool, and he added columns. And because he sat still, men forgot he was there. They said things near him the way they said things near furniture.

He had been furniture for eleven years. He knew all the conversations of the Verrone Trading Company — the ones on paper and the ones between breaths.

On the morning it happened, the fog off the harbor was thick enough to drink, and the counting house smelled of ink, cold tea, and the tallow candles that never quite burned clean. Fenwick arrived before seven, as always, because Mr. Quill disliked latecomers the way cats dislike water, and settled into his chair with the small grunt of pain he had trained himself not to let become a groan. His hip ached worse in wet weather. It had been shattered — or near enough — when a cargo net snapped on the *Meridian Rose* and dropped a ton of Borneo rubber on a boy of fourteen who had no business being under it. The company doctor set it badly. The company paid him a clerk's wage thereafter, on the reasoning — Mr. Quill explained it once without any cruelty at all, which somehow made it worse — that a man who could not carry could count, and counting paid the same.

So Fenwick counted. He counted freight and duty, tare and gross, wages owed and wages docked. He counted eleven indentured children in the dormitory above the boil house, though the company books listed only nine, because two had been carried off the rolls when they died and no one had bothered to correct the ration figures, and Fenwick quietly kept them fed on paper for a month before anyone noticed. He counted everything. That was the trouble with counting: eventually you noticed what didn't add up.

The whispering started before the second bell.

Mr. Hargreaves came in first, shaking fog from his coat like a wet dog, and behind him came young Pell, whose father had bought him a clerkship the way other fathers bought ponies. Pell was twenty and knew nothing, but he knew the shipping news before anyone, because his father drank with harbormasters.

"They've named him," Pell said, and his voice had the particular relish of a young man delivering bad news about someone he'd never liked. "Reyes. Tomas Reyes. Treason."

Fenwick's pen stopped. He kept his eyes on the column — forty barrels gutta-percha, twelve casks indigo, entered at Liverpool rates — and did not look up. But he stopped writing, just for a heartbeat, just long enough.

"Old Reyes?" Hargreaves hung his coat and rubbed his hands. "The head clerk? Reyes who signs the Christmas bonuses?"

"There is no Christmas bonus," Fenwick said mildly, to the ledger. "There is a discretionary allocation. It is taxed."

"He sold port charts," Pell went on, dropping into his chair with the satisfaction of a man sitting down to a feast. "To a foreign navy. That's the charge. Drawings of the channel, the tide tables, where the gunboats moor. A fisherman found papers in a buoy off the Point. The Constabulary's been searching his lodgings since dawn. He's run — gone in the night, door open, fire cold."

"That's not proof," Hargreaves said. "A cold fire."

"It's enough for a warrant. It's enough for a hanging, if they take him." Pell leaned back until his chair creaked. "Eleven years I watched that old spider creeping through these rooms, taking notes on everything. Always watching. Always writing. You know what I think? I think he was selling more than charts."

*You know nothing,* Fenwick thought, with a clarity that startled him. *You know nothing at all, boy, and you will go far here.*

He began writing again. Forty barrels gutta-percha. Twelve casks indigo. His hand was steady, because he had practiced steadiness the way other men practiced golf or prayer, and it held even now, even with the word *treason* sitting in the warm air of the counting house like something dead on a road.

Tomas Reyes, who had once spent an entire afternoon teaching Fenwick a method of cross-checking manifests against tide logs — "the sea keeps honest time, Fenwick; men don't, so let the sea audit the men" — was a traitor. Tomas Reyes, who every Sunday put coins in the chapel box with his left hand so the right would be free to shake the hands of men too poor to be shaken with. Tomas Reyes, who had once, six years ago, stood up in this very room and said, in his soft accented voice, *the girl stays*, when Mr. Quill wanted to send a drowned diver's daughter to the workhouse to settle a debt the workhouse paid thirty shillings against.

Fenwick had been there for that. He remembered it the way you remember the few times you have seen a man do a thing that costs him.

"The Director's been called to the Constabulary," Pell said. "Ten o'clock. My father says there'll be searches. All the company premises. They think he had help inside."

The word *inside* dropped into the room and sank.

"Well," said Hargreaves, uncomfortably, "he was head clerk. Everything inside passed through him."

"And everyone," said Pell.

Fenwick turned a page. The paper sounded loud in his ears, louder than it was. Through the east window the fog was thinning, and he could see the yard below beginning its morning industry: the pearl crates coming off the drays, the lines of porters, and among them — small, quick, head down like always — the Marchetti girl.

Silas. He had watched her grow from a silent eleven-year-old into a silent nineteen-year-old, and he had come to understand that her silence was not emptiness. It was architecture. She had built herself out of quiet the way he had built himself out of ledgers, one careful entry at a time, until the structure could bear weight.

She was looking up at the counting-house windows. He saw her lips move, shaping a question she would never ask aloud.

Then she took up her rag and her polish tin and came toward the door.

* * *

Company policy required the counting room floors oiled weekly, and the oiling fell to the indentured labor, which meant it fell to Silas, which meant that twice a week Fenwick got to watch her work her way down the rows of desks in that soundless gliding way of hers, kneeling, wiping, rising, moving on, invisible as dust itself. She had been doing it for years. No one ever spoke to her. No one ever saw her. Which was, Fenwick understood, precisely how she preferred it, and precisely why she heard everything.

Today she started at the far end of the room, working toward his desk, and as she came the whispers followed her like gulls follow a boat.

"—searched his lodgings already, took away three sacks of paper—"

"—said the foreigner paid in Spanish gold—"

"—I always thought there was something wrong about a man that friendly—"

Silas knelt at the leg of Mr. Quill's desk, six feet from Fenwick, wiping the floorboards in slow even strokes, her face perfectly empty. But Fenwick had spent eleven years learning to read people who didn't wish to be read, and he saw what moved beneath the stillness: her jaw was set hard, and she was breathing through her nose, slow and counted, the way you breathe when you are holding something heavy and cannot put it down.

She knew. Of course she knew. Of all people, she.

Because it was Reyes who had kept her out of the workhouse. Everyone old enough to remember, remembered. And a debt like that doesn't sit in a ledger. It sits in the chest.

Pell was enjoying himself. "Quill says the Director wants a full inventory of Ledger Nine by noon. Nine especially. Don't ask me why Nine."

Something ticked over in Fenwick's chest — small, mechanical, the sound a lock makes when the wrong key turns anyway. Ledger Nine was freight manifests, five years back. Dull as ditchwater. He himself had helped audit it twice. There was nothing in Ledger Nine worth a noon deadline unless someone wanted to know what had been in it before it was what it was now.

He filed the thought where he filed such things, in the drawer marked *not yet*, which was the fullest drawer he owned.

Silas reached his row. She worked toward him along the floorboards, and when she knelt beside his desk she did the thing she sometimes did — tilted her polish tin so the rag could dip, an operation requiring both hands and total absorption, positioned exactly within earshot of whoever was talking. She had done it for years. He had seen her do it for years. Neither of them had ever acknowledged it, because acknowledgment ruins a thing like that, and because in some unspoken economy of the trading house, the lame clerk and the silent girl had long ago granted each other the courtesy of pretending neither noticed anything.

"Reyes fled with company property, I heard," Pell said. "Charts, seals, maybe money. Quill's furious. Says it makes the whole house look careless."

"Careless," Hargreaves repeated gloomily. "My sister-in-law's boy signed articles with this house last spring. If there's treason in the walls—"

"If there's treason in the walls," said Pell, "then somebody helped him build it. And when they find who—" he made a small, pleasant gesture, like a man turning a page, "—well. Treason's a hanging matter. Aiding it's transportation, at least."

Silas rose. She wrung the rag, tucked the tin under her arm, and moved on down the row, and if her shoulder brushed Fenwick's desk as she passed — a touch light as a moth landing — it was nothing. It was accident. It was the kind of contact that means *I know you're there* without ever saying so.

But her hands, he noticed, were shaking. Just slightly. Just enough that the tin rattled once against the rag, a tiny sound, instantly stilled.

* * *

At ten o'clock Director Verrone came back from the Constabulary, and the whole character of the building changed, the way a house changes when the master of it has been insulted. Verrone crossed the counting room without greeting anyone — which he never did; Verrone greeted everyone, that was half of what he was — and shut himself in the glass-walled office at the head of the room with Mr. Quill, and the blinds came down, and the muffled voice behind the glass went on and on, low and level, never once rising, which was somehow more frightening than shouting would have been.

At half past, Quill emerged with orders. Fenwick, being senior after Reyes's — after — well. Being senior, he received them directly.

"Audit," Quill said. His long face was grayer than usual. "Ledger Nine, complete, entries five years back to present. You and Pell. Nothing leaves this room. Nothing gets copied. Nothing gets spoken of. The Director wishes to know, in his words, 'what that man touched.'"

"Yes, Mr. Quill," Fenwick said. "Shall I fetch the ledger?"

"Fetch it yourself. Carry it yourself. And Fenwick—" Quill paused at the glass wall, and for one moment his clerkly mask slipped and showed something underneath that might have been fear. "If you find anything irregular. Anything at all. You bring it to me. Not to the Constabulary. Not to anyone. To me. Is that understood?"

"With respect, sir," Fenwick said carefully, "if it's treason, isn't the Constabulary the proper—"

"This is a matter touching the honor of the house," Quill said, in a voice like a door closing. "The house will handle matters touching the house. Understood?"

Understood, Fenwick did not say, though God alone knew what it was he understood.

He fetched Ledger Nine himself. Carried it himself, hugged against his chest, heavy as a paving stone, feeling absurd and conspicuous crossing the record room with it, as if the ledger shouted its own name. He set it on his desk, broke the company seal, and opened to the first page, and began, with Pell chattering beside him about arrests and gold and hanging judges, to audit five years of pearl freight.

It took him four hours to find it, and he found it only because of a dead man.

The entries were clean. That was the first wrongness — too clean, cleaner than any five-year stretch of real commerce had ever been in the history of trade. Freight in, freight out, weights consistent, ports consistent, duties assessed and paid. Then, at the bottom of the August 1908 page, a signature certifying inspection: *J. Coldbridge*. Josiah Coldbridge, former cargo superintendent, a fat good-humored man who had died — fallen down his own stairs, dreadful business — in July of 1908.

Six weeks before he had certified this cargo.

Fenwick sat very still. Behind the lowered blinds, the Director's office was dark. Across the room, Pell was copying pages with the diligence of the incurious. In the yard below, crates thudded onto scales. And Fenwick looked at the impossible signature, and then — slowly, because his heart had begun to behave strangely, beating high and hard in his throat — he turned back. Page by page. Month by month. Checking every certification against what he knew of the roster of the house, which he knew the way he knew his own scars.

Coldbridge certified cargo in September 1908. October. Dead, dead, dead. And then others — signatures he recognized, dates that fit, but the weights, the *weights* — casks of pearl shell entering at tonnages no dive fleet in the Pacific could produce, manifesting out again as shell, arriving empty of profit and heavy of... of what? What weighs forty tons, ships under pearl freight, pays shell duties, and leaves no pearls?

He knew. Sitting at his desk by the east window, with his crooked leg throbbing and his pen steady in his fingers because he had trained it to be steady, Fenwick knew what weighed forty tons and traveled armed.

And he understood, in the same cold spreading moment, why Tomas Reyes was a traitor now, and why Ledger Nine wanted auditing by noon, and why nothing must leave the room, and why a man who had spent his life letting the sea keep honest time had run in the night with a cold fire behind him.

Reyes hadn't fled from the charts. Reyes had fled because he had found what Fenwick had just found, and the house had found out that he'd found it, and the charge of treason was not the law catching a criminal. It was a knife being sharpened.

He closed the ledger gently, as one closes a coffin.

The question — the enormous, terrible, ridiculous question — was what a lame clerk in a counting house could possibly do about it. He could bring it to Quill, as ordered. Quill, who had relayed the Director's wishes with fear showing through his face like a bone through torn skin. That path led nowhere but deeper into the house.

Or—

Across the room, the door opened, and Silas came in with the day's post on a tray, and set it on Quill's desk, and left, and the whole operation took eleven seconds and was entirely invisible unless you were watching for it, and Fenwick was watching for it, had been watching her for years, and saw what almost no one else in the building would have seen: that she glanced, once, quickly, at the great ledger lying closed on his desk — at the number stamped on its spine — before she fixed her eyes on the floor again and glided out.

Nine. She had looked at Nine.

That evening, when the counting room emptied, Fenwick remained at his desk as he always did, finishing entries, and when the last of them had gone he sat in the lamplight and took out a fresh sheet of paper and did not write on it for a long time. Down in the yard he could see Silas hauling the last crates under the yard lamps, small and tireless, counting her breaths the way her father had taught her, keeping her head down, keeping herself alive.

They say he sold charts to a foreign navy, Fenwick thought. But charts show where things are hidden.

And somewhere out there, a man who once said *the girl stays* was running from a rope, carrying whatever he had been able to save from the truth. And somewhere in this building, a girl who owed him everything was listening at doors for news of him with hands that shook.

Fenwick dipped his pen. On the clean sheet, in the smallest hand he owned, he wrote a single line, not an accusation, not a confession, only a fact, the way an entry is a fact:

*J. Coldbridge certified cargo on 3 September 1908. J. Coldbridge died 21 July 1908.*

He folded it small, and did not burn it.

Some things, he decided, were worth more kept than destroyed — and he only prayed, watching the yard lamps gutter and Silas's shadow pass and pass again below his window, that when the moment came he would find the courage to be worth more kept too.
