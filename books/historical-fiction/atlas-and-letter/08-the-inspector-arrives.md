## 8. The Inspector Arrives

The knock came at half past seven in the morning, and it was not a servant's knock.

Silas knew every knock the trading house made. The cook's boy rapped twice on the kitchen door when the fish cart came. Fenwick tapped his knuckle against the banister each night on his way up, a small ritual like a heartbeat in the dark. Even the wind had its knock — the loose shutter on the third floor that Brigid swore she'd fixed and never had.

This knock was three blows, evenly spaced, struck by someone who expected the door to open because he had already decided it would.

Silas was in the scullery with Atlas wrapped against her chest inside her shawl, the way she'd learned to carry her in the eleven days since the bundle had appeared on the servants' step. Eleven days of learning to breathe around another person's breathing. Eleven days of Brigid slipping extra milk from the morning pail and looking the other way, always the other way, as if the baby were a debt between them that neither would name aloud.

Atlas stirred at the sound of the knocking. Silas pressed her palm flat over the small back and felt the tiny body still itself, obedient even now.

"Open in the name of the Crown Constabulary."

Brigid was already moving, wiping flour from her hands onto her apron, arranging her face into the dull courtesy of a woman who had nothing to hide and less to say. "Coming, coming," she called, in the voice she used for late deliveries and drunk sailors. "No need to break my door."

Silas did not move. Moving was the first mistake. She stood among the pots with the baby warm against her sternum and counted her breaths the way her father had taught her — four counts down, four counts up, the rhythm that kept a diver calm when the water above turned black. *You don't fight the pressure, little bird,* he'd said once, his hands shaping the words in the air above their supper. *You become the pressure. Then it has nothing to push against.*

Boots in the hall. Voices. The particular authority of men who carried papers.

* * *

Inspector Hale was not what she expected, which unsettled her more than cruelty would have. She had imagined the Constabulary as a fist — thick men with thick voices, all knuckle and bellow. The man standing in the courtyard of Verrone Trading House was lean and gray-templed, with a clerk's neat hands and eyes that moved through a room the way Fenwick's finger moved down a column of figures: nothing skipped, nothing assumed.

He wore a plain dark coat, no braid, and he removed his hat before speaking to Brigid, which no company man had ever done.

"Inspector Hale," he said. "I have a warrant to search these premises. We're looking for correspondence, documents, or persons connected to one Tomas Reyes." He said the name without venom, which was somehow worse. A man who hated you shouted. A man who didn't simply watched.

Behind him stood six constables in blue, and behind *them*, to Silas's private horror, stood Mr. Quill, the company's under-steward, wringing his soft white hands and radiating the eagerness of a dog hoping to be praised for pointing.

"The Director extends every cooperation," Quill said. "Every cooperation. If Reyes kept anything here, we want it found. We are loyal subjects, Inspector, and—"

"Yes," said Hale mildly. "So I've been told. Twice."

He unfolded the warrant and read it aloud, and Silas listened with her head bowed and her hands folded, the posture of invisibility she had worn for eight years like a second skin. *Search the dormitories. Search the counting rooms. Search the cellars.* And then a phrase that made her blood go cold and slow: *and any outbuildings, stores, or freight held on the premises.*

Freight. Pearl crates. Forty of them stacked in the long storehouse awaiting the coastal steamer, each one lined with sacking and packed with shell.

One of which, since dawn, had not contained pearls.

* * *

She had known something was coming. Not this — not so soon, not so formally — but a wrongness in the air for two days, the way the sea went glassy and strange before weather. Quill had been asking questions about the servants' door, about who came and went after dark. A constable had been seen talking to the harbormaster. And last night, Brigid had stood at the scullery window a long time, watching the street, and finally said, without turning: "If they come, girl, you'll need somewhere better than a blanket drawer."

So at dawn, while the house slept, Silas had gone into the storehouse and done what a pearl diver's daughter does: she had thought about water.

A crate of pearl shell going to market is heavy, but not uniformly heavy. It has hollows. She had chosen crate thirty-one, near the wall, third row up — high enough that a searching hand would have to reach, low enough that it wasn't worth a ladder. She had emptied the top layer of shell, laid fresh sacking in a shallow basin she'd stolen from the laundry, and settled Atlas into it the way her father had once settled an oyster into wet moss for transport across the island — snug, dark, breathing. Then she had poured shell around the edges, leaving a pocket of air above the sleeping child like the pocket of air above a diver's face, and closed the lid without latching it fully, because a lid shut too tight looks shut, and a lid shut just-so looks ordinary.

It had seemed, in the gray light of dawn, like a clever thing. Clever things look different at half past seven in the morning with a warrant on the table.

* * *

They searched the way water rises. That was how Silas experienced it, standing in the courtyard with the other servants while boots moved overhead — floor by floor, room by room, an inexorable tide filling the house. Drawers opened above her. Mattresses were stripped. Fenwick's cramped desk was emptied onto the floor, and she heard him say, in his dry voice, "Mind the third ledger, it bites," and heard one of the constables laugh despite himself.

Brigid stood beside her, arms crossed, wearing her best expression of offended domesticity. "Eighteen years I've cooked for this house," she muttered, too low for anyone but Silas. "Eighteen years, and they're going through my linen like common thieves."

"They're looking for a traitor," Silas said.

"I know what they're looking for." Brigid's jaw worked. "I'm saying what I think of their manners."

But her hand found Silas's arm and gripped it, once, hard — a message passed flesh to flesh: *steady*.

Quill fluttered at Hale's elbow, offering the counting rooms first, then reconsidering, then offering them again. Hale ignored him with a patience that looked almost kind and was not. He questioned the servants himself, one at a time, briefly, courteously, and Silas watched each of them come back to the courtyard line slightly paler than they'd left it, and understood that his questions were not really questions. They were mirrors. He held them up and waited to see what flinched.

When her turn came, he gestured her toward the shade of the archway, away from the others, and asked her name.

"Silas Marchetti, sir. Diving crew, freight handling."

"How long indentured?"

"Eight years, sir."

"And Tomas Reyes? You knew him?"

Her heart performed a single enormous beat and then went very quiet, the way the sea goes quiet over a wreck. *Careful.* "Everyone knew Mr. Reyes, sir. He was a clerk here twenty years. He signed our chits. Paid out our letters home."

"You liked him?"

"He was fair," she said, and let the word sit there, neither loyalty nor accusation, just a fact, smooth as river stone. Fair. What could be made of *fair*?

Hale studied her a moment. His eyes were pale gray, unremarkable, and utterly unhurried. "Fair," he repeated, as though tasting it. "That's a careful answer, Miss Marchetti."

"It's the true one, sir."

"Mm." He glanced past her shoulder at the storehouse, where two constables had begun on the ground-floor storerooms. "You work the freight?"

"Shell sorting and hauling, sir."

"Then you'll know if anything in that building is out of order. Anything misplaced. Anything that doesn't belong." His gaze came back to hers, mild as milk. "Would you tell me if it did?"

*Four counts down. Four counts up.* "Yes, sir."

He nodded and dismissed her, and she walked back to the line of servants with her spine straight and her lungs burning, and only Brigid saw how carefully she set one foot in front of the other, like a woman crossing ice.

* * *

They reached the storehouse at mid-morning.

Silas could do nothing but stand in the courtyard and listen. Sound carries strangely in a stone yard; it pools and echoes, and she catalogued every noise the way her father had taught her to catalogue the sounds of a reef — what belongs, what doesn't. Crates dragged. Lids thumped. A constable swore softly at a splinter. Ordinary sounds. All ordinary.

Then: "Inspector! You'll want to see the manifest wall."

Quill's voice, shrill with usefulness. Footsteps. And Silas understood with sick clarity that the manifests listed every crate by number and weight, and that crate thirty-one's recorded weight was the weight of shell alone, and that any man with a scale and ten minutes' patience would find forty pounds of discrepancy where a child lay sleeping.

She began, very quietly, to pray. She hadn't prayed since her father drowned — had concluded, at eleven, with a child's brutal arithmetic, that either God had not been listening or had been listening and declined to act, and neither merited further conversation. But she prayed now, wordlessly, the way you hold your breath: *not her, not her, take anything else, take me, not her.*

Inside the storehouse, Hale's voice, level: "Who verifies outbound weights?"

"The stevedore foreman, sir, and a company clerk countersigns—"

"Get me the counter-sign book."

Footsteps again. Silas's vision narrowed to a gray tunnel edged with light. Beside her, Brigid's hand crept over and gripped hers, crushing hard enough to hurt, and Silas realized distantly that the old woman was shaking.

And then — a sound that did not belong.

A cry. Thin, muffled, unmistakably infant, rising from within the walls of the storehouse like smoke through a floorboard.

* * *

Later, Silas could never reconstruct the next ninety seconds except as fragments, bright and disconnected as shell shards.

A constable's voice: "Something in the crates, sir?"

Hale: silence — a listening silence, terrible in its attention.

Brigid moving. Actually moving, stepping out of the line, shouting in her kitchen voice, the voice that stopped dockhands mid-brawl: "The cats! For God's sake, Inspector, the whole harbor's overrun with them, they get in the walls after the rats and carry on like banshees — Tom! Tom, fetch the broom and clear them out before they foul the shell!"

And Silas, moving without deciding to move, because there is a kind of courage that is only terror wearing work clothes — walking toward the storehouse door with her arms full of nothing, calling out, "Beg pardon, sirs, beg pardon — if there's cats got into the shell crates they'll ruin a whole shipment, the company'll have our wages for it, may I see to it?"

She had never spoken so many words to a company officer in her life. Her voice sounded foreign in her own ears, high and servile and exactly right.

Hale stood in the aisle between the crate rows, hat in hand, head tilted, listening. The cry had come again — fainter, angrier, a hungry newborn's complaint — and Silas's whole soul hung on the space between one wail and the next.

"Cats," Hale said.

"Begging your pardon, sir, yes. Gray ones, mostly. They get under the pier and—" She was beside crate thirty-one now. She could hear Atlas breathing through the wood, a fast small rasp, and beneath it the tick of cooling shell, and beneath everything her own heart slamming like surf.

Hale looked at her. Not at the crate. At her. And Silas understood, in a flash of cold clarity, that he had heard the cry perfectly well, and had identified it perfectly well, and was now watching her — watching her hands, her breathing, the pulse in her throat — to learn what *she* would do about it.

The storehouse went very quiet. Six constables. An under-steward. An inspector. A lie hanging in the salt-smelling air, thin as wire.

"A cat," Hale said again, softly. "In crate thirty-one."

Silas put her hand on the lid. It was the maddest thing she had ever done, and she did it slowly, deliberately, letting him watch her do it — because a guilty person snatches, and an innocent person merely works. She lifted the lid. Reached into the shell. And drew out — by a margin she would spend the rest of her life not thinking about — a handful of shell and, cupped beneath it, the small gray body of an actual cat.

There were cats in the storehouse. There had always been cats in the storehouse. This one had been asleep in crate nineteen, two rows down, and had wandered, or God — who apparently answered prayers after an eight-year silence, and with a cruel sense of theater — had arranged otherwise. It blinked at the daylight with yellow indignation and yowled directly into Inspector Hale's face.

"That's the one," Brigid said from the doorway, with the conviction of scripture. "Vicious brute. Shoo, now!" She advanced, flapping her apron, and the cat leapt from Silas's arms and fled into the rafters, trailing outrage.

Silas let the handful of shell fall back into the crate. Closed the lid. Latched it — properly, fully, the click loud as a pistol — and stepped back with her eyes lowered.

"Sorry for the trouble, sir," she murmured. "Shell's dear this season."

Hale was still watching her. The gray eyes moved from her face to the crate and back, and for a moment — one long, tidal moment — Silas believed it was finished, one way or the other, that he would open the lid himself or turn away forever.

He put on his hat.

"Well," he said pleasantly, to the room at large. "The Crown thanks you for your cooperation. I'll want the counter-sign books sent to my office by evening. And—" his gaze rested on Silas one last instant, light as a gull touching water, "—someone ought to see to those cats properly. They make a deal of noise for creatures that aren't there."

He walked out into the sunlight. The constables followed. Quill scurried after, still promising cooperation to a closing door.

* * *

Silas made it four steps before her legs informed her they were finished negotiating.

She sat down hard on an empty crate, and Brigid was there instantly, one arm around her shoulders, holding her up the way you hold up something that might shatter. Neither spoke. In the rafters, the cat complained once more and fell silent.

Then, from inside crate thirty-one, a small, furious, magnificent squall — the full-throated cry of a baby who has slept through her own funeral and woken hungry.

Brigid laughed. It was a broken, gasping sound, half sob, and she pressed her floury hand over her mouth to stop it, and failed. "Cats," she wheezed. "God forgive me. Cats."

Silas opened the crate with fingers that would not obey her, lifted Atlas out of the sacking — warm, damp, indignant, alive — and held her against her chest, and felt the tiny heart hammering against her own like a second pulse, like a diver's line, like something tethering her to the surface of the world.

"She heard it," Silas whispered. "The inspector. She cried and he heard it, and he looked right at me, and he—" She stopped. Replayed it. The tilt of his head. The hat going on. *Someone ought to see to those cats properly.*

"He knew," she said. The words came out flat with wonder. "Brigid. He knew, and he walked out anyway."

Brigid's laughter died. She looked at the doorway where the inspector had stood, and her old face folded into something grim and thoughtful. "Then that's worse, girl," she said quietly. "A man who doesn't know can be fooled forever. A man who knows and waits—" She crossed herself, quick and old-fashioned, a habit from a country she never named. "A man like that isn't hunting a traitor anymore. He's fishing. And he's just decided the pond's worth his time."

Outside, across the courtyard, through the high storehouse window, Silas watched Inspector Hale pause at the gate. He took out a small notebook, wrote something — one line, unhurried — and put it away.

Then he glanced back at the storehouse. Once. As if confirming an entry.

And walked away down Rope Street, unhurried as the tide coming in.
