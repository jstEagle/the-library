## 9. What the Diver Knows

The inspector's line in his notebook had a weight that did not lift when he walked away. It settled instead into the storehouse rafters, into the grain of the pearl crates, into the sacking Silas still could not stop smelling on her hands — lanolin and brine and the faint sweetness of an infant's scalp. All evening she worked with the baby's warmth ghosted against her chest, though the child herself lay two floors up, sleeping in the false bottom of Brigid's linen trunk, fed and drowsy and utterly unaware that a man from the Crown Constabulary had stood six feet from her crate and written something down.

Silas hauled crates until her arms shook. She counted breaths the way her father taught her — *in for four, hold for four, out for four* — because a diver who panics dies, and a girl who trembles is seen.

When the last crate was stowed and the lamplighter had made his rounds, she climbed the narrow stair to the dormitory attic she shared with three other indentured girls, sat on her pallet with her back to the wall, and let herself remember.

Because there was a question under her ribs now, sharp as a barnacle's edge, and it would not be ignored:

*Why me?*

Why would Tomas Reyes — clerk, traitor, fugitive, the man who once paid a bribe to keep an eleven-year-old out of the workhouse — leave his most dangerous burden at a servants' door? There were safer houses. Richer houses. Houses without inspectors walking their courtyards writing single lines in notebooks. He had chosen the Verrone Trading Company dormitory, and he had chosen her, and he had written *she is yours now if you are brave*, as if bravery were something he knew she carried like a coin sewn into a hem.

She closed her eyes and went down into memory the way her father had gone down into water: slowly, equalizing, hand over hand along the anchor line.

* *

Her father, Aurelio Marchetti, had hands like old rope — braided, salt-stiffened, stronger than they looked. He dove the pearl beds off the southern shoals for eleven years before the debt drowned him, and in all that time he never once told Silas the sea was beautiful.

"It is not beautiful," he said, the night before her seventh birthday, sitting on the edge of the harbor wall with his feet dangling over black water. "It is honest. That is better than beautiful."

He worked then for an independent captain, before Verrone's contracts swallowed every boat on the coast. And it was in those years — she understood now, turning the memories over like stones — that Tomas Reyes came into their lives.

She had been small. What she remembered was impressions: a thin man with ink stains who smelled of paper instead of salt, standing awkwardly on a dock as if docks were foreign countries. Her father laughing — rare enough that each instance had been preserved whole, like insects in amber. The ink-stained man laughing too, badly, as if laughter were a language he'd learned late.

"Who is that?" she had asked once, tugging her father's sleeve.

"A clerk," her father said. "A man who writes things true." He said it the way other men said *a man who fights bravely* or *a man who prays devoutly*. She had been too young to hear the reverence in it. She heard it now.

There was a story her father told only twice, and only after rum, which was rarely, because rum cost money and money was debt. The second telling was the winter before he died, when she was ten, and she had thought at the time it was just another of his sea fables — the kind with too much God in them to be believed.

A storm, he said. Four years back, maybe five. A boat caught outside the breakwater when it came up fast from the southwest, and a man overboard — not a diver, a landsman, a company man caught wrong place wrong hour, swept off a jetty with a case of documents chained to his wrist because company men chained their papers to themselves even in storms, God preserve them from their own caution.

"They pulled the jetty ladder down looking for him," her father said. "Half the harbor turned out with lanterns. Not me. I didn't need a lantern." He tapped his temple. "I know what water does with a man. It doesn't drop him where he falls. It takes him along the bottom current, north-northeast, past the third piling, and lays him in the scour hole under the old ferry pier where the tide pools everything it steals."

"You went down in a storm," Silas said. Even at ten she knew what that meant. Divers died doing that. Good divers. Her mother had been a good diver, and the sea had taken her on a calm day, so what chance in a storm?

"I went down in a storm," her father agreed, and drank.

"And?"

"And I found him. Caught in the pier pilings by the chain, tangled in netting, full of water and mostly gone. I cut him loose and brought him up. His heart was still arguing about it." Her father smiled at his cup. "The papers were gone. Torn free, scattered. He wept more for those papers than he'd have wept for his own legs, I think. Company man."

"What was in the papers?"

Her father had looked at her then — one long look, the look he gave the water before a dive, measuring — and said, "Things that are true. Which is why a certain kind of man chains them to his wrist, and why another kind of man pays to make them untrue." Then he laughed, ruffled her hair, and called her *piccola granchietta*, little crab, and would say no more.

But she remembered the rest of that night. She remembered that within the month, her father's contract had been bought — bought out from the independent captain, transferred to Verrone Trading, and the terms rewritten so that his debt deepened instead of shrank. She remembered her father going quiet after that, working double dives, coming home with his ears bleeding. She had thought it was grief for the drowned man's fate. She had been a child. Children think sorrow is simple.

Now she sat in the attic on Rope Street, nineteen years old, holding a burned letter's ash-weight in her chest, and she did the arithmetic she should have done at eleven: the storm. The rescued man. The transfer to Verrone. The debt that grew teeth. And years later, the bribe from the same ink-stained clerk that kept his friend's daughter out of the workhouse — paid quietly, through channels, unasked.

Tomas Reyes had spent fifteen years paying her family back in installments. Or — the colder thought arrived and stayed — keeping them close. Keeping his rescuer inside the company walls, where he could watch him. Gratitude and strategy wear the same coat; you cannot tell them apart until the weather changes.

And the weather had changed. Reyes stood accused of selling port charts to a foreign navy. The company whispered *traitor* in every office. Director Verrone wanted him buried.

So why leave the child with the daughter of the man who pulled him from the scour hole?

*Unless,* Silas thought, *the charts are not what they say they are.*

*Unless the papers chained to his wrist and the papers he copies now are the same kind of papers: things that are true. The kind a certain kind of man pays to make untrue.*

Her father's voice, from the harbor wall, across nine years: *It is not beautiful. It is honest.*

She pressed her palms against her eyes until she saw salt-patterns.

* *

Sleep did not come, so she did what her father did before a dive: she prepared.

Not the body. The mind. A diver checks his weights, his knife, his air line, his partner's eyes. Silas had no knife, no line, no partner. But she had a floorboard near the wall that lifted, and beneath it the small hoard of a lifetime's invisible economy: three coins, a fishhook, a ribbon gone brittle, and a tin snuffbox that had not been opened in eight years.

She had taken almost nothing from her father's effects. The company took everything else — debts swallow estates whole in this city — but the company's inventory men had no interest in a diver's personal tokens, worthless brass. She had kept one thing and hidden it even from herself, because to open the box was to open the harbor wall night, the storm story, the smell of rum and blood-ears and honesty.

She opened it now.

Inside, on a bed of dust, lay half a diving token.

Brass, palm-sized, stamped with the crest of the old Southern Shoal divers' guild — a conch shell split by a trident — and cut clean down the middle, jagged-edged, the way such tokens were sometimes broken in the old days. Before the companies standardized everything, divers sealed partnerships by breaking a token in two: half for the man who holds your air line, half for the man who goes down. Two halves made one promise. *Your breath is mine to guard; my body is yours to find.*

Her father's half. Given to her the week before he drowned, without explanation, folded into her hand at the door like a man settling accounts.

"He told you stories," Reyes's letter had said — no, the letter had said less than that, but the letter was ash and its meaning remained: *he trusted your father.* She hadn't understood the shape of that trust until this moment, holding the broken brass to the moonlight through the attic's one window.

And then she turned it, and saw what eight years of grief had kept her from seeing.

The token was engraved. On the face, the guild crest. On the reverse, along the break-line, fine letters worn nearly smooth: numbers. A latitude and longitude, half of one — cut off clean at the break, as if whoever engraved it had intended the other half to carry the rest.

And below the numbers, smaller, a word in Spanish that her father had taught her to read without knowing he was teaching her, the way divers' children learn the names of things:

*Faro.*

Lighthouse.

Silas sat very still. In for four. Hold for four. Out for four.

This was not her father's keepsake. Or rather — it was, but it had not begun as his. She remembered now, dredging the storm story's margins: after the rescue, after the papers were lost, there had been something given in thanks. She had assumed coins. Children assume coins. But her father had come home that week with a brass token in his pocket and a new expression, part wariness and part oath, and within a month the company owned his debt.

Reyes had given him the token. Reyes, whose sister's family were island people, lighthouse people, the kind who still broke tokens and traded coordinates the old way. Half a token was a map that required a partner. You could not follow it alone. That was the entire point — a promise that could only be kept by two.

Her father had died before explaining. Or had chosen silence, because silence was survival, and he had a daughter to keep alive.

But Reyes had not forgotten. Fifteen years later, hunted, accused, his own neck worth less than a forged chart, he had carried the child not to a safe house but to the door of the one family he believed still held his half of a promise. *She is not mine, but she is yours now if you are brave.* Not sentiment. Recognition. He had looked at the grown silhouette of little Marchetti's daughter and seen the other half of a broken token.

Silas closed her fist around the brass until its edges bit.

The child upstairs was not a burden dropped on her. She was a message. And somewhere in this city or beyond it, there was a matching half — and beside it, scratched or engraved or waiting, the rest of the coordinates that pointed at a lighthouse, and whatever truth Reyes had chained to his wrist and refused to drown.

* *

Dawn found her in the courtyard with the first crates, hands moving, ledger neat, head down. Invisible. She had gotten very good at invisible; it had kept her fed for eight years. But invisibility, she was learning, has a twin: watching without being watched, and she practiced that now with every sense she had.

The storehouse manifest board listed incoming freight. She read it in passing, the way clerks read weather. Three coastal packets due within the fortnight. And among the berthing notices chalked by the harbormaster's runner, one entry her eye snagged on and would not release:

*Pier 4 — closed for piling repair — by order.*

Old ferry pier. North-northeast past the third piling. The scour hole where the tide pools everything it steals — including, perhaps, a matching half of brass, nailed or mortared or sunk where a grateful man and a clever one might have agreed, years ago, that a message could wait.

They had closed Pier 4. Which meant either the company suspected it, or the constabulary was watching it, or both — and none of that mattered as much as the fact that the tide would be low tonight, low enough to reach under the pilings, low enough that a girl who had learned breath-counting from the best diver on the southern shoals could hold hers long enough to search the dark water where her father had once carried a drowning clerk toward the light.

Brigid passed her in the corridor with the breakfast pails and did not look at her, which between them meant *the child slept, the child is fed, we say nothing.* Fenwick was already at his desk, pen scratching, one leg braced on his stool, wearing the careful blankness he thought hid his fear. Above them all, in the corner office with the good window, Director Verrone's shutters stood open to the morning like a polite mouth.

Tonight, then. Low tide at half past eleven; she had checked the tide table while pretending to file shipping notices, and the irony of using the company's own ledgers to plan against it sat in her chest warm as contraband.

She counted crates. Counted breaths. Counted the hours until dark.

And in her apron pocket, wrapped in a scrap of sacking, half a token rode against her hip like a second heartbeat — jagged, patient, waiting eight years underwater for someone to come down and finish the sentence her father never lived to speak.
