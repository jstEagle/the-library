## 10. Company Men

The office at the top of the Verrone Trading House had been built to do half its work before a man opened his mouth. Augusto Verrone had designed it himself, down to the last brass fitting: the desk of black island walnut placed so that any visitor must sit facing the tall windows and the harbor beyond, so that the director's silhouette would always stand between his guest and the light; the shelves of leather-bound ledgers arranged not for reference but for effect — twenty years of immaculate record-keeping, gleaming like a cathedral wall; the single chair across from the desk set lower than any man expected, so that even Inspector Hale, who was not a small man, found himself looking up by an inch he could not quite account for.

Verrone liked that inch. He had bought it with the rest.

"Tea?" he asked, lifting the porcelain pot before Hale could answer. He poured anyway. He had learned long ago that a man offered tea in the right room almost never refused it, and every refusal you did not have to fight for was a small victory banked against the day you needed a large one. "You'll forgive me if I don't take sugar. The doctor lectures me."

"You look well, Director," said Hale, in the tone of a man stating a weather report.

"I am well. I am fifty-one years old, my gout is behaving itself, and my house is the most profitable it has been in nine years." Verrone settled back and folded his hands over his waistcoat. "Which brings me, naturally, to treason."

Hale's cup paused halfway to his mouth. It was a good performance. Verrone gave him credit for it. The inspector was better than most of the constabulary's products — careful, courteous, with eyes that moved around a room the way a customs man's fingers moved through cargo. But he was still an employee of the Crown, and the Crown, whatever else it was, was an institution, and institutions understood the language Verrone spoke fluently.

"You find treason amusing?" Hale asked.

"I find it *expensive*." Verrone let the word sit on the black walnut between them. "Do you know what happens when word gets round the wharves that a clerk of this company sold port charts to a foreign navy? I will tell you, because you are a methodical man and I respect method. First, the insurance syndicates reprice our hulls. Second, two of the shipping houses we carry for begin taking quiet meetings with Halloran & Sons, who have been circling our contracts for three years like gulls after a gut bucket. Third — and this is the part that keeps me at my desk past midnight, Inspector — the Admiralty reviews its charter agreements with every trading firm in this harbor, including mine, and asks itself whether it wants to trust its charts to a house that cannot keep its own clerks honest." He spread his hands, a gesture of helpless reasonableness he had practiced in front of the mirror until it looked like something no one had taught him. "Whether Reyes is guilty or innocent matters enormously to him, to the courts, and presumably to God. To me it matters only how quickly it stops being discussed."

"And how quickly would you like it to stop being discussed?"

"Before the month's end. A trial, a verdict, a sentence. Tidy and fast."

Hale drank his tea. Outside the window, a crane swung a net of pearl shell over the quay, and the shells clicked together like teeth. Verrone watched the inspector watch the harbor, and waited. Patience was the whole art of this business. Men thought commerce was about money, but money merely kept score; commerce was about knowing how long to let silence do your arguing.

"The trial date is set by the assizes," Hale said at last. "Not by me."

"The assizes respond to the completeness of the file before them. A complete file goes quickly. An incomplete one wanders." Verrone rose, crossed to the shelf, and drew down a ledger — not Ledger Nine, never Nine, Nine was ash and river mud by now, or close enough — but a volume from 1907, chosen for its thickness. He laid it on the desk as though it were evidence. "May I show you something?"

"If you like."

Verrone opened the ledger to a ribboned page and turned it toward Hale. Columns of figures, neat as prayer. "Every manifest that entered this harbor under my seal in 1907. Every tonnage, every duty paid, every signature. When the Crown comes to my house and says *one of your men betrayed us*, I can open this book and say: here is everything, all of it, in order. That order is my reputation, Inspector. It took twenty years to build and it is worth more than the ships, more than the warehouses, more than the name on the door. A scandal that drags on for a season does not merely embarrass me. It invites every rival, every auditor, every ambitious junior official in this city to start turning pages out of idle curiosity." He closed the book gently, the way one closes a coffin. "And pages, once turned by curious men, have a way of being misread."

There it was. Not a threat — Verrone did not issue threats, threats were the currency of men without assets — but a map of consequences, drawn politely, with the borders marked in advance.

Hale set down his cup. "Are you telling me there are pages in these ledgers that might be misread, Director?"

"I am telling you that complexity breeds error, and error breeds delay, and delay is what ruins reputations — mine, and if I may say so, yours." Verrone returned to his chair and lowered himself into it with care; the gout was in the left foot today. "You have been in this city eighteen months, Inspector. Your superiors in the Constabulary know you by your conviction rate, which is excellent, and by your discretion, which I am told is also excellent. A treason case concluded swiftly reflects credit on the man who concluded it. A treason case that sprawls — witnesses scattered, accomplices rumored, documents disputed — begins to smell like incompetence. Whose incompetence? The arresting officer's. That is the arithmetic they do in the Commissioner's office, and they do it without sentiment."

He watched Hale receive that. The inspector's face did nothing vulgar, nothing so legible as a flinch, but something behind the eyes recalculated — the small shift of a man checking his own accounts. Good. Hale had ambition; everyone did; ambition was simply the lever, and Verrone had spent thirty years learning where each man's lever was set.

"I investigate where the evidence leads," Hale said.

"Of course you do. I would expect nothing less." Verrone smiled, warm as lamplight. "I ask only that you distinguish between *leads* and *noise*. There will be noise in this case, Inspector. There always is, when a man flees. Servants claiming they saw him. Clerks remembering letters. Some pearl-diver's daughter or fishwife swearing she knows something, because in this city everyone believes themselves a witness to history. Noise. You are too good an officer to chase it into every alley."

"And if the noise turns out to be signal?"

"Then you will follow it with my blessing and my full cooperation." Verrone spread his hands again. "I hide nothing, Inspector. My books are open to the Crown whenever the Crown wishes. That openness is precisely why I can afford to be patient about everything except gossip. Ask yourself — what does a fleeing traitor take with him? Money, false papers, a berth on some tramp steamer. What does he not take? Ledgers. Ledgers are heavy, and they convict. If Reyes had anything true to trade, he would have traded it already, to you, to the Admiralty, to the newspapers. He hasn't. Therefore he has nothing. Therefore the case is simple. Simple cases deserve quick conclusions, for everyone's sake."

It was, Verrone reflected, one of his better constructions. Every sentence load-bearing, every joint hidden.

Hale stood, buttoning his coat. "I'll see the file complete before the assizes."

"I know you will." Verrone walked him to the door himself — a courtesy he extended rarely, and never without purpose. At the threshold he paused, as if struck by a small afterthought. "One thing more. The household staff here — the indentured workers, the dormitory children. Reyes had access to them for years. If he left anything behind — messages, materials, confidences — it will surface among the small people first. They notice everything and report nothing; it is their nature. Be thorough with them. Thoroughness protects them, too. A girl who sheltered a traitor, knowingly or not, would find her life very short and very ugly once the mob heard of it. Better she is cleared early, don't you think?"

"A girl," Hale repeated. His voice gave away nothing at all now, which told Verrone something useful: he had already spoken to someone among the small people, and filed it.

"There is always a girl," Verrone said mildly. "In every story ever told in this city, there is always a girl. Usually holding something she shouldn't. Good day, Inspector."

He watched from the window as Hale crossed the quay below, unhurried, pausing to speak to the crane operator, then to a tally-clerk, then standing a moment at the foot of Rope Street with his head slightly tilted, the posture of a man listening to a building. Verrone's smile faded by degrees, like heat leaving iron.

* * *

Alone, he poured himself brandy he did not offer to anyone, and permitted himself, for exactly four minutes by the clock on the mantel, the luxury of fear.

Reyes. Tomas Reyes, who had been with the house nineteen years, who had signed manifests in a hand so small and regular it looked printed, who had once sat in the very chair Hale had just vacated and thanked Verrone, with tears in his old stupid loyal eyes, for advancing him three months' wages when his sister was ill. Reyes, who had gone into the counting-room cellar on the night of the fourteenth of March and come out again at two in the morning, and Verrone's own night man had sworn afterward — after it no longer mattered — that the clerk had carried nothing but a portfolio thin enough to slide beneath a coat.

Thin enough. That was the phrase that fed the four minutes of fear. A thick ledger could be recovered; a thick ledger was furniture, it stayed where it was put, and men could be persuaded to put things back. But copied pages, folded small, were a contagion. They replicated. They traveled. God alone knew where the original portfolio had gone, and copies — copies bred in dark rooms by candlelight, and every copy was another mouth that could open.

And what was in those pages — Verrone drained the brandy and let himself think it plainly, because a man who could not think plainly to himself was already finished — what was in those pages was not charts. Charts had been convenient. Charts were treason, and treason brought the Crown down on a man's head like a dropped chandelier, and while the whole city stared up at the glittering wreckage, nobody examined what lay underneath the floor.

Pearl freight, the manifests said. Pearl freight, twelve consignments over fourteen months, crates weighed and sealed and stamped by the house, insured as pearl shell, taxed as pearl shell. Rifles and cartridges, in fact, crated in straw, moving up the coast to buyers whose names existed nowhere in ink and whose payments arrived in gold through channels that made the ledgers sing. The margin had been obscene. The risk had been manageable — had been *nothing*, truly, because every document was forged, every witness purchased or promoted or dead, and the one clerk sharp enough to reconcile crate weights against manifest tonnage had been the most trusted man in the building.

Until he wasn't. Until the fourteenth of March, when the most trusted man in the building had spent two hours in the cellar with Ledger Nine and walked out carrying the difference between what the house declared and what the house did.

So: the accusation. Sell it as treason, the one crime the Crown pursued faster than fraud. Let the Constabulary hunt Reyes across three ports while the Crown's own fury buried the question of *what else* the man had seen. It had worked for five weeks. It was still working. But working cases decayed, like fish in the sun, and Verrone had built twenty years of order on the certainty that everything decays unless it is nailed shut.

Hale worried him more than he showed. The man was not corruptible in the ordinary ways — Verrone's people had checked, quietly, through three separate intermediaries, and the report came back distasteful: no debts, no women, no drink, no aged mother in a rented room. A clean man was expensive to move. But clean men could still be *aimed*, and Verrone had aimed him today at his own career, which was the surest target. Ambition, properly addressed, did the work that gold could not.

The clock gave its small dry click: four minutes ended. Fear dismissed, filed, folded away until its next scheduled appointment. Verrone rang the bell.

"Send Mr. Corvo up," he told the boy. "And then go down to the dormitory floor. Count the children. All of them, by name. Report to Mr. Corvo, not to me."

The boy blinked at that — counting children was laundry-matron work — but a boy raised in this house learned early that instructions arrived without explanations, and went.

Corvo came up the stair soft-footed, hat in hand, a gray unremarkable man whose genius was that no one had ever described him accurately twice. Verrone did not offer him a chair. Corvo did not expect one.

"The inspector is following the case properly now," Verrone said. "But he is thorough by temperament, and thoroughness is a tide — it rises wherever there is low ground. So we will drain the low ground before it reaches him. Three things."

Corvo produced a pencil and a fold of paper, ceremonially.

"First: Reyes's old quarters, his desk, the cellar strong-room — anything the Constabulary has not already catalogued should be catalogued by us first, and adjusted where adjustment is needed. Second: the missing ledger. Ledger Nine. I want it found before the Crown finds its absence remarkable, and I want whoever removed it found faster. It did not walk out on its own legs. Someone carried it, or someone copied it, and either way that someone has been eating my bread for six weeks."

"And third?"

Verrone crossed to the window again. Below, the evening shift was coming onto the quay, and among the small figures moving between the crates he could pick out, by size and stoop, the indentured ones — the children of the drowned and the indebted, the ones the house owned on paper until their paper ran out. One of them would be the girl. There was always a girl. Hale had gone quiet at the mention of one, and quiet men had already found something.

"Third," Verrone said, "there is a rumor I want confirmed or killed tonight. That a bundle was left somewhere on my property in the week Reyes fled, and that it was taken in rather than handed to the authorities." He watched the quay, watching the small figures. "A child, most likely. Reyes was sentimental in the ways that ruin careful men. Find out who holds it, and where, and how much light falls on that doorway at night. Nothing more. No hands on anyone yet — the house is watched too closely just now. But I want the answer before Inspector Hale thinks to ask the question. Do you understand the shape of it?"

"Before, not after," said Corvo.

"*Well* before." Verrone turned from the window, and his voice remained pleasant, unhurried, the voice of a man discussing freight rates. "Because when the Crown finally pulls a thread out of this house, Mr. Corvo, I intend that every thread it finds leads somewhere harmless — and that the one thread it never quite reaches is tied off, cut short, and burned before it can be pulled. Go."

When the door had closed behind the gray man, Verrone stood a moment longer among his beautiful ordered ledgers, listening to the harbor sounds rise up through the floor — winches, gulls, the click of shells like teeth.

Somewhere below his feet, in the dormitory warrens of Rope Street, a child was sleeping who should not exist, and a girl with diver's hands was keeping a secret that belonged to him.

He did not know her name yet.

He resolved, pouring a second brandy against the doctor's advice, that by week's end he would.
