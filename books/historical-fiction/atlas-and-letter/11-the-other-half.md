## 11. The Other Half

The token had not left her hand all day.

Silas had learned long ago how to carry a secret in plain sight — the way she carried hunger, the way she carried grief — folded small and pressed flat against the body where no one thought to look. The diving token was no bigger than a coin, but heavier than any coin she'd ever held, and by lamplight that evening, after Brigid had gone up to bed and Fenwick's candle had guttered out behind his ledger room door, she turned it over for the hundredth time and finally understood what it was asking of her.

It was brass, worn nearly smooth on one face, stamped with the crest of the old Pearl Divers' Guild — a shell split open, a star above it — and along the rim, cut deep enough to survive decades of salt water, ran a line of engraving so fine she'd needed to hold it at an angle to catch it:

*THE SEA KEEPS WHAT THE SEA IS GIVEN.*

Her father used to say those words. He said them the way other men said prayers, before every descent, before he slipped beneath the green-black water with a rope around his waist and his breath held like a coin in his fist. *The sea keeps what the sea is given,* he'd murmur, and then he'd be gone, down into the cold, and Silas would count — one, two, three — counting his breaths for him from the surface because he'd taught her that even when a man is underwater, someone above must breathe for him.

She hadn't known until tonight that the words were also an address.

Because there, beneath the guild crest, half-hidden by a scratch that might have been age or might have been deliberate, was a second mark: a small circle bisected by a line, like a coin seen edge-on. Like a moon over water. And below it, three tiny nicks — one, two, three — as if someone had counted something. Breaths. Piers. Tides.

Tomas Reyes had given this token to her father. She remembered now — remembered the evening clearly, though she'd been small, seven or eight, standing in the doorway of their rented room while a thin man with ink-stained fingers and a clerk's soft voice sat across from her father at the table. They'd spoken low. Her father had laughed once, a short bark of a laugh she rarely heard, and then the man had pressed something into Papa's palm and closed Papa's fingers over it, and Papa had looked at the thing for a long time without opening his hand, and then he'd put it away inside his shirt, against his skin, where he kept the only things that mattered: her mother's ring, and later, the paper of his debt.

The debt that drowned him. The debt that had made her property of the Verrone Trading Company at eleven years old.

And now Tomas Reyes — the man who paid the bribe that kept her out of the workhouse, the man who stood accused of selling port charts to a foreign navy, the man who had left a baby at her door with instructions to forget its face — was asking her, through a piece of brass her father had carried against his heart for years, to remember instead.

*She is not mine, but she is yours now if you are brave.*

Brave. The word sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

Upstairs, in the linen closet behind the third-floor washroom, the baby slept. Silas had fed her at dusk — Brigid's goat's milk warmed in a chipped cup, dripped through a cloth into the small furious mouth — and the child had gone down without complaint, which felt like a mercy and a rebuke at once. She didn't know yet what she was. A secret. A crime. A reason.

Silas wrapped the token in a scrap of sacking and tucked it into the lining of her boot.

Then she waited for the house to sleep.

* * *

Rope Street never truly slept, but it pretended to. By midnight the lamps were down to embers behind their glass, the taverns had gone quiet except for the sound of a man weeping somewhere near the cooperage, and the tide had come in far enough to swallow the mudflats and slap softly, patiently, at the pilings of the harbor piers. The air smelled of brine and tar and rotting fish heads, the smell Silas had been born into, the smell that meant home to no one and everything to her.

She moved the way her father had taught her to move through a market crowd: shoulders loose, eyes down, hands busy-looking. A girl alone at midnight drew attention; a girl who walked like she had errands did not. She'd stolen Brigid's shawl off the drying line and pulled it over her hair, and she carried an empty milk pail in one hand because a girl carrying a pail was invisible in a way a girl carrying nothing could never be.

The harbor opened before her like a black mouth.

She had forgotten, in four years of warehouse walls and ledgers, how vast the water was at night. By day the harbor was commerce — cranes and crates and shouting stevedores, the company's barges riding low with pearl freight that everyone knew wasn't only pearl freight, though no one said so. But at night the water took everything back. It lay flat and gleaming under a half-moon, and the masts of the moored ships scratched at the sky like a forest of dead trees, and somewhere out past the breakwater a bell buoy tolled with the swell, slow and patient, like a heart beating under the world.

One, two, three. She counted the swells without meaning to. Counting breaths for a man underwater. Old habit. Older than memory.

Three nicks on the token. Three piers.

There were eleven piers along the harbor front, numbered from the customs house eastward, but only three of them belonged to the old days — the days before Verrone bought the waterfront lock, stock, and barrel. Pier Two had burned and been rebuilt twice. Pier Seven was the company's own, guarded even at night. But Pier Four — Pier Four was the oldest, the one the divers had used back when the Guild still held its own charter, the one with the rusted crane nobody used anymore and the steps down to the water worn hollow by a century of bare feet.

Pier Four. Where else would a diver's token point?

She told herself this on the walk down, and she almost believed it, and believing it was the only thing that kept her feet moving. Because another part of her — the part that kept her head down and her ledger neat, the part that had survived four years of indenture by wanting nothing and asking less — that part was screaming at her to turn around. To go back to Rope Street. To climb the stairs to the linen closet and stand watch over the sleeping child until morning, and let the brass token stay buried in her boot until it wore through the leather and dropped into some gutter and was done with her forever.

But the baby's face floated behind her eyes. Small, red-lidded, furious with life. *Forget this face.* As if faces worked that way. As if anything worked that way.

The sea keeps what the sea is given. Fine. Then let the sea keep her fear. She had given it over, walking down here, and she would not take it back.

Pier Four rose ahead of her out of the dark, black timber on black water, its pilings furred with barnacle and weed. No lamp burned at its head. The crane loomed against the moonlight like a gallows that had repented too late. She stopped at the top of the pier steps and listened — the slap of water, the creak of old wood settling, the far-off toll of the buoy — and heard nothing that breathed besides herself.

She went down the steps slowly, testing each one. The third step gave slightly under her weight, rotten at the core, and she froze, one hand on the rail, until the wood stopped groaning. Below her, under the deck of the pier, the space between the pilings was a cathedral of shadow — crossbeams overhead, water below, and everywhere the drip and suck of the tide working at the structure like a patient animal gnawing a bone.

The token said *under*. She was sure of it now, standing here, feeling the logic of it settle into place the way a key settles into a lock. A man who wanted to leave a message that would last — a message for another man of the sea, a message that no constable or company spy would ever think to look for — nailed it under a pier, below the waterline of ordinary attention, where only someone who knew the language of divers would ever go looking. *The sea keeps what the sea is given.* You give your secret to the sea, and the sea holds it, and only the ones who know how to ask get it back.

But the tide. Her stomach tightened. If the token was nailed below the high-water mark, it might be underwater right now. She looked at the pilings nearest her, following them down into the black water, trying to judge the line of the tide by the wet shine on the timber. High tide had been an hour ago. The water was already retreating, leaving a glistening band of weed and barnacle exposed, dripping.

She crouched at the bottom step and reached out over the water, running her fingers along the underside of the first crossbeam she could reach. Rough wood. Crusted barnacles that bit like teeth. Nothing.

The second beam. Nothing.

The third beam was farther out, over deeper water, and reaching it meant leaning out past her balance with nothing below her but the harbor. She looked down at the water — black, opaque, holding whatever it held — and her father's voice came to her, unbidden, from a summer morning ten years gone: *Never reach for what you're afraid of, little fish. Step toward it. Reach, and you fall. Step, and you arrive.*

There was a ledge — a rotted plank jutting from the pier's edge, wide enough for one foot, maybe. She set her milk pail down. She took off her boots, both of them, and tied their laces together and hung them around her neck, because her father had taught her that too: never work the water in boots. Barefoot, she could feel what the wood was telling her. Barefoot, she was her father's daughter and not a warehouse drudge, and the difference mattered more than she could say.

She stepped onto the plank. It held. The harbor moved beneath her, breathing.

She leaned out along the beam, arm extended to the shoulder, fingers walking blind through barnacle and slime, and the cold spray of the water touched her knuckles, and she counted — one, two, three — counting her own breaths now, because no one above was breathing for her —

Her fingers found metal.

Not smooth metal. Nailed metal — a disc, small, flush against the wood, driven home with two nails whose heads she could feel like tiny eyes staring back at her. Her heart slammed so hard she was sure the whole pier heard it. She worked her fingernails under the disc's edge. It resisted. Salt had welded it to the timber, eight years of tides sealing it shut like a wound healing over a splinter. She dug. The barnacles cut her fingertips. She dug harder, and the disc shifted, groaned, and tore free with a sound like a tooth coming out.

She pulled it to her chest and stepped back onto the pier, shaking, bleeding, grinning like a madwoman in the dark.

* * *

She knelt at the top of the steps where the moonlight reached, and she held the two halves of the token side by side, and her breath went out of her in a long white ribbon.

They matched. Of course they matched — the same brass, the same wear, the same guild crest split cleanly down the middle, her father's half bearing the shell and the words, this half bearing the star and the numbers. When Tomas Reyes had pressed the whole token into her father's palm that evening long ago, it had been whole. Later — she couldn't know when, couldn't know why — it had been broken in two, and each man had kept his half, the way divers broke coins and shared them with partners, so that neither could claim a salvage alone. Proof of partnership. Proof of trust.

Proof that whatever her father had done for Tomas Reyes — whatever secret he had kept, whatever silence he had sold at the price of his own drowning debt — Tomas Reyes had never forgotten it. Had carried his half for years. Had trusted it to find her.

And beside the star, scratched into the brass with a sharp point in a hand she recognized — the same careful, clerkish hand that had written *forget this face* — were the coordinates.

Numbers. Degrees and minutes, latitude and longitude, etched shallow but precise, the handwriting of a man who spent his life making figures legible. She read them twice, three times, fixing them in her mind the way she fixed ledger columns, because she would not write them down, would not risk them, would carry them the way her father had carried his half-token: against the skin, where only death could take them.

North of the harbor. Up the coast. Far enough that it wasn't a walk — it was a journey. Somewhere past the headland, past the fishing villages, past the edge of the map she knew. A place marked to the minute, as precisely as a man could mark a place without drawing a chart.

A place where a fugitive could wait.

She sat back on her heels. The buoy bell tolled out past the breakwater, slow and patient. Her fingers were bleeding freely now, stinging with salt, and she found she didn't care. Above her the crane stood silent against the stars, and the water slapped the pilings, and somewhere behind her the city slept on top of its secrets the way the sea slept on top of its drowned.

It was one thing, she thought, to hide a baby. Hiding was survival, and survival she knew — she had been schooled in it since she was eleven years old, drilled in it daily, examined on it hourly. Hiding required nothing but silence and busy hands.

This was different. This was a direction. Numbers pointing north along a coast patrolled by company men, toward a man the crown called a traitor, carrying a secret that could hang them both. Following these numbers meant leaving Rope Street in the dark. Meant lying to Brigid, or worse, telling her. Meant money she didn't have, papers she couldn't show, roads she had no right to walk on an indenture that bound her body to the Verrone Trading Company like a fence binds a field.

Meant becoming, for the first time in her life, visible.

She looked at the two halves of the token lying in her bleeding palm, the broken coin made whole again, and she thought about her father at the bottom of the harbor with his rope cut and his lungs full, and how the company had written *misadventure* in the ledger and charged his debt to his daughter, and how she had signed her name — or made her mark, she'd barely been able to write then — on the paper that sold her. She had been given things her whole life. Debts. Orders. Silence.

This was the first thing anyone had ever *left* her. Not a debt. A door.

She closed her fist around the brass until the edges bit her cuts, and she made the promise again, out loud this time, a whisper the tide took instantly: "I'll find him."

Behind her, back up Rope Street, a whistle sounded — two notes, short and sharp, the night watchman's call that meant *all quiet*, that meant *nothing moving, nothing wrong*. Except something was moving. Something had been moving since the night a bundle appeared at the servants' door, and it was moving now, north along a scratched line of numbers, and it would not stop.

She put her boots back on. She picked up her empty milk pail. She became, once more, a girl with errands, walking home through the dark with her head down and her hands full of nothing.

But inside her boot, wrapped in sacking, the two halves of a broken token rode against her ankle, warm from her skin, and every step toward Rope Street was also, somehow, a step north.

She did not see the figure at the end of the pier road — a broad man in a company coat, smoking, watching the harbor with the idle patience of a man being paid to notice things. He saw her, though. A servant girl, a milk pail, nothing worth a second glance. He flicked his ash into the water and looked back at the ships.

He would remember her tomorrow.
