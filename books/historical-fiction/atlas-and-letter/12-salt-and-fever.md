## 12. Salt and Fever

Brigid Coyle had cooked for forty mouths through two dockside fevers, one flood, and the winter the Verrone Trading Company let three of its indentured children go hungry rather than admit a bookkeeping error, and she had learned long ago that sickness in a crowded house traveled faster than gossip. So when Silas came down to the kitchen on Thursday morning with the baby wrapped against her chest and a look on her face like a woman carrying water in a cracked jug, Brigid knew before she touched the child's brow that the week was about to get worse.

"She's hot," Silas said. Just that. As if saying more would make it true.

Brigid wiped her hands on her apron and held them out. "Give her here, then."

The baby came into her arms — small still, though she'd been filling out these last weeks, getting the fat pads on her thighs that meant milk was doing its work, getting a personality too, a little imperious scowl she'd inherited from nobody anyone would ever name aloud. Brigid had raised four of her own back in County Clare before the sea took two of them and poverty took the other two in slower ways, and her arms remembered everything. They made the cradle without asking her permission.

The heat came off the infant's skull like a stone left near the fire.

"How hot," Brigid said, "and since when."

"Since last night. I thought it was just — she was fussy. She wouldn't nurse properly. And this morning she—" Silas's voice did something Brigid had never heard it do in eight years of knowing the girl: it broke. "She cried all night and then this morning she went quiet, and quiet is worse, isn't it? Quiet is worse."

"Quiet can be worse," Brigid admitted, because lying to a frightened mother — and that's what Silas was now, whatever papers said, whatever either of them pretended on ordinary days — helped no one. She turned the baby gently, looked at her eyes, at the skin pulled taut over flushed cheeks, at the shallow fluttering breath. She pressed her lips to the small forehead. Fever, yes, and deep in the chest a rattle when the child breathed out, a sound like sand shifting under water.

"There's wet in her lungs," Brigid said. "Or coming. That's what the quiet is. She's spending all her strength on breathing."

Silas's hands had gone white where they gripped the edge of the worktable. "What do we do?"

*We*, Brigid noted. The girl said *we* now, not *I*. Somewhere along these past weeks the secret had stopped being Silas's alone and become a shared thing, passed between them in the dark like a coal too hot to hold alone. Brigid supposed she'd let that happen. She supposed she'd even wanted it.

"What we do," Brigid said, setting the baby down carefully in the laundry basket lined with clean sacking where the child slept most days, hidden behind the flour sacks whenever footsteps came unexpected, "is what any sensible woman does with a sick child. We get medicine."

"From where?"

And there it was. The whole rotten problem laid out in six words, hanging between them in the steam of the morning's porridge pots.

Because medicine wasn't like milk. Milk could be got quiet — Brigid had been buying a pint here, a pint there from a widow off Rope Street whose own babe was weaned, paying with coins skimmed from the housekeeping ledger where a careful eye might call it waste or spoilage but never theft. But a fever in the lungs wanted quinine, or ipecac, or at minimum the camphor liniment and a proper fever draught from an apothecary, and apothecaries in this city did not sell to servant girls with coin and no questions. They sold to households. To names. A purchase of medicine was written down, entered, signed — a paper trail with a date at the top, sitting in some shopkeeper's book like a lit fuse waiting for someone to come along with a match and a warrant.

Everyone knew the Constabulary had been back twice since Inspector Hale's search. Everyone knew the clerks upstairs were asked to account for every face in the dormitory, every mouth at every meal. Fenwick had spent an entire evening rewriting the roster so it would show exactly the number of children it was supposed to show, no more, no fewer, his bad leg propped on a stool and his pen scratching like a trapped moth until dawn.

"We cannot buy it as ourselves," Silas said. It wasn't a question. She'd already done the arithmetic; Brigid could see it running behind her eyes.

"No," Brigid said. "We can't."

* * *

The solution, when it came, was ugly in the way honest solutions usually were.

Brigid had been keeping the kitchen accounts for eleven years, and in eleven years she had learned every seam in the company's paper. There were chits — authorization slips — for household purchases above a certain sum, signed by the housekeeper or, in her absence, by any senior servant trusted with the seal. The company seal itself lived in Director Verrone's outer office, but the housekeeping stamp, a battered brass thing shaped like an anchor, lived in a drawer in Brigid's own pantry, because the company had never considered a cook important enough to steal anything, and importance, Brigid had found, was mostly a matter of other people's imagination.

There was also Mrs. Quill, the housekeeper, who took her gin in town on Thursday evenings and did not return until the lamps were lit, and whose signature Brigid had watched often enough across eleven years to reproduce — the hard downstroke of the Q, the impatient tail on the final *s* that always trailed off the line like a woman leaving a room mid-sentence.

"I'll write it myself," Brigid said, in the pantry, with the door shut and the baby breathing that terrible small rattling breath in Silas's arms. "Chit for medicinal supplies, household use, authorized under Quill's hand. You take it to Halloran's on Candle Street — not the apothecary on the harbor road, that one's friendly with the company men. Halloran's doesn't ask names, just stamps his own ledger and takes his money."

"And if anyone checks—"

"Then Mrs. Quill denies it, and I lose my post, and likely you're questioned, and likely they find her." Brigid nodded at the basket, at the small flushed face. "That's the shape of it. I won't lie to you about the shape."

Silas stood very still. This was the thing about the girl that had always set her apart from the others in the dormitory, back when she was small and silent and hauling crates twice her size: she never flinched from the true weight of a thing. Other children would have cried, or begged, or promised not to let it happen. Silas just stood and held the whole truth of it until her arms got used to the load.

"The fever could break on its own," Silas said quietly. "You said yourself sometimes it does. If we wait—"

"If we wait and it doesn't break," Brigid said, "you'll be standing at her grave with a chit you never forged, and you'll wish to God you'd forged ten." She reached up to the high shelf, brought down the brass anchor stamp and the ink pad worn smooth as an old coin, and set them on the shelf beside a half-pound of butter like a woman laying out tools for any other kind of work. "Some things you pay for going in, love. Some you pay for going back. This one you pay going in."

Silas looked at the stamp. Then at the baby. Then at Brigid, and what was in her face was worse than fear — it was guilt arriving early, taking its seat before the crime was even done, making itself comfortable.

"This is mine," Silas said. "If it goes wrong. You understand that. It's my promise. It shouldn't be your name on it."

"It won't be your name on it either, that's the whole point of forging, girl." But Brigid softened, because underneath the stubbornness she heard what the girl was really saying — *don't let this cost you, you've paid enough, everyone I love pays for what I chose.* "Listen to me. Eleven years I've cooked for this company, and in eleven years it has taken my knees, my eyes, and the better part of my life, and given me a room and a roster number. It has never once asked me what I loved. Well, now I know the answer, and it's sleeping in a laundry basket, and if the price of it is a signature that isn't mine on a slip of paper, then the company can send the bill to hell."

She wrote the chit while Silas watched. Her hand only shook a little. The Q came out hard, the final *s* trailed off the line like a woman leaving a room mid-sentence, and Brigid thought, absurdly, *Mrs. Quill, forgive me, you old gin-soaked dragon, I've made a better version of you than you make of yourself.*

"Powdered quinine, fever draught, camphor liniment," she said, folding the chit once. "Say it's for the cook's own chest complaint. Cooks get sick. Nobody wonders why a cook buys medicine. And take the harbor road after all — no. No, Candle Street. Mind me. And don't look at the cost, just pay it, and count nothing in front of the shopman, counting makes people remember you."

* * *

Silas went, and the house settled into the particular silence of a house holding its breath.

Brigid worked, because working was how she prayed. Kneaded bread that didn't need kneading. Scrubbed a pot that was already clean. Kept one ear turned toward the laundry basket the whole time, toward that small ragged breathing, the way a sailor keeps one ear toward weather.

It was during the third pot that the guilt caught up with her, and she let it, because guilt was also a kind of prayer if you didn't drown in it.

She thought about her daughter Aoife, dead at nine months of a fever just like this one, in a cottage where medicine was a thing priests talked about and landlords sold. She had held that child through a night exactly like the night Silas must have just endured — the crying, then the quiet, the quiet like a door closing one inch at a time. She had sworn on Aoife's small cooling body that no child within reach of her two hands would die of want while she drew breath, and she had kept that oath in the only currency available to a poor woman: soup, smuggled bread, extra blankets, small mercies logged nowhere.

This was different. This was forgery. This was, if it went wrong, the workhouse for certain, maybe the magistrate, maybe worse, because the Constabulary was circling this house like a gull over fish scraps and Inspector Hale missed nothing, nothing, she'd seen his eyes moving over her kitchen shelves during the search like a man pricing the silverware.

And she found, examining the fear honestly, the way she examined meat before buying it, that she was not afraid for herself. Eleven years of obedience had bought her nothing she couldn't carry out the door in one bag. What she was afraid of was the cost to the others — to Silas, who had made a promise alone in the dark like a fool and a saint; to Fenwick, who was already limping extra on the nights he kept watch; to the little ones who'd have no one to burn their porridge if Brigid went to the workhouse.

That was the arithmetic of love, she thought grimly, scraping the pot. It never balanced. Somebody always paid more than they were shown on the bill.

She heard Silas come back an hour later by the quickness of her step on the stairs, and the smell of camphor arrived before she did.

Halloran's had filled the order without comment. Quinine powder, draught, liniment, all of it real, all of it now sitting in Silas's hands wrapped in brown paper like the most dangerous cargo in the port — because it was, because somewhere in Candle Street a shopkeeper's ledger now held one line in a stranger's handwriting that could unravel three lives if anyone ever read it closely.

They dosed the baby together. Brigid mixed the quinine into a spoon of honey — honey, the last of it, another small treasure spent — and Silas held the child upright and coaxed, and after a long gray hour the fever began to sweat instead of burn, which was the good sign, the sign Brigid had waited twenty years to see again on a small forehead, and she had to leave the room and stand in the pantry for a moment with her fists pressed to her eyes.

When she came back, Silas was watching her from across the kitchen, and the girl's face had changed. Something had hardened in it, or perhaps clarified — the way stock clarifies when you finally skim it.

"I saw what it cost," Silas said. "The chit. Your name, even a false one. Fenwick's roster. The coins you've been skimming for milk since the first week — yes, I knew, Brigid, I keep ledgers in my head, it's what I'm for. Every one of you has been paying for my promise in pieces, and I let you, and I told myself it was necessary, and tonight I watched you press your fists into your eyes like a woman at a graveside and I understood something." She swallowed. "I have been spending other people's courage. And courage spent on credit comes due."

Brigid dried her hands slowly. Nineteen years old, this girl, indentured since eleven, taught by the company to be small and silent, and here she stood talking like a director of something — like a person who had decided her own life was hers to risk on purpose.

"And what will you do about it?" Brigid asked. "Now that you owe the debt?"

"I'll stop hiding behind it," Silas said. She looked down at the basket, where the baby slept, sweating, alive, the rattle in her breath gone soft as tide on far sand. "Hiding was survival. I know how to survive. But surviving isn't the same as being brave, and I think brave is the only currency that clears this debt. Whatever comes — Hale, Verrone, the whole rotten company — I'll meet it standing up, not dusting shelves and pretending to be furniture. Because furniture can't protect any of you. And I intend to protect every one of you back."

Brigid laughed — short, wet, surprised out of her. "Lord save us. A speech. From the girl who didn't say ten words a day for her first three years here."

"You talk enough for both of us," Silas said, and for a moment, just a moment, they were only two tired women in a warm kitchen with a sleeping child, laughing low so as not to wake her, and it was almost — almost — an ordinary evening.

Then came the knock at the kitchen door.

Three raps. Even. Unhurried. Not Fenwick's knock, not the widow's, not any friend's knock at all, because friends knocked quick and hopeful and this knock had the patience of a man being paid by the hour.

Through the frosted pane in the door, distorted by the old glass, Brigid saw a broad shoulder and the pale smudge of a face, and heard a voice pitched politely, terribly calm:

"Kitchen staff? Constabulary. Just a few questions about a purchase made today in Candle Street."
