## 13. Hale's Questions

Inspector Elias Hale had a theory about hands.

Faces lied, in his experience — cheerfully, fluently, with tremendous commitment. Voices lied. Even bodies lied, in the way they arranged themselves: the slouch of false ease, the squared shoulders of manufactured innocence. But hands were honest. Hands had spent their years doing what they did, and they could not quickly learn to be something else. He had once cracked a counterfeiting ring in Calloway Bay because the engraver's thumb carried an ink stain no amount of scrubbing could reach, sunk deep into the whorls like a birthmark. The man claimed he was a fishmonger. Fishmongers did not carry ink in their skin.

So when Silas Marchetti was shown into the small room above the counting house at Verrone Trading — a room Hale had requisitioned that morning with Director Verrone's gracious and slightly oily cooperation — the first thing Hale looked at was her hands.

She held them folded in her lap as she sat, which was itself interesting. Most people under interrogation didn't know what to do with their hands; they gripped the chair, or flattened them against their thighs, or hid them entirely beneath the table as though hands were accomplices. This girl folded hers neatly, palm over palm, like a woman settling in for church.

She was nineteen, perhaps twenty. Dark hair scraped back without vanity, a face that would have been called plain only by someone who hadn't learned to look properly — it was a face built for not being noticed, which meant someone had built it deliberately. Her eyes moved around the room once, unhurried: window, door, the two constables by the wall, the table, him. Then they settled on his face and waited.

"Miss Marchetti," Hale said. "Thank you for coming."

"You didn't give me a choice, sir," she said. Her voice was level, low, the accent of the harbor streets sanded down but not erased. "The constable said I'd lose my position if I refused."

"The constable exaggerated. Though I appreciate that fear is more efficient than politeness." He opened his notebook, more for theater than need; his memory was better than his shorthand. "This won't take long. I'm speaking to everyone employed in the shipping offices this week. Routine."

"Routine for a treason investigation," she said.

It was not a question, and it was not impertinent — or rather, it was impertinence wearing the clothing of a question, delivered so flatly he might almost have missed the challenge inside it. Almost. He smiled.

"Especially for a treason investigation," he agreed. "Treason makes everything routine, you'll find. Every clerk becomes a suspect, every ledger a weapon. It's very inconvenient for the innocent and very useful for me." He tilted his head. "You've worked here eight years?"

"Since I was eleven. Indentured."

"Your father was a diver."

"He was." Something crossed her face then — quick, gone before he could name it. Grief has many speeds, Hale knew. Hers moved fast.

"And you work in receiving. Pearl crates, manifests, freight tallies."

"I count what comes off the boats, and I write what I count. That's all."

"That's all," Hale repeated pleasantly, and let the words sit there like a hook in calm water. Then he said: "Show me your hands, please."

Her chin lifted a fraction. For the first time, something unguarded flickered through her composure — surprise, and beneath the surprise, calculation. She unfolded her hands and turned them over, palms up, resting them on the table between them.

Hale leaned forward and looked, and what he saw rearranged the shape of the afternoon.

* * *

They were working hands, certainly. Anyone's would be, in a place like this. There were calluses along the base of the fingers, the roughened pads that came from hauling rope and gripping crate edges, the small nicks and scars of dock labor. A casual eye would have seen exactly what he ought to see: a girl who had grown up among crates and salt water.

But Hale had made a career of refusing to be casual.

The crate-hauler's calluses were there, yes — but they were old ones, softened at the edges, the body's history rather than its present. And layered over them, fresher, harder, unmistakable to anyone who had spent three years prosecuting forgery cases in the capital, were the marks of the pen. The middle finger of the right hand carried a writer's callus, a firm ridge on the inner knuckle where a nib rests hour after hour. The ink stains were faint but present, worked into the cuticle of the thumb and forefinger — iron gall, he judged, the cheap stuff trading houses bought by the gallon. And the way she'd folded her hands: right over left, thumb tucked, the habitual posture of someone protecting a writing hand without thinking about it.

He had interviewed forty-one employees of Verrone Trading in five days. Dockhands, tallymen, cooks, clerks' boys. He had seen a hundred versions of these hands. Never both sets of marks on the same pair. Crates made one kind of hand. Ledgers made another. This girl's hands belonged to both lives at once, and neither life admitted the other.

"Interesting," Hale said.

"Is something wrong with them, sir?"

"On the contrary. They're the most honest thing in this room." He sat back. "You count pearls off the boats. Who writes the entries?"

"The receiving clerk writes the master manifest. I copy into the freight book. Everyone in receiving copies — Fenwick checks the arithmetic." A pause so slight it was nearly invisible. "It's required."

"Mm. And who taught you your letters? Indentured children usually aren't taught."

"My father, some. The rest—" she shrugged, a small precise motion, "—I stole it. From listening. From copying things I wasn't supposed to touch. You can punish me for that if you like, but you'd have to punish half the dormitory."

"I'm not interested in punishing literacy, Miss Marchetti. If I arrested everyone in this port who'd stolen an education, I'd need a second prison." He watched her. She watched him back, and behind her stillness he could feel the mind working — fast, orderly, weighing him the same way he weighed her. It was rare, in his trade, to be weighed back. Rarer still to enjoy it.

"Tomas Reyes," he said, and dropped the name like a stone into still water.

The ripples were tiny. That was what struck him most. A lesser interrogator would have reported no reaction at all and moved on. But Hale had spent twenty years learning to read the surface of people, and the surface of Silas Marchetti trembled — barely, precisely, like water touched by a fish rather than a fist. Her breathing changed. Not faster. Shallower. Controlled. She was rationing her own air.

He knew that trick. Divers used it.

"What can you tell me about him?" she asked.

"I asked first."

"He was a clerk here. Senior. He—" the smallest hesitation, "—he was kind to the indentured children. Paid for medicines sometimes. Brigid will tell you the same. I don't know anything else about him."

"When did you last see him?"

"Eleven days ago. In the shipping office. He asked me for the freight totals from the northern runs."

"Did he now." Hale wrote nothing down, which he knew unsettled people more than writing. "And did you give them to him?"

"I gave him what my supervisor authorized."

"Which was?"

"Which was whatever Mr. Corrigan told me to give him. I don't make decisions, Inspector. I'm property. Ask the company." There it was again — the flat voice, the level eyes, and underneath it, faint as a heartbeat under a coat, something hot. Anger, maybe. Or grief's cousin. "I copy numbers. I don't read them."

That was a lie. He was almost certain it was a lie. People who truly didn't read numbers said it with relief, with the gratitude of the unburdened. She said it like a door being closed and locked.

"May I ask you something instead?" she said.

"You may ask."

"Why does the Crown Constabulary search a trading house for two days over charts?" Her tone stayed perfectly respectful. "Charts go missing from ports every month. Half the pilots in this city sell outdated ones to foreign captains and call it retirement planning. Nobody sends you." She gestured, a small economical motion taking in the room, the building, the armed men at the door. "Someone sent you here for Reyes, and someone with rank decided the whole house should be turned over. I'd like to know why, if I'm expected to have an opinion worth recording."

For a moment Hale simply looked at her, and something in his chest — the old muscle, the tired one — gave a slow turn.

Because it was a fair question. Because he had asked it himself, in the Director's office, and received in answer a smile and a glass of imported sherry and nothing else. Because Verrone wanted Reyes closed quickly, wanted him closed loudly, wanted the word *traitor* nailed over the man's name before any clerk with good eyesight started reading ledgers too carefully — and Hale had accepted the assignment anyway, because a Chief Inspector who declined assignments from companies with the Crown's ear did not remain a Chief Inspector long.

"Reyes is accused of selling port defenses to a hostile navy," Hale said evenly. "That elevates missing charts considerably."

"Do you believe it?"

"That," he said, "is not your concern."

"No, sir," she agreed, mildly. But her eyes said otherwise — politely, thoroughly, otherwise.

* * *

He kept her another quarter hour. It was not cruel and it was not idle; he circled, the way he always circled, asking the same questions in different clothing and noting where the seams shifted. She was consistent. That alone was remarkable — liars drifted; they redecorated their stories each time they walked through them. She gave him the same answers in the same words, which meant either she was telling the truth, or she had rehearsed until the story had become true in her mouth.

He asked about the night of the seventeenth, whether she had heard anything at the servants' door. Nothing, she said; she slept like the dead after twelve hours of crates. He asked if Reyes had ever given her gifts, letters, tokens. Nothing, she said; kindness, occasionally, and once medicine for a sick child in the dormitory, paid from his own pocket. That much he verified later and found true — Brigid Doyle confirmed it with the fierce defensiveness of a woman guarding a chapel.

He asked, near the end, the question he had come to ask: "If Tomas Reyes came to you — if he appeared at this trading house tomorrow and asked you for help — what would you do?"

Silence. Not the empty kind. The full kind, the kind that has weight and temperature. The two constables by the wall shifted. Rain ticked at the window. Silas Marchetti sat with her honest, dishonest hands folded in her lap, and Hale watched her decide — visibly, deliberately — what to be.

"I'd report it," she said. "To you. To the Director. Whoever pays my wages owns my loyalty; that's what indenture means, isn't it?" And then, quieter, with the first and only warmth she had shown him: "He was kind to children nobody was kind to. I hope you're wrong about him, Inspector. For the company's sake, I mean. It would be embarrassing, wouldn't it — a man like that sitting at your own tables for fifteen years?"

"Yes," Hale said. "It would."

She rose when he dismissed her, dipped a curtsy calibrated to the exact millimeter of respect owed a Crown Inspector by an indentured tally-girl — he had seen lawyers botch that arithmetic; she had not — and went to the door. Her hand was on the latch when he spoke again, mostly because he could not help himself.

"Miss Marchetti. One more thing."

She turned. Waited.

"Your hands," he said. "Crate calluses fade when a girl stops hauling and starts writing. Yours haven't faded. Which means you still do both — the heavy work and the fine work — and you do them in the same day, and you make sure no one notices." He held her gaze. "A person who works that hard at not being noticed is either hiding something, or hiding from something. In my experience those are different creatures, and I should very much like to know which one you are."

For three full seconds, Silas Marchetti looked at him, and he felt — absurdly, professionally, undeniably — understood. Weighed, measured, filed. As though she had opened him like one of her freight books and copied the figures.

"I'm neither, Inspector," she said. "I'm just tired. May I go?"

"You may."

The door closed behind her. Hale sat a while in the rain-lit room, turning his pen, and did not write her name in the suspect column of his notes, and did not write it in the cleared column either. He wrote it in the margin, beside no heading at all, and underlined it once.

* * *

That evening he walked the perimeter of the trading house himself, alone, without notifying Verrone's people, which had become his habit and which he suspected was becoming his signature.

The rain had eased to a drifting mist. Lamps burned in the dormitory wing — he counted windows out of reflex — and in the third window from the end, second floor, a shadow moved that was not the shadow of a grown person. Small. Swaying. The particular rhythm of an adult walking a child to sleep, back and forth, back and forth, patient as tide.

Hale stood in the wet street and watched that window for longer than a Chief Inspector should watch anything that was not yet evidence.

He had forty-one interviews behind him and a Director who wanted the case closed by month's end, wanted it closed the way a man wants a wound cauterized — fast, hot, and without examination of what caused it. He had Reyes's charts locked in an evidence room, and for two nights running he had lain awake over them, because the tide tables penciled in the margins did not match the dates in the company manifests Verrone had so helpfully provided. A four-day discrepancy. Small. Professional men did not make four-day discrepancies in documents meant to hang a traitor — unless the documents had been made to hang someone else.

He had a clerk named Fenwick whose arithmetic was perfect and whose eyes, when Hale had interviewed him, had gone twice to the stairs leading to the records vault, in the precise direction a man glances when he is afraid something is missing from a place he knows too well.

And he had a tally-girl with a diver's breath control and a writer's callus, who had asked him the one question nobody in Verrone's employ had dared ask, and who had looked at him as though she already knew the answer and was deciding what to do about it.

The lamp in the third window went dark.

Hale pulled his collar up against the mist and made himself walk away, one deliberate step at a time, the way he did everything now: patiently, watching, waiting for the seam in the fabric to show itself. He did not know yet what Silas Marchetti was hiding. But he knew the shape of hiding when he stood next to it, and tomorrow — quietly, without Verrone's knowledge — he would begin pulling records on the freight books of the northern runs, and on every hand that had touched them.

Including, he thought, glancing back once at the dark window, the small capable hands of Miss Silas Marchetti.

The hunter's patience, his old sergeant had called it, the year they buried the sergeant. Everything confesses eventually, boy. Your job is to still be listening when it does.
