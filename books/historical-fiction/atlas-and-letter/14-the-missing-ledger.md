## 14. The Missing Ledger

There is a particular ache that lives in a lame leg on nights when the weather turns, and Fenwick had learned long ago that the only cure was work. So he worked. He sat at the counting desk after the clerks had gone home, lamplight pooled yellow around his elbows, and he reconciled columns of figures that did not need reconciling, because the alternative was to sit in the dark and think about Tomas Reyes.

Nine days since the news came down from the constabulary like a dropped plate: *Reyes has fled. Reyes is a traitor. Reyes sold port charts to a foreign navy.* Nine days of whispers in the ledger room, of Mr. Quill spitting into his handkerchief, of Director Verrone's portrait in the front hall seeming — to Fenwick alone, he suspected — to watch everyone who passed beneath it with a kind of polished satisfaction.

Nine days of watching Silas Marchetti dust shelves she did not need to dust, listening with her whole spine.

He had known her since she was eleven years old, brought sobbing and furious into the dormitory on Rope Street with her father's debt pinned to her like a number. He had watched her learn the company's first lesson — *be invisible* — faster than any child he'd ever seen, and it had broken something in him quietly every time she did it well. She was nineteen now, and she moved through the trading house like smoke through a keyhole, and for nine days she had been asking questions without asking them, and Fenwick had been answering them without speaking, because his silences were getting heavier and heavy silences, in his experience, always found a way out.

Tonight the rain came off the harbor sideways. The dormitory was asleep. Fenwick sat at the counting desk with a cold cup of tea and the ghost of Ledger Nine open behind his eyes.

The door at the top of the stairs creaked. He knew the weight of that step. Small, careful, barefoot on the boards to keep from waking Brigid's board of sleeping children.

"Couldn't sleep either?" he said, not turning.

Silas came down into the lamplight with a blanket around her shoulders and her hair loose, which he had seen perhaps four times in eight years. She looked younger without the braid and the busy hands. She looked, he thought, like exactly what she was: a girl carrying something far too heavy, alone, by choice, because she trusted no one enough to set it down.

"You should be asleep," she said.

"So should you." He gestured at the chair across from him. "Sit. If we're both going to be fools tonight, we may as well be comfortable fools."

She didn't sit. She stood at the edge of the lamplight the way she stood everywhere — as though light cost money and she wasn't authorized to spend it. Her eyes went to the ledger room door, then to the window, then to him. Measuring. He had watched her measure things for years; he had simply never been one of them before.

"Fenwick," she said. "Who copied the charts?"

The question landed in the room like a coin on a counter. Not *did* someone copy them. Who. She had already done the arithmetic somewhere in that silent head of hers, and she had come out with a sum she didn't like, and now she wanted him to check her figures.

"What makes you think anyone copied anything?" he asked, which was stalling, and they both knew it, and her face said so.

"Because charts don't leave this building," she said. "I've hauled crates past the map room since I was eleven. There's a man at the door day and night. If Reyes took them, someone let him take them. And if someone let him take them—" she paused, and the blanket shifted on her shoulders, "—then either he stole from the company with help, or the company gave him away. And I've been trying all week to decide which answer frightens me more."

Fenwick looked at his tea. Then at the ledger room door. Then at the rain crawling down the black windows of Rope Street.

"Sit down, Silas," he said again, and this time she did.

* * *

He began, as he began everything, with the numbers.

"There's a ledger called Nine," he said. "You won't have seen it. It doesn't live in the general room — it lives in Verrone's own cabinet, and only three keys open that cabinet, and I've never had one of them. But ledgers are like people. They have to go somewhere, eventually. Audits. Inspections. The Crown sends an examiner every third year, and even Director Verrone can't refuse the Crown's examiner, only confuse him."

"And you confused him."

"I made tea," Fenwick said, and heard how small the confession sounded, how ordinary, which was precisely the horror of it. "Spring before last. Examiner comes, wants to see the freight books, Verrone sends Ledger Nine down with Quill to show him — gunpowder doesn't hide well in a pearl manifest, you understand, so what the examiner sees must be very dull indeed. And while Quill is walking the examiner through six hundred entries of tallow and copra, I am in the copy room making tea. And Tomas Reyes is in the copy room."

Silas had gone very still. Stillness was her gift; he had seen her hold still through beatings, through inspections, through the death of her father's memory. But this was a different stillness. This was the stillness of a woman standing on ice and hearing it begin to speak.

"He asked me for two hours," Fenwick said. "That's all. Two hours and my silence. He had the ledger — I never learned how he got it out of the cabinet and I never asked, because asking makes you guilty and silence only makes you tired. He had a copying glass and good paper and the fastest hand I've ever seen, and he sat at that table where your elbow is right now, and he copied. Page after page. And he let me read what he was copying, Silas, because he said — I remember it exact — he said, *'A man should know what his silence weighs.'*"

The lamp guttered. Rain pressed at the glass.

"It was pearls on the left-hand column," Fenwick went on, quieter now. "Pearls, pearl shell, mother-of-pearl blanks, diving stores. Everything proper. And on the right-hand column, where the weights ought to have matched the pearls, there were weights that matched nothing that swims. Hundredweight after hundredweight of crate mass that had no cargo name. Shipped north on the *Verrone Star*. Shipped south on the *Cormorant*. Manifests signed by a clerk named Aldous Byrne."

Silas frowned. "Byrne. There's a Byrne grave in the paupers' yard. The cook before Brigid used to say—"

"Aldous Byrne died four years ago," Fenwick said. "Fell from the loading pier. Or jumped. Or was helped. The company paid for a stone, which the company never does, and closed his desk within the hour, and his signature kept signing manifests for eighteen months afterward. Dead men write very steadily, if you pay them to."

Silence. Down the hall, one of the children coughed in sleep, and they both froze until it passed, old reflexes dying hard.

"Arms," Silas said at last. It wasn't a question.

"Guns and powder, going places the Crown had sworn no guns would go," Fenwick said. "Sold under pearl freight, insured as pearl freight, taxed as pearl freight. And when the profit margin is that wide, you don't need me to do the sums. That's Ledger Nine. That's what Tomas copied for two hours while I stood at the door with my bad leg aching and my heart going like a rabbit. And when he was done he put every page inside his coat, and he thanked me, and he said if anything ever happened to him, if his name ever came up crooked, I should remember that I knew what my silence weighed."

"And you told no one."

"And I told no one." Fenwick spread his hands — the hands that had balanced honest columns and dishonest ones for twenty years, ink-stained, precise, useless. "Because he was kind to me. Because in eleven years no one in this building above stairs ever once asked whether my leg hurt, and Tomas asked it the first hour he worked beside me. Because the alternative to silence was accusing the house that feeds these children, and I am lame, Silas, and lame men do not survive on principle. We survive on roofs."

* * *

She didn't speak for a long time. He watched her take it in the way he'd seen her take crate after crate onto the dock scale — testing the weight before committing her back to it.

"Then the charts," she said slowly.

"The charts are the beautiful part," Fenwick said, and his voice came out bitter, which surprised him. "Think it through. Tomas has copies of Ledger Nine. Copies that would hang half the directorate. What does Verrone do? He cannot search for pages — searching admits pages exist. No. He makes Tomas famous instead. He makes him a *traitor*. Charts sold to a foreign navy — treason, which brings the Constabulary, which brings Inspector Hale, which means that if the copies ever surface, no court will believe them. Who trusts the papers of a traitor? The forgery isn't the evidence against Reyes, Silas. The forgery *is* Reyes. They've turned the man into the lie, so the lie can bury the truth."

"But Hale—"

"Hale believes what he's shown. So did I, for twenty years." Fenwick leaned back, and his leg throbbed its agreement with the rain. "Verrone doesn't need to prove Reyes guilty. He needs everyone too frightened and too busy to prove him innocent. And it's working. Quill burns correspondence now without reading it. The *Star*'s crew signed articles of silence. Even Brigid has stopped singing in the kitchen, and Brigid sings through funerals."

"That's why they'll never look here," Silas said. "In the ledgers. Everyone's looking at charts and harbors and navies, and the whole time the crime is sitting in a book with a number on it."

"Now you're doing my job better than I do it," Fenwick said. "Yes. The truth isn't hidden, girl. It's merely filed."

She looked at him then — a long, level look, and he felt it the way he imagined a defendant feels the jury turn. Because there was a question under her question, and they both knew it was coming, and it came.

"Do you still have anything?" she asked softly. "Anything at all. A scrap. A date you memorized. Anything that proves what was in Ledger Nine."

And there it was — loyalty versus truth beginning its quiet war in his chest, the same war he'd been fighting for nine days, fought out loud now between the two of them over a dead man's tea. Because he *did* have something. One thing. One page, kept back on the last night of copying, when Tomas had torn the final sheet in half and handed Fenwick one piece and said, *Keep this where no fire will find it. Half a proof is worth more than none.* Fenwick had sewn it into the lining of his mattress frame three springs ago and had lain on the truth every night since, and every night the question had been the same: what was a lame clerk's safety worth against a hanging man's name?

And every night, until nine days ago, the answer had been easy.

"Why?" he asked her, because he needed, just once, to hear her reason spoken before he handed her the power to destroy everyone under this roof. "Why does it matter to you? Reyes is fled or dead. The company stands. Keep your head down, keep your ledger neat — you taught yourself that lesson better than anyone. Why are you asking?"

Silas pulled the blanket tighter. For a moment he thought she wouldn't answer. Then she said, in a voice so low the rain nearly took it:

"Because he saved my father once. Because whatever else he is, he trusted somebody in this house enough to leave something behind. And because I've been invisible for eight years, Fenwick, and I'm starting to think invisibility isn't survival. It's just a slower way of drowning."

Fenwick closed his eyes. Twenty years of neat columns. Twenty years of tea.

"Go to the stair landing," he said. "Third step from the bottom, the loose one. Under it there's a gap in the joist. Bring me what you find, and bring the lamp close, and Silas — whatever you see, don't cry on it. The ink runs."

She was back before the kettle could have boiled, holding a folded half-sheet of good rag paper, sealed with wax gone pale with age. He nodded at it.

"Tomas's own hand," he said. "Half of Ledger Nine's final page — the *Cormorant* shipment, northbound, the weights, the signatures, including one signed by a dead man. The other half Tomas kept. Put the halves together and you have a document that could pull this company down to its keel." His mouth was dry. "It could also pull down everyone who ever kept quiet. Brigid. The children. You. Me."

Silas sat down slowly across from him, the paper in both hands as if it were a bird with a broken wing.

"Fenwick," she said. "Someone came to this house nine days ago and left me something too. Something of Tomas's."

He looked up sharply. "What something?"

But her eyes had gone to the window, where the rain had suddenly changed — where below the sound of it, faint along Rope Street, came the crunch of boots. More than one pair. Moving unhurried. Stopping outside the trading house door.

The knocker fell three times, iron on oak, loud in the sleeping dark.

Fenwick blew out the lamp.
