## 15. A Room Above the Boil House

The landlady's name was Mrs. Quill, and she had the eyes of a woman who had learned long ago that questions were bad for business. She kept rooms above the boil house on Tanner's Slip, where the fish-oil smoke rose all day and coated everything in a smell that got into wool and hair and never quite left. Nobody respectable came to Tanner's Slip. That was precisely why I had come.

I counted my treasures on the walk over, there in the lining of my coat where I'd sewn them against my ribs like organs. My father's diving ring, brass worn smooth as river stone, stamped with the Verrone mark he'd been so proud to earn. Three months of coins skimmed from the washing-money Brigid let me handle. A length of good linen. And under my tongue, folded no bigger than a wafer, the memory of a promise: *she is yours now if you are brave.*

The baby — I had not yet dared to give her a name out loud, though in the dark of the dormitory loft I whispered syllables to her and tried them like shoes — the baby slept against my chest inside my shawl, her breath a small damp heat above my heart. She had learned, in her six weeks with me, the art of silence too. Children learn fast here what keeps them safe.

Mrs. Quill opened her door on the second knock and looked me over the way a horse trader looks at a mare: teeth, hooves, pedigree or lack thereof.

"Room," I said. "One room. Quiet."

"Quiet costs more."

"I know what things cost." My father taught me that too, in his way. Everything costs; the sea takes its tithe from every diver; you pay or you drown. "I can pay two weeks in advance. No visitors. You never see me come or go except at odd hours, and if anyone asks, you've had no lodger since spring."

That made her mouth twitch, the smallest acknowledgment of our transaction's true shape. "There's a room back. Third floor. Window looks on the slip, not the street." She held out a hand like a knot of driftwood. "Coin first."

* * *

I had meant to pay in coin. I had counted the coins three times on the walk over, had made the sums work with a clerk's precision — Fenwick would have been proud, or sad, or both, because Fenwick understood better than anyone alive that arithmetic is just grief arranged in columns. Two weeks' rent, milk, bread, a bottle of the cod-liver oil Brigid swore by. It worked. Barely. It worked the way a drowning man works the water.

But when I opened my coat to reach the coins, my knuckles brushed the ring through the fabric, and I stopped.

It is a small thing, a diving ring. A circle of brass, an inch across, stamped with a company mark and a number. My father wore it on a cord around his neck for twenty years. When they brought him up from the water the last time — and this is the part I do not tell anyone, the part I keep in a room inside myself with the door shut — the ring was still around his neck, and the company man at the dock took everything else, the knife and the boots and the coat off my mother's shoulders before she was cold in her grave, but he did not take the ring, because the ring was worthless. Because it only proved what my father was: a man who went down into the dark holding his breath for money, and came up when the sea allowed it, until the day it did not allow.

He put it in my hand when I was nine years old, on a Sunday, on the shingle beach where we used to watch for his boat. He said, *This means you come from divers. Whatever they make you into on land, remember what you come from.* I was eleven when they made me into a ledger-keeper. I was nineteen and I had kept the ring through six years of inspections and thefts and one terrible winter when I could have traded it for coal, and I had told myself I would die with it, that it was the one thing that was mine —

And there in Mrs. Quill's doorway, with the baby's breath fogging my collarbone, I took the cord over my head and held it out.

"What's this?" Mrs. Quill said.

"For the room. The ring and the coin both. A month instead of two weeks, and you saw no child. Ever."

She turned it in her fingers. She was old enough to know what a diving ring was, old enough perhaps to have buried a husband or a son with one. Her thumb moved across the Verrone stamp, and something crossed her face — not kindness exactly, but its poor relation, the cousin who shows up at funerals.

"Diver's daughter," she said. Not a question. The smell of the sea gets into us and never leaves either.

"Aye."

She closed her fist around the ring. "Month's paid. There's a kettle upstairs. If the little one cries," she said, looking at a point slightly to the left of my shawl, where a child was most definitely not sleeping, "the neighbors are deaf. Fish-oil fumes. Ruined their hearing years ago."

* * *

The room was four steps wide and five steps long, and I measured it without meaning to, the way I measured everything now — in hiding places, in exits, in how many seconds it would take to wrap her and go out the window if boots came up the stairs. The window looked down on Tanner's Slip, where men hauled barrels of reeking oil and gulls screamed over the guts they dropped. The bed had a real mattress, straw but whole, and no fleas that I could find in a minute's inspection. There was a chair. There was a hook on the door for a coat.

It was a palace. I sat down on the bed with the baby still asleep against me and I wanted to weep at the luxury of a door that locked from the inside, and I did not weep, because she needed me steady, and because I had learned young that tears are a currency and I could not afford the exchange rate.

I laid her down on the mattress, in the nest of sacking I'd brought, and she frowned in her sleep — she had a frown like an old tax collector, this child, this stranger's daughter who was mine now by every law that mattered — and I set about making the room ours.

The linen went up as a curtain over the lower window pane. My spare shawl became a rug. I arranged the three books I owned — a primer, a psalter, and a volume of shipping arithmetic Fenwick had given me for teaching myself double-entry — on the sill, spine out, because a room with books on the sill is a clerk's room, respectable, and respectability was the costume we would wear here. Then I sat in the chair and let myself feel the other thing, the thing I had been carrying since morning like a stone swallowed whole.

This morning I had woken in the dormitory loft on Rope Street, with Fenwick coughing two beds over and the O'Donnell girl muttering figures in her sleep — she dreamed in numbers, that one, she'd go far if the company didn't grind her down — and Brigid's tread on the stairs at half past five, heavy on the left foot, light on the right, a rhythm I had known for six years and could have picked out of a crowd of thousands. This morning there had been porridge with a secret spoonful of sugar in my bowl that nobody mentioned. This morning I had been somebody's girl, in the loose and unspoken way of that house — Brigid's girl, Fenwick's colleague, the big sister the little ones went to when they lost a button or a coin. Tonight I was a woman alone in a rented room above a boil house, and tomorrow I would go back to Rope Street as a visitor in my own life.

Because Inspector Hale had stood in the courtyard on Tuesday with his gray coat and his gray eyes, and he had looked at the pearl crates, and I had stood in the doorway with my heart going like a trapped bird while the baby slept twelve feet away in sacking, smelling of pearl-shell, and he had passed within arm's reach of her. He had *passed within arm's reach*. And afterward, in the loft, in the dark, I understood with a clarity that felt like cold water that it would happen again, and next time I might not be so lucky, and if they took me they would take her, and if they questioned Brigid — Brigid, who cannot lie, whose face announces every truth she owns like a town crier — they would unravel us all in an afternoon.

The trading house was a shell I had thought was safety, and Hale's visit had cracked it, and through the crack I could see the truth: I was not protecting her there. I was gambling with her, every night, on dice I didn't load.

So. The room above the boil house. The ring. The coins. The separation.

It was right. I knew it was right the way my father knew a bottom was wrong for diving — not by argument but by the pull in the gut. And knowing it did not make it hurt less, which is another thing the sea teaches you, if you pay attention.

* * *

I went back to Rope Street that evening to fetch the rest of it, and I told them in the kitchen after the little ones were down, because they deserved the truth even if the truth endangered them — no. That is not honest. I told them a version of the truth shaped like a lie: that I'd found work as a seamstress's copyist on Tanner's Slip, evening hours, that I'd be lodging out some nights for the convenience of it. Fenwick looked at me over his ledgers with those tired, kind eyes and said nothing, which from Fenwick was a shout. And Brigid dried her hands on her apron very slowly, and said, "Will you take your supper with us still, Sundays?" in a voice so carefully ordinary that I nearly broke then and there.

"Every Sunday," I said. "You'll be sick of me, Brigid Halloran."

"No such thing," she said, and turned back to her sink, and scrubbed a pot that was already clean, and did not look at me, and I saw her jaw working the way it does when she is swallowing something large.

But she knew. Of course she knew. She'd known since the night she found the baby and did not report her, since the night she showed me how to mix the milk with water and honey, how to feed a child from a pierced spoon, how to burp her against your shoulder — Brigid, who raised three of her own before the fever took two and the company took the third to its school in the north, Brigid who had somehow, impossibly, decided at fifty-some years of age to commit treason of the heart for a stranger's orphan. She knew there was no seamstress. She knew what the coins in my pocket meant and where I was really going. She kissed my cheek at the door and pressed a bundle into my hands — bread, the cod-liver oil, a knitted cap I hadn't seen her make, which meant she had made it in the dark after the household slept, which meant she had known for days that this moment was coming — and she said, "Mind the stairs there, they're steep in old buildings," and that was all, that was everything, that was Brigid entire.

Fenwick walked me to the corner. He walks with the stick, always, since the cart accident when he was fourteen — the company's cart, the company's fault, the company's settlement of eleven pounds, which is what a lame leg costs — and he moved beside me slow through the evening fog with the lamps coming on one by one along Rope Street.

"You'll write," he said. Not a question either. Everyone in my life had stopped asking questions and started issuing quiet decrees.

"There's nothing to write."

"Then write nothing," he said, "often." And then, at the corner, under the lamp with the moths already drunk around it, he stopped and looked at his stick and said, "Silas. If it goes wrong — whatever it is that we are not mentioning — you send word, and I will come, and I will bring the ledger knowledge, and I will be slow but I will be there. Do you understand me? Slow, but there."

I understood him. I put my hand briefly on his shoulder, the good one, the one that wasn't hurt in the cart, and I think we were both embarrassed, because we were people of the trading house and the trading house does not teach touching, and then I walked away into the fog toward Tanner's Slip with my heart dragging behind me on a cord like a weight.

* * *

Upstairs, the baby woke hungry at midnight and I fed her by the light of one candle stub, economized down to its last inch, and she looked up at me while she sucked with those grave dark eyes that see nothing yet and everything, and I talked to her, because Brigid says children should hear voices, because my father talked to me before I could understand words too.

"You have cost me a fortune," I told her. "You have cost me a ring and a family and any hope of a quiet life, and you don't even know it. You'd better grow up worth it." She blinked at me, milk-drunk, magnificent. "You will. I can tell already. You have the face of a girl who will be expensive and worth every penny."

When she slept again I did not sleep. I sat by the curtained window with the candle out, watching the slip through the gap, and I let myself do the accounting I had been avoiding. The rent bought a month. One month. Then the ring was spent and the coins nearly gone and I would need wages, wages I couldn't earn openly without papers naming me, papers the company held, papers Director Verrone could revoke with a signature if he ever learned what I carried hidden in this city like a second heartbeat. Every plan I built ended at the same wall: I was an indentured woman. My time belonged to Verrone Trading until 1917. Everything I loved was contraband.

And beneath that wall, deeper, a darker stratum I kept turning over like a stone in the shallows: Tomas Reyes had chosen me. Out of everyone in this port — every widow, every childless couple, every friend he must have had in forty years of clerking — he had carried his burden to my door at midnight and trusted me with it. *She is not mine, but she is yours now if you are brave.* Why me? Gratitude to a drowned diver only stretched so far. Men do not entrust infants to the dead's daughters without reason. There was something I did not yet know, sitting somewhere in the past between my father and Tomas Reyes, and I was increasingly certain that the answer was not in the city but in the sea, in the camps, in the places where divers dove and clerks recorded — and that if I wanted to keep this child truly safe, someday, sooner than I liked, I would have to go and find it.

Below, on the slip, a lantern swung. Two men walking, unhurried, and though there was nothing remarkable in two men walking at midnight in a port city, I watched them the whole length of the slip, motionless, my hand cupped over the baby's small rising back, until the lantern light guttered around the corner of the fish hall and the dark took them.

Only strangers. Probably strangers. The city was full of them.

I lay down beside her then, on the straw mattress with the shawl-rug over us both, and the fish-oil smell sealed us in like a second blanket, and she sighed in her sleep and gripped my finger with her whole hand, all five fingers, fierce as a limpet, and I thought: this is what I am now. Not a ledger-keeper. Not the company's girl. A woman lying in the dark holding on to someone with everything she has left.

I had just closed my eyes when I heard it — footsteps on the stairs outside, light ones, hesitant ones, and then a soft knock, twice, and a woman's voice through the door, low and urgent:

"Forgive the hour. I'm looking for a child. Please — I mean her no harm — but I think you know where she is."
