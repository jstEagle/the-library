## 16. Verrone's Offer

The summons came at half past six in the morning, delivered by a boy so young his company coat swallowed him whole.

"Director wants you. Office. Eight o'clock." He did not wait for an answer, only turned and ran, boots slapping the wet cobbles of Rope Street, and Silas stood in the dormitory doorway with a pail of dishwater in her hands and watched him go, feeling the way she had once felt as a child when the tide went out too fast — the sense of a vast thing pulling back before it came crashing in.

Directors did not summon pearl girls. Directors summoned clerks, factors, captains. In eight years indentured to the Verrone Trading Company, Silas Marchetti had spoken to Director Ambrose Verrone exactly twice: once at eleven years old, when her father's debts had been read aloud to her like a sentence, and once at fourteen, when she had been commended for catching an arithmetic error in a freight manifest and had learned, from the flatness of his praise, that being noticed was not the same as being valued. Being noticed was being weighed.

She set down the pail. Her hands were steady. She made herself notice that, because noticing her own steadiness was a trick her father had taught her without ever meaning to — *count your breaths, little fish, and your hands will believe you're calm even when you're not.*

Brigid was already in the kitchen, sleeves rolled, attacking a mountain of porridge oats with a wooden spoon the size of an oar. She looked up when Silas came through, and her eyes did the thing they always did now: flicked past Silas's shoulder, down the empty corridor, toward the room where the baby wasn't anymore. The baby was three streets away, above the boil house, paid for with her father's diving ring, guarded by a widow who asked no questions because Silas paid her silence in the only coin she had left. But Brigid's eyes still checked, every morning, the way a tongue checks for a broken tooth.

"Eight o'clock," Silas said quietly. "The director's office."

The spoon stopped. Flour dust hung in the light from the high window like something suspended mid-fall.

"What does he want with you?"

"I don't know."

"He doesn't want anything with pearl girls," Brigid said, and then, lower, with the bluntness of a woman who cooked for forty mouths and had no patience for softening things: "He wants something with the girl Reyes wrote to. That's what he wants."

Silas had not told Brigid about the letter's contents. She had burned it within the hour of receiving it, had fed it page by page into the kitchen stove while Brigid stood beside her stirring soup, and neither of them had spoken, and that shared silence had been its own confession. But she had never said the name aloud. Tomas Reyes. The traitor. The man whose handwriting curved like water going over a stone.

"You don't know that," Silas said.

"I know directors," said Brigid, and went back to her oats with a violence that suggested the oats were standing in for someone else entirely. "Eat first. And wear your good collar. If a man's going to buy you, make him look at a clean collar while he does it."

* * *

Verrone's office occupied the top floor of the counting house, and getting there meant climbing past every layer of the company's hierarchy like ascending a ship's rigging — the cargo clerks on the ground floor with their ink-stained fingers, the factors on the second with their ledgers chained to their desks, the accountants on the third behind glass doors, and above them all, at the very top of the stairs, a single door of dark mahogany with a brass plate polished so brightly it held Silas's reflection like a bowl of water.

She looked smaller in it than she felt. Thin. Young. A girl in a mended collar.

A secretary showed her in without announcing her, which was itself a kind of message: *he has been expecting you since before you were sent for.*

The office was larger than the entire dormitory common room. Maps covered the walls — coastlines, shipping lanes, the long spine of the archipelago drawn in faded ink — and between the maps hung framed bills of lading, yellowed with age, the company's first contracts displayed like hunting trophies. Behind the desk, in the tall window, the harbor lay spread out below: masts, cranes, the pearl fleet riding at anchor, and beyond the breakwater the open sea, flat and silver in the morning light.

Silas looked at that sea for one heartbeat longer than she should have. Her father had died in it. Her father had loved it. Both facts lived in her at once, the way a shell holds both the animal and the emptiness.

"Miss Marchetti."

Ambrose Verrone was not what strangers expected. Men of his wealth were imagined heavy, slow, upholstered by money; he was instead narrow and quick, sixty years old and straight-backed, with small bright eyes and hands that never quite stopped moving — adjusting a paperweight, aligning a pen, smoothing the surface of a map as if the world itself had a crease in it. His voice was soft. Everyone knew to be more afraid of the soft voice than the loud one.

"Sir." She bowed her head the correct amount. Not servile — he disliked groveling, it bored him — but low enough.

"Do sit." There was only one chair facing the desk, placed precisely where a supplicant's chair should be. She sat. He remained standing, moving to the window, and for a moment let her sit in the particular silence of a man arranging his thoughts into order, like coins into stacks.

"You've been with us eight years," he said. "Your father — Dario Marchetti, the diver. Died owing the company two hundred and forty crowns. You've been working it off at nine crowns a year against your keep." He recited it without consulting any paper, and the recitation was the point: *I know your number. I have always known your number.* "You would have been free at twenty-three. Another three years, four months."

"Yes, sir."

"And yet I'm told you have a talent for ledgers. Fenwick says your columns balance to the halfpenny. Fenwick does not say that about anyone." A thin smile, there and gone. "A wasted talent, hauling crates. I confess I've wondered why you never petitioned for clerk's training."

Because clerk's training required a sponsor's signature, and sponsors cost money, and she had learned early that asking this company for anything was how one drowned slowly instead of quickly. She said none of this. She said, "Crates needed hauling, sir."

Something flickered across his face — approval, or amusement; with men like this they wore the same expression. He turned from the window at last and sat, folding his hands on the blotter, and the morning light fell across his face and made it briefly, deceptively gentle.

"Miss Marchetti, I will come to the point, because I find that honest people are embarrassed by preamble and dishonest people are relieved by it, and I prefer to learn which you are as quickly as possible." He aligned his pen with the edge of the blotter. "Tomas Reyes."

Her heart did not leap. She had spent eight years training it not to, and it obeyed, dropping instead into a deep slow rhythm like a diver conserving air. She kept her eyes on his hands. Eyes could lie; hands rarely bothered.

"You knew him," Verrone continued. "Company records show he visited the dormitory on four occasions in the year before his flight. Charitable visits, we assumed — the man fancied himself a patron of unfortunates. But charity scatters broadly. He came here. To Rope Street. Four times." The small bright eyes came up. "Did he speak to you?"

"He gave out sweets at Christmas," Silas said, which was true. "And once he fixed Fenwick's boot, sir. The clasp. He was good with small mechanisms." Also true. Every word true, arranged like crates to conceal the cargo behind them. She was surprised at how easily it came, and frightened by the same thing.

"Small mechanisms," Verrone repeated softly, as though tasting the phrase. Then, briskly: "You'll have heard what he's accused of. Selling port charts and defense soundings to a foreign navy. Treason. The penalty, if he's taken alive, is hanging." He let that sit in the room the way one lets a smell sit. "The Constabulary believes he may attempt contact with confederates in the city. Accomplices. Persons holding materials of his — documents, correspondence." A pause, measured to the half-second. "Perhaps even family effects."

*I know,* Silas thought, with a clarity like cold water. *You are not asking whether I've heard from him. You already believe I have.*

"He never gave me anything, sir," she said. "Sweets and a boot clasp."

"Mm." Verrone rose, walked around the desk, and did the last thing she expected: he leaned against its front edge, close enough that she caught the scent of him — bay rum and paper and something faintly chemical, ink-eater, the smell of a man who lived inside documents. "Then here is what kindness looks like, Miss Marchetti, and I ask you to consider it carefully, because I am not known for offering it twice."

He produced, from inside his coat, a folded paper. Did not hand it over. Held it.

"A discharge," he said. "Full settlement of your father's debt. Your indenture dissolved today, this morning, with the company's seal and my own signature. Free papers. You could walk out of this building and no one — no factor, no foreman, no constable — could tell you where to stand ever again." He tilted his head. "You're nineteen. Most girls your age are free and married and tired already. You'd be free and unspent. That is worth more than you can currently imagine. It may be worth more than you will ever imagine again."

The paper hung in the air between them. Silas found that her body had gone very still — the stillness of the crate, the stillness she had discovered hiding the infant from Inspector Hale, breath counted and shallow, everything compressed into a space no bigger than a held note. Part of her mind, the part that balanced columns to the halfpenny, was doing arithmetic: *three years, four months of labor versus a sheet of paper. Two hundred forty crowns versus a sheet of paper. What does he think I have?*

Nothing costs nothing. Her father's other lesson, learned in the last month of his life, when he had taken the deep-water contract that killed him because the bonus was written in beautiful round figures.

"What would I owe for it?" she asked.

"Nothing," said Verrone pleasantly. "That's the beauty. You owe me nothing either way. If Reyes never contacts you — and truly, Miss Marchetti, why would he? — then you go free simply because I dislike waste, and a girl who can balance to the halfpenny is not made for crates. Consider it patronage. Companies should do some good in the towns they feed on; it keeps the knives quiet." He smiled at his own cynicism. "But if he does contact you — a letter, a message through a stranger, a knock after midnight—" and Silas felt the words go through her like a blade finding the gap under a rib— "then you come to me. Before anyone. Before the Constabulary. Before you speak to your cook or your lame friend Fenwick or the widow you've been paying in diving rings."

The room did not change. The light did not dim. But Silas understood, with a sensation like the floor tilting one degree, that she had walked in believing herself the hunter's blind spot and had instead walked directly into the center of his lens.

The widow above the boil house. He knew about the diving ring. Which meant he knew about the room. Which meant—

Or meant nothing. Meant he watched harborside pawnshops, and the ring had passed through one, and suspicion was cheaper than proof. She could not know. That was the design of it: not knowledge, but the *flavor* of knowledge, poured over her like brine so that everything she did from this moment would cure in it.

"You seem to know a great deal, sir," she said quietly.

"I own a great deal," Verrone corrected. "Knowing comes with it, mostly." He set the discharge paper on the desk, squared it with one finger, and stepped back. "Take it. Or don't. Take until tomorrow noon if you like — leave your answer with my secretary. But understand what you'd be refusing. Not merely freedom. Safety. Because I will tell you plainly, Miss Marchetti, since plainness is the one kindness left to me: the Constabulary is pulling this city apart thread by thread looking for Reyes's accomplices, and Constabularies are blunt instruments. They pull threads. Sometimes the cloth tears. A girl with no papers and no protector tears easily." He returned to his chair and sat, and the gentleness drained out of his posture like water out of a cracked hull, leaving only the shape of the man beneath. "Whereas a girl holding my seal walks through any search in this port untouched. Think on that. Freedom isn't only the absence of chains. It's the presence of cover."

Silas stood. Her legs did it without consulting her, eight years of obedience rising through her like tide.

She looked at the paper on the desk. Her father's debt, dead on it. Her name, free on it. All she had to do was reach out one calloused hand and trade a burned letter, a hidden child, a sickening fever risk three streets away — trade the whole secret weight of her promise — for clean air.

And the terrible, honest truth, the one she would carry alone all day and all night, was that for three full seconds she wanted it. Wanted it the way the drowning want air, simple and total and without shame. Three seconds. Then she thought of a small face she had been instructed to forget, and of the letter's curve and fall of its handwriting — *she is yours now if you are brave* — and the wanting closed over and sank, and what remained on the surface was only a nineteen-year-old girl in a mended collar, bowing the correct amount.

"I'll take until tomorrow noon, sir," she said. "Thank you for your kindness."

"Kindness," Verrone echoed, and picked up his pen. "Do give my regards to Mrs. Brigid. The kitchens run well under her. It would be a shame to lose good staff in a season of... disruptions."

Dismissed, she crossed the office, and her reflection in the brass plate on the door watched her go — thin, young, a girl who had just been offered everything and understood, finally and completely, that Director Ambrose Verrone did not want her to report on Tomas Reyes at all.

He wanted her to lead him to whatever Reyes had left behind. And he suspected — he *knew*, the way sharks know blood in water at parts per million — that she already carried it.

* * *

The stairs took a long time to descend. On the second-floor landing she passed Fenwick coming up with a stack of manifests, his bad leg swinging in its old stiff arc, and he read her face in the single glance available to a man who had spent his life reading other people's faces from a seated position.

"Well?" he murmured.

"Later," she breathed.

At the ground floor doors she pushed out into the morning street — fish carts, stevedores, gulls screaming over the gutting tables — and stood a moment letting the noise close over her like surf.

Then she saw her.

Across Rope Street, leaning against the post of the fish-weigher's stall with a canvas satchel at her feet and a traveler's dust still on her boots, a woman was watching the trading house door with the fixed, unhurried attention of someone who had been watching for a long time. Dark-haired, sun-browned, with a jaw like a figurehead and eyes that moved from the door to Silas's face and stopped there, and stayed.

The woman pushed off from the post and started toward her through the crowd, and Silas's hand went without thinking to her bodice, where her father's half-token lay against her skin — and the woman's eyes tracked the movement, and narrowed, and she said, in an accent from far down the coast:

"You're the one with the child."
