## 17. The Woman on the Ferry

The ferry to Port Amalia left from the wrong side of the harbor, and Marisol Reyes had learned long ago that the wrong side was usually the honest one. The deck stank of fish guts and tar, the passengers were fishermen with salt-scoured hands and market women with chickens trussed in baskets, and no one looked twice at a woman in a gray traveling dress with a shawl pulled low over her hair. That was the whole art of it, her brother used to say — be someone whose face slides off the memory like water off oil. Tomas had learned that lesson in counting houses. Marisol had learned it in worse places.

She stood at the rail as the city assembled itself across the water: first the masts, a forest of them, thin as needles; then the rooflines, the chimneys, the smoke lying over everything like a gauze bandage; then, last and largest, the granary clock tower of the Verrone Trading Company, its gilt face catching the morning sun and throwing it back like a coin flipped into the sky.

"There you are," she murmured to it. "Still standing. Still rich. Still lying."

A fisherman beside her glanced over, and she gave him a smile — brief, warm, forgettable — and turned back to the water.

Eight days she had traveled. Eight days since the telegram reached her in Corazón Bay, addressed in a code she and Tomas had invented when they were children hiding letters from their father: *SOLD THE BOAT STOP BUYER WILL KEEP HER SAFE STOP DO NOT COME STOP.*

*Do not come.* In the Reyes family, that had always meant *come quickly.*

She had come. Of course she had come. She had sold an actual boat to pay for the passage, which struck her now as a kind of prayer the world had answered too literally.

The ferry bumped the pier. Gulls screamed. Marisol stepped onto Port Amalia soil with her single bag and her heart beating in her throat, and made herself walk slowly.

Because they were here already. She had spotted them before the ferry cleared the breakwater — two men in matching gray coats who did not match any trade this harbor needed, standing at the foot of the customs stairs with the studied idleness of men being paid to wait. Verrone men. They had been on the pier at Corazón Bay too, three days behind her, then two, and now, apparently, ahead of her. Either she had grown careless or someone at the telegraph office had a second income.

She did not climb the customs stairs. She walked instead along the mud-slick apron beneath the pier, where the crab pickers worked and nobody asked a lady for her papers, and came up into the city through the fish market — a canyon of awnings and shouting, silver bodies splitting open on every block, the air thick enough to chew. Good. Crowds were a country that welcomed her.

*Tomas, what have you done?*

The telegram told her almost nothing, but telegrams always lied by omission, and Marisol had spent forty years reading what her brother did not say. *Sold the boat.* He would not use the word *sold* for anything he regretted — he had used it, once, for his share of the family house, given up to pay a debt that was never his. *Buyer will keep her safe.* *Her.* Not *it*, not *the goods*. A person. And there was only one person in the world Tomas would entrust to a stranger rather than bring to Marisol.

The child. His son's daughter. The baby whose photograph he kept folded inside his watch cover, though he believed no one knew — a small solemn face, weeks old, taken the one time he'd been permitted to visit. Marisol had seen the picture exactly once, over his shoulder, and had never asked, because some things a brother deserved to keep even from a sister. Especially from a sister whose work meant that names got asked after.

And now the boy was dead — she'd had that confirmation in Corazón Bay, from a shipping clerk who owed her, for a price she didn't want to think about. A crane accident at Pier Nine. A company accident. The words were synonyms in this city.

So Tomas had taken the child and run, and when running stopped working — when the net drew tight around him and they named him traitor to make the whole city hunt him gladly — he had passed the baby to someone. Someone brave, he'd judged. Someone inside the walls of the beast itself, if her guesses held any weight at all.

Which meant the baby was in the Verrone trading house. Which meant Marisol had to get her out. And between those two facts stood the entire machinery of the richest enterprise on this coast, plus two gray coats who were not looking for Marisol Reyes yet but would be soon.

*Do not come,* indeed.

* * *

She took a room above a noodle house on Rope Street itself, three doors down from the company dormitory, because the best place to hide was inside the shadow of the thing you watched. The landlady asked no questions; Marisol paid a week in advance and tipped enough to buy a lifetime of incuriosity. By evening she had a window with a view of the dormitory's servants' door and a cup of tea going cold beside her, and she settled into the chair the way she'd settled into surveillance posts in three ports and two wars that no one had officially declared.

The servants' door opened often. Girls went out with laundry bundles, came back with marketing baskets. A lame young man — a clerk, by his ink-stained cuffs and the careful way he favored his left leg — left twice and returned once. A big Irish woman with flour on her forearms crossed to the cookshop and back.

Marisol watched all of it with the patience of her trade, and none of it interested her, until the girl came out with the pearl crates.

She was perhaps nineteen, dark-haired, with the deep-chested stillness of someone raised around divers — Marisol recognized it instantly; her own mother had moved like that. But it wasn't the girl's carriage that caught her. It was the way she carried the crate.

Divers' daughters hauled crates balanced on the hip, casual as bread loaves. This girl carried hers against her chest, both arms wrapped around it, angled so that nothing could press against the front of it — the way women carry infants, the way Marisol herself had carried things she must not drop and must not show. And when a man jostled past her on the step, too close, the girl turned — quick, total, a mother's pivot — putting her body between the crate and the street before her face remembered to go blank again.

Marisol set down her tea.

For an hour she watched the girl make three trips, each crate handled like a sleeping child, each trip ending at the same covered cart bound for the company warehouse. On the third trip, the girl paused at the corner, in the gap between lamplight and shadow, and Marisol saw her lips move — a whisper, bent down toward the top of the crate, gone as fast as it came.

Then the girl looked up, directly at Marisol's window, and for one heartbeat their eyes met across fifty feet of dusk, and Marisol felt the jolt of it go through her like a current: *she knows I'm watching. Or she knows someone is. She knows what she's carrying, and she knows it's worth watching for.*

The girl walked on, unhurried, invisible again. But Marisol was already pulling on her shawl.

* * *

She nearly lost her twice. Once in the market crush by the weighhouse, where the girl slipped through gaps no full-grown adult should have fit through, and once at the harbor steps, where she simply stopped and stood for a long moment staring at the water as if it owed her something, and Marisol had to melt behind a stack of lobster pots and become a woman examining lobster pots with great scholarly interest.

But the trail was there, under the trail, and Marisol had spent a lifetime reading under-trails. The girl went to the apothecary on Cooper's Lane — Marisol noted the shop, noted the chit the girl handed over, noted the way the apothecary's eyes flicked to the signature and lingered half a heartbeat too long before he fetched the milk-thistle syrup that only nursing infants need. Then the girl doubled back through the tannery row — clever, using the reek as a wall no gentleman agent would willingly cross — and emerged at last on the mudflats below Rope Street, where the tide had gone out and left the world smelling of old iron.

Marisol hung back at the seawall and watched her meet the lame clerk.

They stood well apart, the way conspirators do, but conspirators who trusted each other. The clerk said something that made the girl laugh — actually laugh, brief and startled out of her, the sound carrying flat across the mud — and Marisol felt something twist in her chest that she refused to examine. Tomas laughing like that, once, on a dock in another life.

Then the girl handed the clerk something small, and even at this distance Marisol's eye caught the flash of metal, and her breath stopped.

She knew that shape. She knew it the way you know your own name written in someone else's hand.

A diving token. Half of one — the broken-tongue silhouette of the old pearl guild tokens, stamped with a compass rose split down the middle, each half worn by a partner who agreed to trust the other with his life at depth. Her father had carried one. Her father's partner — the quiet island boy who dove deeper than any man had a right to and came up smiling — had carried the other. Silas. That was his name. The girl's name, if Marisol's information was right: Silas Marchetti, diver's daughter, indentured at eleven.

Her father and Tomas Reyes had split a token thirty years ago, and here was the daughter holding half of one, meeting a crippled clerk on the mudflats at dusk, carrying a hidden infant through a city crawling with agents hunting her brother.

*Oh, Tomas,* she thought, and her eyes stung, and she let them, just for a moment, alone on the seawall with the tide coming home. *You clever, impossible man. You gave her your half.*

* * *

The trouble came later that night, and it came, as trouble did, in gray coats.

Marisol was returning to the noodle house by the back alley when she heard them ahead — two voices, low and professional, and the particular quality of silence that meant a third person pressed against a wall. She should have walked away. Every discipline she owned said walk away; you cannot save anyone by getting arrested on the first night.

But the silence had a shape, and the shape was slight, and the alley mouth framed it: the girl from the dormitory, backed against the tannery fence, and the two gray coats from the customs stairs closing in.

"—just want to ask about a letter," one was saying, in the tone men use when they mean something else entirely. "From Tomas Reyes. You've had contact. We know."

"I dust the clerks' room," the girl said. Flat. Perfectly flat. "If letters fall behind desks, that's Mr. Fenwick's business."

Marisol was already moving, because she had done this before, in Alexandria, in Corazón Bay, and because the alternative was watching this girl learn what happened to people who stood between Verrone money and Verrone fear. She came around the corner loud — drunk-loud, stumbling, a woman who had spent her wages badly — and collided spectacularly with the nearer agent, grabbing his coat, weeping theatrically about her husband, her lost husband, Emilio, Emilio, has anyone seen Emilio—

The agent shoved her off with an oath. The second one turned to look. And in that half-second of divided attention the girl did something that Marisol filed away forever: she didn't run. Running would have confirmed everything. Instead she simply picked up her market basket and walked toward them, straight through the middle of the pair, muttering *"pardon me"* like a servant late for supper, and was gone around the corner before either man's brain caught up with his eyes.

Marisol let the agents disentangle themselves from her performance, apologized for her disgraceful condition, wished their mothers good health, and drifted off up the alley in the opposite direction, listening until the gray coats' footsteps faded toward the harbor without turning back.

Then she stood a long time in the dark, heart hammering, and laughed once — a short, amazed breath of a laugh.

*Two of us,* she thought. *He found two of us.*

* * *

She caught up with the girl at the mouth of Rope Street, where the lamp burned badly and the fog was coming in off the water in slow gray banks. Marisol didn't grab her, didn't call out, didn't do anything the agents might have anticipated. She simply fell into step beside her, matching her pace, and said, quietly:

"The syrup won't work unless she takes it warm. My sister-in-law swore by it. Warm, with a drop of honey."

The girl stopped walking.

She didn't turn at first. Marisol watched her hands instead, and what she saw there frightened her more than the gray coats had — the girl's fists closing around the basket handle, knuckles whitening, her whole small body gathering itself like a wave deciding whether to break. She had the look of someone calculating distances: to the dormitory, to the knife Marisol hadn't noticed at her hip until now, to the dark water at the end of the street.

"My name is Marisol Reyes," Marisol said. "Tomas is my brother. And I think you're carrying my niece."

Silence. Fog. Somewhere behind them a dog barked twice and quit.

Then the girl turned, and up close her eyes were extraordinary — dark, level, older than nineteen had any right to be, eyes that had counted a thousand breaths underwater and never wasted one. She looked at Marisol the way a diver looks at the surface from depth: measuring how far, how fast, whether it could be trusted.

"Prove it," Silas Marchetti said.

Marisol had prepared proofs. Photographs she didn't have, documents she couldn't risk carrying. What she had was simpler and better. She reached into her collar and drew out the chain she wore against her skin, and let the pendant swing free into the bad lamplight: the other half of a diving token, compass rose complete, the break-line matching — as she had known it would — the edge of the token the girl had passed to the lame clerk on the flats.

"He gave your father the half your father died still wearing," Marisol said. "I know, because I was there the night they split it, and I soldered the rings myself. Your father kept my brother's secrets at the bottom of the sea for six years, and my brother has never forgotten it. Neither have I."

The girl stared at the token. Her hand rose halfway toward it and stopped, as if touching it might cost something.

"She has my brother's eyes," Marisol went on, softer now. "Everyone says the baby's eyes will change, that all infants' eyes are gray at first. His weren't. They were black as tide-pools from the day he was born, and if his granddaughter is carrying them, I'll know her in the dark. Let me see her, and you won't need any proof from me at all."

Something moved behind Silas's face — fear and hope grinding against each other like two stones, neither winning. When she spoke, her voice was very quiet.

"If you follow me, if you watch me, if you tell anyone—" She stopped. Started again, and Marisol heard beneath the steel, for just a moment, the terrified girl underneath: "I burned his letter. I don't know why she matters to him or to you. I only know I was told to forget her face." Her jaw tightened. "I can't. So whoever wants her safe had better mean it."

"More than anything I have ever meant," Marisol said.

Above them the dormitory windows showed one light burning late — the cook's window, probably, or the clerks'. Below, the tide was coming in over the mudflats, filling the prints of two sets of footsteps, erasing the meeting before it had quite finished happening. Down at the harbor, faint through the fog, a bell buoy tolled, and Marisol thought she saw — or imagined, or feared — a gray coat at the far corner of Rope Street, standing too still beneath the gaslight, watching the two women in the lamplight the way spiders watch the edges of webs.

Silas saw it too. Marisol watched her see it — watched the girl's face go smooth and empty and servant-plain, watched her lift her market basket, and heard her say, in a voice pitched for anyone listening, perfectly bored:

"This way, miss. The kitchen door's faster than the front, if you're wanting the night porter."

And Marisol Reyes, who had circled secrets on four coasts and trusted almost no one in forty years, put her arm through the arm of a nineteen-year-old pearl-crates girl and walked with her into the fog, toward the door, toward the child — while behind them, unhurried, the figure at the corner began to follow.
