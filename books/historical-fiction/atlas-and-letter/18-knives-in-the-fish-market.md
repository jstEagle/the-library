## 18. Knives in the Fish Market

Market day came with a wind off the harbor that smelled of brine and diesel and the sweet rot of fish left too long in the sun. Brigid was still locked away somewhere behind the Constabulary's iron doors, and the dormitory kitchen had gone silent without her — no clatter of pots before dawn, no voice calling us down like gulls to scraps — and so it fell to me to fetch what we needed, because a house full of children still had to eat.

Fenwick insisted on coming.

"You shouldn't walk alone," he said, and there was no argument in his tone, only fact, the way he stated everything. He took his stick and his hat and limped down the stairs ahead of me as though I were the one who needed looking after.

"I've walked to market a thousand times," I said.

"Not while Director Verrone knows your name."

He was right, of course. Fenwick was usually right about the movements of dangerous men; he had spent eleven years keeping their ledgers. But I had the baby's milk money sewn into my petticoat and a basket on my arm and the particular stubbornness of a woman who had decided, three nights ago on the mudflats, that fear would no longer be allowed to make her decisions for her.

The market spread along Rope Street like spilled cargo — awnings sagging under the wind, crates of silver-skinned mackerel flashing wet in the light, women shouting prices over each other's heads. Gulls wheeled and screamed. Somewhere a barrel organ played something jaunty and slightly out of tune, and children wove between legs with hands quick as pickpockets' thoughts.

I bought onions. I bought milk from the dairy cart because it was harder to trace than the shop. I haggled over salt pork and let the vendor think he'd won, because a woman who wins every haggle gets remembered, and being remembered is the first step toward being found.

It was Fenwick who saw them first.

He didn't say anything. He simply changed direction, angling his body between mine and the mouth of the alley behind the fish stalls, and said, too lightly, "Let's try the cheesemonger on the far side, shall we? Brigid always said his prices were robbery anyway."

I looked. Three men stood at the alley mouth pretending to examine a crate of crabs. They were dressed like dockworkers — canvas trousers, rolled sleeves, knuckles scarred from work that wasn't work — but they stood like company men, feet planted, weight balanced, the loose readiness of hired muscle. And the tallest of them wore a brass pin in his collar shaped like a pearl in an oyster shell.

Verrone Trading's mark.

I had seen that pin my whole life, on clerks and captains and the director himself. I had never once seen it on a man standing in a fish market looking at me the way a cat looks at a hole in the wall.

"Walk," Fenwick murmured. "Don't run. Running tells them what they want to know."

We walked. The organ music seemed very loud and very far away at once. My heart was doing the thing my father taught me to master underwater — the slow deliberate counting, four beats in, six beats out, because panic spends air faster than fear does. *Count*, I told myself. *You have held your breath in darker water than this.*

They let us get halfway down the row of stalls. Then they moved.

It happened the way these things always happen — faster than thought, messier than any story would later tell it. A hand closed on my basket strap from behind. Another man stepped out from between two barrels of ice, cutting off the lane ahead. Fishwives went quiet stall by stall, the silence spreading outward from us like oil on water, and the barrel organ, with the unerring instinct of musicians everywhere for trouble, stopped mid-phrase.

"Silas Marchetti." The tall one said my name like he'd practiced it. Perhaps he had. "Got a minute?"

"I'm expected back," I said. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "Put down my basket."

"In a moment." He smiled. He had a face like a closed ledger — nothing written on it that he didn't want written there. "There's a matter of some correspondence. A letter, come into your possession recently. The company would like it returned."

"There's correspondence in my basket," I said. "An onion. You can have the onion."

His smile didn't move but something behind it did — a small tightening, the flicker of a man deciding how much of this needed to be gentle. "Miss Marchetti. I'm going to say this plainly, because plain men understand plain words. Mr. Verrone knows you received a letter from Tomas Reyes. He knows what was in it. He knows you burned it." He leaned in, close enough that I could smell the tobacco and the fish-stall reek on him. "Burned paper can be read again, if a man has the right friends. So here's the friendly version: tell us what it said, and where you're keeping what he gave you, and you walk home with your basket and your pretty skin intact."

*What he gave you.* So they knew — or suspected, or were fishing — that there was more than a letter. The coordinates. The token. The child. My blood went cold and slow, the way seawater does at depth, and in that coldness my father's voice spoke clear as a bell across eleven drowned years: *When the current takes you, girl, don't fight it straight. Go slack. Let it think it's won. Then move.*

I let my shoulders drop. Let my eyes go wide and frightened — not entirely acting; there was enough true fear in me to fill the role honestly. "I don't know what you mean," I whispered. "A letter? Someone left papers at our door weeks ago, bills, I burned them with the kindling — I didn't read them, I can't read well—" I let my voice crack on the lie, a small ugly sound, the sound of a girl trying to be small. "Please, I'm indentured, I'll lose my place—"

For a moment I saw him believe it. Nineteen years of being invisible had taught me exactly how invisible girls are expected to tremble.

But the third man — the one who hadn't spoken, squat and heavy-jawed with a nose broken flat — wasn't watching me. He was watching Fenwick. And Fenwick, God help him, had never learned to hide anything on his face, which is why he'd survived eleven years in the counting house and would survive nothing else.

"She's lying," Flat-Nose said. "Ask the cripple. He does her thinking for her."

"Sir," Fenwick began, "I assure you—"

The tall one hit him.

I will write what happened next plainly, because plain words are all it deserves, though my hands shake even now setting them down. It was not a fight. Fights have two sides. It was a beating, and it was meant for me, and Fenwick stepped into it — stepped *in front* of me, stick raised uselessly, shouting "Run, Silas, run, you fool, *run*" — and then it was fists and boots and the terrible wet percussion of them landing, and Fenwick going down among the fish guts and the melting ice with his arm over his head, and the crowd pulling back in that awful way crowds do, making room for violence the way water makes room for a stone.

I did not run.

I want that recorded somewhere, even if only in this account that no one may ever read: I did not run. I swung the basket. I brought it down across the tall man's head with everything nineteen years of hauling pearl crates had put into my shoulders, onions scattering across the cobbles like shrapnel, and he staggered and swore and turned on me with his lip split —

— and that was when the fishwife moved.

She was a big woman, gray-haired, forearms like hams, and she stepped between me and him holding a mackerel in each fist like a pair of holy relics, and she said in a voice that carried the length of Rope Street: "Three men beating a lame clerk in broad daylight? On market day? You want the whole harbor down here, boy? Because they'll come. They always come. And they don't ask whose side you're on first."

And the crowd — the beautiful, dangerous, ordinary crowd — pressed in. Not attacking. Just *present*. Forty pairs of eyes, and above them a hundred more at windows, and somewhere a whistle starting up, the constable's two-note call from the corner of Wharf Road. The tall man looked at the fishwife, at the gathering faces, at the growing noise, and did the arithmetic that company men always do: the cost of witnesses against the value of the prize.

"We'll finish this conversation," he said to me, spitting blood onto the cobbles, "another time."

Then they were gone, swallowed by the lanes, and the crowd's noise broke over us like a wave — questions, outrage, someone offering brandy from a flask, someone else shouting for a doctor.

I dropped to my knees in the fish slush beside Fenwick.

* * *

He was conscious, which the doctor at the charity surgery later said was fortunate, and stubborn, which she said was less so. His left eye was swelling shut and already purpling; two ribs were cracked, maybe three; his forearm, raised to guard his head, had taken the rest. He lay on the cot gripping my hand with fingers that felt terribly thin and refused, absolutely refused, to stop talking.

"They knew about the letter," he rasped. "Not just the letter. *What Reyes gave you.* That means someone talked. Someone close."

"The medicine chit," I said, hollow. "Hale traced the chit. Brigid—"

"No." He winced as he shifted. "Hale knows about the chit, yes. But Verrone's thugs asking after a letter Hale hasn't mentioned in any deposition — that's a different ear, Silas. That's someone inside the walls. Someone who reads what I read."

The dormitory. The counting house. Eleven years of belonging purchased by obedience, and now every familiar face turned over in my mind like a coin I suddenly couldn't trust. The boy who swept the shipping office. Mrs. Calloway who managed the servants' hall. The night watchman who had nodded to me a hundred times and never once asked why my errands ran late.

"Fenwick," I said, "you took a beating for me. Why? You could have walked away. You should have."

He tried to laugh and it turned into a cough that bent him double until I steadied him. When he could speak again his good eye found mine, and there was something in it I had never been shown by anyone — not my father drowning in debt, not the company buying my childhood, not one single soul in nineteen years.

"Because on the mudflats you told me the truth," he said, "and truth is rarer than pearls in this town, and worth more. Because there's a child sleeping above the boil house who laughs when Brigid sings off-key, and I have kept books for eleven years and never once entered anything in a ledger that mattered until her." His grip tightened on my hand, weak as it was. "You're not paying for your promise alone anymore, Silas. None of us are. That's what family means. That's the whole entry. Debits on both sides."

I bowed my head over our joined hands so he wouldn't see my face break open, and outside the surgery window the gulls screamed over the harbor, and the wind carried the smell of salt and blood and fish, and I understood that Verrone's men would come again — that they would keep coming, patient as tide, until they had the letter that no longer existed and the secret that very much did.

The doctor cleared her throat at the door. "There's a woman asking after you," she said. "Says she's a friend of the family. Dark-haired, foreign accent, wouldn't give a name — but she's been standing across the street since before those men followed you, miss. Before the trouble started."

My blood turned to harbor ice. *Before.* She had been watching before the thugs ever moved.

"She says," the doctor went on, frowning at the scrap of paper in her hand, "that you have something that belongs to her brother. And that you should meet her tonight — at the old pier, where the diving tokens are nailed."
