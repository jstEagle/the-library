## 19. Confessions at Low Tide

The tide went out an hour past noon and left the harbor's skirt exposed — a mile of grey-brown mudflats stitched with creeks, littered with the skeletons of boats too broken even for scrap. Silas came down the Rope Street stairs at low water because it was the one place in the port where no one could approach without being seen from a distance. Her father had taught her that much about flats: you can read a footprint in mud the way a clerk reads a ledger.

Fenwick was already there, of course. He could not walk fast, but he could walk early, and he sat on an upturned dory half-buried in silt like a heron waiting for fish, his bad leg stretched before him, his good hand shading his eyes against the flat white light off the water.

"You got my note," she said.

"I dust the same shelves you do." He didn't look at her. "I know which books get moved."

* * *

Fenwick had rehearsed this conversation forty times in the dark of the clerks' dormitory, lying awake with his lame leg aching and the words turning over like pages he couldn't stop reading. Forty rehearsals and none of them survived the sight of her standing ten feet away in the mud with her sleeves rolled and her diver's hands hanging quiet at her sides.

"Sit," he said. "It's a long story, and I don't want to watch your face while I tell it."

She sat on the mud beside the dory, not on it — beside him, close enough that their shadows joined on the silt. That small thing nearly undid him.

"You know what I do here," he began. "You've seen me. I copy manifests. I check weights. I initial things that other men decided years before I was hired. Eleven years I've done it, and I told myself that was honest work, because ink is honest even when the hands aren't."

"Fenwick—"

"Let me finish or I'll never start." He turned his collar up against the wind coming off the water. "Last autumn, Tomas Reyes started staying late. You'd have noticed it if you'd been watching — he kept odd hours, asked for freight records going back three seasons, requisitioned candles like a man reading the whole library. I thought he was studying for advancement. Men like Tomas don't advance, so I should have known better. Men like Tomas study for other reasons."

Silas said nothing. The tide gurgled somewhere under the mud, filling a channel.

"One night in December I came back for my gloves. His office lamp was burning, and the door was open, and he had Ledger Nine spread across two desks." Fenwick's good hand closed slowly around nothing. "Do you know what Ledger Nine is?"

She shook her head.

"It's the prettiest book in the company. Pearl freight out, specie and finished goods back, all of it neat as scripture. Except pearls don't need crates lined in lead, Silas. Pearls don't need armed escorts from the foundry district. Pearls don't weigh four hundred pounds per case when the manifest says eighty." His voice cracked and he let it. "Nine is guns. Rifles and cartridges and field pieces, shipped out under pearl seals to buyers the Director won't name even in his own hand. And some of those buyers fly colors the Crown doesn't care for."

The wind moved across the flats, pushing ripples of shadow through the shallow pools. Somewhere behind them, up on the seawall, a gull screamed once and gave it up.

"He saw me standing in the door," Fenwick said. "And instead of raising the alarm, Tomas did the worst thing anyone has ever done to me. He trusted me. He said, *Fenwick, you have eyes for numbers. Tell me if I'm mad.* So I read. All night. And by dawn I wasn't reading anymore, I was copying — dates, tonnages, seal numbers — while he stood watch at the window. We made duplicates of thirty-one pages. He took most of them. I kept a few, because he said if anything happened to him, the copies would need a second home, and no one searches the desk of a lame clerk they've stopped seeing."

"And then he ran," Silas said quietly.

"And then he ran." Fenwick looked at her at last. "Or was pushed toward running. The treason charge came down eleven days later, and the charts the Constabulary produced — foreign navy charts, marked in his hand — I saw them when Hale's men laid them out. Silas, the handwriting is right. It's *too* right. Nobody's handwriting is that perfect unless someone practiced it. And the dates on the charts cross the dates on the manifests like two rivers that shouldn't meet. Tomas couldn't have sold charts to a foreign navy on nights he was copying Ledger Nine with me. But saying so would mean producing the manifest dates, and producing the manifest dates means opening Nine, and opening Nine—" He gestured at the harbor, the warehouses, the whole grey city above the mud. "Means all of this comes down."

He had imagined, in forty rehearsals, that confessing would feel like setting down a weight. It felt the opposite — like handing her something heavy and watching whether her arms held.

"I haven't told you the worst of it," he said. "The worst is smaller than treason. The worst is that I kept initialing manifests after December. I knew what the crates were, and I signed anyway, because I'm a coward with rent to pay and one good leg. Until—" He stopped.

"Until what?"

"Until whatever it is that's changed you these past weeks." He said it gently, without accusation. "You burn candles down to nothing in your room. You disappear between the second bell and supper. Brigid hoards milk like a woman with a secret calf, and she looks at you when she does it. And yesterday you asked the harbormaster's boy, casual as Sunday, which piers have pilings loose enough to pry." He smiled, tired and fond. "I dust the same shelves you do, girl. Tell me what we're carrying, because I've been carrying mine alone for months and my back is bent enough already."

* * *

The mud sucked at Silas's boots when she shifted, holding her ankle with a soft wet grip, as if the flats themselves wanted her to stay put and say nothing. She thought about her father, who had once told her: *the sea keeps everything you drop in it, but only gives back what it wants to.* She had been dropping things into herself for weeks — the baby, the letter's ashes, the token, the coordinates, the woman named Marisol who was even now somewhere in the port asking questions. Every secret a stone. She was heavy enough to sink.

But Fenwick had just handed her his whole weight, and asked for hers.

"There's a child," she said.

His eyebrows rose, but he waited, and she was grateful for that — grateful he didn't fill the silence with questions.

"A baby girl. Someone left her at the servants' door in the night, seven weeks ago. Wrapped in good wool, fed before she was abandoned, with a letter pinned to the blanket." Her voice stayed level the way her father had taught her breath to stay level underwater — measured, rationed, controlled by discipline rather than calm. "The letter said to forget her face. One line. And the handwriting was Tomas Reyes's."

Fenwick breathed out through his nose, slow.

"She's hidden in the east dormitory. Brigid knows. Brigid found her and chose not to report her, and since then Brigid has stolen milk and taught me to feed her and forged a chit for fever medicine when the baby sickened — which is how you'll all hang someday, so I need you to understand what you're agreeing to before you agree." She made herself keep going. "The chit will trace back. Chits always trace back. That's why I came to you now, because if the Constabulary pulls that thread, they'll find Brigid, and Brigid will say nothing to protect me, and they'll take her apart for it."

"They might already be pulling it," Fenwick said grimly. "Hale's not stupid."

"No. He watched my hands when he searched the house. He noticed they're clerk's calluses, not crate calluses." She flexed her fingers. "He let me go, but he let me go the way a man lets a fish run with the hook set."

"So Reyes left his own—"

"She's not his daughter. The letter said so. *She is not mine, but she is yours now if you are brave.* I burned it, Fenwick. Read it twice and burned it, and I can tell you every word anyway." She pulled at the mud around her boot, needing her hands busy. "And there's more. My father dove for pearls twelve years, and Tomas saved his life once — long story, older than me — and when my father died, Tomas gave him a diving token, half of a brass pair, engraved with a star and a depth mark. I still have it. Three weeks ago I found the other half nailed under the third pier on the north quay, and scratched beside it, coordinates. Latitude and longitude, running south along the coast."

"To Tomas," Fenwick said. "He left you a map."

"He left my *father* a map. He left the baby to me. Whether those are the same act, I don't know yet." She looked out across the flats, where the returning tide was beginning to slide up the channels, patient as anything. "There's one more thing and then you can decide if you want any of this. A woman came to the market asking after me last week. Dark-haired, Reyes jaw, moves like a sailor. She found my trail through the medicine chit — she'd bribed an apothecary clerk. Her name is Marisol, and she says she's Tomas's sister, and she says the baby is her niece — her brother's late sweetheart's orphan, smuggled out of the house the night the company burned it down and called it an accident."

"Company accident," Fenwick repeated flatly.

"That's her word. Given what you've just told me about Nine, I believe her." Silas finally turned to face him fully. "So. Now you know everything. A baby, a burned letter, a brass token, a map, a sister hunting us, a forged chit, and thirty-one pages of proof sitting wherever you hid them. If you walk back up those stairs and forget this conversation, I will never blame you. I'll carry it alone. I've been carrying it alone."

Fenwick laughed — short, rusty, disbelieving. "Carry it alone. Girl, I've been waiting eleven years for someone to ask me to be brave, and now that it's happened, you think I'd decline?" He pushed himself up off the dory, wincing, and stood with both hands on the gunwale like a captain taking his deck. "I copied those pages knowing what they meant. I just never had a reason to use them. Now I've got a baby, a cook, a fool girl with diver's lungs, and a one-legged bookkeeper, and that's the finest crew I ever heard of."

"It's not a crew. It's a disaster with a schedule."

"Same thing, on the water." He sobered. "The pages. I hid them inside the dormitory wall — the cavity behind the old flue brick in the linen room. Third course up, loose on the left. If Verrone burns anything, it'll be the records wing first, so they need to come out soon and go somewhere safer."

"Marisol knows a place. There's a lighthouse on the southern reach, decommissioned—"

"Then we move them tomorrow night, when the watch changes and Brigid has everyone in the washroom." He said it fast, committing himself, the way a man steps off a dock. Then his voice gentled. "And Silas. This family of ours — the strange little thing we've built inside those walls, with the milk and the candle stubs and now a fugitive's map — I want you to know I love it. I love it the way lame men love stairs, awkwardly and completely. Whatever happens, I don't regret a page of it."

Silas's throat closed. Out on the flats the tide had found its rhythm, sliding in over the channels, erasing footprints one by one, and she understood that this conversation was already being erased too — that by morning it would exist only in two bodies and nowhere else in the world. Trust was exactly that, she thought. Writing your name in mud below the tideline and choosing to believe it mattered anyway.

"Tomorrow night, then," she said.

They climbed the stairs together, slow, his bad leg and her heavy heart keeping the same careful time.

Neither of them saw the figure at the top of the seawall — dark-haired, Reyes-jawed, watching — nor noticed that Marisol had heard enough to know two things: where the pages were hidden, and that they were not safe there another day.
