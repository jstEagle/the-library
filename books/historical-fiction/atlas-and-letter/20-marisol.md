## 20. Marisol

She came at the change of tide, the hour when the harbor loosens its grip and every boat in the roads swings on its mooring like a sleeper turning over. I had learned to mark that hour the way other people marked church bells — it was when the dormitory's work thinned, when Brigid's kettle sang unwatched, when a body could stand at a window and simply breathe. I was doing exactly that, three days after Fenwick took the beating meant for me, three days of watching him wince over his ledger with his ribs strapped in linen, when I saw her cross Rope Street.

I knew her before she reached the door. Not by face — I had never seen her face — but by the way she moved. She walked like someone reading a page laid flat on the cobbles: eyes down, then up, then down again, taking the street in lines. She paused at the Verrone sign. She looked at our windows the way my father had looked at water before a dive — measuring depth, weighing what might come up.

Then she came through the door as if she owned it.

The receiving room was empty but for me and a crate of mother-of-pearl buttons waiting for inventory. She stopped two paces inside, and I saw her take me in the same way — hands first, face second, the way sailors judge a stranger. My hands were crate hands. Diver's daughter hands. Whatever she read in them, it made her close the door behind her without hurry.

"You're the Marchetti girl," she said.

Not a question. The sound of her voice went through me like cold water finding a seam in a boot. Spanish rolled under her English, softened at the edges but not softened enough to hide anything.

"There's no Marchetti here," I said. "There's an indentured girl, number 114, property of the Verrone Trading Company until such time as—"

"I know what you are." She pulled off one glove, finger by finger. She had rings under the leather; they caught the gray window light. "I've been eleven days in this city. I know what you are, what you were, what your father owed, who paid the bribe that kept your small bones out of the workhouse. I know which door of this building faces the alley." Her chin lifted toward the back of the house, toward the servants' entrance, and my heart stopped against my ribs like a bird hitting glass. "And I know that in April a child came through it."

* * *

I have stood on the bottom of the world. That is what we called it, my father and I — the moment a diver's feet touch seabed, forty fathoms of black water pressing down, and the whole sky reduced to a coin of light overhead that you cannot reach. He taught me that panic is just breath spent badly. He taught me to count. Four counts in, six counts out, and the fear becomes arithmetic instead of screaming.

I counted while the woman watched me. Four in. Six out.

"I don't know what you mean," I said, "by child."

"Then you won't mind if I look."

She started toward the back of the house and there was nothing theatrical about it, no bluff, and I understood with terrible clarity that she had already looked everywhere else in this port. Eleven days. She had turned over this city like a stone and found nothing underneath but me.

I put myself between her and the corridor. It was a foolish thing physically — she was taller than I was, broader through the shoulders, and her hands were working hands despite the gloves and rings — but my body did it before my mind could vote, the way a hand closes around a knife when it slips on a deck.

"She isn't yours to find," I said.

The word *she* did what all my careful silences had failed to do. Something moved behind the woman's eyes — not triumph. Relief, raw and ugly and beautiful, like a wound finally allowed to bleed.

"So it's true." Her voice cracked on the last syllable and she hated it; I watched her hate it. "God in heaven. Eight months. Eight months I've been looking, and you're standing in front of me like a wall." She pressed her gloved palm flat against her sternum, and for a moment she wasn't reading the street anymore, she was just a woman breathing. Then the wall went back up, because hers was a wall built the same way mine was, and I recognized the masonry. "You'll forgive me," she said, "if I don't trust walls in a company house."

"And you'll forgive me," I answered, "if I don't trust anyone who says the name Reyes out loud in a room with thin partitions."

That stopped her. Her gaze flicked to the ceiling, the floor, the thin plaster walls with their water stains shaped like countries on no map that exists. When she spoke again it was barely above breath.

"Marisol Reyes," she said. "Tomas is my brother. The child is my niece. Her mother was Ana Ferro, and Ana Ferro is dead because a cargo crane's chain was cut on a Tuesday when no cranes were meant to run, and the company paid three men to say otherwise." Each sentence landed like a diver's foot on seabed. "Now. Are you going to make me say all of this twice, in a hallway, where any clerk can hear?"

* * *

We ended up — of all the places in that city — in the boil-house yard, among the steam kettles and the stink of rendered fat, because it was the one place no clerk voluntarily went and the noise covered voices. Brigid saw us pass through the kitchen and asked nothing with her whole face asking everything, and I gave her the smallest nod I owned, and she put herself in the doorway to the hall like a mountain range deciding where to be.

Marisol stood in the steam with her gloves off now and her sleeves pushed up, and she studied me the way I imagined she studied everything — as something that might be forged or might be true.

"Tomas wrote to you," she said. "Before he ran."

"He didn't run." It came out of me fast and fierce, and I hadn't known I was going to say it. "Everyone says he ran. Men who run don't leave their niece on a doorstep with instructions for her care. Men who run don't—" I stopped myself, but she had already leaned forward into the gap in my sentence like a swimmer finding current.

"Don't what?"

Four counts in. Six counts out.

"He left a letter," I said. "It's gone. I burned it within the hour. But I remember every word, because I have that kind of mind, and one line of it was this: *she is not mine, but she is yours now if you are brave.*" I made myself hold her eyes. "He didn't ask me to keep her safe from the company, madam. He asked me to keep her safe *for* something. There's a difference, and I've spent four months learning what it is."

The steam rolled between us. Somewhere beyond the yard wall a bell buoy clanged, slow and patient, counting the tide.

"Ana trusted him with everything," Marisol said quietly. "When she understood what was happening — the shipments, the men watching her husband, all of it — she wrote to Tomas, not to the constabulary, not to the consulate. And when she died, Tomas took the baby because the baby was the only living witness to anything, do you understand? The child is proof that Ana existed, that Ana married, that Ana feared for her life. A court can do things with that. So can a knife." Her jaw tightened. "He got her out of the city. He couldn't stay with her and couldn't leave her anywhere the company held paper — which is everywhere — except one place. One person."

"Why me?" The question came out of me the way it had lived in me since April, worn smooth from handling. "I was nobody. I'm still nobody. An indentured girl who hauls crates and keeps her head down."

"Are you?" Marisol said. "Because my brother has told stories about a pearl diver's daughter for fifteen years. About a man named Marchetti who dove the Verrone beds before the company bought them, who found something on the bottom once that could have sunk the company in its cradle, and who chose to bring it to a young clerk named Reyes instead of selling it to men who would have used it worse." She tilted her head. "Your father kept my brother's secrets, and my brother kept his, and when your father drowned owing money to devils, Tomas paid the bribe that kept his daughter out of the workhouse. He never explained why, to me. But he trusted your house. He trusted your blood." Her eyes were very steady. "Apparently he trusted you with the last thing on this earth he loves."

My hands had closed without my permission, right fist around left, around nothing — except that it wasn't nothing. Since the mudflats, since the night I'd pried the token loose from under the pier, I had carried both halves of my father's diving token sewn into my waistband, against my skin, the way other women carried coins. Brass halves worn smooth, the broken scallop engraving matched along the fracture, and beside the matching half, scratched into metal too old to remember being new: numbers. Coordinates. My father's handwriting or Reyes's, I had never decided which.

I had told Fenwick. I had told no one else alive.

I thought about the fish market. About Fenwick going down under boots for a letter that no longer existed, about the sound his ribs had made, about the price that everyone around me kept paying for a promise I had made alone at a servants' door. I thought: this woman has been hunting my secret across eleven days and eight months of ocean, and she will not stop, and if the company finds her first they will pull everything out of her, and Atlas will end up in some company orphanage with a number instead of a name, and everything — Fenwick's ribs, Brigid's risk, the burned letter, the coordinates under the pier — will have bought nothing at all.

Or I could stop carrying it alone.

Four counts in. Six counts out.

I untied the cord at my waistband and held out my hand, palm up, with both halves of the token lying in it.

Marisol went very still. Steam drifted between us. Then she bent over my palm as though over scripture, and her breath came out ragged.

"That's Tomas's half," she whispered. "He wore it on a cord around his neck since we were children. Our father cut the pair from a ship's fitting — brass off a wrecked brigantine, he claimed, though he lied beautifully so who knows." Her fingertip hovered above the metal without touching, as if touching might undo it. "He sent you his half."

"He gave it to my father, years ago. My father kept it in his kit. After the pier—" I stopped. Recalibrated. Some truths were still mine to ration. "I found where he'd hidden the rest. Under the third pile from the seaward ladder, nailed to the joist. There are coordinates scratched beside the matching marks."

"Coordinates." She said the word like a woman tasting it. "To where?"

"I don't know. North, along the coast. My father taught me to read bearings off charts before I could read words off pages, and these aren't harbor marks. They're a long way north. Days' travel."

"To a camp," Marisol said slowly. "An abandoned pearl camp, maybe. The company stripped the northern beds five years ago and left the camps standing." She straightened, and I watched the whole shape of it assemble behind her eyes, watch her arrive at the same shore I had been circling alone for weeks. "He's there. He's been there. Waiting to see if anyone was clever enough, or loyal enough, to follow the token."

The bell buoy clanged. The kettles hissed. In the doorway, Brigid stood her quiet mountain watch, pretending to scrub a pot that had been clean for ten minutes.

"If the coordinates lead somewhere," Marisol said, "and if my brother is at the end of them, then every day he waits is a day Verrone's men get closer. They're not looking for a traitor anymore — they know better than anyone that there is no traitor. They're looking for a witness. And witnesses," she said, "have short lives in this port."

"I know," I said. "One of them limps to breakfast every morning."

Her face changed — softened and hardened at once, guilt and fury sharing the same ground. "The lame clerk. I heard. I'm sorry. If I could have come sooner—"

"You came when you could. Everyone always does." I wrapped the token halves back into my palm. "What is it you want from me, Mrs. Reyes?"

"Marisol," she corrected. "And I want what you want. My brother alive, my niece safe, and Director Emilio Verrone swinging from the same gallows he built for Tomas." She said it without heat, which frightened me more than any threat could have. "But I want to hear it from you first. What do *you* want, Silas Marchetti?"

Nobody had asked me that. Not once in nineteen years, I think. The company wanted my labor. My father had wanted my survival. Brigid and Fenwick wanted my safety. Tomas, in his letter, had wanted my courage. All of those wants could be carried by someone else's hands.

This question required mine.

"I want her to grow up," I said, and my voice came out lower than I intended, rougher. "Somewhere the sound of a boat engine doesn't make me pick her up and stand against the far wall. I want her to have a name she didn't have to hide. I want the people who hurt her mother to be afraid for once." I swallowed. "And I want to finish what I promised a sleeping baby in April. That's all. That's everything."

Marisol looked at me for a long moment, steam curling past her face, and then she did something I did not expect. She extended her hand — bare, ringed, scarred across the knuckles like a woman who had hauled lines — and waited.

"Then we go together," she said. "Tonight, before the tide turns. You know how to move through this city without being seen; I have money and papers that will get us up the coast. We find my brother, we bring back whatever he's kept, and we end this — one way or another, we end it." Her grip, when I took it, was warm and hard as deck timber. "My brother trusted you with the most precious thing he had. I can see he wasn't wrong."

From the doorway, Brigid made a noise that was not quite a cough. I turned, and she jerked her chin toward the stairs — the universal signal of the trading house that someone official was asking for someone unofficial, and that someone unofficial had better decide quickly whether to exist.

"It's Hale," she said quietly. "The inspector. Asking after you by name, cool as you please, sitting in the parlor drinking tea like he owns the place." Her eyes went from my face to Marisol's and back, and I watched her choose, again, not to ask. "Fenwick's keeping him talking about button inventories. You've perhaps ten minutes, love. Less, if he runs out of patience, and that man's patience is shorter than it was."

Marisol was already pulling on her gloves. "Ten minutes," she said. "Where is the child?"

"With a woman two streets east who owes Brigid more than she owes the company. Safe for now. I collect her after dark."

"Then here is what happens." Marisol spoke fast and low, a sailor's economy, no wasted rope. "You go up, you face your inspector, you give him nothing and look bored doing it. I'll arrange passage north on a coastal packet — there's a captain at the fish quay who owes me from Cádiz and asks no questions. Tonight, the east alley behind the chandlery, after the lamplighter passes. Bring the child, bring the tokens, bring whatever courage you've been rationing." She paused at the yard door, and for one moment the iron went out of her and I saw the sister underneath, eight months hungry for a face. "Silas. If my brother is alive at the end of those coordinates—"

"He will be," I said. "I've been counting on it since April."

She nodded once, sharply, the way sailors nod — agreement compressed into geometry — and was gone through the boil-house steam like something the sea had sent and reclaimed.

Upstairs, Inspector Hale was asking Fenwick genial questions about mother-of-pearl buttons, and his eyes found me the moment I crossed the threshold, patient as a hook in deep water.

Ten minutes. By tonight, a chandlery alley, a child, two halves of a brass token, and a road north that would either deliver us to Tomas Reyes or deliver us all into the hands of men who burned ledgers and called it weather.

I curtsied to the inspector, and began to count.
