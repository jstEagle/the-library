## 23. Bargaining with Brigid

The cell smelled of wet stone and someone else's fear.

Brigid O'Donnell had cooked for forty mouths in a room the size of a chapel, had stood over boiling vats in August with sweat running into her eyes, and had never once fainted, wept, or asked anyone's pardon. She sat now on a plank bed bolted to the wall of the Crown Constabulary's holding cells beneath the harbor station, hands folded in her lap like a woman waiting for bread to prove, and she did none of those things either.

Her wrists were red where they'd bound her. She'd made them do it twice — the first constable had been gentle about it, apologetic even, and she'd told him plainly: *If you're going to arrest Bridget O'Donnell, you'll do it properly, not like you're handing her down from a cart.* He'd flushed and tightened the irons. Good. She wanted no softness muddying the record. Softness was how they got their hooks in.

The lamp outside the bars guttered. Somewhere above, gulls argued over the morning's fish offal. The tide was coming in; she could feel it in the stones, the way she'd felt weather in her knees for thirty years. High water around eleven. She found herself planning a menu around it — nothing. There was no kitchen here. There was no one to feed but herself, and that, she was discovering, was the loneliest arithmetic she had ever done.

Footsteps on the stair. Measured ones. She knew them already — he'd walked her down these stairs himself four hours ago, courteous as a bishop, his hand light at her elbow as though she were an aunt being shown to a pew.

Inspector Hale came into the lamplight carrying two things: a chair, which he set down across from her bars without ceremony, and a tin cup of tea, which he passed through them.

"Milk, no sugar," he said. "The matron tells me that's how you take it."

"The matron talks too much."

"She does." He sat. He was a lean man, gray at the temples, with the kind of face that gave away nothing because it had decided long ago to give away nothing. "I've brought the charges. I thought you'd want to hear them read properly rather than shouted at arraignment."

"Thoughtful."

"I try to be." He unfolded a paper. "Concealment of a person under suspicious circumstances. Forgery of a company dispensation chit — that's the medicine slip, Mrs. O'Donnell, and before you say a word, the pharmacist on Cordwainer Lane has described the hand that wrote it, and the hand is yours. And obstruction of an inquiry into treason against the Crown." He folded the paper again, unhurried. "Taken together, with a conviction, that is a sentence of seven years' penal servitude. Transportation is possible. You are fifty-three years old."

"Fifty-one."

"My error. Fifty-one."

"Seven years," Brigid said, "puts me at fifty-eight. If I live." She drank the tea. It was stewed and weak, prison tea, tea made by people who didn't care about tea, and she drank it anyway because refusing would look like fear. "My sister went out on a transport ship when we were girls. Australia. She lived. Wrote to us every Christmas until the ink ran out of her."

"And your family here?"

"I have none here."

"That's not what the cook's boy says. That's not what any of them say. They say the dormitory on Rope Street runs on you. That the children go to you with scraped knees and broken hearts and stolen apples, and you fix all three." He leaned forward slightly, elbows on knees. "Mrs. O'Donnell. Seven years is a long time to be missed."

Brigid set the cup down on the plank bed with a small, deliberate click.

"You've done your homework," she said. "Read my letters. Talked to my children — and they *are* my children, whatever the papers say, every last one of them bought and bound and handed to me like parcels, and I have fed every one of them since the day they arrived crying for mothers they'll never see again. So yes, Inspector. Ask me what you came to ask me, and then watch me not answer it, and we can both get on with our mornings."

Hale studied her for a moment. Something moved behind the stillness of his face — not sympathy exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man who has hunted enough people to know when he's caught something that will fight to the end of its line.

"A girl came to this house eight months ago," he said. "An infant. Left in the night. You knew within a week — the milk ledgers show double rations beginning the tenth of November, and you don't waste company milk, Mrs. O'Donnell, your accounts are the cleanest in this building's history. You helped conceal that child. You taught the Marchetti girl to feed her, hide her, keep her off the books. And when the child sickened in February, you forged a chit so she could be seen by a doctor without a name attached." He paused. "Where is the child now?"

Brigid looked at him.

She looked at him the way she looked at a pot about to boil over — assessing, unimpressed, already deciding what to do about it. And what she decided was nothing. What she decided was the hardest thing she had ever done in a life full of hard things, harder than the voyage out, harder than burying her husband, harder than the first time she'd held an indentured child who flinched from her touch: she decided to give this man nothing at all.

"I don't recall," she said.

"You don't recall."

"My memory's been going. Fifty-one years old, Inspector. Ask anyone."

"It was three months ago."

"Time moves quickly when you're busy. I was very busy. Forty mouths."

"The chit is in your handwriting."

"Then someone's gone to a deal of trouble to write like me. I'm flattered."

Hale exhaled through his nose — not quite a laugh, not quite patience running out. He rose, crossed to the bars, and stood there looking down at her, and when he spoke again his voice had changed. The courtesy was still in it, but underneath the courtesy was steel, and underneath the steel, if she wasn't mistaken, was something almost like regret.

"Mrs. O'Donnell. Let me tell you how this goes, so you can't say later that nobody warned you. Director Verrone has engaged counsel. Three of them. They are very good and very expensive, and they have one job, which is to make sure that when this trial ends, every ounce of blame that can be moved has been moved onto someone who cannot afford to defend herself. A cook. An indentured girl. A lame clerk. The company's lawyers will argue — and the court will be inclined to believe them — that the servants of Verrone Trading acted alone, out of some sentimental conspiracy, in aid of a traitor. Your name will be in every newspaper from here to the capital. *Cook Hid Traitor's Get.* They'll dig up your sister's transportation record. They'll find out your husband died in debt and suggest you took company money. Whether or not you ever see the inside of a transport ship, Mrs. O'Donnell, this will follow you for the rest of your life."

"Is that the part where I weep?" Brigid asked.

"It's the part where I ask you again where the child is."

"And it's the part where I don't recall."

He turned away. Stood at the barred window slit, where a blade of gray harbor light fell across his shoulder. For a long moment neither of them spoke, and Brigid used the silence the way she used all silences — working. She thought about Silas Marchetti, nineteen years old, all elbows and watchfulness, who had come to her kitchen at eleven with her father's drowning still wet on her and hadn't cried once, not once, in eight years, and who had taken a stranger's baby into her arms in the dark and said *yes* before her mind could talk her out of it. She thought about Fenwick, who limped through his ledgers like a man carrying furniture up stairs forever. She thought about the baby — Atlas, though she didn't know the name yet, wouldn't learn it for months — with her furious little fists and her hair like wet feathers.

*You hold what's given to you,* her mother had said, in a kitchen in County Cork smaller than this cell. *That's the whole of it. Everything else is commentary.*

"Here is what I can offer," Hale said, without turning around. "Cooperation. Name the child's location, name everyone involved, testify to what you know about Tomas Reyes, and I will speak for you at sentencing. Not a promise of immunity — I won't insult you with a promise I might not keep. But a voice. Mine carries weight. Seven years becomes eighteen months. Eighteen months becomes a fine, possibly. And the company's lawyers will have no use for you, because a cooperating witness is worth more to the Crown than to them."

Brigid rose. Her knees cracked — fifty-one years, honest work, honest cracks — and she came to the bars and stood close enough to see the pores in the inspector's skin, the tiredness he kept folded away behind his rank.

"Inspector Hale," she said quietly. "Do you have children?"

The question landed. She saw it land — a flicker in the jaw, a fractional stillness.

"No," he said.

"No. Well. I've had thirty-one. None of them born to me, all of them given into my keeping by a company that buys children the way it buys copra, by the hundredweight, and works the price out of them before they're grown. And in thirty-one years, do you know how many times I have been asked to trade one of them to save myself?" She smiled, and it was not a warm smile. "Never. Until today. So let me educate you, since you've been kind enough to bring me tea. What you're offering isn't mercy. It's a bill of sale. You want me to sell a child — a baby, an infant who never harmed a soul on this earth — to buy myself out of a punishment for having kept her alive. And you think seven years versus eighteen months is the arithmetic that should decide it."

"The arithmetic of survival decides most things, Mrs. O'Donnell."

"Aye," she said. "It does. That's the whole rotten engine of this city, isn't it. Survival deciding everything. Well, I've survived, Inspector. I've survived famine and passage and widowhood and thirty-one years of other people's griefs served up with breakfast. And I'll tell you what survival has taught me, since you're taking notes." She put her hand flat against the cold iron bar. "It's taught me that there's one thing dearer than staying alive, and it's being able to look yourself in the face at the end of it. I've known women who traded and lived long comfortable lives, and every one of them went gray-eyed and quiet, because somewhere inside them was a door they couldn't open anymore. I'll keep my door, thank you. All of it. Even locked."

Hale regarded her for a long time.

"You understand," he said finally, "that I can't let this stand indefinitely. If the child isn't found through cooperation, she'll be found through procedure. Warrants. Searches. The harbor district is not large, and my men are thorough, and every hour you sit silent makes the net wider — wider around you, wider around the Marchetti girl, wider around everyone who ever passed through your kitchen. Silence doesn't protect them, Mrs. O'Donnell. Silence just means I find them on my own schedule instead of yours."

"There it is," Brigid said softly. "There's the truth of you. You're not threatening me, Inspector. You're warning me. You've been warning me since you walked me down these stairs with your hand at my elbow like I was your maiden aunt." She tilted her head. "You don't much care for what you're doing. Interesting. I wonder who told you to do it."

Something shifted in his face — quick, quickly mastered, but she'd spent thirty-one years reading faces over soup kettles, faces of children trying to hide stolen plums and grown clerks trying to hide fear, and she caught it plain as a burn on a saucepan.

"I serve the Crown," he said.

"Aye. And the Crown serves whoever pays its taxes. And who pays the taxes in this port, Inspector? Who built the courthouse you'll try Reyes in? Whose name is cut into the lintel of the customs house in letters big enough to read from the water?" She stepped back from the bars and folded her hands again. "You go and ask yourself that question tonight, over your supper, in whatever tidy room they lodge you in. And when you've answered it honestly, you come back and tell me whether it's my silence that stinks in this building, or something else entire."

For a moment — just a moment — Hale looked at her the way a man looks at a chart when he suspects the coastline has been drawn wrong.

Then the shutter came down, polite and gray, and he collected his chair.

"Matron will bring your supper at six," he said. "If you reconsider—"

"I won't."

"—the offer stands." He paused at the foot of the stairs, half-turned. "The tea," he said. "Was it truly so bad?"

"Worse than bad," Brigid said. "Bring leaves next time. Loose, in a paper twist. And a proper pot, if you mean to keep interrogating me — I'll be here a while, by the look of it."

And — God forgive her — the corner of his mouth moved. Almost. Then he climbed the stairs and was gone, and his footsteps faded into the gull-cries and the slap of tide against the harbor wall, and Brigid O'Donnell sat back down on her plank bed in her wet-stone cell and allowed herself, at last and only then, one long shudder from crown to heel.

*Fifty-eight,* she thought. *If I live.*

She had meant it when she said it. Every word. But courage said aloud in daylight is one thing, and courage at two in the morning with the rats working the walls and the sea booming somewhere overhead is another thing entirely, and she knew — she knew with a cook's certainty, the certainty of a woman who could smell a stew going wrong before it burned — that the nights ahead were going to test her past anything the inspector had managed today. They would come gentler next time. Or crueler. They would bring lawyers with soft voices. They would bring the company's men, maybe, the ones who didn't bother with charges at all. And every hour she kept silent was an hour Silas was out there alone with a baby and no Brigid at her elbow, and that thought hurt worse than any sentence they could pass.

*Hold what's given to you,* her mother said, in the old voice, in the old kitchen. *That's the whole of it.*

"Aye, Mam," Brigid whispered to the empty cell. "Holding."

She lay back on the plank bed, folded her hands, and began — because a body must do something with its fear, and hers had always worked best through her hands — to plan a menu. Bread first. Bread for strength. Then the good brown stew, onions browned properly, none of this rushing—

Boots on the stair again. Fast ones, not Hale's measured tread. Two men, and a third whose step she knew — light, uneven, favoring the left side, the walk of a clerk who'd spent nine years on a lame leg among crates.

Fenwick. Here. In the cells.

They put him in the bar opposite hers, and through the gloom she saw the blood at his temple and the way he cradled his right hand, and her heart went down like a dropped kettle.

"Fenwick," she said. "What have they done to you?"

He lifted his head. His eyes found hers in the dark, and they were terrified — and behind the terror, unmistakable even now, was the fierce stubborn gleam of a man who had hidden something well and meant to go on hiding it.

"Brigid," he whispered. "Did you tell them anything?"

"Not a word. You?"

A ghost of a smile touched his split lip. "Not a word."

Above them, boots crossed the office floor, and a voice — smooth, polished, a voice that had signed away more lives than the sea itself — said something low to Inspector Hale that Brigid couldn't catch, except for the last of it, carried down the stairwell clear as a bell:

"Then see it done before the girl comes to her senses. Make the offer today."
