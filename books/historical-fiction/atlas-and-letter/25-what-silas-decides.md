## 25. What Silas Decides

The room above the boil house held the day's last light the way a cupped hand holds water — loosely, and only for a moment. Silas sat on the edge of the narrow bed with her boots off and her back to the wall, and watched the infant sleep in the middle of the mattress like a small island in a gray sea.

She had been sitting there for an hour. Perhaps longer. The light had changed; that was how she knew time had passed at all.

The baby — she had stopped thinking of her as *the baby* some days ago and had begun, dangerously, to think of her as *she*, as *hers* — slept with one fist curled under her chin and her breath coming in small even pulls, the rhythm of a child who did not know what it meant that three separate kinds of men were looking for her. Fever was gone. Brigid's forged chit had bought the medicine, and the medicine had done its work, and now the girl slept the heavy, boneless sleep of the recovered, and Silas sat awake beside her doing arithmetic.

It was not money she was counting. She had learned long ago that money was never the real sum. It was this: every road out of this room, and what each one cost, and who paid.

* * *

She laid them out the way Fenwick laid out columns, one beneath the other, each figure neat.

The first road was the simplest. She could walk away tonight. Take the ferry at dawn with her father's ring sold and a third-class ticket north, find work in a cannery or a laundry in some gray city where nobody knew the name Marchetti, and let the child be found by whoever found abandoned children. Someone kind, perhaps. Someone who would feed her and teach her letters and never tell her where she came from. That was the road Verrone had, in his polished way, offered to pave for her — report any contact from Reyes, and the company would buy out her indenture early, set her free, put coin in her pocket. Freedom. Eight years of her life traded back to her in exchange for one small betrayal she could perform without ever saying a word aloud.

That was the thing about it. She would not even have to lie. She would simply have to *say nothing*, which was the skill she had been perfecting since she was eleven years old. Silence was the trade she knew best. She had survived on it. Her whole indenture was built on it — head down, hands busy, ledger neat.

And the child would be gone by morning. Taken into custody as material evidence, or taken quietly by company men before anyone official ever knew she existed. Either way, she would vanish the way Tomas Reyes's letter had vanished in the stove — burned cleanly, completely, leaving nothing but the memory of warmth on Silas's palms.

Silas looked at the small fist curled under the small chin.

No. Not that road. But she made herself finish the column anyway, because her father had taught her that a sum you refuse to finish is a sum that will come back to drown you.

* * *

The second road was Brigid's road, Fenwick's road: stay, keep hiding the child, keep lying in small careful increments, wait for the storm to blow over. Storms blew over. Port cities forgot. Inspector Hale had questions, but questions were not proof, and proof was not conviction, and surely a man could hide one baby through one bad season.

Except that the season was not weather. She understood that now, had understood it since the fish market, since Fenwick's arm in a sling and the bruise going yellow along his jaw. Verrone was not waiting for the storm to pass. Verrone was *making* the storm, and storms made by men did not blow over; they widened until they took in everyone standing near you. Brigid had already forged one chit. Fenwick had already bled. Every week she kept the child hidden, someone she loved moved one step closer to the constabulary, or worse, to the quiet men Verrone kept the way other men kept dogs.

And there was the other thing, the thing she had been carrying since the lighthouse file lay open between her and Marisol in the lamplight: Ledger Nine. Arms under pearl manifests. A clerk's signature on documents signed by a man already dead. Charts doctored to point the treason at Tomas Reyes like a finger pointing away from the house that paid for the pointing. She knew now. Knowing was itself a weight, and weights did not set themselves down because you closed your eyes.

If she stayed silent — her oldest, safest art — then she was no longer merely surviving inside the company's lie. She was holding the door of it shut from the inside.

Her father had died in their water, hauling their crates of light off the sea floor, and they had charged her for the privilege. Eight years. And now they wanted her silence too, and silence, she was beginning to understand, was the one thing she had left that was actually hers.

* * *

The baby stirred, made a small sound like a question, and settled again when Silas's hand came to rest — almost without her deciding it — on the rise and fall of the blanket.

There was a third road, of course. The honest one, if you could call it that. Go to Hale. Put everything on his desk: the token, the coordinates, Marisol, the pages from the lighthouse cache. Let the Crown do what crowns were built for.

But she had sat across from Hale twice now, and she had watched him the way her father had taught her to watch the water — not for what it showed, for what it covered. Hale was not a fool and not, she thought, a bad man, but he was a man whose whole architecture was duty, and duty pointed wherever the crown above it pointed. Tomorrow that might be truth. Today it was Verrone's tea served in Verrone's offices, and a case that important men wanted closed quickly. If she handed him everything and he handed it upward, she would have surrendered the only leverage keeping Brigid out of chains and Tomas swinging from no rope at all — and she would have done it believing in a system whose first document about her had been a bill for her father's corpse.

No. Not Hale. Not yet. Maybe not alone.

Which left the fourth road. Her road. The one that started here, in this rented room paid for with her father's diving ring, and ran through the trial of Tomas Reyes like a rope bridge over a gorge.

She had promised. That was all. A promise made alone, in the dark, over the body of a burned letter, to a man who wasn't there to hear it: *she is yours now if you are brave.* Nobody had witnessed it. Nobody could hold her to it. By every law of the port, by every ledger she had ever kept, she owed the child nothing. She was nineteen, indentured, friendless in law, and the sensible sum of her life said: save yourself. You have spent eight years learning how.

But her father had taught her something else besides counting breaths. He had taught her what divers know — that the bottom does not care how badly you want air, and the only diver who dies is the one who panics and bolts for the surface too fast. You go down slow. You do the work at depth. You keep your head while the pressure tries to fold you, and you come up when the work is done, not before.

Every safe exit was the surface, bolted for too soon. And bolting now meant drowning everything she loved, just slowly, just out of sight, where she wouldn't have to watch.

* * *

She got up, quietly, and went to the window.

Below, the harbor lay black and breathing, masts swaying against the gaslight, the tide going out over the mudflats where she and Fenwick had spoken truths neither of them could afford. Somewhere out there, past the breakwater, Marisol was arranging passage, papers, the machinery of Tomas's defense. Somewhere behind her, in the city, Fenwick was copying testimony in his careful lame clerk's hand, and Brigid was sitting in a cell for refusing to say Silas's name, and Silas had not yet fully let herself feel what that meant — that Brigid, who owed her nothing either, who had discovered an illegal infant in her kitchen and responded by teaching a girl of nineteen how to hold a bottle, was sitting in the dark right now *for her.*

They had all chosen. That was the terrible, beautiful fact of it. Brigid had chosen. Fenwick had chosen, and bled for it. Marisol had crossed the sea. Even her father, dead nine years, had chosen — had kept Tomas Reyes's secrets in the diving camps, had earned a half-token and a trust that had arrived at her door one midnight wrapped in sacking. She was not alone at this crossroads. She was the last one to arrive at a decision the whole family had already made around her.

All that remained was for her to stop pretending she might choose otherwise.

She pressed her forehead briefly against the cold glass. She thought about Verrone's office — the polished wood, the offer delivered like a kindness, the way his eyes had gone to her hands. He knew more than he'd said. He probably knew everything. Which meant the trade he'd dangle next — the pages and the child for Brigid's freedom and her papers — was not a trade at all. It was a trap with two doors, both of which led down into the company's belly, and once she walked in with the evidence, there would be no evidence left, and no witnesses left, and eventually no Silas left, just another entry in a ledger somewhere, *account closed.*

Unless she made the exchange into a stage. Unless the moment they thought was the end of her became, instead, the moment she stood up in front of God and the Crown and the whole rotting apparatus and said what she knew, with the page sewn into a baby's blanket and Hale watching from his post with those patient hunter's eyes that had already, she was nearly certain, begun to see the seams in Verrone's story.

It might kill her. Perjury charges, obstruction, the company's long reach. She was not naive; she had seen what happened to people who inconvenienced Director Verrone, and Fenwick's ribs were the least of it. She might lose everything — her freedom, such as it was; her safety, which she had never really had; possibly her life.

And still. When she turned the sum all the way to its final line, there was only one figure left standing.

Tomas Reyes had saved her father's life, and her father had saved him back in the quiet way of poor men, and the debt had traveled nine years through drowning and fire and treason to arrive as an infant on a servants' step with an instruction: *forget this face.* She had burned the letter. She could not burn the promise. Some things, once read, are written somewhere deeper than paper.

* * *

Behind her, the baby woke — not crying, just waking, turning her head toward Silas with the solemn, unblinking attention of the very young, as though she had been listening to the whole ledger being tallied and wished to hear the result.

Silas came back to the bed and sat. She gathered the small warm weight against her shoulder, and the child sighed and gripped a fistful of her collar, and that was that. There are decisions the mind makes and decisions the body ratifies, and this one was ratified by a hand smaller than the palm that held it.

"All right," Silas said softly, to the dark, to the harbor, to her father wherever drowned men keep their accounts. "I'll go down slow. I'll do the work at depth."

She would answer Verrone's summons. She would let him believe the trade was working, let him arrange the meeting where he expected to collect the pages and the child like so much freight. And when the moment came — in his office, or wherever his caution placed it — she would not bargain. She would testify. To Hale, to the magistrate, to whatever court would hold still long enough to look at a true page against a forged one. She would spend her silence, her one possession, buying back a man's neck and a cook's freedom and a child's future, and whatever it cost her afterward, she would pay it standing up.

It was, she thought, the strangest feeling of her life: fear and certainty arriving together, in the same breath, occupying the same chest, and neither one canceling the other. Like the moment at the bottom of the sea when your lungs burn and your hands are steady anyway.

The baby had gone back to sleep against her shoulder, trusting, entirely and without appeal, and Silas found that this — not courage, not principle, just this small absolute trust — was the thing that made the decision unbreakable.

She laid the child down, banked the lamp low, and was reaching for her boots to walk back to Rope Street before the dormitory counted heads — when she saw it.

Across the black water, beyond the breakwater, the sky over the warehouse district had turned the color of a coal. A dull orange bloom, swelling silently, and beneath her feet, faint as a heartbeat, the harbor carried the first far tremor of bells.

Fire. In the direction of Rope Street.
