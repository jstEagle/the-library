## 26. Smoke Over the Warehouses

Fenwick had counted the ledgers of the Verrone Trading Company for nine years, and he had learned this: a company does not burn what it wishes to keep. It burns what it wishes to forget.

He understood it first as a change in the rhythm of the house. Rope Street had its weather — the groan of crates on the dock, Brigid's kettle, the small stampede of children's feet at supper — and beneath that weather, a deeper tide that men like Fenwick learned to read the way sailors read the sky. On Thursday the tide went wrong. The porters stopped joking. Mr. Calloway from the second floor walked the corridor twice without looking at anyone, which meant he was carrying an order he did not like. And Director Verrone's carriage came to the door at an hour when carriages did not come, its lacquer gleaming like something wet.

Fenwick sat at his desk with his lame leg stretched into the shadow beneath it, copying freight entries into the fair ledger, and he listened with the whole of his body. His hands moved. His pen did not falter. Nine years of being the clerk nobody watched had taught him that a man could hear everything so long as he appeared to be nothing.

"—from the top," Calloway was saying to the under-clerks in the passage. "Every storeroom, every dormitory trunk, every mattress if it comes to that. The Director believes a page has left this building that never should have left it."

"Which page?" someone asked, too softly for a name.

"You weren't listening. Every page."

Fenwick wrote *forty crates, pearl shell, mixed* in his neat gray hand and thought: *Ledger Nine.* He had not seen Ledger Nine since the night he and Tomas Reyes had copied it by shuttered lamp — the arms shipments dressed in pearl manifests, the ports that matched no tide table, the signature of a clerk three years dead still appearing month after month like a man refusing to stay buried. He had told himself the copies were gone, scattered, safe. He had told Silas the same. But Verrone believed otherwise, and Verrone's belief was a weather system all its own, and it was settling over Rope Street like the pressure before a storm.

The search began at noon.

They were polite about it, which was worse than roughness. A man from the Director's office with a list; two porters with crowbars for the floorboards; a boy to hold the bags. They went through the dormitory while the children stood in the yard under Brigid's eye, and Fenwick watched through the window as strangers lifted the little ones' mattresses, shook out their spare shirts, opened the tin where Pip kept his marbles and poured them back wrong. One of the porters found Silas's old corner empty — she had moved out weeks ago, and thank God for it — and shrugged.

Brigid stood in the yard with her arms folded and her face like a closed door, and Fenwick loved her for it. When the searcher asked her, not unkindly, whether anything in the house belonged to Tomas Reyes, she said, "Everything in this house belongs to the Company. That's the whole cruelty of it, isn't it," and turned away before he could decide whether he'd been insulted.

By evening they had found nothing, and that frightened Fenwick more than finding something would have. Because Verrone was not a man who accepted *nothing*. Verrone was a man who, denied a thief, would settle for an ash heap.

* * *

Fenwick did not sleep. He lay in his cot in the clerks' room above the counting floor and listened to the building breathe, and around two in the morning he heard the breathing change.

Boots below. Not the watchman's shuffle — a working pace, several men, moving freight. He rose, leg protesting, and brought the candle to the window that overlooked the rear yard, and there he saw the lanterns gathered at the doors of the records annexe — the long low brick shed where the old ledgers lived in their oiled cloth, where the duplicate books were shelved by year against the north wall. Four men. No list. No crowbar-wielding porters with a boy to hold bags. Just men with lanterns and kegs, and Mr. Calloway standing apart from them with his hat in his hands like a mourner.

Fenwick knew then, with the terrible clarity that comes to men who have spent their lives among numbers, exactly what sum was being worked. He had helped copy those pages once. He knew which duplicates mattered. And he knew — because Reyes had told him, laughing sadly, years ago — that in the whole run of Ledger Nine's duplicates, one book had been misfiled in 1908, shifted a shelf down between the wool contracts, forgotten by everyone but the two men who had copied it.

Reyes was beyond the walls. Silas was across the city with the child. Brigid slept. There was no one.

There was only a lame clerk who had spent nine years learning how to be invisible, and one brick shed, and perhaps ten minutes.

He went down the back stairs the way he always did, sideways, both hands on the rail, hating the noise of his own body. The yard was cold and smelled of tar and the sea beyond the seawall. He crossed in the blind angle behind the rain barrels, heart slamming, and reached the annexe's side door — the one whose lock he had quietly broken months ago and reported as rusted, because a man who keeps honest books likes to keep one dishonest door.

Inside, the dark smelled of paper and lamp oil, and the lamp oil smell was already wrong, too strong, fresh-poured.

"Calloway," Fenwick called softly, because some part of him still hoped for a world where he was mistaken.

No answer. From the far end came the scrape of a keg being positioned, and a voice, low: "Along the north wall. All of it. He wants ash, not salvage."

Fenwick stood in the dark among thirty years of the Company's memory and made his choice in less time than it takes to write a sentence.

He found the misfiled book where it had waited six years, wedged between WOOL CONTRACTS 1907–09 and a water-stained volume of harbor dues. Ledger Nine, duplicate, second fair copy. He pulled it, and the shelf beside it came loose with a shriek of old nails, and a lantern bloomed in the far doorway.

"There's someone—"

Fenwick ran. Not toward the door — toward the coal chute at the annexe's end, the narrow iron hatch the stokers used, which he could never have fit through on his best day and could not possibly fit through now. He ran anyway, because the other direction held men and lanterns and the beginning of shouting, and because under his arm he carried the only true account of what the House of Verrone had done.

Behind him, flame took the first keg with a sound like a held breath released — a soft *whumf*, almost gentle — and then the dark filled with orange light and the paper shelves began, one by one, to become torches.

The chute hatch was bolted. Of course it was bolted. He dropped the ledger, wrenched at the bolt with fingers gone stupid, and the heat arrived at his back like a living thing, and the smoke came down low and black and fast, faster than fire has any right to move when it has thirty years of dry paper to eat.

He got the bolt. He got the hatch up. He got his shoulders through and stuck there — hips jammed, lame leg useless behind him, fire climbing the shelves, the ledger clutched against his chest with both arms because letting go of it was the one thing his body refused to do even to save itself. Smoke filled his mouth. He thought, absurdly, of Brigid's kettle. He thought, less absurdly, *Silas will need this page. She can't do it without this page.* He thought: *so this is what I chose, and I'd choose it again,* and then he thought nothing at all for a moment, only the red roar and his own blood in his ears—

—and then hands on his ankles. Two pairs. Pulling. The hatch rim tore at him and he came loose all at once, sliding down the chute in a clatter of coal dust, out into the frozen yard, onto his back under a sky full of sparks, gasping like a landed fish.

The watchman knelt over him, and young Osei from the stables, both of them coughing, both staring at the annexe where the windows now glowed like furnace doors.

"What in God's name were you doing in there?" the watchman rasped.

Fenwick looked down at himself. The ledger was seared along one edge, its cover scorched, its pages hot against his chest. He had done the arithmetic of his life without meaning to: thirty years of records burning, four men setting the fire, one man stealing the proof, and the proof now singed and real and hidden inside his shirt before the watchman's eyes had adjusted from the flames.

"I was taking inventory," Fenwick said, and coughed until he wept.

* * *

By dawn the annexe was a black ribcage. The whole street came to look, because fire draws a crowd the way blood draws gulls, and the story assembled itself with the smoothness of a story that has been rehearsed: a faulty stove, an old building, paper everywhere, a tragedy of the trade. Mr. Calloway said it to the constables with his hat in his hands again, and his voice did not shake, and Fenwick, watching from the steps wrapped in a horse blanket, thought: *he rehearsed it in the carriage on the way here. Before the match was struck. Which means the order came down yesterday, which means Verrone decided last night, which means—*

Which meant the search had never been a search. It had been an audit of what remained to be burned. And if Verrone was burning trails, trails led somewhere, and the somewhere was people.

The constabulary took statements. Fenwick gave his: he had gone to retrieve a private notebook from the clerks' room, smelled smoke, raised the alarm. The inspector — not Hale, a local man with sleepy eyes — wrote it down without interest. Fire was easier than arson. Arson required someone to insist, and no one insisted.

Brigid found him on the steps. She did not ask what he had been doing. She looked at his blistered hands, the burn along his jaw where the hatch rim had caught him, the blanket, the ash in his hair, and she sat down beside him on the cold stone and put her arm around his shoulders as if he were one of her children, and for a long time neither of them said anything while the Company's past drifted overhead in flakes.

"You'll be wanting to send word to her," Brigid said at last, very quietly, watching the constables instead of him.

"Yes."

"Not from here. Not by the usual boy." She pressed something into his blistered hand — a coin, folded in paper, and a scrap of an address in her blunt schoolroom script. "Mrs. Adeyemi, on Cordwainer Row. Her nephew runs messages and owes me for thirty suppers. And Fenwick—" She paused, and for once the iron in her face showed the fire beneath it. "Whatever you pulled out of there, you get it off this street today. You hear me? Today."

"I hear you."

"They'll come back for it," she said. "Men who burn their own ledgers don't stop at ledgers."

She was right. He knew she was right before she finished saying it, because across the yard, apart from the crowd, stood a man in a good coat who was not watching the fire at all. He was watching Fenwick — had been, Fenwick realized, since the moment the blanket slipped. One of Verrone's from the second floor. The quiet kind. The kind that counted.

* * *

He hid the page within the hour.

Not the whole book — he had torn free only what he could carry, and in truth only one gathering of pages had survived the chute and the heat intact enough to matter: the entries for the autumn of 1910, arms under pearl manifests, the dead man's signature, four lines that contradicted everything the Crown had been shown. One gathering. He separated a single leaf from it — the worst, clearest, most damning leaf — and he thought about Mrs. Adeyemi's messenger boy, and he thought about the man in the good coat who was surely still somewhere behind him, and he made a clerk's decision.

A courier could be followed. A message could be intercepted. But there was one place in the world where Fenwick had spent nine years being invisible, one place no searcher had looked twice because it was too dull to look at: the dormitory wall behind his own cot, where the plaster had always bulged, where generations of bored indentured children had carved their initials, where a loose lath had rattled since before he arrived.

He went up the back stairs one more time, leg screaming, lungs still full of tar. The room smelled of yesterday's search — turned mattresses, opened tins, the violation of small lives. He pried the lath wide with his penknife, set the folded leaf into the gap between plaster and brick, and sealed it with the wad of old newspaper he'd kept for exactly such purposes since the night he and Reyes copied Ledger Nine, because Tomas had laughed at him for it — *"You keep a go-bag of newspaper, Fenwick? What do you think you are, a smuggler?"* — and Fenwick had said, "A bookkeeper," and both of them had believed that was the safer profession.

He pressed the lath home. Smoothed the paper over it. Sat down on the cot and let his shaking have him for one full minute, no longer, because there was work left and shaking was a luxury.

Then he took clean paper and wrote to Silas, in the plain gray hand that no one had ever bothered to read over his shoulder:

*The annexe is ash. I saved what matters — one leaf, and more words than they think survived. It is safe where children's names live. Do not come for it yet. Come for it when you come with Marisol and the means to use it. Burn this.*

And underneath, because the fire had burned something out of him along with thirty years of careful neutrality, he added:

*You asked me once why a man like me helps a man like Reyes. It is because I have kept the Company's accounts for nine years and my own for none. This is the first entry in mine. F.*

* * *

The messenger boy came at dusk and went at dusk, coin and letter both gone into the city's maze. Fenwick watched from the window until the small figure turned the corner by the chandlery, and only then allowed himself to believe, in the provisional way of bookkeepers, that the entry would post.

Below, the yard was busy with the ordinary theater of disaster: constables finishing their notebooks, porters hauling scorched salvage, Brigid marching children back inside out of the cold. Everything almost normal. Almost.

It was Osei from the stables who came pounding up the stairs, white-faced, still smelling of the fire he'd helped fight.

"Mister Fenwick — sir — the constables are at the gate again. Not the local ones. The Crown man. Hale." The boy swallowed. "And they're not asking about the fire, sir. They've got Brigid's medicine chit in a paper bag. The forged one. And they're asking for her by name."

Fenwick stood. Below, in the blue evening, Inspector Hale stood at the trading house gate with his courteous, patient face tipped up toward the windows, and beside him two constables, and between them, small and straight-backed and not looking at anyone, Brigid was already walking to the carriage with her chin up as if she were going to market.

They had not come about the fire at all. The fire had been the noise; this was the hand. And Fenwick understood, with the cold certainty of a balanced column, that the page in the wall behind his cot was now the only proof left in the city — and that he had just become the last man alive who knew where it lived.
