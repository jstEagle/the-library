## 27. The Diver's Road

The road north of the port was not a road so much as a habit — a track worn into the cliffside by generations of carts hauling pearl shell down to the harbor, abandoned now that the beds had played out. Silas walked it with the baby strapped against her chest in the shawl Brigid had knitted years ago for a child that never came, and she counted her breaths the way her father had taught her, one for the wave, one for the pause between waves.

Marisol Reyes walked ahead, boots planted like a woman who expected the ground to argue with her. She carried a canvas bag with a loaf of black bread, a knife, and the brass half-token wrapped in oilcloth, and she consulted the coordinates scratched beside the pier as though they were scripture: two marks east of the drowned chimney, where the creek met the sea at low tide.

"You walk like someone listening," Marisol said, without turning.

"My father taught me. On the bottom you can't see far. You learn to hear the current before it moves you."

Marisol glanced back then, and Silas saw again what she'd seen on the ferry landing — Tomas's eyes, set in a harder face. "He told me about your father once. The diver who kept his mouth shut when keeping it open would have been worth money." She said it without softness, but not without warmth either; warmth in Marisol seemed to arrive only after the facts had been verified. "I thought he was inventing you. A girl who'd take a stranger's baby off a doorstep because a note asked nicely."

"It wasn't the note."

"No?"

Silas shifted Atlas's weight — Atlas, though Marisol didn't know yet that Silas had begun calling her that in her head, for the maps that had damned them all. "It was the hand that wrote it."

Marisol grunted, which from her appeared to be a full paragraph of assent, and walked on.

They made eight miles the first day, which was seven more than Silas had expected. The coast unspooled in gray and green — gorse, slate, the occasional croft with a dog that announced them and an old man or woman who watched them pass and asked nothing. Word traveled differently out here, Silas realized. Not in ledgers and manifests but in looks, in doors left unlatched or firmly barred. Marisol read every door like a page.

Atlas slept through the afternoon and woke at dusk, hungry, and Silas fed her sitting on a milestone with her back to the wind, and when the baby pulled away and looked up at her with milk-drunk solemnity and then laughed — a sudden, absurd bark of laughter at nothing, at the wind maybe, at the sheer luck of being alive — both women went quiet.

"She doesn't know," Marisol said finally. "What any of this costs."

"Not yet."

"Good." Marisol looked out at the darkening water. "Let her have a while."

* * *

Marisol had crossed an ocean to find a child she'd never met, and somewhere on the second day, on a stretch of beach littered with the ribs of old boats, she decided Silas deserved to know why.

"Tomas wrote me last spring," she said. They were sharing bread and hard cheese while Atlas sat propped against Silas's knee, conducting her serious business with a limpet shell. "Just a few lines. He said if anything happened to him, there was a child, and that a girl at Verrone's would keep her safe, and that I should come if the girl ever stopped writing. He never mentioned any letters coming, of course. There were no arrangements. That was Tomas — he'd spend his last coin buying time for someone else and never think to tell anyone where he'd gone."

"He paid a bribe for me when I was eleven," Silas said. "To keep me out of the workhouse. I didn't know until later. He never told me either. Fenwick did."

"That sounds like him." Marisol turned a stone over with her boot, studied the crabs underneath, let them be. "Our mother died when I was six and he was sixteen, and he raised me on clerks' wages and borrowed books. He taught me navigation off salvaged charts. Do you know he can draw the whole coastline from memory? Every light, every shoal? That's what they say he sold — his own head, to a foreign navy." Her jaw tightened. "It's a lie so stupid it almost works, because he *does* know those waters better than any man alive. Liars always build on something true."

"And the child?" Silas asked quietly. "Do you know whose she is?"

Marisol was silent long enough that the tide filled it. "His letter didn't say. Only 'she is not mine, but her people are gone, and God has no other hands here.'" She shook her head. "He signed it with our mother's name. That was how I knew it was true. No forger would pick *that* name."

Atlas dropped her limpet shell, was outraged, and recovered upon receiving it back, bestowing on Marisol a smile of such unearned forgiveness that the older woman had to look hard at the sea for a moment.

"She laughs easily," Marisol said, voice roughly rebuilt.

"Brigid says babies laugh at whatever survives."

* * *

On the third day the rain found them.

It came in sideways off the water, a fine needling rain that got through wool and oilcloth alike, and by noon Atlas had started to fret — the small escalating complaint of a child getting cold — and Silas's heart climbed into her throat, remembering fever, remembering forged chits and Brigid's steady hands and the price of medicine. But Marisol, to her surprise, knew this country.

"There," she said, pointing to a fold in the cliffs where a stream cut through. "Fisher's bothy, or there was, ten years ago. My brother drew it on a chart once. He called it 'the place where the rain goes to think.'"

The bothy still stood — a dry-stone hut half-turfed into the hillside, door hanging on one hinge, smelling of old peat smoke and sheep. It was dry inside, mostly, and there was wood in the box, split and stacked, which meant somebody still kept it, and that somebody would find three sticks laid crosswise on the hearthstone when they left, which was how travelers paid in country without coins enough to spare.

Marisol got a fire going with a competence that silenced conversation. Silas stripped Atlas out of her wet wrappings, rubbed her warm and pink with the dry shawl, fed her, and held her near the flames until the fretting dissolved into drowsy hiccups and then into sleep.

"Your brother charted this whole coast," Silas said, watching the fire. "Every bothy. Every bed."

"And they used it to hang a treason charge on him." Marisol fed the fire another stick. "There's a joke in there somewhere. Neither of us is in a mood to laugh at it."

Silas was quiet awhile. Then: "When we find him. What then? A trial isn't a rescue. Even with the pages, even with everything — they'll try him with the crown's evidence first, and the crown's evidence was built by men who own the courthouse."

"I know."

"They could hang him before anyone reads Ledger Nine."

"I know that too." Marisol looked at her across the fire, and for a moment the hardness cracked and Silas saw the girl who'd lost a mother at six and been raised by the gentlest clerk in the company's employ. "But I've crossed an ocean on a letter and half a brass token, and you've hidden a child under the nose of Director Verrone himself for the better part of a season. Neither of us came this far to discuss odds."

Atlas stirred, sighed, and laughed in her sleep — one bright syllable, there and gone, like a match struck in a cave.

Both women stared at her.

"Well," Marisol said at last, gruffly. "That's settled, then."

* * *

They found the drowned chimney on the fourth day.

It rose out of a shallow cove like a broken tooth — all that remained of a pearling station burned or abandoned twenty years past, its lower courses swallowed by the tide, its chimney standing alone in the shallows with the sea combing through the empty windows. Around the curve of the cove, half-hidden by gorse and gull-cries, lay the camp: a row of ruined divers' huts, a slipway green with weed, a sorting shed with its roof fallen in like a collapsed lung.

Silas stopped dead on the path above the beach.

She knew this kind of place. Not this place — but places like it. Her father had worked camps like this, had described them in the flat voice he saved for the past: the drying racks, the shells heaped white as bones, the men who went down and the men who came up and the arithmetic between the two. She had been born within earshot of surf like this. Some part of her body recognized the geography before her mind did, and grief arrived without permission, sharp and salt.

"You all right?" Marisol asked.

"This is where my father's kind of people lived. Died, mostly." Silas breathed, counted, steadied. "The coordinates said two marks east of the chimney. East is the sheds."

They went down carefully, watching the footing, watching the tree line, watching each other's backs the way they'd learned to on the road — Marisol ahead, Silas behind with Atlas snug against her chest and her hand closed around the little knife in her pocket. The camp had that particular silence of places recently inhabited by nobody who wanted to be known: no fresh tracks in the weed, no gulls fighting over the sheds, but also — Silas noticed, and her pulse ticked up — a fish trap reset in the shallows, its stake cut clean within the month. And smoke-smell, faint and cold, threading the gorse.

Someone was here, or had been very lately.

Marisol signaled with two fingers and moved along the ruin line. Silas followed, breath counted — one for the wave, one for the pause. The second mark, when they found it, was scored into the lintel of the largest hut: two deep lines in the old stone, recent, deliberate. The door beneath it was closed, which no ruin's door ever is by itself.

Marisol put her mouth close to Silas's ear. "If it isn't him—"

"Then we run for the trees and you don't stop for me."

"I absolutely will stop for you, and that's exactly the sort of thinking that gets people killed. On my count. Three—"

The door opened.

A man stood in the frame, thin as a lath, sunken-eyed, beard gone to gray iron, one arm bound in a filthy sling — and Silas had never seen Tomas Reyes except in memory of an eleven-year-old's gratitude, blurred across a decade, but she knew him instantly, because he had his sister's exact eyes, and because he looked at the bundle on her chest before he looked at anything else in the world.

For a long moment nobody moved. The sea combed through the drowned windows. Somewhere overhead a gull laughed like a baby.

"You brought her," Tomas Reyes said, and his voice cracked on the last word like a boy's. His good hand came up, trembling, and hovered an inch from Atlas's head without touching, as if she were made of smoke. "You actually — Silas Marchetti. Your father's daughter. You brought her."

Then his legs folded under him, and it was Marisol and Silas together who caught the man who had saved them all before he hit the stones — and it was Atlas, jostled awake, who looked down at the weeping stranger with vast interest and greeted him with a shriek of pure delight that rang off the ruins like a bell.

Tomas laughed through the tears, holding onto them both. "She's strong-lunged," he managed. "Good. She'll need it. Listen — both of you, listen—" His face went suddenly grim, and he pulled them toward the doorway, glancing once at the empty tree line. "You weren't followed?"

"We weren't followed," Silas said.

"Then God is merciful," Tomas said, "because there's a company sloop anchored on the far side of the point, and it's been there since yesterday."
