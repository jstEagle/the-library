## 28. Tomas Reyes

The pearl camp had been dead for eleven years, and Tomas Reyes had made his peace with its ghosts.

He sat in what had once been the foreman's office, a room of salt-eaten planking and a window that no longer held glass, only the memory of it in the pale rectangle of light on the floor. Beyond the window, the sea did what the sea always did — worked at the shore, patient as a creditor. The diving shacks along the beach leaned like old men telling the same story over and over. Somewhere under that water lay the beds where Silas Marchetti's father had held his breath for coin, and where Tomas, younger then and stupid with ambition, had first learned what the Verrone Trading Company did with men who saw too much.

He heard them before he saw them. He had been listening for three days, ever since he'd found the second half of the token gone from beneath the pier — pried loose cleanly, nails left bent outward, someone careful. Someone who understood what half a token meant.

When the figures came up the shingle road through the grey morning, he counted them without meaning to: two women, one small bundle carried against a chest, one tall figure limping slightly on the wet stones. And behind them, further back, a man with the rolling gait of a sailor who had never quite learned to walk on land again.

Tomas stood. His knees cracked. Fifty-three years old, and every one of them announced themselves now.

The woman in front stopped twenty paces off. She was young — nineteen, maybe twenty — with her father's dark hair pulled back and her father's way of standing, weight even on both feet, hands quiet at her sides. He had not seen her since she was eleven years old and being led into the Verrone counting house with a number pinned to her collar. She had grown into a woman who looked like she was holding her breath.

She was still holding it now. He could see the effort of it, the discipline of it, and something in his chest turned over, because he knew exactly where she had learned that. Not from him. From the water. From the man who had taught her to count breaths the way other fathers taught daughters to count coins.

"Silas," he said.

"You're him." Her voice came out flat, controlled. "You're really him."

"I'm really him."

Her eyes went to the bundle at her chest, then back to his face, and he watched the fear move through her like weather — quick, total, gone. "You left her on my doorstep," she said. "In the middle of the night. With a note."

"I did."

"You could have been followed."

"I was followed." He almost smiled. "They lost me at the fish market. Old men are easy to underestimate."

* * *

They settled in the foreman's office because it was the only room with a roof that mostly held. Marisol built a fire in the rusted iron stove with the efficiency of a woman who had made camp in worse places, and Brigid — because of course it was Brigid, the cook's broad face he'd memorized from years of company ledgers listing her wages — took the baby and walked her gently along the wall, humming something low and tuneless that was less song than heartbeat.

Tomas watched all of this and understood that whatever he had set in motion on that midnight doorstep had grown roots he had never planned for. He had meant to hide a child. He had accidentally built a family.

"Tell me," Silas said. She sat across from him on an overturned crate, spine straight, hands folded. A ledger-keeper's posture. "All of it. I've been hunted for weeks because of you. Fenwick was beaten. Brigid—" her voice caught, just barely, "—Brigid is in a cell right now because of your note. So you'll tell me everything, and you'll start at the beginning."

"The beginning," Tomas said, "is a night in April, four years ago. Do you remember the *Cormorant*?"

"The freight ship. Pearl runs to the southern islands."

"Pearl runs." He shook his head slowly. "I was senior clerk on the harbor manifests by then. Twenty-two years with the company. I checked every crate that crossed Pier Three, weighed it, logged it, signed it. In April of '07, the *Cormorant* began running crates that were logged as shell but weighed wrong. Too heavy. Too evenly heavy — pearls don't pack even, they scatter in their beds, but these crates balanced like they were full of iron. I opened one."

The fire popped. Outside, a gull screamed once and gave up.

"Rifles," Tomas said. "Wrapped in oilcloth, packed in salt to keep the barrels from rusting, packed under a top layer of shell so any inspector who lifted the lid would see mother-of-pearl and close it again. Forty crates a month, going south to a buyer in the archipelago who paid Director Verrone in gold and asked no questions about where the guns came from or whose wars they fed. I stood there with my hand inside a rifle crate on Pier Three, and I understood that I was holding my own death warrant, because I had seen it, and the company does not permit seeing."

Silas hadn't moved. But her hands had come unfolded, and her knuckles were white where they gripped her own knee.

"Why didn't you go to the constabulary then?"

"Because of what happened to the last man who tried." He looked at the fire. "A junior clerk named Aldous Wren found the same discrepancy a year before me. He wrote to the Crown magistrate. His letter never arrived. Three weeks later he fell from the warehouse catwalk — an accident, they said, a man who had worked those catwalks for six years, misjudging a step. They paid his widow a month's wages and closed his file." He met her eyes. "That was when I learned the shape of the thing I was inside. You don't fight it head-on. Head-on, you're Wren, falling. You survive it the way your father survived the water — by breathing slow, watching everything, and waiting for the tide to turn."

"My father," Silas said quietly. "What did my father have to do with it?"

And there it was. The circle, closing. Tomas had known this moment would come, had rehearsed it on sleepless nights in hiding, and still his throat tightened around it.

"Your father dove for the company for nineteen years," he said. "You know that. What you may not know is that in all those years, he never once came up short. Never sold a single pearl off the books, though God knows the temptation, the debts, the hunger. Verrone trusted him absolutely — trusted him the way you trust a tool. And in the spring of '06, a year before the *Cormorant*, Verrone used that trust. He sent Emile Marchetti down to the southern beds alone — no tender boat, no witness — to retrieve a sealed chest from a marked position and bring it up without opening it. Your father was a diver, not a fool. He opened it."

"What was in it?"

"Ledgers," Tomas said. "Pages from Ledger Nine. Records Verrone needed moved out of the counting house before a Crown audit — records of payments, buyers, routes. Your father was the courier because a courier who can't read well and can't be traced is worth ten clerks. He brought the chest up, and instead of delivering it straight away, he sat on the tender boat with it in his lap for one hour, and he showed it to me."

"Why?" The word came out almost angry. "Why would he risk that?"

"Because we were friends," Tomas said simply. "Because thirty years earlier, when we were both young men on the southern boats, I pulled him out of a squall that should have drowned him, and he never forgot it, and I never let him forget it — that's the joke between us, the whole architecture of our friendship, built on one night of rope and rain. And because Emile Marchetti looked at those pages, and looked at me, and said: *This will hang somebody someday. Better it hangs honest.*" Tomas's voice roughened. "He put the chest back. He delivered it. And he kept my confidence, and I kept his, and for a year we watched the company together, two men pretending to be blind."

Silas was staring at him. Beside the stove, Marisol had gone very still, and Brigid had stopped walking, the baby drowsing against her shoulder.

"When the drowning debt came," Tomas went on, softer now, "when they took you at eleven and pinned the number to your collar — I paid the bribe that kept you out of the workhouse because I owed your father more than money can carry. And when I found the rifles in '07, I already knew what kind of man your father was. What kind of blood you came from. I didn't know then that I'd ever need to know it. But I filed it away. Men like me, we file things away."

* * *

The baby woke and fussed, and Brigid fed her from a flask of milk warmed at the stove, and the small domestic sounds filled the ruined office while Tomas gathered himself for the part he had never told anyone, the part that lived behind his ribs like a swallowed hook.

"The child," Silas said, when the silence had stretched long enough. "Whose child is she? Whose is she, Tomas?"

He closed his eyes.

"Her name was Amaya Ferro," he said. "She was a laundress at the company dormitory on Rope Street — you'd have passed her a hundred times in the yard. Two years ago she married a clerk named Paulo Ferro, a good boy, quiet, ambitious the way poor men are ambitious. Last winter, Paulo found the same discrepancy I found. Weighed crates on Pier Seven. He came to me — frightened, furious, wanting to go to the magistrate, wanting to be Wren." Tomas opened his eyes. "I told him no. I told him what happened to Wren. I told him to breathe slow and wait for the tide. And he tried, Silas. He tried. But he'd already written one letter, and the letter was intercepted, and on the fourteenth of January, the scaffolding outside his lodging house collapsed. Both of them. Paulo and Amaya. An accident. The company paid for the funeral — Verrone himself sent flowers."

The fire had burned down to embers. Nobody moved to feed it.

"The baby was in the lodging house when it came down," Tomas said. "A beam fell across the cradle corner and stopped — stopped, do you understand, wedged on a nail keg, and she lay under it for hours in the dust and the screaming until I dug her out with my own hands. Eleven months old. She didn't cry until I lifted her. Then she cried, and it was the finest sound I had heard in twenty years, because it meant the world had failed to kill her." His voice broke and he let it. "Verrone ordered the infant taken to the parish orphanage. Company wards die in that place, Silas. Slowly, quietly, of fevers and neglect — or quickly, if someone remembers whose daughter she is. I could not allow it. So at midnight I carried her through streets I've walked for thirty years, to the one door in this city where I knew — I *knew* — that the blood inside the house would hold truer than the fear."

"And the note," Silas whispered. "*Forget this face.*"

"Because a face remembered is a face that can be tortured out of you. Because I wanted you safe, and safe meant ignorant, and ignorant meant alive." He laughed, short and bitter. "It was cowardice dressed as wisdom. I asked you to forget her, and you — you, Emile's daughter — you looked at that child and refused to forget. You hid her in a pearl crate while Inspector Hale walked past close enough to touch. You spent your father's ring on a rented room. You let yourself be beaten by company thugs rather than give up a letter you'd already burned." He shook his head. "I chose the right door after all. Or perhaps I didn't choose anything. Perhaps your father chose it, thirty years ago, in a squall, when he grabbed my hand instead of letting go of the mast."

Marisol spoke for the first time. "The pages. The ones you copied from Ledger Nine. Where are they?"

"In a lighthouse," Tomas said. "North point, the old beacon the company decommissioned when they built the new light. There's a cache in the lamp room floor — oilskin packet, wax-sealed. Copies of forty-one manifests, the payment schedules, the buyer's letters, and my own sworn statement, witnessed and dated. Everything needed to hang Verrone — if it reaches a court that isn't bought." He looked at Silas, and then past her, at the sleeping child. "But documents alone won't do it. Verrone's forgers are good. He'll produce counter-evidence, forged charts, a treason case against me built brick by brick. Documents need a living witness who can authenticate them under oath. Someone who handled Ledger Nine itself."

"Fenwick," Silas said.

Tomas blinked. "How—"

"He helped you copy them. He told me on the mudflats. He's carried it alone for weeks, and he took a beating in the fish market rather than let them shake it out of him." Something fierce moved through her tired face. "Fenwick, Brigid, Marisol, the baby, me. That's your case, Tomas. Ordinary people. That's what you assembled without knowing it."

Tomas Reyes looked around the ruined room — at the lame clerk's absence that was a presence, at the cook rocking a dead woman's child, at his own sister standing guard at the door, at the diver's daughter with her father's steady hands — and felt the circle close so completely that he could hear it hum.

"Yes," he said. "Ordinary people. It's the only army I ever believed in."

Then Marisol, who had been watching the shingle road through the broken window frame, said quietly:

"We have company. Riders. Four of them, coming up from the south road — flying no colors, but riding like they're paid to."

Tomas was on his feet before she finished, old knees forgotten, reaching for the oilskin satchel he had kept packed for eleven days.

"The lighthouse," he said. "We ride for the lighthouse tonight — and pray we reach it before Verrone remembers it exists."
