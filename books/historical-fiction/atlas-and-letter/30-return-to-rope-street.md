## 30. Return to Rope Street

The city came back to us in pieces, the way a drowned thing rises — first the masts, then the roofs, then the whole gray sprawl of it breathing smoke into a pale dawn. I had spent eleven days on the diver's road telling myself that Rope Street would be exactly as we'd left it: Brigid's kettle singing, Fenwick's pen scratching, the dormitory children squabbling over the morning porridge. Distance does that. It lets you build a little house of *perhaps* and live in it.

Marisol stood beside me at the rail of the coastal packet, the baby wrapped against her chest under a fishmonger's oilskin. Atlas had learned a new sound on the road — a wet, delighted gasp she made at gulls, at lanterns, at Marisol's earrings. Every time she made it my heart did something complicated and useless. A child who laughed at gulls did not yet know what a constable was. I meant to keep it that way.

"We go in separately," Marisol said for the fourth time. She had a way of repeating instructions until they sounded like scripture. "You take the fish lane. I take the hill road. We meet at the boil house at noon if nothing's wrong."

"And if something is wrong?"

She looked at me the way she looked at weather. "Then we meet at the lighthouse, and we do not come back for anything."

I nodded, and lied with the nod. Because I already knew I would come back. For Fenwick, for Brigid, for the crooked little family stitched together inside those brick walls — I would come back until they stopped me. That was the arithmetic of love, and no one had ever taught me a different sum.

* * *

The fish lane smelled of ice and brine and the particular rot of a port in autumn. I kept my head down and my hands busy, an old habit fitting itself around me like a coat. A woman with a basket and a stoop could pass through half this city without being seen; I had been that woman since I was eleven years old.

But Rope Street was wrong before I even reached it. I knew it the way my father knew a bad bottom before his lead line touched it — by the surface of things. There were too few carts. Too many men standing in doorways with their hands folded and their eyes working. Two constables at the mouth of the street, talking quietly, wearing the particular patience of men on a watch that has already caught something.

I bought a herring I didn't want from a stall near the corner and asked, with the idle air of a wife sent out by a hungry husband, why so many blue coats on a Tuesday.

The fishwife leaned across her crushed ice. "They took the lame clerk last night," she said. "And the cook woman from Verrone's. Carried them out like parcels. Whole street heard the clerk shouting he wanted his ledgers brought along — as if they'd oblige him." She laughed, then dropped her voice. "And the big house on the corner's got its doors shut since dawn. Company business, someone said. Someone else said forgery. Someone else said treason." She wrapped my herring as though it were a funeral flower. "Bad fish year," she added vaguely, and turned to her next customer.

I walked away at a widow's pace while my blood rang in my ears like a struck hull.

Fenwick. Brigid. Both of them. The net hadn't tightened — it had *closed*, and I had been out on the coast chasing truth while it came down over the two people least able to run from it. Fenwick, who couldn't outrun a slow tide. Brigid, whose hands had fed my daughter when I couldn't.

I made myself walk past Rope Street. Past the trading house with its shutters drawn like lowered eyelids. Past the servants' door where a bundle had once been left in the dark, and where now a chalk mark sat on the lintel — small, deliberate, the kind of mark a man makes to know a door again. Hale's men had marked the house. Marked it the way you'd mark a crate you intended to open later, or a grave you intended to dig.

At the end of the lane I let myself stop, herring basket in hand, and look back once.

That was when I saw Fenwick.

Not Fenwick himself — his books. Two constables were carrying his ledgers out through the side gate, stacking them in a handcart with the reverent care of men handling evidence. Ledger after ledger, years of neat columns, all the quiet honest work of a crooked-backed man who had chosen one dishonest act in his life and done it out of love. They took the books and left the man, and somehow that was worse. It told me what they wanted. Not Fenwick. Not even Brigid.

They wanted pages. And names.

* * *

Noon found Marisol and me wedged in the stinking lee of the boil house, between a mountain of empty barrels and a wall warm from the furnaces within. She nursed Atlas in the shadows while I told her what the fishwife had said, and what I'd seen, and what the chalk mark meant, though she understood marks better than I did and finished my sentences more grimly than I could.

"Both of them," she said softly, when I was done. She looked down at the baby, and something moved across her face — grief arriving ahead of schedule, the way it does in families that have buried too much. "My brother sent his child to you because he thought your house was safe."

"It *was* safe." My voice cracked on the second word. "It was safe because Brigid made it safe. Because Fenwick forged a chit and took a beating in a fish market and never once asked me what I was carrying around inside all this trouble. And now they're sitting in some cell answering questions with my answers, and I wasn't there."

"Being there wouldn't have saved them. It would only have filled a third cell."

"Then three cells would be honest."

Marisol put her hand over mine. Her fingers were rougher than they looked; she'd been diving too, in her girlhood, off her brother's boat. "Listen to me, Silas Marchetti. Tomas told me about you before he ever told me about the baby. He said: *there is a girl at Verrone's who counts breaths.* He said you were raised under water and it taught you not to panic." She squeezed. "So don't panic. Think like your father. When the current takes you, you don't fight it blind. You find the bottom. You follow the anchor line."

"The anchor line," I repeated.

"What holds this whole mess together? What do they want?"

"Pages," I said slowly. "Ledger Nine. The copies Reyes made. The testimony that says Verrone shipped guns under pearl freight and framed a loyal man to bury it." I stared at the barrels, at nothing. "They've taken everyone who's *seen* those pages. Brigid never saw them. Fenwick copied them — that's why he's taken. They're cutting the legs out from under the truth before anyone can bring it to a courtroom."

"A courtroom," Marisol said, and went very still.

"Treason trials are held in courtrooms," I said. "Public ones. With judges. With rules." I felt the thought assemble itself the way a dive assembles — plan, breath, descent. "Reyes isn't going to simply disappear, Marisol. Verrone needs him tried and convicted, publicly, or the frame doesn't hold. Which means there will be a trial. Which means there will be a place where the truth is *allowed in the room* — if it can survive long enough to get there."

"Then we get it there."

"With Fenwick's testimony. He's the witness who can authenticate the copies — his hand copied them. Without him, the pages are paper. So we don't just need to save him." I swallowed. "We need to save him *into* that courtroom."

Marisol was quiet a moment. Above us, gulls screamed at the boil-house chimneys. Then she said, in a voice like her brother's, dry and steady: "You understand what you're proposing. We're two women and a baby, and they're the crown and the company."

"I understand the odds," I said. "I've read enough ledgers to know how numbers lie."

* * *

We couldn't reach them. That was the plain cruelty of the afternoon. I tried every door I knew — the tradesman's entrance at the company annex, where a boy I'd shared bread with for eight years looked through me as if I were already dead; the records office, where a new clerk with clean cuffs told me all personnel matters were suspended pending inquiry; even the constabulary steps, where I stood in a queue of wives and mothers with the baby hidden under my shawl and heard a sergeant say, for the hundredth time that day, *no visitors till arraignment, move along.*

Arraignment. The word followed me out into the street. Arraignment meant charges. Charges meant the machinery had started turning and would not stop for mercy, because the crown's machinery had never once stopped for mercy in all the years I'd watched it grind along this waterfront.

By dusk I had learned three things, each bought with a different coin of fear.

First: Fenwick and Brigid would be arraigned within the week, held on conspiracy — harboring a fugitive's effects, falsifying documents. Small charges, tidy charges. The kind that keep a person locked away and pliable until a bigger trial needs them.

Second: Director Verrone himself had signed the complaint. His name, in his own hand, at the bottom of the charge sheet, which the records clerk showed me upside down through the grille before snatching it back. Verrone did not send underlings to do this killing. He wanted his signature on it.

Third — and this was the coin that hurt most — Inspector Hale had conducted the arrests personally. Quietly, the fishwife's cousin said, who cleaned at the constabulary. No rough stuff. Tea served first. He'd sat in Brigid's kitchen and drunk her tea and thanked her for it, and then he'd taken her away gentle as Sunday.

I sat on the harbor wall after full dark with Atlas asleep against my chest and let the despair come, because Marisol was right that panic was useless, but she had never said despair was. Despair had its own honesty. I sat in it and let it tell me the truth: that I had found the coordinates, crossed the coast, climbed down into an abandoned pearl camp and put my hand in Tomas Reyes's living hand — and come home to find the price collected anyway. That courage was a currency and someone had always paid before me. That my father had died in debt to this company, and I had inherited the debt, and now the people who loved me were paying down the balance one arrest at a time, and the ledger still was not closed.

Atlas stirred and sighed against my collarbone, and her breath — that small, even, counting-breath my father had taught me to hear — pulled me up out of the dark the way a rope pulls a diver off the bottom.

Because that was the thing about being on the floor. There was nowhere further down. Everything after this was ascent.

I thought about Hale drinking tea in Brigid's kitchen. About a man whose courtesy was a kind of armor, who had noticed my calloused hands knew ledgers better than crates, who had let me go — but *watched*. A hunter, yes. But hunters, my father used to say, know the ground better than the hunted do. Know the tracks. Know what's real and what's been dragged through the brush to fake a trail.

Hale had examined the evidence against Tomas Reyes. Hale had read the charts, the manifests, the sworn statements. Hale was a careful man in a case built by careless liars, because Verrone was powerful, not clever — powerful men rarely need to be both, until the day they meet a man who is merely careful.

"He sees the seams," I said aloud to the harbor dark. "He just hasn't decided what seeing them costs him."

Marisol's boots came soft on the cobbles behind me. She sat, handed me half a loaf wrapped in newspaper, and waited, because she had learned on the road that I sometimes needed to arrive at sentences in my own time.

"There's going to be a trial," I said. "For Reyes. Verrone needs it too much for there not to be. And when it comes—" I tore the bread, gave her half of the half, "—Brigid and Fenwick will be sitting in a cell, useful as leverage, alive because dead witnesses convince nobody. Which means we have days, not weeks. Days to turn Fenwick from a hostage into a witness. Days to make sure that whatever Hale has begun to suspect, he finishes suspecting *out loud*, in front of a judge."

"And how," Marisol asked, "do two women and a baby do that?"

I looked down at the sleeping weight against my chest — at the blanket Brigid had hemmed, at the small fist curled around the fabric the way I once curled around my father's finger. Somewhere under this city, in a cell, Brigid was keeping silent for us. Somewhere Fenwick was probably apologizing to his captors for the inconvenience. They had paid, and were paying, and the only tribute I could offer them now was the one thing they had both taught me without ever saying it:

You protect your people. However it must be done. Whatever it costs.

"We start," I said, "by getting inside that courthouse."

Far down the quay, a bell tolled the changing of the harbor watch, and somewhere beyond it — though I could not yet see how — the machinery that had swallowed my family began, unknowingly, to grind toward the light.
