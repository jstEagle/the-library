## 31. The Trial of Tomas Reyes

The courthouse on King's Square had been built to make people feel small, and it did its work well.

Inspector Elias Hale stood at the rear of the central chamber at a quarter past eight in the morning, two hours before the proceedings began, and watched the room fill the way he had watched harbors fill in his years on the coast watch — with an eye for what floated in on which tide. The galleries filled first with clerks and stevedores who had abandoned their shifts for the spectacle; then the merchants' boxes, silk and wool and the smell of pomade; then the press men, ink-stained, jostling for the rail. Somewhere among all those bodies sat the girl. He would find her when he needed to. He already knew where she liked to sit.

The chamber smelled of floor wax and cold stone and, beneath both, the sea — the whole city smelled of the sea, as if the harbor had soaked up through the foundations over a century and never dried. Hale had chosen this posting once and had never quite decided whether he regretted it.

Six constables lined the wall beneath the judge's bench. Hale had selected them himself, one by one, from the roster: men without debts, without mistresses in the Verrone warehouses, without cousins on the company payroll. It had been harder than it should have been. That fact he kept folded away in the same inner pocket where he kept everything else that troubled him — and the pocket was getting full.

At nine o'clock exactly, the doors at the far end opened, and Tomas Reyes was brought in.

Hale had interviewed a hundred accused men and knew the taxonomy of their entrances: the shamblers, the strutters, the ones who had to be carried because their legs had given out overnight. Reyes did none of these. He walked the way old clerks walk, slightly bent, hands loose at his sides, and he looked about the chamber once — slowly, like a man checking a ledger for errors — before fixing his gaze on a point just above the magistrate's head and keeping it there. His hair had gone fully white since his arrest. He was fifty-three. He looked older, but not broken. Hale noted the distinction and did not enjoy it.

*Accused of treason*, the charge sheet said. *Selling port charts and coastal defenses to a foreign navy.* The evidence, Hale knew, sat locked in a cabinet not ten feet from where he stood: six charts, beautifully drawn, annotated in three languages, each bearing markings of gun emplacements and channel depths that no civilian should have possessed. Each bearing, in the corner, the Verrone Trading Company's watermark.

The trial of the decade, the papers were calling it. Hale thought of it, privately, as the trial where every seam would be on display for anyone patient enough to look at the stitching.

He looked at the stitching now. And then, because he could not help it, he let his eyes travel up to the third row of the public gallery, where a young woman in a gray dress sat with her hands folded in her lap like a woman at church, and a bundle wrapped in a shawl at her breast that she held so naturally, so unremarkably, that no one else in the chamber gave it a second glance.

Silas Marchetti. Nineteen years old. Calloused hands that knew ledgers better than crates.

Hale turned back to the front and did not look at her again for a long time.

* * *

From the gallery, Silas counted the exits the way her father had taught her to count breaths.

Two main doors. One judge's door behind the bench. One small door by the witness box, presumably for the prisoner's passage. Four tall windows, too high to reach, glazed with diamond panes that cut the morning light into pieces and scattered them across the floor like spilled coin. She catalogued all of it and understood none of it would matter, because there was nowhere to run to; there was only here, and the next hour, and the next.

Atlas slept against her chest under the shawl, a warm weight rising and falling, rising and falling. Brigid had fed her well before dawn and dosed her with a drop of the syrup that kept a baby drowsy and quiet, and Silas hated the syrup and was grateful for it in the same breath. *You'll sit still, little one,* she had whispered into the downy crown of her head that morning. *You'll sit still while I do the bravest thing anyone has ever done, which is nothing at all, for as long as I can bear it.*

Fenwick sat beside her, his bad leg stretched into the aisle where no usher had yet had the heart or the diligence to correct him. He had dressed in his only good coat, brushed until it shone, and he held his walking stick across his knees like a rifle. He had not spoken since they entered. His eyes moved constantly — to the dock, to the prosecution's table, to Director Verrone, who occupied the front of the merchants' box like a man attending the christening of a ship.

And there was Brigid, called as a witness for the Crown, seated in the reserved rows below the bar, wearing her Sunday dress and an expression that could have curdled milk at forty paces.

Silas made herself study the front of the chamber instead, because dread left untended grows teeth, and hers had been growing teeth for three weeks.

Tomas Reyes sat in the dock between two constables. Up close he was smaller than her memory of him — memory inflates its saviors — but his hands were the same, broad-knuckled clerk's hands with ink stains that no soap would ever fully lift. Those hands had signed the note that had changed her life. *She is not mine, but she is yours now if you are brave.* She had burned those words within the hour of receiving them, and here they were anyway, present in every line of the proceedings, invisible and enormous.

The crown prosecutor rose. His name was Aldous Crane, and he had the voice of a man who had been paid by the syllable his entire career.

"My lord," Crane said, "the Crown will demonstrate that the accused, Tomas Reyes, a clerk of nineteen years' service to the Verrone Trading Company, did, between the months of March and September of this year, prepare and convey to agents of a foreign power certain documents of the gravest consequence to the defense of this realm." He paused, letting the words settle like silt. "The Crown will produce those documents."

A constable unlocked the cabinet. A second constable carried the charts to the evidence table with the reverence of an acolyte handling relics. When they were unfolded and pinned to the great display board, a murmur ran through the gallery like wind through wheat.

Silas felt Fenwick go rigid beside her.

She looked at the charts. She forced herself to look at them properly, the way her father had taught her to read water — not the surface but the shadows beneath it. They were beautiful. That was the first thing. Whoever had drawn them had a surveyor's hand and a cartographer's patience: soundings in neat columns, channel markers, the new breakwater rendered in fine hatched line. And in red ink, precise and damning, the positions of guns that did not exist, marked at points along the harbor where any foreign captain might dream of landing men.

In the lower corner of each chart, half-hidden by the fold, sat the Verrone watermark — a pearl in an open oyster shell.

*They are lies,* she told herself. *They are beautiful lies, and everyone in this room is about to admire them.*

But her body had not received the instruction. Her heart hammered against her ribs, and against Atlas's small back, and the baby stirred, and Silas bent as if to adjust her shawl and pressed her lips to the warmth of her and whispered nothing, absolutely nothing, only breathed.

* * *

Director Augusto Verrone watched the charts being pinned up and permitted himself the smallest satisfaction, the kind a man allows himself in church or in court — a fraction of a degree of easing in the shoulders, no more.

It had gone precisely as designed. He had known it would, because he had overseen the design himself, and Verrone designs did not fail. Six months of work: a surveyor hired quietly in the spring, charts drawn from genuine company surveys, the red annotations added by a hand that no longer existed to be questioned — poor Wrenn, drowned so conveniently off the mole last summer, whose handwriting had graced a thousand manifests and could grace a thousand more. The treason of Tomas Reyes was, in a sense, real. Someone had committed it. The someone simply happened to be dead, and the someone's pen happened to belong to Augusto Verrone.

Reyes had seen the arms shipments. That was the intolerable fact at the bottom of everything, the stone in the boot. Guns under pearl manifests, forty crates of rifles logged as mother-of-pearl rough, bound for a client across the strait whose money spent as sweetly as anyone's. Reyes had copied pages. Reyes had to be buried under something so heavy that no one would ever dig down to the true cargo. Treason was suitably heavy.

Verrone glanced toward Inspector Hale, standing sentry at the rear of the chamber, and found Hale looking not at the charts, nor at the accused, but at him. Their eyes met for perhaps a second. Hale inclined his head, courteous as ever, and returned his attention forward.

Verrone's satisfaction dimmed by another fraction of a degree.

*After the verdict,* he reminded himself. Hale was a complication for after the verdict. Everything was a complication for after the verdict. In four days — five at the outside — Tomas Reyes would hang, the missing ledgers would be ash in a warehouse stove, and the girl Marchetti, who was cleverer than was comfortable but indentured and therefore owned, would be managed. There were ways to manage indentured girls. There always had been.

He smoothed his gloves and settled in to watch the Crown's machinery work.

* * *

The morning passed in formality, and the formality was its own kind of terror.

Crane read the charges in full. The registrar swore the jury — twelve men, ten merchants or retired merchants, a customs official, a shipwright. Silas watched their faces as the charts were explained, chart by chart, annotation by annotation, and saw what she expected to see: twelve men looking at beautiful work and believing it, because beauty was the currency they traded in, because none of them had ever hauled a crate or mended a net or wondered what became of a clerk named Wrenn.

"The accused's hand is known to this court," Crane said. "Samples have been entered into evidence." A clerk's ledger page was passed forward, and Silas saw Tomas's writing marching across it in tidy columns, and her chest tightened, because it *was* his hand, unmistakably — and yet the annotations on the charts were not quite it. She had studied that handwriting for weeks. She knew the way he crossed his sevens, the slight forward lean of his letters when he wrote quickly, the peculiar flat-topped *a*. The red annotations leaned further. The *a*s were rounder. It was his hand the way a portrait is a face — a likeness, not the man.

*Wrenn's hand imitating Reyes's hand,* Fenwick had told her on the mudflats, mud to his waist, voice shaking. *Reyes taught Wrenn lettering himself, years ago. Everyone said they wrote alike.*

Twelve merchants would not see the difference. But someone might. Someone standing at the back of the room with a hunter's patience and a pocketful of doubts.

The first witnesses were called after the recess: a harbormaster's assistant who had seen Reyes near the pier archives at odd hours; an agent of the Crown's intelligence service who spoke of intercepted correspondence in generalities that made the gallery lean forward and learn nothing; a handwriting expert from the capital who pronounced the annotations "consistent in character" with the accused's samples, and whose fees, Silas noted, were listed on the court schedule as paid through the office of the Crown Prosecutor — an office whose recent banquet, if Brigid's market gossip was to be believed, had been catered courtesy of the Verrone Trading Company.

Then, as the afternoon light slanted gold through the high windows, Crane said the words Silas had been bracing against all day:

"The Crown calls Mrs. Brigid Doyle."

* * *

Brigid rose from her seat below the bar like a mountain deciding to stand.

Silas's hands closed on the shawl, on the sleeping warmth beneath it, hard enough that Atlas stirred and sighed and settled again. *Don't look at me. Don't look at me. Don't—*

Brigid did not look at her. She walked to the stand with her chin level, took the oath in a voice roughened by twenty years of kitchen steam, and answered Crane's preliminary questions — name, residence, position as cook at the Verrone house on Rope Street — in monosyllables worn smooth as beach stones.

"You were employed at the trading house during the period of the accused's alleged offenses."

"I was."

"Did you observe the accused behaving strangely? Meetings at unusual hours? Correspondence he sought to conceal?"

Brigid considered him. The silence stretched long enough that the registrar shifted, and somewhere in the gallery a press man's pencil scratched eagerly at nothing.

"I cook," Brigid said at last. "I don't spy. If a man takes his supper at nine instead of seven, that's a matter for his stomach, not the Crown."

A ripple of laughter went through the public gallery and was suppressed. Crane's jaw tightened by a professional millimeter.

"Mrs. Doyle. Did the accused, at any time, confide in you?"

"He was not a confiding man. He ate his cabbage without complaint. That's the whole of what I know of Tomas Reyes, and I'd say it under any oath you like."

"And yet," Crane said, and now his voice dropped, softened, became almost gentle — the voice of a man offering a rope to a drowning woman, "you are aware that inquiries have been made regarding certain irregularities at the Rope Street premises. A forged dispensary chit, Mrs. Doyle. Medicine obtained under false papers. You were questioned in this matter. You declined to answer. I ask you again, before this court, where a refusal carries consequences: who was the chit for?"

The chamber went very quiet. Silas stopped breathing. Beside her, Fenwick's knuckles whitened on his stick.

Brigid looked at Crane for a long moment. Then she looked past him — up, deliberately, to the place in the gallery where a gray dress sat holding a shawl — and her eyes stayed there for one full heartbeat, steady and fierce as a lit lamp in a window, before returning to the prosecutor.

"I don't recall any chit," she said.

"Mrs. Doyle, the document exists. Your hand is on it."

"Then someone's gone to a deal of trouble to write like me," said Brigid, "and I'd count that a mercy, as my hand's not much to look at."

The laughter came again, louder, and this time the magistrate rapped for order, and Crane abandoned the line with the expression of a fisherman watching a large fish take his hook, run with it, and snap the line clean. Silas let her breath out in increments, counting them the way her father had taught her, and understood that she had just witnessed something she would remember for the rest of her life: loyalty performing itself, loudly, as contempt.

* * *

Hale watched Brigid Doyle step down — unhumbled, unbowed, charged in effigy with contempt that the magistrate, mercifully or prudently, declined to enter — and added a name to the list in his head.

The list had started as a duty roster of things to verify. It had become, without his quite noticing the moment of transition, something else. On it were written: *Wrenn, dead eleven months, charts annotated after his death per tide-tables; manifests signed in a dead man's hand; expert fees routed through Crane's office; Verrone banquet, catered; chit, forged, purpose unclear, protectress silent.* And at the bottom, underlined twice, the item he kept coming back to: *Marchetti.*

Because here was what troubled Hale, what had troubled him since the search of the trading house, since the interview in the narrow office with dust on her sleeve: everything in this case fit together too well, like a story told by someone who had rehearsed it, and the only people behaving like guilty people were behaving guilty of something other than treason. The cook protected someone at cost to herself. The lame clerk flinched every time the word *manifest* was spoken. And the girl in the third row of the gallery — the girl with ledgers in her hands and a bundle at her breast that she guarded with a ferocity she thought was hidden — the girl had come to the trial of a man she had no visible connection to, sat in plain view despite every instinct of invisibility he had catalogued in her, and watched the charts with the expression of a woman reading accounts she knew were cooked.

Hale had spent twenty-two years learning the difference between a liar and a person carrying a truth that will destroy them if it drops. Liars watched the jury. This girl watched the evidence.

"Your Excellencies will hear testimony tomorrow as to the manner of transmission of these documents," Crane was saying, gathering his papers with the confidence of a man who believed the trial already won. "The Crown calls its final witness of the day, on the matter of the accused's movements: Mr. Ambrose Quill, chief clerk of accounts, Verrone Trading Company."

A small, damp-palmed man took the stand. Hale had interviewed Quill once, briefly, months ago, and had come away with the impression of a man reciting answers he had memorized in front of a mirror.

Quill testified to Reyes's late hours, his access to the archive, his unexplained absences. He testified smoothly. Too smoothly. And then, near the end, unprompted, he added the sentence that made Hale's list grow one line longer:

"—and of course, Mr. Reyes was not the only one with access to Ledger Nine, though the others' involvement is, I'm sure, a matter beyond this court's concern."

Beyond the court's concern. Offered freely, by the company's own chief clerk, into a treason trial's record: the number of a ledger that appeared nowhere in the charges, nowhere in the exhibits, nowhere in the Crown's case at all.

Hale looked at the gallery. The girl in gray had gone white to the lips. The clerk beside her was staring at Quill as if a corpse had stood up in court and begun to speak. And at the front of the merchants' box, Director Verrone had gone very still — still in the way of a man watching a crack appear in a wall he had sworn was load-bearing.

The magistrate adjourned for the day. The chamber emptied in a rustle of coats and speculation, and Hale remained at his post by the door as the crowd funneled past him, watching the girl in gray rise carefully, arrange the shawl, and move toward the exit with Fenwick limping at her side.

She passed within arm's reach of him. Close enough that he caught the scent of the shawl — milk and lavender and something underneath, salt, the sea that got into everything in this city. She did not look at him. Of course she didn't.

But as she stepped out into the late light of King's Square, Hale heard her say, low, to Fenwick, in a voice pitched for no ears but a friend's — and heard it anyway, because twenty-two years of listening had given him ears that betrayed his manners:

"*Ledger Nine. He said it aloud. God help us — he said it aloud.*"

Hale stood alone in the emptying chamber, in the gold dust of the dying light, and admitted to himself what the list had been telling him for a week.

Tomorrow the Crown would present its proof of transmission. Tomorrow, if he was asked, he would swear to the security of this court and the integrity of its evidence. And tomorrow night, quietly, without orders and without permission, Inspector Elias Hale intended to pull on the thread named Wrenn — and see whether the whole shining fabric of the Crown's case came apart in his hands.
