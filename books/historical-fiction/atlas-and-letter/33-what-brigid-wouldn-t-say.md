## 33. What Brigid Wouldn't Say

They came for her at half past five in the morning, which Brigid O'Cuinn considered a coward's hour for arrests and said so, twice, while the constable waited for her to finish lacing her boots.

"You'll want a shawl," the constable said. He was young. His mother had raised him to be polite to cooks, and it showed in his face like a stain.

"I'll want my kettle," Brigid said, "and my own two feet under me, and neither of those is going to happen in your custody, so let's not pretend this is a visit." She took the shawl anyway. Wool was wool, and courthouse benches were famously cold. She folded it over her arm with the particular care she gave to good linens, looked once around her kitchen — the copper pots she'd polished for eleven years, the dough she'd set to rise before dawn, rising still under its cloth like something faithful — and then she walked out the servants' door of the House on Rope Street without looking back again.

Looking back was a luxury. She'd learned that at sixteen on a coffin ship out of Galway, when her whole family was reduced to what could be carried and her mother's face was the last thing visible above the rail. You did not look back at things you could not keep. You kept your hands busy and your mouth shut and you got where you were going.

The trouble with Silas Marchetti, Brigid thought as the constable's carriage rattled through waking streets, was that the girl had never learned that second part properly. Hands busy, yes — nobody's hands were busier, nobody's ledgers neater, nobody scrubbed a floor within an inch of its life the way that girl did when her mind wanted killing. But the mouth. The mouth held things. Eleven years in a trading house and the girl had never once learned to let a thing go the way other people did, loose and easy, the way you'd drop a coin in the harbor rather than be caught carrying it.

She held things. And now she was holding a baby, or had been, and Brigid knew — she'd known from the first night, God help her, she'd known the moment she saw that girl's face over the bundle — exactly how long a thing like that could be held.

* * *

The holding cell smelled of wet stone and other people's fear. Brigid had cooked for three hundred men in a fever camp during the cholera year; there was no smell that could make her flinch, and she informed the matron of this, and the matron, who had been prepared for weeping, seemed almost disappointed.

Inspector Hale came at nine.

She'd seen him before, of course — the Crown Constabulary man with the careful eyes and the courteous manner, the one who searched the dormitory last spring and thanked her for the tea he did not drink. She'd liked him better then, when he was only a threat.

"Mrs. O'Cuinn," he said, sitting on the stool across from her cell as though he'd been invited. "Thank you for coming."

"I wasn't aware I had a choice."

"There's always a choice." He set a leather folio on his knee but didn't open it. That was a technique too; she recognized it from years of getting sulking kitchen boys to confess to stolen sugar. Silence, patience, the appearance of having all morning. "You've been cook at Verrone Trading for eleven years."

"Twelve in September."

"During which time you've held the position of—"

"Cook. It says so on the papers you've obviously read."

A flicker. Not quite a smile. "During which time," he continued, "you have also purchased, over the past eight months, quantities of milk, farina, and infant gruel inconsistent with any need recorded in the house accounts."

Brigid looked at him the way she looked at a boy who'd come into her kitchen complaining of hunger an hour before dinner. "I bake bread every day for nineteen people. Milk goes missing the way buttons go missing. If you've never run a kitchen, Inspector, I can't explain it to you, and if you have, you already know I'm telling you the truth."

"The chit for the apothecary on Cooper Lane," Hale said. "Dated the ninth of March. Signed by a clerk named Aloysius Fenwick, authorizing disbursement of medicine for a child not listed in any register of the company."

"Fenwick signs everything. The man would sign your death warrant if you asked him to hold your pen."

"A child, Mrs. O'Cuinn." His voice didn't change at all. That was the frightening thing about Hale; he never turned hard, he simply stopped being soft. "Sick enough to need medicine from an apothecary rather than the house stores. Fed, evidently, for months on quantities of milk that appear nowhere. In a building I personally searched. Where is the child?"

And there it was. The whole conversation laid bare, the way she could see the whole of a recipe from tasting the sauce: they didn't have the baby. They didn't have Silas. They had Brigid, because Brigid was catchable — a woman of known address, known employment, no husband, no protectors, a foreign name on a manifest of suspects. Bait, she thought. They mean to fish with me.

She folded her hands in her lap, the same hands that had kneaded eleven thousand loaves, and considered.

She was not a clever woman, whatever the clerks thought of cooks. But she knew the weight of things. She knew that a secret was like a pot on the stove — you could lift the lid early and spoil everything with steam, or you could keep it covered and trust the heat, and the trick of a lifetime was knowing which moments needed the lid off and which needed it down tight. This was not a lid-off moment. Somewhere in this city a girl with calloused ledger-hands was doing something desperate and dangerous, and every question Brigid answered here would be a rope thrown around that girl's neck.

"Inspector," she said pleasantly, "do you take sugar?"

* * *

They moved her to the courtroom on the third day, and Brigid understood then the shape of the thing: she was not the point, she was punctuation. The trial of Tomas Reyes had opened with all the ceremony the Crown could afford — judges in wigs, the Director of Verrone Trading sitting prominent as a bishop, port charts displayed on easels like paintings at an exhibition. Treason. Charts sold to a foreign navy. Even Brigid, who trusted the Crown's paperwork about as far as she could throw the courthouse, felt the weight of it, the terrible officialness. That was the thing about lies told by rich men: they arrived wearing uniforms.

She sat in the witness row between a harbor pilot and a customs officer, listening to days of testimony she barely understood and understood completely. She heard the prosecutor describe Tomas Reyes — old Reyes, quiet Reyes, who had once stood in Brigid's kitchen drinking tea and saying nothing much, the way good men often don't — as a parasite upon the trade of the port. She watched Director Verrone give evidence in a gray suit, sorrowful and precise, a man grieving the betrayal of a friend, and she thought: *there is a man who has practiced that face in a mirror,* and she thought: *Silas, whatever you're doing, do it faster.*

On the fourth day they called her.

The walk from the witness row to the stand was perhaps thirty feet, and Brigid made it slowly, deliberately, the way she walked through the market when she intended to squeeze a vendor down two pennies on fish. Let them look at her. Cooks were invisible to such men until they stood up, and then, suddenly, they were enormous.

"State your name for the record."

"Brigid Aoife O'Cuinn. Cook. Twelve years at Verrone Trading, and before that the Sisters of Charity boarding kitchen in Millhaven, and before that you don't need to know."

The prosecutor — a thin, quick man named Corr whose voice was made of paper — smiled as though she were a country bumpkin amusing the court. "Mrs. O'Cuinn. You are acquainted with the accused?"

"Mr. Reyes drinks tea in my kitchen when the clerks let him. Drank. Past tense. He takes — took — two sugars and complained about the cost of raisins. That's the extent of our acquaintance."

"When did you last see Mr. Reyes?"

"Last autumn. He complimented my soda bread and I told him it was my mother's recipe and he said all good recipes were inheritance. Then he left."

Corr leaned toward the jury. "No further contact? No messages passed? Nothing given to you by Mr. Reyes, or by anyone acting on his behalf?"

Here it was, then. Earlier than she expected, and blunter. Brigid looked at Corr, and past him at Inspector Hale standing by the doors with his careful face, and past Hale at the gallery, where she found — of course she found — Silas Marchetti sitting with her spine straight as a mast and a bundled blanket in her lap that was either laundry or the whole world.

"No," Brigid said.

Corr produced a paper. "Can you account, Mrs. O'Cuinn, for this requisition chit, signed by clerk A. Fenwick, disbursing company funds for infant medicines on the ninth of March?"

"I cannot account for other people's signatures. I'm a cook, not a magistrate."

"It authorizes medicine for a child. Is there a child at Verrone Trading, Mrs. O'Cuinn?"

"No children at Verrone Trading," Brigid said. "Only indentured persons, and their papers say they're not children at all, they're investments."

Someone in the gallery laughed — one short bark, quickly strangled. The judge rapped his gavel. Corr's smile thinned.

"Let the record show the witness declines to answer."

"The record shows wrong, then. I answered. I answered very clearly."

"Mrs. O'Cuinn." Corr came around the front of the bench, paper fluttering. "Milk. Purchases of milk, eight months' worth, unrecorded. Farina. Gruel. A woman of your experience does not buy infant gruel for the fun of it. Who was fed?"

And Brigid O'Cuinn, who had crossed an ocean on the truth that survival meant silence, who had kept other women's secrets through two employers and one scandal and a marriage that ended at the dockside, looked directly at Silas Marchetti in the gallery — looked at her the way she used to look at her over the top of the stew pot when some clerk came sniffing around the kitchens, a look that meant *hold steady, girl, I've got this end* — and said:

"That would be me."

The courtroom went very quiet.

"I beg your pardon?" said Corr.

"The milk was mine. The gruel was mine. I'm fifty-three years old and I've buried one husband and no children, and eight months ago I decided I'd had enough of feeding clerks and started feeding someone who needed it. There's no law against feeding hungry mouths that I've ever heard of, though I'll grant you rich men write laws faster than I can read them."

"And the child? Where is this child you fed?"

"I don't know," Brigid said.

Which was true. Which was beautifully, perfectly true, because Silas had moved the baby to the room above the boil house weeks ago, and Brigid had not seen the child since the fever, since the forged chit, since the night Fenwick came limping to her kitchen white as a dishrag. She had held the baby exactly four times in her life. She could not testify to where the child was because she genuinely did not know, and she watched the realization move across Corr's face like weather — he could hear that it was true, everyone could hear it was true, and it bought her nothing, because the question behind the question was still coming.

"Do you know who the child belongs to?"

"I know who the child belongs to the way anyone knows who bread belongs to. To whoever's willing to get up in the dark and tend it."

"Mrs. O'Cuinn, I will ask you plainly. Did Silas Marchetti keep an infant hidden inside Verrone Trading House?"

The judge leaned forward. Hale, by the door, had gone entirely still. And Brigid understood the trap in its final shape: deny it, and she perjured herself the moment anyone proved otherwise; confirm it, and she handed them the girl. There was no answer that was safe. There was only the answer that was right.

She thought of her mother above the rail of the coffin ship, getting smaller. She thought: *we do not look back at what we cannot keep, but this one, this one I can keep.*

"I won't answer that," Brigid said.

"Madam witness—"

"I understand the question perfectly. I decline it. You may write that down however fits your paper best."

Corr turned to the bench with the air of a man presenting a specimen. "Your Honor, the Crown requests the witness be directed to answer."

"Mrs. O'Cuinn," the judge said heavily, "you are directed. Answer the question."

Brigid folded her hands. Eleven years of kneading had made them broad and scarred and utterly steady, and she put them in her lap like a woman settling in for a long day's work.

"No, Your Honor."

"You are directed by this court—"

"And I am refusing by me." Her voice did not rise. That was the thing the newspapers would later try and fail to capture — she never raised it, never wept, never performed. She spoke as she had spoken to a thousand kitchen boys: patiently, as to someone slow. "I've been silent for fifty-three years, Your Honor, mostly for wages. I know how it's done. I'll take whatever you give me for it, but I will not hand that girl to you on this stand, and every hour you sit me in that cell for contempt is an hour the court spends learning what I'd bet my life on. I'd think a room full of lawyers might find that interesting."

The silence afterward had a texture. Brigid felt it settle on the courtroom like flour dust.

Contempt, then. Ten days, the judge said, his voice dry as old biscuit, perhaps longer pending further obstruction. The constables took her arms and she shook them off — she could find her own way to a cell, thank you, she'd been walking to places she didn't want to go since Galway — and as she passed the end of the witness row she allowed herself one small thing. She stopped, turned to the gallery, and looked at Silas Marchetti full in the face.

She did not wink. Winking was for conspirators, and they were not conspirators; they were something older and less forgivable than that. They were family. What passed between them was smaller than a nod and larger than testimony: *hold steady, girl. I've got this end. See to yours.*

Then she walked down the steps to the cells below, and she was thinking so hard about the shape of the judge's face, cataloguing what the courtroom had heard and hadn't, that she nearly missed it — nearly walked straight past the two men speaking low by the cells' entrance, the young constable and the inspector, Hale, whose careful mask had slipped just far enough, in the empty corridor, to show her something underneath.

He was frowning at the prosecution's chart display through the doorway. His lips moved slightly, silently, the way a man's do when he is checking arithmetic against a ledger.

"—tide tables," he was saying, very quietly, to the constable. "Pull the tide tables for the eighth of November. Every port from here to Cape Sorrow. And the duty roster for the customs office that month — I want to know who countersigned the manifests, and I want to know whether that man was alive when his signature was made."

He felt her looking. He turned, and for one moment cook and hunter regarded each other in the lamplight, and Brigid saw him understand that she had heard, and saw him decide — visibly, the way a man steps off one plank onto another — not to care.

"Ten days," Brigid said to him, conversational, as the constable took her elbow. "For ten days of silence. Some men sell their whole lives for less."

"Some men," Hale said quietly, "are starting to wonder what their silence has been costing them," and he turned back toward the courtroom, toward the charts and the forged tides and the dead man's signature gleaming on the easel, leaving Brigid to descend alone into the dark of the cells, smiling to herself like a woman whose bread had finally, finally begun to rise.
