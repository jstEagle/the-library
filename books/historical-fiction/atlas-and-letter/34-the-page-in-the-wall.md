## 34. The Page in the Wall

The dormitory on Rope Street had walls of plaster over brick, and like everything the Verrone Trading Company built, the plaster was cheap and the lies beneath it were not. I had lived inside those walls for eight years and I knew their sounds the way my father had known the sound of the sea before a storm — the settling groan at night, the drip in the northeast corner that Brigid never managed to get mended because Fenwick's requisition forms always went missing, the hollow knock of a loose lath behind the shelf where the cleaning rags were kept.

That last one I had known about since I was thirteen.

I stood in the washroom at four in the morning, two days after Fenwick was arrested beside Brigid on the courthouse steps, and pressed my palm flat against the wall above the rag shelf. The plaster was cold. Behind it, somewhere between the studs, a page torn from Ledger Nine waited in its wrapping of oilcloth like a seed in frozen ground.

Fenwick had told me where, in the way Fenwick told me everything — sideways, buried in something practical. When they took him, his wrists already manacled, he had looked past the constables' shoulders at me as if I were furniture, as we had both agreed he would, and he had said, "Mind the damp in the washroom, Miss Marchetti. It gets behind the shelving. Ruins anything you store there."

Anything you store there.

* * *

I had gone back to the rented room above the boil house first, because Atlas was there, sleeping in the borrowed cradle with her fists curled under her chin, and Marisol sat awake beside her with a knife across her knees. Marisol did not sleep much these days. Neither of us did. The trial began tomorrow morning, and Tomas Reyes would stand in the dock while Director Verrone's forged charts were laid out before the bench like the bones of a saint, gleaming and false.

"Is it done?" Marisol asked. Her voice was low. She spoke English with the salt of the southern islands in it, and when she was frightened — which she would never admit to being — the salt got thicker.

"Fenwick is in custody," I said. "Brigid too. Hale has them both."

"I heard." She turned the knife over. "Then the page is in the wall until one of them tells someone where it is."

"They won't tell."

"You can't know that."

"I can." I sat down on the edge of the cradle and watched Atlas breathe. She slept the way children sleep who have never learned yet what the world costs — completely, with her whole small body, her mouth open, one foot escaped from the blanket. "Fenwick held his tongue through a beating from company men rather than give up less than this. And Brigid—" My throat closed for a moment. Brigid, who had taught me to feed a newborn with a cloth and sweetened milk at three in the morning, whispering like we were doing something wicked; Brigid who had forged a medicine chit with hands that trembled and eyes that didn't; Brigid who had looked at Inspector Hale in his own questioning room and given him nothing but her silence, and silence of that size does not break in a cell overnight. "Brigid would let them hang her first."

"Then we go and cut it out of the wall ourselves."

"No." I shook my head. "Rope Street will be crawling by now. Hale's men, company men — Verrone doesn't know if Fenwick hid more than one page, so he'll tear the building down to be sure. If either of us is seen going back into that dormitory tonight, the trial is over before it starts. They'll find some charge. They'll take the child." I said that last part quietly, because saying it loudly felt like an invitation to fate. "It has to be someone who belongs there. Someone invisible."

Marisol looked at me for a long moment. "You're not invisible anymore, Silas. You've been seen."

"Not yet," I said. "Not where it matters. To Hale's men I'm a servant girl with calloused hands. To Verrone I'm the indentured nobody he tried to buy last month and failed to. Nobody watches a woman carrying laundry." I touched Atlas's foot, tucked it back under the wool. "And she comes with me. Babies are better than laundry. No one searches a baby."

Marisol understood then. I saw it move across her face like weather. "You'll carry it in with her."

"In her blanket." I lifted the blanket from the cradle — the good one, the one Brigid had knit before any of us knew what was coming, wool dyed the deep brown of wet sand. "Sewn into the hem. Flat, against the lining, where a hand patting her back would feel nothing but a seam."

"They search bags at the doors. They'll have constables at every entrance, and Hale will be watching the gallery like a hawk over a henhouse."

"Yes," I said. "He will."

"And if he finds it?"

I thought about my father then. Not the drowned man, not the debt, but the diver — the way he described the moment on the seabed when the air in your lungs turns against you, when your body screams at you to bolt for the surface and bolt straight into the bends or worse. He said every diver learns one thing deeper than fear: that panic spends air faster than work does. That when the bottom drops away beneath you, you do not rise. You go slower.

"If he finds it," I said, "then I've lost everything I have left to lose, except her. So I make sure I don't lose her."

* * *

Getting back onto Rope Street was easier than it should have been, which told me how arrogant Verrone had become. There were men at the main door of the trading house — two constables and a company supervisor I knew by sight, a soft-bellied man named Prewitt who had signed my indenture papers eight years ago without ever once looking at my face. But the servants' entrance around the alley side, the door where an infant had been left to me on a winter night three years ago, was guarded by exactly one boy, fifteen perhaps, holding a lantern and trying to look older.

I knew him. Tam Osei, second-year indenture, who once stole dried figs from the kitchen and shared them out among the little ones with the solemnity of a priest distributing relics. He saw me coming up the alley in my shawl with a bundle in my arms and his eyes went wide and terrified and loyal all at once, and he turned his body to block the lantern light and said, very loudly, "Nothing back here, miss, door's locked, you'll have to come round front," while his hand found the latch behind him and turned it.

"Thank you, Tam," I murmured as I slipped past him into the dark.

"Don't know you," he said loudly, to the empty alley. "Never seen her in my life."

* * *

Inside, the dormitory was a corpse of itself. Furniture shoved aside, mattresses slit open, straw pulled out and scattered like entrails. They had searched the place top to bottom — Verrone's men first, rough and thorough, and then, later and quieter, Hale's constables, going through the wreckage again with more patience. The fire in the warehouse district two nights ago had burned Ledger Nine's duplicates to ash along with half a city block, and everyone in authority was now hunting for whatever scraps the fire hadn't eaten.

They had pulled the rag shelf away from the washroom wall. Of course they had. But they had looked *behind* it, not *through* it, because the loose lath was nailed back down and plastered over — Fenwick had done that himself, months ago, when the page first went in. He'd told me once, in his dry way, that the best hiding place isn't a hole, it's a hole that looks repaired.

I set Atlas down on a folded blanket atop the washtub, where she lay blinking at the ceiling, working her gums, making her small sounds of commentary. Then I took the bread knife I'd brought wrapped in my shawl and worked it into the seam of the fresh plaster.

Plaster comes away easier the second time. It took twenty minutes of slow, quiet scraping, pausing whenever a floorboard creaked overhead — the upper floors were being used as a watch post; I could hear boots pacing above me like a heartbeat in the ceiling. Twenty minutes, and then the lath swung loose, and I reached into the dark between the studs, past the cold brick, and my fingers closed on oilcloth.

I drew it out. Unwrapped it there by no light but the gray seeping under the door.

One page. Ledger Nine. In Fenwick's cramped, immaculate clerk's hand: dates, manifest numbers, freight weights that matched no pearl cargo ever landed at that wharf — and in the margin, in red ink, the annotation Fenwick had risked dying for: *Received per order D.V. — arms, crated, manifested pearl. See consignment 44-C. Witness: F. Aldous, junior clerk.* Below that, the signature of the receiving officer whose name appeared nowhere in the Crown's evidence, because that officer's handwriting also appeared on documents dated six weeks after his recorded death at sea.

A dead man signing manifests. A company shipping rifles as pearls. A clerk framed for treason to keep anyone from asking why.

I held the page and I understood, with a clarity that felt almost physical, like cold water closing over my head, that this single sheet of paper was worth more than the entire House of Verrone — and that every man in that building upstairs would kill me without hesitation to keep it out of a courtroom.

Atlas sneezed. Small, furious, perfect.

"All right," I whispered to her. "All right, little one. Time to get dressed."

* * *

I will not pretend my hands were steady. They were not. When I threaded the needle Marisol had put in my pocket, the thread missed the eye four times, and each failure made my breath shorter, and each shortening breath brought my father's voice back to me from the seabed of his stories: *Count, Sila. Count your breaths. Fear is just air leaving too fast.*

So I counted. Four counts in, six counts out, the rhythm he'd taught me at seven years old in the shallows off Meridian Point, my face in the water, his hand on the back of my head. I sewed by feel and by moonlight through the high window, laying the folded page flat against the inner lining of the blanket's hem, the long bottom edge and both ends, stitch after small stitch, pulling each one tight so the seam would read to any groping fingers as ordinary quilting. Wool over paper over linen. A blanket like a thousand other blankets. A hem like any hem.

Twice Atlas fussed and I stopped to rock the washtub with my foot, humming the wordless tune Brigid hummed over her pots, and twice she settled, and the third time she did not settle, and I picked her up and held her against my shoulder with the needle still warm in my fist, and I stood in that violated, gutted dormitory — the place that had been the only home I owned in the world, purchased with eight years of obedience — holding the child I had promised a burning letter I would keep, and something in my chest reorganized itself permanently.

I had spent nineteen years learning to be invisible. Standing there in the ruins of Rope Street with my daughter's heart beating against my collarbone, I understood that I was finished learning it. Invisible women survived. They endured, they kept their ledgers neat, they counted their breaths, and they drowned quietly, one indentured year at a time, and the world called it prudence. My father had died invisible, his debt worth more than his life on the company's books. Brigid was sitting in a cell right now being invisible, being silent, spending herself like coin to buy my safety.

No more. Tomorrow the world could look at me. Tomorrow I would stand up in front of the bench and the bar and Director Verrone in his polished gray coat, and I would let them see exactly what the Verrone Trading Company had spent eight years trying not to notice: a woman who could read, who could remember, and who was done being owned.

I finished the last stitch as the sky went from black to iron. I bit the thread. I ran my palm over the hem — nothing. Warm wool, a slight ridge, no more suspicious than any blanket's quilted border. Then I wrapped Atlas in it, snug against my chest in the carrying sling Brigid had sewn from a flour sack, and I fed her, and I changed her, because God only knew when I'd next have the chance, and I gave her my finger to grip, and she gripped it the way she always did, ferociously, as if letting go were a thing she refused to learn.

"There," I told her, my lips against the wool that covered the truth. "You're going to court, my love. Try to sleep through the boring parts."

* * *

Tam Osei was still at the alley door when I came out, and he was not alone — Prewitt stood beside him in the graying light, checking a pocket watch, and my heart performed a slow, deliberate dive.

But Prewitt looked at me the way he had looked at me for eight years: as scenery. An indentured woman, a baby — the docks were full of them, half the fishwives brought their infants to work. His eyes slid over the bundle on my chest, over my shawl, over my bowed head, and dismissed the entire contents of me in under two seconds.

"You," he said to Tam. "This door stays shut. Anyone comes, they come round front and get logged. Understand?"

"Sir," said Tam.

I walked past them both, out of the alley, into the street that sloped down toward the harbor and the courthouse rising beyond it, gray stone in gray morning, its clock tower cutting a pale sky. Constables at the doors. Barristers arriving in carriages. Somewhere behind those walls, Brigid in a cell, refusing to speak. Fenwick beside her, his ribs still strapped from the beating, keeping his books balanced even now, I had no doubt, in his head. Somewhere in a dock below the courtroom, Tomas Reyes waiting to hear whether he would hang for a company's sins.

And in the blanket against my heart, one page of Ledger Nine, riding on the breathing of a child who did not yet know she was carrying the weight of a trading empire.

At the courthouse steps, a constable raised a hand. "Bundle," he said. "What's in it?"

I looked up at him. Nineteen years of practice arranged my face into mild, tired, harmless nothing — and underneath it, steady as bedrock, something new.

"My daughter," I said. "She's teething. Do you want to hold her?"

He did not want to hold her. He waved me through with two fingers and a grimace, and I climbed the steps of the courthouse of the Crown of the realm with the sun coming up behind me and the truth sewn into a baby's blanket, and through the great doors ahead I could hear the bailiff's voice calling the court to order, calling the name

of Reyes, Tomas — and somewhere beyond the gallery rail, already seated, already watching the doors, sat a thin man in a good coat with patient hunter's eyes.

Inspector Hale saw me come in. He did not look away.
