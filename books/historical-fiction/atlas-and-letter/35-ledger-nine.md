## 35. Ledger Nine

They came for me at dawn, and I was glad of it, because I had not slept.

The night had passed in the cell beneath the courthouse with the baby's blanket sewn against my ribs, the page stiff inside the lining like a second spine. I had unpicked the seams twice and sewn them again, my fingers clumsy in the dark, terrified that a guard's search would find it and terrified worse that it would not be there at all — that I had dreamed Fenwick's whisper through the bars of the remand wagon, *the wall by the window frame, third stone up, she's clever enough*, and that the page was still sleeping behind the plaster of the dormitory while I sat here holding an ordinary blanket over an ordinary heart.

But it was there. I had felt its corners all night, sharp as teeth.

Atlas had cried for an hour when Marisol took her before dark — one long, offended wail that seemed to name me traitor to every promise I'd made since the knock after midnight — and then she had stopped, the way she did, sudden and complete, and looked at me over Marisol's shoulder with her father's grave eyes. As if she understood that some separations are the shape of love. As if she forgave me already. Babies are supposed to be innocent of everything except hunger, but that child has never once been innocent; she was born knowing what it costs to be carried through a dangerous world.

"Marchetti." The turnkey's fist on the door. "Crown versus Reyes. You're called at ten."

I stood. I smoothed Brigid's borrowed dress — grey wool, high collar, the kind of dress that asks no questions — and pressed my palms flat against my stomach until I could feel the page's edge through the cloth.

*Forget this face,* Tomas had written. I had disobeyed him from the first hour. Today I would disobey everything else too: Director Verrone, Inspector Hale, the crown itself, the careful arithmetic of survival that had governed nine years of my life. My father taught me to count breaths underwater. He never taught me what to do with the last one.

I counted anyway. It was the only prayer I knew.

* * *

The courtroom smelled of wax and wet wool and the river that seeped up through the stones even here, in this temple of ledgers and law. Every bench was full. Word had traveled — word always travels faster than any ship in that port — and the gallery held pearl merchants with their hats off, fishwives with their hats on, clerks from three rival houses pretending they were there on business, and old Mrs. Adeyemi from Rope Street who used to give Fenwick rides on her cart when his leg was bad. She caught my eye and did not smile. She nodded once, slowly, like a woman watching someone walk out onto ice.

Tomas sat in the dock between two constables, and the sight of him struck me harder than I expected, because I had built him enormous in my memory — the man who saved my father, the man who saved me, the man whose handwriting burned in my stove two winters ago — and the real Tomas Reyes was small now. Grey. His clerk's shoulders had curled inward like paper near a flame. But when they brought me up the aisle he lifted his head, found my face in the crowd, and his mouth moved around one silent word.

*Silas.* Not thanks. Not pleading. Just my name, spoken into the air where no one else could hear it, the way you check that a thing is real.

The prosecution's case had been laid out across three days like a banquet of poison. Charts recovered from Reyes's lodgings, marked in foreign notation. A letter of credit drawn on a Karstic bank. And the crown jewel, produced yesterday with maximum ceremony: a page from Ledger Nine, Verrone Trading Company, in Tomas's own hand, recording the sale of port soundings — depths, channels, tide-tables — to agents of a hostile navy. Director Verrone himself had testified to its authenticity, silver-haired and sorrowful, mourning his poor deluded clerk in a voice pitched perfectly for the gallery. *It grieves me beyond expression. The company is the crown's servant; Mr. Reyes betrayed both.*

The forgery was beautiful. That was the horror of it. Fenwick had wept when he described it to me on the mudflats — *he kept samples of Tomas's hand for years, Silas, practice copies, I found them once and thought nothing of it* — and beauty, my father used to say, is how the sea lies about its depth.

I was called as a witness for the defense at half past ten.

* * *

Walking to the stand, I passed within arm's reach of Director Verrone, seated in the front row with his counsel, and he turned his head and looked at me the way a man looks at weather. Then his gaze dropped, briefly, to my hands.

Everyone knew my hands by then. Inspector Hale had made much of them in his quiet way — calloused from crates, yes, but ink-stained at the nail beds, a ledger-keeper's stain, a girl who could read figures the way other girls read faces. Verrone had offered me freedom for my silence in a room that smelled of orange peel and money, and I had said, *I will consider it,* and walked out with his ring of confidence closing behind me like water.

Now he watched me take the oath, and I watched him watch me, and I understood that he believed I had considered it and chosen the sensible thing. That was his error, and it was the same error the sea makes: it believes everything that breathes must eventually surface.

The defense counsel, a hollow-cheeked man named Okafor whom Marisol had scraped up from somewhere south of respectability, asked his questions gently. My name. My position. Nine years indentured to Verrone Trading. My duties.

"I keep the receiving books," I said. "Pearl freight in, pearl freight out. Weights, grades, manifests."

"And how long have you kept them?"

"Since I was twelve." A murmur through the gallery; the arithmetic of a child bound to a counting house interested them more than treason did. Let them have it. Everything true today, whatever it cost.

"Are you familiar with the handwriting in the company's ledgers?"

"Every hand in the house," I said. "Mine, Mr. Fenwick's, Mr. Reyes's, the late Mr. Corbett's, and the director's own initials on the monthly certifications. You learn the hands the way you learn voices."

Okafor let that sit. Then he nodded to the prosecution's table, and a constable brought the exhibit forward: the ledger page, the damning one, mounted behind glass. *Sale of hydrographical soundings, per arrangement, payment to follow via Karstic draft. T.R.*

"Do you recognize this document, Miss Marchetti?"

Here is what I had planned to say, in the version of today where I survived it: *I recognize the hand as Mr. Reyes's, but the entry is wrong, the ink is wrong, the certification initial is misplaced.* Technical. Careful. A witness, not a confession.

I looked at the glass. Behind it, Tomas's forged signature lay on the page like a drowned man.

And I heard my father's voice instead of my plan. *When the current takes you, girl, don't fight sideways. Swim with it or dive through it.*

"No," I said.

The word dropped into the room and everything stopped breathing.

"No?" Okafor said, carefully. He didn't know. I hadn't told him. Marisol had argued for telling him and I had refused — a lawyer can be made complicit, and besides, I had not trusted anyone with this page except the people already bleeding for it. "You do not recognize the hand?"

"The hand, yes. It imitates Mr. Reyes's very well." I turned to face the bench. "But that page never existed in Ledger Nine. I know because I copied Ledger Nine's index with my own fingers in the spring, and because entries in the ninth ledger were certified on the last day of each month, and this bears its certification mark on the fourteenth. Because the ink on the figure column is iron-gall and the ink on the date is logwood, and no clerk in that house mixed inks in a single entry. And because—" my voice held, God help me, it held — "because Ledger Nine does not record soundings sold to anyone. It records guns."

The gallery erupted. The gavel came down like a cleaver. Somewhere in the roar I heard Verrone's counsel on his feet — *irrelevant, improper, the witness is a confessed thief of company records* — and I heard, under it, thin and clear, Mrs. Adeyemi saying *hush, hush now* to the whole room as if it were a market squabble.

The judge leaned down at me. "Miss Marchetti. You are either in possession of company documents obtained unlawfully, or you are inventing testimony to save a man's neck. Both are crimes. Do you understand me?"

"Yes, my lord."

"Do you wish to withdraw your answer?"

I thought of Atlas's laugh in the abandoned pearl camp, startling the gulls. Of Brigid in custody, who had been shown the noose of charges and had closed her mouth like a mussel at low tide. Of Fenwick's broken fingers learning to write again so that someday, he said, he could keep honest books, and what honest meant, and what it cost.

"No, my lord," I said. "I wish to enter evidence."

* * *

They nearly didn't allow it. This is the part no broadsheet will print, the part that happened in the space of ninety seconds while the room shouted: the judge threatening me with contempt and perjury both, Okafor objecting to his own witness, the prosecution demanding I be searched on the spot, and Director Verrone rising from his seat with his face gone the color of tallow, saying, *my lord, this is an outrage, that woman is a known associate of—* and stopping himself, because finishing the sentence would admit there was something to associate with.

Marisol stood up in the gallery. She didn't shout. She simply stood, arms folded, and a dozen fishwives stood with her, and the sound of that — the sound of Rope Street on its feet — changed the temperature of the room. The judge was a company man; everyone knew it. But he was also a vain man, and vanity has this virtue: it cannot abide looking bought in public.

"The witness will surrender the document," the judge said finally, "which will be examined for authenticity before it is entered. Constable."

"It has to come from me to the bar," I said, and my hands were shaking now, finally, usefully, like a diver's before the drop. "It's sewn into my daughter's blanket. I wasn't going to trust it to a drawer."

*I said it.* Daughter. In open court. Let Verrone chew on that word along with the rest.

They took the blanket from me — I had carried it in openly, a woman with a baby's things draws less searching than almost anyone — and cut the stitches right there on the clerk's desk, and unfolded the page, and the room went so quiet I could hear the river under the floor.

One page. Fenwick's salvage from the fire, torn at the edge where the plaster had held it, smoke-browned at one corner. Manifest 114, dated last March. *Cargo: pearl shell, 40 crates, gross weight 2,140 lb.* And beneath, in the same hand, the internal tally that should never have left the house: *actual: crated rifles, 38 cases, powder, 12, disposition Port Sable via lighter, manifest per D.V.*

*Per D.V.* Per Director Verrone.

Verrone's counsel demanded examination by the court's document man. The judge agreed — and then Inspector Hale stood up.

I had watched Hale all morning the way you watch a current: without moving your head, feeling for its direction. He had arrested Brigid and used her as bait; he had questioned me with terrible courtesy and let me go with his suspicion riding my shoulder like a gull. I had assumed he was Verrone's instrument, because instruments are what institutions make of men.

He crossed the well of the court and took the page from the clerk, and he did not look at Verrone, which in that room was the loudest thing anyone had done all day.

"My lord, I've handled the Verrone house's correspondence for six weeks of investigation," Hale said. "Requisitions, manifests, the director's private letters. I can speak to the hand on the internal tally." A pause, measured to the inch. "It is Director Verrone's."

"You are instructed by the Crown's prosecution—" began Verrone's counsel.

"I am instructed by the evidence," Hale said, "which is, my lord, supposed to be the same thing."

The judge stared at him for a long moment. I watched Hale stand under that stare, and I thought: he has just chosen. Right there, in front of everyone, between the career and the truth, and whatever it costs him he has already paid it, and he stands differently now, lighter, like a man who has put down something heavy.

"The court will compare the hands," the judge said at last, heavily. "Court is adjourned for one hour."

They did not adjourn Verrone. I saw him lean toward his counsel, mouth moving fast, and then rise and push through the crowd toward the corridor, and I saw Hale signal two constables with two fingers, quietly, the way you'd signal men on a deck — *follow him* — and they went.

One hour. They gave me a bench at the back, in custody of nobody in particular, because nobody quite knew what to do with a witness who might be a criminal. Marisol sat down beside me close enough that our shoulders touched.

"Well," she said. Her voice shook, just slightly. "You dove."

"I dove."

She put something in my hand, small and warm. Atlas's stocking cap, smelling of her, of milk and salt. "She's asleep at the lodging," Marisol said. "She slept the whole night. She's not afraid of anything, that child."

"She comes by it honestly," I said, and then I bent my head over the little cap and let myself shake for exactly one minute, because the hour was long but not that long, and after a minute a person can be brave again.

* * *

They reconvened at noon and the document man — a dry stick named Mr. Pell who had testified in a hundred forgery cases and enjoyed every one — delivered his findings to a room so packed that people stood along the walls on top of benches.

"The internal tally is in the hand of Emiliano Verrone beyond professional doubt. The manifest entry matches the surviving exemplars of Ledger Nine's format precisely. The paper stock is consistent with the company's ninth ledger series." He removed his spectacles with a small flourish. "As for the prosecution's exhibit—" he indicated the mounted page with the weary contempt of a craftsman asked to admire bad work — "the certification mark placement is anomalous, the inks are mixed within a single entry contrary to house practice, and the letterforms show the hesitation of imitation. Drawn, not written. Someone practiced this hand. Someone practiced it extensively."

"From whose documents," the judge said, in a voice with no appetite left in it, "would one practice a clerk's hand, Mr. Pell?"

"From samples, my lord. Kept over years." Pell shrugged, fastidious. "One cannot copy what one has never possessed."

I looked at Tomas in the dock. He had not moved through any of it, sitting straighter now than he had in three days, and when Pell finished, Tomas turned his head deliberately and looked at Director Verrone's empty chair. Then he looked back at the judge, and waited. He had waited two years. He could wait out the reading of a verdict.

It took the judge forty minutes. When he came back in, the room rose, and I gripped the back of the bench in front of me and counted breaths — four in, hold, seven out, my father's rhythm, the rhythm that keeps a body alive where the air isn't — and the judge read out the words in his dry official order: the chart evidence discredited, the credit letter explained, the charges against Tomas Reyes dismissed in full, and a warrant issued for the arrest of Emiliano Verrone on charges of forgery, conspiracy, and the unlawful shipment of arms under false manifest, *the witness Marchetti's conduct in this matter to be addressed separately at the court's discretion.*

*Separately.* I heard that word the way you hear a fin cutting water behind you. I had risked perjury and theft and contempt, and the court would collect; I had known that since I cut the first stitch. Fine. Let it come. Because across the room the constables were uncuffing Tomas Reyes, and the gallery was making a sound I had never heard in a courthouse — not cheering, something rougher and truer, the sound of a hundred people exhaling nine years of held breath — and Tomas pushed through it, ignoring every reaching hand, straight to me.

He took my face in his clerk's hands, ink-stained and trembling, and looked at me the way I imagine he looked at my father once, in some drowned year I'd never know the whole of.

"She is not mine," he said. "But she is yours. I never doubted you'd remember that."

"You told me to forget her face," I said.

"I told you to forget it if you weren't brave." He smiled, and for a moment the grey fell off him and he was the man from the stories. "There was never any doubt to test, Silas. Only a door only you could open."

Then the doors at the back banged open, and the sound came through the noise like a knife through canvas, and every head turned.

Inspector Hale stood in the doorway, hat in hand, flanked by constables, and behind him — wrists bound, coat immaculate even now, chin high as a man ascending a scaffold he intended to address — came Emiliano Verrone. The gallery roared. Verrone's eyes swept the chaos and found mine, and he stopped between the constables, and he looked at me for a long, cold moment with something that was not hatred. Hatred would have granted me too much standing.

"You'll want to enjoy today," he said, and his voice reached me across the room, level and pleasant, a merchant quoting a price. "Courts dissolve contracts, Miss Marchetti. They don't undo debts. Ask anyone in this port who really owns it, once the paperwork settles."

Then they took him down the steps, and Hale met my eyes over the crowd — one nod, brief, a debt acknowledged between people who understand each other — and the room erupted again around me.

And under all of it, steady as tide, I felt the page-shaped weight gone from against my ribs, and in its place nothing at all: no blanket to carry home, no secret, no hiding place left anywhere in the world.

Only a cell of my own waiting downstairs, and a baby asleep across town who was, by every law of blood and none of paper, entirely, absolutely mine.
