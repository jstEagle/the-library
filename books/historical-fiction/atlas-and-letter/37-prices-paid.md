## 37. Prices Paid

The verdict came on a Tuesday, and by Wednesday the whole port had found something new to talk about, which was how Silas Marchetti learned that even a scandal as large as the fall of the House of Verrone could not hold a harbor's attention longer than a single tide.

She stood in the courthouse corridor while clerks carried out boxes of evidence and lawyers embraced one another with the cautious warmth of men who expected to bill each other again soon. Tomas Reyes had walked down those steps a free man two days before, leaning on Marisol's arm, blinking in the daylight like a creature raised underground. The crowd had cheered him. They had not cheered Silas, though her name had been in every broadsheet: *the ledger girl*, they called her, as if she were a curiosity rather than a person, as if she had been found in a crate along with the page sewn into the blanket.

Atlas had slept through the whole climax of it, which Silas thought was probably the correct response to history. Babies did not care about justice. Babies cared about milk and warmth and whether the person holding them meant it. Atlas had known she meant it. That was the only audience that had ever mattered.

Now, in the gray morning after, she counted what remained.

That was the diver's habit in her — her father's habit — the inventory taken before surfacing. *What do you have. What did you spend. Can you make it back to the boat.* He had taught her to count breaths; she had taught herself, over eight years of indenture, to count everything else. Debts, favors, hours, lies. She had spent nearly all of hers.

She had her freedom, which was the thing everyone kept telling her to be glad about.

* * *

Fenwick was propped up in the infirmary bed at St. Brendan's charity ward like a piece of furniture someone had leaned against a wall and forgotten to finish placing. His leg, which had been lame since childhood, was now worse — the beating in the fish market had done damage that no court could adjudge, and the doctors spoke of *chronic pain* and *a permanent alteration of gait* in the same flat voices they used for weather.

"You're staring," he said, without opening his eyes.

"I'm auditing."

That earned her half a smile. Fenwick had been the trading house's best bookkeeper for nineteen years, and he liked nothing more than an honest accounting, even when the numbers were ugly. "Read me the totals, then. I've been trying to do it myself and I keep losing my place."

Silas sat on the edge of the visitor's chair and pulled it close. "One walking stick, ash wood, purchased Tuesday from the carter on Rope Street. One position at Verrone Trading, terminated — although terminated is a generous word for a company under crown seizure; nobody's position exists anymore. Eight years of ledgers reconciled to the penny, none of which anyone will pay you for now." She paused. "One bruised kidney, per Dr. Lowell. Two cracked ribs. A limp that will not improve."

"And the girl?" he asked. "The one who got me into all this?"

"Fled the jurisdiction," Silas said. "Last seen heading north with an infant and a letter."

"Good," said Fenwick. "I never liked her."

They sat with the joke between them like a coal passed hand to hand. Outside the window, the harbor worked its ordinary miracles — cranes swinging, gulls wheeling, a barque from the north easing toward her berth. The world had not stopped for any of them. Silas found that both bitter and strangely comforting. If the tides kept their schedule through the collapse of everything, then maybe a person could keep hers too.

"They're saying the company contracts are voided," Fenwick said, when the joke had cooled. "All of them. Every indenture dissolved by order of the Admiralty court. You know what that means."

"It means I'm nobody's property."

"It means," Fenwick said carefully, "that for eight years somebody told you where to sleep and what to eat and how long to stand in the cold, and now nobody will. Some people find that harder than they expect. There's no ledger for it. No columns to keep yourself straight in."

Silas looked at her hands. They were resting in her lap, palms up, and she saw what Inspector Hale had seen in the courtroom — hands that knew pens better than pearl crates, calloused in the wrong places, ink-stained at the edges of fingers that should have been rope-burned instead. Her father's hands had been ruined by salt water. Hers had been ruined by arithmetic. Neither set of ruins had been chosen.

"I've been free since Tuesday," she said. "It doesn't feel like anything. It feels like standing on a dock after the ship has already gone."

"That's because freedom isn't a thing you're given," Fenwick said. "It's a thing you fill up afterward. And filling takes time." He shifted against the pillows, winced, kept going anyway, because Fenwick never let pain interrupt a sentence he considered necessary. "Brigid comes this afternoon. She'll bring food that the nurses will confiscate and we'll eat anyway. You should be here."

"Where else would I be?"

"Nowhere," he agreed. "That's rather my point."

* * *

Brigid arrived at four o'clock with a basket, an expression like a closed door, and news she delivered while unwrapping a boiled ham as though the ham required supervision before anything true could be said.

"I'm turned off," she said. "Officially. The constabulary cleared me — Hale himself came and signed the paper, very courteous, wouldn't meet my eye — but the boarding house matron says a woman who's been in custody is a woman who can't be trusted around other people's silver. So that's finished. Twenty-two years feeding company men, and my references amount to a note saying I obstructed a crown investigation and was ultimately vindicated." She snorted. "Vindication. It's a word for people who don't need the work."

Silas felt the guilt arrive on schedule, punctual as a debt collector. "Brigid—"

"Don't." Brigid pointed the carving knife at her, not unkindly. "Don't you dare apologize to me for doing the right thing. I knew what the risks were when I taught you to feed that baby behind the flour sacks. I'd do it tomorrow. But I'll thank you to notice that I did lose the post, because noticing is what friends are for. We tally honestly in this family or not at all."

*Tally honestly.* Silas took the words and held them. "What will you do?"

"There's a widow on Culler's Lane renting rooms above her own kitchen. Cheap because the chimney smokes. I've saved enough to take the lease for a year if I'm careful." Brigid's hands went still on the ham knife. "I keep thinking I'll take lodgers. Cook for paying guests. Feed people who choose to eat at my table instead of eating because they're contractually obligated." Something moved across her face, quick as a shadow under water. "It frightens me more than the constabulary did, if you want the truth of it. Twenty-two years of bells telling me when to start the bread. Now there's no bell."

"No bell," Silas repeated softly. That was it exactly. That was the shape of the emptiness she could not name in front of the lawyers and the broadsheet men: not grief exactly, not fear exactly, but the absence of structure where a life had been. For eleven years — first her father's deckhand, then the company's child, then the company's ghost — someone else had drawn the map of her days. Now the map was blank and the compass in her hand was her own pulse, and she kept waiting for it to point somewhere.

"The baby's well?" Brigid asked, gentler now.

"She has a tooth coming. She bites everything. Yesterday she bit Marisol."

"God help Marisol." But Brigid smiled when she said it, and the smile cracked the closed door of her face open just wide enough to show the room behind it: a woman who had smuggled milk and forged chits and held her tongue in a courtroom until silence became her testimony, all for a child who was not hers and never would be called hers, and who had lost her livelihood and gained, apparently, nothing except the knowledge that she had been right to do all of it.

Silas reached across the infirmary blanket and gripped her wrist. "Come Sunday. All of us. Wherever I'm staying — I haven't found the anywhere yet, but I'll find it by Sunday. We'll eat together. Nobody contractually obligated."

"Sunday," Brigid agreed, and her eyes were wet, and neither of them pretended otherwise.

* * *

She walked home — *home*, the word still a stranger — through the fish market, because she was tired of avoiding places. The stalls were up and the blood was hosed away and the thugs who had cornered her there were nowhere to be seen, scattered like rats when the Verrone money stopped flowing. A woman sold oysters from a barrel and called prices in a voice like a gull's. Life went on being ordinary at the speed of commerce, indifferent to the fact that Silas had burned her only future in a courthouse three days ago.

Because that was the truth under all the rest of it, and she made herself look at it while she walked, the way her father had made himself look at the bottom before diving.

She had no papers. Her indenture document — the thing that had owned her since age eleven — was voided, which meant it protected her no longer and defined her no longer, and there was nothing in its place. No reference, no trade certification, no letter of character except from a man convicted of treason and acquitted, which the respectable world would count as worse than nothing. She had a father's diving ring, traded and re-traded and finally returned to her by Marisol with a shrug. She had a diving token, half of a pair, worth perhaps a shilling to a collector. She had a child who needed feeding and lodging and, eventually, explaining to.

And she had a letter.

Tomas's letter — the original draft, the one he had shown her in the abandoned camp, the one he had pressed into her hands at the courthouse steps along with the guardianship papers she had signed with her own name, the first document in her whole life that belonged to her and described her as belonging to no one. She had carried it against her ribs for three days the way her father used to carry his last coin.

She stopped at the harbor rail and looked at the water. Gray-green, patient, holding its secrets the way it always had. Somewhere under that surface her father had died owing a company that had deserved worse than a drowning debt. Somewhere beyond the breakwater lay the coordinates Tomas had scratched beside a half-token under a pier — coordinates that had started all of this, that had turned a clerk's shadow-life into a story with a body count.

The cost, tallied honestly, as Brigid demanded:

Her father's life, eight years gone. Her childhood, spent hauling crates for men who shipped guns in pearl freight. Fenwick's leg, permanently altered. Brigid's post, twenty-two years, gone in an afternoon. Her own name dragged through broadsheets that called her *ledger girl* and would call her worse once the sympathy wore off. And ahead of her: nothing. No bell. No map. No next entry in the columns.

Freedom felt like falling. Everyone had warned her it might, and everyone had been right, and none of them had mentioned that the falling didn't stop once you noticed it. You just learned to breathe on the way down, the way her father had breathed underwater — slow, counted, deliberate — until the falling revealed itself to be something else entirely.

She was still learning.

A shadow fell across the rail beside her, and Silas did not flinch, because she knew the tread: uneven, careful, a man navigating the world with a walking stick and a ledger in his head.

"You're supposed to be resting," she said.

"I rested for four hours. It was interminable." Fenwick settled against the rail with a grunt, laid the ash-wood stick across the top bar like a fence rail, and looked out at the water. For a long moment neither of them spoke. Then: "Marisol found a ship."

Silas turned. "A ship for what?"

"For us, if we want it. For whoever wants it." He kept his eyes on the horizon, delivering the information with a bookkeeper's neutrality, as though the numbers were not enormous. "Coastal packet, the *Meridian*, out of the northern ports. Marisol knows the master. They need a cook — Brigid's name came up, imagine that — and a purser who can keep honest books, which is apparently rarer than pearls. There's a cabin. Small. Two berths, and room for a cradle, if a person had a cradle."

The harbor noise seemed to recede, the way sound receded when her father took her under for the first time — everything suddenly close and clear and quiet.

"Fenwick." Her voice came out strange. "What are you saying?"

"I'm saying," he said, "that the falling is easier with company. And that Marisol sails Thursday on the evening tide, north, to see her brother settled somewhere the newspapers haven't memorized, and that there is space aboard for a girl who keeps excellent accounts and an infant who bites." He finally looked at her, and behind the dry humor in his face was something older and more frightened and more hopeful. "No one is making you go. That's rather the entire point of everything we did. But the tide won't wait past Thursday, and I wanted you to have time to count."

Time to count. Time to stand on the dock and look at the empty map and decide, freely, for the first time in her life, what went in the columns.

Silas put her hand in her pocket and closed it around the diving token — her father's half, worn smooth, engraved with a mermaid whose tail curled into a number. Half of a pair. It had always been about halves, hadn't it? Half a token, half a family, half a life — and the other halves out there somewhere, under piers, on ships, in the pockets of people who chose you.

"Thursday," she said.

"Thursday," Fenwick agreed.

Far out past the breakwater, the evening light broke through the cloud cover and lay down across the water in a long gold road, pointing north.
