## 39. Departures

* * *

Brigid O'Halloran had never in her life owned a door that locked from her own side.

She stood in the doorway of the boarding house on Culler's Lane — three stories of salt-bleached brick with a sign she had painted herself, crooked but legible: **THE ANCHOR'S REST. ROOMS BY THE WEEK. MEALS INCLUDED.** Her thumb found the key in her apron pocket and turned it over and over, feeling its teeth, its small cold weight, the way other women might finger a rosary.

The company had voided her post along with every indenture on Rope Street, and for three days after the trial she had wandered the dormitory like a ghost whose haunting was finished, touching the great copper kettles, straightening the children's cots out of habit, waiting for someone to tell her where to be. No one did. Freedom, it turned out, arrived without instructions.

Then Fenwick had come limping into the kitchen with a newspaper folded under his bad leg and an expression she distrusted on principle — hopeful.

"There's a house," he said. "On Culler's Lane. The widow who kept it died in spring and the nephew wants it gone cheap because he lives in the capital and hates the smell of fish. I've done the sums, Brigid. Boarding house. Sailors pay promptly, widows pay late, and you can cook for both."

"I don't know how to keep a house that isn't someone else's."

"You know how to feed forty people off a stove built for twelve," Fenwick said. "That's most of it. The rest is just arithmetic, and I'll do the arithmetic."

Now she walked through her own rooms counting them like pearls: six beds upstairs, four of them already let to merchant sailors who smelled of tar and left their boots in tidy rows because she'd told them once, only once. A kitchen with a range wide enough to roast a whole goose. A parlor with two armchairs by the window, one of which was hers and hers alone, and in the evening she sat in it with mending or nothing at all, and no supervisor's footsteps ever came down the hall.

What she missed was the noise. Not the sailors' noise — she could summon that easily enough, they were boys at bottom, all of them, loud as weather. She missed the particular sound of a household she had built out of strays: Silas's quiet feet on the stairs before dawn, Fenwick's pen scratching through the wall, the baby's sudden laugh like something breaking open.

They were all still in the port. That was the trouble with freedom; everyone had it now, and none of them knew what to do with it except drift apart slowly, politely, like boats cut loose in the same tide.

Brigid solved the problem the way she solved everything: she cooked. On the first Sunday of every month she laid a table for ten in her parlor and sent word down to Rope Street, and they came — Fenwick first, always, because he could not resist her soda bread; then Silas with Atlas riding her hip like a small captain surveying new waters; Marisol when she was in port; even Tomas Reyes once, sitting careful and formal at the edge of things until Brigid put a second helping in front of him without asking and he understood he was expected to stay.

This month the goose was good and the bread was better, and afterward, while Fenwick showed Silas some figure in a ledger he carried everywhere now like a talisman, Brigid held Atlas on her knee by the fire and sang the low nonsense song her own mother had sung, and the child's eyes went heavy and dark, watching her mouth move.

"She has your stubbornness," Brigid said to Silas across the room. "God help us both."

Silas looked up. For a moment — Brigid saw it happen — the girl's face did the thing it always did when anyone spoke of the future: a flicker of fear, quickly mastered. Then it softened.

"We're going north," Silas said. "After the month's end. There's work there — a shipping concern that wants honest ledgers, if such a thing exists, and Fenwick says the harbor master owes him a favor from the trial." She said *the trial* lightly, the way people say words that have been worn smooth from handling. "It's time."

Time. Brigid bent her head to the child's hair and breathed in the warm milk-and-soap smell of her, and did not say what rose in her throat, which was *stay*, which was *you are the family I chose at forty-five years old after thinking myself done with choosing*. She was not a woman for saying those things. She said them in flour and butter and a locked door.

"North is cold," she said instead. "I'll pack you dried apples. And there's a woman up that way, a midwife I nursed through a fever years ago — I'll write you her direction."

"Brigid." Silas's voice had gone quiet the way it did when the girl meant to be unbearable. "Come with us."

"And leave my house?" But her hands had stopped moving on the child's back.

"Bring your house," Silas said. "That's the whole point of it being yours."

* * *

Fenwick kept his books balanced to the farthing, which was, he had come to understand, a form of prayer.

He worked now from a rented desk above the chandler's on Quay Street — honest books for honest clients, mostly small boat owners and fishwives who needed someone to read their contracts aloud and translate the lying clauses into plain speech. His leg ached in the cold and the stairs were a daily insult, and he had never been happier in his life. Every page he entered was his own. Every column summed to truth, or as near to truth as arithmetic permitted, and no Director Verrone would ever again lean over his shoulder with a soft voice and a softer threat.

Verrone himself was in the capital now, awaiting trial. The papers called it *the Pearl Scandal* and printed the company's name in blackletter shame, and every morning Fenwick read the accounts with his tea going cold, checking the reporting against what he knew, finding it accurate, and being astonished all over again. He had spent nine years believing that the men who ran the world were too large to fall. Watching Verrone's forged charts come apart in court under Hale's flat testimony — *the tide tables do not support these dates, my lord* — had rearranged something fundamental in Fenwick's understanding of the universe. The large could fall. It required, apparently, only ordinary people refusing, in concert, to look away.

His body had paid its portion. The beating in the fish market had healed crooked; the ribs on his left side ached when the wind came east, and Dr. Pruell said the damage to the kidney was permanent, said it in the tone doctors reserve for facts they consider interesting rather than tragic. Fenwick had made a joke about it at the time — something about how he'd always wanted to be a man of consequence — and seen Silas's face go white and wished immediately that he hadn't. She carried enough. They all pretended she didn't, and she let them pretend, and the pretending was itself a kind of love.

He climbed the chandler's stairs this grey afternoon with the month's accounts under his arm and found Silas waiting at his desk, Atlas asleep against her shoulder, a folded paper in her free hand.

"The contract," she said. "Read it before I sign anything. I know how to read contracts now, but I want it read twice."

"That," Fenwick said, settling onto his stool with the sigh of a man whose leg had opinions, "is the wisest thing anyone has said to me all week, and I include my own conversation."

He read it through. Wages fair, terms fixed at two years, no clause binding lodging, no penalty-of-debt language anywhere — he checked three times, running his finger under each line the way he'd been taught by a man he'd later learn was a thief. He read it a fourth time anyway.

"It's clean," he said. "You could sign it as it stands."

"But?"

"But you're entitled to travel costs, and they haven't listed them. Ask for passage north for three, plus freight allowance for your goods. They'll grant it. Firms courting clerks with reputations like yours always do."

"What reputation do I have?" Silas asked, and there was genuine puzzlement in it, which moved Fenwick more than he could say. Nineteen years old, the girl who had broken the House of Verrone with a page sewn into a baby's blanket, and she genuinely did not know what the port said about her.

"A reputation," he said, "for being unbuyable. In trade, Miss Marchetti, there is no higher recommendation."

While she folded the contract away, he drew out a second document — smaller, creased soft from handling — and slid it across the desk. She looked at it without touching it. It was a letter of reference, written in his best copperplate: *Silas Marchetti served the undersigned faithfully and with exactitude... her character is beyond price... any firm engaging her services engages honor.*

"You wrote me a reference," she said slowly. "Like a freed servant."

"You are a freed servant. That's rather the entire point of the last six months." He smoothed the page with both hands, suddenly embarrassed. "Also, it is the first true thing I have ever put in a letter of reference, and I wanted you to have it. Sentiment. Blame the ribs; they make me soft."

Silas read it standing, the baby breathing against her shoulder, and when she finished she did not thank him — she never thanked properly, it was one of the things he loved in her — she simply said, "You'll take the room above us in the north. The shipping firm has housing. I asked."

Fenwick looked down at his ledger, at the neat columns of his honest new life, and found his eyes had gone unreliable.

"Well," he managed. "Someone will have to check their figures."

* * *

Marisol Reyes stood at the harbor rail at dawn with seven letters in her coat and the *Paloma* loading behind her, and allowed herself, for exactly one minute, to grieve.

Home was three weeks' sail south: a yellow house above the water at Puerto Cielo, bougainvillea gone riotous over the gate, her mother's grave in the churchyard behind it, and her father's chair empty on the porch for eleven years now because Tomas had been gone eleven years, and she had crossed an ocean to find out why. She had found out. She had found more than why — she had found a niece with her brother's exact frown rendered in miniature, and a nineteen-year-old diver's daughter with the moral spine of a cathedral, and a lame clerk, and a cook who fed strangers like it was ammunition, and an inspector of constabulary whom Marisol had decided, against all evidence, was a decent man having a very difficult decade.

The letters were for home. One for her mother's sister, who kept the house now. One for the parish priest, who would weep. Five for the family of Elena Vasquez, the young woman who had died in the Verrone warehouse "accident" — the woman who had been Atlas's mother, though the child would never carry that name unless Marisol chose to tell it, someday, carefully, when the telling could be a gift instead of a wound. Elena's family deserved to know how their daughter's daughter lived, and that she lived laughing, and that a stranger had gone to prison-risks for her before she could walk. Marisol had rewritten those five letters eleven times. They would never be right. She would send them anyway, because wrong letters beat silence, which was the whole lesson of her brother's life.

"You're staring at the water like it owes you money," Silas said behind her.

"My brother used to say that." Marisol did not turn around yet. Below them, longshoremen swung crates aboard with a rhythm like slow music. "I'm sailing Friday. Ten days to Puerto Cielo if the trades hold."

"I know. Fenwick told me. He pretends he doesn't keep track of departures but he keeps track of everything."

Marisol turned then and studied the girl — the wind pulling at her dark plait, the child on her hip solemnly investigating a gull as though filing a report on it. "And you're north."

"North. With Brigid, if she'll come — she's pretending to think about it, which means yes. And Fenwick." Silas hesitated, and Marisol watched her decide to say the true thing, the way Silas always eventually decided. "Tomas won't sail with us. He says the child is settled and he would unsettle her. He says a man newly acquitted should be quiet somewhere for a year." Her jaw tightened. "He says."

"He's afraid," Marisol said. "Eleven years of fear doesn't drain out of a man in a season. Give him the winter." She touched Silas's cheek, briefly, the way you touch a sister. "Write to me care of the consul. And listen to me, niña — if the north goes wrong, if any door closes, you send for me and I will come through whatever wall I have to. You know this."

"I know this."

Atlas chose that moment to reach for Marisol with both arms and an imperious syllable, and Marisol took her and held her face to the sunrise and felt the minute of grief pass clean through her and out, because grief, she had learned, was just love with nowhere to stand, and there was nowhere it needed to stand today. The child babbled. The gulls screamed their ancient grievances. Behind them the city bells began the hour, and somewhere on Culler's Lane a goose was surely meeting its end.

"Ten days to home," Marisol told the baby. "And next year, we come back. Your aunt Marisol keeps her promises. Ask anyone I've threatened."

* * *

Inspector Edmund Hale had resigned.

Not dramatically — he had learned discretion from the best liars in the port — but the morning after the verdict he had sat at his desk in the Constabulary annex and drafted a letter to the Commissioner that said, in essence, that a man who had spent twenty years learning to detect forgery could no longer in conscience serve an institution that had rewarded him for detecting it too late. He had signed it, sealed it, slept on it, and in the morning added one line: *I am content to remain through the spring assizes as a witness.* Because duty, whatever else it was, did not permit leaving a case half-testified.

Now the assizes were done, Verrone was bound over to the capital, and Hale's last official act in the port of his birth was to stamp the closing entry on the file of *Crown v. Reyes*: **ACQUITTED**, in his own hand, in ink he had tested against forgery for thirty years and never once had cause to doubt.

He was carrying the file to the records room when he passed Silas Marchetti in the corridor — by design, he suspected, since the girl did nothing by accident. She stood by the window with the child, ostensibly admiring the rain.

"Inspector."

"Miss Marchetti." He stopped. Protocol said he should keep walking; protocol had lost its authority over him some months ago, in a courtroom, at the precise moment he had chosen a dead clerk's handwriting over a Director's smile.

"The medicine chit," Silas said quietly. "The one Brigid forged, last autumn. It's still technically outstanding. An offense against the company's dispensary regulations."

Hale regarded her. Rain ran down the window in silver threads. Somewhere below, a ship's bell rang.

"I have reviewed that matter," he said, "and find the documentation irregular. Specifically, I find the chit in question was issued under authority of the Verrone Trading Company, an entity whose records have been declared void by the Crown. An authorization from a void entity," he continued, with the mild precision of a man laying cards on a table, "is no authorization at all — and therefore no forgery of one can be prosecuted. The law is very clear on this. Or unclear. Depending entirely on whether one wishes it to be."

For a moment something moved behind Silas's eyes that might, in a less disciplined face, have been laughter.

"That's a very convenient reading, Inspector."

"I am a very convenient man." He tucked the file under his arm. "Good luck in the north, Miss Marchetti. If your ledgers are ever questioned — and they will be, firms always question a woman with your history — write to me at my brother's practice in Ashcombe. I find I have experience testifying on your behalf, and it would be wasteful to let the skill rust."

He walked on before she could answer, because he was sixty-one years old and English, and there were limits. But at the records room door he paused, and set down the file, and took from his pocket the small brass token he had carried since the trial — the diving token, the matched pair reunited as evidence, released to him afterward by the court with a note in Fenwick's handwriting that said only *she thought you should keep it.*

Hale had turned it over many nights. Half a token, split between two men, one of whom had drowned keeping faith and one of whom had nearly hanged breaking it. And a girl in between who had assembled the halves into a case.

He dropped it in the post to Ashcombe the same afternoon, addressed to no one, and let the port sort it out.

* * *

They gathered on the north quay on the last Friday of the month, under a sky scrubbed clean by three days of rain: Silas with Atlas and a trunk containing one diving ring, one legal guardianship, and forty-one letters she had written and not sent to a father dead nine years, which she intended, she said, to read to the sea. Fenwick with his ledgers wrapped in oilcloth and his heart doing poorly-concealed gymnastics. Brigid, arriving last in a hired cart with her copper kettle lashed down like cargo of state — she had closed the Anchor's Rest for the season, posted a sign reading *REOPENS SPRING, INQUIRE AT MY NIECE'S IN THE NORTH,* and told her sailors to write if the new cook couldn't manage a proper loaf.

Marisol stood with them until the last possible bell. Tomas stood further off, hat brim low, and when the gangplank went down he came close enough to press something into Silas's hand — a folded paper she knew by shape before she opened it, another draft, another version of the note that had begun everything, and beneath it his voice, roughened: "When she asks, one day. Tell it in order. All of it."

Then the lines were cast off, and the port fell away in layers — the fish market where Fenwick had bled, Rope Street with its dormitory windows dark, the courthouse tower where the truth had finally been entered into evidence — and the gulls followed them out past the breakwater, complaining, until even they gave up.

Atlas stood at the rail in Brigid's arms, waving at the water with the whole-arm seriousness of the very young, and ahead the sea ran silver to the horizon, and north, somewhere past it, a city waited with honest books to keep and rooms to fill and a life, at last, that belonged to no one's ledger but their own.

Silas took out the letter — Tomas's original instruction, *forget this face,* and beneath it now, in fresh ink, his amendment: *or remember it well, and build it a name.*

She read it once, folded it away, and turned her face to the wind coming off the open water, cold and clean and full of distance, like the first breath after holding too long.

The child laughed at the spray. The bow lifted. They went north.
