## 3. Orders Come Down

The papers came on a Tuesday, because of course they did. Amaya Vasquez had long ago noticed that the government reserved its cruelties for Tuesdays, as if Mondays were needed for recovery from Sunday and Wednesdays were too close to market day to be wasted on bad news.

She was beating a rug off the back porch when the courier came up the lane — a boy on a mule with a leather satchel and the particular slouch of someone who delivers misery for a salary and has stopped apologizing for it.

"Vasquez?" he called, before he'd even reached the fence.

"Which one?" she said, because there were two Vasquezes in that house and she wanted, just for a moment, for it not to be hers.

"Amaya Vasquez. Widow's pension on file, three dependents." He said it like a recipe. "You're to sign."

"I haven't ordered anything."

"Nobody orders anything." He swung down off the mule and pulled an envelope from the satchel, and she saw the seal before she saw anything else — the army's crossed-line stamp, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper. "You're selected. Berths on the armistice ferry. Three berths." He glanced up at her face and added, with the ghost of his old, pre-salary courtesy, "Congratulations are apparently in order."

*Selected.* That was the word armies used when they meant *taken*. She signed where he pointed, took the envelope, and stood in the yard until the mule's hoofbeats faded, holding it the way you hold a wasp you've agreed not to swat.

Three berths. She had four people.

She did not let herself think about the fourth person yet. She put the thought in a drawer and shut the drawer, and went inside, and made lunch, and smiled at her children, and hid the envelope inside the flour tin behind the dried chilies, which was the one place in the valley Noor would never look, because Noor believed cooking was a form of weather — something that happened to you.

* * *

Her brother was in the workshop, of course. He was always in the workshop now. Since Elias died — since the *accident*, everyone said, though no one in Amaya's household said it in that tone of agreement that makes a word mean its opposite — Noor had been spending his days in there like a man keeping a vigil that had gotten out of hand.

She brought him coffee because that was what sisters did, and also because bringing coffee gave her a legitimate reason to look at him, which these days felt like checking a fever.

He had ink on his fingers, his jaw, and inexplicably one ear. There was a smudge of red at the edge of the map on his table that he covered with his forearm as she came in — quickly, casually, the way a card player gathers his hand.

"You've got ink on your ear," she said.

"Do I?" He rubbed the wrong side.

"The other—no, leave it. It's very becoming. The town will talk."

"They already talk," Noor said, smiling, and turned over a blank sheet as if he'd been doing nothing at all. Her brother had never been able to lie with his mouth, but his hands were getting better at it, and that frightened her more than anything the courier had brought.

"Eat something," she said. "You look like one of Elias's old charts. All lines and no color."

"I ate."

"You drank coffee standing up and called it a meal. That's not eating, that's fueling. A mule fuels. My son fuels. You're forty-one years old and you have a stove."

"Yes, Amaya."

"Don't *yes Amaya* me in that voice. I've buried a husband, I won't bury a brother who died of soup deficiency." She set the cup down and stayed there a moment longer than the errand required, watching him bend back over the table, watching his forearm settle protectively over whatever he was hiding.

Guilt is a strange spice, she thought on the way out. It gets into everything, even the watching.

Because while her brother bent over secrets in here, she was keeping a larger one in a flour tin ten steps away, and she was beginning to understand that the two secrets were probably the same secret wearing different coats. The army did not send ferry berths to widows out of charity. The army sent them out of arithmetic. Somebody upstairs had drawn a line around this valley, and then drawn another line inside it marking who was meant to be gone before anyone could ask why, and her family's name had fallen inside the second line.

They wanted them out. Politely. With paperwork. Before the ice broke.

And Noor — stubborn, brilliant, soup-deficient Noor — was in here inking something he wouldn't show her, red-handed, literally red-handed, in the workshop of a man the army had already killed once.

She washed the coffee pot twice that afternoon. It hadn't needed it either time.

* * *

That evening she told Mateo.

Not everything. She told him there were berths, and that they were going to use them, and that he was not to speak of it at the mill or the market or the well, and especially not to Uncle Noor. Mateo was nine and took secrets seriously; he nodded like a small magistrate and then immediately ruined it by asking, "Why not Uncle Noor?"

"Because—" Amaya began, and discovered she didn't have an ending for the sentence that wasn't itself a secret, so she said, "Because some news travels better after it arrives," which was nonsense but had the confident rhythm of sense, and Mateo accepted it the way children accept parental nonsense: filed under *adults*, drawer shut.

The girls were easier. Rosa was six and could be managed with a story about a surprise voyage; Lita was four and could be managed by anyone holding a sweet. It was astonishing, Amaya reflected, scrubbing pots after dinner, how much of motherhood was just careful distribution of information. A household was a map too. You decided what went on it. You decided what stayed in the margins.

Noor joined them for dinner for once, sitting in Elias's old chair because nobody had the heart to move it and nobody else would sit in it, so it had become his by silent treaty. He ate — actually ate, two full bowls, which she counted as a victory and possibly a symptom. He asked Mateo about school. He laughed at something Lita did with a spoon. He looked, for nearly a whole hour, like a man who was going to be fine.

Then the wind changed, came down off the ridge with the first real bite of winter in it, rattled the shutters, and Noor looked toward the window — toward the bridge road, though from the table you couldn't see it, and both of them knew it anyway — and something in his face closed like a ledger.

The storms were coming. When they came hard enough, the army would close the bridge, and then the valley would be a bowl with one lid, and everyone inside it would be exactly where somebody in a uniform wanted them.

Including, said the flour tin, in the voice of a Tuesday envelope, her children.

* * *

She took the envelope out at midnight and read it properly for the first time, by candle, in the pantry, like a criminal.

The paper was heavy stock, the kind the army used for things it expected to be argued with. *By authority of the Provisional Administration:* passage for three persons, named below, aboard the armistice ferry *Meridian*, departing upon notice, prior to freeze. *This document constitutes final determination of eligibility.* Final determination. Even their mercy was nailed down.

Below, in a clerk's patient hand: *Amaya Vasquez. Matéo Vasquez. Rosalind Vasquez.*

And beneath that, a fourth line. Not struck through — nothing so honest as a strike. Just omitted. Left blank, the way you leave a field blank on a map when what's actually there is something you'd rather not draw.

Adelita Vasquez, age four, did not exist.

Amaya read it five times. She held the candle closer, as if light were a legal argument. But the line stayed blank, and the truth of it worked its way into her slowly, coldly, like water finding a seam in stone: they had counted her children and miscounted on purpose, or counted correctly and cut deliberately, and either way the army's arithmetic had concluded that a four-year-old girl was not worth a plank seat across a frozen river.

She sat down on the flour bin and laughed — one short, ugly syllable of it — and then pressed her knuckles against her mouth because Lita was asleep one wall away.

Something in her chest changed shape at that moment, and it never entirely changed back. She had spent six weeks since Elias's funeral being afraid in a general way — afraid of soldiers, afraid of winter, afraid of the way Noor looked when he came in late with ink on his ear. It had been a foggy fear, comfortable almost, the kind you can carry while still making bread.

This was not foggy. This was a specific fear with a specific answer, and the answer was ferocity. They had made a mistake that mothers are built to correct. There were three berths and four Vasquezes, and somewhere between here and that ferry dock, Amaya intended to make it four berths and four Vasquezes, by petition, by bribery, by forgery if it came to it — and oh, she knew who in this house could teach her forgery — or by standing on the dock with such a face on that some requisition clerk would rather invent a fourth berth than endure looking at her.

But all of that began with a condition, and the condition was the thing she couldn't say out loud even to herself in a locked pantry:

It only worked if they left before everything broke. Before the thaw, before the armistice, before whatever Noor was hiding under his forearm came out into the light and made this valley a battlefield instead of merely a trap.

If she showed him the papers, he would do the arithmetic himself. Three plus him equaled none of them safe, or three minus him equaled three of them safe, and her brother — who had inherited a dead man's atlas and a dead man's war and wore both like a coat he refused to take off — her brother would stand in that kitchen with ink on his ear and say something quiet and unbearable like *you go, all of you, I'm where I'm needed,* and he would mean it, and he would stay, and staying, in this valley, this winter, with those men garrisoned at the foot of the bridge, might very well be the death of him.

So she folded the heavy paper along its creases, and put it back behind the chilies, and blew out the candle, and stood a moment in the dark pantry hugging her own elbows.

*You're protecting him,* said one voice, her own reasonable one.

*You're lying to him,* said another, which sounded inconveniently like Elias, who had always been able to make two words carry a sermon.

Both were right. That was the trouble with guilt done properly: it didn't pick a side. It just moved in, and paid rent in sleep.

* * *

In the morning she was up early and aggressively normal. She baked — real baking, not fueling — because the smell of bread made a house tell lies about itself, made it smell like nothing was hidden anywhere. Noor came in drawn by the scent like everyone else in creation, and she fed him, and teased him about the ink, and asked cheerful questions about the survey work he claimed to be doing, and hated herself with great efficiency the entire time.

"Rosa wants to know if we'll get snow good for sledding," she said, sliding the last of the batter onto the griddle.

"Up on the pastures, probably. Ask me again after the next storm."

"If it comes to sledding, someone's breaking a wrist. Someone always does."

"Statistically, yes." He smiled into his coffee. And then, because he was her brother and brothers are fitted with an instrument for detecting exactly this, he looked up and said, "You're all right, Amaya?"

"Fine."

"That was fast."

"I'm a fast woman."

He laughed, and let it go, and she watched him go out to the well with the yoke over his shoulders like any peaceful citizen, and she thought: every day I don't tell him is a day I spend him without his permission. Every day I do tell him might be the day I lose him anyway.

She turned the flat cakes. Behind her, the flour tin kept its counsel. Out the window, far down the valley, the river ran gray and quick between its banks, carrying pieces of the first thin ice seaward, and above it the mountains had put on the white that meant *soon*.

Mateo came thundering down the stairs, smelled breakfast, and skidded to a halt beside her at the stove.

"Mama," he whispered, with the gravity of a magistrate, "is the secret still secret?"

"Hush," she said, and glanced at the door where Noor had just gone out, and pulled her son close, and kissed the top of his head, and lied to everyone she loved at once with a single smile.

"After breakfast," she said brightly, to the whole kitchen, to nobody, "I think we'll walk down and see how high the river's come."

She needed to know. The ferry manifest, the dock, the water itself — her new maps, though she'd have bitten her tongue off before saying so. Because if the storms held off another week, she could keep the envelope where it was, keep the peace a little longer, keep her brother safe from her own rescue.

And if they didn't — if the wind came down hard tonight and the snow started and the army sealed the bridge with her family on the wrong side of it — then she would have to choose, faster than she wanted, between the truth and her brother's life.

The wind, being winter, chose for her. By dusk the sky had gone the color of wet slate, and the first flakes came down thick and sideways, and far off toward the crossing she could hear — or imagined she could hear — the sound of hammers, soldiers nailing the season's first closure boards across the tollgate.

Amaya stood at the window with Lita on her hip and watched the storm roll in over the ridge, and thought, with terrible clarity: *Tomorrow I run out of Tuesdays.*
