## 4. The Bridge Closes

The storm came in on a Tuesday, the way bad news always did in our valley — first a rumor on the wind, then a gray wall over the western ridge, then everyone in town talking about it at once as if talking could hold it back.

I was in the workshop when it arrived, which is to say I was exactly where Elias would have found me if ghosts bothered with finding people. Three weeks since we'd buried him, and I still caught myself listening for his tread on the stairs, that particular creak of the fourth step he refused to have mended because, he claimed, a mapmaker ought to know when someone was approaching his borders.

"Fourth step's gone quiet," I said to the empty room. "You'd hate that."

The atlas lay open before me under the good lamp, its sealed folios waiting like doors in a house I'd suddenly inherited but hadn't yet dared to walk through. I had opened two of them in three weeks. Two. Grief, I was learning, moves at the speed of ink drying — you cannot rush it, and if you try, everything smears.

Outside, the wind began to sing against the shutters in that low, hollow note the river valley made when winter decided to stop being polite about it.

* * *

By evening the whole town had crowded into Delia's tavern, because when the world gets small, people huddle toward warmth and other people, in roughly that order. Amaya had dragged me out of the workshop by the sleeve — my sister has never once in her life asked me to do anything; she informs me, and my body complies before my mind files an objection.

"You look like a candle left burning in an empty window," she said, steering me through the door. "Sit. Drink something warm. Talk to living people."

"Living people talk about the weather."

"It's a very significant weather."

And it was. The storm had come down hard and fast off the passes, and with it the cold — the kind of cold that got into iron and timber and made them shrink and groan like an old ship. The bridge across the Vessel River was the only way out of the valley: one span, eighty years old, built by engineers who had never met our winters. When the ice ran thick in the current and the wind got its teeth into the cables, the crossing closed. Every year it closed. Usually for a week, sometimes two.

But old Mateo the ferryman stood by the fire with a mug in both hands and a face like a man doing arithmetic he didn't like, and when Amaya pressed him, he said what everyone was afraid to say.

"Early this year. Earliest I've seen. And the water's already running with pancake ice." He held up his hands to show how wide. "If it seals early, it seals *deep*. Could be March before a boat moves. Could be April."

The room went quiet in that particular way rooms do when a whole town recalculates its future at once. Eighty souls or so, give or take the garrison.

Ah, yes. The garrison. I should mention them, because they were the second thing the storm had brought us, and unlike the storm they did not blow through.

* * *

Soldiers had come up the valley road ten days after Elias's funeral — a survey detachment, their papers said, sent to "verify boundary markers" ahead of armistice negotiations. Twelve men under a lieutenant with a voice like a filing cabinet. They had requisitioned the granary at the bridge's foot and spent their days walking the farmland with instruments on tripods, measuring things that had been measured correctly for a hundred years.

Elias's maps were correct. I knew that now in a way that sat in my chest like a swallowed stone. I knew what the army wanted verified, and I knew why verification might require a mapmaker to fall down stairs he'd climbed safely ten thousand times.

The official finding had been an accident. The official finding was, I suspected, drawn up by the same hand that drew the new border lines.

So when the storm shut the bridge and stranded twelve soldiers on our side of the river for the winter — well. A town can survive being trapped with weather. Being trapped with weather *and witnesses* is another arithmetic entirely.

* * *

I saw Reyes for the first time properly on the third morning of the storm, though I had seen him before. Seen him once, at night, through the gap in our workshop shutters — a tall officer standing under the streetlamp while two others went up the stairs to Elias's rooms, holding his cap in his hands like a man at a funeral he hadn't wanted to attend. I had told myself afterward that I imagined the reluctance. Grief makes us generous to strangers.

He came into Delia's tavern that third morning with the lieutenant and two corporals, shaking snow off his greatcoat, and the whole room performed the small theater of pretending not to watch them. Amaya's hand found my arm under the table. Her grip said everything her face could not: *Sit still. Be furniture.*

Reyes ordered coffee. He drank it standing, looking out the frost-furred window at the bridge — or where the bridge had been; the fog was so thick you could see perhaps thirty feet of it before the world simply stopped, white and absolute. He looked at that blankness the way a man looks at a page he cannot read.

Then he turned, and his eyes crossed mine, and I watched recognition move through him like weather through a valley. Not surprise, exactly. Confirmation.

He knew my face. Which meant he knew whose apprentice I was. Whose atlas sat unfinished in the workshop above the chandlery, behind a lock Elias had changed the week before he died.

The captain crossed the room. The tavern went very quiet, the specific quiet of forty people trying to be eavesdroppers without appearing to have ears.

"Vasquez," he said. Not a question. "Noor Vasquez."

"Captain." My voice behaved better than I deserved.

"I'm sorry for your loss," he said, and here was the strange thing — it didn't sound like the phrase men are issued. It sounded rehearsed, but rehearsed the way a speech is rehearsed, not the way a condolence is. As if he had been carrying those four words around for weeks, turning them over, unable to find somewhere to set them down. "Elias Duarte was—" He stopped. Started again. "The finest cartographer in the province."

"He was," I said.

"The army will need his work. There are negotiations in the spring. Borders." A muscle moved in his jaw. "Accurate borders matter in negotiations."

"Do they," I said.

It was not really a question, and he heard that it wasn't, and for a moment we simply looked at each other across the space where a dead man should have been standing between us. Snow ticked against the window. Somewhere behind me, Delia wiped a glass with enormous, pointed concentration.

"The bridge is closed," Reyes said at last, as if reporting it to himself. "Nothing comes in. Nothing goes out. Until thaw."

"Until thaw," I agreed.

He put on his cap. At the door he paused, half-turned, and I will spend the rest of my life wondering what he meant to say, because what came out was only: "Keep warm, Mr. Vasquez. It will be a long winter for everyone."

Then the door swallowed him and the cold snapped in after and Amaya's fingers dug into my wrist hard enough to leave marks.

"Noor," she whispered.

"I know."

"We're trapped in here with them."

"I know," I said. And then, because gentle humor is the family disease and it surfaces worst at the worst moments: "On the bright side, so are they."

She did not laugh. Neither did I. But something in my chest had begun, quietly, to organize itself — the way a chart organizes a coastline, one bearing at a time, into something a person might navigate.

* * *

That afternoon I climbed to the workshop and did what mapmakers do when the world shrinks: I mapped it.

I took down the great valley sheet — Elias's master survey, inked in his careful hand — and pinned it flat, and looked at our prison rendered beautifully. The river, freezing north to south. The bridge, a single hairline stroke, now erased by weather no pen could argue with. The town, a cluster of tiny rectangles. The garrison at the bridgehead, marked in Elias's shorthand: *granary, 12 men, 1 lieutenant.* He'd surveyed it in October, before he died. Even then he'd been counting soldiers.

I worked outward from the center, the way he taught me. *When you're lost, Noor, don't chart the edges. Chart what you can stand on.*

What I could stand on: one town. One family. One atlas with sealed folios and a spine that I now noticed — for the first time in three weeks of looking — sat wrong on the table. Too heavy at the binding. Sewn thick, sewn recently. Sewn by someone with a reason.

I put my thumb against it and did not open it. Not yet. Fear and curiosity were arm-wrestling in my arms and fear was winning, but only on points.

Below me, boots in the street. I lifted the shutter's edge and looked down: Captain Reyes again, alone this time, standing in the falling snow and looking up — not at my window, I told myself, at the roofline, at the valley, anywhere but my window — a man measuring the walls of a cage he shared.

The storm had closed every road out of the valley. It had also, I realized, watching him turn his collar up against the white, closed every road *in*. No new orders. No reinforcements for whatever the granary men intended. For one whole winter, the truth in my workshop and the lie at the bridgehead were locked in the same small room together, and neither of us could leave.

The fourth step creaked behind me.

I turned, heart hammering — but it was only Tomas, the chandler's orphan boy who ran errands and swept floors and worshipped Elias's memory with the fierce, uncomplicated devotion of the young. He stood in the doorway red-nosed and snow-crusted, holding out a folded paper.

"This officer gave it to me at the door, sir. The tall one. He said—" Tomas frowned, reciting carefully, "—he said, 'For the mapmaker. Tell him accurate borders matter.' That's all, sir. Then he walked off toward the bridge."

I took the paper. It was not army stationery. It was a corner of a survey sheet, torn clean, and on it, in a hand I recognized — because a student never forgets his master's master, and Elias had taught us both, years apart, in this same room — was a single coordinate pair and three words:

*I know what's true.*

Tomas watched my face. "Bad news, sir?"

"I honestly don't know yet," I said, and reached for my coat, because some conversations cannot be conducted across a frozen street, and the world had shrunk small enough now that every remaining distance mattered.

Downstairs, Amaya called my name. Outside, the snow kept falling on the one bridge in the world, sealing it deeper.

I went out into the white to meet the man who might have killed my master — or might, just possibly, be the only soldier in the valley telling the truth.
