## 5. A Guest in Uniform

Captain Reyes had requisitioned many things in his career — barns, horses, a piano once, for reasons no one ever explained to him and he never thought to ask — but he had never before requisitioned a dead man's workshop while the dead man's apprentice watched from the doorway.

"You can't simply walk in," Noor Vasquez said. He was younger than Reyes expected, thinner too, with ink on his fingers and frost in his hair where he'd come too fast through the cold without a cap. "There are procedures."

"There are," Reyes agreed. He held up the paper so the seal caught the gray morning light. "This is the procedure."

He watched the boy read it. Watched the jaw tighten, the eyes move twice over the same line because the first reading hadn't been believed. Reyes knew that look. It was the look of a man discovering that the world he had carefully mapped did not match the world as drawn by someone with more authority and less honesty.

"Requisitioned for military cartographic services," Noor read aloud, flatly. "Effective immediately."

"The armistice negotiations require accurate surveys of the valley. Roads, bridges, elevations, waterways." Reyes stepped past him into the workshop, into warmth that still remembered Elias Marchetti the way a coat remembers its owner's shoulders. A kettle hung cold on its hook. Two mugs sat on the shelf, one chipped, both dusty. Someone had not been able to bring himself to wash them.

"And you need my master's workshop for this," Noor said behind him. "Not the garrison offices. Not the survey tent at the bridgehead. This room. His desk. His instruments."

"I need his maps."

The words came out more plainly than Reyes had intended. That was the trouble with this town, this frozen valley, this whole assignment: it kept pulling plainness out of him like a splinter. In the capital he could speak all day and say nothing. Here the air itself seemed to object to evasion.

Noor moved to stand between him and the long cabinet against the north wall. It was not a subtle maneuver. It was, in fact, the maneuver of a man who had spent his life around maps and understood exactly which point in a room was worth defending.

"The official maps are adequate," Noor said.

"The official maps are wrong."

Silence. The stove ticked as it cooled. Somewhere outside, a soldier laughed at something another soldier would deny saying later, and a cart horse stamped ice off its shoes.

*Wrong.* Reyes had said the word aloud in a room containing exactly one other person, and that person was Elias Marchetti's heir. He could have kicked himself down the stairs. Aldana would have had him flogged for less than what he'd just done with four syllables.

But there was a second feeling underneath the self-reproach, and it took Reyes a moment to name it, because it had been so long since he'd felt it. Relief. The specific, shameful relief of a man who has been carrying a heavy thing alone in the dark and finally sets it down, even if only to pick it up again in front of a witness.

"Wrong how?" Noor asked quietly.

"Captain," Reyes said, using his own rank like a shield, "I didn't come here to discuss cartography with an apprentice."

"No," Noor agreed. "You came to take it."

* * *

Reyes had trained under Elias Marchetti for eleven months in the year before the war ate everyone's plans. He had been a lieutenant then, ambitious in the harmless way young officers are ambitious — he wanted to be *good* at something rather than merely promoted — and the army had sent him to the old mapmaker because Marchetti's surveys were famous and the General Staff wanted their fame attached to their files.

He had learned three things in those eleven months.

First, that a map is an argument. Every line drawn is a line chosen; every choice serves someone. Marchetti said this on the first day, tapping the table with his pipe, and Reyes had nodded the way students nod, understanding nothing yet.

Second, that Marchetti could tell when a man was lying by watching his hands. "Your mouth does what your office requires," the old man said, catching Reyes mid-evasion about a deadline. "But your hands always file the honest report."

Third — learned last, learned too late, learned in fact only after the man was dead — that some mapmakers believe the argument so completely they will die for the drawing. Reyes had assumed it was a figure of speech, *a map is an argument*. Then he had stood in the rain beside the canal in November and looked at what remained of his teacher, and understood it was instead a job description.

They told him it had been thieves. River thieves, desperate men, a robbery gone badly. Reyes had stood there in the rain and looked at the arrangement of things — the body placed, the papers scattered but selectively missing, the careful theater of it — and his hands filed their honest report. Thieves do not fold stolen documents neatly before carrying them away. Thieves do not work in the rain with lanterns shaded military-style. Thieves, whatever else may be said of them, rarely leave a man's boots dry.

He had reported theft by unknown persons. His mouth did what his office required.

That had been two months ago. Now the war was dying of exhaustion rather than victory, armistice commissioners were being dispatched like apologies, and Captain Reyes — competent, thorough, conveniently local, and inconveniently aware of certain irregularities in the border surveys — had been assigned to support the negotiations with accurate geographic materials. General Aldana's phrasing. Reyes had read the order three times, translating it each time into plainer language, until the plain language stood revealed:

*Get Marchetti's true maps. Bring them to me. If they say what I think they say, burn them.*

And if they don't, the order did not add, then you have risked your career rummaging through a dead schoolmaster's drawers for nothing, Captain, and we shall both pretend this never happened.

* * *

"This desk stays where it is," Noor said. He had begun moving through the workshop as Reyes inspected it, closing folders, squaring papers, performing tidiness the way frightened people perform calm. "His instruments stay on their shelf. And the cabinet—"

"Is locked."

Noor stopped mid-stride.

"Is it not?" Reyes asked pleasantly. He ran a finger along the cabinet's edge, feeling the joinery. Marchetti had taught him that too: good furniture tells you where it keeps its secrets. The lock plate was newer than the wood. "Three locks, actually, unless he changed his habits. One visible, one disguised as a drawer stop, and one — I never found the third, and I'm embarrassed to admit it occupied me for a week."

"You were his student." Noor's voice had gone strange.

"For eleven months. He called me 'the soldier' to distinguish me from 'the boy,' who I understand was you." Reyes turned. "He said you had better hands. I resented it enormously at the time, mostly because it was true."

"He never mentioned—" Noor stopped himself. Recalculated. Reyes could nearly see the arithmetic: what did the old man say about me, and to whom, and does it matter now that he's gone. "He talked about his students?"

"He talked about his maps. Students came up mainly as complaints about our handwriting." Reyes allowed himself the smile; it cost something. "Though near the end of my time with him, he said something I've thought about since. He said the army draws lines and calls them borders, and God forgives soldiers because they're only following, but a mapmaker has no such excuse. He wasn't looking at me when he said it. I assumed he was talking about the weather. Old men get in the habit of saying important things where fools won't hear them."

The stove ticked. Outside, the wind came down off the ridge and tested the shutters like a landlord.

"The third lock," Noor said at last, "is under the drawer. Behind a false bottom in the pencil tray. You have to lift the tray out entirely."

"Ah," said Reyes. "Eleven months, and he never showed me."

"He never showed anyone. I found it by accident. I broke the tray."

"He'd have forgiven that. He liked accidents that led somewhere. He used to say error was just territory arriving early." Reyes heard himself quoting and made himself stop. This was the danger of the man's workshop: it kept making him into the person he had been at twenty-three, the one who wanted to be good at something.

He straightened, and let his posture do what his mouth had been doing all morning — except honestly, this time.

"Lieutenant Vasquez. Apprentice. Whatever you call yourself now that you've inherited the place." He set the requisition order on the desk, squared it to the edge with a precision that would have earned Marchetti's grunt of approval. "The army will occupy this workshop during the negotiations. Myself and two draftsmen. We'll conduct surveys of the river approaches for the commission. Your cooperation will be noted favorably. Your non-cooperation will also be noted, though less favorably, and the outcome will be identical, minus the courtesy."

"And the atlas," Noor said. "The unfinished atlas. Does your requisition cover that?"

There it was. Reyes met his eyes and saw the whole shape of the thing between them, laid out as cleanly as a survey: *I know, and you know I know, and now we discover what either of us is worth.*

"The atlas is precisely why I'm standing in your workshop instead of drinking bad coffee at the bridgehead," Reyes said. "The commission needs the border sections. All of them. The current ones and—" he paused, choosing, and chose, because the plainness had him again, or perhaps he had it— "and any corrections the previous surveyor may have been working on. In the margins. Under the official lines. Wherever he kept them."

The color left Noor's face in stages, like a tide going out. But he didn't step back from the cabinet. Reyes noted that. Filed it. *Hands filing the honest report.*

"You know what killed him," Noor said. It wasn't a question, and his voice cracked on the last word, and he hated the crack, Reyes could see him hating it, and Reyes found, unexpectedly, that he hated it too.

"Yes."

"And you're here to finish the job."

Reyes considered several answers. The requisition answer. The military answer. The answer that kept a frightened boy cooperative and a captain's conscience upholstered.

"I'm here because General Aldana ordered me to find those corrections and destroy them," he said. "And I'm telling you this because you deserve to hear it from someone who knows it's true, and because I am tired, Vasquez, profoundly tired, of being a man whose hands disagree with his mouth."

* * *

Afterward, Reyes could not have said why he'd said it. There were strategic explanations available — trust as leverage, confession as disarmament — and he deployed them to himself in the evening, at the garrison, over the bad coffee the bridgehead produced in industrial quantities. They were even partly true. But underneath the strategy sat the simpler fact: he had walked into Elias Marchetti's workshop, smelled the linseed oil and pipe smoke that ten years had not fully scrubbed from the air, and become again the lieutenant who had once believed that being good at a thing and being good were the same profession.

He had left that belief somewhere between the canal in November and now. He was beginning to suspect he might want it back.

* * *

The draftsmen arrived the next afternoon: Corporal Brandt, who was competent and mourned everything he'd left in the capital including, apparently, his laundry; and Private Osei, who was nineteen, brilliant with elevation contours, and constitutionally incapable of entering a room without commenting on its ceiling. The workshop received a ceiling rating of "adequate, suspiciously well-braced," and Osei was assigned to the window table.

Reyers established the fiction briskly. Survey of river approaches, soundings deferred until thaw, preliminary tracings from existing materials. The two soldiers worked. Reyes worked. And through all of it, Noor Vasquez worked too — because Reyes had required his assistance, formally, as a civilian consultant attached to the survey — three feet from the locked cabinet, guarding it with his posture and his silences.

They developed a routine within days, the way people do in confined spaces, the small accommodations of shared territory. Noor made coffee in the mornings that was markedly worse than the bridgehead's, and Reyes drank it and praised it, and neither acknowledged the ritual's meaning. Brandt complained about the damp. Osei rebraced nothing. The wind came down off the ridge nightly and the shutters held.

On the fourth day, tracing river channels at the long table, Reyes reached across for a weight and found Noor's hand already placing it exactly where he needed it, without being asked, without either of them looking up from the work.

Marchetti's old trick, Reyes thought. Watch the hands. He looked down at his own, holding the pen steady over the river's honest course, and wondered what they were filing.

On the sixth evening, sending Brandt and Osei back to the garrison early on a pretext involving a supply ledger, Reyes remained behind. Noor remained too, deliberately, arms crossed by the cold stove, and the two of them faced each other across the desk of a dead man who had taught them both, in different years, with different patience, and who had trusted the boy with the locks and the soldier with nothing but the argument.

"My family has evacuation papers," Noor said abruptly, as if continuing a conversation from days ago. "Berths on the ferry. I found them hidden in the flour tin. Amaya thinks I don't know."

Reyes waited. It was, he had learned from the old man, the most useful instrument in the kit.

"There are three berths," Noor said. "We are four people."

The stove ticked. The wind leaned on the shutters. And Reyes, who had come to this valley to bury one truth and found himself instead excavating others, understood suddenly that the question coming toward him across that desk was larger than the one he'd been sent to ask.

"Sit down, Vasquez," he said, pulling out the second chair. "Let's talk about manifests."
