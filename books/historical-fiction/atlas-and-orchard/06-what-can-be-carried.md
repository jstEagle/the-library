## 6. What Can Be Carried

Amaya Vasquez had learned to measure the world in armsful.

It had started the day she married Mateo and moved into a house half the size of her mother's, and it had been refined through two children, one flood, and the general principle that everything a family owns is either carrying you or waiting to be carried. Now, on a gray morning in early winter with evacuation papers folded small beneath the lining of her sewing basket, she stood in the middle of her kitchen and took inventory like a quartermaster preparing for a long retreat.

The copper kettle: two hands, warm even cold, worth its weight. The walnut dresser from Mamá's house: beautiful, beloved, impossible — four men and a mule, and even then it would sulk. The children's winter coats: already packed in oilcloth by the door, because some things did not wait for decisions. The photograph of Papá standing stiff as a fence post outside the survey office where he'd worked his whole life: one hand, thin as a wafer, and heavier than the dresser.

She set that last one face-up on the table so she wouldn't forget it, and kept moving.

"You've been at this since before light," observed her daughter Lucía, who was seven and missed nothing, sitting on the counter with her heels drumming the cabinet doors. "You put the spoons in a bag."

"They're good spoons."

"We have other spoons."

"These are *our* spoons," Amaya said, with the finality of a woman who had already argued with herself about it twice and lost both times, and Lucía rolled her eyes in a way that proved beyond doubt she was Amaya's daughter.

Little Mateo — Tío, they called him, to spare the household the confusion of two Mateos, though he was five and answered to nearly anything if it came with bread — was underfoot in the useful way of small boys, handing her items she hadn't asked for. A wooden horse. A burnt-down candle. One boot.

"Thank you," Amaya said gravely, accepting each, setting each in the pile that meant *no* but that she hadn't the heart to say aloud. The pile grew. The pile was mostly love, when she thought about it. Most piles were.

What could be carried. That was the whole arithmetic now. She had read the papers three times by candlelight while Mateo snored, reading them the way you read a diagnosis: slowly, then again, hoping the words would rearrange themselves into something kinder. *Three berths.* Not four, not five — three, on the last ferry out before the armistice froze the valley shut for who knew how long. Three berths for her family of four, which meant the papers were not salvation but a question, and she had spent two days refusing to ask it.

There was a fourth person in this valley who was hers, though he was thirty-one years old and would scowl if he heard himself counted among her dependents. And there was the matter of the atlas.

She could hear him now, through the wall, through the yard, across the frozen garden path in Elias Marchetti's workshop: the small industrious sounds of Noor working, which she had known since she was four years old and he was six, hunched over scraps of paper at the kitchen table, tongue between his teeth, drawing coastlines of places he'd never seen while she drew horses. He had never stopped. He had simply gotten better paper.

Burn it, she wanted to tell him. Burn the thing and come with us. What is a map against a brother?

But she knew, the way she knew the weight of the kettle, that it wasn't a map. It was Elias. It was the only inheritance a man with no children ever leaves, and it had teeth in it, and Noor had gone quiet in the way he went quiet before doing something irreversible. She had watched him go quiet like that when he decided, at eleven, to walk to the ridge to see where the river began. He'd come back after dark, feet bloody, luminous with triumph.

She tied off the bag of spoons and went across the yard to quarrel with her brother, because in this family love had always been conducted primarily through argument, and someone had to open proceedings.

* * *

The workshop smelled of ink and linseed oil and, underneath, faintly, of the apples that had rotted into the ground behind it every autumn for forty years — Elias's orchard going back to the earth, unbothered by anyone. Noor sat at the master's desk with a loupe screwed into his eye like a jeweler, surrounded by folios laid out in careful fans, and he looked up when she came in with an expression of such transparent guilt that she knew immediately the atlas was somewhere in the room more secret than the surface of the desk.

"Don't stop on my account," she said. "I've just come to watch you work yourself to death for a dead man."

"That's a cruel way to put it."

"It's an accurate way to put it. I learned accuracy from you." She hung her shawl on the peg by the door — Elias's peg; the workshop still organized itself around a man six weeks buried — and surveyed the room. Nothing here could be carried, or rather everything here could be carried and none of it should be, because the whole room was evidence. "Have you eaten?"

"I had bread."

"When?"

Noor considered the ceiling, calculating. This was his honesty: literal to the point of uselessness. "Yesterday? There was soup."

"There was no soup, there was broth with delusions, and it was yesterday." She went to the little stove in the corner where Elias used to warm his glue pot, and found — of course — that Noor had not touched it. She built the fire with the economy of long practice. "I'm making coffee, and you're going to drink it, and while you drink it I'm going to say something you don't want to hear."

"This is how all our conversations begin lately."

"Then we're consistent, and consistency is your religion." The stove ticked and caught. She crouched before it, warming her hands, and made herself turn around and look at him properly. He had lost weight since the funeral. His collar stood away from his neck. Her little brother, whom she had once fished out of the millrace with a boat hook and her heart in her mouth, was dissolving into somebody else's unfinished work. "Noor. The ferry comes before the thaw. There are berths on it. Real ones, with our names available on them, if we move quickly and don't ask too many questions."

His hands went still on the folio. "How do you know about berths?"

"I know about everything in this town. I'm the one who buys the salt." Which was true, and also a deflection, and she watched him notice both. She had never lied to him well; there hadn't been enough practice. "I'm saying: whatever is in those margins, it isn't worth staying for. It isn't worth *dying* for. Elias thought it was, and look where his convictions got him. Face down in his own workshop with the door locked from—"

"Amaya."

"—outside. Yes. I said it." Her voice had risen and she let it. There were times for lowering one's voice and this was not one. "Someone locked that door, and that someone wears a uniform now and eats at the garrison table at the foot of the bridge, and you sit here every night with the very thing he killed for spread out under a lamp like a Sunday supper. You think he doesn't know? You think the walls are thick enough?"

"He doesn't know what I've found." Noor's voice was low and infuriatingly level, the voice of a man reading coordinates. "And what I've found isn't a conviction, it's a fact. Facts don't stop being facts because they're dangerous. The army moved the border line. Forty years of surveys, redrawn. The Ortega fields — the Delgados' bottomland — the whole eastern terrace — Elias proved it, page by page, and they killed him for the proof, and you want me to burn it."

"I want you to *live*, which is a considerably rarer ambition in this family lately!"

The words came out louder than she intended and cracked in the middle, and for a moment neither of them said anything. The fire ticked. Somewhere outside, a soldier's boots crunched past on the frozen lane, unhurried, and they both listened to him fade like people listening for a second knock.

"Noor," she said, quieter. "Listen to me. When Papá died, you stood at the graveside and you said, *he taught me that a line drawn honestly is worth more than a life lived carefully.* You were nineteen and full of Latin. Well, I was there too, and I remember what else that day taught us, which is that the people left behind don't get to hold the beautiful idea. We get to hold the empty chair. Mamá held that chair for eleven years. I watched her. I am asking you, as the person who will have to hold yours — burn it. Take one of the berths. Come with us. Draw maps on the other side of the river like a normal man, maps of harbors you'll never visit, and grow old, and be my brother at my table, and let the valley keep its own rotten secrets."

He was looking at her the way he used to look at Elias's best instruments — with longing and refusal together. "If we run," he said, "the line stays where they drew it. The Ortegas lose their farm legally, permanently, with our silence as the seal. Tomás Delgado's daughters grow up on land their grandfather plowed, owned by some colonel who never saw it. And I will know. Every year, Amaya, I'll draw something, or read something, and think: I could have corrected this. I had the plate in my hands."

"You had children in your hands once too," she said, and saw it land, and hated that she'd thrown it, and did not take it back. That was the trouble with truth in this family: everyone handled it carelessly because everyone was certain it would survive.

Noor stood. He crossed to the window, where the frost ferns had grown up the glass overnight like a border of their own, decorative and merciless. Beyond it the valley lay white to the river, and the river lay grey, and the bridge — that single iron sentence connecting them to the rest of the world — wore its new garrison like a bad punctuation mark.

"Elias sewed a letter into the spine," he said at last, not turning around.

Amaya felt the floor tilt slightly, though it was perfectly level, having been so for a hundred years. "What letter?"

"One I haven't opened yet. I found the stitching two nights ago. I haven't been able to—" His shoulders moved; it might have been a breath or a laugh. "Two hundred pages of proof I can read straight through, and one sealed page addressed to me, and I can't make myself break the thread. Isn't that ridiculous?"

It was not ridiculous. It was the most sensible thing he'd said in weeks, and it frightened her more than everything else combined, because a man afraid of a letter is a man who already knows what it says. She rose and came to stand beside him at the window, shoulder to shoulder as they'd stood at a hundred windows, watching weather decide the valley's affairs.

"If I asked you to choose us over the atlas," she said, "you'd choose the atlas, and then feel guilty, and then find some third thing that lets you believe you chose us. You've done it your whole life."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed. "It's accurate. I told you, I learned accuracy from you." She leaned her head briefly against his shoulder, the old gesture, older than grief. "I'm going to pack my house, because that's mine to do. And you're going to open that letter, because it's yours. And Noor — when it tells you to do the dangerous thing, and it will, men like Elias always leave instructions for their own funerals — I want you to remember that you are not the only person in this valley who loves you. Some of us have done no planning whatsoever for your funeral and intend to keep it that way."

Behind them, in the yard, Lucía's voice rose in the particular shriek that meant Tío had done something spectacular, probably involving the woodpile. Amaya smiled despite everything, the way mothers do, automatically, at the sound of children being alive.

"The coffee's ruined anyway," she said. "You've let it boil. Like your judgment."

"And yours is perfect?"

"Mine keeps children alive. Yours draws lines. Between us we might manage something."

* * *

She walked home through the falling afternoon with her breath hanging in front of her like a curtain she kept walking through. The packing resumed, but differently now, the arithmetic altered. She wrapped the good spoons and then unwrapped them and put them back in the drawer — why, she couldn't have said, except that taking them felt like admitting the leaving was real, and leaving them felt like a bet placed against herself.

Under the bed, in the deepest corner, wrapped in a petticoat no self-respecting woman had worn since the flood, she kept the papers. Three berths. She took them out and read them a fourth time, and this time she noticed what exhaustion had hidden from her before: the manifest clause, the fine print about registration, the requirement that names be confirmed at the harbor office *before the thaw*.

Before the thaw. Which gave them weeks. Except that the thaw was a rumor, and rivers kept their own schedules, and Captain Reyes's soldiers at the bridgehead did not strike her as men who honored fine print.

She was refolding the papers when Lucía appeared in the doorway, coatless, solemn, wearing the face she wore when about to report a crime.

"Mamá. Uncle Noor is taking the big book down to the river."

Amaya's hands went still. "What do you mean, the river?"

"He's got it in the survey crate, the wooden one with the stencils, and he's putting rocks in with it. I saw from the window. He's crying a little, I think, or it's cold."

Or it's cold, Amaya thought, and also crying a little, because he was her brother, incapable of doing one simple thing at a time. She put the papers back under the bed, pulled on her coat, and followed the sound of her heartbeat out into the snow — toward the river, toward the crate, toward whatever her impossible family had begun without her.
