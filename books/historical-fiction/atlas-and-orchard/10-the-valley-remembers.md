## 10. The Valley Remembers

Amaya Vasquez had not intended to walk so far.

She had told herself she was only going out for lamp oil — a small, respectable errand, the kind of errand that made a woman invisible. The soldier at the corner of the mill road barely glanced at her. The grocer gave her oil and a heel of stale bread "for the children," which was the town's way of saying *we know you are counting spoons*. She took both with a smile she kept in a drawer for exactly these occasions, the polite one, the one that closed doors gently behind her.

But the oil was in one hand and the bread in the other, and her feet, which had lived in this valley for thirty-one years, apparently had opinions of their own. They carried her past the mill, past the last crooked fence post of town, and out onto the lane where the farming families lived — the lane that appeared on no map she had ever seen Noor draw.

That was not quite true, she corrected herself, walking. It appeared on his maps. She had seen it there as a girl, watching her brother bend over his drafting table with his tongue between his teeth, drawing roads with the seriousness of a priest. It simply did not appear on *the* maps anymore. The new ones. The army ones. On those, this whole stretch of valley was labeled, in small confident print, *UNIMPROVED TERRITORY — MILITARY RESERVE*.

Unimproved. Amaya said the word under her breath, tasting it. As if forty years of plowing were a smudge on paper.

The lane was frozen hard, rutted like an old scar. Smoke rose from chimneys she could name: the Herrera chimney, thin and gray; the Okonkwo chimney — no, that family had gone in autumn; the chimney of old Marta Ferro, who was eighty and stubborn as a fence staple and had announced at market that she would be moved only by undertakers, and even then they should expect commentary.

Amaya had not meant to stop at Marta's. Her feet stopped anyway.

Marta was on her porch, wrapped in a shawl that may once have been red and was now the color of experience, cracking walnuts against the rail with more force than the walnuts strictly deserved.

"You," Marta said, without looking up. "The mapmaker's sister."

"The same."

"Come sit before you fall down. You have the look of a woman carrying something heavy, and it isn't groceries."

Amaya sat. It was alarming how good the chair felt, how quickly the body betrayed its own resolutions. Marta pressed walnuts into her hands the way other people pressed coins into the hands of beggars — firmly, and with the implication that refusing would be an insult to God.

"I heard your brother finished his apprenticeship years ago," Marta said. "Yet here you all still are, in that drafty workshop."

"We're saving to build elsewhere." The lie came out smooth. Amaya had been polishing it since autumn.

"Mm." Marta cracked another walnut with a crack like a rifle going off, which Amaya tried not to flinch at, given current circumstances. "And the army is building where?"

"What do you mean?"

"I mean soldiers walked my north field yesterday measuring it," Marta said. "Stuck little flags in my ground. My ground, where my husband is buried under the pear tree he planted the year we married, and where my son is buried beside him because nobody would give me back his body otherwise. They put a flag in the middle of my graves and called the field unimproved." She brushed shell fragments from her lap with terrible calm. "I asked the young officer what he thought would improve it. He didn't answer. They never answer. Answering requires knowing something."

Amaya held the walnut she'd been given and did not eat it. Her stomach had quietly closed for business.

"They showed my Tomás a paper once," Marta went on, softer now. "My grandson. A map. He said, *Grandmother, this says our farm belongs to the reserve.* And I said, *Tomás, papers are written by men, and men die, and land doesn't.*" She looked out at her frozen fields. "I am beginning to wonder if I raised a fool. Land doesn't die. But it can be stolen while it's living."

* * *

Amaya meant to go home after that. Truly.

But the lane continued, and the afternoon light came slantwise across the valley, gilding the frost so that every furrow looked like it had been drawn in gold leaf, and she thought — unwisely, dangerously — *I will just see it. I will just walk as far as the Herrera place and then turn back.*

Serafina Herrera was hanging laundry despite the cold, because Serafina held the theory that sheets froze dry faster than they air-dried and that any man who objected to crisp sheets could be retrained. She saw Amaya at the gate and waved her in with the laundry pegs still in her mouth.

"Amaya Vasquez," she said around them, spitting the pegs into her palm. "Nobody walks this lane by accident anymore. What's happened?"

"Nothing. I was getting lamp oil."

Serafina looked at the oil. Then at Amaya. The look lasted approximately four seconds and contained an entire conversation.

"All right," Serafina said. "Come in and drink coffee you don't need, since you bought oil you already had, and tell me nothing about why you're really here. We'll both enjoy it."

Inside, the kitchen was warm and smelled of cinnamon, which Serafina claimed was for a cake and which Amaya suspected was simply for hope. Two other women were already at the table — Pilar from the dairy cooperative, and young Ines, whose husband was somewhere north in uniform and whose letters arrived monthly and said only *I am well*, which everyone agreed was either very reassuring or very alarming depending on the day.

They made room for Amaya without ceremony. That was the thing about the farming families — Amaya had forgotten, living in town among the shopkeepers and clerks, how little ceremony there was out here. Town people invited you to things. Valley people simply moved over.

"We were discussing the flags," Serafina said, pouring. "Again."

"There's a lot to discuss," Pilar said darkly. "They've flagged the orchard now. Benicio's orchard. Forty years of grafting. He keeps saying he'll talk to somebody official, and I keep telling him, Benicio, you ARE talking to somebody official, that's the problem, they're the ones doing it."

"My deed is in a trunk upstairs," Ines said. "Signed by a judge who is now dead, witnessed by two men who are also dead, stamped by an office that I believe has been turned into a tea house." She laughed, but it was the thin kind. "Sometimes I think about burning it. What's the use of a paper nobody reads?"

"Don't you dare burn anything in this valley," Serafina said sharply. "We have lost enough to fire and flood. Papers stay. Papers are patient."

"Papers are patient," Amaya repeated, without meaning to say it aloud.

Four women looked at her.

"My brother," she began, and then found she did not know how the sentence ended. *My brother has inherited a dead man's atlas full of proof that everything being done to you is written down somewhere.* She could not say it. Saying it aloud in this kitchen would make it real, would make it dangerous, would hang it over her children like weather. "My brother draws maps," she finished, feebly.

"He does fine work," Pilar said politely.

And then, because Amaya had come out here to learn something she hadn't let herself know, she asked the question sideways, the way women in this valley had always asked dangerous questions — over cups, under talk of weather. "Do you ever wonder," she said, "whether someone might fix it? The maps. If the lines were drawn wrong on purpose, whether anyone could draw them right again?"

Silence. The kettle ticked on the stove. Outside, a crow commented on everything with great authority.

"If someone could," Serafina said slowly, "they'd want to be very sure before they spoke. Because the last man who knew where the true line ran —" She stopped herself. Even now, months later, they said Elias Marchetti's name carefully, the way you stepped around ice.

"The surveyor," Amaya said. "Yes."

"A good man," Pilar said. "He came out here the spring before he died. Walked every field. Stood at my boundary stone and copied the carving on it into his book — the stone my grandfather set, with the date cut in it. He said, *Madam, stones don't lie, though men do.* I liked him." She turned her cup. "Then he was dead, and the new surveyors came with their flags, and nobody says stones anymore. Nobody says anything. You notice how quiet the valley's gotten? Even the dogs bark less."

Ines was looking at Amaya with the unnerving directness of the young. "Why did you ask that?" she said. "About fixing the maps. Why that question, today?"

Amaya looked into her coffee. She was, she realized, a poor liar in this company, among these women who read weather and soil and each other for a living. She could lie to soldiers and grocers. Not to this table.

"Because I think someone is trying," she said quietly. "And I think it might kill him."

None of them gasped. That was the farming way, she was learning. Nobody gasped. Pilar simply refilled her cup. Serafina got up and latched the door, casually, the way a woman latches a door when the wind changes. Ines reached across and covered Amaya's hand with her own chapped one.

"Then tell him," Ines said, "that we'll say nothing. Tell him whatever he needs — quiet houses, cellars, alibis, eggs. This valley remembers every acre that was ever taken from it. It will remember whoever stands up for it too."

"And tell him," Marta Ferro said from the doorway, where she had evidently materialized with the silent menace of the very old, having walked over from her porch on legs that pretended infirmity only at convenient moments, "that if he fixes my husband's grave back onto the map of the living world, I will bake for his funeral feast the best cake this valley has ever tasted. And I intend that feast be fifty years late."

* * *

She walked home in the blue hour, when the frost came back out of the ground and the smoke stood straight up from every chimney like drawn lines.

She had gone out for lamp oil. She was coming back with the weight of an entire valley sitting on her chest, and the strange thing — the thing she turned over and over as she walked — was that the weight did not feel entirely like fear. Some of it did. Fear for Noor, cold and specific as a knife. Fear of what she already suspected about the atlas on his workbench, the sealed folios, the hours he spent hunched over it at night with his door barred, coming out looking like a man who had seen his own future in the ink.

But some of the weight was something else. All winter she had thought of escape as the shape of safety: berths, ferries, a city where her children would grow up without checkpoints in their dreams. She had packed the household in her mind a hundred times, sorting their lives into *carried* and *left behind*. It was a comforting arithmetic. Everything reduced to weight and necessity.

Except now she had stood in Marta Ferro's yard, where a pear tree marked a grave the government insisted was empty ground. She had drunk coffee in a kitchen where four women were keeping, without calling it that, a census of everything the new maps had erased — deeds, boundary stones, orchards, sons. The valley had been keeping its own atlas all along, she realized. Written in memory and gravestones and the carvings on rocks, patient as paper, waiting for someone who could copy it out.

*Papers are patient,* Serafina had said. *Papers stay.*

And Noor had a paper. Perhaps the paper. And her brother — whom she had bathed as an infant, whose fevers she had nursed, who still ate standing up like a boy stealing moments — was carrying all of it alone in that locked workshop while she counted spoons and polished lies about saving money.

By the time she reached the edge of town her breath was ragged and it wasn't the hill. She passed the bridgehead, where the sentries' brazier glowed orange against the dark water, and she made herself look at it, made herself feel the cold fact of it: the way out, closing a little more each day, the river already wearing its collar of ice.

Home. Lamplight in the workshop window. Through the shutters, the shadow of Noor at his table, bent over the work, his pen moving in that slow deliberate stroke he'd learned from Elias — draw the line once, truly, and never go back over it.

She stood in the street a long moment with her bottle of lamp oil and her heel of bread, a woman who had gone out for one thing and come back carrying another.

Then she noticed, further down the lane, half in shadow beneath the bare sycamore, a second figure also watching the workshop window. Tall. Uniformed. Collar up against the cold. An officer, standing alone in the dark, studying her brother's light the way she had just spent the afternoon learning to study the land — as if reading something written there that other people couldn't see.

Captain Reyes lifted his hand — not quite a salute, not quite a wave — and turned away into the dark.

Amaya's heart went very small and hard in her chest. She climbed the stairs to the workshop, knocked twice — their old childhood knock, *shave-and-a-haircut*, from the days before anything needed codes — and when Noor opened the door, lamplight and the smell of ink behind him, she said the only true thing she had:

"Noor. We need to talk about what you're carrying. And then we need to decide — tonight — whether you're planning to take that ferry."

Her brother went pale, then whiter, and stepped aside to let her in.
