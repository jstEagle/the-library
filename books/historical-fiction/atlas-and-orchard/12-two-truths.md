## 12. Two Truths

General Aldana's headquarters had been a granary before the war, and it had not entirely surrendered its old profession. Sacks of grain still lined the far wall, and the smell of them — dry, sweet, faintly of dust and summer — filled the room like an accusation that the men in uniform were the temporary ones. Captain Emilio Reyes stood at attention before the general's desk and tried to think of the room as what it was: a barn with better stationery.

"You wanted to see me, sir."

Aldana did not look up. He was a broad man gone soft at the edges, the way a loaf rises and then settles, and he had a habit of reading documents with his lips moving slightly, as though tasting each line before swallowing it. What he was tasting now, Reyes realized with a small cold drop in his stomach, was a folio of maps. The workshop maps. Noor Vasquez's careful, beautiful, official work.

"Sit," Aldana said, without looking up, which meant stand. Reyes stood.

"The cartographer," Aldana said at last. "The apprentice. The Vasquez boy."

"Noor Vasquez, sir. Yes."

"He's good." Aldana turned a page. "Elias trained him well. Elias trained everyone well, that was the trouble with him." He closed the folio and folded his hands on top of it, and only then raised his eyes, and Reyes understood that everything before this had been the pleasant part of the meal. "Tell me about the atlas."

Reyes had prepared for this conversation the way he prepared for all his conversations with Aldana: by deciding in advance exactly how much truth the moment could hold, like water carried across a frozen field in an open bucket.

"He is completing his master's work," Reyes said. "The survey plates for the armistice portfolio. I've reviewed his drafts. They're precise. Better than we have from the engineering corps, frankly."

"Mm." Aldana picked up his pen, examined the nib as though it had personally offended him, set it down again. "And the border sections? The valley boundary?"

"Drawn to the provisional specification, sir."

Something flickered across the general's face — disappointment, or relief, or the particular expression of a man who has caught a fish and isn't yet sure whether it's the fish he wanted. "Provisional specification," he repeated. "Which is to say, the lines the ministry drew last spring. Which is to say, the lines I told everyone to draw."

"Yes, sir."

Aldana leaned back in his chair until it creaked like rigging. Outside, wind moved through the granary's eaves, and somewhere below a soldier laughed at something, briefly, and then remembered where he was.

"Elias," Aldana said, "was also very precise. Do you know what precision is, Captain, at the end of a war?"

"I imagine it depends on who it serves, sir."

"So do I." Aldana smiled, and it was almost warm, which was the most dangerous thing about him. "The ministry drew those lines for reasons that are above both of us and will remain so. When the armistice is signed on those lines, they stop being drawings and become the country. Every farm inside them becomes ours; every farm outside becomes somebody's story. That is not cruelty, Reyes. That is how peace gets made out of paper. It has always been made that way. Elias couldn't accept it. He wanted his maps to be more true than his country." He paused. "You remember what happened to Elias."

Reyes remembered. He remembered a night of rain, an order he had passed along without reading closely enough, or perhaps without letting himself read it closely, which amounted to the same thing in the ledger of his conscience. He remembered the workshop dark, and the sound — he did not let himself think often about the sound.

"I remember, sir."

"Good. Then you'll understand me when I say I'd hate for the apprentice to be as careless as his master." Aldana slid the folio across the desk. "When the armistice delegation arrives, I want clean plates. Official lines. Nothing else in the portfolio, nothing else anywhere. And if the boy should happen to have kept any of Elias's — corrections — any marginalia, any working copies —"

"He doesn't, sir."

"— then you'll find them." Aldana said it gently, the way one reminds a junior officer to polish his buttons. "Search the workshop after the signing. Thoroughly. Take whatever shouldn't exist and make sure it stops existing. Paper burns, Captain. It's the one honest thing paper does."

"And if he resists?"

Aldana looked at him for a long moment, and Reyes felt the granary shrink around him, the grain sacks lean in.

"Then he'll have learned the trade too well," the general said. "You trained under Elias too, didn't you, Reyes? Before your commission. I forget sometimes, you have such an officerly manner." He picked up his papers again. "That's all. Dismissed."

Reyes saluted. His hand did not shake, which surprised him. It was only when he reached the door that Aldana spoke once more, softly, to his documents rather than to him:

"The river thaws soon, Captain. After that, everything gets easier to move and easier to lose. Don't let the season get ahead of you."

* * *

Reyes walked back through the garrison town with his collar up and his thoughts in disarray, which was, he had long ago decided, the natural condition of an honest man in the army. Honesty, in his experience, was not a wall but a dam, and dams failed slowly and then all at once, and you rarely got to choose which kind of day it happened on.

He passed the bridge — shut now, furred with ice, sentries stamping at the foot of it — and passed the bakery, where the baker's wife gave him the nod she gave all officers, which contained neither warmth nor hatred, only arithmetic. He passed the schoolhouse requisitioned as a mess hall. He passed the window of a house where Amaya Vasquez was teaching her children some lesson, their heads bent together over something invisible from the street, and he quickened his pace as though the sight had scalded him.

Back in his quarters — a farmer's spare room, a bed, a desk, a candle stub — he sat for a long time doing nothing at all, which was the hardest work he knew.

*A map is a promise,* Elias used to say, back in the years when Reyes had been a boy with ink-stained fingers learning hachures and contour intervals from a patient man who smelled of linseed oil. *Anyone can draw a line, Emilio. The question is whether you'd put your name under it.*

He had put his name under a great many lines since. None of them were promises. Most of them were alibis.

And now there was the boy. Noor. Reyes had watched him across three weeks of requisitioned workshop and strained politeness, had handed him reference requests and received exact answers, and beneath the correctness between them ran something older and stranger — the recognition of two people who had loved the same teacher, standing on either side of the fact of his death. Reyes did not know whether Noor knew. He suspected Noor suspected. Suspicion was a kind of knowledge that could not be arrested.

And Reyes had seen the margins.

Not all of them. But weeks ago, turning the atlas's leaves while Noor fetched tea, he had caught a single glimpse of red ink beneath black — a river drawn twice, once obediently and once truly, the true course wandering east into farms that the official line had assigned to nowhere. One glimpse. It had lived in him since like a splinter working toward the surface, because he had known instantly what it was. Elias had spent twenty years correcting the ministry's geography in secret, and here was the inheritance, sitting in a workshop full of lamp oil and soldiers.

After the signing, Aldana would search. Whatever Reyes found, he would burn. That was the order, and orders were the currency he had chosen to live in, and the exchange rate never favored the buyer.

Reyes got up. He took out his own field kit — the leather case, the pens, the good paper he was technically required to account for — and laid it on the desk, and then stood looking at it the way a man looks at a rope or a raft, something that can save you or hang you depending entirely on the water.

What he did next he did quickly, because slowness would have killed it.

He began, from memory, to draw.

It was not forgery and not theft; it was worse and better than either — testimony. The fragment of the doubled river. The ridge line where the official boundary jogged inexplicably north, carving three farms out of existence. The churchyard he himself had surveyed in his first year of service, which appeared on no current map at all, as though a whole congregation could be un-churched by decree. He worked fast and rough, no artistry, just the facts his memory had been hoarding against his will for weeks, and as he worked he heard Elias's voice over his shoulder — *lighter hand, Emilio, the land isn't shouting* — and had to stop once, briefly, and press his knuckles to his eyes.

The candle guttered. Outside, the wind had turned, and he could hear it in a new register, wetter, softer. Thaw-wind. Days away now, not weeks.

He finished the last fragment and sat back, and looked at what he had made: four scraps of paper that constituted treason, or honesty, or both, depending on which side of the line — the drawn one or the other one — a man chose to stand.

That was the trouble. That was the whole trouble, laid bare under a candle stub in a borrowed bedroom. He had spent thirty years believing there was a line, and that he knew which side of it he lived on. Officer and gentleman. Orders and conscience. And now he saw the truth of it, the cartographic truth Elias had tried to give him when he was fifteen and too proud to take it: borders are not walls. They are arguments. And every argument must eventually be settled by someone willing to say which side of the river the church actually stands on.

He thought of Aldana's gentle voice. *Paper burns. It's the one honest thing paper does.* And he thought of Noor's hands moving over the drafting table, steady as tide, drawing the valley the way a man draws the face of someone he loves.

There would be a search. Reyes could delay it, misdirect it, salt it with false drawers and decoy folios — he was, after all, trained by the same dead man. Or he could warn the boy. Warning was the smaller treason and the larger risk; Aldana forgave incompetence, he even forgave disobedience, but he never forgave being anticipated.

Reyes gathered the four fragments, considered the stove in the corner — one match and the whole question would be elegantly resolved, the way Elias's question had been resolved — and then folded them instead into the lining of his uniform coat, over his left breast, where a less sentimental man would keep a photograph of his mother.

He blew out the candle and lay down in the dark and did not sleep, and near dawn he heard the thaw begin: a long cracking sigh from the direction of the river, like the valley clearing its throat before it spoke.

In the morning, he would go to the workshop. In the morning, he would look a young man in the eye and decide, finally and irreversibly, whose mapmaker he was.
