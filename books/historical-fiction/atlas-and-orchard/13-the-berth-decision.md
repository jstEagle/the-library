## 13. The Berth Decision

The thaw was coming. Amaya could smell it in the river — that green, muddy breath the water gave off when it began to think about moving again — and she hated it for arriving. Every morning of ice had been a kind of mercy. Frozen water meant a closed bridge meant no ferry meant the question could stay folded up in her apron pocket where she had been carrying it for six days like a stone.

But the ice was going soft at the edges. She'd heard it groaning in the night, a long low sound like an animal turning over in its sleep, and by noon there was open water past the second bend. The ferryman had been down at the dock with a pole, testing depths, whistling. Whistling men were dangerous men. Whistling men had good news.

So she took the papers out of the apron pocket and laid them on the kitchen table, and she waited for Noor to come in from the workshop, because this was not a thing she could do alone, though God knew she had tried.

* * *

He came in smelling of ink and lamp oil, his cuffs stained the particular gray-black of a man who has been hunched over copper plates all day, and he stopped in the doorway the way he always did, taking inventory of the room — Mateo's boots by the fire, Lucía's doll on the chair, Amaya's hands flat on the table the way their mother used to put her hands flat on a table before saying something that would change everything.

"The manifest," Amaya said. "I went to the dock office."

Noor's eyes went to the table. To the papers. He didn't move.

"There are three berths left on the last ferry," she said. "Not four. Not five." Her voice held steady, which cost her; somewhere below her ribs something was tearing steadily, like wet paper. "Three berths. Four of us if you count yourself, which I do, stubbornly, every day of your life."

"Amaya—"

"Sit down."

To his credit, he sat. He had always known when she meant it. Even as children, when he was up a tree or under the floorboards with Elias's practice plates, one word from her in that tone and he came down. It was the only authority she had ever wanted: not over him exactly, but over the part of him that forgot he had a body that could break.

She pushed the papers across the table. Three cream-colored sheets with official stamps, official signatures, an official date eleven days away. The last ferry before the armistice signing, before whatever Reyes and Aldana intended came to pass, before soldiers came to search the workshop. Eleven days. The ink looked so calm about it all. Official ink was always calm. That was how it got you.

"Read them," she said. "Then we're going to decide, together, out loud, like people, who takes them. Because I have been deciding alone for six days, Noor, and I am done. Do you hear me? Done. My teeth ache from clenching. I dreamed last night that I was counting us like spoons and there weren't enough and I woke up counting actual spoons just to be sure."

A small smile touched his mouth and died. He read the papers slowly, the way he read maps, top to bottom, edge to edge, as if looking for the lie in the margins. There was no lie in these margins. That was the terrible thing. For once the paperwork told the plain truth: three berths. One family. A valley full of neighbors who had no berths at all.

"Mateo takes one," Noor said finally. "Lucía takes one. You take one."

"And?"

"And," he said, not looking up.

"Noor."

"I've been thinking about this longer than you have, sister."

"Six days?" she said. "Because I promise you I've been thinking about it since the bridge closed, and I'll tell you what I decided in all those weeks of deciding, which is nothing, which is why we're doing this at the table like civilized people instead of me shoving you onto the boat and waving from the dock."

That startled a laugh out of him, brief and cracked, and then they were both quiet again, listening to the fire tick and the river mutter to itself down the hill.

* * *

Here is what Amaya had learned about impossible choices, having now made several: they did not arrive wearing horns. They arrived looking like laundry. Like a list of names on a cream-colored page. Like your brother's face across a table, younger than his years, older than his trade.

She named herself first, because she had rehearsed it.

"I go," she said. "I take Mateo and Lucía. You stay."

"You can't take three berths and leave me—"

"Two berths. Two children. Me. That's three, that's all three, and you stay here with your atlas and your war and your dead master's ghost, which is apparently the family you've chosen, and I will write to you. From somewhere with a postal service."

"That's not—" He stood up, sat down, stood up again. He was pacing now, four steps each way, the workshop pace, the map-drawing pace, as if the kitchen were a chart he was surveying for a route through. "You'd be alone out there. A woman with two children and no trade, in a country that's still at war, on papers that say this valley, that say *refugee from this valley*—"

"I've been a woman with two children and no trade since Tomás's father died, and I managed, which is more than you know, because I didn't tell you, because you had enough to carry." She hadn't meant to say that. It came out anyway, flat and true, and she watched it land on him like cold water. Good. Let him see she wasn't made of glass. Glass broke; she was cast iron, dented but holding.

"You should have told me," he said quietly.

"You should have noticed," she said, without heat. "That's different, and we're not here to settle it. We're here to decide who gets on the boat."

"Mateo and Lucía," he said. "That part isn't negotiable. The children go."

"Yes." Relief moved through her, treacherous, sweet — relief that he agreed, immediately followed by shame that relief was even possible, that agreeing to send her babies away could feel like anything but amputation. But it did feel like relief, and she let herself have it for one breath, because mothers were permitted exactly one breath of selfishness per crisis and she was taking hers. "The children go. Now: do they go with me or alone?"

"With you," he said instantly.

"Wrong," Amaya said. "Try again."

* * *

She laid it out the way she laid out sums, because Noor trusted numbers the way other men trusted saints.

"If I go, someone must stay with you or you must come too, and you won't come, so I stay with you, and the children go alone into the arms of whoever the ferryman hands them to. Eight and five, Noor. Lucía still calls the soup pot 'the soup house.' She cannot read a ticket. She will follow anyone who smells of bread."

"She could come back in spring—"

"When? After the armistice? If the armistice holds? If Aldana doesn't decide the valley needs fewer farming families and more garrisons? If the bridge reopens and stays open?" She shook her head. "You're mapping a coastline from memory, brother. I'm standing on it. I know what the water's doing."

He stopped pacing. He looked at her the way he looked at a plate he suspected had been cut wrong — that long, level look, hunting the flaw — and she met it, chin up, hands still flat on the table, while inside her the tearing thing worked at itself and she thought, *I am arguing to send my children away from me, and I am winning, and I hate that I can win this.*

"Then the children go with Tomas," Noor said slowly. "Tomas and — someone. An adult."

"Tomas is sixteen."

"Tomas has been running river cargo since he was twelve. He knows the ferry crew. He knows the town downstream. And he's family, Amaya, whether either of us ever got around to saying it out loud." His voice caught slightly on the name. The orphaned apprentice, Elias's other orphan, the boy who swept the workshop and ground the pigments and had cried once, only once, where Noor could see, and never again since. "Tomas takes a berth. Mateo and Lucía take two. And you—"

"No," Amaya said.

"—and I stay," he finished, talking over her now, both of them talking, the way they had as children, voices climbing the same ladder. "And I stay because I am the one holding the atlas, and the atlas does not travel well, and if it leaves this valley it leaves as evidence in somebody's strongbox instead of as truth in the world. You said it yourself. You're standing on the coast. Well, I'm drawing it. And if I draw it wrong, or don't draw it at all, then the berth you take leads to a country that will hand this whole valley to Aldana's line within a year, and your children grow up in a fiction. I won't do it. I won't trade the truth for our skins, Maya. I watched Elias die for less reason than this."

"You watched Elias die for *exactly* this," she flared, "and look what it bought him. A hole in the churchyard and an apprentice who talks to his maps more politely than he talks to his sister—"

"I talk to you—"

"You argue with me! There's a difference! Arguing is what you do instead of hearing!" She was on her feet now too, and the fire had gone low, and outside a dog was barking at the river as if it could scold the ice into lasting another winter. "You want to give me the noble arithmetic, Noor, the beautiful equation where the atlas is saved and the truth is saved and the children are saved and the only cost is me spending the rest of my life not knowing whether my brother is in a ditch. I've done that sum. Tomás's father did that sum. The answer is always the same. The answer is a widow doing sums alone."

The words rang out and faded. The dog stopped barking. Somewhere down the street a door closed, softly, the way doors close when whole towns are pretending not to listen to each other.

Noor sat back down. He looked, suddenly, very young, and very tired, and very much their mother's son.

"I'm sorry," he said. "About the maps. About the — politeness."

"I know." She sat too. Her legs felt strange, like the argument had used them up. "I'm sorry about the ditch. Mostly."

That earned half a smile. Then his face went serious again, and she saw him gathering himself, and she realized with a lurch that he was going to say something reasonable, something final, something that would end the argument in his favor the way careful men ended arguments, and she did the only thing left to her.

She begged.

* * *

"Noor. Look at me." She reached across the table and took his wrists, both of them, ink-stained and cold, and held on. "Choose. I need you to choose. Not the atlas, not the truth, not the valley, not the ghost — *you.* Choose who goes on that ferry, and say it out loud, and make it your decision, because if it stays mine I will spend every night for the rest of my life wondering if I chose wrong, and I cannot — Noor, I *cannot* carry that too. I've carried the household and the hiding and the papers in my apron for six days and I am telling you as plainly as I have ever told you anything: I am at the bottom of my strength, and the choice will break me if it stays mine."

His throat moved. Outside, the light was failing; the window had gone the color of old pewter, and the first lamp in the street opposite bloomed yellow, and she heard the ice on the river give another of those long shifting sighs.

"I can't choose between you and Tomas," he said hoarsely.

"You're not choosing between us. We're choosing among all of us. That's different, and you know it's different, stop pretending the arithmetic is harder than it is." She tightened her grip. "Say it. Say who sits where."

He closed his eyes. When he spoke, his voice had changed — steadier, lower, the voice he used when he was reading a contour line and the land had finally told him its shape.

"Mateo and Lucía take two berths. Tomas takes the third. You go with them — not as their guardian on paper, the papers won't care, but as their mother, on the boat, to the dock, to wherever you find shelter, because eight-year-olds don't cross a war on kindness alone and you know it better than anyone living. And then—" His hands turned under hers and gripped back, hard. "And then you come back on the ferry. It makes the return run, Maya. Same day, before dark, before the armistice closes everything. You put the children ashore with the Sisters at Saint Brigid's — I'll write to Mother Agnes tonight, Elias repaired her chapel roof twice, she owes this workshop favors — and you ride the ferry back, and you stand next to me when they come for the atlas, because I don't want to do any of it without you."

Amaya stared at him.

It was not surrender. It was not the answer she had rehearsed against. He had found a seam in the rock she hadn't seen, a route through that kept her body beside his and her children alive and the berths filled, and it was mad, and it might work, and she hated him for seeing it and loved him for seeing it and could not for the life of her find the flaw in the margins.

"The Sisters," she said, testing it. "Saint Brigid's."

"Mother Agnes. Tonight. I'll walk there myself, after the lamps."

"And the return ferry runs—"

"Weather permitting. Which in March means nothing. Which means you'd have to decide on the dock, in the wind, in ten minutes, whether to come back to me." He almost smiled. "I'm asking you to gamble a crossing, sister. Not a life."

Amaya let go of his wrists. She sat back. She looked at the three cream papers lying on the table between them, calm as ever, official as ever, and she thought about the dock, and the wind, and the ten minutes, and she understood that he had handed her the cruelest gift imaginable: a plan in which nobody was sacrificed, and therefore everything still could be.

"All right," she said.

He blinked. "*All right?*"

"All right. The children go. Tomas goes. I escort them and come back — God forgive me, I come back, I choose to come back, let the record show I chose it with my eyes open." She stood, and her knees held, and she gathered up the papers and squared their edges against the table, once, twice, the way she squared everything. "But Noor — hear me. If that ferry is late leaving, if the wind turns, if there's one thing on that dock that smells wrong, I stay on the boat and I send Tomas back alone to tell you why, and you will not get a vote. That's my price. That's the line, and I'm drawing it, and unlike some people I draw mine straight."

For a moment he just looked at her. Then he laughed — really laughed, short and helpless, the boy he'd been surfacing through the mapmaker he'd become — and came around the table and hugged her, hard, ink and all, and she held on and did not cry until his shoulder was safely past her eyeline.

* * *

He left for Saint Brigid's before full dark, coat collar up, and she stood in the doorway watching him go down the hill toward the lights of the town, and she let herself shake, briefly, privately, like a door rattling in wind no one else can feel.

Then she went in and started packing the children's things, because eleven days was not eleven days, it was ten and a bit, and everything must be measured now by whether it could be carried.

She had the little bundle half-wrapped when she heard footsteps on the porch — quick ones, uneven, and a knock that didn't wait.

Through the window she saw a uniform. Not Reyes's. A soldier, young, mud to the knees, breathing like he'd run the whole hill.

"Señora," he said through the glass, "is the mapmaker home? Captain Reyes needs him at the bridgehead *tonight*. General Aldana's men arrived on the north road an hour ago." He swallowed. "They're measuring the workshop for a search."
