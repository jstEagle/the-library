## 14. Counterfeit Coastlines

The trick to aging paper, Elias used to say, is not to make it old but to make it tired. Paper, like people, shows its wear in the places it has been handled most — along folds, at corners, wherever a thumb has worried the same spot for years. You cannot fake that with tea and an oven. You have to *wear* it yourself.

So on the first morning of my great deception, I sat at Elias's workbench with a fresh sheet of rag stock and wore it out by hand. I folded it and unfolded it until the crease went soft as cloth. I rubbed the corners between my fingers while I thought about Amaya's face when she'd begged me to choose who took the last berth, so I wouldn't have to. I held it near the stove until it smelled faintly of smoke, then set it under the window where the winter light could bleach it unevenly, one edge paler than the other, the way a map fades if it has hung for a decade in a surveyor's office nobody visits.

By evening it looked exhausted. It looked like it had survived something.

"There," I told the empty workshop. "Now you're ready to lie."

The workshop answered with the tick of cooling metal and the wind worrying the shutters. Somewhere below, in the town, soldiers were stacking firewood against the garrison wall, and Captain Reyes was — I had no doubt — thinking about me. That was the arrangement now, ever since he'd stood in this room with his gloves still on and said, with terrible courtesy, that he required the atlas. The true borders, the corrected folios, the proof that the army had redrawn the valley's lines to swallow farms whole. He needed them, he claimed, for the armistice negotiations. And then he would destroy them, because the armistice could not survive the truth any more than ice could survive the thaw.

I had told him I would consider his offer. He had given me until the signing. The thaw would come before the signing. Everything in this valley came down to which arrived first: the water or the ink.

* * *

Elias taught me forgery the way priests teach prayer — not as a skill but as a discipline of attention.

"Anyone can draw a lie," he said once, tapping my knuckles with his brass rule because I'd drawn a coastline too smoothly. "A lie is easy. A river doesn't curve like that. What's hard is drawing a truth well enough that a liar believes it." He turned my sheet toward the lamp. "You want Reyes to believe your forgery? Then don't make it perfect. Make it *his*. Give him the mistakes he expects to find."

So I built the false atlas the way you build a trap for a specific animal.

Reyes had seen Elias's work — I was nearly certain of it, though he'd never admitted as much. He knew the master's hand: the way Elias lettered his place-names with a slight forward lean, like a man walking into wind; the way his rivers thickened toward their mouths; the way he always, always left a margin of exactly two finger-widths, because Elias believed a map without room for correction was a map that had stopped being honest.

A forger's instinct says: reproduce all of that perfectly. Elias's teaching said otherwise. So I gave the false atlas Elias's lettering but leaned it a hair too far — the way a copyist leans when he's imitating rather than writing. I made the margins a half-finger narrow, the error of a man working from memory. On the third folio I let myself commit the mistake every apprentice makes in their first year: I drew the Río Serrano's tributaries branching upward like veins instead of downward like roots, because you forget, when you're copying, that water runs downhill even on paper.

And then — this was the part I was proudest of, the part I imagined explaining to Elias someday, in whatever workshop exists past the end of a life — I hid the corrections badly.

Because that was what Reyes wanted, wasn't it? Not a clean atlas. An atlas with the true borders in it, so he could destroy them and sleep, or hand them to Aldana and be believed. If I gave him a forgery with nothing hidden in it, he'd know instantly. But if I gave him one with the corrections sitting right there in the margins — visible, findable, *conveniently* findable — he would feel the particular satisfaction of a clever man outsmarting a desperate one. He would stop looking. Clever men, Elias said, are the easiest people in the world to deceive, because they trust their own eyes more than anyone else's.

Under the real border lines of the false atlas, in faded red ink that I aged with iron gall and a whisper of vinegar, I wrote corrections that were almost right. The Vásquez orchard line moved north — but too far north, past the millstream, a border so generous it insulted the intelligence. The Ferro holdings swallowed land that had never belonged to anyone but the river itself. Every correction was plausible, flattering to the army's greed, and wrong by exactly the amount a man in a hurry would fail to check.

I worked three days. My hands cramped. I burned through six sheets before the first folio satisfied me, and when it finally did — when I held it to the lamp and saw a tired, honest-looking lie staring back at me — I laughed out loud, alone in the cold workshop, and the laugh startled a pigeon off the roof.

It felt good. That frightened me a little, how good it felt. Deception, done with craft, turns out to feel very much like devotion.

* * *

Amaya found me on the fourth morning, asleep at the bench with my cheek on the false folio and the true atlas wrapped in oilcloth beneath the floorboards under the drying rack, where I'd pried up a plank Elias had surely loosened for some purpose of his own long before I needed it.

She stood in the doorway with a basket of bread and a face like a closed door herself.

"You're doing it," she said. "You're building him his forgery."

"I'm building him a reason to leave us alone."

"You're building him a reason to hang you." She set the basket down harder than bread requires. "Noor. If he opens that book and sees through it —"

"He won't."

"Elias trained him too."

That stopped me. She watched it stop me, and I watched her watch, and neither of us pretended otherwise. "He never said —"

"He didn't have to say. I've seen officers' hands, Noor. Reyes holds a pen like a man who was taught to hold it properly and resents it." She pulled the stool over and sat, uninvited, the way she'd been doing since we shared a cradle. "You think you're the only one who learned from him?"

"Elias never mentioned other apprentices."

"Elias never mentioned his own birthday either. That man kept secrets the way other men keep receipts." She reached over and turned the false folio toward her, studying it with the frank, unsentimental eye she brought to everything — ledgers, children, brothers. Her finger traced the Río Serrano. "This tributary runs uphill."

"It's meant to."

"Mm." She looked at me for a long moment. "And if he notices it's *meant* to?"

"Then I've failed, and we run for the ferry, and you get your berth after all."

"That's not funny."

"It wasn't a joke. It was a contingency. There's a difference; ask any cartographer." I took the folio back from her gently. "Amaya. I have to do this. Not for the valley — all right, partly for the valley, I've stopped pretending otherwise, you'd only see through it. For Elias. He sewed a letter into the spine of that atlas telling me a map is a promise. I intend to keep his. And I intend to keep yours too — you and Tomás and Lucía on that ferry, safe downstream, before the thaw. Both promises. I've measured them. They fit."

"They don't fit," she said quietly. "Nothing fits anymore. That's what none of you mapmakers understand. You think everything can be drawn to scale." But she stayed another hour, and she ground ink for me while I worked, and when she left she took the burnt sheets away to feed her stove so there'd be no evidence in the ashes. Love, in our family, had always been expressed primarily through logistics.

* * *

The days took on a rhythm that I can only describe, looking back, as liturgical.

Mornings: the false atlas, at the front bench, in full view of the window, because a deception you hide is a confession and a deception you display is simply work. Any soldier passing on the street could look in and see the mapmaker bent over his commission, cooperating, useful, harmless.

Afternoons: the true atlas, up from under the floorboards, and here I permitted myself honesty. I corrected. I finished what Elias had started — the plate of the eastern terraces, the orchard boundaries, the little annotations in his hand that I now completed in mine, matching his ink, his pressure, his breathing. Two hands on one page, thirty years apart. Sometimes I forgot which lines were his and which were mine, and I decided that was the finest thing I would ever make.

Evenings: I aged. This sounds like a joke but it was the least joke-like part of the whole enterprise. I carried the false folios against my body inside my coat so they'd take on the warmth and faint dampness of a living owner. I read them at night by candle until the candle grease spotted a corner in a way no forger would dare plan. I dropped the atlas once — deliberately, wincing, apologizing aloud to it — so the spine would show the scar of a fall down cellar stairs. Elias's atlas had such a scar. Mine needed one too.

The work steadied me. That was the strange mercy of it. Fear had been living in my chest since the night Elias died, a cold tenant paying no rent, and craft evicted it hour by hour. When my hands were measuring, they could not shake. When my mind was calculating how vinegar behaves in cold air, it could not rehearse the sound of boots on the bridge. Precision, I discovered, is a kind of courage for cowards. You cannot tremble and rule a straight line at the same time. Try it.

On the sixth day I bound the false atlas.

I used Elias's second-best binding — good enough to be believed, not so fine that its absence from the shelf would be mourned. I sewed the signatures with linen thread, waxed, double-stitched at the headbands. And then, because I could not help myself, because devotion will out, I did one thing more.

In the spine, where Elias had sewn his letter into the true atlas, I sewed a slip of paper into the false one. Blank. Just blank paper, folded small, stitched in the same pattern, in the same place. If Reyes ever opened the spine of my forgery — and he might, he was trained, he would know to look — he would find the hiding place and find it empty, and he would conclude that Noor Vasquez had removed the letter, or never known of it, or destroyed it out of fear. He would conclude anything except the truth: that the letter existed, and lived in the *other* atlas, under the floorboards, wrapped in oilcloth, keeping its promise.

A map is a promise. A forgery, it turns out, is also a promise — a promise to the enemy that you are exactly as afraid and as clever as he expects you to be.

* * *

On the seventh day, Reyes came.

I heard the gate before I heard the knock — he announced himself the way weather does, by pressure. I had time to do the last thing: close the false atlas, set it square on the center table, weight it with the brass rule, and stand with ink on my fingers and the expression of a man interrupted at honest labor. Under the floorboards, the true atlas lay in the dark like a heart in a chest.

He entered without waiting for my answer to the knock, which I suppose is a privilege of uniforms. Snow came in with him and melted on the boards. His eyes went around the workshop the way a customs officer's go through luggage — shelves, drying rack, window, table — and settled on the atlas.

"You've been busy," he said.

"The army asked for maps," I said. "The army gets maps."

He crossed the room. I confess I held my breath, and I confess further that holding it was unnecessary, because he did not open the atlas. He put one gloved hand flat upon its cover, the way a man quiets a nervous animal, and looked at me instead.

"Do you know what I admired about Elias?" he said. "Everyone thought he was careful. He wasn't. Careful men make careful mistakes, and careful mistakes are invisible. Elias made *bold* mistakes. Mistakes with opinions." His hand did not move from the cover. "I've been wondering all week which kind of mistake you'd make."

My heartbeat performed a small irregularity that I hoped did not reach my face. "Captain," I said, "if you have doubts about the work, open it. Check it. That's what it's for."

"Oh, I will." He lifted his hand at last, and smiled — a thin, tired, genuine thing, which was somehow worse than a cruel smile would have been. "After the signing. I want to examine it properly, with light and time, and I want you standing beside me while I do." He turned for the door, and paused there, snowmelt dripping off his cloak. "One piece of advice, mapmaker, free of charge. When a man hides something under a loose floorboard, he should remember that floorboards creak. And this valley is very quiet at night."

Then he was gone, and the cold came in behind him, and I stood in my workshop surrounded by my beautiful, meticulous lie, understanding suddenly and completely that I had been caught — or spared — or warned —

— and that I would not know which until he opened the book.
