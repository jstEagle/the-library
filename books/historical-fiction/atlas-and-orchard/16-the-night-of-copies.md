## 16. The Night of Copies

**Noor**

The lamp between us had burned down to a thumb's width of oil when Amaya finally said the thing she had been circling all evening.

"You're doing it wrong."

"I have been doing this since I was nine years old," I said. "Elias put a brush in my hand before he let me hold a spoon."

"Then Elias taught you to flood the plate," my sister said, and reached across the printing frame, and with two fingers — the same two fingers she used to wipe oatmeal off her children's chins and flour off my collar — turned my roller ninety degrees and lifted it clean. "There. Even pressure. You're not painting a picture, Noor. You're asking the paper to remember something. Be polite to it."

I looked at her hands. Ink to the second knuckle. She had rolled up her sleeves an hour ago with the resigned air of a woman who had decided that if her brother was going to commit a crime, he was at least going to do it badly without her.

"You've done this before," I said.

"My husband's family printed wedding invitations in Obrera for eleven years." She set the roller down with terrible gentleness. "I know what ink does to a table. I know what it does to hands. I also know you were going to be here until dawn making forty smudged copies that any clerk could tell were forged, because you get so tender-hearted about every line you can't press hard enough."

"Tender-hearted," I repeated. It was not a word anyone in my family had ever applied to me. Stubborn, yes. Moonstruck. *The one who talks to paper.*

"Don't argue. It's true. You grieve over every river as if it might be offended." Amaya pulled a stool to the far side of the press and sat, and that was how the quarrel we had been carrying for weeks finally died: not with an apology, but with her taking the second shift.

We had perhaps four hours before the town woke and perhaps six days before Aldana's men came through the workshop door with a warrant and boots. Reyes's warning had arrived at dusk, folded into a delivery of lamp oil we hadn't ordered — a single line in a clerk's hand: *Search after signing. Count nothing on your shelves.* No signature. He didn't need one.

So we worked. The plan was simple in the way desperate plans are always simple: seed the valley with copies of the true borders before anyone could seize the originals. Forty copies, no — Amaya said thirty-two, because thirty-two was how many farms the falsified maps erased, and each copy should go where it would be defended like a deed, which is exactly what it was. One to the church vestry. One sewn inside the mattress at the Posada del Puente. One wrapped in oilcloth in the millrace, if old Ferran could be trusted, and Amaya believed he could, because Amaya knew things about this town I had never thought to map.

I pulled the first proof while she inked. Elias's border lines came up dark and sure, the corrected course of the Río Sal running where it had always run, before some officer's pen moved it three hundred meters east to swallow the Herrera orchards.

Amaya leaned over the wet sheet. She was quiet a long moment.

"That's Mateo Herrera's eastern field," she said. "Where his wife is buried. The new maps put her under army ground."

"Yes."

"And nobody in Obrera knows. They think the maps just — happened. Like weather." She touched the edge of the page, careful, the way you touch a sleeping child. "Print faster."

* * *

**Amaya**

Here is what I told myself at midnight, inking plates in my dead master-in-law's workshop while my children slept upstairs: I am not becoming my brother. I am simply refusing to raise cowards.

It wasn't entirely fair to Noor, but fairness is a luxury of people whose houses aren't scheduled for search.

For weeks I had been packing. Every object weighed against the question *can it be carried*, and most things losing, and my heart learning to lose them. I had begged him to burn the atlas. I had said words in this room that I would take back tonight, though not aloud — Noor and I didn't apologize, we simply worked side by side until the argument drowned in the labor, the way our mother used to end her silences with bread.

Now I pressed the roller and watched the true river come up out of the blank paper, and I understood, finally, why he couldn't burn it. This wasn't a map. It was a witness. And you cannot carry a witness across a frozen river; you can only carry its testimony, or fail to.

"Noor." I held up the third proof. "This margin note — Elias wrote 'surveyed 1911, witnessed by two elders of the Herrera grant.' Is that true?"

"He wouldn't invent a citation."

"Then we don't trim the margins. The notes go in every copy." I had been cutting the pages close, saving paper. Now I stopped. "If they come for these, let them come for the whole truth, not the tidy part."

My brother gave me a look I hadn't seen since we were children — the look of a boy discovering his older sister can read.

"What?" I said. "I married a printer's son. Some of it soaks in."

"It's not that." He wiped his hands and stood there a moment, awkward, the lamplight catching the ink stains climbing his forearms like a tide coming in. "It's that I've been so afraid for you. Since the papers. Since the berths. I keep thinking, if I can just finish it, just hide it well enough, none of this touches you. And instead you're in it. Up to the elbows."

"Noor." I took his wrist, ink and all. "The day they redrew the border, they put their line through my children's future same as yours. There was never a version of this where I stayed dry. You just finally let me in the boat."

He laughed — a wet, surprised sound — and went back to the press, and we did not speak of it again, because in our family love is a thing you do with your hands.

* * *

**Noor**

By two in the morning we had eleven good copies and one ghost. The ghost was my fault — I misregistered the second plate and the river wandered drunkenly across the farmland, and Amaya held it up to the lamp and said, "Congratulations, you've invented a new country. Shall we found it?"

"We could call it Vasquezlandia."

"Population: two fools and a cat." But she was smiling, and the smile did something to the room. The workshop had felt like a tomb since Elias died; tonight, with ink drying in rows and my sister humming something from Obrera under her breath, it felt like what Elias had always claimed it was — a place where the world got written down honestly.

At half past two she sent me up for bread and more of her coffee substitute, and while I was gone she did something to the press, because when I returned my next proof came out perfect and she looked insufferably calm about it.

We worked out the distribution like a campaign. Amaya knew every household on her list the way I knew coastlines — where the current ran, where the rocks hid. Señora Ruiz at the vestry, who feared God but resented the army more. The schoolteacher, Pilar, who would read the margins aloud to her oldest students if Amaya asked, and would ask nothing better. The ferryman's widow, who owed nobody anything and hated being reminded.

"They'll know it came from somewhere," I said. "Thirty-two copies. Someone talks."

"Someone always talks," Amaya said. "That's rather the point. We just need it to talk slower than the soldiers move." She paused, roller suspended. "And Noor — the real atlas stays out of this house. Not under the floorboards, not in the mattress. Somewhere no one who has ever met you would think to look."

"That's hurtful."

"It's practical. You are many things, brother, but surprising is not one of them. Wherever you're hiding it right now, I could find it in twenty minutes."

I did not confirm that the atlas was, at that moment, inside the false bottom of the paper cabinet, which she had indeed discovered in twenty minutes flat two nights ago. I changed the subject to drying racks.

Toward four o'clock the cold deepened and the fire needed tending and Amaya went up to check on the children. I heard her footsteps overhead, then silence, then her voice, low, telling them something — a story, probably, or a lie about why Mama smelled of ink. When she came back down her eyes were shining and dangerous.

"Mila woke up," she said. "She wanted to know what we were doing. I told her we were drawing the valley so it couldn't be taken away." She sat down heavily at the press. "She asked if she could help when she's bigger."

"What did you say?"

"I said the valley would be very lucky." Amaya blinked twice, fast, and reached for the roller. "Fourteen more copies. Then dawn."

* * *

**Noor**

The last copy came off the press as the sky behind the east window turned the gray of wet slate. Thirty-two sheets, plus the ghost of Vasquezlandia, which Amaya refused to destroy and which I suspected she intended to keep — a joke between us now, folded into a pocket like contraband tenderness.

We hung the copies to dry in rows along the drying lines, and standing back, the workshop looked briefly like a laundry full of rivers. Every one of them running where it should. Every farm on the correct side of the truth.

Amaya washed the plates. I sponged the table. Neither of us spoke, but it was the good silence now, the kind our mother made on baking days, two people tired in the same direction.

"There's ink under your jaw," she said at last, inspecting me. "You look like you lost a fight with a newspaper."

"I feel like I won one."

"You did." She wrung out the cloth. Then, quieter, without looking up: "When the men come to search — and they will come — you give them the forgery and you let me answer the door. Soldiers expect a frightened woman with children. They don't expect someone who spent eleven years around printers."

"Amaya—"

"Hush. It's not bravery, it's logistics." She snapped the cloth flat over the rack. "You are the heir. Elias chose you to keep the promise, and the promise needs you alive and unarrested. I need you alive too, but I'm willing to share you with the dead man for a few more weeks. That's what family is now. Not the ones you'd carry out of the valley. The ones you'd stand in the doorway for."

I wanted to argue. Instead I blew out the lamp, because the light coming through the window was strong enough to work by, and working by daylight suddenly seemed like the whole point.

Below us, far off, the river groaned under its ice — the thaw beginning its slow argument with winter. Six days, maybe less. And somewhere downstream, a crate with a survey stamp waited in the dark for what we would trust to it.

Amaya put her hand briefly on the back of my neck, the way she did when I was small and feverish, and said, "Sleep two hours. Then we deliver."

But we did not sleep, because even then there came a knock at the workshop door — soft, deliberate, and in no soldier's rhythm — and through the frosted glass we could see a figure in a captain's coat, standing very still, waiting to be let in.
