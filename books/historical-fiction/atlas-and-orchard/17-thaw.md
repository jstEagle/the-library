## 17. Thaw

The river spoke first.

I had lived beside it my whole life, and I had learned its winter silence the way you learn a sleeping child's breathing — so familiar that any change woke you. On the third morning of the thaw, the change came. A groan from under the ice, deep and tectonic, like the valley clearing its throat before saying something it had rehearsed all winter. Then a crack that ran the length of the water, sharp as a ruler snapped across a knee.

I was at the workshop window before I was fully awake, ink still on my fingers from the night before, watching the river shrug off four months of white.

"Amaya," I said to the dark stairwell. "It's breaking up."

She appeared with a shawl over her nightdress and her eyes already doing arithmetic — she had been doing arithmetic since November, when the evacuation papers arrived and the arithmetic became the only subject our family studied. "How long?"

"A day. Maybe two, if the cold comes back tonight."

"And the soldiers?"

I looked down the valley toward the bridgehead, where the garrison's cook fires made smudges against the pale morning. The bridge itself was still furred with ice along its cables, but the road beyond it — the road that had been a rumor all winter — lay open and black and waiting.

"They'll move as soon as their wagons can," I said. "Which means today."

Amaya did not say *then burn it*. We had had that argument in December, and again in February, and sometime around the Night of Copies we had stopped having it altogether, because we had discovered that we were not arguing about the atlas at all. We were arguing about what kind of people we intended to be, and she had decided — grudgingly, in lamplight, with ink stains on her own hands now — to be the same kind as me.

She said instead: "Then we don't waste the morning."

* * *

The survey crate had taken me eleven days to build, which was nine days longer than a crate should take, if you wanted it to be perfect. It was a plain pine box, the kind the army itself used for instruments, stenciled on one end with a surveyor's mark I had copied from a genuine case Elias kept for sentimental reasons and I now kept for practical ones. Inside, fitted into a bed of wood shavings, lay the atlas.

Not the whole atlas — you could not hide an atlas; an atlas has a spine and a shape and a smell that men who ransack workshops know how to find. I had broken it into its gatherings, wrapped each in oilcloth, and laid them flat like strata. Above them, to give the crate weight and purpose, I placed three brass theodolite fittings and a bundle of honest, useless field notes about drainage ditches. Below them, sewn back where no inspector would think to unpick a seam, went the pages that mattered most: the corrected borders, the red-ink margins, and the letter I had cut from the spine two months ago and read so many times the creases were going soft.

*A map is a promise; keep mine.*

Elias had written it knowing what was coming for him. He had chosen me knowing what would be asked. Some mornings that letter felt like a hand on my shoulder. Most mornings it felt like a boot on the small of my back, and this morning it was a boot, and I was grateful for it, because boots make you move.

Tomas came while the frost was still on the grass, slipping through the orchard gate the way he had learned to, all elbows and held breath, fourteen years old and better at secrecy than boys twice his age, which is what happens when your family is scattered by a war and you survive by noticing things.

"It's moving," he said. "Two wagons left the bridgehead before dawn. Reyes's orderly told the ferryman there'd be 'an inspection of premises' by noon." He said *inspection* the way you say *hailstorm* — not as an accusation, just weather to be planned around.

"The dock?"

"Fish market's open. Nobody looks at a man buying fish." He patted his coat, where a mackerel wrapped in paper gave the errand its costume. "Ferryman owes my uncle. He'll take the crate on the noon barge, no manifest, downstream to Herrada."

"No manifest," I repeated, and made him say it again, because Tomas had once nearly gotten us both arrested by writing his real name on a flour sack out of pride.

"No manifest," he said, wounded. "I'm not stupid, Noor."

"You are extremely clever," I told him, "which is worse, and requires supervision."

He grinned, and for a moment the workshop felt like it used to feel — Elias at the big table, me at the small one, the kettle going, the day shaped by careful work. Then the river groaned again, longer this time, and the moment passed through us and out the window toward the water, and we got to work.

We carried the crate down through the orchard on the garden barrow, under the bare apple trees that Elias had mapped once, purely for love, in a plate nobody had ever paid for. At the bottom of the garden the bank ran steep to the fish landing, where half a dozen skiffs knocked against the pilings and the river — freed ice grinding past in plates the size of doors — made every hull complain. The barge was already there, low in the water with crates of late-pickled cabbage.

The ferryman, old Bruno with his beard like a storm cloud that had given up, looked at our survey crate exactly as long as a man looks at survey crates, which is not at all.

"Noon tide takes it to Herrada," he said. "If it's instruments, keep 'em dry. If it's not instruments, I never saw it."

"Bruno," I said, "if it's not instruments, thank you."

He spat expertly into the current. "Your master mapped my father's boat landing when the army said there wasn't one. Put it on the official chart. Got my family compensation for the wreck of '09." He turned back to his ropes. "Noon tide. Don't be late."

We hoisted the crate aboard. The moment it left my hands I felt the absence physically, the way you feel a pulled tooth — a socket of air where weight used to be. Two months of nights, Amaya and Tomas and I bent over the packing, the oilcloth, the false seams; and now the whole inheritance of it sat on a cabbage barge, and all I could do was stand on the muddy bank and watch it be tied down among the barrels like something ordinary.

Tomas touched my elbow. "You're making the face."

"What face?"

"The face Master Elias made when a map left the shop. Like you're sending a child into weather."

"I am sending a promise into weather," I said. "It's close enough."

* * *

The soldiers came at half past eleven, which meant they had waited until the town was busy with market and then hurried anyway, because General Aldana's men had the particular punctuality of people who enjoy their work.

I heard them before I saw them: boots on the cobbles in the too-even rhythm of men who have been shouted at about marching, and then the knock — not a knock, really, a battering courtesy, three blows with the flat of a rifle stock.

There were six of them at the door, and behind them, hatless in the raw wind, Lieutenant Ossa, whom I knew by reputation as Aldana's broom. He had a paper in his hand and the expression of a man who hoped the paper made him innocent.

"By order of the Provisional Boundary Commission," he read, "premises registered as cartographic works are subject to inspection and sequestration of documents pertinent to—"

"You can come in," I said. "The door opens. It's been opening for forty years."

Ossa blinked at me. His men did not blink; they poured past me into the workshop like water finding cracks, and then they began — with the methodical joylessness of professionals — to destroy it.

I want to be honest here, because honesty is the trade I inherited. There is a version of this story where I stood serene while ignorance rummaged through my life, above it all. That is not the true version. The true version is that it hurt. It hurt to see a sergeant flip through Elias's field folios with fingers greasy from his lunch. It hurt to watch my drafting weights — brass birds, Elias called them, and he had named every one — swept into a canvas sack like so many dead sparrows. When a soldier wrenched open the plan chest and dragged out the great unfinished plate of the northern valley, twenty years of Elias's hand in every contour line, and rolled it wrong, against the grain, cracking the paper along the fold of the river — I heard myself make a sound, a short one, and the soldier looked up, startled, as if the furniture had spoken.

"Careful," I said. "That's worth more than your horse."

"Nothing's worth more than Corporal Vega's horse," said another soldier, and they laughed, and I let them laugh, because laughing men do not look in false seams.

That was the discipline of it, the part Elias had never taught me but that apprenticeship turns out to include: while they searched, I catalogued them. Not their names — their habits. The sergeant in charge liked dramatics; he held every document up to the light as though testing for secret writing, which meant he had been briefed about invisible inks and therefore understood nothing else. The young one with the plan chest was frightened of paper; he handled maps as if they might go off. And Ossa — Ossa kept drifting to the window, checking the light on the river, checking his watch. He was not here for the atlas. He was here to be able to report that he had come, and he wanted very much to be somewhere else before whatever happened next happened.

Good, I thought. Be somewhere else. It helps me more than it helps you.

They found the decoys, because I had built the decoys to be found. In the drawer where a searching man finds himself rewarded, they discovered a folio of maps — genuine old surveys, accurate, harmless, five years out of date — bound in good leather with the workshop's stamp. The sergeant held it up to the light for a full theatrical minute and announced, "This is it. This will be what Aldana wants." Ossa glanced at it, glanced at me — and here was the strange mercy of that hour, the thing I turned over all evening afterward: Ossa looked at me *too long*, and there was something in the look that was not triumph. It might have been apology. It might have been only a tired man's envy of anyone who still owned a workshop worth raiding. But he closed the folio, tucked it under his arm, and said, "That will do."

"That will do," I repeated politely.

"The rest is scrap," Ossa said, gesturing at the wreckage of shelves. "Leave it."

They marched out with their sack of brass birds and their leather folio of outdated truth, and the door banged, and the house exhaled.

I stood in the middle of the workshop among the leavings — drawers gaping, shavings of torn paper on the floor like the world's worst snow — and I let myself shake for exactly as long as it took the boots to fade up the hill. Then I got the broom. Amaya arrived ten minutes later with her shawl and her fury and started stacking folios without being asked, and Tomas came in through the orchard gate white-faced, saying he'd watched the whole thing from the wall, and why hadn't I signaled, and I said the signal was I didn't get arrested, and he said that was a terrible signal, and for a while the three of us cleaned and quarreled and were, in the way of families, entirely happy.

At two o'clock, Bruno's barge went downriver with the tide.

I stood at the fish landing and watched it shrink between the banks, the little survey crate invisible among the cabbage, carrying everything — the red margins, the true lines, the letter in the spine, the whole weight of a dead man's promise — toward Herrada, where a newspaper office kept a typesetter who owed Amaya a favor, and beyond Herrada a valley full of farmers whose names the army had erased from the land.

The ice ran with it, plates and shards, hurrying seaward like the whole winter's lies finally in motion.

"Go on," I said to the water, quietly, like a fool, like a mapmaker.

And then the bell at the bridgehead began to ring — not the thaw bell, not the ferry bell, the *alarm* — and up the valley road I saw riders coming fast, and behind them, dust where wheels would be, and Tomas, breathless again at the orchard gate, with the worst sentence of the season:

"Noor — they've closed the bridge. They're saying nobody crosses. They're saying the armistice party is coming — and there's a berth on the last ferry, today, *now*, and Amaya says you have to come to the dock *immediately*."
