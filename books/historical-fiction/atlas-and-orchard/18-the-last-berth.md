## 18. The Last Berth

The ferry landing smelled of thaw and tar, of river mud waking up after four months of pretending to be stone. Amaya stood at the foot of the gangplank with a carpet bag in one hand, Mateo's collar in the other, and the berth papers — her berth papers — folded so many times in her coat pocket that they had gone soft as cloth.

The *Merced* sat low in the water, riding heavy with the last cargo of the season: crates of preserved pears, a piano somebody had refused to leave behind even for an armistice, and forty-odd passengers who had spent the winter telling themselves they would go when the ice went. The ice had gone at dawn. There were no more excuses left in the valley.

"Amaya." Noor's voice behind her, careful. He had walked down from the workshop with her without saying much, which was unlike him, and he kept touching his coat where the atlas was not, because the atlas was already on its way downstream in a survey crate, and some habits of the body outlast the facts.

"I know," she said.

"You've been folding those papers since Tuesday."

"I fold when I think."

"You fold when you've already decided and you're waiting for the rest of us to catch up." He came to stand beside her. Her brother had always been good at standing beside people; it was in front of people that he went quiet as a locked drawer. "Mateo first," he said. "Then Lucía. Then we'll talk."

There was no arguing with that, which was why she had suggested it herself on the walk down. Children first. Whatever else the winter had done to them — the quarrels, the ink-stained nights, the terrible arithmetic of the manifest — they had agreed on children first the way two mapmakers agree on north.

She knelt at the gangplank and took Mateo's face in her hands. He was nine and trying very hard to look brave about it, which broke her heart more thoroughly than crying would have.

"Listen to me," she said. "Your uncle is going to give you a job. You are going to carry this bag, and you are not going to set it down, and if anyone asks, it contains surveying instruments worth more than their horse."

"It's got my socks in it."

"Socks are extremely valuable right now. Ask any soldier."

Lucía, fourteen and furious about being fourteen in a year like this, stood with her arms crossed and her chin up, doing her impression of her mother, which was better than Amaya liked. "And what am I carrying?"

"Your own self," Amaya said, "with dignity, please, and your aunt Rosa's address sewn into your hem like I showed you. If anything happens — anything — you get to Rosa's and you wait. You don't come back for us. You wait."

"And if you don't come?"

The question landed the way Lucía's questions always did: precisely, in the softest place. Amaya smoothed her daughter's collar down and made herself answer it honestly, because she had promised herself she would not lie to these children about large things; there were enough small lies in the world keeping the border lines straight.

"Then you wait anyway, and you become somebody astonishing, and you tell the story right. That is the whole duty of our family, Lucía. We tell the story right."

Her daughter looked at her a long moment, and then, horribly, hugged her — briefly, fiercely, the way teenagers hug, as though affection were contraband to be moved quickly — and walked up the gangplank with her spine straight as a rule line. Mateo followed, clutching the sock bag, looking back exactly twice, which Amaya counted, because counting things was how she kept from running after him.

Noor put his hand on her shoulder. She let it stay there.

"They'll be all right," he said.

"That's not the part I'm worried about."

"I know."

* * *

The boarding was slow and loud. A sergeant with a clipboard called names off the manifest; a woman argued about a birdcage; somewhere a baby decided the whole enterprise was an insult. Amaya watched the gangplank groan under feet and bags and thought, not for the first time, about weight — what could be carried, what could not, who could.

She had packed the household twice over the winter. Once in January, when the evacuation papers came and she hid them in the flour tin like a coward with a conscience. Once last week, after the night of copies, when she and Noor had printed the true maps until their fingers were gray and their quarrel had dissolved somewhere around the third hour into something easier and older — siblings working side by side the way they had as children, when the work had been mending nets and the stakes had been dinner. Everything in the house now lived in two states: packed, or given away. It was a strange way to keep a home, keeping it ready to vanish.

The sergeant called her name. "Vasquez, Amaya. One berth. Papers."

Here it was, then. She had rehearsed this on the walk down, rehearsed it in the bath, rehearsed it while kneading bread that nobody ate with any attention because everyone was thinking about ferries and forged coastlines. And still, when she pulled the soft-folded papers from her pocket and turned to her brother, her hands shook.

"Noor."

"Don't," he said.

"You don't know what I'm going to say."

"You're going to say my name and then try to hand me something. I know your openings."

She shoved the papers against his chest anyway, hard enough that he had to catch them or watch them fall. "Take them."

"Amaya—"

"Listen to me." Her voice came out low and fierce, the mother voice, the one that had survived two children and one war and any number of broken windows. "I have done the sums. You are the one they will hang if Aldana's men decide the atlas needs a witness. You are the one whose name is in Elias's letter, in his handwriting, as heir. And I am — I am a widow with a house half emptied and nothing left in this valley but a brother and a debt. My children are on that boat. This is not sacrifice, Noor, this is bookkeeping. Take the papers."

He looked down at them in his hands. For a moment — a long, awful moment — she thought he might take them. She saw him want to. She saw him imagine it, the way he imagined coastlines: the crossing, the far bank, Rosa's kitchen, Mateo's socks drying by a stove that wasn't theirs. Wanting isn't treason, she reminded herself. Wanting is just the heart doing its sums too.

Then he folded the papers once, neatly, along her creases, and held them back out.

"No," he said.

"Noor Vasquez, if you make me fight you on a public dock—"

"There's someone here who needs them more than either of us."

And Amaya turned, following his gaze down the landing, and saw Tomas.

The boy was sitting on a bollard at the end of the pier with his knees drawn up and a canvas roll clutched to his chest — his pens and brushes, Amaya realized, the apprentice's kit, everything the boy owned now that the workshop had been searched and the dormer room he'd slept in belonged to a garrison clerk. He was watching the ferry the way hungry men watch bakery windows: without hope, on principle. He had been orphaned before the winter; Elias had been the second father the war took from him. Nobody had put his name on any manifest. Nobody had remembered to.

"He's twelve," Noor said quietly. "He has the trade in his hands already — you've seen him draw. And he has no one. Not a Rosa in any town. Not a hem to sew an address into." He turned the papers over once in his fingers. "You told Lucía the duty of our family is to tell the story right. Elias used to say a map is a promise. Well. Here's mine."

"Tomas doesn't need to go," Amaya heard herself say, and hated how it sounded even as she said it. "He's nobody's son anymore. Nobody's looking for him. He could stay hidden in—"

"In a valley where the army burns farms to make their maps come true?" Noor didn't raise his voice. He almost never raised it. "You taught me this, do you remember? When Papá died and I wanted to run away upriver on a barge, you sat me down and said: family isn't the people you were born stuck with, it's the people you refuse to leave off the page." He smiled, a little crooked, a little wet. "I'm refusing, Amaya. He goes on the boat. You stay with me. That's the whole map."

Amaya looked at her brother — her difficult, quiet, maddening brother, who had spent a winter choosing truth over safety and was now, apparently, finished choosing anything smaller — and felt something in her chest unclench that had been clenched since the flour tin. She had spent months measuring every possession by whether it could be carried. It occurred to her, standing on the cold dock with the river running brown and free beside her, that she had been measuring the wrong inventory entirely.

"You're insufferable," she said.

"It's inherited."

She laughed, and it cracked in the middle, and she wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand like a woman swatting flies, briskly, before anyone could make a ceremony of it.

* * *

Tomas did not want the berth. This surprised no one who knew him and should have surprised everyone who didn't.

"I can't," he said, backing up a step from Noor's outstretched hand until he bumped the bollard. "Master Elias — the atlas — I'm supposed to help finish it. That's my apprenticeship. You can't apprentice by post."

"The atlas is in a crate on a barge," Noor said, "and the man finishing it is staying, and what you're supposed to do is live long enough to become a mapmaker, which requires, as a first principle, not being here when Aldana's men arrive." He crouched to the boy's level, the way he never crouched for adults. "Tomas. Elias chose you too. He just chose you differently. Someone has to carry the trade east, past the falsified lines, into the towns that will get new maps someday and deserve true ones. That's not exile. That's advance work."

The boy's jaw worked. Amaya saw him glance at the ferry — at Mateo peering over the rail, sock bag and all — and then at Noor, and then, unexpectedly, at her. Asking permission, or asking for rescue; with children it was often the same thing.

Amaya knelt down beside him on the wet planks, not caring about her coat.

"Here's what I know about you, Tomas Reyes—" she caught herself, because the boy flinched at nothing, at everything; the name was wrong, she'd used the soldier's surname by accident, God, the winter had scrambled all their names — "Tomas. What I know is that you sharpened every pen in that workshop every morning for two years and never once complained, and that you can hold a rule line steadier than men three times your age, and that when soldiers turned out your drawers you stood in the doorway with your kit under your arm like it was a heartbeat. That's not a boy who hides. But it is a boy who deserves somewhere safe to sleep, at least until the sleeping is safe everywhere." She pressed the papers into his hands and closed his fingers over them. "We tell the story right. All of us. Go tell it east."

Tomas looked down at the berth papers as if they might change under inspection. Then he did the thing that undid her completely: he bowed. Formal, workshop-style, the bow apprentices gave masters — head down, kit held to chest — to both of them together, as though they were a guild.

"I'll carry the bag," said Mateo's voice from above, matter-of-fact, already descending three steps of the gangplank before the sergeant waved him back. "It's instruments. Very valuable."

"Socks," said Lucía, from the rail, with devastating accuracy.

Tomas went up the plank. At the top he stopped, turned, and raised one hand — not waving, Amaya thought. Saluting the shore. Saluting the valley he was leaving the way you salute a colleague.

The whistle blew. Long, low, final as a signature.

* * *

Afterward, the dock felt enormous. Emptied of her children, of the boy, of the piano and the pear crates and the argument about the birdcage, it was just planks and pilings and the river hurrying south with everything that mattered aboard. Amaya stood at the very edge and watched the *Merced* turn midstream, small figures at the rail. She did not wave dramatically. She raised one hand and held it steady, the way she'd taught Lucía to hold a needle — no flourish, just presence — and she kept it up long after she couldn't tell which figure was which, longer than dignity strictly allowed, because some messages only work at distance if you refuse to lower them first.

When she finally let her hand fall, Noor was still beside him — beside her. Beside her. She was tired down to the marrow and she suspected she would cry properly tonight, alone, into the one pillow she hadn't packed. But underneath the exhaustion was something new and clean, like the smell of the thaw.

"We should go back," Noor said. "There's the signing tomorrow at the bridgehead. Reyes sent word at dusk — he wants us nearby. He wouldn't say why."

"Reyes never says why. He says 'be somewhere' and then history happens there."

"Yes." Noor hesitated. "Amaya. About the papers. If it had been your name I was refusing—"

"It was," she said. "That's the point. You refused me, and I'm going to be insufferable about forgiving you for approximately the rest of our lives." She hooked her arm through his elbow and turned them both toward the town, where the lamplighter was working his way up the hill and the workshop window glowed faintly yellow above the roofs, waiting for them, holding its shape in the gathering dark like a line drawn honestly. "Come on. If we're staying, we're staying properly. There's a floor to scrub and a door to mend and I intend to cook something with all the food we can't carry, and then tomorrow—"

Above the bridgehead, far downriver, a signal flare went up — white, then red, hanging in the violet sky over the water. Noor stopped walking. Somewhere ahead, boots were suddenly running.

"That's early," he said. "The signing isn't until morning."

The flare faded. In its place, bells — the garrison's bells, urgent and wrong.

Amaya tightened her grip on her brother's arm, and together they turned toward the bridge.
