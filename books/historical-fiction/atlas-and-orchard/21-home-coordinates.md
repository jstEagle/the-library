## 21. Home Coordinates

The ferry landing smelled of wet rope and river mud, and Amaya Vasquez stood at the end of it with her children's hands in hers and her berth papers still folded in her pocket, watching the boat that was supposed to carry her away grow smaller on the water.

She had put the children aboard. She had kissed their heads, adjusted Mateo's collar for the fourth time, told Lucía to be brave and polite and to eat whatever the kind woman in the gray shawl offered her. She had done everything a mother fleeing a valley does. And then, at the last moment, with the gangplank groaning under the feet of the final passengers, she had taken them both by the hand and stepped back onto solid ground, and the deckhand had called her mad, and she had agreed with him.

"Mamá," Lucía said now, tugging. "Why did we get off?"

Amaya looked down the length of the river, north toward the bridge, which stood open for the first time in five months, its timbers dark with thaw water, trucks already rumbling across it like a pulse returning to a numb limb. She looked south toward town, where the workshop sat on its hill with new glass in its windows and Noor inside it, probably hunched over the atlas, probably forgetting to eat.

"Because," she said, "your uncle is hopeless without us."

"That's not a reason."

"It is the best reason there is." She squeezed their hands. "Also because this is our home, and I have decided that no map, no soldier, and no boat gets to tell us otherwise. Now come. We have a house to get back to before your uncle starves among his papers."

Mateo, who was six and took everything literally, said, "Can papers starve you?"

"They can," Amaya said gravely. "I have watched it happen. It takes years, but it happens."

They walked up from the landing through streets that were already changing, the way streets do when fear lifts — shutters opening like eyes, a woman beating a rug off a balcony with the violence of someone who has been waiting months to beat something, two old men outside the bakery arguing about the war with the contentment of men who finally had fresh news to argue about. Someone had chalked the true border line on the wall of the customs house, right over the army's painted version, and nobody had washed it off. Nobody was going to.

At the corner of the square, Señora Ibarra caught Amaya's sleeve.

"You're the mapmaker's sister," she said. It wasn't a question. Nothing had been a question in this town for weeks; everyone knew everyone's story by heart now, had rehearsed it in kitchens. "The maps are back. The farms are back. My cousin's orchard — they'd written it into army territory, can you imagine, forty years of figs, redrawn into a garrison." She pressed a paper-wrapped parcel into Amaya's hands. Almonds. "For your brother. Tell him the whole valley owes him, though he'll never collect, he doesn't seem the collecting type."

"He isn't," Amaya said. "But I am. Thank you."

She carried the almonds up the hill feeling something she hadn't felt since before the bridge closed: light. Not happy exactly — happiness required forgetting, and she wasn't ready to forget — but unweighted. For five months every object in her life had been sorted into two piles: what could be carried, and what must be left. She had packed and repacked the same valise so many times the latch had worn smooth. She had taught her children to sleep in their clothes. She had measured her whole existence by the width of a ferry berth.

And then she had given the berth away — well, Noor had given it away, to Tomas, which was so perfectly Noor that she could have strangled him and embraced him in the same motion — and stayed, and discovered that the piles dissolved the moment you stopped packing. Some things couldn't be carried because they were too large. A valley. An orchard. A brother who talked to dead men through their handwriting.

* * *

The workshop door was unlocked, which meant Noor had forgotten it again, which meant he'd been working. She pushed in with the children behind her and stopped on the threshold.

They had left the place wrecked — that was soldiers' doing, the search after the armistice signing, drawers emptied, folios sliced, the great drafting table scarred where a bayonet had gone through a stack of blank charts. But in the ten days since, Noor and Tomas had been repairing it in odd hours, and the room had come back to itself the way rooms do: imperfectly, stubbornly, with visible seams. New glass in the east window, slightly greener than the old panes, so the morning light came in striped. Shelves rebuilt with lumber that didn't quite match. The smell of ink and cedar and wood shavings, underneath it all unchanged, as if the room itself refused to acknowledge it had ever been touched.

Noor was asleep at the drafting table.

He was asleep with his cheek on Elias's atlas — the real one, returned to him now, safe past any argument — and a pen still in his hand, and a half-drawn plate under his elbow, and an expression of such profound peace that Amaya almost hated to wake him. Around him the table was an archipelago of teacups, three of them, all containing something that had once been tea and was now essentially a plant.

"Tío fell asleep drawing," Lucía whispered, delighted. This was her favorite spectacle in the world.

"Hush. Let him." Amaya set down the almonds and began, quietly, to work.

This was the part no one wrote songs about, she thought, rolling up her sleeves and gathering cups: the coming back. The dramatic parts were over. The ice had broken, the atlas had sailed downstream in a survey crate and surfaced in the *Clarín*'s offices like a message in a bottle, the general's lie had collapsed in public with Captain Reyes standing at the bridgehead holding the proof up to the light. What came after was cups. Shavings. Windows. You swept the floor of the life you'd chosen and you kept sweeping it, and somewhere in the sweeping the choice became ordinary, and the ordinariness was the whole reward.

She was scrubbing the third teacup when Noor woke with a snort, lifted his head, and got ink on his cheek.

"You're supposed to be at the landing," he said blearily. "On a boat."

"I got off the boat."

"I know. Tomas told me. I mean—" He rubbed his face, spread the ink further, and gave up. "I know why you got off. I just thought you'd say it louder."

"I'll say it louder when you've eaten. When did you eat last?"

"There are almonds."

"Those arrived four minutes ago and you were unconscious." She put bread and cheese in front of him with the efficiency of long practice. "Eat, and then help me move the big shelf back against the north wall, and then you can tell me what you're drawing."

"Plate forty-one," he said, mouth full. "The confluence. Elias left it unfinished — there's a stretch of the river he never surveyed properly, he had it dotted, provisional. I walked it Tuesday. I'm correcting it."

"With whose feet? You nearly drowned in February walking a frozen river, and now you're wading it in spring flood—"

"I borrowed a boat," Noor said, with dignity, "from a fisherman who came and shouted at me until I agreed to take him along. The whole valley is auditing my fieldwork, Amaya. I have never been so supervised in my life."

"Good," she said. "You deserve supervision. You're a man who sleeps on priceless documents."

"It's not priceless anymore. It's public." He said it slowly, tasting it, the way he'd been tasting it for ten days. "Do you know how strange that is? I spent a winter hiding it under floorboards, and now schoolchildren copy from it."

"Then sleep on it with a clear conscience." She handed Lucía a broom. "Mateo, dust. We are restoring order, and none of us leaves until it's restored."

* * *

By afternoon the workshop had remembered its shape. The big shelf stood where it belonged, loaded again — Elias's reference volumes, the rolled charts, the jar of crow quill pens that Noor refused to discard despite the fact that half of them had nibs like broken hearts. Tomas arrived at four with his arms full of split bamboo for stretching paper and his usual expression of a boy perpetually astonished by his own good luck, and Amaya fed him before he could claim not to be hungry, because she had raised Noor and recognized the species.

They worked while the light went long and gold through the mismatched window. Noor at the drafting table, inking the confluence. Tomas trimming sheets. Amaya, who had announced she would merely tidy and had somehow ended up reorganizing the entire flat file, called out observations from within it.

"Noor. Why are there eleven drawings of the same orchard in here?"

He didn't look up. "Which drawer?"

"The second. All the same trees. Different dates, though — some of these are twenty years apart."

"Let me see." He came around the file and stood beside her, and she watched his face do the thing it did when it touched Elias's work: soften and sharpen at once. He sorted through the sheets. "Same orchard, yes. Behind the workshop. Elias drew it every year, sometimes twice. Same six rows, same well, same wall. Look—" he fanned them out on the table, and she saw it, the decades laid side by side, a tree here grown vast and then gone, replaced by a sapling, the wall mended in one drawing and crumbled in the next and mended again. "It's the only thing he drew that wasn't for the atlas. Practice, maybe. Or—" he paused, "or it was his home coordinates. The place he checked himself against. Every survey starts from a fixed point you trust. I think this was his."

Amaya looked at the orchards. Twenty years of one small square of earth, faithfully attended, in the strong careful hand of a man who had died for lines on paper and never once drawn a line dishonestly.

"And yours?" she asked. "What's your fixed point?"

Noor considered the question with the seriousness he brought to all questions of measurement.

"For years I thought it was him," he said. "Then I thought it was the atlas. But the atlas is public now; anyone can hold it. It turns out—" he glanced up, at the green-striped window light, at Tomas humming over his bamboo, at her, standing in the flat-file dust with her sleeves rolled — "it turns out my fixed point keeps moving around the room making tea and interrogating my eating habits."

"Don't flatter yourself. That's surveillance, not devotion."

"In cartography," Noor said, "they're identical."

Tomas, overhearing, snorted with laughter and tried to turn it into a cough, badly.

* * *

In the evening she sent the boys out to the well and stood alone in the middle of the workshop, which was clean now, ordered, whole in the way of things that have been broken and repaired honestly — seams showing, stronger at the joins. Her valise still sat packed in the corner of the room they rented across town. Tomorrow, she decided, she would unpack it. All of it. Put the winter clothes away as if there would be a next winter and they intended to be there for it, coats on hooks, boots by the door, the audacity of luggage opened.

Lucía came back in with the water and asked, apropos of nothing, whether they could plant something. The soldiers had trampled the yard.

"We'll ask Señor Ibarra for cuttings," Amaya said. "His cousin grows figs. Apparently forty years of them."

"Will we stay long enough for figs?" Lucía asked. "Figs take forever. Mateo says."

"Figs take exactly as long as they take," Amaya said, "and we will be here for every minute of it, so it will feel like no time at all."

From the drafting table, Noor cleared his throat. He had turned to a fresh sheet, and there was a particular tilt to his shoulders that Amaya had learned to read across a crowded room: her brother, embarking.

"Actually," he said, "there's something I've been meaning to show you both. Plate forty-two. The last one." He rotated the sheet to face them.

It was the orchard behind the workshop. Six rows of trees, the well, the wall — drawn in two hands, unmistakably. The confident deep strokes of a master, and threaded among them, lighter, learning, a second line following the first like an apprentice following a road: Noor's hand, finishing what Elias had begun, or perhaps beginning what Noor would finish. In the lower corner, in the margin where Elias used to hide the truths the army couldn't bear, were two small words in pencil:

*Home. Verified.*

Amaya looked at it a long time. Then she went to the cupboard, found the good lamp oil, and filled the lamp all the way to the top, because it seemed suddenly important that the light not run out tonight.

"Well then," she said. "You'd better sharpen every pen in that jar. Both of you. If we're staying, we're staying properly."

Noor smiled and reached for the whetstone, and outside, the river ran on toward the open bridge, carrying nothing away that mattered.
