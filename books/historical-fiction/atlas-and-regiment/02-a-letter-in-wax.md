## 2. A Letter in Wax

The solicitor came on a Tuesday, which was itself an omen, because nothing good had ever come to Saltmere Bay on a Tuesday. The fish market was closed. The tide was out and the flats stank of sulfur and rotting kelp, and the whole town seemed to be holding its breath against the heat.

Lior saw him first from the drying racks, where he was hauling nets heavy with the morning's catch. A stranger walking the shingle road toward the Winterbourne house: gray coat, gray hat, a leather case chained to his wrist like something stolen. He walked the way company men walked — not hurrying, because hurry was for people who could be made to wait.

"Winterbourne," the man said when Lior met him at the gate. Not a question. He consulted a card from his breast pocket as though Lior's face were not confirmation enough. "Mr. Pell, solicitor, acting under instruction of the firm of Harrow & Pell, Port Calloway." His eyes went past Lior to the house — the sagging porch, the salt-bleached shutters, the pearl shells piled by the door that his mother used for mending mortar. "Your mother is home?"

"She's at the wash."

"I'll wait."

He waited on the porch, refusing the chair Lior's mother offered with a small bow that managed to insult both the chair and the offer. Maren Winterbourne dried her hands on her apron and stood with her arms folded, and Lior stood beside her, dripping net-water onto the boards, and Mr. Pell unlocked his case with a small brass key and drew out a letter.

It was a beautiful thing. That was Lior's first thought, and it embarrassed him even then. The paper was heavy cream stock, thick as sailcloth, and it was sealed with wax the color of old blood, pressed with a crest he didn't recognize — a ship's wheel quartered with something else, some device too fine to read at a distance. The lettering on the front was copperplate:

*To the heirs of Edric Winterbourne, of Saltmere Bay.*

His grandfather's name. Dead thirty years. Lior hadn't heard it spoken aloud since his father's funeral, and hearing it now was like hearing a floorboard creak in an empty house.

"What is this?" his mother asked. She did not take it.

"A communication regarding certain property interests," Pell said. "I am instructed to deliver it into your hand personally, madam, and to witness its receipt. There is no obligation beyond that. You may read it or not, as you see fit."

"Property interests." Maren said the words the way she'd say *weevils*. "We're divers, Mr. Pell. We own our boat, our house, and what's under our fingernails. Whoever sold you my husband's family name sold you a bad debt."

"The letter concerns a charter," Pell said, and there it was — the smallest pause before the word, the care of a man handling something fragile in a room full of children. "A shipping charter, granted under seal in the year 'sixty-one. I'm not at liberty to discuss its contents. Only to say that the firm has reason to believe the Winterbourne claim is—"

"Burn it."

The word dropped between them like a stone into still water. Lior turned to look at his mother. Her face hadn't changed. That was the terrible thing about her, the thing he loved and feared in equal measure: she could say anything — *burn it, drown him, let the sea take the boat* — in the flat voice she used for weather.

"Madam—"

"You heard me. We don't want it. Take it back to whoever sent you and tell them the Winterbournes are dead to charters and everything else that comes in wax." She held out her hand, palm up, waiting.

Pell looked at her for a long moment. Then, with the unhurried precision of a man who had been refused before and would be again, he set the letter down on the porch rail instead of in her hand.

"I'm instructed to leave it regardless," he said. "Refusal of delivery must also be witnessed. It's all the same to the firm." He clicked his case shut. "Good day, madam. Good day, young man."

He walked back down the shingle road without looking at the sea once. Lior watched him go, and thought: *he never looked at the water. Who comes to Saltmere Bay and doesn't look at the water?*

* * *

His mother burned the letter that evening, in the iron pot they used for boiling shell.

She didn't do it secretly. That was the lesson, Lior understood later — she wanted him to see it. She fed the letter to the coals a corner at a time, holding it with tongs, and the cream paper curled and blackened and the blood-red wax bubbled and ran like something wounded, and the smell of it filled the kitchen, hot and mineral, like the inside of a furnace.

"You don't sign things you don't understand," she said, watching it burn. "You don't touch things with crests on them. Your grandfather learned that. It cost him everything but the boat, and the boat only because they couldn't lift it."

"Who couldn't?" Lior asked. "Mother, who is *they*?"

She gave him a look over her shoulder — the look that ended conversations in this house, that had ended every conversation about Edric Winterbourne since Lior was old enough to ask. But tonight something moved behind it. Something almost like regret.

"Eat your supper," she said.

He ate his supper. And all the while the letter burned down to ash in the iron pot, and Lior sat across from his mother and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water closing over his head, that his family had been *in* history all along. Not outside it. In it, the way a drowned man is in the sea — deeply, and against his will, and kept there by other people's hands.

* * *

But here is what his mother did not know, because she was watching the fire and Lior was watching her:

When she had carried the pot out to the midden behind the house, and the ash had gone cold enough to handle, Lior knelt in the dark among the fish bones and broken shells and sifted it with his fingers.

Most of the letter was gone. The fire had taken the words entire — whatever names, whatever sums, whatever year-old promises lived in that copperplate script, they were smoke now, and no sifting would bring them back. But the seal had been thick wax, pressed deep, and wax does not burn so much as surrender. Near the bottom of the pot he found a scrap of paper the size of his palm, scorched brown at one edge, and fused to its corner a lump of cooling wax with half the crest still legible in it.

He held it up to the last light. The wheel of a ship, quartered. And in the surviving quarter, where the wax had protected the ink from the flame, three letters of a word whose beginning and end were lost:

*—rid—*

Not a word. A fragment. A piece of a name, maybe, or a place, or a promise. It meant nothing. It meant less than nothing; it was garbage, ash-adjacent, worth less than the smallest seed pearl in his dive bag.

He wrapped it in oilcloth anyway, and sewed it into the lining of his diving belt, where he kept the one pearl he'd ever found that was too perfect to sell — white as a held breath, round as a small moon. The belt went under his bunk, under the loose board, where a boy keeps the things that make no sense and cannot be thrown away.

He told himself it was nothing. He told himself his mother was right, that crests and charters were for admirals and merchants, that history happened in Port Calloway and the capital and aboard ships with guns, and that the Winterbournes dove for pearls and paid their debts and stayed out of the reach of men like Mr. Pell and whatever firm had sent him.

But he lay awake that night listening to the tide turn, and the belief wouldn't sit right anymore. It was like a tooth with a crack in it — sound enough to chew with, if you were careful, if you didn't bite down hard. Someone had written his grandfather's name in copperplate and sealed it in wax and sent a man two days' travel to deliver it into their hands. Someone believed the Winterbournes owned something worth owning. Someone, somewhere, had decided that his family stood *inside* the map of the world after all.

And someone else — his mother's flat voice again, *burn it* — someone else had spent thirty years making sure they never found out what.

Three days later, a Meridian Trading Company sloop anchored in the outer roads, flying the factor's pennant, and a boat put off for shore carrying Mr. Corvane, who asked, very kindly, to be told whether the Winterbournes had received any correspondence lately.
