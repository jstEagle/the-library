## 5. Declared Enemy

The news reached Sable Ashkani in the form of a newspaper she was not supposed to be able to read, folded under a fishmonger's slab of ice to keep the ink from curling in the heat. She read it in the alley behind the shop while the monger argued with a customer about the price of mackerel, and by the time she reached the third paragraph her hands had gone cold in a way that had nothing to do with the ice.

*TREASON AT SEA*, said the headline. *ASHKANI HOUSE STRIPPENED OF CHARTERS — ASSETS SEIZED BY ORDER OF THE ADMIRALTY.*

There was more, but the words began to swim after that. *Persons of interest.* *Warrants issued for principals and agents of the house.* Her father's name spelled wrong, which somehow made it worse — as if they could unmake him with nothing more than a clerk's bad spelling and a stamp.

She put the paper back under the ice. She bought two mackerel she did not want, because a woman who read treason notices in alleys and then walked away empty-handed would be remembered, and being remembered was the one thing she could no longer afford.

Three years since she had left the northern capital. Three years learning to move like water through rooms full of men who owned the ships, the docks, the governments that blessed them both. Three years since the first whisper that her family's rivals were not merely ambitious but hungry, and that hunger had found its way into the ear of the Crown.

Now it had a headline.

*Saltmere Bay,* she thought, walking slowly, carefully, a woman with fish and nowhere urgent to be. *He is still in Saltmere Bay.*

She knew this because knowing things was what had kept her alive, and she paid for knowledge the way other women paid for bread. Lior Winterbourne had never left the port. He dove for Meridian's pearls, the same as his father before him, chained to the company's ledger by an indenture older than he was. She had wept for that once, when she was young enough to weep freely. Now she only counted it: one diver, one harbor, one promise made under a sky full of stars by two children who did not yet know what the world cost.

The morning tide ship sailed from Saltmere Bay every ninth day. She would have to learn which day it was.

* * *

She came into Saltmere Bay on a grain barge out of Corrun, riding low in the water and lower still in her own skin, dressed as a widow in gray wool that itched abominably in the heat. A widow could travel alone without inviting questions. A widow could keep her head down and her face shadowed beneath a plain black scarf, and if her eyes were the wrong color for a Corruni woman — too pale, too green, her father's foreign grandmother showing through after three generations — well, grief made people forgetful of details.

The bay opened before her like a hand she had once trusted.

It had not changed, and that was the cruelest thing. The terracotta roofs stepped down the hillside exactly as they always had, red-gold in the late light. The pearl-fleets rode at anchor in their patient lines, masts like a winter forest. Gulls screamed over the fish market. Somewhere on the water a diver's tender called out depths in the flat singing cadence she'd grown up hearing across the strait, and her chest tightened so hard she had to grip the barge rail.

Home. Not hers anymore. Perhaps it had stopped being hers the moment the Ashkani name appeared in print beside the word *treason*, or perhaps it had stopped years before that and she had only been too proud to notice.

She watched the harbor with a professional's eye as the barge slid toward its mooring. Too many men on the customs pier. Two of them wearing the slate-gray coats of Meridian Trading rather than any crown livery — interesting, and alarming, that the company's colors should fly where the government's ought to. A clerk checking papers twice over. A boy running messages between the customs house and the Meridian factor's office with a frequency that suggested someone was nervous.

Someone was looking for something. Or someone.

"Grain for the meridian warehouses," the barge master said to the customs man, and Sable stood among the deckhands with her eyes lowered and her heart beating in her ears like surf.

They let the barge tie up. They let the crew disembark. They let a grieving widow in itchy gray wool step onto Saltmere Bay soil for the first time in six years, and no one gave her a second glance.

That night she slept in the loft of a chandler's shed she had broken into at fourteen on a dare, and woke at dawn to count what remained of her money, and understood with terrible clarity that she had perhaps nine days before the net finished drawing closed along the coast. Less, if Meridian's reach was what she feared it was.

Nine days. One ship. One promise.

* * *

Finding Lior Winterbourne proved harder than finding Saltmere Bay itself.

She could not ask after him — a stranger asking about an indentured diver would be odd at best, dangerous at worst. So she did what divers' families did: she went to where the boats came in, and she watched.

For two days she haunted the shingle beach south of the fleet anchorage, selling nothing, buying little, a widow with time on her hands and the sea for company. She learned the rhythm of the dive crews, which tenders belonged to which boats, which men came ashore at dusk salt-bleached and exhausted. She learned that Meridian's overseers wore brass badges shaped like compass roses and that the divers did not look at them directly, and something old and hot kindled in her chest at the sight — Lior, answering to a badge, to a ledger, to men like these.

On the second evening, she saw him.

He came up the shingle with his gear slung over one shoulder, thinner than she remembered, darker from the sun, moving with the particular economy of a body that had learned to spend nothing it didn't have to. His hair was longer. There was a new scar on his forearm, white against the tan. And when he paused at the tideline to wring out his cap, he looked out over the water with an expression she recognized instantly, because she had worn it herself for three years — the look of someone standing in one life while staring hard at the horizon where another one might be.

Her breath left her all at once.

Every plan she had built on the passage north — the careful approaches, the coded messages, the way she would test him before trusting him — all of it dissolved in the space between one wave and the next. It was Lior. It was just Lior, and she was just Sable, and six years ago on a headland above this same bay they had promised each other something children should not have had the authority to promise, because promises like theirs could only be kept at a price neither of them had imagined.

She did not approach him. Not there, not on an open beach with Meridian badges glinting along the shore. But she followed at a distance, hood up, and watched him climb the crooked lane toward the Winterbourne house — their house, the little stone place with the blue door his mother repainted every spring — and she memorized his schedule the way she had memorized patrol rotations in the capital: dawn dive, return at dusk, market errands on the fourth morning.

The fourth morning, she left a note.

She wrote it in the cipher they had invented at twelve years old, letters shifted by the tide tables they'd both known by heart, and she tucked it into the crack in the sea-wall where the Winterbourne boys had hidden things since before either of them was born. The note said only: *Old rock, high light, after dark. An old friend owes you nothing and asks everything. — S.A.*

Then she went to the headland and waited, and discovered that waiting for Lior Winterbourne was the most frightened she had been in three years of genuinely frightening days.

Because the war could take everything else. The seized charters, the forfeited ships, the family name dragged through Admiralty ink — those were losses, grievous but survivable, losses she had already grieved in advance. But if Lior looked at her the way the world had taught him to look at traitors' daughters — if the promise had curdled in him, if prudence had eaten the boy who swore any ship, any tide — then she would finally understand that she had no home anywhere at all, and she did not think she could survive that particular drowning.

The sun went down behind her. The light on the old rock turned gold, then rose, then failed.

Footsteps on the path. Hesitant. Then quick.

"Lior," she said, before he could speak, before her nerve could fail, rising from behind the rocks with her hood thrown back so he could see her face plainly in the last violet light. "You came."

He stopped as if struck. Even at twenty paces she could see him remake her in the failing glow — the widow's gray gone, the disguise gone, six years gone — and she watched his mouth form her name without sound, watched the cap fall from his hand onto the rocks.

"Sable," he breathed. "Sable, they're saying—" He crossed half the distance between them and stopped again, as if afraid she might be smoke. "The newspapers. The Ashkani name. Tell me it isn't—"

"It is." Her voice held. She was proud of that; inside, everything was falling. "All of it, and worse that hasn't reached the papers yet. My father is fled north with a price on his head. Our ships fly crown colors now. I'm not Sable Ashkani anymore — I'm nobody, a widow from Corrun, and I will be nobody for the rest of my life if that's what it takes to stay breathing." She made herself say the rest. "I've come to call in the promise."

The wind moved between them, carrying the smell of salt and tar and the far-off fish market, the whole ordinary evening going on below while her world balanced on a boy with rope-burned hands.

"The morning tide ship," she said. "When does it sail?"

His face answered before he did. Something crossed it — fear, calculation, dread — and she saw him arrive at the same arithmetic she had arrived at on the barge: the watch doubled on the docks, the Meridian coats on the customs pier, the net drawing shut.

"Three days," he said quietly.

"Three days." She nodded slowly, and felt the fear in her chest do a strange thing — stop churning and set, hard and bright, like a keel laid true. Fear was useless past a certain point; she had learned that in the capital, in rooms where trembling meant death. What remained after fear burned down was simply the work. "Then I need you to do something impossible, and I need you to do it in three days. Any ship. Any tide." Her voice cracked at last, just barely, just enough. "You promised."

Below them the harbor lamps came alight one by one, and out on the black water the Meridian fleet rode at anchor, patient as a debt.

Lior picked his cap up off the rocks. When he raised his head, the boy she had sworn under starlight with was gone, and in his place stood someone she had never seen before — someone arriving, even now, at a decision.

"Sable," he said, "there's something you need to know about my indenture first."
