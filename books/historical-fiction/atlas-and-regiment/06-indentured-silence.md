## 6. Indentured Silence

The summons came at dawn, folded inside a diver's glove left on the Winterbourne doorstep — grey kid leather, far too fine for work, and tucked into one finger a card bearing only the words *Mr. Corvane requests the pleasure of Lior's company. Office of Meridian Trading, nine o'clock.*

There was no pleasure in it. There never was.

Lior dressed in his good shirt, the one without pearl-burns at the collar, and ate nothing. His mother watched him from the hearth with her hands knotted in her apron, and neither of them said what they were both thinking: that when the Company invited you gently, it had already decided what you were worth.

The walk to the Meridian house took him along the harbor road, past the drying racks where the morning's catch hung silver and stiff, past the pilers of shell heaped like small white mountains that would be ground into buttons in some factory he'd never see. The tide was going out. It always felt to him like the bay exhaling — the whole sea drawing breath before it spoke again. This morning he wished it would hold its breath forever.

Mr. Corvane's office sat on the second floor above the counting room, and it was everything the Winterbourne house was not: high-ceilinged, warm without being comfortable, lit by a brass lamp even though gray daylight came through shutters adjusted to admit exactly as much light as Corvane required and no more. He was a man made of angles and patience, silver at his temples, ink on his fingers that no amount of washing ever seemed to remove. He gestured to the chair across from his desk as if offering a gift.

"Lior Winterbourne. Sit, please. Tea?"

"No, thank you, sir."

"Good. It's terrible tea." Corvane smiled as though this were wit, then drew a folder from beneath a ledger bound in green baize. "Do you know how old your indenture is?"

Lior's hands went still in his lap. "Nineteen years. Signed by my father. I've seen the summary."

"The summary." Corvane turned the word over like a coin of suspicious weight. "Yes. Summaries. Your family has been reading summaries for two decades while the actual document sat in our vault." He opened the folder and rotated it to face Lior. "Today I thought you should read the actual document."

The paper was heavy, foxed at the edges, dense with clerk's script. Lior's eyes went down it the way a diver's eye goes down a line — looking for the snag, the thing that catches. He found it in the fourteenth clause, buried between provisions about tool maintenance and Sunday observance, in language so dry it almost hid its teeth:

*…the Indentured Party shall be held to render true account of all Persons of Interest entering or residing within the Port of Saltmere Bay, such Persons to be named on Schedule C annexed hereto; failure of report constituting breach of Contract entire…*

"Schedule C," Lior said. His voice came out level, which surprised him.

"Annexed," Corvane agreed, and slid a single sheet across the desk.

It was short. Three names. Two were dead men whose debts had died with them. The third was written in a hand that did not bother pretending neutrality:

*Ashkani, Sable — daughter of House Ashkani — declared enemy of the Crown, assets seized under writ of the Admiralty Court.*

The room got very quiet, the way water gets quiet right before it goes deep.

"You'll understand," Corvane said mildly, "that this clause predates current events. Your father signed it when Sable Ashkani was merely the daughter of a rival house, a girl who raced you along the breakwater. Nobody imagined she'd become a matter of state." He laced his fingers. "History does that, Winterbourne. It takes the people we knew and turns them into categories."

*She isn't back,* Lior wanted to say. *I haven't seen her. I don't know where she is.* Every word was true and every word was a lie's cousin, because he had seen her — two nights ago, in the shadow of the salt sheds, her hood back just far enough that he'd have known those eyes anywhere, storm-colored and unrepentant — and he had not reported it, and the not-reporting had felt like the first honest act of his life.

"I don't understand why you're showing me this," he said instead.

"Don't you?" Corvane leaned back. For a moment something almost human moved behind his face — fatigue, maybe, or the memory of being a smaller man in a bigger world. "Because I'm going to be honest with you, which is a currency in short supply around here. Because I think you deserve to know precisely what you are."

"What I am."

"An instrument, Winterbourne. A very well-maintained one. Nineteen years we've invested in your lungs and your shoulders and your ability to hold your breath longer than any diver on this coast. Do you think that's an accident? That your family's debt kept getting restructured, extended, forgiven in part, never quite extinguished? You're the best asset this bay produces, and the Company protects its assets." He tapped the contract. "And an asset that can also see, and speak, and be placed — well. That's not a diver. That's a watchman who dives."

Lior looked at his own hands. They were steady. He noted this from a great distance, the way he noted things underwater — pressure, cold, the direction of the current — data gathered by a body while the mind attended elsewhere.

*You were never paying down a debt,* he thought. *You were being kept.*

Kept poor. Kept close. Kept diving the Company beds while the indenture clause sat in the vault like a hook baited for years, waiting for the day a name on Schedule C swam within reach of it.

* * *

"The letter," Lior said slowly. "The solicitor. The charter claim. My mother burned it—"

"Yes, she did. Sensible woman." Corvane's tone didn't change at all. "We wrote it."

The sentence entered Lior like cold water enters a wetsuit — not all at once but finding every gap, filling every seam, until there was nowhere on him it did not touch.

"We wrote it," Corvane repeated, "because rumors travel faster than ships, and we heard the Ashkani girl might come home. We needed to know who'd shelter her. So we offered your family a poison apple and watched who reached for it. Your mother burned it — loyal, frightened, wise. But someone in your house kept a scrap of it. We know because our solicitor counts his seals, and one seal came back to us short a corner." Corvane smiled without warmth. "Sentimental, keeping a scrap. Or careful. Either way, it tells me the Winterbournes aren't finished asking questions. And questions, in my experience, precede persons of interest."

Lior kept his face still the way he kept it still when the bottom silt came up and the water went to brown blindness — no movement, no panic, wait for the current to clear. His heart was a fist against his ribs.

*He doesn't know she's seen me. He knows she's in the port, but not where. Not yet.*

"She hasn't come to me," Lior said. "If she does—"

"If she does," Corvane said, "you will come to me. Within a day. To this office, or to the harbor factor, or to any man wearing the meridian pin." He rose, and coming around the desk he put a hand on Lior's shoulder — a gesture that on another man might have been kindness. "Understand what happens otherwise. Breach of contract entire. Your mother's tenancy void. Your younger brother's apprenticeship revoked. The debt — the full original figure, with two decades of interest recalculated at the Company's discretion — falls due immediately." He squeezed, once. "I'd hate that. Genuinely. You've been useful to us, and I value useful things."

"And if I do report her," Lior said, each word placed carefully as a footing on wet rock, "what happens to her?"

Corvane released his shoulder and returned to his chair, and for the first time all morning he looked tired. "What always happens to categories, Winterbourne. Ships. Prisons. Sometimes less. The Crown doesn't distinguish between the daughter of a trading house and a smuggler of enemy agents, and I am not in a position to explain distinctions to the Crown." He opened the ledger again, dismissing him. "A day, Winterbourne. Consider it a tide. It comes in whether you're ready or not."

* * *

Lior walked out through the counting room where clerks scratched at columns of other people's lives, out onto the harbor road where the sun was fully up now and gulls screamed over the fish racks as though the morning were ordinary, as though the world had not rearranged itself inside his chest.

He passed the breakwater where a girl had once outrun him, laughing, her dark hair loose, and shouted back over her shoulder: *One day you'll owe me a passage, Lior Winterbourne, and I'll collect!*

He had promised. He had been eleven years old and he had promised, hand over heart in the solemn grammar of children, and he had thought — all these years, diving, hauling, paying — that promises were the one property a debtor was allowed to keep.

Now he understood the architecture of it. The whole shape of his life laid bare like a wreck at low tide: the debt designed never to close, the dive sites chosen to keep him in the bay, the clause sleeping in the vault for nineteen years until it could be woken and aimed. He had not been living outside history. He had been *held in reserve* for it — a tool stored in oiled cloth, waiting for the day power needed a boy from Saltmere Bay to betray a girl with storm-colored eyes.

They walked him along the harbor road in his own skull, all the old certainties, and he broke them one by one as he went. Not history's absence. History's *harness*. And he had worn it so long he'd called it his own weight.

By the time he reached the salt sheds his fury had stopped shaking and gone cold, and cold fury was a thing he knew how to use, because the sea had taught him: panic kills divers, but anger channeled through discipline gets you through the cave, through the dark, through the squeeze, out the other side into air.

Sable was there, of course — she'd chosen the meeting place, back of the third shed where the shell piles blocked the sightlines. She pushed her hood back and searched his face and found whatever she found there, because her jaw tightened.

"They talked to you," she said.

"They showed me my own chains," Lior said. "Nineteen years, and today they let me feel the links." He glanced once along the shed wall, then back at her, and his voice dropped to iron. "There's a price on your name and my signature is supposed to collect it. They gave me a day."

"A day until what?"

Lior looked past her, out to the harbor, where the tide was turning — where three days from now the morning ship would take on cargo in the gray half-light, and where every dock would be watched by men wearing the meridian pin.

"Until I stop being their instrument," he said. "There's a way out of this bay that isn't a dock, Sable. Divers know it. The company doesn't. But we move tonight."
