## 7. The Diver's Route

The caves beneath Saltmere Point had no name, because names invited company. The divers called them *the throats* — three flooded passages running under the headland like veins of dark glass, filling and emptying with the tide twice a day. Every diver knew the first throat. A few had braved the second. Only Lior Winterbourne, and his father before him, had ever swum the third.

He went out at dusk to prove he still could.

The water was cold for the season, and it closed over him like a hand. Below the surface the world simplified: pressure, breath, the pale ribbons of light where the last sun found cracks in the rock. He moved through the first throat with practiced economy, past the shelf where the big pearl oysters clung, through the squeeze where the rock nearly touched his shoulder blades, into the second — longer, colder, bending left where a careless man would expect it to bend right. His lungs began their slow argument. He let them argue. He had spent nineteen years learning the exact weight of a breath, and he knew better than most what a man could do with one.

At the junction where the second throat forked, he hung weightless in the black water and felt the third passage breathing against his face. It exhaled cold, a faint current from somewhere deeper in the headland, the tide's hidden plumbing. His father had shown it to him at fourteen, gripping his wrist hard enough to bruise before the final turn, as if to say: *remember this, even if you never use it.*

He had never used it. He had believed, until this week, that he would never need to.

Lior turned back. On the long swim out, surfacing gasping into the purple evening, he made himself say the plan aloud to the empty water, because a plan spoken is a plan committed.

Three days. The morning tide ship — the *Halcyon*, Meridian-owned, which meant Corvane would have her manifests memorized — sailed on the dawn ebb. The docks would be watched; they were already watched. But the *Halcyon* anchored two hundred yards off the mole in deep water to load her cargo by lighter, and two hundred yards off Saltmere Point was the seaward mouth of the second throat. A diver could reach it. Two swimmers could, if one of them knew the water the way Lior knew his own hands.

Get Sable into the caves at slack low tide. Through the second throat. Out the seaward mouth. A lantern signal to the ship's boat that came for oyster-shell ballast every sailing morning — he'd bribed such men before, for small things, smuggled brandy, undeclared pearls. Then gone, across the sea, before Corvane's agents understood what had been taken from them.

It was a good plan. It was also built entirely on the one thing he couldn't buy: someone had to move the harbor watch off the mole for the hour of the ebb, and there was only one man who could do it honestly enough not to draw suspicion.

Old Tam Voss, pilot of Saltmere Bay, who had guided ships across the bar for thirty years and owed Meridian nothing but habit.

* * *

Tam kept a shack on the pilings at the river mouth, hung with nets that hadn't been mended since his wife died and smelling of tar and old coffee. Lior found him at his table at lamplight, doing nothing, which was how Tam did most things now.

"I need the mole watch called away," Lior said, "the morning after next. Dawn ebb. One hour."

Tam didn't look up from his cold cup. "Company pays me to bring ships in, boy. Not to make ships disappear."

"Company doesn't pay you anything anymore. They pay you less than they owe you and call it kindness." Lior sat down across from him. "You've watched them squeeze this harbor dry for ten years same as the rest of us."

"Watched it, yes." Tam raised his eyes then, pale and patient as a heron's. "Watching's survivable. Asking why you're wanting the watch blind at dawn — that's not."

"They're hunting a girl."

"Ah." The word fell flat as a dropped oar.

"Not a criminal. Not a spy. A girl whose family name got changed by a war nobody here voted for. And they'll put her on a company ship or in a company grave, and either way it'll be signed for properly, stamped, entered in the ledger — because that's what the company does. It writes people down and closes the book." Lior heard his own voice shaking and didn't try to steady it. "I made her a promise, Tam. Before any of this. When I was young enough to think promises were free."

For a long moment the pilot studied him, and outside, the river muttered against the pilings.

"There was a time," Tam said slowly, "when I'd have thrown you out for talking treason on my floor."

"And now?"

"Now I'm old enough to know the difference between treason and arithmetic." He turned the cup in his hands. "But wanting isn't doing, boy. The watch sergeant on the mole is Pell. Pell doesn't blink unless Corvane tells him to, and Corvane's doubled the watch already — you've seen it. I can pull Pell off for an hour with a false grounding report upriver, maybe. Maybe. But if it goes wrong, I'm a dead man, and worse, so are you, and worst of all, so is she." He leaned forward. "So don't come to me with wanting. Come to me with weight. What are you carrying?"

Lior reached inside his shirt.

His mother's last pearl sat in its chipped tin box, and he had carried it against his chest for three days without letting himself understand why. It was the pearl she'd refused to sell through three hungry winters, the one she'd told him she was keeping for the day one of her children needed to become somebody. It was perfect — round and heavy-lustered, the color of moonrise, worth more than everything else the Winterbournes owned together.

"My mother doesn't know I've taken it," he said. "She'd have given it anyway. She'd have given both eyes." He set the open box on the table between them. "This is the weight. This and everything else I have, which after tomorrow is nothing but my two hands and what they know about water."

Tam looked at the pearl for a long time. Something moved behind the heron-pale eyes — not greed; Lior had expected greed and felt oddly wounded by its absence. It looked more like grief, the specific grief of an old man watching a young man pay an adult's price for the first time.

"That buys your hour," Tam said quietly. "And it buys my silence after, whatever happens." He closed the box and pushed it back across the table. "Keep it till it's done. I don't take wages for a job unfinished."

Lior took the box. His hand wanted to shake and he forbade it.

"You understand what you're choosing," Tam said. "Corvane doesn't lose gracefully. Whatever comes of this, there's no walking back to the company afterward with your hat in your hands. You'll be a name on a list."

"Yes," Lior said. "I've been on their list since I was born. At least now I get to pick which column."

* * *

He walked home along the mole in full dark, and the walk was a lesson in what fear becomes when you stop feeding it excuses.

The docks were wrong. He'd grown up reading this harbor the way other men read scripture — every lantern its familiar sentence, every moored hull a word in place. Now the sentences were broken. Extra lamps burned on the quarterdecks of two company hulks. Men stood at the foot of every gangway, and they weren't dockmen slouching through a night shift; they stood like soldiers, still, facing outward, watching the shore instead of the ships. Watching for someone coming to the water.

And at the mole's end, where the pilots' skiffs tied up, a figure in a gray coat stood apart from the watch, gloved hands folded, looking out at the anchorage with the unhurried patience of a man counting money in his head.

Corvane.

Lior kept walking. It cost him everything he had to keep walking — one boot in front of the other, the pace of a tired diver going home, eyes down, shoulders rounded into the shape of a man with no plans larger than sleep. The wind off the bay carried the factor's voice once, faintly, giving some instruction to Pell, and the words were lost, but the tone wasn't: mild, reasonable, absolute.

*He knows,* Lior thought, and the cold that went through him had nothing to do with the wind. *He doesn't know where. He doesn't know when. But he knows she's here and he knows I'm the door.*

The letter had been bait. The silence papers had been the trap closing. And now the whole machine of the Meridian Trading Company was being arranged around the harbor like a net around a school of fish, patiently, legally, with every knot properly tied — and somewhere in the stews above the waterfront Sable Ashkani was hiding in borrowed clothes, trusting him, and the tide would not wait and neither would Corvane.

Lior stopped at the end of the mole. Ahead the black water breathed against the stones, in and out, the same rhythm as the throats under the point, the same rhythm it had kept since before the company existed, before any charter or crown or ledger. The tide didn't care whose name was on what paper. The tide kept its own accounts, and it settled them twice a day, every day, and it had never once missed a payment.

He had spent his whole life believing history belonged to admirals and merchants, to men in gray coats with folded gloves. Standing there, he understood — with a clarity that felt like breaking surface after too long below — that history was simply the sum of what people did when the watching men weren't looking. It happened in the dark, in the water, in the space between a promise and its keeping. It had always been happening to him. The only choice he'd ever truly owned was whether to be carried by it or to swim.

He chose.

Tomorrow he'd take Sable down to the point at dusk and show her the first throat dry-footed, teach her the count of breaths, drill the route until she could do it half-blind and terrified, because she would be both. Tomorrow he'd check the lighters, fix the lantern signal, learn the *Halcyon*'s boat crew by sight. And the dawn after, while Corvane's watch stood its careful post on the mole, two swimmers would slip into the cold veins of the headland and vanish.

Behind him, boots on stone. He turned, hand tightening around the tin box in his pocket.

Pell the watch sergeant was bearing down on him through the lamplight, four men behind him, and Pell's face held none of a constable's boredom — it held recognition, and something like apology.

"Lior Winterbourne," Pell said. "Mr. Corvane would like a word."
