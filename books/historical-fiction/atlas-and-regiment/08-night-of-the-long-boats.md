## 8. Night of the Long Boats

The pilot's boat smelled of tar and old lies.

Lior crouched in the bow as Hask Ferrow sculled them toward the black seam of the headland, where the cliffs swallowed the sea and gave it back changed. Above them, Saltmere Bay glittered with more lanterns than Lior had ever seen on a night without a festival. The company had doubled the watch. Every dock hand, every customs runner, every man who owed Corvane money stood along the wharves with a shuttered lamp and instructions.

But not out here. Out here there was only water, and the caves, and the route only eleven living divers knew.

"Two strokes past the leaning rock," Ferrow murmured, his voice pitched for the water alone. "Then down."

Lior looked back at Sable, wrapped in his mother's oilskin, her face pale beneath the hood. He'd told her everything he could — the count of breaths, the pull of the channel, the way the flooded passage narrowed where a diver had to exhale and trust the sea to make room. She'd listened like a woman memorizing scripture.

"And you'll be waiting," she said. Not a question.

"On the other side. Before you surface." He touched her wrist once. "Any ship. Any tide. I said it when we were children, and the world hasn't made me a liar yet."

Ferrow shipped his oars and did not move to help her over the side.

That was the first wrong thing. Small as a dropped stitch. Lior noticed it the way a diver notices a change in current — not with his mind but with his skin.

* * *

Sable slid into the water and the cold closed over her like a fist. She'd swum the warm rivers of her father's country as a girl; this was nothing like that. This was a sea that wanted something from her.

She dove.

The passage was exactly what Lior had promised: a throat of stone lit by nothing, guided by nothing but the rope he'd run through it three nights ago, knotted every five arm-lengths so she could count her way blind. She pulled herself along the knots. The walls pressed close. Her lungs began their small argument, and she answered it the way he'd taught her in the shallows years ago, laughing then — *don't fight the water, be something it carries.*

Fifty knots. Sixty. Then the rope went slack in her hand.

She stopped. Felt along the wall. The knot-line ended in a clean cut — someone had severed it, tied off the end neatly, deliberately, twenty lengths short of the opening.

Above her, far above, filtered down through forty feet of black water, came a sound she didn't understand yet: oars. Many oars. And lantern-light, blooming on the surface like a wound.

* * *

Lior knew before he surfaced.

The long boats were already there, three of them, riding the dark water inside the sea-cave's cathedral mouth, their lamps hooded but leaking gold at the seams. Men lined the gunwales with boathooks and coils of line. And standing in the bow of the nearest, immaculate even at midnight, coat collar turned against the damp, was Mr. Corvane.

"Mr. Winterbourne." His voice carried off the wet stone, gentle as ever, which was how Lior knew the mask had come fully off. "You've saved me a great deal of walking."

Behind Lior, Ferrow's boat backed water. The pilot wouldn't meet his eyes. "Debt," Ferrow said thickly. "They held mine over me since 'ninety-four. I'm sorry, lad. The pearl wasn't enough. They knew about it before you ever brought it to my door."

*They knew.* Of course they knew. Corvane had known every step, let them walk every step, because a trap works best when its door is left open.

And Sable was still under. Still in the passage. Twenty lengths of unknotted black water between her and air, and now between her and the men who would drag her onto a charge of treason.

Corvane's men began shipping oars. One of them swung a lantern toward the cave mouth — toward the calm patch of water where a swimmer would have to rise.

Lior went under without a plan. That was the thing about drowning; you didn't need one.

He found the cut rope by memory instead of touch, swimming the passage he'd run a hundred times since boyhood, faster than sense allowed. His chest was already burning when he reached the place where the knots stopped, and there — a pale shape in the dark, hanging motionless, hands searching stone that offered no answers.

He caught her arm. Pulled. She came toward him with the desperate violence of the nearly-drowned, and he got his shoulder under hers and drove upward, both of them clawing through the last stretch, and the world broke open into grey air and rain.

She coughed until she wept. He held her against the rock shelf at the passage's inner lip, half in the water, and above them the cave rose like a throat, and at the mouth of it, lamps.

"He cut the line," Sable gasped. "Lior, he—"

"I know."

"There's no other way out?"

There was one. He'd been trying not to think of it. At the back of the cave, below the old guano ledge, the divers' siphon ran — a flooded tunnel barely wide enough for a man's chest, ninety feet long, no air pocket, no light, the kind of passage that had killed old Tam Weller in 'eighty-one and been sealed in every chart since. Only a diver in full command of his breath could run it. Only a fool would try.

Only someone carrying another person couldn't.

"I can get you to the tide ship," he said. "But not with me beside you."

Her hands closed on his collar. Rain fell through the cave's broken roof in silver threads. "Don't," she said. "Whatever it is, don't."

"The siphon comes up in the kelp pool past Gull Point. The morning tide ship stands off there before dawn to take on ballast — I checked it twice. You come up, you swim to her ladder, you say you're the new cook's niece from Harwick. You have the papers."

"And you?"

Boathooks scraped stone. Voices echoed down the cavern. Lanterns were moving along the ledges above the inner water, and there was no longer time for anything except the truth.

"I hold the passage open," Lior said. "That's all I've ever done. Held things open. Let me do it right, just once."

She kissed him — quick, hard, tasting of salt and fear — and then she was gone into the dark at the back of the cave, and Lior turned toward the light.

* * *

From the water, Sable heard it begin.

Shouts. A splash. A man's voice raised in pain or fury — Lior's, though the words scattered across the stone. They had found him at the siphon's mouth, and he was making noise, making himself the whole of the world's attention, buying her minutes with his body.

She swam into the tunnel.

It was worse than anything the sea had ever asked of her. The rock pressed on all sides; her shoulders dragged the walls; there was no up, no down, only the rope of her own resolve pulling her forward while her lungs went from argument to mutiny. Ninety feet. She counted strokes because counting was the last thing that was hers. Forty. Thirty. Her vision sparked at the edges. She thought of her father's face, of the burned warehouses, of a boy on a breakwater promising her the impossible with total seriousness — *any ship, any tide* — and she thought: *he is behind me in the dark, and I will not waste what he is buying.*

Twenty. Ten. Her hand broke surface first, then her head, and she hung in the kelp pool gasping like a landed fish, alive, absurdly, gloriously alive, with the black hull of the tide ship rising against the stars a hundred yards offshore.

She swam.

* * *

They held Lior at the siphon's mouth, and he fought them, because fighting was the last coin he had to spend.

A boathook caught his leg. Hands wrenched his arms back. Corvane stood on the rock shelf above, unhurried, watching him struggle with the mild interest of a man inspecting a ledger.

"The Ashkani girl," Corvane said. "Where is she."

Lior laughed. It hurt to laugh; something in his ribs was wrong. "Gone ahead," he said. "Ask the pilot. Oh — wait."

Corvane's expression didn't change, but two of his men were already hauling themselves back into the long boats, and the lamps swung toward the cave mouth, and Lior understood with sudden fierce joy that it was too late, that the arithmetic had already tipped, that somewhere beyond the headland Sable's arms were cutting through black water toward a hull that would weigh anchor with the dawn.

"Bring him," Corvane said, turning away. "The debt survives the night. The girl—" a pause, thin as a knife, "—is a matter for the Admiralty now."

They put him in the stern of the middle boat. The rain was easing. Through the cave mouth he could see the headland, the grey line of coming morning, and the tide ship's lights standing off Gull Point — and then, impossibly, movement: a figure at her rail, small, dripping, waving one arm in a gesture he knew. The old signal from the breakwater, from the summer they were children. *I'm here. It's done.*

Something in his chest unclenched that had been clenched for years.

He was still looking when the boat struck the swell at the cave mouth. The boathook — still tangled with his leg — jerked. He went over the side into water cold enough to stop the heart of a lesser man, and the sea took him the way it takes everything, impartially, hungrily.

He did not fight it at first. That was the strangest part. Down in the green dark, tangled line around his ankle, lungs flooding slowly, he felt almost peaceful — the resignation he'd carried all his life finally made physical. *This is what happens to people like us,* some old voice said. *History belongs to admirals and merchants.*

But there was a second voice now. Newer. Hers.

*You held it open. Now get out.*

Lior tore at the line. His fingers were dead things, wooden, stupid, but he worried at the knot the way he'd worried at every knot the company ever tied him with, and it gave — and he kicked for a surface he could no longer see, and found it, and breathed.

When they pulled him out at the point below Gull Head, half-dead and retching seawater, the tide ship was already running for open water with the morning tide under her, and Corvane was standing on the shingle above him, reading the horizon, and for the first time in all of it, the factor's face showed something other than patience.

"You knew," Corvane said quietly. "All of it. The clause, the letter, the bait. And you chose anyway."

Lior lay on the stones, broken-ribbed, nameless as far as the company was concerned, papers forfeit, family exposed — and smiled up at the grey sky with blood in his teeth.

"Any ship," he said. "Any tide."
