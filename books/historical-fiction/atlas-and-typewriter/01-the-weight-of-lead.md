## 1. The Weight of Lead

The lamp on Coral Lockridge's bench burned with the particular stubbornness of a wick trimmed too many times, its flame no taller than a thumbnail, and by that small light she cut letters into steel.

She had been at it since before dark — since before the church bells rang six, in fact — because the order from the customs house wanted a new face for their manifests, something crisper than the old Caslon her father had bought secondhand from a bankrupt shop in Braille-sur-Mer twenty years ago, and because crisp faces did not cut themselves. The graver moved in her hand the way a pen moves in a scholar's: certain, unhesitating, each stroke shaving a curl of steel so fine it drifted down to the bench like ash. *A*, then *B*, then the *C* she was working now, its open mouth demanding a curve she could feel in her wrist before she ever saw it.

Outside, Vellamare was being loud about something.

She could hear it through the shutters — the harbor bells, a crowd's low animal murmur, once a whistle, twice a shout that rose and broke like a wave finding rocks. Somewhere toward the Fish Gate a drum was beating, not a festival rhythm. She knew festival rhythms. She had grown up on festival rhythms, when the whole city smelled of fried dough and gunpowder and her father would lift her onto the composing stone so she could watch the fireworks over the roofs while he pretended not to watch her near the open flame of his lamp.

This drum was different. This drum had the patience of a thing intending to keep going.

Coral turned the graver and cut the *C*'s spine.

The letter took shape under the lamplight, and there was a comfort in that which she could not have explained to anyone without sounding mad, or stupid, or both. Steel did not care about drums. Steel did not care that the third provisional government in eighteen months had been announced by proclamation on Tuesday and was already, by Friday, being called provisional by everyone except the men who had proclaimed it. Steel held its shape. A letter cut true stayed true whether the man who ordered it wore a king's colors or a committee's cockade, whether the paper it printed was a love letter, a shipping manifest, a death sentence, or a lie.

Her father used to say that. He'd said it in a hundred ways across thirty years at this bench, but it always came back to the same idea: *Ink is patient, Cora-Lee. Ink waits. Men are weather; type is climate.* He'd said it the week the bread riots closed the eastern quarter. He'd said it the night Aldous Ren was taken, when even the old men at the Café Delorme had gone quiet and sat looking into their glasses as though the answers were poured in with the wine. He'd said it — thinner, grayer, his cough a wet bellows by then — the morning he told her the debts were worse than she thought and smiled as if that settled something between them.

He had been dead eleven months. The debts had outlived him, as she had known they would. Debts, unlike men, were immortal.

She finished the *C*, blew the filings from the matrix, and held it up to the lamp, turning it. The letterface caught the light along its bevels and threw a tiny, perfect shadow into the counter — the negative space inside the letter, which her father had always insisted mattered more than the letter itself. *Anyone can make a mark,* he'd say. *The art is the hole you leave in it.*

"It needs a hair more weight in the foot," she told the empty shop, because talking to herself had become a habit sometime around the funeral and she had stopped pretending it would go away. "And the serif wants shortening."

The shop agreed with her in the way empty rooms do, by repeating nothing.

It was a long room, deep as a ship's hold, running back from the Rue des Casses to the type cellar, and it had seen better decades — that was a polite way of putting it that the tax assessor had found useful. Two presses stood against the far wall: the old Albion, her father's firstborn as far as he was concerned, its iron frame gone brown with age and oil, and the newer Columbian they'd bought on credit in the good year, the only good year, when the wine merchants' guild had kept them busy enough to hire two apprentices and believe, briefly, that the world made sense. The Columbian's eagle finial stared down at the room with its gilded, half-blind dignity. She owed nine hundred livres on it. She owed four hundred to the paper merchant, one hundred and sixty to the ink supplier, and a sum she tried not to name to the bank of Duval & Fils, whose head clerk had a way of removing his gloves slowly when he discussed terms.

Eleven months of keeping the wolf from the door by feeding it the smaller furniture of her life, one piece at a time. The good brass fittings went first. Then her mother's silver, such as it was. Last month, the horse.

The presses stayed. The presses would stay until her hands fell off. That was not sentiment, whatever Tibo thought, whatever anyone thought. It was simply that a press without a presswoman was worth forty livres as scrap and a press with one was worth its weight in work, and work was the only currency in Vellamare that had not been devalued by the revolution — yet.

The drum outside stopped. Started again. Stopped.

She listened despite herself, the graver poised above the steel, and in the pause she heard the sea doing what the sea always did beneath everything else in Vellamare: breathing. In and out against the harbor wall, indifferent as lead.

Whatever it was, it was not hers. That was the rule she had built her last eleven months on, stone by stone, like a breakwater: history was a tide for other people's houses. Let the students march. Let the committees argue themselves into and out of existence. Let Aldous Ren have died for whatever he died for, God rest him, poor firebrand, poor fool, poor saint, depending on which café you drank in. None of it reached the bench. At the bench there was only the steel and the lamp and the next letter, and the next, and the quiet arithmetic of survival, which was the only mathematics she had ever been any good at.

She set the graver down and pressed the heels of her hands into her eyes until the darkness bloomed orange.

Eleven months, and she still caught herself listening for his step on the stair. Not the real step — the real step she had watched fail, growing slower down the years until the last winter it was barely a shuffle and the doctors at the charity ward had used the word *growth* in that soft voice they saved for the families — but the old step, the young one, the quick double-knock of a boot heel that meant her father was home and pleased about something and there would be talk at supper about matrices and margins and the scandalous price of Spanish rag paper. Grief was a compositor's error, she had decided. It kept setting the same dead line over and over, and no amount of correcting would take it out of the form. You just had to let the page print wrong and turn to the next.

"Coral?" Tibo's voice from the top of the cellar stairs, where the boy slept in the loft above the paper store because his family's single room in the Tanner's Row held seven people and no corner for a fifteen-year-old with ambitions. "There's a fire signal on the Point. Marin says the fleet's refused to land the grain shipment. Marin says there's going to be—" He stopped, the way boys stopped when they heard their own voice saying a word too large for it. "—trouble."

"Marin," said Coral, "says a great many things."

"He says the Committee's called an assembly for tomorrow on the Place Royale. He says everyone will be there."

"Then it will be very crowded, and I will be here, setting the customs house manifest." She pulled the lamp closer and took up the graver again. "Is the kettle on?"

"You're not coming down to hear?"

"I am cutting a *D*, Tibo. The *D* does not care about assemblies."

She heard him breathe, wanting to argue, hearing in her flatness the permission he actually needed to run off and join the noise — she was not so old, at twenty-six, that she had forgotten how the blood sang when a crowd chanted, how the whole street seemed to become one body with your own heart inside it. Go, then, she almost said. Go and shout. Shout costs nothing tonight. But the boy was already clattering down the stairs, and a moment later the street door opened and shut and his footsteps sprinted away toward the harbor, toward the drums, toward the enormous bright thing happening to other people.

The silence he left behind was enormous. Shops were never truly quiet — presses ticked and settled, the roof timbers talked to each other, the sea kept up its slow argument below — but this was a human absence, and it sat in the room with her like a customer waiting to be served.

Coral cut the *D*. It came out well. Better than well; the foot carried its weight and the bowl was generous without going soft, and if she lived to be eighty she did not think she would cut a better curve, which should have pleased her and instead felt like a small cold hand laid flat against the back of her neck.

Because here was the thing she did not say, even to the empty shop: the orders were drying up. Not dramatically — nothing in Vellamare happened dramatically anymore, everything happened the way water happened, seeping, finding cracks — but the customs house manifest was the first substantial commission in five weeks, and the customs house paid late, and every merchant she spoke to lately had the same careful eyes, the same lowered voice, the same habit of glancing at the door before discussing anything larger than the weather. Paper was getting scarce. Rag collectors had stopped coming into the quarter. And twice this month, men she didn't know had paused outside the shop window to read the faded samples in the case — her father's finest work, the Ren pamphlets, the famous ones, displayed behind glass like relics — and one of them had smiled, and the smile had been worse than any frown.

She had cleaned the window the next day. Left the manifest specimens and the wedding invitations. Left everything neutral, everything safe.

*History is a tide for other people's houses.*

But tides did not knock. Tides did not stand smiling at your window in the rain.

She put the finished *D* in the box with its brothers and sat back and rubbed her inking-stained fingers on her apron, and looked down the length of the shop to where her father's proof books stood shelved beside the press — forty years of test pulls, every face he ever cut, every job he ever ran, bound in gray boards in his own neat hand. The archive of a modest life. When she was a girl she had thought those shelves were boring as scripture. Now she caught herself standing in front of them some nights the way other people stood in front of altars.

Tomorrow, she told herself, she would sort the accounts. Write to Duval & Fils. Chase the customs house payment. Ordinary things, survivable things, things with edges she could grip.

Beyond the shutters, the crowd's murmur swelled all at once into a roar — thousands of voices, one voice — and somewhere in that vast sound, faint as a thread pulled through cloth, she thought she heard her own name being shouted, or a name like it, though that was impossible, absurd; nobody in the Place Royale knew her name; nobody in the Place Royale knew the shop existed.

The roar rolled away over the rooftops toward the sea and left the drum behind, patient, keeping its time.

Coral trimmed the lamp, locked the till, and went down to the type cellar to check the damp before bed — the cellar was her nightly ritual, the racks covered, the temperature judged by touch, the whole dark alphabet sleeping in its cases like a congregation. She was halfway down the stairs, one hand on the cool stone of the wall, when someone knocked at the street door above.

Three knocks. A pause, measured precisely. Three more.

Not Tibo — Tibo knocked like a boy, a scatter. Not a creditor; creditors knocked softly, hoping to be mistaken for the wind.

She stood in the dark of the stairwell with her hand on the stone and the whole sleeping alphabet around her, and the knocking came again, three and three, unhurried, certain, the knock of a person who knew someone was awake inside and intended to wait for the rest of the night if necessary — and against every rule she had built, every stone of her breakwater, Coral Lockridge climbed back up toward the light and unlatched the door.
