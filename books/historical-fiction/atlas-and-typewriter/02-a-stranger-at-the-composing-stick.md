## 2. A Stranger at the Composing Stick

The rain came off the harbor in slantwise sheets that evening, and Coral Lockridge watched it through the shop window the way she watched everything lately — as though from behind glass, as though it were happening in another city entirely. Beyond the breakwater the fishing boats had all been hauled up or darkened, and somewhere past the Customs House a drum was beating, irregular and far off, the sound she had spent three weeks teaching herself not to hear.

History, her father used to say, is what happens to people who don't have work to do. He had died with a composing stick in his hand and a job half-set on the stone — funeral notices for a woman whose family had never come to collect them — and Coral had decided, in the nine months since, that he had been right about most things except dying.

She turned back to the typecase. The lamp guttered once, righted itself. In her left hand: a graver; in her right, a blank of soft steel no larger than a thumbnail. She was cutting a lowercase *g* by lamplight, which was either devotion or madness, because no one in Vellamare was ordering fine bookwork anymore, and the double-pica she'd cast last spring for shipping manifests sat in its case like coins in a dead man's pocket. But her hands needed the work. Her hands always needed the work. They were the only part of her that hadn't learned to wait.

The bell over the door had not rung all day. It rang now — a hard, urgent jangle, half-swallowed by wind — and a man came through it sideways, carrying the weather in with him.

He was soaked past any pretense of dryness. Sea-water and rain had gotten into everything: his coat hung off him like kelp, his boots left standing puddles on her father's floorboards, and his hair was plastered flat in strings against a long, weathered face somewhere between thirty-five and fifty, depending on how much sleep a person imagined he'd had. He stood a moment just inside the door, dripping, getting his bearings among the hanging proofs and the smell of ink and oil, and then he saw her and crossed the room in four strides with the urgency of a man who had been walking toward this door for a very long distance.

"You're the printer."

It wasn't quite a question. Coral set down the graver with great care, because setting it down carelessly would mean admitting to being startled.

"I'm the Lockridge," she said. "Which is nearly the same thing. If you've come about a job, the answer depends on the job. If you've come about the riots, the answer is no."

"The riots." He said it strangely, tasting the word. "Is that what they're calling them."

"What should I call them?"

"Nothing." He pulled off his coat, considered the state of it, and draped it over the chair by the door rather than the floor — a small courtesy that surprised her more than anything else about him had. Beneath it he wore a wool shirt gone grey at the elbows and a canvas satchel, baggy and waterproofed, slung across his body so that it rode against his ribs. His eyes went around the shop the way a man's eyes go around a church. Not at the stock of paper, not at the press — at the cases. At the type. "Lockridge," he repeated. "Aldous Ren's pamphlets were printed here. The early ones. *The Chainbreaker.* The *Letters to the Fishwives.*"

Coral felt something tighten under her sternum, an old reflex, like hearing a doctor clear his throat before speaking.

"Aldous Ren is dead," she said. "Two years. They put his head on the Prison Gate and his name on every wall in Vellamare, and now they've put his face on flags, which I imagine he'd have found funny. I don't print for martyrs. I print wedding invitations."

"I'm not asking you to print for him." The man unslung the satchel with both hands, the way one handles something heavier than paper. "I'm asking you to look at something. You're the only printer left in this city who knew his hand when it was alive."

"Knew his—" She stopped. "I never met Aldous Ren."

"No," he agreed. "But you cut his matrices. The italic he used for his signature block. Swash on the terminal *n*, ligature on the *ld* that your father hated and Ren refused to give up. That hand didn't come out of a foundry catalogue, madame. It came out of this shop, out of that bench, and there are maybe two people in Vellamare who could identify it on sight, and one of them is buried in the Salters' Yard."

The rain filled the silence that followed, drumming on the skylight above the press, running in bright threads down the black panes. Coral looked at this stranger who had walked in off the packet boat with her father's private craft in his mouth, and made herself speak slowly.

"Who told you that?"

"He did." A thin, humorless smile. "Ren himself. I was his courier for four years. He talked about the girl who cut steel better than men twice her age. He said if he ever needed the truth verified — truly verified, past all forgery — he'd trust the steel before he trusted any living witness. Steel doesn't change its story."

"My father cut the matrices," Coral said quietly. "I was eleven."

"And you recut the swash-*n* yourself two years later when it chipped. He told me that too. Said you did it without being asked, sitting on a crate because the stool was too tall for you."

Her hands had gone cold on the bench. Nine months of practicing disbelief — of letting drums beat and crowds shout and flags go up the flagpoles while she set chandlers' bills and kept her head down — and this stranger had undone it in four sentences, because he had reached back into the one part of her life she had never given away.

"What do you want me to look at?"

* * *

He laid the letters out on the galley with the deliberation of a surgeon laying out instruments. There were seven of them. Each was a single folded sheet, cheap paper gone soft and foxed at the edges, each addressed in a different direction — one to a name in Sarrance, one to a ship's master, one simply *To the one who carries this* — and each signed at the foot with a flourish Coral knew better than her own reflection.

She did not touch them at first. She stood over them with the lamp pulled close and let her eyes do the work her father had trained them for: stroke weight, entry curves, the pressure of the pen at the joins, the particular forward lean of a man writing fast because the thought was outrunning him.

She had cut that signature into steel at fourteen years old, working from Ren's own hand copied onto tracing paper by her father, and she had spent three weeks on the swash alone. To cut a letter is to learn it more intimately than any reader ever knows a letter — to know where the writer's breath falls, where his wrist turns, where impatience thins the ink. Aldous Ren wrote the way rivers flooded: fast, deep in the middle, ragged at the banks. The *A* always broke its own bowl. The final *n* threw its tail up and to the right like a man hailing a friend across a street.

Every letter on those seven sheets broke its bowl. Every tail hailed across a street.

"That's impossible," she said.

"So I've been told. Repeatedly. By people who then refused to look twice." The stranger — he still hadn't given her his name — leaned over the galley beside her, careful not to let his wet sleeves drip. "Look again. Take your time. I've got nowhere the rain won't find me."

She touched the paper then. The ink had browned with age; the fiber of the cheapest rag stock; the folds worn soft from being carried, opened, refolded, carried again. She held the first letter up against the lamp, tilting it, reading not the words but the hand beneath the words the way a musician reads beneath the notes.

"This one's older," she said, moving to the second sheet. "Two, maybe three years. The pen's different here — broader nib — but the same fist. This one—" she stopped.

She had reached the fourth letter. Its date sat at the head in Ren's habitual style — the day, the month, the year written out fully, as if he distrusted numerals — and Coral read it twice, and then a third time, feeling the floor of the shop tilt gently beneath her feet the way the floor tilted on boats she'd ridden as a child.

The date was eight months after the execution.

"Say it," the stranger said softly.

"This is dated the ninth of Thaw-month. Two years past." Her voice sounded far away to her, someone else's voice coming out of someone else's shop. "They executed Aldous Ren on the third of Seed-fall. I remember because my father closed the shop that day and drank half a bottle in silence, which he never did, and wouldn't tell me why." She set the letter down as though it might go off. "This is dated eight months after they killed him."

"Yes."

"That's not possible."

"No," he agreed. "And yet."

She turned on him. Up close, under the lamp, she could see the exhaustion banked in his face like coals — days of travel, days of something worse than travel — and beneath it a watchfulness that never quite switched off. "Who are you? Truly. What is this? Because if this is some committee trick, some—some relic trade for the faithful, I want you out of my shop and I'll thank you to take your forgeries with you."

"Forgeries." He said the word the way other men said blasphemy. "Madame Lockridge. Look at the hand and tell me it's forged."

And that was the cruelty of it, the trap of it, and he must have known it when he chose her. She looked. She couldn't stop looking. The break in the *A*'s bowl — she had asked her father once why Ren never mended it, and her father had laughed and said, *because he thinks a flaw is a fingerprint*. The slight rightward drift of the baselines, worsening down the page exactly as fatigue worsened in a long letter, correcting after the paragraph break where the writer had paused and gathered himself. The ink pooled in the terminals the way it pooled when a man wrote quickly with a pen cut left-handed for a right-handed world—

No forger alive could have invented all of it. And no forger alive, she realized, with a sensation like stepping into cold water, would have needed to. Because there was exactly one source on earth from which a counterfeiter could have studied that hand closely enough to copy it.

"My proof books," she said slowly. "My father's archive. Every specimen we ever printed. Every draft he ever set for approval."

"Yes," the stranger said again, and this time he didn't smile at all.

* * *

The archive lived in the flat cabinets beneath the proof press, drawers her father had oiled every quarter like a man tending animals. Coral knelt before them with the lamp while the stranger stood back — he had the sense, at least, not to crowd her — and she worked through the folders with hands that were steadier than she had any right to expect.

*The Chainbreaker*, issue one, proof pull, Ren's manuscript corrections in the margin. Issue three, with the famous passage on bread and dignity struck through and rewritten in that flooding hand. The broadside declaration, the fishwives' petition, a dozen scraps and drafts. She laid them on the stone beside the seven letters and compared until her eyes burned, and the conclusion assembled itself in her the way a page assembles in the chase — piece by piece, each element locking the next, until the whole form was set and there was no taking it apart again.

The hand was genuine. All of it. The old sheets and the new ones alike, separated by months and hundreds of miles and, impossibly, by a public execution on the Prison Gate steps that half the city had witnessed and all the city mourned.

She sat back on her heels. Above her the rain worked the skylight, and the drum beyond the Customs House had stopped, or she had stopped hearing it, and she understood suddenly that the shop had changed around her in the space of an hour. For nine months it had been a refuge — the one room in Vellamare where nothing happened, where history was a thing that happened to other people behind other windows. Now there were seven pieces of impossible paper lying on her stone, and the room had become something else. Evidence. A place where a fact lived that powerful people might very much want dead.

"There's water in the kettle," she heard herself say, because her father's voice was in her head saying *you feed whoever brings you trouble; it's the only payment trouble understands*. "And I'll want the whole story. From the boat, from the beginning. Who you are, where these came from, and why you brought them to me instead of to your committees and your flags."

"The committees are why I brought them to you." He lowered himself onto the crate by the stove — her father's crate, the one she'd sat on at eleven — and for the first time he looked tired in a way he couldn't take back. "My name is Emeric Voss. I carried Aldous Ren's letters for four years, and I carried these last two across three borders, and I have come to the only person alive who can swear to what they are. Because someone forged a corpse, madame. Or faked one. Either way—"

He drew a breath, and outside, faint under the rain, the drum began again.

"—either way, the martyr the whole revolution stands on may never have died on that step. And the men who built their flags on his grave will burn this city's archives to keep it that way."

Coral rose, wiped her inky hands on her apron, and went to put the kettle on the plate.

"Then you'd better start at the beginning, Mr. Voss," she said. "And talk fast."
