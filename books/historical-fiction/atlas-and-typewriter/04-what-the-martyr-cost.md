## 4. What the Martyr Cost

Emeric Voss had carried a great many things for Aldous Ren — pamphlets, letters, bribes wrapped in oilcloth, once a pistol he was too frightened to ask about — but the heaviest thing he had ever carried was the man's reputation, and he had carried it without knowing its weight until the night Ren died.

Or did not die. That was the question now, sitting in a print shop that smelled of lamp oil and cold lead, in the hands of a woman with ink-stained fingers who looked at his letters as though they were ghosts. He had watched her recognize the hand. He had seen it in her face, quick as a struck match: not surprise exactly, but recognition of something impossible made ordinary. She had cut those matrices herself. He hadn't known that when he came to her door. Providence, or cruelty, arranged it either way.

While she bent over her proofs, he let himself remember. It was a discipline he had practiced for two years, the way other men practiced fasting or prayer — going back into those years deliberately, testing each memory against the legend to see which would break.

* * *

He had been nineteen when the committee first sent him to Ren, a courier with good legs and no family worth arresting, carrying dispatches up the cliff road to the house above Polder Cove where the great pamphleteer lived in what everyone imagined was heroic seclusion. What Voss found instead was a man of fifty-three with a cough that rattled like gravel in a tin cup, sitting at a desk buried under other men's requests.

"You're the new legs," Ren had said, not looking up. "Sit down. You'll wait while I decide which of these fools I can afford to disappoint."

Voss had expected fire. The pamphlets were fire — *The Chainbreaker's Letter*, *A Word to the Harbor Workers*, the famous *Catechism of the Free Citizen* that students copied out by hand and pinned to church doors in towns where printing one would earn you the galleys. The prose was all iron and dawn. It had made Voss a revolutionary at fourteen, reading smuggled copies in the loft of his uncle's rope-works with his heart hammering so hard he thought the words themselves might be audible.

The man who wrote them took four hours to answer three letters, drank chicory because coffee cost too much, and complained about his knees with the detailed grievance of a sailor discussing weather.

"He isn't what I expected," Voss confessed to Mada Quist, the committee's secretary, on his return.

"No one is," she said. "That's what the posters are for."

But over the months — he became Ren's regular courier because he was reliable and because Ren, having decided Voss could read, began lending him books with the gruff generosity of a man giving away bread from his own short loaf — over the months the disappointment curdled into something more complicated. Not disillusionment. The opposite, almost. Because Voss began to see that the fire in the pamphlets was real; it simply had to pass through an exhausted, coughing, disappointed man to reach the page, the way water through bad pipes comes out muddied but wet. Ren wrote *the dawn belongs to those who rise for it* and then sat rubbing his hands over a brazier because the committee had cut his coal allowance to pay for another printing run.

"They love me in the districts," he said once, wry as a bent nail. "They've never met me. It's the only healthy relationship I've ever had."

* * *

The compromise came later, and it was smaller than anyone would believe, which was precisely why it worked.

The old regime had never managed to catch Ren. They caught his printers, his paper suppliers, two of his couriers — Voss had attended the trial of the second one, a girl named Orla Fenn who stood silent through the sentencing and spat on the floor of the dock, and Voss had gone home and been sick and come back the next morning asking for more work. But the man himself stayed beyond their reach, moving between safe houses, until the winter when his cough turned from gravel to blood and the committee grew frightened enough to make arrangements.

It was Cassian Mereau — young then, already smooth, already beloved at meetings for the way he could make a room feel like a congregation — who brokered what was called *the arrangement*. A doctor. A warm room in the city, better than any safe house. In exchange, certain of Ren's future writings would be reviewed before publication. Nothing more than review, Mereau insisted. A courtesy. A way of protecting the movement's greatest voice from careless words at a careless hour.

"I don't like it," Voss told Ren directly, the only time he ever presumed. "Letting them read you first."

Ren had looked at him for a long moment, and there was something in the look Voss would spend years decoding — not anger, but a tired kind of gratitude, the gratitude of a man who is glad someone still expects him to be clean.

"Boy," he said, "do you know what I write with? This hand." He held it up; it shook slightly, and not only from age. "Mereau knows it too. He reads my drafts and changes nothing, because there's nothing he needs to change. I'm dying slowly in front of the whole country and they're watching me do it, and every month I don't die is a month the movement has a voice." He set the hand down. "You want your heroes pure? Write your own pamphlets. Mine are written by a sick old man who takes what help he can get and hates himself on Tuesdays."

"And the other days?"

"The other days I work." He turned back to his desk, then paused. "Don't tell the districts about Tuesdays."

* * *

The last year was the one Voss could hardly bear to remember, and remembered most.

Because the review arrangement did not stay innocent. Voss saw the evidence accumulate the way one sees a tide coming in — each wave small, deniable, the whole undeniable. A paragraph missing from a planned address. A pamphlet delayed so long it arrived after the event it answered, answering nothing. And Ren changing, too: growing quieter in company, writing at night with the doors barred even against committee visitors, keeping his own copies of his own manuscripts in his own hand, which he had never bothered with before, because before he had trusted the machine he built.

In the spring — six months, give or take, before the world was told he died — Ren summoned Voss at odd hours, three times in one week, and had him copy documents in a hand disguised as clerical script. Lists. Dates. Names of printers arrested within days of requesting committee approval for Ren-related material. Names of committee members who had signed the arrest warrants' underlying informations under pseudonyms Voss recognized, because a courier learns to recognize everything.

"He's selling us," Voss said stupidly. "Ren is selling us to the regime."

"No," said Ren, who had come up behind him silently as a man grown used to not being heard. "Mereau is selling me, and selling the ones below me to show goodwill, and keeping the ones above him dry. I'm the last thing of value I have left to trade, and I intend to be a very expensive commodity." He sat down heavily. "I'm going to publish it, boy. All of it. The arrangement, the vetoes, the names. My last pamphlet will be aimed inward, and it will be the truest thing I ever wrote, and it will cost me whatever I have left, which admittedly isn't much."

"Why tell me?"

"Because you'll carry it, and you won't leak it, and you won't misunderstand it. And because someone should know that I knew. Whatever story they tell afterward — and they will tell one, they're already drafting it, both sides are — someone should hold the true version somewhere the fire can't reach." He coughed, long and hollowing, and waved off Voss's offered arm with irritation. "Not sentiment. Logistics."

Then, before the testament could be finished, distributed, or even fully written — Ren vanished.

The official account came within days: Aldous Ren, betrayed by an informer, seized by regime agents at a safe house near the border, tried in haste, executed in haste, buried in an unmarked grave. There were no witnesses Voss could find. There was a coffin shown briefly to two journalists. There was Mereau on a platform, weeping beautifully, converting grief into fuel before the ashes of the man were even cold — or weren't.

And there was the uprising, three weeks later, lit by that execution like a field lit by lightning. Voss had stood in the crowd on the first night and heard ten thousand people howl Ren's name and understood, with a horror that has never entirely left him, that the death was worth more to the cause than any living year of the man had ever been. The regime had given the revolution a martyr. Or the committee had. Or the committee had accepted whatever the regime offered, and the offer had been made possible by someone inside.

He had searched for two years. Quietly, after the first months — because the new government had no more appetite for awkward questions than the old one, and less elegance about discouraging them. He followed the coffin story and found the grave registry blank. He found the border clerk who supposedly processed the prisoner transfer and found the clerk had been promoted sideways into a post with no duties. He found, eventually, through a retired harbor pilot who owed him a debt, a packet boat manifest from the week of the disappearance listing a male passenger recorded only as *Mr. Tuesday*.

Tuesdays. *The other days I work.* Don't tell the districts about Tuesdays.

It might have been nothing. Coincidences were cheaper than conspiracies and far more common. But Voss had spent two years learning the difference between a legend and a ledger, and legends were written by survivors, and ledgers were written by clerks, and clerks did not joke, and Mr. Tuesday had sailed north on the ninth of Floréal with one trunk and no return passage.

And now these letters. Signed in that same shaking hand, dated after the death, carried across borders in Voss's own luggage at risk of the kind of cell Orla Fenn had stood in so silently. Written by a dead man, or a man pretending to be dead for reasons Voss could guess at — hiding, waiting, or simply done — and addressed to no one, which was somehow worse. Letters to nobody. A voice calling out and expecting no answer, which was what happened to men who had been mourned too thoroughly to be allowed to live.

He believed Ren escaped. He believed it the way he had once believed the pamphlets — completely, and against evidence, and with the growing suspicion that the belief itself would cost him something he wasn't finished paying for. Because if Ren lived, then Ren had chosen silence. Had let the crowds howl, had let Mereau weep, had let the uprising burn through three neighborhoods and two hundred dead, and had said nothing. And that was a truth Voss did not know how to carry alongside the other one, the older one, the boy-in-the-loft one: that the words were fire and the fire was real.

Perhaps both things were true. Perhaps that was what being a man cost, as opposed to being a martyr: you got to be real, and real things contained their own contradictions, unedited, unreviewed.

* * *

"Here," Coral Lockridge said, breaking into his memory the way daylight breaks into a cellar — abruptly, and changing everything it touches. She had the letters laid beside her father's proof sheets, and her finger was steady on the downstroke of the *R*, and her face had gone carefully, professionally blank in the way of people who have decided fear is a problem for later. "This is his hand. No question. No question at all. Which means either you're the finest forger on the coast, or—"

She stopped. Looked up at him. And Emeric Voss, who had spent two years practicing how to say *he might be alive* out loud without his voice breaking on the word, realized he was about to say it to the one person in Vellamare whose whole life was built on telling the difference between true type and false.

"Or the man you cut those matrices for," he said, "has been writing letters for eighteen months after they hanged him in the square."

Outside, somewhere over the harbor, the revolution's bells were ringing for some victory or anniversary — they rang so often now the sound had become weather, unnoticed. Coral Lockridge looked at the letters again, then at the door of her shop, then back at him, and pulled the shade down against the street.

"Start from the beginning," she said. "And quietly."
