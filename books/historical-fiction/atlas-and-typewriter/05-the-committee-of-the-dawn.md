## 5. The Committee of the Dawn

He came on a Tuesday, which Coral would remember afterward, because Tuesdays were when she cleaned the ink stones and the shop smelled most of itself — tallow and linseed and the cold mineral bite of type metal — and she had always imagined that if the revolution ever noticed them at all, it would come on a day when the shop was at its best.

It was not at its best. The window glass was fogged with salt bloom, the press's iron frame wore a scab of rust where the paint had gone, and the sign above the door — LOCKRIDGE & SON, in letters her father had cut himself and never bothered to amend — was missing its ampersand, pried loose by some enterprising child and never replaced. Coral had thought about replacing it. She had thought about a great many things in the eleven months since her father's funeral, and done perhaps a third of them.

The bell above the door had a cracked voice, half a note, and it announced the man imperfectly, so that Coral looked up from the ink stone expecting a customer and found instead a tall figure in a good grey coat shaking rain from his shoulders with the unhurried economy of someone who had never once been kept waiting.

"You're the Lockridge shop." It was not quite a question. His accent was Vellamare born and bred, harbor vowels softened by years of speaking in large rooms. "I'd begun to think the city had swallowed you whole."

"Most days I'd say it has." Coral set down the ink knife. "Can I help you, monsieur?"

"You can." He crossed the shop the way a man crosses a room he intends to own — pausing at the type cabinets, running one finger along the edge of the composing stone, tilting his head to read the spines of the proof books on their shelf. He did not touch the proof books. Coral noted that. A man who wanted to touch them and made himself not to. "Cassian Mereau," he said, and offered his hand.

She had seen his face before, of course. Everyone in Vellamare had, by now — chalked on walls in profile with the dawn breaking behind it, printed on the committee's proclamations, rendered in a woodcut that made him handsomer than he was, though not by much. He was perhaps fifty, with silver coming in at the temples like frost on a window, and eyes the color of the harbor in February: grey, patient, and cold all the way down. The woodcut gave him a jaw like a cliff. In person the jaw was there, but so were laugh lines, deep ones, the kind a man earns by laughing often or by practicing.

"The Committee of the Dawn," she said, because he was waiting for her to say it.

"At your service. Though these days the committee mostly serves itself." He smiled as he said it, a smile that invited her to share the joke, and Coral understood with a lurch that this was the famous charm, the thing that had pulled a city of dockworkers and clerks and fishwives into the streets behind him — that he made every person he spoke to feel they were being let in on something. "And I'm here on committee business, Mistress Lockridge, though I hope you'll forgive the informality of the visit. I prefer to see a print shop with my own eyes before I trust it with anything."

"Trust it with what?"

"Work." He turned a slow circle, taking inventory. "Victory broadsides. The city has won its revolution, Mistress Lockridge, and a victory that isn't printed isn't a victory at all — it's only a rumor with blood on it. I want the proclamation of the new order set in type worthy of the occasion. Not the committee's usual jobbing hands." A small, fastidious wince. "They set type the way a farrier shoes a horse. Functional. Loud. I want something with dignity. I'm told your father was the finest hand in Vellamare."

*Was.* She felt the word go through her like a bodkin through a signature. "He was," she said. "I cut his matrices. I can match any style he ever set."

"I don't doubt it." Mereau's gaze came back to her and settled, warm as a lamp, attentive as an auditor. "The daughter of Aldous Ren's printer. You know, when I learned that, I confess it decided me. There's a symmetry to it. The man whose words lit the fire, printed by the family that first gave him letters."

Coral kept her face still. It was not difficult. Her father had taught her that much without ever meaning to — the year the customs men came asking about a seditious ballad, and he had stood in this same spot and discussed paper weights with them for twenty minutes until they left convinced the shop printed nothing but invoices and hymnals. *The face is a forme, Coral,* he'd told her afterward, his hands shaking only once they'd gone. *You lock it up tight and you don't let it shift under pressure.*

"The shop printed for Mr. Ren," she said. "As it printed for a dozen others. We were a commercial house."

"You were," Mereau agreed pleasantly, "a great many things."

He drifted toward the press, and Coral moved with him without deciding to, the way a body moves to stand between a lamp and the wind. He admired the iron frame. He admired the frisket. He remarked, with what sounded like genuine feeling, on the difficulty of keeping a shop alive these past years, with the old regime's paper taxes and the harbor closed and half the city's merchants gone quiet as graves. He spoke of her father — had he died before the uprising? A loss, a true loss; the city owed men like him a debt — and Coral answered in the flat currency of facts: yes, in the winter, of his chest, before anything changed, before any of it mattered.

*Before any of it mattered.* She heard the words leave her mouth and wondered if they sounded as false to him as they did to her.

Mereau did not blink. He simply turned from the press and said, in the tone of a man asking after the weather: "And his papers. His correspondence, his proofs, his accounts. I imagine a shop like this kept everything."

The question landed in the room like a slug of type metal dropped on glass.

"Everything?" Coral made herself laugh, and was surprised at how easily the laugh came, how much it sounded like her father's. "Monsieur, if we'd kept everything, you'd be standing knee-deep in invoices for mourning cards. My father kept what he had to keep and burned the rest for heat, like every printer since Gutenberg. Paper is dear. Sentiment is free. Guess which one we could afford."

"Of course." He smiled. He let the silence sit for a moment — one beat, two, three, long enough that a lesser interrogator would have rushed to fill it — and Coral, who had spent her life in the company of silence, let it sit with him. "It's only that the committee is compiling an archive, you understand. A proper one. The history of the revolution, gathered while the people who made it are still alive to be asked. Ren's papers would be the crown of such a collection. The martyr's own hand." His voice softened on the word *martyr*, and Coral watched him do it, watched him handle the word the way a priest handles a relic, and understood that he knew exactly what the word was worth and to whom.

"He didn't correspond with us," she said. "He sent copy. We set it. He paid. That was the whole of our acquaintance."

"Paid," Mereau repeated, mildly. "Yes. He was scrupulous about that, I remember. A proud man." He turned to the window, where the rain had thickened, blurring the rooftops of Tanner's Row into grey suggestion. "You know, Mistress Lockridge, I met him only twice. Once at the beginning, when he was all fire and no caution — you could feel the words coming off him like heat off a forge. And once near the end." A pause, delicate as a compositor's thumb. "He was different near the end. Tired. Careful. A man who had begun to count the cost of what he'd asked of others." He looked back at her. "I've often wondered what he would have made of all this. The statues. The songs. The children named for him in every parish from here to the border."

"He'd have hated it," Coral said, before she could weigh it.

Mereau's eyebrows rose — and then he laughed, a real laugh, startled out of him, and for one unguarded moment he looked like a man and not a monument. "You're probably right. God forgive us, you're probably right. We've made him into a flag, and no man enjoys being a flag." He wiped his eye. "Well. You see why I came myself. A shop that tells the truth about a martyr is a shop worth trusting with a proclamation."

He laid the commission on the composing stone like a man laying down a winning card: five hundred broadsides, the declaration of the provisional committee, to be ready within the fortnight, payment on delivery, committee paper supplied. The figure he named was generous — generous enough to clear a third of the debt her father had left coiled around the shop like ivy around a dead tree. Coral heard herself accept. She heard her voice do all the things a businesswoman's voice does: confirm quantities, discuss paper stock, mention the state of her ink supply in a way that invited a small advance. Mereau agreed to everything with the smoothness of a man for whom agreement costs nothing.

At the door he paused, one hand on the frame, and the rain made a grey curtain behind him.

"One more matter, and then I'll leave you in peace." The charm was still in his voice, but underneath it now, plainly, was something else — the thing under the lacquer. "If anything of Ren's should surface. A letter, a draft, a stray page in an old proof book. Anything at all in his hand." He smiled. "The committee would take it as a personal kindness. And the committee, Mistress Lockridge, remembers its friends. It remembers its friends the way a press remembers its forme — everything set, nothing forgotten, every letter exactly where it was placed." He touched his hat. "Good day. I'll send the paper stock Thursday."

The bell gave its cracked half-note behind him, and he was gone into the rain.

* * *

Coral stood in the middle of her shop for a long time without moving.

Then she went to the window and watched him cross the street, unhurried, pausing to exchange a word with old Sabine at the chandlery, who laughed and touched her cap — *there it is again, the charm, spent freely, like small change* — before turning down toward the quay and out of sight.

She had lied to him. She was almost certain she had lied well. And yet her hands, when she looked at them, were not steady, and she realized she had been standing with her back to the type cabinets — to the third drawer of the second case, where, wrapped in oilcloth beneath a false bottom her father had built and she had never until this week thought to question, lay four letters in a dead man's handwriting, dated after his death.

*The committee remembers its friends the way a press remembers its forme.*

He had chosen the image deliberately. He had stood in this shop, admired this press, and told her, in the language of her own trade, that nothing she said or did would ever be un-said or un-done in his ledger. It had not been a threat. That was the terrible elegance of it. It had been a courtesy — the kind of thing one gentleman says to another, or to a woman he has decided is worth treating as one.

She thought about the way he had asked. Not *do you have* — he had asked *if anything should surface*, the conditional tense, the grammar of a man giving her room to come to him voluntarily, later, with her dignity intact and her debt to him already incurred. He had offered her the shape of her own surrender and called it friendship.

And underneath all of it, one question had been turning over and over like a forme under the platen: *How did he know to ask?*

Not why — that was obvious enough. A martyr's papers were power; whoever held them held the right to say what the martyr would have said, and dead men were the only men who never corrected the record. But the *how*. The committee was compiling an archive, he said. Committees compiled archives the way rivers eroded banks — slowly, indiscriminately, everything in time. But Mereau had not asked about archives in general. He had walked into a failing print shop on Tanner's Row on a rainy Tuesday and asked about Aldous Ren's papers with the precision of a man who already knew there was a drawer to look in.

Coral looked at the third drawer of the second case for a long moment. Then she did not open it. Opening it was what he would be watching for, if he was watching — and he would be watching. Men like Cassian Mereau did not visit a shop and leave it unwatched; the visit itself had been the watch, the first taking of the measure, and everything after would be quieter.

Instead she climbed the stairs to the flat, took her father's heavy coat from its peg because the shop was cold and the coat was warm, and sat down at the kitchen table where he had died of his chest in the winter, in the chair by the window, and tried to think the way he would have thought.

*The face is a forme. Lock it up tight.*

But her father had never sat across a table from Cassian Mereau. Her father had lied to customs men about ballads, small lies, lies with no weight in them. This was different. This was a man who had just bought five hundred broadsides and with them, whether she had signed for it or not, a claim on her silence — and who had made the purchase so smoothly that she had thanked him for it.

She thought of the letters upstairs in their oilcloth. She thought of the stranger, Voss, who was due back tomorrow with questions she did not yet have answers to, and whose very existence in her shop had suddenly become a thing with a temperature, dangerous to touch.

And she thought, with a clarity that frightened her more than anything Mereau had said: *He knows something is here. He doesn't know what, or he'd have taken the shop apart. But he knows. And he'll be back.*

The rain ran down the window in long grey threads. Somewhere down on the quay, the packet boat from the eastern islands would be making harbor in this weather, if it made it at all.

Tomorrow, then. Tomorrow she would have to learn to lie better — or learn, at last, what the truth was going to cost.
