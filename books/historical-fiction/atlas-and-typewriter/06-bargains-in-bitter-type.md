## 6. Bargains in Bitter Type

He came back the next morning before the shutters were even down, and he brought coffee.

Not the chicory-and-ash that passed for coffee in Vellamare's third year of revolution — real beans, dark and oily, from a sack he set on the counter as though it were a legal document he wished to enter into evidence. The smell of it filled the shop in an instant, pushing back against the cold-metal smell of type and ink that Coral had lived inside since she was six years old. It was, she suspected, exactly the effect he intended.

"Master Voss," she said, not looking up from the galley she was cleaning. "The shop is closed."

"The sign is out," he agreed. "I noticed it last night when I stood across the street for two hours deciding whether to knock."

She did look up then. He was damp again — the rain had never truly stopped, only changed its mind about intensity — but this morning he had shaved, badly, and done something futile with his hair. Under the exhaustion he had the look of a man who had spent a sleepless night arriving at conclusions he did not enjoy.

"You should have knocked," she said. "I'd have told you what I'm about to tell you now. No."

"You haven't heard—"

"I don't need to hear it." She set the galley down with a care that was louder than slamming it would have been. "Yesterday a man came into this shop who could have me hanged for what's sitting under my counter, and I lied to his face with my heart going like a trip-hammer. That was one day of this, Master Voss. One day. You're proposing I make a career of it."

"And if I told you the letters name him?" Voss said quietly.

The rain filled the silence that followed. It ran down the window glass in grey threads, it ticked against the shutters, it pooled somewhere in the street outside with the patient insistence of water finding every low place in the world.

"Name him how," Coral said. Not a question. A door opening one inch, against her own better judgment.

"Sit down," Voss said. "Make the coffee. I'll show you."

* * *

They took the letters out one at a time, on a clean cloth spread over the stone of the imposing table, because Coral insisted — her father's rule, and his father's before him: nothing touches the stone bare. The coffee sat cooling in two chipped cups that had belonged to her mother, and neither of them drank it while they worked.

"Here." Voss's finger hovered over the third letter without touching the paper. "Dated the ninth of Brumaire. Read the second paragraph."

Coral read it. Then she read it again, because the first time the words had refused to arrange themselves into sense.

*Our friend in the Committee has proven more useful to the Crown than to his cause, and I write this so that if anything befalls me, the record will show whose hand held the lantern for the King's men on the night the print houses were named.*

"The ninth of Brumaire," she said slowly. "That's — eleven days before the uprising."

"Nine days before the raid on the Rue Cendrier presses. Three days before Ren disappeared." Voss turned his cup in a slow circle, not drinking. "And four months, give or take, after they hanged him on the Customs House steps."

"That's not possible."

"No," Voss agreed. "And yet."

She looked at the letter again, at the strong forward slant of it, the peculiar doubled *l* like a man leaning into wind, and felt the same vertigo she had felt the first evening — but colder now, because this was no longer only about whether a dead man wrote letters. This was about who had opened the door for the King's soldiers on the night the Rue Cendrier presses were smashed and their owners dragged to the citadel. Two of those printers had been her father's friends. One of them, old Master Quillon, had taught her to mix ink before she could write her own name. He had died in the citadel by winter.

"Who is 'our friend,'" she said. "The letter doesn't say."

"Not in that letter." Voss drew out the fourth one — the oldest by its date, written nearly a month before the others — and laid it beside the third. "But Aldous Ren was a pamphleteer, not a cryptographer. When he wrote three letters in the same month about the same treachery, he got careless with his anger. Bottom of the page."

She read it aloud, because some part of her still believed that words only became real when set down properly or spoken in a clear voice: *"Mereau drinks with the intendant's secretary and calls it intelligence-gathering. I call it what it is. If I vanish, look no further than the Committee of the Dawn's own chair."*

The rain went on ticking. Down the street, somewhere, a cart wheel complained against wet cobbles, and a gull screamed over the harbor with the idiot persistence of gulls everywhere, indifferent to the fact that the world had just tilted beneath the floorboards of Lockridge & Son, Printers.

"Cassian Mereau," Coral said. "The hero of the Barricade Weeks. The man who stood on a wagon in Victory Square and wept for Aldous Ren in front of forty thousand people."

"He did weep," Voss said. "I was there. They were very fine tears. I've thought about them a great deal these past two years."

She looked up at him. In the grey light of the rain-darkened shop his face had lost the careful pleasantness he wore like a borrowed coat, and underneath it was something older and harder and much more tired.

"You loved him," she said. "Ren."

It was not really a question either, and he did not treat it as one. "I carried his words for six years. Through checkpoints, inside fish barrels, sewn into the lining of a coat I was wearing when a sergeant of dragoons searched me at Sable Bridge and missed it by the width of a prayer." He turned the cup again. "When they executed him I stopped sleeping properly, and I told myself it was grief. Grief is comfortable. Grief means the story is finished and you can mourn it honestly. This—" he touched the edge of the letter, still not touching the paper itself, having learned her rules faster than most men learned anything "—this is worse. This is the story starting again after you buried it."

"Why come to me? Truly. You could take these to any of the new government's clerks. Or abroad. Why does this need a printer?"

"Because it needs to be *printed*, and printed properly, and printed by someone whose craft can't be questioned." He said it flatly, without flattery, and she found herself respecting him for it. "If I stand in a tavern and say Aldous Ren lived four months past his hanging and named Cassian Mereau a traitor, I am a madman or an agent of the old regime, and either way I hang. But if it comes off a press — if it comes off *your* press, in your father's face, on your stock, with the colophon that half this city trusts from thirty years of almanacs and shipping manifests — then it is a document. Documents are harder to kill than men. You know that better than anyone. It's the whole reason your trade exists and the whole reason governments fear it."

"My trade," Coral said, "has debts against it that would sink a brig. My trade has a government minister's attention on it like a sunburn. And my trade is currently all I have of my father, and you are asking me to burn it."

"I'm asking you to spend it."

"There's a difference?"

"There is," Voss said, "if the thing you buy with it is worth more than the thing. I've spent my whole life making that calculation badly, so I won't pretend to be a reliable guide. But I'll tell you what I see." He leaned forward, elbows on the table, and for the first time since the packet boat had deposited him soaked and desperate into her shop, he spoke like a courier instead of a petitioner — quick, economical, laying out the route. "Mereau suspects you. He'll be back within the week, and next time he won't ask questions, he'll send men to answer them himself. The amnesty is coming — everyone in the ministries whispers it — and when it comes, every private archive in Vellamare burns, officially, for reconciliation. Your proof books. Your correspondence. Everything. After that, these letters are the only testimony left in the world, and if they're destroyed, Mereau's version is the only one that ever existed. He wins forever. Not just power — *history*. He becomes the man Ren trusted, mourning Ren. And Ren becomes what Mereau needs him to be: silent."

The fire was laid and waiting in the little stove behind the composing bench; she had not lit it that morning because coal cost money and grief kept the shop warm enough. Now she got up, struck the match, and watched it catch, and thought about her father teaching her to set her first line — *the truth takes longer to justify than the lie takes to set, which is why liars always go to press first* — and understood, with a sensation like a matrix seating home in its groove, that she had already decided. She had probably decided the moment he said *name him*. Everything since was just the ceremony of it.

"Conditions," she said, sitting back down.

"Name them."

"First. We verify everything before a single sort is pulled. Every date cross-checked against my father's proofs, every claim in Ren's letters tested against what's known. If we're going to commit treason in lead, it will be accurate treason."

"Agreed. It's what I came for."

"Second. My name goes nowhere. Not in the colophon, not in the text, not in a whisper. If it fails, it fails as an anonymous broadside, and the soldiers kick in someone else's door."

Voss hesitated, and she saw the argument rise in him — *the colophon is the credibility* — and then, to his credit, die. "Agreed. Though it weakens us."

"It keeps me alive. Third." She wrapped both hands around the cold cup and finally drank. The coffee was bitter and slightly burnt — he had made it himself, badly, in her mother's pot, using water from her rain barrel — and it was the best thing she had tasted in a year. "Third. When this is done — whatever done means — you tell me everything. Not the version couriers tell widows. All of it. Who Ren was, what he planned, why my father's name might be in any of this. Because I've seen how you look at that letter, Master Voss, and you know more about my family than you've said, and I don't build on ground I haven't inspected."

For a long moment he studied her the way she had once seen him study the letters — looking for the forgery, the flaw in the hand. Whatever he found, it satisfied him, or at least persuaded him.

"Tibo," he said. "The boy who watches doors. He told me your father received letters from the east islands twice a year for the last three years of his life. Private courier, never the mail packet. He burned them after reading."

The stove ticked. The rain thinned, as though the weather too had decided to lean closer and listen.

"I don't know what those letters said," Voss went on quietly. "I know that Aldous Ren used a private courier to the eastern islands, twice a year, for the last three years of his life. And I know that in one of the letters on this table, dated five months ago, he writes: *tell the printer's debt is paid and the rest will follow.*"

Coral set down the cup. Her hands were entirely steady, which surprised her, because inside she could feel something enormous shifting its weight, slow as a ship coming about.

"So," she said. "You do build on inspected ground. Fine. Then here's the whole survey, Master Voss, and you may quote it in whatever testament you're carrying around behind your eyes: my father took money from a hunted man and never told his daughter, and I intend to find out whether he sold the man or sheltered him, and God help us both if it's the first." She stood, and began clearing the letters back into their oilcloth with the brisk economy of a woman who has work to do and intends to survive long enough to do it. "Now wash your hands and pull on a pair of gloves. If we're setting this, we start with the dates, dates first and claims second, and you'll learn to handle a composing stick without bending the sorts, because I'm not doing all of this myself and Tibo can't set a clean paragraph yet."

Voss rose. Something in his shoulders had changed — not lighter, exactly. Committed. The difference, she thought, between a man swimming and a man who has decided which shore.

"One more condition," he said. "Mine."

She waited, oilcloth folded, letters safe.

"When Mereau comes back — and he will come back — you let me speak to him first." His mouth twisted, not quite a smile. "I'm better at smiling at dangerous men than you are. I've had practice. It's the one skill six years of courier work leaves you with, and I'd rather spend mine before yours."

Above the shop, a floorboard creaked — Tibo, awake in the loft, moving carefully the way he did when he didn't want to be heard and always was. Coral glanced toward the stairs, then at the window, where the rain had finally broken and a thin, unconvincing light lay along the wet roofs of Vellamare like solder on tin.

"Done," she said. "But Master Voss — if he comes today, or tomorrow, or any day before we're ready — remember that he asked about Ren's papers standing right where you're standing. He knows something is here."

"Then we'd best be fast."

"We," she said, reaching for the apron her father had worn until the year he died, "will be faster than that. We'll be gone through. There's a proof book downstairs with every job this shop ran between the year I was born and last spring, and if Ren's letters connect to anything my father touched, it will be in there — dates, payments, delivery marks. Bring the lamp. The cellar stairs are steep and the rats are republicans, but they bite anyone."

She had meant it as a joke. It came out sounding like a prophecy, though she would not understand why for another three days — not until the sky over the Rue Cendrier turned orange in the small hours, and the bells began, and she stood in the street in her nightclothes watching a neighboring print house burn with its archives still inside, and understood that Mereau had decided not to wait for the amnesty after all.
