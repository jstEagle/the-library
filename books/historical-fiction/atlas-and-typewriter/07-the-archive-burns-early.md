## 7. The Archive Burns Early

The notices went up on a Tuesday, pasted to the customs house in neat rows like a sheet of composed type gone wrong — all block capitals, no kerning, the work of someone who had never set a line in his life. *BY ORDER OF THE COMMITTEE OF THE DAWN: ALL REGISTRIES, LEDGERS, DEEDS, AND PRIVATE CORRESPONDENCE PERTAINING TO THE OLD REGIME ARE TO BE SURRENDERED FOR REORGANIZATION.* Coral read it twice, her breath fogging the cold morning air, and noticed that whoever had printed it had used a ten-point roman with a cracked *e* — she could see the hairline flaw even from the street, a small white seam through the counter of the letter. She knew that face of that letter the way a mother knows a child's profile in a crowd. It belonged to a fonder press across town.

*Reorganization.* Her father had had a word for language like that. He'd called it type that lies about its own size — words cast smaller than their meaning, so they slip into a sentence without waking anyone.

She went home through the fish market, where the stalls were doing brisk business and nobody mentioned the notices, which was itself a kind of mention. The revolution was three months old and the city had learned the new arithmetic: say nothing, sell everything, keep your eyes on your own scales. A boy was selling roasted chestnuts near the fountain and singing something about the dawn, off-key, and an old woman threw a crust at him not out of malice but out of some older habit Coral couldn't name.

At the shop, Tibo was sweeping the composing room with the exaggerated diligence of a boy avoiding a task he'd already decided not to do.

"You've seen them," she said. Not a question.

"Seen what?" He swept harder.

"Tibo."

He leaned on the broom. "Maman says they're only after the nobles' papers. Land deeds. Tax rolls." He said it the way he said his catechism, wanting it to be true because saying it might make it so.

"Your mother may be right," Coral said, and heard the lie in her own voice, smooth as fresh ink. She thought of the letters folded in oilcloth beneath the third floorboard of the proof cabinet — Voss's impossible letters in Aldous Ren's impossible hand — and of her father's proof books stacked in the back room, forty years of specimens and trials and corrections, every hand that had ever passed through Lockridge's press recorded in ink. Including Ren's. Including, if Voss was right, things the Committee of the Dawn would very much prefer unrecorded.

"Go home to your mother," she said. "Take the evening meal there tomorrow too. And the day after."

Tibo looked at her with the quick animal intelligence she usually admired and today distrusted. "Something's going to happen."

"Something is always going to happen. That's why we sweep the floor." She took the broom from him gently. "Go."

He went, but at the door he turned, and for a moment he looked so much like her father — that same set of the chin before a question too large for the asker — that she nearly called him back.

"Maman says the Quill and Anchor is on the surrender list," he said. "Madame Ferrand's place."

The Quill and Anchor was not a noble house. It was a print shop, old like hers, that had printed almanacs and tide tables and, in the last year of the old regime, two of Aldous Ren's pamphlets when no one else dared. Madame Ferrand was seventy-one years old and kept every proof she'd ever pulled, catalogued in leather spines going back to before Coral was born.

"They're only after the nobles' papers," Coral said, and hated how the sentence sounded the second time around.

* * *

She dreamed that night of her father's hands. In the dream they were whole and ink-stained as always, and he was showing her the difference between two sorts of silence: the kind you keep because you are afraid, and the kind you keep because the time isn't right. *One of them rusts you,* he said, or seemed to say, *and one of them tempers you, and from the inside you can't always tell which is which.* Then the dream shifted, as dreams do, and his hands were open, empty, palms up on the composing stone, and the type had all been distributed back into the case, every word he ever set returned to its compartment, indistinguishable, unreadable, gone.

She woke before dawn with her heart going hard and lay listening to the harbor bells count the hour. Four. The house creaked. Somewhere below, in the cellar, the press stood under its canvas sheet like a shrouded thing.

She got up and lit the lamp.

* * *

The proof books filled two crates and part of a third. She had forgotten how many there were — her father had been methodical to the point of devotion, pulling proofs of everything, dating each in his small upright hand, noting the client, the typeface, the quirks of the press that week. *March 14th, wet wool weather, the K casting thin.* Forty years of weather and metal. She sat on the cellar steps with the first book open on her knees and read it the way other people read scripture or letters from the dead, and understood, with a slow cold settling, exactly what she was holding. Not sentiment. Evidence. Every pamphlet, every broadside, every unsigned squib that had ever come through this shop was fixed here in proof — including work whose authors believed, had been promised, that no copy survived.

Including the early Ren pieces. Including, dated eleven days before the execution that made him a martyr, a single proof sheet in that same unmistakable hand, annotated by her father: *A.R., private commission, no imprint, paid in coin.*

And then — she had not let herself look at this page since Voss left — the later entries. The ones that didn't fit any honest calendar.

She closed the book. Sat in the dark with the lamp guttering and listened to the rain start against the coal chute cover.

Her father had known. Whatever else was true, whatever bargains he had made or payments taken, he had known Aldous Ren walked out of that square alive, or heard it from someone who did, and he had pulled proofs of the man's letters anyway and filed them away like the coward's treasure he perhaps was — keeping the truth without having the spine to print it. She wanted to be angry at him and found instead that she was angry at the shape of the age: men who set truth in metal and hadn't the courage to pull the lever; committees that spoke of dawn while confiscating paper; a world in which the only safe place for a fact was under a floor.

*Reorganization.*

She began to plan the way her father had taught her to plan a job: backwards, from the finished sheet. What did she need? Dryness, darkness, and indifference to time. The type cellar was walled in fieldstone, older than the shop, older than the street; the northeast corner stayed cool even in August because the foundation ran down into the old cistern channel. Beneath the floorboards along that wall ran a crawl space for the drain pipes, dry in summer, damp now but not flooded. If the books were wrapped — waxed canvas, then oilcloth, then tarred sacking — laid flat, raised on brick battens off the stone, they might survive twenty years down there. They might survive her lifetime. It would have to be enough.

She worked until the bells rang seven. By lamplight, kneeling on the cold flags with her sleeves rolled and her hands black with old dust, she wrapped each volume and wrote nothing anywhere — no list, no key, no map. The location lived in one skull only, and she caught herself thinking, grimly, *so much for reorganization,* and laughed once, a short ugly sound in the empty cellar, because grief and laughter had lately begun using the same throat.

* * *

Madame Ferrand came the next morning, unannounced, in her good coat, carrying a wrapped parcel the size of a hymnal.

"I'm not stopping," she said on the doorstep, rain dripping off her bonnet. "I'm not even here. I came to return a galley gauge I borrowed from your father, oh, six years ago."

Coral took the parcel. Inside was the gauge, and beneath it, folded small, a single proof sheet: the tide table for a week that had passed years ago, marked in pencil with a pattern of numbers that meant nothing to her — yet.

"Tell your father I'm obliged," Madame Ferrand said, loudly, for the benefit of the street, where a young man in the armband of the Civic Watch was leaning against the pump with studied idleness. Then, quietly, with her eyes on Coral's: "They came Monday. Took my ledgers. Very polite. Very thorough. They were especially interested in anything touching the pamphleteer." A pause the length of a heartbeat. "I expect you'll want to check your stock against mine, girl. Printers should always know what editions exist. It saves terrible confusion later."

"Confusion," Coral repeated.

"Ink is patient," Madame Ferrand said. "People forget that. Paper burns, men die, but a well-cut matrix will outlive every government you or I will ever see. Mind the damp in your cellar. Your father never would fix that drain." And she was gone, small and upright in the rain, not hurrying, an old woman walking home who had never been there at all.

* * *

The Quill and Anchor burned on Thursday night.

Coral saw the glow from her bedroom window before she understood it — a wrongness in the sky over Rue des Carmes, an orange pulse behind the rooflines like a fever showing through skin. By the time she reached the corner, half-dressed, boots unlaced, the fire had the building by the throat. The Quill and Anchor's sign hung burning over the door, the painted anchor blistering, and heat came off the street in waves that pressed her back onto the cobbles.

There was a crowd, because there is always a crowd, and the crowd was silent, which she had never seen before and hoped never to see again. Fires draw noise — shouting, orders, the bucket lines, children, dogs. This fire drew none. The bucket chains worked with the grim economy of people performing a duty they knew was theater, saving the neighboring roofs and letting the print house go, and everyone, everyone, looked at the ground or the flames and never at each other.

Because everyone knew. You could see the knowledge moving through the crowd like a current under ice. No cry of *arson* was raised, and none needed to be. The watchmen of the Dawn arrived in good time to hold back the spectators and in no time at all to fight the fire. One of them was smoking.

Coral stood in the wet street until her hair soaked through and her feet went numb, and watched Madame Ferrand's forty years of paper become light and heat and a column of sparks that rose and bent seaward and scattered over the harbor like a black snowfall of ash, flakes of it landing on the water, on the boats, on her outflung hands. Each flake had been a word. Tide tables, almanacs, marriage bills, the two Ren pamphlets pulled in defiance of the censor — proofs, corrections, the marginalia of a long careful life in letters — all of it ascending in one bright exhalation and coming down as gray smut.

Someone beside her — a journeyman printer she half-knew, Gaspard from the chandlery press — said, without turning his head, "They found pamphlets. Supposedly. Seditious stock, hidden in the archive room. Found them the same night the fire started, imagine the luck." His voice was flat, pitched to carry no further than her. "Imagine."

"Was anyone hurt?"

"Madame F. wasn't there. Staying with her sister since Monday, since the soldiers came." He paused. "Her cat wouldn't leave. Someone tried."

Coral closed her eyes. Behind them the fire printed itself in red negative, an image that would not distribute, that would stay set in her forever.

"The notice board has a new one up," Gaspard said. "Effective immediately, all private archives of print houses are state property pending review. Voluntary surrender, they call it." He snorted, softly, terribly. "Voluntary."

* * *

She walked home through the ash-smelling streets with her mind working the way it worked during a difficult press run — coldly, sequentially, one problem at a time, because the problems would not solve themselves and panic jammed the machine. The Quill and Anchor had surrendered its ledgers Monday and burned Thursday. Surrender had not saved it; surrender had *dated* it. Which meant the fire was not punishment but erasure — the Committee did not merely want the papers, it wanted the absence of papers, a clean page on which to compose the history it had already typeset in private. And her shop, her father's shop, held the one archive in Vellamare that could contradict that composition line by line.

They would come for Lockridge's. Perhaps not this week; Mereau had smiled at her, had commissioned broadsides, had asked his too-precise question about Ren's papers and received her lie. Charm was his instrument, and instruments are set aside when blunter tools serve. Sooner or later, the blunt tools would walk through her door with a receipt for voluntary surrender, and she would hand over the proof books or she would not, and either answer ended a certain version of her life.

Unless the books ceased, beforehand, to exist.

By the time she reached the shop the rain had thickened, and she stood a moment in the doorway looking at the dark windows, at the faded gold letters her father had gilded himself — LOCKRIDGE & SON, though there had never been a son, only her, and he had died before correcting the plate — and felt something shift in her chest with an almost audible click, the way a forme locks into the chase. Fear was still there; fear would always be there. But the fear had changed employment. It was no longer sitting idle in her, rusting. It was working.

Down in the cellar she relit the lamp, knelt at the northeast wall, and pried up the boards she had not yet nailed down. The wrapped volumes lay in their trench of brick and canvas like bodies prepared for burial — and no, she thought, not like burial; like seed. Her father had buried the truth out of cowardice and it had sat there growing nothing. She was burying it out of resolve, and resolve was different: resolve intended a springtime. These books would come up out of the ground. She would see to it personally, with ink and paper and the old flatbed press under its shroud, and what the Committee of the Dawn proposed to erase would be set again, line upon line, in type they could not recall.

She laid the last volume — the one with *A.R., private commission* in her father's hand — flatest of all, deepest, safest. Over it went the canvas, the oilcloth, the sacking. She replaced the boards, swept the seams, scattered planer shavings and a spill of waste lead over the disturbed dust, and stepped back to inspect the wall. It looked like what it had always looked like: the neglected northeast corner of a failing cellar. Even she could scarcely see what slept there. Good. Let it rest. Let it wait in the dark with its forty thousand words, patient as its medium, patient as ink.

Above her, muffled by floors, the shop bell rang — the front door, past midnight, in this rain.

Coral blew out the lamp in one breath and stood absolutely still, listening. Footsteps crossed the composing room overhead, unhurried, familiar in a way that stopped her heart and then restarted it at double time: not soldiers' boots. Softer. Wet cloth and a walking stick's tap she had heard only once before, at midnight, with impossible letters in a satchel.

Voss was back. Two weeks early, dripping on her floor at one in the morning — and from the weight of his tread, heavier now, he had not come alone with good news.

She took the stairs in the dark, three at a time.
