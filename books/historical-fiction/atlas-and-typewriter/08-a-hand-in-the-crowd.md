## 8. A Hand in the Crowd

The rumor came to Voss the way most truths came to Vellamare — sideways, soaked in salt, and smelling faintly of herring.

He heard it from a woman named Odile Ferrant, who kept a stall of dried cod two aisles from the fishmongers' row and who had once, in another life, carried letters for the Committee of the Dawn the way Voss had. She had found him at the back of the Salt Cellar tavern, where he'd been nursing a cup of wine he had no intention of finishing, and she had sat down across from him without asking, the way old comrades do when the asking itself would be a confession.

"You still ask after him," she said. Not a question. She said it the way one might say *you still limp on that leg*.

"I ask after the truth," Voss answered, which was the answer he always gave, and which had the advantage of being almost true.

"Mm." Odile turned her cup in her hands. She was a broad woman gone soft at the edges, with a face like a dropped plate — cracked but holding. "There was a man at the fish market this morning. Before dawn, when the boats come in. Bought nothing. Stood at the end of the row where the light comes off the water, just watching. And when the gulls came down after the offal, he walked away." She paused, and Voss understood that the pause was the whole of the message, the way a printer understands that the space between words is also ink. "He walked the way Ren walked."

Voss set down his cup very carefully, because his hand wanted to shake and he would not permit it in front of a witness.

"You saw his face?"

"I saw his back. I saw his shoulders. I saw—" She stopped, and her voice dropped into something older and more careful. "I carried his satchel for two years, Emeric. I know how the man moved. The left foot comes down flat, like he's testing the ground for a lie. The right follows quick. Like a man perpetually about to change his mind and never quite doing it."

Voss knew the walk. Of course he knew the walk. He had followed that walk through nine cities and twice as many betrayals, had watched it cross a courtyard in Brasseny with a warrant out on it, had memorized it the way Coral Lockridge had memorized the cut of a letter — not as a thing seen but as a thing *known*, carried in the body below thought.

"You're sure it wasn't a trick of the morning light," he said. "A fisherman with a bad ankle."

"Perhaps." Odile stood, dropping a coin beside her untouched cup — a courtesy to the house, since she'd bought nothing either. "But you'll go look. I know you'll go look, because you're still that kind of fool." At the edge of the tavern's gloom she turned back, and the lamplight caught the scar along her jaw, an old one, from the days when carrying pamphlets could cost more than a beating. "Emeric. If it's him — and it isn't, because dead men don't buy mackerel — but if it were, what then? What would you do with a man who let the whole world believe he was dead?"

Voss had no answer. He watched her go, and the question sat down in the chair she'd left warm, and it stayed with him all night, turning over and over like a boat at anchor in a swell.

* * *

The fish market at Vellamare began before the sun did.

Voss arrived in the blue-grey hour when the boats were still unloading, when the quarter smelled of brine and tar and the particular sweetness of fish gone too long on ice, when the lamps along the quay burned in little halos of fog. He had dressed for the part — a fish porter's oilskin, sleeves rolled, hands stained at the knuckles with the grease he'd rubbed into them that morning from a lamp he had no business owning. He carried an empty basket. A man with a basket is furniture; a man without one is a question, and questions get answered in Vellamare these days by men with batons.

The market spread along the harbor's inner curve in a long crooked row of stalls under striped canvas, and beyond it the water lay flat and pewter-colored, breathing. Gulls screamed and wheeled and dropped. Porters shouted prices in the shorthand of men who have said the same numbers ten thousand times. Somewhere a knife was being sharpened against a steel rod with a rhythm like a slow heartbeat.

Voss took up a position near the end of the row — the end Odile had described, where the stalls ran out and the light came clean off the water — and made himself into furniture.

He watched.

He watched the fishwives with their red knuckles and their voices like gulls. He watched a boy of perhaps ten gut mackerel with the bored precision of a surgeon. He watched two men in plain coats who were not watching fish at all — he marked them early, the way you mark anything in a city under a provisional government: by what they *didn't* look at. They stood near the customs shed, smoking, turning their faces along the crowd in slow sweeps. Mereau's men, or the city watch, or both — the distinction had grown thin since the uprising. The revolution had learned to watch its own, and it watched with the same patient appetite the old regime had, only now the watchers wore red cockades and called it liberty.

An hour passed. The light came up off the water, turning the fog to gauze, turning the harbor to hammered tin. Voss bought a mackerel he didn't want, to keep his basket honest. He let his eyes do their old work — the work of two years as a courier, when noticing had been the whole of his trade — cataloguing faces, gaits, the geometry of the crowd.

And then, at the far end of the row, where the stalls gave way to the net-menders' benches —

A man stood looking at the water.

He was ordinary in every particular that mattered: middle height, grey coat, a fisherman's knit cap pulled low. He held nothing. He bought nothing. He stood at the exact place Odile had described, at the seam between the market and the open light, and he stood the way no fisherman stands — with his weight settled and patient, like a man waiting not for a boat but for a *moment*.

Then he turned, and began to walk away, up the lane toward the salt warehouses.

The left foot came down flat, testing the ground for a lie. The right followed quick.

Voss's heart did something he had not permitted it to do in two years. It *leapt*. It was a shameful, boyish, unguarded thing, that leap — the heart of a courier who had once believed that Aldous Ren was the finest man in Vellamare, perhaps in the world, and who had spent two years since learning better and had never quite finished unlearning it. The rumor, the hope, the impossible arithmetic of a dead man's walk — all of it went through him in a single hot pulse, and his feet were already moving, the basket abandoned on a stall's corner, the mackerel rolling free onto the cobbles behind him.

He did not run. Running was a confession. He walked — fast, too fast, weaving between porters and carts — keeping the grey coat in his sightline as it moved up the lane between the salt warehouses, where the street narrowed and the smell of brine gave way to the dry, mineral smell of stacked cod.

The man turned once. Not fully — a half-turn of the head, a glance back along the lane, the kind of glance a man makes when he knows the grammar of being followed. And in the half-light of that turn, for one instant, Voss saw the line of the jaw beneath the cap's brim, the particular set of the mouth —

*Aldous.*

Not said aloud. Said in the blood. Two years of grief and anger and the slow, corrosive work of revising a hero into a man — all of it burned away in that instant, and what was left was the courier again, the young man in Brasseny, the believer.

The lane bent. Voss took the bend at the edge of running, one hand out to catch the wall, and the lane —

— was empty.

The grey coat was nowhere. The lane ran forty yards to a gate in the warehouse wall, and the gate stood ajar, and beyond it was nothing but stacked crates and fog, and no man in a grey coat had passed through it in the time it had taken Voss to round the bend. He knew it with the sick certainty of a man who has just watched a door close. The lane had no other exits. The walls were high. The fog sat in it like wool.

He stood in the middle of the lane, breathing hard, and felt the hope drain out of him the way heat leaves a stone at dusk — not all at once, but steadily, and leaving the stone colder than the air around it.

*You imagined it.* The thought arrived in Odile's voice. *Dead men don't buy mackerel. Dead men don't stand in fish markets. You are a man who has wanted to see a ghost for two years, and the fog gave you one.*

But the walk. The flat left foot. The quick right. He had seen it. He had *followed* it. A trick of the light did not walk forty yards up a lane.

He was still standing there, still arguing with himself in two voices, when the whistles began.

* * *

They came from the quay — three notes, high and sharp, repeated — and Voss knew the sound the way the body knows a fall before the mind agrees to it. The sweep. The quarter-clearing. The provisional government had grown fond of them since the amnesty debates began: a whistle, a cordon, and every man in the market asked for his papers while soldiers moved through the stalls with the unhurried confidence of men who already knew what they would find, because finding it was their trade and their pleasure both.

He should have stayed in the lane. The lane was empty and the lane was quiet and a man standing alone in an empty lane is no one at all. But the market was behind him, and the market was where he'd left a basket and a mackerel and, more damningly, an oilskin and a face that a woman named Odile had watched him wear all morning, and if the sweep had been called because of the grey coat — if the man in the grey coat had been *bait*, and the watchers by the customs shed had been waiting for exactly the fool who would break from the crowd and chase him —

The thought arrived a half-second too late, the way all his worst thoughts did.

He turned back toward the market and the cordon was already closing. He saw it from the mouth of the lane: soldiers in the blue-grey of the new republic walking the row in pairs, and the plain-coated men from the customs shed now moving with purpose, and the crowd compressing itself toward the center like a fist closing. A fishwife was shouting. The boy with the knife had gone still, his blade flat on the board, his eyes wide and professional — a child who had already learned what a sweep meant.

Voss did the arithmetic of escape the way he'd once done it in nine cities, and the arithmetic came back the same ugly number every time: the quay was closed, the lane behind him was a trap with one door, and the only way out was through the net-menders' benches at the lane's end, where the warehouses backed onto a yard of stacked crates and drying line.

He went through the gate. He was three crates deep into the yard, looking for a gap in the wall, when the first of them came around the corner.

What followed was brief and unglorious and had very little of the hero about it.

There were two of them — watchmen, not soldiers, which was worse, because soldiers followed orders and watchmen followed their appetites. The first one swung a baton and Voss caught it on his forearm, the blow numbing the arm to the elbow, and the second one took him around the legs and put him down onto the wet cobbles with the full indifferent weight of the state. He got an elbow into someone's teeth. He was, in his younger years, not a bad fighter, and some of it was still in him — enough to make them angry, which was a mistake, because anger is what turns a beating into a lesson. The baton found his ribs twice, three times, a fourth in a place that made the world go white at the edges. Someone knelt on his back and went through his pockets with the thoroughness of a customs man, and found the empty basket tag, and the few coins, and the folded paper he had been a fool to carry — the note of the letters' dates, the thing he'd written out for Coral Lockridge's comparison, in his own hand, with the words *Aldous Ren* on it in his own writing.

The kneeling man read it. Voss felt him read it — felt the stillness come into the weight on his spine, the particular stillness of a man who has found more than he was sent to find.

"Turn him over," the man said.

They turned him over. Voss looked up into a face he half knew — a watch sergeant named Brac, a man who had been a smuggler before the revolution and a revolutionary before the amnesty, and who was therefore exactly the kind of man who understood what a name on a paper was worth.

"Emeric Voss," Brac said, and smiled with the satisfaction of a fisherman feeling a line go taut. "The courier. They said you'd come back to the city." He crouched, forearms on knees, and held the paper up between two fingers. "And here you are, chasing ghosts through the fish market. Or is it ghosts?" He tapped the name. "You tell me, courier. Who is Aldous Ren to you?"

"Dead," Voss said, through blood in his mouth. "They hanged him. Your side made a saint of him. Ask your own committee."

"Oh, I know the official version." Brac folded the paper with great care and put it inside his coat, against his chest, where papers of value go. "I also know what a morning's work is worth, and this is worth more than a morning." He stood. "Here's what happens now. You're going to lie there and think about your ribs. We're going to report that we caught a drunk resisting the watch — a nothing, a formality, no name attached, because names go up the chain and up the chain is where the *committee* lives, and I am not a man who shares." He smiled again, wider. "But I'll keep the paper. And one day soon, courier, someone will ask me what I know, and I will tell them, and you will wish you'd drowned on the packet boat. Hold him until we're clear of the quarter, then let him crawl."

They let him crawl. It took him the better part of an hour to get upright, and longer to get moving, one hand pressed to his ribs, walking the long way through streets that smelled of tar and rain, past shuttered windows and the painted faces of Mereau on the walls, *THE DAWN IS OURS*, the letters neat and triumphant, set by some printer who had never once wondered what the dawn cost.

* * *

Coral Lockridge opened the shop door before he could knock.

He had meant to go to his room above the Salt Cellar, meant to lick his wounds in private the way a courier does — alone, in the dark, with a bottle and a ledger of his own stupidity. But his feet had carried him through the printing quarter without his consulting them, and there was the shop with its lamp lit in the window and its sign — *LOCKRIDGE & SON*, the paint gone grey, the lie of it softened by her father's death into something more like a prayer — and there was Coral behind the glass, setting type by lamplight, her hands moving in the small precise dance he had begun, without deciding to, to find beautiful.

She opened the door and looked at him, and whatever she saw in his face made her step back and let him in without a word.

He sat down on the type bench because his legs declined to do anything else. The shop smelled of ink and oil and cold metal, and the press stood in the corner like a patient animal, and it was, absurdly, the safest room in Vellamare, and he was shaking now — the shaking that comes after, that the body saves up and spends once the danger has passed, like a debt called in.

"They swept the fish market," he said. "Because of me. Or because of him. I don't know which, and that's the worst of it — I don't know which."

"Slowly," Coral said. She poured coffee from the pot that lived on the stove — bitter, burnt, the coffee of people who work past midnight — and put it in his hands, and wrapped his fingers around the cup because they wouldn't close on their own. "From the beginning."

He told it. The rumor, the market, the man at the end of the row. The walk. The lane. The empty lane. The whistles. The yard. The paper with the name on it, and Brac's smile, and the weight of the sergeant's knee, and the paper going into the coat, against the chest, where papers of value go.

Coral listened without interrupting, which was the thing about her that Voss had come to trust before he'd come to trust anything else — she listened the way she cut type, completely, giving the whole of her attention to the single stroke in front of her. When he finished she stood for a while with her arms folded, looking at the press, at the cases of type, at the lamplight lying along the composing stone.

"You saw the walk," she said at last.

"I saw the walk."

"You're certain."

"I followed it forty yards, Coral. A trick of the light doesn't have a gait." He drank the coffee and it scalded his split lip and he was grateful for the pain, because it was a simple pain, an honest one, and the other kind — the kind in his chest — was not. "But here's what I keep circling. If it was him — if Aldous Ren stood at the end of that row this morning — then he wanted to be seen. Or he wanted to be *followed*. And the sweep came too fast after. Too convenient." He looked up at her. "Someone knew I'd be there. Odile told me yesterday. Odile, or the stall keeper, or the man in the grey coat himself. It was bait, Coral. And I took it, and now there's a watch sergeant in this city holding a paper with Ren's name on it, in my hand, and a story about a drunk who chased a ghost, and sooner or later that story is going to climb the chain. And at the top of the chain—"

"—is Mereau," Coral said quietly.

"Is Mereau."

The lamp hissed. Outside, somewhere in the quarter, a cart went by, its wheels loud on the stones, and then the street was quiet again. Coral came and sat beside him on the bench, not close, but near enough that he could feel the warmth of her, and they sat in the smell of ink and metal like two people keeping a vigil.

"Then we have less time than we thought," she said. "If Brac sells that paper, Mereau knows someone is asking after Ren. He knows it's you. And he knows—" she paused, and Voss watched her arrive at it, watched her reach the same cold ledge he had been standing on since the yard — "he knows it's the shop. You come here. Everyone in the quarter can see you come here."

Voss said nothing. The coffee had gone cold in his hands.

He had come back to Vellamare carrying letters and a certainty, and he had told himself the certainty was about the dead — about proving what happened to Aldous Ren, about setting the record straight against the historians of the victors. He had not told himself the truth, which was that hope was not a lamp he carried but a fuse he'd lit, and that everything it touched would burn with it: the shop, the woman on the bench beside him, the boy apprentice asleep somewhere above them, the whole small fragile world of type and paper that Coral Lockridge was trying to keep alive in a city that had decided what the truth was and meant to burn everything that disagreed.

He had wanted to see a ghost. And now the ghost had a price, and the price had a face, and the face was sitting next to him, drinking cold coffee, already thinking — he could see it in her hands, the way her thumb moved along the edge of the composing stone — already thinking about what to set in type next, and how fast, and how to hide it.

"Tomorrow," he said, "I want to go through your father's proof books again. Every letter. Every date. There's something in the pattern I missed — something about where the letters were *sent from*."

Coral looked at him. And then, from the shadows at the back of the shop, where the type cellar door stood ajar, came a small sound — the scrape of a foot on the stair, quick and light, and then stillness. A foot that had been on the stair a long time. A foot that had been listening.

Coral rose, and her voice, when she called into the dark, was the voice of a woman who already knew.

"Tibo. Come down here."
