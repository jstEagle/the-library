## 9. The Apprentice Who Watched

He came down the stairs the way a boy comes down when he knows he has been caught — one step at a time, hand dragging along the wall, as if the wall might be persuaded to hide him. He was holding his cap in both hands, crushing it into a shape no cap was ever meant to hold. His eyes went to Coral first, then to Voss, then to the floor, which was where they stayed.

"I wasn't—" he began.

"You were," Coral said. Not unkindly. She had rehearsed anger on the walk from the door to the foot of the stair, had built it up like a forme locked tight, and found it already loosening in her hands. Fear did that. Three days of fear — of Mereau's smooth questions, of the letters that could not exist, of the way her own shop had turned strange to her — and there was nothing left over for fury at a fourteen-year-old boy. "Sit down, Tibo."

He sat on the paper stool by the guillotine, perched on its edge like a bird ready to fly, cap still wringing in his fists. The lamp on the composing stone threw his shadow long across the cellar floor, so that a giant sat hunched behind the small boy, and Coral thought: *that is exactly how it is with all of us.*

"How much did you hear?"

"All of it." His voice cracked on the second word and he pushed through anyway, the way he pushed through everything, stubborn as a knot in green wood. "Since you started talking about the proof books. I wasn't spying. I was banking the fire, like you told me, and then I heard the name—"

"Aldous Ren."

"Aldous Ren." He said it carefully, reverently, the way everyone in Vellamare said it now, though Tibo had been nine years old when Ren died and should have been too young to carry the name with any weight. But children carried things here. The whole city was a shop full of apprentices carrying things too heavy for them.

Voss had not moved. He stood at the end of the composing stone with his wet coat still dripping — he never seemed to fully dry, this man, as if the packet boat had followed him inland — and he watched Tibo with the particular attention of a man who has spent years watching people decide whether to tell the truth. Coral had learned to read that attention. It was not suspicion. It was patience, held at arm's length, ready.

"Before you say anything else," Coral said, "understand what you have walked into. What we spoke of tonight can cost lives. Yours included. Boys have been taken for less than listening at doors, since the amnesty talk started, and no one would ask questions after. Do you understand me?"

Tibo nodded. Then, because he was Tibo, he said, "I understand, but I think you should hear me first."

Coral looked at Voss. Voss raised one eyebrow a fraction — *let him speak* — and something in the gesture, the small democracy of it, decided her.

"Go on, then."

The boy took a breath, and let go of the cap, smoothing it flat against his knee over and over as he talked, as if the words needed something to press them out.

"It was last winter. Before your father died." He stopped. Started again. "You remember I used to run parcels for him. Errands. When his hip was bad and he didn't want to climb Quay Street."

"I remember," Coral said. Her father's hip had been bad for six years; the errands had been running nearly as long. Ink from Serrano's. Paper stock from the Harborside warehouse. A ledger she had always meant to ask about and never had. A hundred small deliveries, each one ordinary, each one beneath notice — which, she understood now with a cold settling feeling, was precisely the point of them.

"This one was different. Your father came down here himself — down these stairs, bad hip and all, holding the rail like it owed him money — and he took a parcel off the shelf behind the proof books. The high shelf. The one nobody reaches." Tibo pointed, and his finger shook slightly. "Wrapped in oilcloth, tied with tarred cord. Heavy. Books, or metal, something with corners. And he says, *Tibo, tonight you're going to earn a month's wages.*"

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, in the street, a cart rattled past toward the fish market, and Coral waited until it had gone before she trusted her voice. "What night was this?"

"First of Thaw-Month."

Coral did the arithmetic without wanting to. Everyone in Vellamare had done that arithmetic for two years, at every tavern table and washline in the city. Aldous Ren had died on the twenty-second of Frost-Month. The execution was the fixed point around which the whole revolution now swung — the date carved on the martyr's stone in the Place of Bells, the date Mereau spoke every year with his head bowed just so. First of Thaw-Month was eight days later.

Eight days after the state killed Aldous Ren, her father had sent a parcel — heavy, secret, wrapped against salt water — to Gray Skerry.

"What was in it?" Voss asked. His voice was very quiet. Quiet the way deep water is quiet.

"He didn't say. He made me swear on my mother's grave I wouldn't open it, and I didn't — I wanted to, mind, I held it under my coat the whole way and it pressed against my ribs like it was alive — but I swore." The boy's chin came up, defiant against the expectation of mockery. "I'm not sorry I listened tonight. I've been carrying it two years, that parcel. Not knowing where it went. Only knowing it mattered more than anything your father ever gave me, because of how he looked when he handed it over. Like a man handing off a brand from a fire."

"And you delivered it?" Coral asked. "To Gray Skerry? In winter?"

"Tomas Odo's boat. He goes out twice a month with stores for the light, weather allowing, and doesn't ask names." Tibo almost smiled; it was an old man's smile on a young face. "Weather allowed, barely. I was sick twice over the rail. The keeper met us at the landing — old man, white beard, hands like tree roots. He didn't thank me and he didn't speak, hardly. Just took the parcel and weighed it in his hands like your father had done, and looked at me a long time. And then he said one thing."

The boy paused. Coral realized she had come forward without noticing, off her stool, drawn to the edge of the lamplight like a moth that had forgotten caution.

"What did he say?"

Tibo looked down at the cap again. When he spoke, he dropped his voice, though they were three souls and a lamp in a sealed cellar and no one living knew what he was about to say.

"He said: *Tell Lockridge the light still burns.* That's all. Tell Lockridge the light still burns."

* * *

For a long moment nobody moved. The press stood in the corner of the cellar, her grandfather's press, iron and oak, patient as a headstone, and Coral felt it watching her the way it had watched her all her life — the whole weight of the shop's dead generations gathered behind her shoulders.

"The light still burns," Voss repeated. He said it slowly, tasting it, turning it over the way he must once have turned over the letters in his coat. "That's not code for a lamp, is it, Tibo. Think. Did your father ever use the word *light*? In the shop? On proofs?"

Tibo frowned with the effort of memory. Coral found she could not think at all. Her mind kept returning to a single image, absurdly small: her father's hands on the high shelf, dust on his knuckles, the oilcloth crackling. Her father, who had taught her that type was honest because it could not lie about its size. Her father, who had limped down these stairs in the dark of a thawing winter and put a parcel into a child's keeping rather than trust any adult in Vellamare.

*He knew who to trust,* she thought. *And it wasn't anyone grown.*

"There was a phrase," Tibo said slowly. "When he corrected my setting, when I'd spaced a line wrong and squeezed the words — he'd tap the stick and say, *mind the light between the letters.* The space. The counters. He called empty space *light*, the way the old compositor terms do." He looked up suddenly, eyes wide. "You don't think—"

"Words set with light between them," Coral said. Her mouth was dry. "A message hidden in spacing. Or a book. Something printed." She thought of the parcel's weight, its corners. Metal, or pages. Something with edges. "Something only another printer could read properly. Which means whoever holds it on that rock has been sitting on a message they cannot read for two years — unless my father told them otherwise."

"Or unless," Voss said, and now his voice had changed, quickened, the scholar's excitement cutting through the courier's caution, "the keeper was never meant to read it. Only to keep it. A cache, Coral. A dead drop with a lighthouse on it. Your father didn't send a parcel to Gray Skerry eight days after Ren died. Someone sent it *through* your father. And if the timing is what I think it is—"

"Then Ren may have been alive when it left this shop." Coral finished it because it had to be finished, because leaving it unfinished would let it grow larger than the room. Eight days. A boat, tarred cord, a white-bearded keeper, a boy sick over a rail. A dead man's belongings moving through her family's hands while the city below burned effigies of him and wept.

She sat down slowly on the composing stone's bench. Her legs simply declined to hold her any longer.

"My father," she said, and stopped. Started again. "My father charged fair prices, paid his taxes, mended the church notices for free, and never once — never *once* in my life — let me see him do a dishonest thing. And he was running messages for a man the whole world believed dead, out of my shop, under my roof, and he never told me."

"He was protecting you," Tibo said at once, loyally.

"He was *using* me," Coral said, and heard the bitterness land harder than she'd intended, saw the boy flinch. "No. No, that's not—" She pressed her palms against her eyes. "He was protecting the shop. And me inside it. If I didn't know, I couldn't betray it, couldn't be made to betray it. Ignorance was the wrapping paper." She lowered her hands. "It's what I would have done. That's the worst of it. That's exactly what I would have done."

Voss crouched down beside her bench, so their heads were level. Up close his face had the grain of a woodcut — every line cut deliberately, meaning something.

"Coral. Listen to me. This changes everything. You asked me why I came to you, of all the printers in Vellamare. I told you it was your matrices, your father's reputation for discretion. But there was more." He hesitated, and she watched him choose honesty the way a man watches weather choose rain. "Ren had a list of safe houses and trusted trades. It was old — from before the committee split — but names persist in that world longer than people do. Lockridge & Daughter, Vellamare. I came here because your shop was on a dead man's list."

"Then why wait until now to bring the letters?"

"Because I thought the list was cold. Because I thought everyone on it was dead or scattered." He glanced at Tibo, then back. "I was wrong. There's a thread running from Aldous Ren to a lighthouse on Gray Skerry, and it runs through this cellar. And whatever sits at the other end of it has been waiting two years for someone to come pull it."

"The keeper said the light still burns," Tibo said softly. "Maybe that's not just a message. Maybe it's an invitation. Maybe he's been waiting too."

Outside, above them, the tide was turning; they could hear it in the bones of the building, the harbor breathing against the seawall. Coral thought of the archive smoke rising over the Harborside district two nights ago, the provisional government's men feeding history into the flames page by page, and she thought of her father's proof books upstairs, and the high shelf, dusty, unreached, holding whatever it had held since before she inherited these debts.

She had thought inheritance meant the shop. The press, the type, the bench, the ledger of what was owed. Four walls and a trade. She had grieved it like a millstone.

But inheritance, it turned out, was not property. It was a parcel wrapped in oilcloth, passed hand to hand in the dark, with a fire in it that someone had to carry and someone had to keep. Her father had carried it. Now the weight sat in her own hands, and there was nowhere left to set it down.

She stood.

"Tibo." The boy straightened as if pulled by strings. "The high shelf. Behind the proof books. Is there anything there now?"

His eyes went enormous. "I don't know. I never looked. I swore I never—"

"Fetch the ladder."

He was up the stairs two at a time. Voss rose beside her, and for a moment they stood together in the lamplight, and he said quietly, "Whatever's up there — it may name the men who sold him. Including Mereau. Are you certain you want to know?"

"No," Coral said honestly. "But my father never asked my permission to carry his part. I won't ask his to carry mine."

Above them, boards creaked. The ladder scraped. And then Tibo's voice came down through the floor, breathless, cracking in the middle, pitched higher than she had ever heard it:

"Mistress Lockridge! Come quick! There's writing on the underside of the shelf — fresh wax over it, but it's cut deep — and it says, *For the daughter, when she asks.*"
