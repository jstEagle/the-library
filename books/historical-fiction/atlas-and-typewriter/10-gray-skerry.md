## 10. Gray Skerry

The boatman wanted three times the going rate and half the money up front, and even then he would not look at Voss while he named the price.

"Gray Skerry isn't a place people go," he said, coiling rope with hands like knotted oak. "It's a place people are put."

"I'll pay four times."

That bought him a deck that leaked at every seam, a sail patched with flour-sack cloth, and a departure before dawn from a cove south of the harbor where no one asked names. Voss sat in the stern with his back against wet wood and watched Vellamare shrink behind them — first the citadel, then the bell towers, then only a gray smudge of smoke against a paler sky — and he thought about how many times he had made this crossing in reverse. How many nights he had rowed or sailed toward that city carrying Aldous Ren's words sewn into his coat lining, believing he was carrying fire.

He had been twenty-three then. He was thirty now, and the crossing had not gotten shorter, only heavier.

The sea off Vellamare in autumn was the color of old pewter, and it did not forgive small boats. By midmorning the wind had come around north and begun to shove them sideways, and the boatman — his name was Perrin, or so he claimed — swore steadily under his breath in the dialect of the outer islands, a language composed almost entirely of blasphemies against saints. Spray came over the gunwale in cold sheets. Voss bailed with a wooden pail and tried not to think about drowning, because thinking about drowning led to thinking about what would happen to the letters if he drowned, and to Coral, alone in her shop with evidence enough to hang them both twice over.

"Skerry's got no landing worth the name," Perrin shouted over the wind. "There's a stair cut in the rock on the east face, when the tide's out far enough. Old lightkeeper built it himself. You'll have maybe two hours before it comes back and takes the stair away."

"And the keeper?"

Perrin spat over the side. "You'll see."

* * *

They raised Gray Skerry just past noon, a black fist of granite rising perhaps eighty feet out of a sea gone suddenly calm, as if the water itself were afraid of it. The lighthouse stood at the summit — squat, whitewashed once, now weathered to the color of bone — its lantern room dark, its gallery rail furred with salt and the droppings of ten thousand birds. Below it clung a low stone cottage with a slate roof, and beyond the cottage, terraced down the rock, the ghost of a garden gone entirely to thrift and sea-pink.

Voss saw the stair as the boat came around the eastern shoulder: steps cut into the living rock, descending into a channel of green water that foamed and sucked at their bases. The tide was already turning.

"Two hours," Perrin said. "Not one minute more, or you'll be swimming home and I won't wait to watch."

Voss went up the stair on legs that had not stopped aching since the soldiers swept the fish market quarter three days ago. His ribs still sang where a rifle butt had found them; his left eye had finally cleared but the socket remained tender to the touch. He climbed anyway, hand over hand on the iron ring set into the rock, and told himself the ache was nothing. He had been beaten worse for less important things.

The keeper's cottage door was unlocked. Of course it was. No one locked a door on Gray Skerry.

Inside, the air smelled of cold ash and lamp oil and something else — something sweetish and wrong that took him a moment to identify, and another moment to wish he hadn't. It was the smell of sickness that has soaked into a room and stayed. He knew it. He had smelled it in the garret where they'd hidden Ren the winter before the uprising, when the cough started and wouldn't stop.

There was a table, a chair, a cold stove, a shelf of unopened tins. A cot against the far wall with the blankets still turned back. And on the table, centered precisely, weighted down by a brass lamp base, a package wrapped in oilcloth and tied with cord, and beside it an envelope bearing a single line in handwriting Voss would have known blindfolded, in the dark, at the bottom of the sea:

*For Emeric, who carried everything else.*

* * *

He sat down in the chair because his legs would not hold him, and for a long moment he simply looked at the envelope without touching it. Two years. Two years of rumor and denial and the slow calcification of Aldous Ren into marble, into murals, into a face on banners held by men who had never met him. Two years of Voss telling anyone who would listen — and lately only Coral, who listened better than anyone — that the martyr lived, that the execution had been theater, that Ren had slipped the old regime's noose the way he had slipped everything else.

And here was the answer, sitting on a table on a rock in the sea, waiting for him with terrible patience.

He opened the envelope first. Inside was a single sheet, folded twice, dated six weeks after the execution.

*Emeric —*

*If you are reading this, then either I am dead, or I have failed again to write the letter I promised myself I would write, which amounts to nearly the same thing.*

*You were the best of my couriers and the worst of my believers. I mean that as praise. You believed in me the way sailors believe in lighthouses, and it was cruel of me to let you, knowing what I knew: that lights mark the rocks as much as they mark the way.*

*The fever came with the crossing. The keeper — his name is Anselme; say it aloud sometimes, he gave me two years of his life and never asked why — says it is the same ague that killed his brother. My lungs are filling. I write while I still can hold a pen, because there are things that must be set down by a hand, not spoken into air where they can be denied.*

*You will find my testament in the oilcloth. Read it. Then decide, as I could not, whether the truth serves the living better than the legend serves the dead. I have gone back and forth on this until the pages wore thin. In the end I wrote it all — the committee's bargains, Mereau's meeting with the prefect's man at the Hôtel Delorme, the money, the dates — because a lie of omission is still a lie, and I have told too many.*

*But hear me, Emeric: do not let them make me into a god. Gods ask nothing of their worshippers, and I am asking everything. If the truth is useful, use it. If it is only sad, let it be sad honestly.*

*I escaped the scaffold, yes. Tell them that, if it comforts them. I lived six weeks longer than the prefect's report allows, and in those six weeks I was more honest than I had been in all the years before. That is the whole of my miracle. A man died here, of fever, in a clean bed, frightened and forgiven and mostly at peace. Let that be enough.*

*Your friend, always more than your master,*

*Aldous*

* * *

Voss read the letter three times. The third time his eyes blurred and he let them, sitting in the dead man's chair in the dead man's room, listening to the sea work patiently at the rock below.

So. Not alive. Never alive, these two years — only escaped, only human, only dying quietly on a granite shelf while Vellamare burned and rose and burned again in his name. While Cassian Mereau stood on platforms and invoked the martyr's sacrifice with tears in his eyes, standing beside the very men who had sold him, wearing his face like a coat.

Voss discovered that his hands were shaking, and that he was angry — not the hot anger of betrayal, which he had expected, but something colder and stranger: embarrassment. Grief's awkward cousin. He had spent two years defending a version of events that was true in every particular except the one that mattered. Ren *had* escaped. He simply hadn't survived it. And somewhere in those two years, without noticing the substitution, Voss had stopped loving a man and started worshipping a myth, and myths cannot disappoint you, which is exactly what makes them lies.

He untied the oilcloth.

The testament inside was thicker than he expected — forty pages, perhaps fifty, written in a hand that began strong and deteriorated page by page into a cramped, laborious scrawl, the pen pressed hard into paper as though the writer feared running out of strength before running out of truth. Voss read the opening pages standing, then the middle ones sitting, and near the end he was reading aloud to himself without meaning to, the way one does in empty rooms, wanting the words to have a body again.

Ren named everyone. He named the sums, the intermediaries, the dates. He described the meeting at the Hôtel Delorme in the spring before the uprising — Mereau, then a rising committee man with a reputation for incorruptibility, dining privately with the prefect's own cousin, and walking out at midnight with a leather case that Ren's watchers photographed through the window. He described how the arrest that followed — *my arrest*, Ren wrote, the ink shaky but deliberate — had been arranged from both sides at once, the old regime buying its traitor in the revolutionary committee, the committee buying its martyr for the cause. *They needed a corpse and I was convenient. What neither side calculated was the scaffold crowd, or the speed of the printing presses, or you, Emeric, riding ahead of the news like the angel on an old church roof.*

And then, near the end, in the smallest and steadiest hand of all, the passage that Voss knew he would carry for the rest of his life:

*Mereau did not betray the revolution. That is the tragedy, and I want it understood. He betrayed me, which is smaller and worse. He looked at the movement we had built and decided it needed a dead man more than a difficult one, and he was probably right, and I will hate him forever for being right. History is full of men who did monstrous arithmetic correctly. Hang the arithmetic on him if it helps, but hang the man, not the sum.*

Voss closed the testament. Outside, the light had shifted; the tide was coming back. He wrapped the pages carefully, tucked them inside his shirt against his chest where the letters had ridden for weeks, and went to look for the keeper.

He found Anselme in the lighthouse itself, halfway up the spiral stair, sitting on a step with a coil of wick in his hands, mending it though there had been no light to tend for two years. He was older than Voss expected — seventy, perhaps more — with a face like driftwood and eyes that assessed Voss in one slow pass and filed him away.

"You're the fourth courier," Anselme said. "I was beginning to think the fifth wouldn't come."

"The fourth?"

"He sent word out twice, the summer before last. No one came. Last spring, a boy." The old man shrugged. "The world was busy. He understood. He wasn't bitter about it, your Aldous. Bitterness takes energy." He wound the wick with fingers stiff as crab claws. "You'll want to know how he died."

"If you'll tell me."

"In his sleep, the third of Maring. Or near enough — I'm no physician. He'd been failing a week. At the end he woke once and asked me to open the shutters so he could see the water." Anselme looked past Voss, toward the narrow window and the sea beyond it, flat and silver in the afternoon. "Then he said something I've kept, not knowing who it belonged to. He said: *Tell Emeric the cargo delivered itself.* Does that mean anything to you?"

Voss found he could not speak for a moment. When he trusted his voice, he said, "It means everything."

"He's buried above the light. No marker. He asked for none — said markers are for people who intend to stay." The keeper rose, joints cracking like kindling, and led Voss up the last turn of the stair and out onto the gallery, where the wind hit them like a wall. Below, at the summit's crown, a low cairn of sea-rounded stones sat among the thrift, indistinguishable from a hundred other piles of stone a careless eye might find on any shore in Vellamare.

"He's there?" Voss shouted against the wind.

"He's there." Anselme put a hand on the rail. "Two years I've kept this rock. Nobody pays me now; the light authority is dissolved, the old man who ran it is fled or dead. But somebody ought to keep it until someone comes, don't you think? Somebody always comes, eventually. You came."

* * *

Voss stood at the cairn for a long time.

He had imagined this conversation a hundred ways across two years — Ren alive in some foreign capital, grayer and heavier, explaining himself over wine; Ren in hiding, asking forgiveness for the deception; even Ren dead violently, shot in some alley, which would at least have been dramatic enough to suit the myth. He had never imagined this: a pile of stones, a mended wick, a keeper who talked about bitterness taking energy. The truth was not a thunderclap. It was a small, clean, unbearable quiet.

He knelt and added a stone to the cairn, one he had picked up on the climb, still warm from his pocket.

"I brought your letter," he said. The wind took most of it. "I'll bring the rest. All of it. Every page, in type, in ink, in whatever survives us." He stopped, because the sentence had a future tense he didn't trust, and because promises made to the dead are the easiest promises in the world to make and the hardest to keep well. "Mereau will answer for the arithmetic. You were right about that much. I'm sorry about everything else."

Below, Perrin was sounding the boat horn — one long, urgent note that flattened and bent in the wind. The tide was eating the stair.

* * *

The crossing back was worse. The wind had veered fully north and the pewter sea had turned the color of iron filings, and Perrin ran them under a scrap of sail with his jaw set and his curses abandoned as insufficient to the occasion. Twice Voss bailed when he should have been holding on, and once he held on when he should have been bailing. The testament stayed dry against his chest, wrapped in oilcloth inside his coat inside his arms, and he caught himself doing it — holding the package the way you hold a sleeping child through rough weather — and did not stop.

Somewhere in the gray middle of the afternoon, with the skerry long sunk behind them and Vellamare still hours ahead, Perrin called back over his shoulder.

"Find what you were looking for?"

Voss considered the question with the honesty the day demanded. "No," he said. "Found something better. Something true."

Perrin grunted, unimpressed. Truth, from the set of his shoulders, ranked somewhere below dry socks.

But it was true, and Voss turned it over in his mind as the coast came up out of the haze — the truth was smaller than the legend, sadder than the hope, and heavier than both, because legends weigh nothing and can be carried anywhere, while truth must be hauled like ballast, and it changes how the whole ship rides.

Aldous Ren was dead. Dead of fever, in a clean bed, forgiven and frightened, six weeks free. Not a god. Not even a proper martyr's death — just a death, ordinary as rain, on a rock nobody visited. And the testament in Voss's shirt named Cassian Mereau not as a monster but as a man who had done monstrous arithmetic correctly, which was somehow more damning and would be, Voss understood now, harder to print and harder to believe.

They came into the cove at dusk. Vellamare glowed faintly beyond the headland, lamplight and watchfires, a city rebuilding itself on a story. Voss climbed out into the shallows and stood a moment in the cold water, letting it close around his boots.

Then he started for the print shop, walking fast, one hand pressed flat against his chest over the pages, because there was a woman at a typeface bench who needed to read what he carried, and a press that needed to hear it, and somewhere south of the city a general amnesty was being drafted that would burn every loose page in Vellamare — and the tide, like the truth, was already coming in.
