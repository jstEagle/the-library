## 11. Setting the Truth

The type cellar smelled of lamp oil, cold iron, and the ghost of her father's tobacco, and Coral Lockridge had always found it the safest room in Vellamare.

Tonight it felt like a vault built around a secret too heavy for its walls.

They had come down after supper, when the streets above went quiet in that particular way of a city pretending to sleep — a silence with soldiers in it. Voss carried the oilcloth packet against his chest like a man transporting a live coal. Coral carried two lamps, a box of candles, and the last loaf of bread her flour jar would yield until market day, if market day still came to a quarter under watch.

She hung the lamps from their hooks on the beams, and the light fell in warm circles across the cases, the stone, the press — her father's old proof press, squat and faithful as a mule, its screw worn smooth by thirty years of his palm.

"So," Voss said. He stood at the bottom of the stairs a moment longer, listening. Above them, the floorboards creaked once — Tibo, walking his rounds from window to window, keeping watch the way they'd taught him, which was badly but earnestly. "No one will see us do this."

"No one sees a printer work," Coral said. "That's the whole point of printers."

He unwrapped the packet on the composing stone with the reverence of a priest unfolding relics, and there it was again: Aldous Ren's testament, written in a hand she had cut into brass with her own burin when she was nineteen years old and believed herself merely skilled, not complicit in history.

She had read it twice already. She would read it a hundred times more before this was done; she knew that now. But knowing the words was not the same as setting them, and setting them was not the same as surviving having set them.

"Page by page," she said, pulling her stool to the case. "We compose one galley at a time. Print it. Distribute the type back into the case. Nothing stays standing overnight. Nothing exists but the metal itself."

"And the metal?"

"The metal looks like every other piece of metal in this room." She ran her fingers along the compartments. "Discarded matrices. Damaged sorts. Type we've been meaning to melt for years. If Mereau's men come through that door, they'll find a failing shop and a woman who can't pay her paper supplier. They will not find a book, because a book is made of pages, and pages are made of standing type, and we will never have any."

Voss watched her hands as she spoke — not her face, her hands, and she understood why. Hands could not lie about fear. Hers were steady enough, though her thumbnail kept finding the groove in the composing stick where her father's thumb had worn it, worrying at it like a rosary bead.

"You've done this before," he said. "Hidden work inside ordinary work."

"My father printed pamphlets during the Bread Riots. Nothing seditious — prices, mostly, and who was hoarding. But the magistrates didn't distinguish." She shrugged. "He taught me that the press is only half the trade. The other half is making sure the press is forgettable."

* * *

The first page took them four hours.

It should have taken one. That was the arithmetic she had done upstairs, in daylight, with confidence: forty copies, eight pages each, three hundred twenty impressions spread across two nights of work, assuming the press behaved and the ink held. She had not accounted for the fact that setting Ren's words meant *reading* Ren's words, slowly, letter by letter, in reverse, so that every sentence passed through her twice — once as meaning, once as mirror.

*"I was not betrayed by enemies,"* the testament began, and she had to stop and wipe her eyes before she could pick up the capital B.

"Here." Voss appeared beside her with the cup of coffee she'd brewed before descending — chicory-cut, bitter as regret, the only kind Vellamare had known since the blockade. He did not say anything else. He simply stood there while she drank, and then went back to his side of the case without comment, and somehow that was worse and better than comfort would have been.

They developed a rhythm without discussing it. He read aloud, low and flat, stripping the words of their drama so she could hear them as work rather than wound. She called out letters. He distributed the used type after each impression, sorting the cold lead back into darkness by feel, because his hands had learned distribution in some other print shop, in some other life, and had never unlearned it.

"Courier work," he said, when she asked. "Ren's committee didn't trust the post. I carried sealed packets between printers in four cities. Half of them are dead now. The other half print official histories."

"Do you miss it? The carrying?"

He considered this seriously, the way he considered everything, turning it over like a coin to check both faces. "I miss being trusted with something," he said. "I don't miss believing I understood what it was."

* * *

By the second hour of the second page, the cellar had contracted around them until it held nothing but lamplight, lead, and breath.

Coral was aware of him in the specific, granular way one becomes aware of another body during shared labor — the sound of his breathing changing when a line fought him, the small grunt of satisfaction when a galley locked up true, the way he leaned over her shoulder to check spacing and left a warmth there that lingered after he moved away. It had been a long time since anyone had stood at her case. Longer since anyone had belonged there.

"This line," he said. *"I forgive the men who sold me, because forgiveness costs me nothing and truth costs them everything."* He read it flat, but his voice cracked anyway on the last word. "Do you believe he meant it? The forgiveness?"

"I believe he wrote it." Coral slid a *t* into place. "Whether he meant it is between him and God, and neither of them is here to ask. We're just the mail."

"That's a cold doctrine for a printer."

"It's the only one that lets you sleep." She pulled the galley, checked it against the copy upside down, as her father had drilled into her until checking upside down became a form of literacy. "You print what's given to you. You don't editorialize with other people's souls."

"And if the thing given to you is a lie?"

"Then you print it accurately," she said, "and someday someone sets the correction. That's all history is, Voss. Corrections, set in type, arriving late."

He was quiet for a moment. Then: "My father was a clergyman."

"I know. You told me. On the boat."

"He would have said history is providence."

"Your father never had to set a broadside for a man who'd hanged his brother." She heard her own voice go flat and didn't apologize for it. "Ink remembers, Voss. That's the whole of my theology. Whatever else burns, the ink remembers what we trusted it with. So we'd better be careful what we trust it with tonight."

* * *

They broke at midnight for bread and coffee, sitting on upturned type cases with their shoulders almost touching, because the cellar was small and fatigue makes people careless about distance.

The press stood between jobs like a sleeping animal, its platen raised, its bed holding the ghost impression of the first page — Ren's testament, printed once in proof, waiting for the run tomorrow. Or rather tonight, Coral corrected herself; the hours past midnight already belonged to the working day they were trying to outrun.

"Forty copies," Voss said, staring into his cup. "After all this. Forty copies of the truth, going out on one ship, to a world that has spent two years building a cathedral around a lie."

"Cathedrals fall," Coral said. "Usually on the people standing in them."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be. Comfort is for people with time." She tore the loaf and handed him the larger half without thinking about it, the way her father had always handed her the larger half, and realized as she did it that she had become the giver of halves, that the shop had passed to her not as property but as posture, as a way of standing in a room. "Forty copies is enough. My father always said a lie needs ten thousand copies because no one wants to carry it. The truth travels light. One honest reader per port, and it walks on its own legs after that."

"Your father said that?"

Her mouth was full of bread and the answer arrived before her caution did. "My father said a lot of things. Some of them he even practiced."

Voss looked at her — really looked, with the attention he usually spent on handwriting and margins — and she felt the look land somewhere below her collarbones. There was a question in it. There were several questions in it, and none of them were about type.

"Tomorrow night," he said instead, gently, "we finish page three."

"Tomorrow night we finish pages three through five, if your reading improves."

"My reading is excellent. Your coffee is a war crime."

Despite everything — the soldiers overhead, the burned archive on Tanner's Row whose ash had still been falling when they buried her father's proof books under the cellar floor, the amnesty coming like tide — despite all of it, Coral laughed. It came out rusty, half-swallowed, but real, and she saw it strike Voss like something unexpected and slightly dangerous. He smiled the way men smile when they have decided not to hope and have failed.

The lamps guttered. Somewhere above, Tibo's footsteps crossed the shop and stopped at the head of the cellar stairs.

"Mistress Lockridge?" The boy's voice came down thin through the boards. "There's a lantern in the street. Across the lane. It's been there since the church bells."

The laughter died in the room like a snuffed wick.

Coral rose, crossed to the foot of the stairs, and blew out both lamps before climbing, leaving Voss in absolute darkness with the testament and the press. At the top she eased open the cellar door a finger's width and looked out through the shop's dark front glass.

Across the lane, in the doorway of the shuttered chandlery, a lantern burned steady and unhurried. Not a patrol's lantern — patrols moved. This was a lantern that waited. A lantern that wanted to be seen.

And beneath it, unmistakable even at this distance, even in profile, stood Cassian Mereau, hands clasped behind his back, gazing up at the dark windows of Lockridge & Son with the patient, pleasant expression of a man who already knew exactly what he would find inside, and was simply deciding how best to ask for it.

He raised one hand — a small, courteous gesture, almost a wave — and turned away, and the lantern followed him into the dark like a well-behaved thought.

Behind her, from the blackness of the cellar, Voss's voice floated up, very quiet:

"Coral. How long has he known?"
