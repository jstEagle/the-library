## 12. Mereau's Generosity

The invitation arrived on cream-laid paper, the kind that took ink like a confession takes a priest — willingly and without resistance. It was delivered by a boy too well-dressed to be a messenger and too young to be dangerous, who waited on the doorstep of Coral's shop while Emeric Voss read it twice and felt the words rearrange themselves between readings.

*Citizen Voss is requested at the offices of the Committee of the Dawn, tomorrow at the fourth hour. Cassian Mereau desires his counsel on matters of record.*

*Matters of record.* Voss turned the phrase over the way a moneylender turns a coin, testing for shaving. Nobody desired his counsel. Counsel was what powerful men said they wanted when they had already decided what you would say.

He could have refused. He understood that even then, standing in the doorway with rain coming down in the gray Vellamare manner, fine as sifted flour, dampening the cobbles until they shone like the flanks of seals. He could have folded the letter, fed it to the stove, and told Coral that nothing had come. But refusal itself would be an answer, and answers traveled in this city now faster than any press could carry them.

He went.

* * *

The Committee of the Dawn occupied the former customs house, a building raised in the days when Vellamare had still believed in its own importance, all pale stone and tall windows, and the revolution had changed nothing about it but the flags. The old imperial banners were gone; in their place hung tricolors sewn slightly wrong, the blue too deep, as though dyed in haste by someone working from memory rather than pattern. Perhaps that was the truth of the whole enterprise, Voss thought, climbing the marble stairs past guards who did not check his papers because no one had thought to tell them he might need checking. Everything here was worked from memory. Memory was cheaper than truth and held its color better under lamplight.

A clerk led him down a corridor smelling of fresh paint over old smoke, into a room where maps of the harbor district had been pinned to the walls and corrected in red ink. And there, at a table strewn with proofs and pamphlets, sat Cassian Mereau, reading.

That was the first thing Voss noticed, and it unsettled him more than any display of power could have. Mereau was actually reading — a broadside pulled from one of the city's surviving presses, holding it at arm's length, lips moving faintly, frowning at some infelicity of phrasing. When Voss entered, he did not leap up or summon ceremony. He set the broadside down, marked his place with one long finger, and smiled the smile that had been reproduced in a hundred engravings since the uprising: warm, crooked, the smile of a man who has heard every joke the world tells and forgiven most of them.

"Voss. Sit, sit. Do you drink coffee, or are you one of those ascetics who believes clarity should come unassisted?"

"I drink whatever is offered."

"An honest answer. Rare in this building." Mereau poured from a pot that sat on a trivet, unhurried, and the small domesticity of it — the clink of cup against saucer, steam rising between them — was itself a kind of argument. *I am not what you fear. I am a man with a coffee pot and a proofing table and too much work.* Voss accepted the cup and did not drink.

"You've read my letters," Mereau said. "Or so my people tell me. The Ren correspondence."

"Some of it."

"Some of it." Mereau leaned back, lacing his fingers over his waistcoat. He was smaller in person than his portraits, Voss thought, narrower through the shoulders, with hands that looked more suited to a counting house than a barricade. "You know, that's how everyone answers me now. *Some of it. Part of it. What I've seen.* This city is full of men guarding fractions."

"And what fraction do you want from me?"

Mereau laughed — genuinely, Voss judged, or with a skill indistinguishable from genuine, which after enough years amounted to the same thing. "Straight to trade. Good. I'll be straight back." He drew a folder across the table, untied its cord with deliberate care. "We are going to write the history, Voss. Not the chronicle of the war — that's done, that's dates and dead men — but the history. The meaning of it. Why Aldous Ren mattered. What his death purchased. Every revolution is a story told quickly before anyone can check the sources, and I intend for ours to be told *well*. Told properly. With a man at the center of it who understands type, understands archives, understands—" he gestured at the room, the maps, the red corrections, "—what a record costs and what it's worth."

"And you want me to hold the pen."

"I want you to hold the pen, the archive, the seal, and the salary that goes with them." Mereau slid a paper from the folder. Terms, written fair. A title: *Chronicler of the Dawn*. Offices in this building. Access to every seized document of the old regime — and Voss felt his breath catch despite himself, because access to those papers meant access to the trial records, the prison logs, the names of informers, everything the amnesty was being built to bury. "Read it later if you like. The figure is at the bottom. It's generous because the work matters, and the work matters because—"

He paused, and Voss watched the charm reassemble itself around something harder underneath, the way water refreezes over a stone.

"—because the people need one story, not forty. You've seen what happens otherwise. Rumor multiplies. Some drunk in a fish market says Ren never died, and by evening it's in four quarters and by morning it's a riot. A printer with a grudge sets some half-true thing in type and suddenly widows are weeping over a forgery. The truth, Voss, is like type metal. Useful only when it's cast. Loose in the crucible, it just burns."

*Loose in the crucible.* Voss kept his face still and thought: *he knows. Or suspects. Or suspects that suspicion is enough.*

"The letters," Voss said carefully. "The ones in Ren's hand, dated after the execution. My people tell me you asked whether others had surfaced."

Something flickered behind Mereau's eyes — appraisal, quick as a fish turning. "Did they say that? My people talk too much." He rose, walked to the window, hands clasped behind him. Below, the harbor lay gray and glittering, masts nodding. "Here is what I know about those letters, Voss, and then we will speak of pleasanter things. I know that Aldous Ren is dead. I carried his coffin's worth of grief for two years before it became useful to anyone, and I will not have his name dragged through the mud by forgers and sentimentalists and men who profit from confusion. Whoever made those letters made a weapon. Weapons get found. And when weapons get found," he turned back, smiling gently, "the places that sheltered them tend to burn. Accidentally, usually. Lamp oil is treacherous stuff near a print shop. All that paper. All that dry, beautiful paper."

The coffee had gone cold in Voss's cup without his ever lifting it. He understood now the full architecture of the offer: the salary, the archive, the honor — and beneath it, laid flush against the wood like a second floor, the threat. Accept, and the Lockridge shop stood safe inside the official story, its debts negotiable, its presses protected. Refuse, and it stood outside, among the flammable things.

"You're offering me the chance to write lies with a government seal on them," Voss said.

"I'm offering you the chance to write the *one version* that survives," Mereau said. "Which is what everyone else is doing, Voss — writing their version, hoping it survives. I'm merely honest about the mechanism. Think of it. The trial records alone. You'd learn who informed on Ren, if anyone did. You'd settle it forever, with documents. Isn't that what you want — the truth?" And the cruelty of it, the real craftsmanship, was that Mereau clearly believed this, or had believed it once, or could produce the belief on demand like a coin from behind a child's ear. *The truth,* offered by the man who needed a particular truth. Temptation dressed so finely in honor that honor barely showed beneath.

"I'll need time to consider the terms," Voss said.

"Of course." Mereau returned to the table, recorked the cord around the folder, and pressed it into Voss's hands — the contract, the terms, the whole gilded apparatus. "Take three days. Take two, if you're the decisive sort. Only—" he walked Voss to the door himself, hand light between his shoulder blades, voice dropping into the register of a friend confiding a worry, "—do consider what happens to shops in this quarter that stand empty of purpose. So much rebuilding planned. So many sites needing clearance. One hates to see a good press go to the wreckers for lack of work to feed it." At the threshold he squeezed Voss's arm. "Miss Lockridge cuts lovely matrices, I'm told. Her father's gift, running true in the daughter. It would be a shame to waste it on wedding invitations."

* * *

Rain again on the walk back, heavier now, drumming on awnings and running brown in the gutters. Voss did not go straight to the shop. He walked instead along the harbor wall, letting the wind off the water push at him, and tried to think with the cold clarity the weather imposed.

Three days. Two, if decisive. He turned the contract over in his mind and found the trap in its very generosity: the post came with access, and access meant he could reach the trial records, the prison registers — perhaps find the answer to the question that had eaten him for two years. Whether Ren had escaped. Whether the letters were his. Whether the man Voss had served, feared, admired, and mourned had lived long enough to write anything down. To take the post was to be handed the keys to the very vault the amnesty meant to torch — and to become, in the same motion, the man who locked it.

And if he refused, Mereau would simply find another chronicler, and the Lockridge shop would burn with the same bureaucratic inevitability as the archive already burning elsewhere in this city, one fire at a time, each with its tidy explanation.

There was a third road, of course. There always was, in Voss's experience, and it always ran through worse country than the other two. Accept — accept, and walk into the vault, and read everything, and copy what mattered, and set it in type in a cellar by night while drawing a traitor's salary by day. Complicity as camouflage. Corruption as a door left deliberately ajar. He tasted the idea and found it did not taste like betrayal, which frightened him most of all. It tasted like prudence. Like strategy. That was how it started, he understood — not with a fall but with a step that felt like footing.

By the time he reached the shop the lamps were lit, and through the window he could see Coral at her bench, bent over the case, her hands moving in the small precise economy he had come to watch the way sailors watch weather. Tibo was sweeping. The press stood patient in the corner, iron and oak, older than the revolution and likely to outlast it.

She looked up when he came in, and read him instantly — he saw her do it, saw her catalog his wet shoulders and closed expression and arrive, within a breath, at the right question without asking it.

"He made you an offer."

"He made us both one," Voss said. "He just didn't tell you your half." He put the folder on her bench, beside the scattered type, and watched her untie the cord. "Two days, Coral. And either way we choose, I think the clock he's really winding isn't the one on the wall."

Coral read the terms in silence. Then she read them again, the way he had, and he watched the same rearrangement happen in her eyes — words shifting meaning between readings, the price surfacing slowly beneath the salary like a body rising in dark water.

When she finally spoke, her voice was very quiet and very steady, and it was not the question he expected.

"What exactly does he mean," she said, "*my father's gift?*"
