## 13. The Informant in the Loft

Coral knew something was wrong before she reached the bottom of the stairs, in the way one knows a storm has shifted out at sea — not by sight but by the changed pressure of the air, the sudden hush where ordinary noise should be. The press was silent. The morning light came down through the loft's dusty skylight in a single slanted column, and inside that column stood Tibo, bent over the composing stone with a sheet of paper in his hands and a pencil stub moving across it.

He was copying the testament.

The realization arrived whole and terrible, the way grief had arrived two years ago at this same hour of the morning. She stood on the third stair from the bottom, her hand still on the rail, and watched the boy's fingers fly — quick, careful, reverent almost — transcribing line after line of Aldous Ren's last words onto a scrap of cheap paper he must have taken from the offcut bin. The original lay beside him on the stone, weighted at its corners with pieces of stray type so it would not curl. They had worked through half the night; Coral's eyes still burned from the lamp oil and the fine work of setting letters smaller than grains of barley. She had left Voss asleep in the back room and the testament locked away, or believed she had. The lock, she saw now, hung loose on the hasp. A boy who had been raised in this shop could open that lock with a fingernail.

"Tibo."

The pencil stopped. He did not turn around at once. For three heartbeats he stood absolutely still, the way a mouse goes still when the cat's shadow crosses the floor — and she understood, watching the back of his neck redden above his collar, that he had heard her come down the stairs and had chosen to finish one more line rather than flee. That choice, more than anything else, told her this was no idle theft. He had known the risk and kept writing anyway.

Then he turned, and his face was awful to see. Not guilty. Terrified, and beneath the terror, something that looked terribly like apology.

"Miss Lockridge—" His voice cracked. He was fifteen, and he had grown two inches since spring, so that his wrists stuck out of his sleeves like an apology of their own, and he held the copied page slightly behind his back as if hiding it could undo the fact of it. "I wasn't— I can explain—"

"You were copying a document we agreed would never leave this cellar." She came down the last stairs slowly. Her legs felt strange, detached, as though they belonged to someone else walking into this. "A document that four people in all of Vellamare know exists. And you are one of them."

"I know." His eyes were wet. "I know what it looks like."

"Give it to me."

He hesitated — actually hesitated, and that hesitation rang in her like a cracked bell — before he brought the page forward and laid it on the stone beside the original. His copy was good. That was the first thing she noticed, the craftsman's reflex cataloguing before the woman could feel: his transcription was neat, accurate, the letters formed with the careful discipline she had drilled into him for three years. If it had gone out of this shop under another name, another hand, it would have convinced anyone.

That was the second thing she noticed, arriving cold behind the first: it was good enough to convince Mereau's men exactly what they were looking at.

* * *

She made him sit on the type case while she read his copy twice through, holding it near the window. Then she set it down and looked at him for a long time, and the looking was worse for both of them than shouting would have been.

"Who gave you the paper?" she asked finally.

"No one."

"Tibo."

"No one, miss, I swear it. I took it from the bin." He was twisting his cap now, round and round, the brim going soft in his fists. "It's just — you and Mr. Voss come down here every night now, and you lock the door, and you don't tell me anything except keep watch, keep watch, watch the street, watch the alley. And I keep watch. I've kept better watch than either of you knows. But nobody tells me *what for*." His voice rose and broke again. "My father worked here twelve years before he drowned, and my father before me knew your father, and I sleep in your loft like a dog in a barn, and I thought — I thought if I knew what it was, really knew, then keeping watch wouldn't be like standing guard over nothing. I thought I'd earned—"

He stopped himself, jaw working. Coral said nothing. Outside, a fish cart rattled past on the cobbles, and somewhere down the lane a woman was calling gulls off her washing lines in the sharp scolding voice women used here, and the ordinary sounds of the morning seemed obscene against the small catastrophe in the room.

"Finish it," she said quietly. "What you thought you'd earned."

He swallowed. "I thought I'd earned the truth of what I'm risking my neck for."

"And instead?"

"Instead I'm risking it anyway." He lifted his chin, and for a moment she saw not the boy but the man he would become, stubborn as type metal, unbending once cast. "Only now I'm risking it blind. That's worse, miss. Being trusted without being told anything isn't trust at all. It's just being used."

The words went into her like a gimlet into soft pine. Because he was right, and because she had known he was right for weeks, and because telling herself that secrecy was protection had been easier than admitting that protection was only ever extended downward by people who held power, and she held it over this boy the way her father had held it over her, gently, and completely, and without ever once asking what it cost.

But there was a third question, and she made herself ask it.

"Has anyone spoken to you? About this shop? About me?"

And there it was — the flinch, small as a shutter closing, but she caught it, because she had spent her life reading faces across a composing stick, customers trying to hide how much they could pay, officials pretending their interest in a printer's licenses was routine.

"Sit down," she said, though he was already sitting. "Tibo. Look at me. Has someone been asking you questions?"

* * *

It came out of him in pieces, between long silences, the way splinters come out of flesh.

Three weeks ago. A man calling himself Perrin, from the Office of Public Records, wanting to verify the shop's holdings for the amnesty inventory — a plausible story, dressed in plausible clothes, a gray coat and a gray manner and a clerk's leather satchel. He had bought her a coffee at the corner of the Rue des Filets and asked easy questions that were not easy at all. Did the Lockridge shop hold any of old Lockridge's correspondence? Had she inventoried her father's proof books? Had any strangers — foreign buyers, collectors — come asking after revolutionary papers?

"He knew about the proof books," Tibo said, and his hands had gone still in his lap. "Not that they existed. That they were *my father's trade*, the ones he kept for Mr. Ren before everything. He said it casual, like he already knew the answer and was only checking whether I did."

Coral's mouth had gone dry. Her father's proof books. The archived samples of Ren's hand that she herself had matched against Voss's letters in this very cellar, by lamplight, weeks ago — the comparison that had started everything. If Perrin knew those books mattered, then someone else had done the same arithmetic she had. Someone else had reasoned backward from the martyr's handwriting to the shop that had cut its matrices.

"What did you tell him?"

"Nothing. I told him I swept floors and learned my letters and didn't know what proof books were." Tibo's chin trembled once and steadied. "But that's not the worst of it, miss. He came back. Twice more. Different coat each time, different name, but the same man — I'd know his walk anywhere, he walks on the outside of his heels like a man who's never carried anything heavy. And the last time—" The boy looked down at his boots. "The last time he didn't ask me anything. He just put coins on the table between us. Five of them. Silver. And he said a man in my position could earn five more every week, regular, for information about comings and goings at the shop. Who visited. What was delivered. Whether there was unusual work done at night."

He pulled something from his pocket and set it on the type case beside the copied page. Five silver coins, stacked, still wrapped in their paper sleeve from the mint.

"You didn't take them."

"I took the first lot." His voice was barely audible now. "Two weeks ago. Three coins, he gave me three, and I took them, and I hated myself before I got them up to the loft, and I never spent one, they're under my mattress, I was going to—" He fumbled, dug in his shirt, produced two more worn coins, older, and added them to the stack with shaking fingers. "I was going to give them back somehow. Or throw them in the harbor. I don't know. I kept thinking I'd figure out how to make it right."

"Why didn't you come to me?"

"Because I was ashamed." It burst out of him. "Because I took a traitor's money in the house of the family that fed me, and because — miss, because if I told you, you'd send me away, and I haven't anywhere else to *be*—"

And then he was crying, the ragged silent crying of a boy who has learned that noise brings trouble, hunched over his knees on top of the type case with his sleeves pressed to his face, and Coral stood in the middle of her father's shop and did not know whether she was holding a knife or a rope.

* * *

She made tea, because her hands needed occupation and because it was either tea or shouting, and she had promised herself she would not shout until she had thought it through to the end. The kettle took its time. Tibo's breathing steadied by degrees. When she finally sat across from him at the workbench, two chipped cups between them, the light through the skylight had moved and now lay across the locked cabinet where the testament slept.

"Tell me exactly," she said, "what you told him. All three times. Every word you can remember."

So he told her, and she listened the way she proofed a page — hunting the errant letter, the inverted character, the flaw that would ruin the whole sheet. And what she found, as the account unrolled, was that Tibo had lied to Perrin not clumsily but *well*. He had given the man true things that led nowhere: yes, business was poor; yes, Mrs. Quennel's wedding invitations had been printed in March; yes, ships' manifests and harbor notices came through weekly, nothing unusual. He had invented a fictitious cousin in Serrano who supposedly handled the shop's rare commissions. He had even, on the third visit, volunteered a complaint about wages — "he liked that," Tibo said grimly, "liked hearing a boy grumble, it made him think I was his" — and used the complaint to explain why he never noticed anything worth reporting.

"You didn't mention Mr. Voss."

"No, miss."

"You didn't mention night work."

"No, miss." Tibo raised his head. "I want to say something and I want you to hear all of it before you decide what I am." He took a breath. "When I took those coins, I told myself I was being clever. That I'd take his silver and tell him smoke, and he'd never know, and the shop would be safer with me inside his confidence than outside it. Maybe that was a lie I told myself. Maybe I just wanted to feel like I mattered. But I never gave him one true thing, Miss Lockridge. Not one. You can check every word I said against what's actually happened in this shop and find I lied straight down the line." His jaw set. "I'm not asking you to believe I'm a hero. I'm asking you to believe I'm not his."

Coral turned her cup slowly on the bench. Through the wall came the muffled clank of the harbor bell, counting the quarter-hour, and she thought about her father — about the ledger she had found hidden behind the false back of his desk, the payments recorded in his cramped hand, payments from Ren himself, and the years of silence he had purchased with his compliance. Her father had chosen safety and called it prudence, and the choice had eaten him from the inside, quietly, year by year, until the man who died at that bench weighed less than the man who had lived there. She had sworn, standing over that ledger with the lamp guttering, that she would choose differently. That when the moment came she would know it, and she would not flinch.

This was not that moment. This was smaller and harder — not a grand choice between truth and lies but a domestic one, ugly and human: what to do with a frightened boy who had taken a spy's silver and spied on nothing.

"The men watching this shop," she said at last. "How many have you seen besides Perrin?"

Tibo wiped his face with his sleeve, leaving a streak of ink across his cheekbone that he would wear all day without noticing. "Two. There's a man who buys bread from Madame Ollivier's stall across the lane most mornings — he never buys anything else, and he stands where he can see our door. And sometimes at night there's a lantern in the sailmaker's loft opposite. Nobody works there anymore; the sailmaker left when the fighting started. But the light comes, around eleven, and moves along the windows like someone walking a patrol."

Eleven o'clock. She and Voss had begun setting the testament at midnight, always after midnight, believing the dark itself was cover. All those nights, page after page of Aldous Ren's final words sliding into the chase, and above the street opposite a lantern pacing, patient as tide.

They were not discovered yet. But they were surrounded, and the circle was drawn in silver coins and gray coats, and it had been tightening for three weeks while she congratulated herself on her caution.

"Tibo." She waited until he met her eyes. "Here is what is going to happen, and you will listen to all of it before you speak. You are not leaving this shop. I'm not sending you away, and I'm not turning you over to anyone, and if you ever take that man's coin again I will melt it down and make you wear it as a medallion, do you understand?" Something flickered at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, but its distant cousin. "You are going to keep meeting Perrin. You are going to seem hungry, and grudging, and slightly stupid, and you are going to keep taking his silver, and you are going to bring every coin and every word he says directly to me. Do you know why?"

He shook his head slowly.

"Because a watcher you know about is a watcher you can feed. And because—" she paused, choosing the words the way she chose type, testing each for weight — "because you made a mistake, and you owned it to my face when you could have buried it, and that is worth more than a hundred clean records. My father taught me that lead doesn't lie once it's cast. Neither do boys who cry." She slid the stack of coins back across the bench toward him. "Put these with the others. Every piece of that man's silver belongs to us now — we'll decide together what it buys."

Tibo looked at the coins, then at her, and something went out of his shoulders that had been living there for weeks — she could see it leave, some knot of dread unknotting — and he gathered the silver up with hands that had almost stopped trembling.

"There's one more thing," he said, hesitant on the edge of the confession's aftermath. "Perrin. On the last visit. After the coins, when he was leaving, he said something I didn't understand until right now, sitting here. He said — 'Tell your mistress the amnesty comes soon, and wise printers clear their cellars before the tide.'" He frowned. "I thought it was just talk. Threatening, but general. Everyone talks about the amnesty now."

But Coral had gone cold to the fingertips. *Clear their cellars.* Not the loft, not the press, not the stock of paper — the *cellar*. The type cellar, where she had hidden her father's proof books beneath the floor, and where the testament now lay locked in its cabinet not ten feet from where Perrin's coins sat stacked in a boy's pocket.

They know about cellars, she thought. They know what printers keep in them.

She rose, crossing to the window, and looked out at the sunny lane — at the bread stall, at the shuttered sailmaker's loft with its patient dark windows — and understood that the clock she and Voss had been racing had never been the amnesty's public calendar at all. Somewhere across the city, Cassian Mereau was smiling his watchful smile and consulting a schedule of his own, and the space between *soon* and *now* had just become the narrowest kind of margin.

"Tibo," she said, without turning from the window, "run and wake Mr. Voss. Tell him we print tonight — and tell him we have perhaps two nights, not twenty."
