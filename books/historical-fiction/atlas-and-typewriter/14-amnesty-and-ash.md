## 14. Amnesty and Ash

The proclamation went up on the customs house door at dawn, and by the time Emeric Voss reached the square it had already gathered its first crowd — dockhands with their caps in their fists, fishwives still smelling of brine, a clerk from the harbor office reading aloud in the flat, official cadence that made even good news sound like a sentence being passed.

"By order of the Provisional Government of the Free Harbor," the clerk intoned, "a general and complete amnesty is declared for all offenses committed in the course of the late troubles. No man shall be pursued for what he did. Henceforth, only what he *says* concerns us."

Voss stood at the edge of the crowd with his collar turned up against the harbor wind and felt the words settle over him like ash from a distant fire.

He had heard amnesties before. In the border towns during the first year of the uprising, when the old regime had tried to buy back its deserters with pardons stamped in red wax. The word always sounded generous until you understood what was being forgiven — and what was being taken in exchange.

The clerk continued. "In conjunction with this act of mercy, the government has commissioned an Official History of the Liberation, to be compiled from authenticated records and published for the instruction of posterity. To ensure that this history reflects only truth and not sedition, all private papers, letters, pamphlets, and documents pertaining to the late troubles are hereby declared subject to confiscation. Citizens are encouraged to surrender such materials to their district offices within five days."

Five days.

A murmur ran through the crowd — relief in some throats, unease in others. An old woman beside Voss crossed herself and muttered something about her son's letters from the siege lines. A young man in a borrowed officer's coat nodded along as though the arrangement were perfectly reasonable, as though governments had always been trusted to write the story of how they came to power.

*Only what he says concerns us.* Voss turned the phrase over and found its teeth. They were not forgiving the past. They were purchasing it — every letter, every ledger, every scrap of paper that might contradict the version now being composed in some comfortable office above the customs house — and the price was mercy, which no honest man could refuse without marking himself.

And there, folded into the generosity like a blade wrapped in cloth: five days, and then the fires.

He walked back toward the printing quarter faster than his bruised ribs appreciated, and the streets themselves seemed to have heard the news. At the corner of Rue des Fondeurs, two men from the district committee were nailing a copy of the proclamation to a shutter, and a third was already making a list — a small, neat list in a small, neat hand — of the houses on the street. He wrote down numbers. He wrote down names where he knew them. He did not look up when Voss passed, but Voss felt the man's attention anyway, the way one feels a draft from a door left open somewhere in a dark house.

They were already counting the paper.

* * *

The shop smelled of coffee and lamp oil when he let himself in — Coral had taken to leaving the side door unbarred in the early morning because locking it had begun to feel, she said, like admitting there was something worth stealing — and found her at the type cellar stairs with a candle in her hand, coming up.

"You've heard." It wasn't a question. Her face had the particular stillness he had learned to read these past weeks: the stillness of a woman doing arithmetic she did not want to do.

"The whole city's heard," Voss said. "Five days. Then they burn everything that isn't theirs."

"They're calling it an amnesty." She set the candle on the bench beside the press, and the flame threw her shadow enormous against the cellar wall. "My father lived through two of them. He used to say an amnesty is what a government calls it when it can't find all the bodies." She looked at him, and he saw beneath the steadiness the thing she would not say aloud: that among the papers subject to confiscation, hidden under the floor of this very cellar, lay Ren's testament — the dead man's own hand naming Cassian Mereau as the one who sold him to the old regime, naming the dates, naming the sums, naming the lie upon which the entire revolution had been built into a legend.

"How long to print it?" Voss asked. "All of it. Properly. Enough copies to matter."

Coral didn't answer immediately. She went to the case of discarded matrices — the worn sorts, the cracked molds, the characters her father had retired from service years ago and never thrown away, because printers do not throw away metal — and ran her thumb along them the way a woman might count coins.

"With two of us working? Setting the testament is four days' labor if nothing goes wrong. Something will go wrong. It always does." She closed her hand around a matrix as though she could squeeze the answer out of it. "That leaves no margin. Not for broken type, not for a press that jams, not for soldiers at the door."

"And the ship?"

"The *Meridian* takes the tide day after tomorrow, dawn after next. Captain Orla Henn takes paper bales to Corvane regularly — my father shipped her blank stock for six years. If we could get finished sheets into a bale..." She shook her head slowly. "But we'd need the printing done before she sails. Which means we start tonight, or we don't start at all."

Voss lowered himself onto the bench, and his ribs reminded him of the soldier's boot from three nights ago, the sweep through the fish market, the man with Ren's gait who had vanished into the crowd and out of hope. He was tired in a way that sleep no longer touched.

"There's something else," he said. "Something I heard before the proclamation. Mereau's office has been asking after me. By name."

Coral's head came up sharply. "Asking what?"

"Asking whether I'd be interested in serving the Official History. As a compiler. Someone who knew Ren, who traveled for the cause, whose testimony would give their version weight." He heard how it sounded even as he said it — the shape of the trap, dressed in honor. "If I say yes, I'm inside their offices. I could learn exactly when the burning begins, what they've already collected."

"If you say yes, you're theirs."

"Yes."

She studied him across the lamplit cellar, and he watched her weigh it — not whether he could be bought; she had stopped doubting him somewhere in the long nights of setting type, and he knew it — but what it would cost him to stand in that office and smile.

"Then don't say yes yet," she said finally. "Say *maybe*. Buy yourself the invitation. A man considering an offer isn't watched the way a man who refused one is watched." She turned back to the matrices, began sorting them into a workbox with quick, economical movements. "We'll need every unhurried hour we can steal."

* * *

He spent the afternoon in the streets, because a man seen idling near a print shop too often becomes a man responsible for what the print shop prints.

The city had changed texture overnight. That was the thing about proclamations — they altered the light. In the fish market, sellers called their prices in ordinary voices, but conversations stopped when strangers passed, and started again in lower registers. Outside the district offices, a queue had formed: citizens surrendering papers, as encouraged. Some carried single letters, held out at arm's length as though the paper might bite. One man brought an entire trunk, and the clerks received it with expressions of warm approval and entered his name in a register, and Voss understood that the register was the true document — a census of the compliant, against which all who failed to appear would later be measured.

Sedition, the proclamation said. All private papers deemed seditious.

And who did the deeming? Why, the same committee that had commissioned the Official History. The same hands that would hold the matches. There was a circularity to it so clean it was almost beautiful, the way a well-cut gear train is beautiful: the government defines truth, collects everything else, destroys the remainder, and posterity inherits a history with no rivals. Aldous Ren had written once — in the pamphlet that had first sent Voss running messages through checkpoint shadows — that tyrants fear the press less than they fear the archive, because a press must be answered but an archive can simply be burned. Voss had admired the line without understanding it. He understood it now. Ren had been writing his own epitaph two years before anyone knew to carve it.

At the harbor, he found the *Meridian* riding at her moorings, small and salt-stained, taking on cargo with the unhurried industry of a ship that had survived every regime by belonging to none of them. He loitered along the quay with a pipe he did not smoke and watched Captain Henn supervise the loading — a broad, weathered woman with a voice that could be heard over gulls and artillery both.

He did not approach her. Not yet. Approaching a captain with a proposition was how propositions reached the ears of committees. But he noted the bale hooks, the canvas wrapping, the loose and trusting way her crew stacked the paper shipments from Lockridge & Son — the stencil on the wrappers still bearing the old shop name, faded now, the ink going gray.

Day after tomorrow, dawn. Forty hours, perhaps fewer, once the type was set.

He was turning back toward the printing quarter when he saw the boy — not Tibo, one of the customs runners — hurrying toward him with the particular urgency of someone carrying words too heavy to walk with.

"Monsieur Voss." The boy caught his sleeve, breathless. "There's a notice at the harbor office. About the amnesty lists. They posted the schedule of collections." His eyes flicked left and right, the practiced caution of the waterfront. "Rue des Fondeurs is second. Second street. Tomorrow."

"Tomorrow?" Voss kept his voice level, though something cold slid down his spine. "The proclamation said five days."

"For surrendering, yes. For *collection*, they're not waiting. They go house to house starting tomorrow morning, street by street, and whatever isn't surrendered by the time they reach your door is deemed withheld." The boy swallowed. "Withheld papers get their owners withheld too. That's the word they used. *Withheld.*"

Second street. They would reach the Lockridge shop tomorrow, likely before noon.

Voss thanked him, pressed a coin into his hand, and walked — did not run, though every nerve in him screamed to run — back toward the shop, recalculating as he went. Four days to set the type, Coral had said, if nothing went wrong. He now had one night and half a morning before the knock would come at the door, the polite knock, the knock accompanied by a clerk with a register and soldiers with a wagon, and the clerk would ask to see the premises, and beneath the floor of the type cellar lay the testament of Aldous Ren and the political death of everyone who had touched it.

Unless the type was already set. Unless the pages were already printed and folded and sewn into the heart of a paper bale bound for Corvane. Unless forty hours became twenty, and the labor of four days was compressed into the space between nightfall and dawn.

It could not be done. That was the arithmetic. And yet.

* * *

Coral took the news without flinching, which frightened him more than panic would have. She simply put down the composing stick she was cleaning, stood very still for the space of three breaths, and then began pulling every case of usable type in the shop onto the central bench, arranging them in working order with the speed of long habit.

"Tibo," she called up the stairs, and the apprentice came down rubbing sleep or lampblack from his eyes. "Run to Madame Corvez and buy her silence for a night — tell her I need her back room for sorting rags, tell her anything, pay her from the tin behind the flour jar. Then come straight back and bar the door behind you. We're printing tonight."

The boy looked from her to Voss and back, and Voss watched something resolve in the young face — the same resolution he had seen once in a mirror, years ago, the night he had first carried Ren's words instead of merely believing them. The boy went without a word.

"He'll keep watch," Coral said, almost to herself. "And we'll melt down the type after. Every sort, into the crucible. Let them search; they'll find lead ingots and a guilty conscience's worth of nothing." She glanced at the press, at the cellar floor beneath which the testament lay wrapped in oilcloth, and her voice dropped to something harder than he had yet heard from her. "They gave the whole city five days to decide what it's willing to lose. We get one night. So we don't decide. We already decided."

Voss unwrapped the oilcloth and laid the testament's pages on the bench — Ren's cramped, furious, dying hand, the hand Coral had given metal form years before she knew what it would one day be asked to say. Above them, muffled by floors, the bells of Vellamare rang the evening hour, and beyond the harbor the tide tables were already turning toward the dawn after next.

He picked up a composing stick. Coral set the first line. And outside, somewhere in the darkening streets, the wagons of the general amnesty were already rolling toward the second street on their list.

The clock had started. It was ticking in the click of the type, in the creak of the press screws, in Coral's voice counting pages as the cellar filled with the smell of ink — and every tick spent was a tick they would not get back before the knock came at the door.
