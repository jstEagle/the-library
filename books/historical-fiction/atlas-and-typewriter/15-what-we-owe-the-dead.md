## 15. What We Owe the Dead

The argument began, as the worst arguments do, over something small.

Coral had asked him to move the last of her father's proof books from the type cellar to the loft — a simple request, five words, delivered while she wiped ink from the press rollers with a rag gone gray from years of the same labor. Voss had paused with his hands full of galleys, and she had seen it in his face before he said anything at all: that particular stillness he wore when he was deciding whether to tell her something or spare her. He had been wearing it more often since the amnesty proclamation went up on the customs house door, since the morning tide ship had become not one option among several but the only option left in the world.

"Leave them where they are," he said now, setting the galleys down. "If we're moving anything tonight, we're moving the testament."

"The books stay in the cellar until I move them myself."

"They're your father's proofs, Coral. Old job work. Invoices and funeral notices and half a decade of shipping manifests."

"They're his handwriting." Her voice came out flatter than she intended, and she heard what lived underneath it, the thing she had been refusing to look at for days. "That's not nothing. You of all people should know that's not nothing."

Voss set down the galleys properly this time, squaring them against the edge of the stone sink as if order in small things could hold off disorder in large ones. He had filled out a little since the packet boat, since the drowned-rat stranger who'd stood dripping on her threshold asking after a dead man's hand. There was color in him now, and appetite; he drank her coffee like a man who expected to be offered it again tomorrow. She had come to find that steadiness necessary. That was precisely what frightened her about the conversation that was coming. She could see it coming the way sailors read weather off the water — not the shape of it yet, but the weight.

"Coral." He said her name the way other people said *listen*. "We need to talk about the quay."

"No," she said. "We need to talk about the forty copies you want to print Thursday night, which is two nights sooner than we can do safely, because Mereau's men have been walking past this shop three times a day instead of twice, and the youngest one has started memorizing Tibo's schedule, which means they know when the boy goes home, which means they already suspect—"

"Coral."

"*What.*"

"The ship leaves at first light Friday. The printing takes six hours if nothing breaks. Which leaves us no margin for the checkpoints, the paper bales, the harbor — none of it. So we talk about the quay, and we talk about it now, because there is something we have to decide before Thursday, and I have watched you avoid deciding it for a week."

The rag had stopped moving in her hands. She made it move again.

"Say what you mean," she said.

"I mean that you and I are not both getting on that ship."

* * *

He laid it out the way he must have laid out routes once, back when he was Ren's courier — the same economy, the same refusal to decorate the thing. The bales would need to be carried through two checkpoints, loaded by someone the dockhands knew, and watched aboard by someone who could answer questions without a tremor. That person could not be Coral. The Lockridge name was on the shop's license, on the guild rolls, on the harbor master's ledger going back thirty years; a woman hauling unmarked paper bales onto a tide ship at dawn would be remembered, and being remembered was the same as being taken. Whereas Voss — Voss was nobody. Voss had arrived on a packet boat eleven weeks ago and could vanish the same way, could stand on the quay as a stranger seeing a stranger's cargo aboard, and if soldiers stopped him he was a traveling clerk, an agent for a Vellamare paper concern, a man whose papers were dull and correct and prepared by hands better practiced than his own.

"And you?" she said, when he finished. "After the bales go aboard. Where does the traveling clerk go?"

"That depends."

"On what?"

"On how curious the soldiers are that morning."

She put down the rag. She crossed the cellar behind the press room and stood at the bottom of the stairs with her back to him, because what her face was doing was not something she wanted witnessed.

"You've decided already," she said. "You sat across my table drinking my coffee for eleven weeks and you'd already decided."

"I decided it the night the archive house burned. Before that, honestly. I decided it the day I understood the amnesty was a bonfire with a decree attached."

"Then why—" She turned. The lamplight down here came up yellow and thin, and it caught him standing very straight by the sink, hands empty now, watching her with the patience she had mistaken for kindness in the early weeks and understood lately to be something harder. "Why did you let me think otherwise? We set type together every night, Emeric. We eat at the same table. You let me believe we were building something that had both of us in it."

"We are building something that has you in it," he said. "That's the whole point. The record survives, and the person who can keep making records survives, and the shop—"

"Don't." 

"—the shop is the *only press in Vellamare* that has ever set forbidden type and lived. Do you understand what that makes you? Not me. Me, they take, and they question, and eventually they tire of questioning, and that's the end of the story, and I've made my peace with—"

"*Don't* make peace at me. Don't stand in my father's cellar and make peace at me like it's a virtue."

It came out of her louder than anything she had said in this house since the mourning year, and it rang against the lead cases, against the stone, against the low ceiling where the lamp hung from its hook, and afterward there was a silence with both their breathing in it.

Voss moved first. He pulled out the workbench stool and sat down slowly, like a man whose legs had been holding more than his weight, and when he spoke again his voice had lost its courier's economy.

"My father kept a ferry," he said. "Before everything. Small crossing, river mouth, four boats. When the old regime conscripted the boatyards, he had a choice — hand over the boats or scuttle them. He handed them over. Kept the license, kept the crossing, ferried soldiers and tax men for six years and hated every hour of it, and everyone in the village knew he hated it, and everyone in the village used his crossing anyway, because a crossing that runs beats a principle that sinks." He looked up at her. "He died in his bed. Two of his brothers died at the barricades in the same month. I was twelve. I loved all three of them, and I have spent twenty years unable to tell you which of them I owe more to, and I am telling you this because you are doing what I did at twelve — you are confusing the thing you love with the thing that matters, and the two have split apart, and it is the worst feeling in the world, and no amount of shouting in cellars puts them back together."

"The shop isn't a principle," Coral said. "It's four walls and a press and my father's tools and the matrices of a dead man's hand buried under the floor. If they burn it, those things don't come back. Ink comes back. Paper comes back. But the matrices—"

"The testament doesn't need the matrices. Forty copies, abroad, in foreign gazettes within the month — the truth stops needing a press at all. It propagates. That's the entire reason we're doing any of this. But *you*, Coral — a press that works, a printer who knows how to set dangerous pages and not get caught, a shop with a cellar — that doesn't propagate. That dies once and stays dead."

"So the record matters more than the people keeping it. Is that it? The truth gets its ship and we get our graves, and somewhere in a foreign gazette some clerk writes *two brave printers of Vellamare, may they rest*—"

"I never said graves."

"You didn't have to. It's in how you said the rest." She came toward him, and stopped, and made herself lower her voice, because the walls here were old and the street outside older and sound traveled up the stairwell like smoke. "Listen to me. My father survived this shop for thirty years by never once choosing. Do you know what that costs? I found his ledger, Emeric. I know what he took and what he swallowed and what it hollowed out of him, and I watched him sit at that bench upstairs every night of my childhood with a whole life's worth of silence sitting on his shoulders like wet wool. Survival is not free. Somebody pays. And if I run — if I put the bales on the ship and walk away and let this place burn because *the record propagates* — then I pay for it the rest of my life, in exactly the coin my father paid in, and I will not do it. I am done inheriting debts I never agreed to."

For a long moment Voss said nothing. Then, quietly: "And if staying costs you the same coin anyway? If you defend the shop and lose it and lose yourself with it — who pays then?"

"That's my risk to spend."

"It stopped being only yours the night I walked in with those letters."

"Yes," she said. "It did. So let's settle it between us, honestly, right now, and not by you arranging my survival behind my back like a man booking passage for cargo." She pulled the second stool from under the bench and sat down across from him, knee to knee in the lamplight, close enough to see the exhaustion under his eyes that he managed so carefully everywhere else. "Here is mine, all of it. The testament sails Friday. I'll carry the bales, because I'm the only one the dockhands trust and you know it. And after the ship clears the point, I come back here, and I bar the door, and I print whatever Mereau's men put on my press for as long as they leave me a press to print on — because a shop that submits is a shop that survives, and a surviving shop is a place where the next testament can be set, someday, by somebody. That is not my father's bargain. His was silence. Mine is patience. But you — you cannot be here when they start asking who set the forbidden pages. You're right about that much. Your face is known at the coffee houses, your papers won't survive a real search, and if they take you they take everything you know about the route north, and there are others waiting on that route, aren't there? People beyond the sea who can reprint what we print. You didn't tell me, but you didn't have to."

Something moved through his face — surprise, then the rueful admission that he should have known better than to try to surprise a printer's daughter, who reads a page by what isn't on it.

"There are others," he admitted.

"Then that's the shape of it. I stay and endure. You go and carry. Neither of us gets the ending we wanted, and both of us get the one we can live inside." She reached out and took his hand — ink-stained, calloused at the second finger from the composing stick, warmer than she expected. "That is what I owe the dead, Emeric. Not martyrdom. Continuance."

Voss looked at their hands for a while. When he finally spoke, his voice was roughened.

"Mereau offered me the chronicler's post again. Third time. Through an intermediary."

The floor seemed to tilt slightly. "When?"

"This afternoon. While you were at the paper merchant's. He wants an answer by Friday."

"And you're telling me now?"

"I'm telling you because if I take it, I'm inside the committee rooms by winter, and I can document everything — the amnesty burnings, the purges, all of it, in the official record where no one dares look for truth anymore. Or I refuse, and I run north on Friday with your forty copies, and I spend the rest of my life as a fugitive carrying one true thing." He exhaled. "I don't know which man I am yet. That's the honest answer. That's the whole honest answer."

Coral held his hand tighter, and felt the alliance crack and shift beneath them like ice in a thaw — not breaking, not yet, but no longer the solid thing they had walked out on together.

"Then decide before Friday," she said quietly. "Because the tide won't wait, and neither will he."

Above them, faint through the floorboards, came the sound of the street door — the bell turning, slow and deliberate, though neither of them had heard anyone enter.

Tibo was home already.

Someone else wasn't.
