## 17. Night Press Run

The first hour belonged to preparation, and preparation was its own kind of prayer.

Voss pulled the shutters closed over the front windows while Coral lit the cellar lamps one by one, and by the time he came down the narrow stair the room below had been transformed into something between a workshop and a sanctuary. The type cellar — she had called it that all week, and he understood now that the name was literal, a cellar where type lived like a thing that breathed — glowed amber at the edges and lay in deep shadow at the center, where the press stood waiting under its dust sheet like an animal that had scented what was coming.

"Forty copies," Coral said, not for the first time. She said numbers the way other people said prayers. "Sixteen pages each. Six hundred and forty pages of setting, locking, and impression, not counting make-ready. The forme will need relocking every third pull if the furniture shifts, and it will shift, because this press has opinions."

"And you've done this before."

"I was born behind that press." She touched the frame the way a rider gentles a horse. "My father used to say ink doesn't care how brave you are. It only cares whether your hands are steady." She looked up at him across the lamplight, and her face was pale and set and utterly without self-pity. "So we don't rush. Rushing is how men die in cellars. We work steadily, all night if we must, and when the sun comes up we're finished or we're not."

Voss nodded. He had carried messages through checkpoints with the paper sewn into his coat lining; he had sat in a rowboat off Gray Skerry while the keeper's lantern swung; but he had never done anything that required this many consecutive hours of nerve. Printing, it turned out, was courage stretched thin over time instead of spent in a single moment. He did not know which was harder.

Tibo came down last, carrying the tray of bread and hard cheese Coral had ordered him to bring, and his face was so grave that Voss almost smiled. The boy had dressed for the night as though for a campaign — dark coat, hair plastered flat, boots that made no sound on the stairs. Someone had taught him to keep watch properly. Voss suspected it had not been Coral.

"The loft ladder," Tibo reported, as though delivering intelligence to a general. "I can see both ends of Rue des Ancres from the vent, and half the harbor basin beyond the ropewalk. There's a patrol due through the quarter around the change of watch — I heard the sergeant bawling about it at supper."

"You've been listening to soldiers at supper," Coral said.

"I've been *buying bread* near soldiers at supper," Tibo corrected, with dignity, and took up his post at the foot of the ladder where he could hear the loft above them.

Coral unlocked the case where Ren's testament lay — sixteen pages of a dead man's hand, copied out in Coral's careful transcription onto sheets of cheap paper, because no one but the three of them would ever see the original again once it went back beneath the floor. Voss watched her smooth the first page flat against the composing stone, and he thought: *this is how it begins. Not with trumpets. With a woman squaring a page under a lamp.*

"First forme," she said. "Pages one and sixteen. Hold the light closer."

* * *

Setting type, Voss discovered within the hour, was a form of reading so slow it became meditation, and so exact it became torture.

Coral had insisted he learn the basics before she would let him touch anything that mattered — "You'll be distributing the forms afterward, so you'll ruin them properly or not at all" — and now he sat at the second composing stick, picking letters from the case with a tweezers-like slowness while her hands moved beside him with the speed of long habit. Each letter upside down and backward, each word assembled in mirror, each line justified to the very point where it would hold. The metal clicked against the stick. Click, click, click. He found he could hear the words forming twice — once in his mind as sense, and once in the small bright music of lead finding its place.

"He writes short sentences," Voss said, somewhere past midnight. "Ren. I never noticed until I had to set them."

"He wrote for people who read by bad light," Coral said. "Short lines hold the eye longer than long ones. He knew his trade." Her fingers did not pause. "So do I, and so, tonight, do you."

The testament itself they had read aloud to each other twice already, and Voss knew passages by heart now — knew the moment on the fourth page where Aldous Ren, writing in a lighthouse keeper's cold room with a fever already climbing inside him, described the night he learned the Committee of the Dawn had sold his route to the old regime's secret police. Knew the sentence that named Cassian Mereau not with rage but with a terrible, tired precision: *He did not betray the cause. He calculated that my death was worth more to the cause than my life, and he was not wrong, and that is the indictment.*

That sentence had taken Voss forty minutes to set, because his hands shook every time he reached the name.

"You're pulling too hard," Coral said quietly, without looking up. "On the letters. You grip them like they'll escape."

"They might."

"They won't. Lead stays where you put it. That's the whole point of lead." She reached over, adjusted his hold on the stick with two fingers, brief and businesslike, and went back to her own work. "Steady. The man is dead. The truth is not fragile. Only we are."

* * *

At some black hour past two, Tibo's whisper came down the ladder like a stone dropped into still water.

"Lanterns. Two of them. Stopping at Perrot's door."

The cellar went silent except for the press, which Coral had been inking, its rhythm suddenly obscene in the hush. She lifted the roller clear and held it, frozen, and Voss blew out two of the three lamps, leaving a single flame hooded behind its tin shield.

They listened. Through the floorboards came the murmur of voices in the street, the scrape of a boot sole on cobbles, the particular silence of men looking at a dark building and deciding what to think about it. Voss counted his own heartbeats and got to ninety before the lanterns moved on, their glow sliding along the shutter seams and away.

"Perrot's shop," Coral breathed. "They check Perrot's first because Perrot printed lottery tickets and almanacs and never once anything that mattered. They'll get to us eventually. But they start with the harmless ones." Something crossed her face — grief with the safety catch off — and Voss understood that the neighboring print house, torched with its archive inside during the great reorganization, had been Perrot's brother's shop. Everything in this city was somebody's grief wearing a uniform.

"Keep going," she said, and unhooded the lamps, and rolled the ink.

The press woke.

It began the way a heartbeat begins after a fright — uneven, arrhythmic, then settling into its deep mechanical pulse. Coral worked the press and Voss fed the paper, laying each dampened sheet against the forme with both hands, feeling the platen come down through the whole frame of the machine and the floor beneath it. *Thump.* A page. *Thump.* Another. The sound filled the cellar and climbed the stairwell and Voss could only pray it stayed beneath the threshold of hearing upstairs, swallowed by the old timbers.

Between pulls they worked without speaking, and the silence between the thumps became a language of its own: her hand on the tympan meant *wait*, the flick of her wrist meant *lay it*, the nod meant *good impression*. By the third forme Voss could read her entire mind in the movement of her shoulders, and he suspected she could read his, and there was no time to think about what that meant except that it meant everything, and the press kept beating its slow heart.

*Thump. Thump. Thump.*

Forty copies. He thought of them accumulating on the drying line like moths pinned in rows — pages one and sixteen, then two and fifteen, the formes turned and relocked, the wet sheets hung in the warm dark above the type cases. Forty Aldous Rens coming slowly back to life in a cellar in Vellamare, sixteen pages at a time, while the man who had arranged his death slept four streets away behind a door with a tricolor sash nailed above it.

Somewhere around the sixth forme, Coral began to sing. Barely singing — a thread of sound under her breath, some old counting song printers used to keep rhythm, nonsense syllables marking the strokes. Voss joined in badly. She did not tell him to stop. In the lamplight her eyes were shining and shadowed at once, and he understood that the song was not for rhythm. It was to keep the fear moving through them instead of pooling.

* * *

The trouble came at half past four, in the shape of a knot in the dampened paper.

A sheet caught as Voss laid it; the platen descended on a fold; the crack of torn paper and buckled type cut through the cellar like a gunshot. Both of them stood rigid, listening to the loft. Above, nothing — Tibo knew the difference between disaster and mishap, apparently, and saved his warnings for real ones.

But the forme was ruined. Three lines of the ninth page sprung from the chase, type scattered across the stone, and the forme was the *inside* forme — the pages carrying Mereau's name — and the standing type was nearly exhausted.

"We redistribute," Coral said, already kneeling among the spilled letters. "We have the broken sorts from the discarded matrices. It will look wrong under a lens. It will read perfectly at arm's length."

"It will look forged," Voss said. "If they ever claim it's forged—"

"It *will* be slightly forged," she snapped, sorting lead with blurring speed. "Everything is slightly forged, Emeric. Every official history, every broadside Mereau commissions, every martyr painted braver and deader than he was. The question was never whether ink lies. It's whether anyone sets the counter-truth while there's still ink to set it." She held up a battered letter *e*, worn nearly round. "This e was cut by my father for a pamphlet in '39. It has printed ten thousand lies and one testament, and I am going to let it print the testament, and if some scholar twenty years from now squints at it and says *ah, suspicious*, then let him squint. Let him dig. Digging is how truth survives."

She was right, and he was too tired to argue, and together they rebuilt the ninth page out of salvaged and borrowed letterforms, a patchwork of decades, the whole history of the shop pressed into six lines. When the forme locked, Coral ran her thumb across the name *Mereau* where it stood in mismatched type, and said, softly, "There. Now you're permanent," and pulled the lever, and the press answered.

Dawn found them at thirty-eight copies.

* * *

Voss heard the problem before Tibo announced it — a change in the quality of the boy's silence at the top of the ladder, the absence where breathing should be.

"Monsieur Voss." Tibo's voice came down barely louder than the lamp flames. "Boots. Four pair, maybe five. Coming up the rue from the basin side. Not walking like patrols walk. Walking like men who know where they're going."

Coral's hands stopped on the thirty-ninth sheet. For one heartbeat the cellar was absolutely still, the press holding its breath with them.

"How long?" Voss asked.

"To the corner? Two minutes. To decide about our door? I don't know."

Voss did arithmetic with cold clarity: two copies remained unprinted; the drying pages hung thick and fragrant on their lines, impossible to hide, impossible to explain; the formes sat locked on the stone with a dead man's accusation standing in metal. Nothing here would fit in a pocket. Nothing here would pass as innocence.

"Coral."

"I know." She was already moving — not toward the stair but toward the floor, prying at the plank she'd loosened days ago, the hiding place that had kept the proof books safe. "The formes go under. The dried copies go in the paper bales, top layer, under the wrapping — bales are already staged for the morning cart, they've been *paper* for a week and they stay paper." Her voice was level, quick, a compositor's voice, each word set and justified. "Tibo! Away from the vent. Down here, and when they knock you open the door rubbing sleep out of your eyes, because apprentices sleep in lofts, that's the oldest fact in Vellamare."

"And you?" Voss said.

"And me." The plank came up. She passed him the locked formes, heavy as sin, and he slid them into the earth-smelling dark beneath the floor. "And me, with flour on my hands by the time they reach the kitchen. Emeric—" She stopped, halfway risen from the hole, lamplight catching the ink stains to her knuckles, and whatever the rest of the sentence was going to be, she changed it, or couldn't finish it, or decided it could wait for a world with more minutes in it. "The bales go aboard at seven. Whatever happens in the next hour — *whatever happens* — they go aboard."

The knocking began.

Not a soldier's battering — worse. Polite. Measured knuckles on the street door above, three times, then a pause that was almost courteous, and Voss knew with sick certainty whose courtesy it was before Tibo's footsteps crossed the loft overhead. Mereau's men did not break doors. They knocked the way collectors knock, certain of the debt.

Coral killed the lamps. The press stood in the sudden grey pre-dawn that seeped through the high vent, its iron heart still trembling faintly from its last beat, thirty-nine testaments hanging in the dark around it like sleeping birds. Voss put his hand flat against the frame — one second of contact, flesh to the machine that had beaten all night — and followed her toward the stair, toward the flour bin, toward the knock.

Below the floorboards, in the cold and the dark, the ninth page lay with *Mereau* standing in patched and imperfect type, permanent as anything men make.

Above, the door opened, and a pleasant voice asked whether Madame Lockridge had perhaps been burning oil past midnight, because the whole street remarked on the smell.

Voss, wiping ink from his hands with a rag that would never come clean, stepped up into the last hour before the tide.
