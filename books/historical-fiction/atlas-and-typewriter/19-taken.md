## 19. Taken

The bales went onto the quay at a quarter past four, and Emeric Voss counted them the way a man counts his own heartbeats — not because he doubted the number, but because the counting was the last thing left in his hands to do.

Six bales. Waste paper, said the manifests in Coral's neat vertical hand: rags and spoiled sheets bound for the pulping houses across the strait. Beneath the first layer of each, wrapped in oilcloth and sewn into the sacking with sailmaker's stitches, lay forty copies of Aldous Ren's testament — the true account of how the martyr of Vellamare had lived two months past his execution, and of the men who had sold him to the regime that killed him twice. Forty copies. Voss had set six of those pages himself, his fingers black to the second knuckle, while outside the shuttered windows soldiers' boots rang on the cobbles like a bell that would not finish tolling.

Now the harbor lay gray-pink under the first suggestion of dawn, and the *Meridian* rode at her moorings with steam up, and the soldiers were closer than they should have been.

"They're checking the north gate too," Tibo said. The boy had appeared at his elbow out of the fish-smoke, breathless, cap pulled low. "Two of Mereau's men. Not the regulars. His."

Voss did not look at him. Looking at anyone too long, he had learned in ten years as a courier, was a way of drawing lines between points on a map for anyone who cared to read them. He kept his eyes on the bales, on the stevedores moving them toward the gangway with the bored violence of men who handled everything as if it were already broken.

"Then we do nothing clever," Voss said. "Nothing clever at all. We stand here like men waiting for a shipment of rags."

"And when they cut one open?"

"Then we run, and you get to the ship before I do, because you're younger and I'm tired." He said it lightly, and knew from the boy's silence that it had landed wrong. Fear made jokes either too sharp or too flat; there had been a time, on night roads with Ren's pamphlets sewn into his coat lining, when Voss could carry both fear and humor in the same pocket without mixing them. That time was years gone.

The truth of it — the truth he had been carrying since Gray Skerry, since the cold room above the lighthouse where a dead legend's testament had lain waiting like a confession no one had asked for — was that he had always known it would end on a dock. Couriers end on docks. They end in doorways and ditches and the backs of closed carriages, and the letter goes on without them; that is the entire meaning of the trade. You are the road, not the destination. He had accepted this at nineteen with romantic ease and had spent the decade since learning what acceptance actually weighed.

It weighed, it turned out, about as much as forty printed pages.

* * *

They had argued about it, he and Coral, three nights ago among the type cases in the cellar, their voices kept low not because anyone could hear them but because low voices made the plan feel survivable.

"You're the one they've seen," she had said. "Mereau offered you a post. Your face is known at the harbor office. Let me take them down."

"And when they stop you — and they will stop you, a woman shipping paper days after the amnesty decree, they'll stop you on principle — what then? They search the shop. They find the press still warm. They find the matrices."

"I'll melt them first."

"With what furnace? With what time?" He had gripped the edge of the composing stone until his knuckles whitened, and hated himself for the gesture, which was theatrical, which was the sort of thing Ren used to do, and Ren had been dead two years and then dead one month and now dead properly, and histrionics had saved none of the three deaths. "Coral. Listen to me as if I were your father, since he can't say it. The record is the thing. Not the shop, not you, not me. We are the road. You taught me that yourself, or your father's proofs did, every page of them — ink outlives its makers. Someone has to be standing on the dock when the soldiers come, someone respectable enough to be questioned rather than shot, and that person cannot be the printer whose shop is the source."

"That's coward's arithmetic," she said, but her voice had cracked on it, and he had seen then that she understood him perfectly and hated the understanding.

"It's printer's arithmetic," he said. "Distribution matters more than composition. Your father knew it. Every martyr's publisher has known it." He had not told her the rest — that he had written a letter, folded into the first bale, addressed to the foreign papers under a name Ren had once used for him, explaining everything, naming everyone, so that even if the pages themselves were lost the story would have a second life swimming loose in the world. Some things you keep back even from allies. Especially from allies. Ally-ship, he had learned from the Committee of the Dawn at close range, is a currency, and currencies get debased.

She had relented the way strong people relent: completely, and with a look that made clear she was filing the debt away against a future she intended to collect on.

He thought of that look now, watching the soldiers move along the quay.

* * *

There were four of them at first, then eight. Mereau's men wore no insignia, which meant they could deny themselves, which meant they could do anything. They moved with the unhurried confidence of people who already knew the answer to whatever question they were about to ask, and Voss felt the old courier's instinct rise in him like water in a lock — the mapping of exits, the weighing of the crowd, the calculation of seconds. There was a fish-wife arguing prices twenty feet off. There was a customs officer asleep against a bollard. There was the gangway, and the *Meridian*'s third mate smoking at its head, glancing at the lightening sky with the particular resentment of a man whose sailing time was being eaten by other people's emergencies.

"Paper shipment," Voss said to Tibo, loudly, in the flat voice of commerce. "Rags and spoilage, six bales, manifest's with the purser. If these gentlemen want to inspect waste paper, that's their morning wasted, not ours."

One of the soldiers smiled. It was not a cruel smile; that was somehow worse. It was the smile of a man doing tedious work at a level far above his interest. He drew a knife, hooked it under the sacking of the nearest bale, and tore.

Oilcloth. Under the oilcloth, printed sheets — and Voss's heart performed a long slow drop, because the topmost sheet was exactly what it ought to have been: a spoiled broadside, Mereau's own victory proclamation, misregistered, worthless. Coral's genius, or her cynicism; he suspected they were the same thing in her. She had packed every bale with genuine refuse from her own shop, weeks of it, the ordinary debris of a failing print house, and buried the testament deep.

The soldier leafed through the spoilage with two fingers, the way one leafs through something that smells. He looked at Voss. He looked at Tibo. He looked at the second bale, and cut it more slowly, and Voss understood, in the space of that cut, that they were not searching.

They were waiting. This was theater, staged for the quay, for the witnesses, for the customs officer now very much awake. One did not tear open contraband in public unless one wanted the contraband's owner identified in public. Unless someone above wanted a name attached to a moment.

"Those are mine," Voss said, and stepped forward, and heard himself say it as though from a short distance away — the courier's oldest trick, disassociation, sending the body forward while the self climbed somewhere high and cool to watch. "The shipment is mine. The manifest will confirm it."

"Will it," said the soldier, and nodded, and two of the others moved — not fast, nothing so honest as running — to positions on either side of Voss that made the geometry plain.

Tibo shifted. Voss caught the boy's sleeve without looking at him, a pressure of two fingers, an old signal between old conspirators though Tibo had never been taught it: *still*. The boy went still. Later, Voss thought, later he would understand that this was the only order he gave that mattered anymore, and that the boy obeying it was the whole of his remaining success as a courier of anything.

"Your name?" the soldier said.

"Emeric Voss." Loudly. Clearly. A name spoken on a public dock, in front of a customs officer and a fish-wife and the mate of the *Meridian* and however many of Mereau's quiet watchers stood in doorways pretending to mend nets. A name spoken so that it could not be unwitnessed. "Print broker. And I would like it understood by everyone present that these bales go aboard that ship, and that I am detained, if I am detained, for no reason any of you will be able to write down honestly."

The soldier's smile thinned. Somewhere behind Voss, Tibo made a small sound, half breath, half word, and swallowed it.

Because that had been the design, the final line of Coral's and his shared arithmetic, argued over coffee gone cold at midnight: *they can stop you or they can let the bales go, but if they stop you publicly, after the amnesty decree, with witnesses, they make of you exactly the kind of martyr they've spent two years harvesting from Ren.* Voss could not fight them. Fighting them freed no page. But refusing to be quiet about being taken — making a scene, making a *record*, forcing eight men without insignia to seize a print broker in daylight before a dozen witnesses — that was a weapon of the only kind he'd ever really carried. Courage as performance, Coral had called it once, half in contempt, and he had agreed, and added: *the only performance that makes retreat impossible for the audience, too.* Once a crowd has watched a man taken for speaking, the man's words belong to the crowd. Mereau understood this better than anyone alive. Which was precisely why the soldier hesitated, glancing up the quay toward some doorway, some window, some nodding authority Voss could not see.

The hesitation lasted four heartbeats. Voss counted them.

Then the soldier said, "Hold him," and the geometry closed.

* * *

They were professional about it. That surprised him less than it should have. Hands on his arms, twisted up between his shoulder blades in the manner that permits walking while permitting nothing else; a grip on his collar; a murmured instruction about the alley behind the customs shed. No blow struck on the open quay. Nothing for the fish-wife to describe except men assisting a stumbling man. Mereau's state would be built entirely of such distinctions: violence conducted just out of sight, terror administered at volumes calibrated to the neighborhood.

They walked him past the gangway. This, he understood afterward, was deliberate too — a courtesy of cruelty, or a message to the ship, or simply the shortest route. The *Meridian*'s mate straightened as they passed, cigarette frozen halfway to his mouth, eyes moving from Voss's face to the bales and back with the rapid arithmetic of a working man deciding what he had seen and what he would therefore be required to report and what it would cost him to have seen it.

"The shipment sails," Voss said to him. Conversational. Almost pleasant. "Six bales. Manifest under Lockridge. It's paid through; there'll be no trouble at the far end."

"Voss—" said Tibo, from somewhere behind, strangled.

"Sail with the tide," Voss said, to the mate, to the boy, to the pale sky going gold at its rim over the breakwater, and then the hands turned him and the customs shed swallowed the morning.

In the shed's shadow-stink of tar and rope and old fish, one of them finally hit him — a short, businesslike blow to the stomach that folded him over a coil of cable and taught him, again, the difference between stories and their conduct. In stories the hero takes the blow without sound. Voss made a sound. He made several. He hung on the cable and breathed in shallow installments and heard, above him, muffled through the shed's planking, the ordinary sounds of a harbor waking: gulls, a winch, a ships' bell counting the half hour.

And then, threaded among all of it, unmistakable to a man who had spent ten years listening for it — the *Meridian*'s engine note changing. Deepening. The vibration of it came down through the shed floor and into his knees, into his teeth, into the coil of cable his hands were locked around.

"She's casting off," one of the soldiers said, at the door, peering out. There was satisfaction in it, lazy and complete.

"Not yet," said another. "They'll want to inspect—"

"They've inspected. Orders came an hour ago. Let it go."

Let it go. Voss would think about those three words for a long time afterward, in whatever room they eventually put him in — the casualness of them, the bureaucratic mercy of a machine so confident it no longer needed to chase anything. They had searched the bales in front of witnesses and found rubbish, and somewhere an hour ago someone had decided that rubbish was the correct finding, that the testament could be permitted to leave because a lie does not fear a document; it fears only the audience that has read one. Or else — and this possibility arrived later, colder — Mereau had calculated that letting the pages sail made a better story than burning them: the mad printer's letters, drifting abroad, easily dismissed. He would not know. That was the shape of sacrifice, he discovered, lying on the tarred boards of a customs shed while his own blood hummed in his ears: you pay the price entire and are never shown the receipt.

He dragged himself to the door seam. None of them bothered to stop him; there was nowhere to stop him from going.

Through the gap between the planks he watched the *Meridian* swing her head away from the pier, slow and immense and indifferent, the first sunlight catching her funnel smoke and flattening it into gold. The bales were aboard. He had seen the sixth go up the gangway himself, an hour ago, had rested his palm flat on its sacking for one foolish second, feeling through canvas and oilcloth the faint ridged presence of type pressed into paper — Aldous Ren's last words, the small sad truth of a fevered death on a gray skerry, the names of the men who had sold him, all of it riding in the hold of a coastal steamer like ballast, like cargo, like the most valuable weight ever carried out of Vellamare.

The ship gathered way. The gap of green-gray water between hull and quay widened — a foot, a fathom, a distance he could never cross again in this life or any life his captors allowed him. Voss found that he was smiling. It hurt to do it, and he did it anyway, because there was no one in the shed to see and it was the only prayer he had left in any language.

*Sail,* he thought. *Sail, you ugly faithful tub. Everything I am is on that boat and none of it can be arrested.*

The *Meridian* cleared the breakwater as the sun cleared the hills, and for a moment ship and sun were framed together in the door seam like something composed, typeset, locked in a chase — and then the planks blotted it out as a soldier leaned against them, and the moment was over, and the morning belonged entirely to the men in the shed.

"Up," said the first soldier, almost gently. "Car's waiting. Someone wants to talk to you about printing."

Voss got to his feet. His stomach burned; his wrists would carry bruises in the shape of history. He thought of Coral on the cellar floor among the cases, of her steady hands breaking down the form and melting the matrices before noon, of Tibo keeping his head down and his mouth shut because a dying man's last order had held. He thought: *I have been the road. Let the destination justify me.*

They walked him out into the full bright day, and the quay was crowded now, and every single person on it watched him pass, and not one of them looked away — and Voss understood, with a lift of the heart that felt almost like fear, that the crowd had already begun telling itself the story. That was how it worked. That was how it had always worked, since a pamphleteer climbed a scaffold and became a legend. The victors took the man. But the tale, once witnessed, walked free on other legs.

Behind him, out on the strait, the *Meridian*'s smoke dissolved into the glare over open water — carrying the truth toward foreign presses, toward Coral's shop, toward a reckoning no cell could recall.

Ahead, at the mouth of the street, a black carriage waited, and beside it, unhurried, gloved, smiling the smile of a man greeting an old colleague, stood Cassian Mereau.
