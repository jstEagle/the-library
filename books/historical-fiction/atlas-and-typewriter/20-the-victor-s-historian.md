## 20. The Victor's Historian

The knock came at midmorning, three days after the harbor, and it was the politeness of it that told Coral everything before she opened the door.

Soldiers did not knock. Mereau's men, when they came to a printer's shop in those first raw weeks of the new order, simply shouldered through with the certainty of men who had never once been turned away. But this was three measured raps, evenly spaced, almost tender — and so she knew, standing in the dim of the shop with flour still on her hands from the morning's bread, that Cassian Mereau had come himself.

She wiped her hands on her apron. She thought, absurdly, of her father, who had always said you could judge a visitor by his knock: the impatient rap-rap-rap of creditors, the hesitant single tap of the ashamed, the flat double strike of the constabulary. Her father had catalogued them all across thirty years at this door, and he had never once mentioned the knock of a man who wanted something and intended to be charming about getting it.

She opened the door.

Mereau stood in the thin gray light with his hat in one hand and a wrapped parcel under his arm, and behind him, at a respectful distance, two soldiers who were trying very hard to look like they were not soldiers — plain coats, muskets slung, the posture giving them away anyway. He had aged in the weeks since she'd seen him. Or perhaps, Coral thought, he had merely settled; the man at the Committee of the Dawn's public sessions had seemed to vibrate with purpose, and this one stood still as deep water.

"Mademoiselle Lockridge." He pronounced her name carefully, correctly, as though he had practiced it. "I hope the hour isn't inconvenient."

"The shop is closed," Coral said. It wasn't quite true. The press sat behind her under its dust sheet like furniture in a house of mourning, and Tibo was somewhere above, in the loft, breathing as quietly as a boy could breathe when he'd been told to be silent. But it was true enough for the word it carried: *closed*.

"Then I've caught you at leisure." He smiled — that smile, the one from the broadsides and the commemorative engravings already circulating, the smile that had been reproduced so many times it had begun to look less like an expression than a piece of iconography, like the sun-face on old almanacs. "May I come in? What I have to say is better said sitting, and among printers."

*Among printers.* As if he were one of them. As if the ink in his blood ran anywhere but into speeches.

She stepped aside. She watched him cross her threshold the way she'd once watched a customer handle a rare matrix — noting where his eyes went, what his fingers touched, whether he handled things or merely performed the handling of them. His eyes went everywhere: to the empty composing frames, to the bare walls where proofs had hung, to the swept floor of the workroom where the trapdoor to the cellar lay flush and unremarkable under a rug she had beaten clean that very morning.

He noticed the emptiness. Of course he noticed. She saw it register, a small flicker behind the charm, and she filed it away the way her father had taught her to file a misregistered proof — carefully, against later need.

"You've been busy," Mereau said lightly.

"Housekeeping," Coral said. "There's little call for printing these days. Paper is dear and opinions are dangerous."

"And yet opinions are precisely why I'm here." He set his parcel on the workbench without being asked and unwrapped it with the deliberation of a man unveiling something precious: a manuscript, neatly copied in a fair secretary hand, bound in blue boards. "The official history," he said. "Or rather, the beginning of it. An account of the uprising — its causes, its heroes, its necessary sacrifices. To be printed and distributed freely, at the Republic's expense, in every town that has a press and half the towns that don't."

Coral looked at the manuscript and did not touch it. "You want me to print it."

"I want *this shop* to print it." He let that sit a moment. "Yours is the finest typefoundry in Vellamare — was, under your father, and I'm told the daughter inherited both the matrices and the eye. The history deserves good type. Truth deserves good type." He smiled again, rueful now, self-deprecating, a man aware of the irony he was offering up like a gift. "Yes, I know how that sounds. A revolutionary talking about truth. We've all become very careful with the word lately."

*We*, Coral noted. Not *I*. Never *I*.

"There are other presses," she said.

"There are other presses," he agreed pleasantly. "Half of them burned, the other half staffed by men who can't tell a ligature from a long s. And none of them, Mademoiselle Lockridge, with your particular... resources." The flicker again, quick as a fish turning. "Your father kept remarkable things in that cellar of his. Whole founts discontinued twenty years ago. The Ren matrices — the original ones, cut by his own hand. There are men on my committee who would give a month's salary just to see those."

"They're gone," Coral said. It came out level. She was surprised by how level. "Sold, most of them, after my father died. Debts."

"Ah." Mereau's face performed sympathy beautifully. "Debts. Yes. The old regime was hard on honest tradesmen." He picked up the blue-bound manuscript and held it out to her. "Take some days with it. Read it through. If it satisfies you — print it. And if it satisfies you enough to put your name on the title page as its printer, then the Republic will consider itself in your debt, and the Republic pays its debts, Mademoiselle. Your father's creditors included."

And there it was. Not a threat. Nothing so crude. Just the ledger laid open on the table between them, the arithmetic done in her father's borrowed money and her shop's failing trade, with the Republic's gold waiting patiently on the other side of the column.

Coral took the manuscript. It was lighter than she expected. Lies usually were; ink was heavy, but words weighed nothing at all.

"I'll read it," she said.

"Of course." He retrieved his hat. At the door he paused, and for just a moment the charm thinned like varnish over grain, and she saw the thing beneath it — not cruelty exactly, but patience, vast and unhurried, the patience of a man who owned the tide. "One small thing," he said. "A rumor reached me. Letters. A testament, some say, in Aldous Ren's own hand, said to have left Vellamare on the morning ship." He examined his hat brim with great interest. "Naturally I don't credit rumors. A dead martyr cannot write testaments. That is rather the point of martyrs."

"That is rather the point," Coral agreed.

"But rumors are like damp in a wall. They find their way through anything, given time." He settled the hat on his head. "If such papers existed — if they had been *set in type*, say — well. Metal melts, Mademoiselle. Lead is a forgiving metal. It forgets everything it ever was." He smiled his third smile of the visit, and this one did not reach his eyes at all. "Good day. I'll return within the week for your answer."

The door closed softly behind him. Softer than any soldier would have closed it.

Coral stood a long while in the quiet, the manuscript in her hands, listening to the sound of boots receding down the lane. Then she crossed the workroom, lifted the corner of the rug, and put her palm flat against the cold iron ring of the cellar trapdoor, the way another woman might touch a grave.

* * *

Tibo came down when he was sure the soldiers were gone, taking the ladder too fast as boys do, and found her still crouched there with her hand on the ring.

"He wants me to print his book," she said.

The boy's face went through several expressions and settled on fury, which was easier for him than fear. "After what they did to Voss—"

"After everything," Coral agreed. She rose, knees stiff, and looked at him properly. He had lost weight since that night at the press; there were shadows under his eyes that hadn't been there in spring. They all wore them now, she supposed. The whole city wore them. "Sit down, Tibo. I need to think out loud, and you're the only one I trust to hear it."

He sat on the composing stone, legs swinging, thirteen years old and trying to look older, and she told him the shape of it: the offer, the price, the threat dressed in silk. Print the official history and the shop lived, the debts died, the Lockridge name went on the title page of the victors' version of the world. Refuse, and — nothing happened, officially. Nothing ever would happen officially. Just inspections, and paper shortages, and a fire that started somehow, because fires did, in cities full of amnesty and ash.

"If we print it," Tibo said slowly, "then everyone believes his story."

"If we print it," Coral said, "everyone believes *a* story. That's worse. A lie everyone doubts is a nuisance. A lie with beautiful type and a reputable printer's mark—" she touched the brass plate by the door, her grandfather's monogram, *LOCKRIDGE & FILS*, the *fils* scratched out and re-engraved twice over the generations, "—that becomes the truth people teach their children."

"So we refuse."

"We agree," Coral said.

Tibo stared at her. "But—"

"Listen to me." She knelt so their eyes were level, and she was startled to find her hands steady. "Mereau doesn't want the book, Tibo. He wants the answer to a question: does the truth still exist anywhere he can't reach it? Every refusal is an admission. Every locked cellar, every evasive word tells him yes — yes, it exists, yes, it's worth hunting, yes, the copies that sailed are real and dangerous and must be chased. But if I smile at him and say *yes, Monsieur Mereau, I'll print your history, God preserve the Republic*—" she made her voice smooth and hollow, a parrot's voice, "—then what does he learn? That the Lockridge woman folded. That there was never anything in the cellar. That the rumors are only rumors."

"He won't believe it."

"No," Coral agreed. "Not entirely. He'll always wonder. But wondering isn't knowing, and knowing is what he needs, because right now the testament is surfacing in foreign papers—" she had Tibo's letter from Marseille in her apron pocket, had read it so many times the creases were going soft, "—and every week he doesn't know where the type is, he has to wonder whether the next printing comes from *here*. From his own city. From the cellar under the shop he chose not to burn."

She straightened. Below them, beneath the flagstone under the cold hearth where no one had thought to look, forty pages of melted lead sat in a slag-pan disguised as ballast, formless, anonymous, remembering nothing — just as Mereau wished all lead to remember nothing. Above them the sun moved behind the harbor clouds, and the light in the workroom went silver and cold.

"He's coming back within the week," Coral said. "So we have six days to make the lie perfect. And Tibo — the best lies are typeset in truth. Fetch me the manuscript. If I'm going to print his history, I'm going to read every word of it first, and find out exactly what kind of lie I'm agreeing to tell."

* * *

She worked on the official history for three days, and it was worse than she expected, and better.

Worse, because the prose was skillful — she suspected Mereau had written much of it himself, or supervised the man who had — and skillful lies offended her craft-deep soul in a way clumsy ones never did. The uprising in its pages had no failures, no panicked retreats, no boys shot in the back running from the customs house. It had heroes arranged like type in a forme, each in his place, each justified. Aldous Ren appeared on the fourth page, executed by the old regime, his death "sealing the covenant between the people and the Dawn" — and Coral sat with that sentence a long time, thinking of a fevered man on a gray skerry writing his last testament by tallow light, betrayed by the committee whose broadsides now canonized him. Mereau had even given Ren's death a date. The wrong date. She checked it against memory, against the letters Voss had carried, and it was wrong, deliberately wrong, the kind of error that exists to be believed.

Better, because buried in chapter nine, in a paragraph so dull it could only be deliberate, was a single line about the destruction of "certain private papers of seditious character, whose survival serves only the enemies of concord." Mereau had put his fear into the record itself. He had confessed the thing he feared in the act of legislating against it. Coral read that paragraph four times, and by the fourth reading she was almost grateful to him, because it meant he was afraid, and men who are afraid make mistakes in their composition just as surely as tired compositors do.

On the evening of the fifth day she heard the polite triple knock again.

She had prepared the shop like a stage. The press was uncovered — let him see it, oiled and ready. The blue manuscript lay on the bench with corrections marked in pencil, real corrections, typographic ones, because if she was going to sell the lie she would sell it with craft: *this line sets ragged, this folio is repeated, this em-dash has been cast too thin*. No refusals written anywhere. No trace of anything but a printer considering her work.

Tibo was upstairs. The rug lay square over the trapdoor. Under the flagstone by the hearth, the slag-pan sat where a slag-pan should sit, among other pans of scrap lead, indistinguishable, innocent.

She opened the door. "Monsieur Mereau."

"Mademoiselle Lockridge." He came in carrying a bottle of wine this time, red, with a wax seal, the kind of gesture that was itself a message: *we are civilized, this is a transaction between civilized people.* He set it on the bench beside the manuscript and looked at her with an expression of frank, warm curiosity. "And?"

"I'll print it," Coral said.

The silence lasted perhaps two seconds. She counted them by the drip of the eaves outside, which she could hear because the rest of the room had stopped moving entirely.

"Well," Mereau said. He drew the word out gently, tasting it. "Well. May I confess, mademoiselle, that I rehearsed several versions of this conversation, and in none of them did you agree so quickly."

"Perhaps your rehearsals flattered me."

"Perhaps they measured you." He drifted along the workbench, fingertips trailing, and paused at the pencil marks on the manuscript's margin. He read them. Something in his shoulders eased, fractionally — the relaxation of a man finding craft where he expected defiance. "These are yours?"

"Corrections. If the Republic's history is going to be beautiful, it may as well be correctly set. Your chapter nine runs two lines short on the second forme; either cut eleven words or I cast you a slightly condensed fount for the closing paragraphs." She heard her own voice going bright and technical, chattering the way her father used to chatter with difficult customers, filling the air with expertise until the expertise became a wall. "I can have the first forme locked up within the fortnight."

"The fortnight." He turned from the manuscript and looked at her fully, and the charm dropped away like a coat taken off indoors — not replaced by menace, exactly, but by attention, total and weighing, the attention of a man reading fine print. "Mademoiselle, I am going to ask you a question, and I want you to consider that your answer matters far less than you think, because I have already decided what I will believe. Where is your father's cellar type?"

"Gone," Coral said. "As I told you. Sold for debts."

"Mm." He walked, unhurried, to the rug. To the trapdoor handle beneath it. He stood over it for a moment, and Coral's heart beat so hard she was sure he could hear it, sure it would sound through the floorboards like a drum. Then he bent and pulled the ring, and the trapdoor swung up on its hinge, and the smell of the cellar came up — cold stone, lamp oil, the faint mineral breath of metal.

He looked down into the dark for a long moment.

Coral looked too, over his shoulder, calm as Sunday, and saw what he saw: an emptied cellar. The racks stripped. The frames gone. Dust lying thick and undisturbed on the stairs — she had sifted fresh ash from the hearth over it, a printer's trick, aging surfaces the way her father had aged proof sheets for effect. Nothing down there but shadow and the memory of metal.

"The cellar of a sold-out shop," Mereau said quietly. "Or a robbed one."

"The cellar of a poor one," Coral said. "You may send whoever you like down with a lantern, Monsieur. You'll find spiderwebs and my mother's old preserving jars, and if you're lucky, the cat."

He straightened. He closed the trapdoor himself, gently, and smoothed the rug back into place with his shoe, and when he faced her he was smiling again — the fourth smile, the private one, the one she suspected no engraver had ever captured because there was nothing in it a nation could be asked to admire.

"You know what I admire about you, Mademoiselle Lockridge?" he said. "Your father lied to me once, years ago, in this same room. He was terrible at it. Sweat, stammer, the whole amateur performance. I've often wondered since what he was protecting that night." He picked up his wine bottle, tucked it under his arm — a gift declined without ceremony. "You, however. You lie the way you set type. Clean margins. No broken letters. One would almost applaud."

"Then we understand each other," Coral said. "The history prints within the fortnight. My name on the colophon, as discussed."

"Your name on the colophon." He bowed, shallow and precise. At the door he paused, hat in hand, silhouetted against the pale street. "Foreign newspapers," he said musingly. "Someone showed me a page yesterday. Allegedly a testament of Aldous Ren, alleging all manner of things. You know what struck me about it? Not the accusations. The *composition*. Someone set that text by someone who loved the craft. Even spacing throughout. Beautiful kerning on the names." He settled the hat on. "Beautiful work travels, mademoiselle. It's recognized. It's remembered. Do give my regards to your apprentice."

The door closed. Softly. Always softly.

Coral stood in the middle of her shop, listening to the silence settle back over the room like dust. Then she walked to the bench, picked up the wine, considered it, and poured it out through the window gutter into the lane, where it ran dark and slow between the cobbles like something that had been bled.

Her hand, she noticed, only shook now, afterward. That was new. A year ago it would have shaken all through; a month ago it would have shaken halfway through. She was becoming someone whose hands waited.

Above her, faint through the ceiling, came the small deliberate scrape of Tibo's foot: three taps on the loft boards. All clear. She tapped back once with the poker against the hearthstone — *all clear* — and then stood a moment longer with her palm against the warm stone over the hidden slag-pan, where forty pages of testimony slept in their melted sleep, formless and forgotten by everyone but her.

*Let him wonder,* she thought. *Let him chase beautiful kerning to the ends of the sea.*

Then she took up the pencil, sat down at the bench, and began correcting the victor's history — line by line, letter by letter, making his lie as beautiful as her hands knew how, while somewhere beyond the harbor the true account rode its foreign presses, multiplying in languages Mereau could not burn, faster than any correction could ever chase it.
