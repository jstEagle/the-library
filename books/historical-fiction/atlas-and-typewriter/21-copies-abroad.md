## 21. Copies Abroad

The first word came back on a Tuesday, folded inside a bale of rag paper ordered from the northern mills — paper Coral had ordered partly because she needed it and partly because ships' manifests traveled farther than letters ever dared.

Tibo brought the bale in from the quay himself, hauling it on the shop's one surviving handcart, its wheels complaining over the cobbles like an old man asked to rise before dawn. He was sixteen now by her loose arithmetic, though he carried himself older these days, with a watchfulness she recognized and grieved: the look of someone who had learned that doorways have two sides. He cut the twine with the bench knife, sawed through the knot the way she'd taught him — never toward the thumb — and then stopped, his hands going still on the paper.

"Mistress Lockridge." His voice had that careful flatness he used when he didn't want a thing to be true or not true until he'd said it all the way through. "There's something wrapped inside. Under the top layer."

Coral set down the composing stick she'd been holding — a body of type for Mereau's customs office, an order she took because taking orders kept the shop alive, and every order she took was a small betrayal she had learned to swallow without chewing. She crossed to the bale. The paper smelled of the sea, of tar and cold brine, the way everything smelléd of the sea now that autumn had come back around and the harbor wind had teeth again.

Beneath forty sheets of good rag stock lay a newspaper. Not one of theirs — nothing printed in Vellamare looked like this anymore, not since the presses had been consolidated under the Committee's oversight, their columns marching in obedient lockstep. This was *The Meridian Advertiser*, out of Corvane, three hundred miles north across the water, printed in a typeface so clean it looked embarrassed by its own clarity.

She read the headline twice. Then a third time, because the first two readings had not made the words behave.

*TESTAMENT OF ALDOUS REN SURFACES: THE MARTYR'S OWN HAND ACCUSES HIS BETRAYERS.*

Below it, in dense twin columns, ran the text she knew better than her own breathing. She had set every line of it herself, in the cellar, by lamplight, while Voss read aloud in a voice that grew rougher as the night went on and Tibo kept watch at the street door with a hand mirror angled to catch the reflections of boots passing. She had spaced the words, led them, locked the chase, inked the plate, pulled the lever forty times until dawn came gray through the coal chute. Every comma in that foreign column had passed through her fingers first.

"They did it," Tibo said softly. "It got out."

"It got out," Coral agreed. Her voice sounded strange to her — distant, as if someone else in the room had spoken.

She should have felt triumph. That was what she had imagined for weeks, standing at the bench pulling Mereau's official broadsides off the press, each one a small act of theater: here is the loyal printer, the widow of the trade, who melted her matrices when asked and prints what she is told. She had rehearsed triumph the way an actress rehearses a scene she may never perform. And now it stood in front of her, folded in a bale of paper, and what she felt instead was something closer to grief — grief with an edge to it, grief that had been waiting too long and had grown strange in the waiting.

Voss would never see this. That was the thought that arrived beneath all the others and refused to leave. Whatever dock or prison yard he stood in this morning, whatever they fed him, whatever they told him about how history was being written now — he did not know. He had stood on the quay in the first light and let them take him, and the whole point of his standing there, loud enough for witnesses, refusing pardon loudly enough that even Mereau's own men would carry the story home — the whole point was that he would never know if it worked. He had bought the truth's passage with himself and been denied even the receipt.

"Should I read it to you?" Tibo asked.

"I can read, Tibo."

"I know. Only — you've gone somewhere. You've gone somewhere and left me standing here with a newspaper."

Coral blinked, and the room came back: the smell of oil and lead, the gray light through the front windows, the press squatting in the corner like a patient animal. She sat down on the stool her father had worn smooth with thirty years of sitting, and she read the article properly.

It was more than reprinting. The *Advertiser*'s editors had done what she and Voss had only hoped some foreign editor might be brave enough to do: they had annotated. Beside Ren's testament — his account of the old regime's offer, of the committee man who sold his route to the secret police for a general's favor, of his escape from the execution that had been staged with such theatrical efficiency, of the fever that took him weeks later on Gray Skerry, coughing his last testimony into Voss's keeping — beside all of it, the editors had printed the corroborating documents. The harbor master's altered log. The payment record from the old regime's archive, smuggled out before the amnesty fires, matching sums against dates against names. Cassian Mereau's signature, in the confident looping hand he now used to sign victory proclamations, appearing on a requisition dated eleven days before the uprising that made him a hero.

And beneath it all, an editorial, brief and surgical: *A revolution that begins in truth cannot end in comfortable lies without becoming the thing it overthrew.*

"Oh," Tibo said, reading over her shoulder, "that's going to make them furious."

That, at least, pulled something like a laugh out of her. "That, boy, is the entire point."

* * *

The news did not arrive alone, and it did not arrive quietly. It arrived the way weather arrives over the harbor — first a change in the light, then a pressure in the ears, then the whole sky rearranging itself.

Within four days, copies of the *Advertiser* were moving through Vellamare the way rumors move: hand to hand, folded into coat linings, passed across fish stalls and under tavern tables. The Committee banned it immediately, of course — proclamation posted on the customs house within hours of the first copy surfacing, penalties enumerated, possession equated with sedition — and the banning accomplished precisely what bannings always accomplish. People who had never cared about Aldous Ren beyond his usefulness as a story now wanted to know what was in those columns. A forbidden document acquires a weight no permitted one can match. Coral had known this since she was nine years old, watching her father print pamphlets whose power lay half in their arguments and half in their illegality.

By the following week, the *Advertiser* had been joined by papers from two other ports, and then a Corvane weekly that ran the testament serially across four issues, each installment ending on a line calculated to bring readers back — Coral suspected a fellow printer's hand in that, some journeyman north of the water who understood pacing the way she understood leading. The story was metastasizing. Corrections could not chase it. Mereau's office issued an official rebuttal calling the testament a fabrication of foreign agents nostalgic for the old regime; the rebuttal was reprinted in the foreign papers *as evidence of the cover-up*, with editorial commentary of the gentlest, most devastating kind: *The Committee denies. We merely ask our readers to compare hands.*

Someone, Coral learned through Tibo's network of errand boys and dock children, had even taken the trouble to engrave facsimiles of Ren's handwriting and Mereau's signature side by side, and those engravings circulated on single sheets, cheaply printed, sold for a copper in markets where most buyers could not read a word of the surrounding text but understood pictures perfectly.

"The picture's better than the article," Tibo reported, delighted, on a morning when frost furred the window glass. "Old Mano at the net shed says he can't read but he knows a forged signature when he sees one now. He says everybody at the shed knows. They've got one pinned up by the stove."

"Mano can't read?"

"Can't and won't. Says reading is how they trick you." Tibo grinned. "He's got a point."

Coral found herself smiling, and then found herself, an hour later, standing in the type cellar with the lamp turned low, looking at the swept-clean floorboards beneath which her father's proof books still slept — she had not melted everything, only what needed melting, only what Mereau's men had been shown — and feeling the full strangeness of what they had done settle over her like the cellar's cold.

The truth was out. It was loose in the world, replicating in languages she couldn't read, in towns she'd never see, in mouths of fishermen who trusted pictures over print. It no longer belonged to her, or to Voss, or even to Ren. That was the nature of the thing they had built that night in the cellar: once set and once loosed, type travels. Her father used to say a printed lie dies with its teller but a printed truth outlives everyone, including the people who wished it spoken. He had said it wistfully, she remembered now — she could hear his voice, feel the scratch of his beard against her hair — and she understood the wistfulness differently at twenty-six than she had at nine. Truth outliving you means you don't get to see what it does. You pay the whole price up front and the receipt sails away without you.

* * *

Mereau's allies began distancing themselves the way rats are said to leave ships — discreetly, in ones and twos, and always with an air of having intended to leave anyway.

First it was small things. General Ardaux, whose endorsement had anchored Mereau's authority in the army, gave a speech commemorating the uprising's dead in which he conspicuously praised "those who died for the cause" — a phrase that meant nothing until you remembered that Mereau's official history insisted Ren had died at the execution, whereas the testament insisted otherwise, and that Ardaux had chosen, in public, to say *died* rather than *was martyred*, a distinction that would have been invisible to anyone not listening for it. Everyone was listening for it.

Then Deputy Halloran resigned from the Committee of the Dawn, citing health. Then the Corvane trading consortium postponed — indefinitely, citing logistics — the loan agreement that was to have stabilized the revolutionary government's currency. Then, in the most extraordinary turn, one of the members of the original pamphlet circle, an aging typesetter named Broussard whom Coral had met once years ago at her father's bench, published in the *Advertiser* a letter confirming that he had handled Ren's correspondence personally and could verify the disputed hand beyond any doubt — and adding, with a typesetter's precision that made the letter read like a deposition, that the committee's official account of Ren's death had never matched the physical evidence, and that he had chosen silence for two years out of fear, and that fear was no longer the louder of his obligations.

"He waited until it was safe," Coral said when Tibo brought her the clipping, unable to keep the bitterness entirely out of her mouth.

"Maybe." Tibo considered it with the serious air he wore when he was working out whether the adult in front of him wanted honesty or comfort. He usually chose honesty; she had never trained it out of him and never would. "Or maybe it took the safe thing happening to give him the nerve. One copy lands in Corvane and suddenly two years of silence turns into a letter. Maybe that's how it works. Maybe nobody moves first, they just move next."

She looked at him — this boy who had copied a page in fear and confessed in tears and kept watch at doors all winter — and thought: *he is learning how revolutions actually work, at sixteen, in a print shop.* Somebody should write it down for him. Then corrected herself: somebody had written it down. That was the entire point.

* * *

The vindication, when she let herself feel it, tasted of ash.

She tried to explain it to no one, because there was no one to explain it to — Voss imprisoned, his fate officially "under review" for going on two months now, which everyone understood to mean *as long as convenient*; her few remaining friends in the trade scattered or cowed; Tibo too young for the shape of the thing, or perhaps not too young at all, which was worse. So she explained it to the press instead, talking to the machine the way sailors talk to ships, oiling its joints and telling it, in the privacy of the closed shop, that she had gotten what she wanted and it wasn't what she'd thought she wanted.

Because here was the arithmetic she hadn't anticipated. Ren's name was cleansed — but Ren was dead either way, had died feverish and alone on a rock in the sea, and no amount of vindication would make his last weeks anything but frightened. The testament was everywhere — but Voss had not seen a single copy, might never see one, might already be somewhere the news reached slowly or not at all, in a cell where the guards' instructions were to permit no papers. The betrayers were exposed — but exposed was not deposed. Mereau still walked the quay with his guard, still smiled, still signed things; the foreign papers could accuse all they liked, and the accusation landed in Vellamare as a dull ache rather than a blow, because people here had learned to survive governments and expected to go on surviving this one. Ardaux hedged. Halloran resigned. The loan stalled. But the sun still rose on the Committee of the Dawn, and the customs house still flew its flag, and Coral still printed its broadsides because the alternative was a shuttered shop and a girl with no leverage at all.

Truth, it turned out, was not a hammer. It was water. It found every crack, seeped into every foundation, swelled every board — but slowly. Slowly enough that the people who poured it might drown before the wall fell. She thought of Voss saying that, in the cellar, somewhere past midnight: *We're not lighting a fuse, Coral. We're raising a tide. Fuses are faster but tides reach further.* She had believed him. She still believed him. But belief and comfort had turned out to be different countries, and she lived now in the borderland between them.

On a raw Thursday, Tibo came back from the fish market with news delivered at a run, cheeks burning, breath smoking in the cold.

"Mistress Lockridge. Mistress Lockridge, there's a ship in from Corvane, and the harbormaster's men are searching every vessel, every crate — Mereau's ordered it, they're hunting the newspapers, anyone caught with one is being held for questioning—" He caught his breath, thrust his hand into his jacket, and produced a folded sheet, damp with sea air, pressed flat and warm from riding against his ribs. "But the *Adelaine*'s second mate owes my cousin a favor. And he said—"

He stopped. Swallowed. Held the paper out.

"And he said to tell you the captain put in a piece of cargo special. Said it wasn't on any manifest. Said it was addressed to *the printer's daughter, Vellamare*, and the mate was to put it in no hand but hers."

Coral took the sheet. Unfolded it with fingers that had gone very steady, the way her hands always went steady at moments when shaking would have been reasonable — a printer's hands, trained against tremor at the composing stick.

It was a newspaper page. The *Meridian Advertiser*, the fourth installment of the serialized testament. But in the margin, in pencil, in a hand she had never seen before yet knew instantly — because she had spent a night studying its owner's letters, the testament itself, line by line — someone had written:

*Coral — If this reaches you, then it reached me first, and knowing was worth everything it cost. The tide is higher than we hoped. Look after the boy. Look after the shop. I am looked after — the review goes slowly, which means no one has decided what I am yet, which means there is still time for your ink to finish deciding for them. Set another line for me when you can spare the lead. — E.V.*

She read it three times. Alive. Untried. Undecided-about. Reachable.

Tibo was watching her face the way he watched the street door, missing nothing. "Good news?" he asked. "Or bad?"

Coral folded the page along its crease and slid it inside the false back of the proof shelf, where her father had hidden things before her and where she hid things now, and felt the old familiar cold of resolve settling into place like a locked chase.

"Both," she said. "Fetch your coat. We're going to need paper — and Tibo? Take the long way to the paper merchant."
