## 22. The Open Case

The shutters had not been opened in six weeks, and when Coral Lockridge lifted them on the first morning of the new year, the hinges complained like old men asked to dance. Light fell into the shop in a long dusty blade, catching the motes that hung above the composing stone, and for a moment she simply stood in the doorway of Lockridge & Son, Printers, and let the air of the place move over her — ink and oil and cold iron, the smell of two generations of her family's labor soaked so deep into the plaster that no decree could ever scour it out.

Mereau's men had come twice in the weeks after Voss was taken. The first time they searched, turning over type cases with their bayonet scabbards, dumping her father's chases onto the floor with a carelessness that had made her bite her tongue until she tasted copper. The second time they came only to look, and the looking was worse. But they found nothing, because there was nothing to find: the cellar stood empty, its floor swept clean, the crucible in the corner still holding the dull silvered dross of forty sets of matrices melted down and poured into ingots that now lay, unremarkable and anonymous, at the bottom of the harbor where she and Tibo had dropped them by night.

The testament was gone from Vellamare. It was on the water, and then it was on other presses, and then it was everywhere.

She had read it herself in a foreign gazette smuggled into the city inside a bale of Dutch linen — *The Last Testament of Aldous Ren, set down in his own hand and printed for the world* — and there was a strange vertigo in reading words she had set herself, letter by letter, in the small hours, rendered back to her in someone else's typeface, someone else's country. The truth had crossed the water wearing borrowed clothes. But it had crossed.

And Mereau had not fallen. That was the lesson she was still learning. He had issued a statement calling the document a forgery fabricated by enemies of the Dawn; he had permitted, with great magnanimity, the *debate* of the question in the public papers; he had survived, because men like Mereau always survived, at least for a season. Two of his committee had distanced themselves. One had fled north. The amnesty held, brittle as spring ice, and under its terms Emeric Voss remained in the citadel prison, charged not with sedition but with "obstruction of the historical record," which was the most honest charge Coral had ever seen a government bring.

She crossed the shop, unlocked the case beneath the counter, and took out her father's ledger — the true one, the one she had found folded into the lining of the proof press crate, its entries in his cramped hand recording payments received from Aldous Ren across eighteen months, sums taken for silence and storage and the arrangement of a boat. She did not need it anymore for evidence. The world had its evidence now. She kept it for a different reason: as a mirror. Whenever she was tempted to believe that survival was the same thing as integrity, she opened it and saw what a man looked like after thirty years of choosing safety one payment at a time.

Her father had died before the reckoning came to him. She sometimes thought that was the cruelest mercy the revolution had done him.

Outside, Vellamare was waking. The fish carts rattled up from the quay. Somewhere a gull argued with the morning. The harbor lay flat and bright beyond the rooftops, and out on it, small and white-sailed, the packet boat from the north was making for the mole — the same route, more or less, that a soaked stranger named Emeric Voss had come by nearly five months ago, carrying letters in a dead man's hand.

Coral tied on her apron and went to open the type cases.

* * *

Tibo arrived an hour later, breathless from running, his collar half out of his coat, fifteen years old and growing so fast she could see the change week to week, his wrists poking past his cuffs like shoots from a pot too small.

"You reopened," he said, standing in the doorway as if the threshold were a border he needed permission to cross. "Madame Ferreux told me at the market. She said the sign was out."

"The sign is out."

"Are you sure? After everything—" He glanced over his shoulder at the street, at the corner where Mereau's watchers used to stand. The corner was empty this morning, but empty corners remembered.

"Tibo." She pulled the second stool up to the composing stone and patted it. "They burned the Perrin house with its archive inside. They burned the Quay Street press. They left mine standing because I agreed to print the official history, and I agreed because I am a liar and a survivor, and both of those things are going to be useful if you mean to learn this trade. Sit down."

He sat. He had sat at this stool before, in the old days, sweeping shavings and fetching coffee, but this was different and they both knew it. This was apprenticeship proper, the kind her father had given her, the kind that began with the weight of metal in the hand.

"Read me that line," she said, pointing to a stick of type she had set before he came — a line of the official broadside, the one Mereau's office wanted ready by Friday, full of phrases like *the harmonious dawn* and *the people's forgiven sons.*

He read it. His lip curled slightly on *forgiven.*

"You hate it," Coral said.

"It's not true."

"No. It isn't. And we're going to print it anyway, because a press that prints only truth gets burned before it prints anything, and a press that burns prints nothing ever again. Do you understand the difference between lying and printing lies?"

He frowned at the stick. "Is there one?"

"There is the whole difference." She took the stick from him and held it level, the way her father had once held it for her. "A lie is something you make to hide in. A printed lie is something you make to sell, and you sell it with your eyes open, and every coin it earns buys metal, and paper, and time — and time is what you spend setting the true things in secret, page by page, until the day comes when the true things are stronger than the fear. My father sold lies for thirty years and never spent a sou of it on truth. That was his grave, Tibo. I intend to be buried somewhere better."

The boy was quiet a moment. Then: "Voss said something like that. Before."

"You talked to him about it?"

"He used to ask me what I'd seen. From the loft. He said a watcher who knows why he watches is a sentry, and one who doesn't is just a spy." Tibo turned the phrase over, pleased with it, the way the young are pleased with any wisdom that has been handed to them whole.

"Well." Coral slid the official type back into its case, letter by letter, the rhythm steadying her the way it had steadied her since she was nine years old. "Then let us make you a sentry. First lesson. We begin with the case itself, because everything begins with knowing where the letters live."

* * *

The winter passed in galley proofs and coffee and the slow accumulation of skill. Coral taught Tibo the distribution of the case — the great alphabet laid out like a city map, capitals in their quarter, italics in their lane — and then kerning, and leading, and the black art of imposing a forme so it would not pie when the press bit it. She taught him to feel for a letter upside-down or backward with his fingertips alone, blind, the way her father could, the way she could. She taught him that a compositor reads everything he sets, always, because a man who puts words in metal without understanding them is a machine, and machines can be owned.

In February the foreign papers ran a third extract from the testament, this one naming the sums the old regime had paid through an intermediary to a member of Ren's own committee — the intermediary identified, in Ren's careful hand, only by initials that everyone in Vellamare could expand without help. Mereau's allies in the northern ports began to use the word *alleged.* In politics, Coral had learned, *alleged* was a door opening; in another season it might be a door closing, but for now it hung ajar, and cold honest wind came through it.

In March a petition circulated for the release of prisoners held under the obstruction statutes. It gathered names slowly, quietly, in the backs of shops and the upper rooms of taverns. Coral signed it in the middle of the list, neither first nor last, a signature among hundreds, and felt no thrill in doing it — only the steady pleasure of a woman putting her weight on the correct side of a lever.

And twice a month, on the pretext of delivering printed matter to the citadel garrison — the garrison paid handsomely for its orders of service and its muster rolls and asked no questions about the politics of its printer — she carried a small parcel wrapped in oilcloth to the gate, addressed in a round merchant's hand to a prisoner in the west wing.

Inside each parcel were ordinary things: tobacco, a pencil stub, a boiled sweet. And inside the ordinary things, folded to the size of a stamp, a single sheet of tissue bearing a few lines of news — the state of the shop, the progress of the petition, the health of the fish market, the fact that the lighthouse on Gray Skerry had been relit in December and its keeper, a taciturn man named Ollivier, now flew a small red flag on the first of every month, which meant, in a language of flags that two couriers had invented between them, *still watched for; not forgotten.*

The parcels came back sometimes, returned unopened, which meant the warders had grown suspicious. Then she waited a month and tried again. Once a scrap came back inside the returned wrapping, torn from the margin of a prayer book, bearing four words in a cramped, familiar, gallant hand:

*Still here. Set on.*

Set on. Printer's language. Keep composing. Keep the forme locked. She kept it pinned inside the case lid, beneath the flap where she stored her finest quills, and she looked at it every morning before the lamp was lit, and every morning it was enough.

* * *

In April, when the light came earlier and the harbor turned the color of hammered tin in the mornings, Coral began the work she had been saving. She cleared the evening bench, the good one by the window, and laid out the new metal.

She had cast it herself. That mattered to her. In March she had taken the remaining stock of unallocated lead — the dross ingots were gone to the harbor bottom, but a printer always has metal — and cut three new matrices by hand at her father's bench, working by lamplight the way she had worked on the night a stranger knocked with a dead man's letters. Punch-cutting was dying work, the old craft, the craft before machine-casting came in, and her fingers had remembered it even when she believed they wouldn't. Three letters. She had cut them slowly, checking each against proofs pulled on tissue, refining, recutting, until the serifs sang.

E. V.

Two letters and a stop. Enough.

She set them into a single line with the rest of the type drawn from the ordinary case, and the line read:

*Set by E. V., who taught that the record is the resistance.*

It was not a monument. Monuments required permission, and permission required Mereau, and Mereau would never grant it. It was instead a colophon — the small line at the foot of a page where a printer signs her work — and she intended it to stand at the foot of every job the shop printed for the rest of her life: the muster rolls and the market lists and the almanacs and yes, the official broadsides with all their harmonious dawns. Every page that left Lockridge & Son would carry those eleven words in type too small for the casual eye and large enough for anyone who knew how to read a colophon, which was precisely the audience she wished to address.

Voss would know. When they released him — and she had stopped saying *if* sometime in February, not from optimism but from arithmetic, because governments that burn archives need scapegoats less than governments that keep them — whenever they released him, some compositor in some shop would show him a piece of Vellamare printing, and he would tilt it toward the light, and find his own initials waiting for him at the foot of the page like a chair kept warm.

Tibo watched her lock the forme, his elbows on the stone, his chin on his fists.

"Mister Voss used to say the strangest thing," he offered. "He said a martyr is somebody the survivors agree upon. He said it like it was a warning."

"It is a warning." Coral tightened the quoins, checked them, tightened again. "Look what the survivors agreed upon for Aldous Ren. A hero with no faults and no fever and no last weeks of coughing in a lighthouse, asking after the people he loved. They built him out of marble because marble doesn't contradict you." She lifted the finished forme onto the press bed. "We put him back in lead. Lead weighs what it weighs."

"And Voss? What will they agree upon for him?"

Coral considered the boy — the sharpness coming into his face, the question behind the question, the apprentice learning that the trade was larger than the bench.

"That depends on who's holding the type when the story's finished," she said. "Which is why he's holding some. Pull the lever. Easy, now — let the press do the work."

Tibo pulled. The bed ran in, the platen came down, and the press spoke with the sound it had made all her life, the sound of her childhood and her father's lifetime and every argument and every secret this room had ever held: a great iron heartbeat, once.

She lifted the sheet and carried it to the window, to the morning.

The line sat at the foot of the page, crisp and black and permanent, the new-cut letters sharp as the day they were born. Beyond the glass, Vellamare glittered in the early sun — the quay, the mole, the white sails standing out of the harbor one by one as the tide turned, each of them a small departure, a small survival, a story leaving port for wherever stories go to become history.

History had happened to her. That was the thing she finally understood, wiping the ink from her fingers, watching her apprentice pull the next sheet. For twenty-six years she had stood at this bench believing that history was weather — something that happened elsewhere, to other people, while she kept her head down and her accounts square. Then a stranger had walked in soaked from the packet boat, and the weather had come indoors, and she had learned that the difference between suffering history and making it was nothing more than this: a case of type, a locked door, a lamp, and the refusal — night after night, page after page — to let the metal say anything you did not choose.

"Ink remembers," she said aloud, to the shop, to her father, to the man in the west wing of the citadel, to whichever of them was listening.

"What, Madame?" Tibo asked.

"Nothing. Next sheet. We have four hundred to run and the tide won't wait for either of us."

The press began again, and did not stop.
