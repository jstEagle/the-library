## 1. The Ledger of Small Hours

The river froze in November and stayed frozen the way old men fall asleep in chapel—suddenly, thoroughly, and with no intention of waking before spring. Wendell Delacroix could see it from the hospital window when the steam on the glass permitted: a long white seam stitched between two gray banks, holding its breath.

She did not think of it as a crossing then. She thought of it as weather.

Inside Ward Three, weather was something that happened to other people. Inside Ward Three there was only the stove that ate coal like a consumptive eats bread, the forty-two beds arranged with the optimism of arithmetic rather than space, and the small hours—the ones between two and five in the morning—when the wounded did their truest talking. Men lied bravely by daylight. At three o'clock they told you about their mothers' kitchens and the girl in Lyon who had never answered the last letter, and Wendell wrote none of it down, because some things were not hers to keep. She kept other things.

"Wendell." Private Okafor, in bed nine, his voice sanded down to a whisper by three days of fever. "Am I going to lose it?"

"Your arm? No." She tucked the blanket under his good shoulder. "Your mind was lost before you arrived, but I'm told that's common among poets."

"I'm not a poet."

"No," she agreed. "Poets are usually more frightened of me."

He laughed, which was the point, and coughed, which was not, and she held the cup for him while he drank, and neither of them mentioned that the man in bed eight had stopped complaining an hour ago, because in Ward Three everyone understood the grammar of silence.

Sister Bernadette ran the ward with the tenderness of a drill sergeant and the discipline of a grandmother, and she had one rule above all others: *do not be interesting*. Interesting got you noticed. Noticed got you assigned to the receiving tent, or worse, to the officers' annex, where the tea was better and the dying happened behind doors, politely, so that no one had to look at it. Wendell had learned the rule her first week, when a nurse named Colombe had made a suggestion at rounds—a good suggestion, a correct one—and had been transferred within the month to a depot so far back it was nearly peacetime. The lesson sank in. Wendell became excellent at being furniture. She mopped, she counted morphine tablets twice, she developed a way of standing near walls that made surgeons forget she was there, which was precisely how she heard everything worth hearing.

It was remarkable, the things men said in front of furniture.

* * *

The ledger lived inside the false bottom of her sewing box, and the sewing box lived under her cot, and her cot lived in a lean-to shared with two other nurses who believed she spent her evenings darning socks, which she did, ostentatiously, whenever anyone looked. It was a cheap exercise book, forty pages, purchased from a stationer in Verdun in another century—August, it must have been August—and she filled it in candle stub light with entries like these:

*Pvt. Anton Okafor, 2nd Colonial, admitted 14 Dec. Official roll lists him as A. Okafour, deceased. He is not deceased. He is complaining about the soup, which is proof of life if nothing else is.*

*Sapper Léon Brissac, age unknown, admits to nineteen. No regimental record exists; his colonel fell at the same engagement and half the company's papers burned. He has no name anywhere but here and in his own mouth. He says: tell them I was good with wires.*

*Twin brothers, Raoul and Henri Mesmin, 31st Line. Only Raoul appears in the casualty list. Henri died beside him and no officer thought to write him down separately, as though twins came as one item. I have written them both. They will not travel in pairs through the paperwork of this war if I can help it.*

She did not think of herself as brave. This was important to her. Brave women got noticed, and noticed women got sent away, and someone had to stay to hold the pen. She was, she told herself, a bystander. History was an enormous machine grinding somewhere to the west, all telegrams and generals, and she stood at its edge sweeping up what it dropped. Sweeping up was not participating. Anyone could sweep.

That the machine dropped human beings, and that she gathered them up and gave each one back its name—that seemed to her a modest trade. She asked nothing. She wanted nothing except to remain unremarkable until the war ended and she could carry the little book home and read it aloud in some ordinary room, and people would say, *how strange, why would officers omit names*, and she would say, *because the dead are heavy and paper is light,* and that would be that.

She was twenty-six years old and had not yet learned that history does not tolerate bystanders. It merely postpones introducing itself.

* * *

On the third of February the cold came down like a lid. The stove in Ward Three surrendered entirely and Sister Bernadette ordered every blanket, coat, and curtain requisitioned, and the men lay in their rows breathing white clouds, joking weakly about the comforts of the trenches, which were, they agreed, tropical by comparison.

"Delacroix." Bernadette paused at the foot of bed nine. "You've been on since noon."

"I'm fine, Sister."

"You're fine the way the ice is fine. Walk carefully." But she said it almost warmly, and moved on, and Wendell understood she had been promoted, in Bernadette's private taxonomy, from furniture to fixture. Fixtures were allowed to stay.

Okafor caught her sleeve as she passed. "The soup," he whispered. "Tell me honestly."

"The soup," Wendell said gravely, "is a war crime. I have reported it to The Hague. They are drafting a convention even now."

He grinned into his blanket. Nineteen years old, listed dead since December, grinning. She added a line to the ledger later that night: *A.O. joked today. Note for the record: the official file has him buried. Someone will weep at a grave that holds no one while the man lives to complain about soup. Let the file be wrong. Let this book be right.*

* * *

The candle stub was down to its last thumb-width when she heard the footsteps outside—wrong footsteps, unhurried, too regular for a stretcher party and too soft for an officer. She blew out the flame and sat in the dark listening to her own heartbeat do arithmetic.

The lean-to door opened on a wedge of starlight and a shape that resolved, eventually, into a man. Mud to the knees, a greatcoat that had been fine once, in a country where it didn't matter, and a leather satchel gripped against his chest the way a man holds something he has decided to die holding. Snow had gotten into his hair and half melted there.

He looked at her sitting straight-backed on her cot in the dark, and he did not apologize, which she noted. Most men apologized first and explained after. This one looked at her the way you look at an address you've walked a long way to find, checking it against something in your head.

"You're not what I expected," he said. His French was fluent but foreign, accented somewhere east of everywhere.

"It's late," Wendell said, "and this is the nurses' quarters, and I am armed with a darning needle."

"I believe the second part." He stayed in the doorway, snow dripping off him. "I'm looking for the one who writes."

The sentence went through the room and rearranged it. Wendell kept her face still, because she was excellent at being furniture even in the dark, but inside her something enormous quietly changed its weight from one foot to the other.

"I don't know what you mean," she said.

"No," he agreed. "You wouldn't. But I was told to ask anyway, and to watch the answer." He shifted the satchel to one hand and drew out an envelope—she saw it only as pale paper, nothing more, until he struck a match to see by, and the flame jumped, and the handwriting leapt off the envelope at her across four feet of frozen air.

She knew that hand. She knew the way the capital E leaned like a man walking into wind, the small flourish on the final r like a signature apologizing for itself. She had seen it on postcards from a summer town, on the flyleaf of a borrowed book of poems, on a note left on her table one morning in August that had said only *wait for me, I intend to be insufferable about this war and survive it.* Emile Vasseur's hand. Emile Vasseur, who had been dead for three months—officially, irrevocably, with a form filled out in triplicate and a line drawn neatly beneath.

The stranger watched her face do whatever it did. He nodded slowly, as though she'd answered a question correctly without speaking.

"He said you'd know it was real," the man said. "He said—" and here the stranger hesitated, and for the first time looked almost embarrassed, "—he said you'd know by the way he crosses his sevens. That you teased him about it once, in peacetime, and he never forgave you."

Wendell looked down at the envelope the matchlight offered her. There, in the middle of a date, a seven crossed with the defiant horizontal stroke of a man refusing to be mistaken for a one.

The candle lay cold in her hand. Outside, the river held its breath between the banks, white and patient, waiting the way winter waits—for everything to change.

"Sit down," she heard herself say. "And start from the beginning. And don't leave anything out, because I have all night, and apparently," she set the candle down with a hand that was almost steady, "I have always been the one who writes."
