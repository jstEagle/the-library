## 2. A Stranger at the Checkpoint

The frost came early that November, and with it a particular quality of silence that I had learned to distrust. Silence at the field hospital meant one of two things: either the front had gone quiet enough for men to die of their wounds in peace, or something was about to arrive that would make us long for the noise.

I was on my fourth hour of the small hours—the stretch between two and dawn when the ward belonged entirely to the nurses and the moaning—when Corporal Finch came through the flap with his collar up and his eyebrows doing something alarmed.

"Sister Delacroix. There's a man at the checkpoint."

"There's always a man at the checkpoint," I said, wrapping a fresh bandage around Private Okafor's forearm. "Sometimes several. It's why they built the checkpoint."

"Not like this one." Finch lowered his voice to the register he reserved for reporting broken equipment he'd personally broken. "Came out of the east, on foot. No papers worth the name. Mud to the knees. Carrying a satchel he won't let anyone touch."

"Medic?"

"He won't say what he is. He asked—" Finch hesitated, and I noticed then that his hands were unsteady on the lantern. "He asked for *the one who writes*."

My needle did not stop moving. I am proud of that, still. The stitch went in clean, the knot tied itself with the small mechanical competence that three years of war had drilled into my fingers, and only after all of that did I allow myself to look up.

"The one who writes," I repeated. "An odd request. Half this hospital writes. Letters home, requisition forms, complaints about the coffee."

"He wasn't asking for half the hospital, Sister. He said it like it was an address."

Private Okafor, mercifully sedated, murmured something about his mother's kitchen. I patted his wrist and told him the coffee there was certainly better, which was a lie, because his mother was French and therefore incapable of bad coffee, but lies told gently are a legitimate medical supply.

"Show me," I said.

* * *

The checkpoint was a lean-to of planks and sandbags strung with wire, positioned where the road met the frozen river plain, and it was staffed by sentries who had learned to be suspicious of weather itself. The stranger stood between them like a post someone had forgotten to plant properly—a tall man, or perhaps merely bent by walking, caked head to boots in the grey-brown mud of the eastern roads, which is a mud with its own particular despair in it.

Up close he was young. That surprised me. From Finch's description I had assembled someone ancient and mysterious, whereas this was a fellow of perhaps twenty-five with a week of beard and eyes that had the glassy patience of a man who had decided some time ago not to feel his feet.

"You asked for someone," the senior sentry was saying for what was clearly not the first time. "You'll tell me who, or you'll walk back where you came from, and God help you either way."

The stranger looked past him. At me, specifically. And something in my chest performed a maneuver I had not authorized.

"Sister," he said. His voice was rough as a file. "I was told to find the one who keeps the book. The names."

"I keep no book," I said, which was true in the way that most useful lies are true: technically, from certain angles, if you squinted.

He studied me with an awful, unhurried attention. Then he smiled—not warmly; more the smile of a man confirming a map against the terrain—and said, "You have ink on your left cuff."

I looked. I had ink on my left cuff. Three years of writing letters for men who could not write them themselves, and I had never once managed a shift without adorning myself like a printer's devil. It is difficult to be invisible when you have made a habit of wearing your crime on your sleeve. I noted this for future reference and then immediately resented noting it.

"Come inside," I said to the sentries' visible horror. "He'll freeze before your paperwork thaws, and then you can explain to the major how you lost a source of intelligence to the weather."

* * *

We sat in the supply tent because it was the only place with a stove and no wounded men to alarm. I gave him tea that was mostly hot water remembering better days, and he held the cup in both hands and stared into it as though scrying.

"Your name," I said.

"Tomas." Just that. No family name offered, and I had learned that a man who withholds his surname has usually been generous with it somewhere it cost him.

"Tomas. Where do you come from?"

"Across the river." He said it flatly, the way one says *from the moon*. "There are people there who remember being people. Some of them send things back. Letters. Proof of life. Proof of death." His jaw worked. "Proof of other things."

"And the satchel?"

His hand went to it—slung across his body now, worn leather, scarred at the corners—like a man touching a wound to confirm it hasn't reopened. "No."

"They will take it from you eventually. Better it arrives as a gift than as evidence."

"Sister." He set down the cup. For the first time he looked directly at me, and I understood that everything previous—the checkpoint, the tea, even the question of the satchel—had been prologue. "I don't think you understand why I'm here. So I'm going to do the thing I walked nine days to do, and then we'll both know."

He drew an envelope from inside his coat. Not from the satchel—from his coat, from the pocket nearest his heart, which is where men carry the things they cannot afford to lose and also the things they cannot afford to be found carrying, and often the same thing serves both purposes.

I took it. My hands were steady. I want that recorded somewhere: my hands were perfectly steady, and it meant nothing, because steadiness under fire is just fear that has learned manners.

The envelope was unmarked. Inside, a single sheet, folded twice.

And the handwriting—

I have read a great deal of handwriting in my life. Soldiers' letters, doctors' orders, the furious copperplate of commanding officers and the shy pencil-scratch of boys apologizing to their mothers for worries caused. But there is one handwriting I knew the way you know a voice calling your name from another room: the slight forward lean of it, the *e*s that never quite closed, the way the descenders dropped deep as if the words were reaching for something below the line. Emile Vasseur wrote the way he talked—too fast, too much, leaning toward you.

Emile Vasseur had been dead for three months. Officially. Beautifully, administratively dead, with a form and a number and a chaplain's sympathy delivered secondhand. I had received the news standing in this very hospital with a tray of dressings in my arms, and I had put the tray down, and I had finished my shift, because grief at the front is a luxury item and I could not afford the import duty.

The letter was short. Dated six weeks past. Written in that leaning, reaching hand.

*Sister mine,* it began—he had called me that since the summer we were both twenty and too clever for our own comfort—*if Tomas has found you, then the promise stands, and I am sorry for the manner of my resurrection. I know what they have written of me. I need you to bring me the names. All of them. Before the ice goes.*

There was no signature. There didn't need to be. There was a small ink blot at the bottom right corner where the pen had rested too long, exactly where his pen always rested too long, and I felt the ward tilt beneath me—the whole frozen, orderly world of the hospital, the ledgers and the rotas and my careful, careful distance—tilt like a plate at a badly balanced table.

Three months dead. Officially. And here was his *e*, unclosed, reaching down.

"Sister Delacroix," Tomas said quietly. "You've gone white as the river."

"I'm fine," I said, in the tone nurses use, which means *I am anything but, and I will deal with it after this patient is seen to.* Except the patient was me, and there was no rota for that.

I folded the letter along its existing creases. My fingers knew the motion. Somewhere in the summer before the war, they had folded his letters a hundred times, and muscles are disloyal that way; they keep everything.

"Who gave you this?" I asked.

"A friend." Tomas retrieved his tea and drank it finally, as if some internal matter had been resolved. "A friend who says you're the only person on either side of that river who keeps a true record. He says—" and here the man had the grace to look slightly embarrassed, as though passing along a compliment too large for the container— "he says you believe a name written down is a man who can't be erased."

Did he. Did Emile say that. In the summer, once, under a plum tree, I had said something like it, and he had laughed and called it my religion, and I had thrown a plum pit at him.

"That's a foolish belief," I said.

"Probably," Tomas agreed comfortably. "Most true ones are."

* * *

I walked back through the ward afterward, making my rounds with the letter pressed flat against my ribs inside my apron, where it sat like a coal. Sister Bernadette caught my elbow near the stove.

"You look like you've seen the ghost of someone," she observed. Bernadette is Flemish and believes in directness the way other people believe in saints.

"Worse," I said. "I may have seen the absence of one."

She frowned at this, decided it was English nonsense, and returned to her porridge distribution. I continued down the row of cot after cot after cot, and every man in them had a name, and I knew most of them, and the officers' lists knew almost none, and for three years I had believed—I had built my entire survival on believing—that keeping the ledger was enough. That witnessing from the shadows was a kind of safety. That I was a bystander with good penmanship.

The letter crackled faintly against my ribs with every step. *Bring me the names. Before the ice goes.*

At the little desk in the supply alcove, behind the stacked crates of carbolic, I took out the ledger itself—my hidden thing, my nightly religion, forty pages of small cramped columns of men the paperwork had swallowed—and laid it on the desk, and put my hand flat upon it as though taking an oath, which perhaps I was.

Then I heard footsteps, unhurried and confident, the particular cadence of authority approaching, and I slid the ledger under a blanket roll a full four seconds before Major Renwick's shadow fell across the alcove doorway.

"Sister Delacroix," he said pleasantly. "Working late, I see."

"Always, Major," I said, smiling with a face that had three years of practice.

And behind his shoulder, at the far end of the ward, Tomas stood watching me through the tent flap with an expression that said, quite clearly: *Now you understand what you are.*
