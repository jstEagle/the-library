## 4. The Enemy's Hand

Major Aldous Renwick had a theory about paperwork, and it was this: no one lies in ink who can lie in person. Men under interrogation would look you in the eye and swear to their mothers' maiden names with perfect sincerity, but put a pen in their hands and something in them grew honest, or at least careless. Ink was where people told the truth without meaning to.

It was why he read everything. Requisition forms, laundry manifests, the chaplain's complaints about the quality of communion wine. Especially the boring things. Liars rehearsed their interesting sentences; nobody rehearsed their boring ones, and so the truth leaked out through dull channels like water through bad masonry.

The signal had come to him on a Tuesday, folded inside a routine dispatch about rail schedules, which was precisely the sort of channel Renwick trusted most. A courier station forty versts west had logged an anomaly: correspondence moving eastward toward the river sector, addressed not to any unit or officer but to a description — *the one who writes, at the hospital by the frozen crossing*.

No name. No unit. Just an adjective and a location, traveling through a war like a coin passed hand to hand.

Renwick read it three times, then set it on the stove, watched the corners brown and curl, and fed it entirely to the fire. He had found early in his career that evidence you intended to act on was safer as ash than as file. Files could be subpoenaed. Ash could only be believed.

He poured himself tea that tasted of boiled tin and sat for a long while doing nothing at all, which was, in his experience, the most productive activity available to an intelligence officer. Somewhere out there, someone wanted a letter delivered to a writer. And in his sector, by the frozen river, there was exactly one hospital, and — he was nearly certain — exactly one person anyone would describe that way.

The nurse. Delacroix. The French name alone would have been enough to flag her in a lazier man's book, but Renwick prided himself on not being lazy, which meant he flagged her for better reasons than her name.

* * *

He began, as he always did, by watching rather than asking. Questions were loud things; they frightened the truth into hiding. Observation was quiet, and quiet suited him. He had a face that suited him too — grey-eyed, unremarkable, the kind of face orderlies forgot five minutes after saluting it. He considered this his single great inheritance from his father, along with a set of carving knives no one used and a fatal expectation of disappointment.

He arrived at the hospital two days later wearing his most apologetic expression, claiming an inspection of supply chains. The commanding physician, a harried Belgian named Dr. Feron whose mustache seemed to be attempting an escape from his face in two directions at once, waved him through with the gratitude of a man who hadn't slept in a week and didn't intend to start now.

"Inspect whatever you like," Feron said. "Inspect me. I'd stand up straighter if I could."

Renwick smiled the small smile he kept for civilians, which contained roughly the warmth of a well-polished door handle, and began his rounds.

The ward was long and low, its timber walls breathing woodsmoke and carbolic. Rows of cot frames. The particular silence of men trying to suffer quietly, which was louder than screaming if you knew how to listen. Snow ticked against the windows. Somewhere a kettle worked itself toward a whistle it would never reach before someone needed it for washing wounds.

And there, at the third cot from the stove, was Nurse Delacroix.

Renwick had reviewed her file, of course. Age twenty-eight. Volunteer, attached through some Red Cross channel or other. Competent — the word appeared in every evaluation, sometimes twice, as though her supervisors kept reaching for a more interesting adjective and failing. Unmarried. No dependents. Fluent in French, conversational in German and Russian, which was noted in her file with the same mild surprise one might note a cat that could fetch.

Fluent in four languages, posted voluntarily to a freezing river at the edge of the world. In Renwick's experience, women like that had either run from something or were running toward it, and both categories required watching.

She was changing a dressing when he came near, and he stopped to observe, pretending great interest in the ward's ventilation.

Her hands were what he noticed first. Steady. Not surgeon-steady, exactly — she wasn't performing, she was tending — but steady in the way of someone who had decided long ago what fear was worth and paid it off in full. She spoke to the soldier whose arm she was unwrapping in a low, unhurried voice, and though Renwick couldn't catch the words over the kettle, he caught the effect: the man's shoulders came down from around his ears. She might have been telling him the weather. She might have been telling him anything.

When she finished, she washed her hands in the basin, dried them on her apron, and turned to find Renwick watching her.

"Major." Her nod was correct. Her eyes were polite and gave away nothing whatsoever, which interested him very much.

"Nurse Delacroix." He introduced himself with the supply-chain fiction, and she listened with every appearance of attention. "Long posting for a volunteer," he said. "Most rotate home after six months."

"The wounded don't rotate," she said. "Someone has to stay."

A good answer. He wrote that down mentally, in the column he kept for good answers given slightly too quickly.

"You speak four languages," he said. "That must be useful here."

"It's useful everywhere," she said. "Languages are just people, Major, wearing different coats."

*People wearing different coats.* He turned the phrase over later that evening and could not decide whether it was charming or diagnostic. Spies thought that way. So did saints. The trouble with humanity, Renwick had found, was that the two categories shared handwriting.

* * *

Over the following week he built the picture the way one builds any picture: slowly, in poor light, with the constant suspicion that a stroke had gone in the wrong place.

He learned her routines. She took the night shifts more often than duty required — the night matron confirmed this with a shrug that suggested Delacroix's habits were her own affair, a shrug Renwick filed under *institutional blindness, chronic*. She was awake when the others slept. She moved through the dark wards without a lamp, knowing where every obstacle lived.

And twice, he saw her writing.

The first time, he assumed chart notes. Nurses wrote constantly — temperatures, pulses, dosages, the small arithmetic of keeping men alive until morning. But the second time, he came around the corner of the ward at an angle she hadn't expected, and there was something in the motion of her shoulders that snagged on him. Charts were written bent forward, businesslike, the body already halfway to the next task. She was curled over her page the way people write letters. Or diaries. Or ledgers.

By candlelight, past midnight, when no chart needed updating because no patient's condition changed faster than the cold.

He did not confront her. Confrontation was a tool for finishing investigations, and his had barely begun. Instead he did the quieter work. He checked the mail log — nothing incoming for her, which was itself mildly interesting; everyone got letters, even unwanted ones, even bills. Nothing going out either. A woman fluent in four languages who wrote by candlelight at midnight and sent nothing through official channels.

Where, then, did the pages go?

He interviewed the staff, gently, obliquely, in the tone of a man making conversation. The orderlies adored her — this was universal and, to Renwick, damning. Affection on that scale meant trust, and trust meant access, and access was the whole architecture of espionage. One young orderly, a boy named Piotr with a cowlick and a crushed cap, described her so earnestly that Renwick felt briefly like a man kicking a dog and had to remind himself of the courier log, the description, *the one who writes*, the war going on outside the window.

"She remembers everyone," Piotr said. "Even the ones the officers don't put in the reports. She says every man gets to have happened."

Every man gets to have happened. Renwick thanked him, walked out into the snow, and stood for a moment letting the cold explain his expression to no one.

Because here was the problem, and he turned it over all night like a stone looking for the worm underneath: what she was doing, if the boy was right, was not espionage. It was worse than espionage, from where Renwick stood. Espionage served a side. What she appeared to be doing served only the dead and the disappeared, and there was no predicting a person like that. You could threaten a patriot with her country. You could buy a cynic with his future. But a woman who believed every man got to have happened — what lever fit that handle? What did you hold over someone whose whole motive was that she couldn't bear a name to go missing?

An invisible woman, Renwick thought. Invisible was safe, invisible was harmless — until the day invisibility became the perfect cover, and then it was the most dangerous thing on the ward.

* * *

On the ninth day, the second report arrived.

Not the courier anomaly again — something smaller, meaner, closer. A censors' clerk at the rail depot had intercepted a letter fragment, half-burned, its contents indecipherable, but its provenance suggestive: paper stock matching field hospital requisitions, and handwriting the clerk described, with the confidence of a man paid by the page, as *foreign-trained, French school, slanted left*.

French school. Slanted left. Renwick sat in his quarters with the fragment — or rather with the clerk's careful transcription of the fact that a fragment existed, the original having been forwarded onward and lost, which was either incompetence or design, and Renwick no longer believed in incompetence at this altitude of the war.

Somewhere in his sector there was a left-slanting hand trained in France. He knew of exactly one.

He spread his notes on the table: the courier log, Delacroix's file, the orderly's testimony, the midnight candles, the absent mail. It made no shape yet. It was a handful of dark beads and no string. But beads, he reminded himself, had a way of finding their string the moment you stopped doubting they'd been strung at all.

He thought about her hands. Steady. About her answer — *the wounded don't rotate*. About *people wearing different coats*, and how easily a coat changes allegiance, and how the person underneath stays the same size the whole time.

Tomorrow, he decided, he would inspect the hospital again. Supply chains were endlessly faulty; no one would question a second visit, or a tenth. He would arrive without warning, at night this time, during the small hours when honest people slept and the one who writes did not.

He banked the stove, and as he did, his eye fell on the last line of his own notes, written earlier in a hand far less steady than hers:

*If she is receiving enemy correspondence, she is guilty. If she is merely recording what this army chooses to forget, she is more dangerous still.*

Renwick read it twice, found he could not improve on it, and went to bed.

Across the compound, through the wind, he could just see the hospital's east window, and the small stubborn point of candlelight that refused, night after night, to go out.
