## 5. Ice Over the River

The stranger—Tomas, he had finally admitted to being called, in the tone of a man surrendering a name the way other men surrendered weapons—did not leave after delivering the letter. He stayed. He slept two nights in the woodshed behind the laundry, wrapped in a horse blanket that smelled of carbolic and despair, and on the third morning he appeared at my elbow while I was changing a dressing, quiet as snowfall.

"He wants to see you," Tomas said.

I did not drop the forceps. I want that recorded somewhere, by someone, because it was the single most composed act of my life and no one witnessed it but Private Halloran, who was unconscious at the time and therefore an unreliable witness.

"Who does?" I said.

"You know who."

"Corporal Okafor," I said, nodding at the sleeping man beside me. "He does want to see me. He asked for morphine twice last night."

"Wendell." Tomas said my name as though testing whether it would bear weight. "Emile is alive. He is on the far bank. And he is asking for you."

* * *

Let me tell you what the river looked like that morning, because it matters, because everything afterward happened against that particular grey. The Narew—or whatever name the cartographers gave it; the soldiers called it simply the River, the way one calls a tumor the Growth—lay flat and white between the lines, a mile of ice with the war standing on both edges of it like two quarrelsome families across a dinner table. The frost smoke came off it in low ribbons. Somewhere under all that whiteness was water, moving, patient, indifferent to borders and to promises made in peacetime.

Alive. On the far bank. Declared enemy.

I finished Corporal Okafor's dressing with hands that belonged to somebody else—some competent woman who had never heard of Emile Vasseur—and then I walked Tomas out past the latrines to the willow scrub where nobody stood close enough to overhear, which was itself an incriminating act, and I said:

"Explain."

So he explained. In pieces, in soldier's ellipses, in the maddening grammar of men who have spent too long saying things only in whispers. Emile had been taken at the autumn engagement, not killed—the official record had preferred dead, dead being tidier than missing. He had been exchanged into another unit, or escaped into one, or something had happened involving a uniform and a border and choices that Tomas clearly considered none of my business, and now he stood on the wrong side of the river wearing the wrong color coat, and his own army had struck him from its rolls, and the other army regarded him with a suspicion so thorough it amounted to house arrest.

"He defected," I said flatly.

"He survived," Tomas said. "The rest is paperwork."

"The paperwork says enemy."

"The paperwork," said Tomas, "says a great many things. That is rather the point." He crouched, drew in the frozen mud with a stick: the river, a line, a small square on our side. "There are witnesses coming. A commission, after—all of this ends someday, however long 'someday' takes. When it comes, every name in your ledger will need verifying. By him. Before witnesses. Because he knew these men, Wendell. He served beside half of them before—" the stick paused over the river line "—before the geography got complicated. If he verifies them, they exist. Officially. Forever. If he doesn't—"

"They stay in my book," I said. "In my handwriting. One woman's diary against the entire machinery of two armies."

"Yes."

"And he wants the ledger brought to him. Across that." I pointed at the river with my chin, because my hands had begun, very quietly, to shake.

"There's a crossing place downriver. The ice is thick near the reeds. Men have done it."

"Men have also gone through it," I said. "We've pulled three of them out since November. They came up through a hole the size of a dinner plate and they were dead in four minutes. Four, Tomas. I counted, because counting is what I do instead of screaming."

He had the grace not to answer immediately. The wind moved through the willow wands, a dry whispering like pages turning.

"I can't," I said, and heard how small it sounded, and hated it. "You don't understand what you're asking. I am invisible here. Do you know what invisibility is worth in a war? It is worth everything. Nobody questions a nurse. Nobody reads a nurse's trunk. I write my little book at night and I fold it away in the morning and I go on being furniture, and furniture survives. If I take that ledger onto the ice—if anyone sees me walking toward the far bank carrying a book full of names, half of them names their own officers wanted forgotten—I stop being furniture. I become a story. And stories get told in court-martials."

"Wendell—"

"He'd be declared a spy. I'd be declared his accomplice. Renwick already watches me the way a cat watches a clock, waiting for it to do something unclocklike. And even setting all that aside—" my voice cracked, which infuriated me, so I pushed through it "—even if I wanted to, the ice, Tomas. The ice doesn't care about promises."

* * *

That afternoon I was very good at my job. I want to be honest about this: I hid inside it. I changed dressings and ladled soup and held the hand of a boy from Lyon who kept apologizing for fainting, and I told him fainting was the body's way of taking a sensible tea break, and he laughed, and for one whole hour I did not think about the river at all.

Then Sister Bernadette caught me in the supply tent, counting gauze I had already counted twice.

"You're sulking," she observed. Sister Bernadette was seventy if she was a day and had nursed through three wars she was technically too old for, on the grounds that wars rarely checked credentials.

"I am inventorying."

"You're sulking with arithmetic." She took the gauze out of my hands with the gentle tyranny of long practice. "The stranger in the woodshed. He's still there. Matron thinks he's a deserter. I think he's worse; deserters have somewhere to be." She looked at me over her spectacles, and her eyes were kind and terrible. "Whatever he's asked you, girl, the question isn't whether you're brave enough. You've been brave enough since the day you started that little book. The question is whether you're willing to be seen doing it."

"How do you—"

"A nurse who alphabetizes her patients' names when she thinks no one is watching," said Bernadette, "is a nurse who is writing something down. We notice. We don't say. That's the sisterhood." She pressed the gauze back into my arms. "Being invisible is comfortable right up until it isn't. Then it's just lonely, and late."

She left me alone with the gauze, which I proceeded to count a third time, getting a different number each attempt, which seemed cosmically unfair.

* * *

Night. Candle. Ledger.

I sat with it open on my knees in the linen closet that served as my office, and I read the names I had gathered since October like a miser counting coins that could purchase nothing yet. Ansel Brissac. Yves Tallinn. The Kowalczyk brothers, listed officially as "Kowalski (x2)," as though brothers were a bulk item. Little Halloran, asleep ten feet from me, whose actual surname was Ó hAlluráin and whose sergeant had found the spelling inconvenient.

If I crossed, and was caught, this book would be confiscated. Read. Interpreted. A list of names, some from both sides of the line—because I had never been able to bring myself to omit the enemy wounded who died in our ward, unnamed, unmourned except by me—that list in the hands of Major Renwick would look exactly like what he already suspected: espionage. Treason. Evidence.

And Emile. Emile alive, and asking for me, and I had spent three months grieving a man only to learn that grief had been misfiled, that the true emotion awaiting me was something with no clean name—a fury and relief braided together so tightly I couldn't tell which end to pull. He had let the world believe him dead. He had let *me* believe him dead. There would be explanations, and they might be good ones, and they might arrive too late to matter, because the ice between us was doing what ice does in February: thinning, quietly, from beneath.

I thought about the promise. Summer, the orchard, his hands shaking around a cider cup. *If I ever vanish, Wendell—not die, vanish—someone has to know who I actually was.* And I had said yes the way one says yes to hypotheticals, warmly, cheaply, certain it would never come due.

It had come due. Promises, it turns out, keep books of their own.

I closed the ledger. I held it against my chest and felt it there, small and dense and utterly inadequate, a brick made of paper.

"No," I said aloud to the linens. "Absolutely not. It's suicide with extra steps. It is a spectacularly stupid—"

The candle guttered. Outside, the wind came off the river and moaned through the eaves, and underneath the wind—so faint I might have invented it—came a sound I had heard every night for weeks without truly listening: the ice, settling, groaning, speaking its slow cold language to the dark. Speaking, perhaps, to me.

I thought: in six weeks, maybe eight, the thaw comes anyway. The crossing closes itself. Whatever window exists exists now, and closes forever, and I will spend the rest of my life being excellent invisible furniture in a room where everyone I ever loved was carried out feet first, and the ledger will rot in a trunk, and the names will die twice.

I thought: Renwick's office light was burning late again tonight.

I thought: four minutes. Dead in four minutes. I counted once. I could count again.

There was a soft knock at the linen closet door—Tomas, of course, appearing with his maddening snowfall quietness, holding out a folded paper without entering, as though he'd known I needed the choice delivered rather than the argument repeated. I took it. Not Emile's hand this time. Block capitals, careful, foreign:

**THE ICE AT THE REEDS HOLDS UNTIL THE MOON WANES. AFTER THAT IT DOES NOT PROMISE ANYTHING. NEITHER DO WE.**

Below it, smaller, in that handwriting I knew better than my own—the handwriting I had watched form love letters and grocery lists and one memorably bad poem about my eyebrows:

**Bring the book. Bring yourself. Nothing else matters. —E.V.**

I sat in the linen closet among other people's sheets, holding two kinds of paper, and understood with perfect clarity that I had already decided—that I had decided the moment Tomas said *alive*, and everything since had been my cowardice politely requesting a recount.

"All right," I whispered, to the ledger, to the linens, to the moon going slowly wan outside. "All right. But if I go through the ice, I am haunting every single officer in this army, alphabetically."

Tomorrow night, then. Moonset. The reeds. The crossing.

Which was when I blew out the candle, felt along the shelf for my coat, and discovered that Renwick's boots had stopped outside the linen closet door—and stayed there.
