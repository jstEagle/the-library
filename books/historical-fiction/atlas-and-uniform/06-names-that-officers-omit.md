## 6. Names That Officers Omit

The shelling began at dusk and did not stop until the sky had gone through all its moods—orange, bruise-purple, black—and settled on a color that had no name, which was fitting, because by morning nothing in the ward had a name either.

They came in on stretchers, on cart beds, on the shoulders of men who would themselves be on stretchers within the hour. Forty-one of them before midnight. Wendell stopped counting at sixty because counting had begun to feel like something she did to herself rather than for them.

"Pressure here," she said, pressing Corporal Ives's hands flat against a wound that wanted to bleed the boy out through a hole the size of a plum. "Both thumbs. Pretend you're trying to stop a runaway horse."

"I've never stopped a runaway horse, Nurse."

"Then pretend it's your supper and the horse is me."

That got a wet, astonished laugh out of him, and a wet, astonished laugh was worth more than morphine in a ward where the morphine had run out at eleven. Wendell moved down the line of cots with a lamp in one hand and a pencil behind her ear, and she did the thing she always did, the thing no one had ordered and no one had forbidden: she asked every man who could still speak to say his name, and she said it back to him, and she wrote it down.

"Name?"

"Oskar Brandt. Fourth Silesian." He was enemy, technically. He was also seventeen, and his hand was cold, and she held it anyway.

"Oskar Brandt," she repeated, and wrote it in the ledger that lived inside her coat, against her ribs, where it had warmed to the temperature of a living body.

By three in the morning the ward smelled of carbolic and snow and the particular iron sweetness that no one ever described in letters home. Wendell's feet had stopped being feet and become two blocks of wood she was operating by remote instruction. Sister Aagot, who was sixty years old and built like a Norwegian doorframe, appeared at her elbow with tea that was mostly hot water and mostly kindness.

"You've been writing all night," Aagot said. It was not a question. Aagot had known about the ledger since October and had said nothing, which in Aagot's language was a benediction.

"Someone has to."

"Someone has to sleep, too."

"After."

"You always say after."

"And I always mean it," Wendell said, and Aagot snorted and moved on, and Wendell wrote *Oskar Brandt, Fourth Silesian, wants his mother to know he did not cry*—because Oskar had told her that, urgently, twice—and turned the page.

* * *

Major Aldous Renwick arrived at seven o'clock looking like a man who had dressed for a funeral and found a massacre instead. His boots were clean. That was the first thing Wendell noticed, the way you notice a bird with the wrong coloring: his boots were clean, in a hospital where the mud had its own postal code.

"Situation report," he said, not to anyone in particular, which meant to whoever was lowest in the room. That was Wendell. It was usually Wendell.

"Sixty-one admissions since dusk," she said. "Nineteen dead. The rest are holding, more or less, the way a dam holds."

"And the paperwork?"

"Underway."

Renwick's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "Underway is the problem, Nurse Delacroix. The division wants consolidated figures. One page. Numbers, not—" he gestured at the ward with a hand that took in the cots, the blood, the men, the whole sodden human catastrophe— "not *narratives*."

"Names are not narratives, Major. Names are names."

"Names are delays." He said it mildly, the way a man remarks on the weather, and that mildness was the worst part. "The reporting clerk is three weeks behind. I've reviewed the system. It's inefficient. Individual entries for men who—" he consulted a paper— "who are largely going to die anyway. It doubles the clerk's work when the final tally comes through. Burn the individual admission records. Keep the tallies. That's an order."

The ward went very quiet, the way a forest goes quiet, all the small sounds stopping at once.

"You want us to burn the admission records," Wendell said. Her voice came out level. She was surprised by it. Inside her chest something had stood up and begun, very quietly, to burn.

"I want the reporting simplified. Yes."

"The records are the only proof some of these men were ever here. Their units will list them missing. Their families will list them dead. The admission book is the only place in the world where Private—where *anyone* is written down as a person who arrived and was tended and was seen."

Renwick looked at her for a long moment, and Wendell watched him do the thing she had seen officers do to her before: reclassify her. Not a nurse with a pencil. A problem with a pencil.

"You're tired," he said, almost kindly. "Everyone is tired. Sentiment is fatigue wearing a dress uniform. Burn the records, Sister. That is all."

He left. His boots were still clean when he went. Wendell stood in the middle of the ward with sixty-one men breathing around her—well, forty-two breathing now—and thought: *he said Sister. He doesn't even know my rank, or he doesn't care, and either way he has decided I am furniture that talks.*

The fire barrel in the corner was lit against the cold. It had been lit every night that winter. It was, Wendell thought, the most convenient fire in the history of convenient fires.

* * *

They burned the records at noon.

Not all at once—Renwick had sent a clerk, a pale young man named Feathering who kept apologizing with his eyebrows—and not without ceremony, because Wendell made sure there was ceremony. She stood at the barrel with the admission book open on her arm, and for each page she read the names aloud before she let Feathering feed it to the flames. All of them. Even the ones with ink smeared by blood. Even the enemy ones. Especially the enemy ones.

"Vasil Petrovich Koren. Arrived 0340. Shrapnel, left thigh. Sang to himself. Liked the tea."

"Nurse," Feathering whispered, "the Major said—"

"The Major said burn them. He said nothing about silence." She turned the page. "Henrik Dalgaard. Arrived 0500. Died 0515. His last word was *mor*, which is Danish for mother, in case anyone was going to guess it was something military."

The men who were well enough to listen had begun, without any coordination, to listen. Corporal Ives had propped himself up on his good elbow. Oskar Brandt, feverish, was mouthing his own name along with her like a prayer he was checking for accuracy. Somewhere down the row a man with bandaged eyes said, "Louder, nurse," and she did, and her voice cracked on the third page and she kept going anyway, because a cracked voice reading a name was still a voice reading a name.

Forty minutes. That was how long it took to read them all. Forty minutes against three weeks of a clerk's backlog, against one page of tallies, against a war that had decided these men were arithmetic.

When the last page went into the fire, Feathering was crying, which he tried to hide by saluting, and Wendell put her hand on his shoulder and said, "It's all right. Grief is just accuracy about people," and then she went to the latrine and was sick, and washed her face, and came back, and no one mentioned it, because that was the etiquette of the ward.

* * *

That night she sat with the candle and the ledger and wrote until her hand ached.

She wrote the names she had read aloud—those were safe, those were in her blood now—but she also went backward through the winter, reconstructing. She wrote the names of men whose admission slips had burned in earlier purges she hadn't understood were purges. She wrote the names Sister Aagot remembered, and the names the orderlies remembered, and the name of a French colonial soldier whose unit had never once written to ask after him, whom Wendell had tended in November and whose name—Amadou Sissoko, she had learned it from a letter in his pocket, from a sister who wrote about a mango tree—she copied three times because it felt like a name that had been dropped and needed picking up.

The candle guttered. Outside, the wind came off the river with a sound like a great door being tested on its hinges.

Wendell sat back and looked at what she had made. The ledger was no longer a ledger. It had become, somewhere in the last twenty-four hours, an argument. A case. A small, hand-bound indictment with her handwriting for a prosecutor's voice.

She had thought, all winter, that naming the unnamed was enough. That she could stand at the edge of history with her pencil and her candle and remain a bystander—*I am only recording, I am only witnessing, the river of events flows past me and I am a stone in its bank.* She had believed that the way you believe a floor is solid: without thinking, because thinking about it would be unbearable.

But Renwick had burned the records, and the burning had a logic to it, and the logic was that men could be subtracted. Not killed—subtracted. Killed was what happened to bodies. Subtracted was what happened to names, and subtraction required no shell, no surgeon, no grief. It required only a tidy mind and a clean pair of boots.

And she understood, sitting in the cold with the candle dying, that she was not a stone on the bank. She was in the river. She had been in the river since the first name she wrote, and the water was rising, and there was no edge to stand on that was not itself already flooding.

Emile's name was in this book. She had written it in the first week, in a hand that shook: *Emile Vasseur, listed dead, not believed.* And now somewhere across that groaning black river was a man with her friend's face and an enemy's uniform, and a stranger's satchel, and a promise she had made in peacetime when promises were easy and the ice was a thing you skated on.

She had told herself she would not cross. She had told Tomas—no, she hadn't told Tomas anything; she had told the stranger, the mud-caked man with the satchel she refused to open, that she was a nurse, not a courier, that the ice was murder, that she had sixty patients and a war to lose.

But that was before the fire barrel. That was before she watched a clerk feed the winter to the flames one page at a time while she read the names aloud like a eulogy no one had commissioned.

She looked at the ledger. Sixty-one names from last night alone. Two hundred and forty since November. She counted them the way you count the reasons a dam will hold, and then she counted them the way you count the reasons it won't, and the second number was the one that mattered.

"Not for Emile," she said aloud to the candle, testing the words, finding them true. "Not only for Emile."

The candle went out, as candles do when you need them to make a point. In the dark, the river spoke again—long, low, impatient, a door being tested—and Wendell Delacroix, furniture that talks, problem with a pencil, began to plan how a woman carries two hundred and forty names across a frozen river without dropping a single one.

She would need to leave before dawn. She would need to leave *tomorrow's* dawn, because the ice was still thick enough tonight, and by the night after it would not be.

She had one day. She put the ledger against her ribs, where it had learned the temperature of a living body, and lay down in her boots, and did not sleep at all.
