## 8. Before the Thaw

The ice on the river had begun to sing.

That was how Sister Bramble described it, standing at the ward window that afternoon with her sleeves rolled and her hands still red from the wash basin. "Singing," she repeated, as though reporting a symptom. "Low, like a whale with opinions. My grandmother said when the river starts talking, you'd best listen."

Wendell listened. Beneath the wind, beneath the groan of the hospital's failing stove, there it was: a long, wavering note that rose from the water under its grey lid of ice, the sound of something vast shifting its weight in its sleep. The thaw was three weeks off at most. Perhaps less. The days had grown longer by a cruel fifteen minutes each morning, and the sun now had heat enough in it to make the snow on the checkpoint roof weep by noon.

Three weeks. And Major Aldous Renwick had taken to walking past the ward twice a day at precisely irregular intervals, which was worse than if he'd come at regular ones. Regularity could be planned around. Renwick walked like a man dealing cards—here at ten past four, then not at all, then at dusk, then mid-morning—pausing at the door with his hands clasped behind him, surveying the beds with the mild, terrible patience of a man counting spoons.

Today he had counted Wendell.

"Sister Delacroix." He had stopped beside her while she changed a dressing, his voice pleasant as a winter sky. "You keep late hours, I understand."

"The wounded don't observe office hours, Major."

"No," he'd agreed pleasantly. "Neither, apparently, do you. There is candle wax on the lintel above your bunk. Blue wax. You burn a good deal of light up there for a woman who claims only to sleep."

He had smiled when he said it. That was the worst of it. Renwick never accused; he merely noticed, and let you do the accusing yourself in the small hours, which is where accusations grow largest.

* * *

By nightfall the ward was down to its sleeping quiet—the snorers, the moaners, one boy who talked to his mother in his fever and called her *ma chère* in an accent from Marseille so thick it made the orderly weep quietly into his tea. Wendell sat on her bunk with her boots on, which she had not done since her first week, because boots meant readiness and readiness meant admitting what she intended.

The ledger lay in her lap. It had grown fat over the winter, this school exercise book she'd bought in another life from a stationer who had smelled of lavender and complained about the price of ribbon. Forty-one names now. Forty-one men who had died in Ward C since November whose names appeared nowhere but here—not in the regimental returns, not in Renwick's tidy summaries, not in the burned pages of last week's mass casualty report, which Renwick had ordered destroyed because, as he put it, "the numbers were becoming untidy."

Untidy. As though forty-one men were a drawer of mismatched socks.

She had copied them faster than the fire could eat them, kneeling by the brazier with Corporal Anselm's bloodstained paybook open on her knee, and she had felt something in her chest turn from grief to iron somewhere around the name of Private Lucien Aubry, aged nineteen, who had wanted to be a beekeeper. She did not know why the beekeeping undid her particularly. Perhaps because it was so ordinary. Because wars took farmers and teachers and beekeepers and turned them into arithmetic, and someone—someone—had to write down *he kept bees, he was nineteen, he was afraid of the dark at the end.*

And Emile. Emile, alive on the far bank, wearing a uniform that would get him shot by either army depending on which direction he turned. Tomas's confession two nights ago had stripped the ground from under her—the letters partly forged, Renwick's bait, the whole satchel a trap designed to catch exactly the sort of woman who kept blue candle wax above her bunk—but one letter had been true. She knew it the way you know your own mother's footsteps on a stair. The way Emile had crossed his *t*s like little fences, leaning them left, always left, as though even his handwriting were trying to run somewhere.

*I am on the other side of the water,* the true letter said. *I know what I am now, in their books and ours. Come before the thaw or let me stay erased. But bring the names, Wendell. If I cannot be witnessed, let them be.*

Tomas had gone back across two nights ago, or claimed he had. She hadn't seen him leave. He had a gift for leaving that she supposed came from surviving.

Now she sat in the dark with her boots on and did the arithmetic of courage, which never comes out even.

If she crossed and returned before dawn, no one need know. Except Renwick noticed everything. The wax on the lintel; the hours; the fact that she had asked the quartermaster, three weeks ago, quite innocently, about conditions on the east road. Every innocent act of the past month was being re-sorted behind those pale eyes into evidence. She understood now that invisibility had never actually protected her. It had only postponed the moment when being unseen became indistinguishable from being guilty.

And the ice was singing.

"Grandmother also said," Sister Bramble remarked from the darkness, making Wendell jump half out of her boots, "that people who sit fully dressed on their bunks at nine o'clock are generally about to do something they'll want company for."

"Bramble—"

"I'm too old to cross a frozen river and too sensible to try, so don't ask. But I can hold the fort, miscount the laudanum if anyone checks, and swear you've been in the linen store having some kind of episode since seven." A pause. "Also there's a flask in my kit that has been waiting for an occasion. This appears to be one. Take it. Courage is easier warm."

Wendell found she was crying a little, which was inconvenient. "You could get into terrible trouble."

"My dear girl, I am sixty-one years old and I have survived three typhoid outbreaks and one marriage. Let the Major come at me. I'll confuse him with details about my feet." Bramble pressed the flask into her hand and, unexpectedly, held her face between her palms for a moment, her fingers cold and certain. "Forty-one names. I've seen you writing them. Do you think I didn't know? Go and make them count."

* * *

The checkpoint was unmanned at the change of watch—a gap of twenty minutes that Wendell had verified with the patience of a woman who had spent a year learning to move through hospitals unremarked. She went the long way, along the willow scrub where the snow lay drifted and blue in the starlight, the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, Emile's letter inside it, the flask bumping her hip like a small loyal dog.

At the bank, the river stopped her.

In daylight, from the window, it was a road of white. Up close, in the dark, it was an animal. The ice stretched away into blackness, patched and veined, and under it the water moved with a sound like breathing. Here and there the surface showed darker patches where the current had thinned it from below, and the singing was louder now—that long rising note, then a crack like a whip, sharp and sudden, that sent her heart into her throat and her feet three steps backward before she caught herself.

"You are a grown woman," she told the river conversationally, "and I am going to walk over you whether you like it or not."

The river declined to comment. Somewhere downstream, it laughed anyway.

She stepped onto the ice.

The first hundred yards were easy, hard black ice near the shore, thick as a church floor. Her breath hung around her head in clouds. She kept her weight low, the way Tomas had told her—*flat feet, small steps, like an old woman crossing a chapel*—and she thought, absurdly, of Emile teaching her to skate on the park lake in another century, holding both her hands, walking backward, telling her the secret was not to look at your feet. *Look where you're going, Wendell. Your feet will follow your eyes.* His *t*s leaning left even then.

Two hundred yards out, the ice began to talk back.

It wasn't cracking, exactly. It was more that the whole river had developed a voice and intended to use it—a deep, resonant groaning that traveled through her boot soles and up into her teeth, the sound of the water beneath flexing, testing, remembering that it was water. She stopped. The groan rose in pitch, a violin bow drawn across something enormous, and then—

A crack split the night like a rifle shot, directly ahead, and a seam raced across the surface from bank to bank, a black zipper opening in the world.

Wendell stood very still. Her legs informed her, politely but firmly, that they would like to go home now. Every sensible molecule in her body pointed back toward the bank, toward Bramble, toward the linen store alibi and the small safe invisible life she had built out of candle wax and careful hours. *Turn back*, said every nerve she owned. *You can write to him. You can send someone else. You can be sorry from a distance; everyone does it.*

But nobody else had the names. Nobody else had promised. And the promise had never really been about Emile—it had been about the beekeeper, and the boy calling for his mother, and every man who had become a number in Renwick's tidy columns. Names needed a witness with legs. Tonight, hers were the only ones available.

"If I drown," she informed the ledger through the oilcloth, "you had better float. You're the important one."

She stepped over the crack. On the far side the ice changed—snow-crusted, hiding its own opinion—and her right foot went through to the knee before she knew anything had happened. The cold closed on her leg like a jaw. She went down hard on her elbows, satchel skidding, and for one white instant she watched it slide away across the glass toward the dark seam ahead, and she thought, with perfect clarity: *not the names, please, not the names*—

Her left hand closed on the strap. The satchel stopped. The river sighed.

She hauled herself sideways, out of the hole, gasping, her leg already going wooden with cold, and lay flat on the ice like a starfish because flat spreads the weight, everyone knows that, everyone who has ever lain on a frozen river at midnight knows that. The water in her boot had passed beyond pain into a kind of furious numbness. She began to laugh—quietly, helplessly, lying spread-eagled on a freezing river with a flask of Sister Bramble's courage leaking warmth somewhere against her ribs.

"All right," she said to the ice, to the stars, to the war, to the whole ridiculous arrangement of the world. "All right. Point of no return. I've heard of you."

She got up. Small steps, flat feet, eyes forward. Look where you're going and your feet will follow.

The far bank came on slowly, a black line of pines resolving out of the dark, and with every yard the singing grew quieter behind her and the trees grew taller ahead, until at last her boot crunched onto snow that did not move, and she stood on enemy ground with forty-one names against her heart and one of them, in a manner of speaking, waiting for her among the pines.

There was a lantern signal, three slow blinks, exactly where Tomas had said it would be.

Wendell took a breath that shook only a little, and answered it.
