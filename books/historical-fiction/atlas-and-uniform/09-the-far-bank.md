## 9. The Far Bank

I had learned to measure hope the way one measures frost: by how much of it accumulated overnight, and how easily it shattered underfoot.

For three months I had lived in a dugout on the eastern bank of the river, among men whose language I spoke badly and whose cause I understood even worse. Defector, they called me. Traitor, said the men I'd left. Both were true, and neither was, and somewhere between those two words was the thing I actually was, which had no name yet—which was precisely why I had come to believe I did not deserve one.

My name, you understand, had been a burden long before it became a crime.

*Vasseur.* In peacetime it had opened doors I never asked to walk through. My family's name sat above a bank, above a factory, above a marble plaque in a church where none of us prayed. When the war came, it bought my commission, my boots that fit, my leave when others' leave was denied. I used to tell Wendell, that last summer, laughing in a way that wasn't entirely laughter: "If I die out there, they'll list me as *Lieutenant Vasseur* and no one will know I'm the fool who once fell asleep in her garden and dreamed he was a beetle."

And she had said—she always said things plainly, as if hanging laundry—"Then I'll make sure someone knows about the beetle."

I thought of that often on the far bank, in the dark, listening to the ice groan like an old man descending stairs. I had sent letters through Tomas with a madman's instructions: find the one who writes. Tell her nothing except what she needs to come. I had convinced myself she would not come, actually. That was the coward's arithmetic of it—if she didn't come, then I hadn't truly asked her to cross a frozen river for a man who had made himself into no one.

The night she arrived, I was mending a sock. There is no dignified way to receive your resurrection; I would like to report that I stood and saluted, or wept nobly, or said something worth remembering. In fact I dropped the sock, the needle, and most of my composure, and when I saw her face—wind-scoured, furious, radiant—I heard myself say:

"You're wearing my scarf."

She was. The grey one, mine from another life, wound twice around her neck.

"Hello to you too," said Wendell Delacroix, in the exact tone she'd used when I'd arrived late to her father's library in 1913. "You're alive."

"Technically—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. Her glove was soaked through at the cuff. Her lips were pale. She had crossed the ice at night, alone, because I had asked a stranger to ask her to. "Don't *technically* me, Emile Vasseur. You do not get to be clever at me tonight. Sit down before you fall down."

I sat. It is possible I had already been sitting. The distinction felt important to her, so I let her have it.

* * *

We talked until the candle guttered—no. That is a lie, and this account deserves better than lies. We talked until *I* guttered. She talked; I leaked. I told her about the winter I'd crossed over, about the officer I'd been ordered to become, about the morning I'd looked down at my own signature on a requisition that would send forty men into a ravine and realized I could no longer say which side of the paper I belonged on.

"They'll hang me if I go back," I said. "They should, maybe. And here—" I gestured at the dugout, the frost-furred beams, the distant snoring of men who tolerated me without trusting me, "—here I am a curiosity. A tool. They use my languages, my maps, my memory of the other bank's roads. Nobody here asks who I am, because the answer embarrasses everyone." I heard my voice go flat and strange. "Do you know what I concluded, lying awake? That the record is right. That Emile Vasseur died three months ago, officially, and ought to stay dead. The name did enough harm while it lived. Let them have the uniform. Let the ledger of the world close over my head like ice."

Wendell listened to all of this the way she listened to everything—with the terrible patience of a woman who has spent years writing down exactly what people say.

Then she opened her satchel.

"I carried something across the river tonight," she said. "It nearly took my arm off doing it. The ice cracked under me halfway, Emile. I lay flat on my stomach on a frozen river in the dark, hugging a bag of names, and I want you to appreciate that I hate being cold more than I hate almost anything." She drew out a book—not a book, a ledger, swollen with loose pages, its cover stained, its corners soft as cloth from handling. "So before you finish telling me you don't exist, you're going to listen."

"Listen to what?"

"To them." She set the candle between us and opened the ledger across both our knees, the way one opens a hymnal. "To the ones the officers left out."

* * *

"Piotr Nowak, private, second company," she read. Her voice in the cold was low and even, a hearth-voice. "Nineteen years old. Kept a harmonica in his boot and played it with his gloves on. No one came when they buried him, so I stood witness. He was afraid of the dark and told me so without shame, which is braver than anything I have managed."

"That isn't—" My throat closed. "That isn't my name."

"No," she agreed. "It's the first of two hundred and six. Shall I continue?"

"Two hundred—"

"Henrik Dalgaard, corporal. Wrote letters home for the whole ward because half of them couldn't spell and the other half couldn't admit it. His last letter was to a sister. I copied it into my own book before the official copy went astray, because things go astray here, Emile. Things go astray constantly, and conveniently, and no one is ever quite responsible."

She read on. She read the boy who lied about his age and the old sergeant who never corrected him. The man who sang to horses. The one whose real name nobody ever learned—he had arrived unconscious and left the same way—and whom she had entered anyway, with a description, a regiment, the small human facts: gap in his teeth, laughed in his sleep, someone had loved him once, clearly.

Somewhere in the middle of it I stopped trying to interrupt. Somewhere after that I stopped breathing properly. The candle burned down and she lit another without pausing, and the dugout filled with names the way a room fills with smoke, inescapably, until I was breathing them.

"Wendell." I finally found my voice, and it did not sound like mine. "Why are you reading these to *me*?"

"Because you asked me to make sure someone knew who you truly were." She closed the ledger gently on her finger, keeping her place. "And this is who you are. Not the bank. Not the factory. Not the commission or the uniform or whatever the word is for what you did—you crossed a line, yes, and I'm not going to pretend I understand all of why, and you can explain the rest tomorrow. But this—" she tapped the page, "—this is a man who kept every promise he ever made to me, including the ridiculous ones. Including the beetle. A man who, when I told him names mattered, believed me. You think your name deserves erasure? Your name is in this book, Emile. Not as an entry. As the reason there's a book."

"The book would exist without me."

"It would," she said. "But it wouldn't be *here*. On this bank. In front of you. Because some fool wrote asking for 'the one who writes,' and I came, because—" and here, for the first time all night, her voice wavered, just slightly, like ice bearing weight— "because I have spent three years watching men disappear into paperwork and telling myself that writing them down was enough, that witnessing from a safe distance was the brave and sufficient thing. Then a stranger handed me a letter in your handwriting and I discovered that my safe distance was a story I told myself. So no, Emile. I'm not going to stand here and let you erase yourself. I walked across a freezing river specifically to prevent that. Do you know how much I hate being cold?"

"You've mentioned."

"Twice. I'll mention it again." She put the ledger in my hands.

It was heavier than any book has a right to be. I held it and understood that I was holding two hundred and six arguments against my own disappearance.

* * *

Near dawn she slept—finally, furiously, wrapped in two blankets and still shivering—and I sat with the ledger open on my knees and read it myself, every page, including the entries in her smaller hand: dates, wounds, jokes soldiers told her, a recipe one man dictated for his mother's stew ("in case," he'd said, "the letter doesn't make it but the stew does"). At the back, blank pages waited. She had left room. Even now, she was leaving room.

When the light came up grey through the dugout's vent, I found the ink—a German pot, the nib scratched but serviceable—and I turned to the final written page, beneath the last name, and I wrote, in a hand I hardly recognized as mine because it shook:

*Received and witnessed. Every name herein is true, and I am alive to say so.*

Below it, I signed. *Emile Vasseur.* The full name, unabbreviated, unashamed. It looked less like a brand than I remembered. It looked, in fact, like something that could be carried.

"What are you doing?" Wendell had woken without my noticing. She watched me from the blankets, eyes narrow with exhaustion and suspicion in equal parts.

"Sealing the record," I said. "It needs two witnesses. That was always the trouble with history—one party writes it and the other gets erased." I held out the pen. "Your turn. And then I need to tell you something before you hear it from anyone else. Tomas's letters—the ones he gave you—some of them weren't mine. Renwick had them made. It was bait, Wendell. He's not finished, and I don't think he stopped following you at the river."

She sat up slowly, pulling my scarf tighter, and looked at me the way she must have looked at the ice midway across: measuring exactly how much of her was willing to break.

"Then we'd best start walking," she said. "I have two hundred and six names, and I intend to read every one of them aloud—to the right people, this time."
