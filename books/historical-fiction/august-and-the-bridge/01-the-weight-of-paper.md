## 1. The Weight of Paper

The coastline took four hours, and Elodie Nakamura did not hurry it.

He had learned from Master Aurel that a coast was the most honest thing a map could hold—more honest than mountains, which exaggerated themselves; more honest than rivers, which changed their minds with every season. A coast was where the world admitted what it was. Land ended. Water began. There was no diplomacy in it.

So he bent over the drafting table in the grey light before dawn and let the pen do its slow, exacting work, the nib whispering along the vellum like something alive. Outside, Vellamare was waking in its ordinary way—the first gulls arguing over the fish-market refuse, the harbour bells marking the change of watch, the smell of coal smoke and brine seeping under the workshop door. Inside, there was only ink, and the memory of a dead man's hands.

*Hold the pen as if you are holding a bird,* Aurel used to say. *Firm enough that it cannot escape. Gentle enough that nothing breaks.*

Elodie held the pen like a bird. He drew the eastern shore of the Gulf of Serane in a single unbroken line, because Aurel had drawn all his coastlines that way—one breath, one stroke, no lifting of the pen between the headlands—and a coastline done in pieces was, to his master's way of thinking, a lie told in instalments.

The lamp guttered once. He trimmed the wick without looking up.

When the final curve closed against itself at the southern point—a small hook of rock called the Gull's Tooth, where the deep-water channels ran close enough to shore that a hull could slip in and out on a dark tide—he set down the pen and sat back, and his shoulders ached in the particular way they always ached after a finished plate, as though his body only now remembered it had been holding its breath for hours.

There. The last coastline. Forty-one plates, and the forty-first was done.

Except that *done* was a word with too many meanings, and Elodie had spent three months learning all of them.

He stood, stiffly, and went to the window. The workshop occupied the top floor of a narrow house on Rope Walk, in the quarter that clung to the northern bank of the harbour mouth like a barnacle to a hull. From here he could see the bridge—the old iron bridge, black with a century of salt and soot, spanning the water between the workshop quarter and the city proper. It looked, in the pre-dawn murk, like a spine of a great drowned animal, arched out of the sea to connect two worlds that had long ago stopped pretending to like each other. On the far side rose the city: the magistrate's hall with its copper dome gone green, the customs house, the respectable streets where respectable people lived who would not cross the bridge after nightfall for any reason short of fire.

Elodie crossed it twice a day, most days. He knew it the way he knew his own hands—knew the loose rivet on the third span, the way the iron sang in a west wind, how the planking dipped just slightly at the midpoint as though the bridge itself grew tired halfway across.

Everything, these days, he measured by whether it could be carried across that bridge.

It had become a strange new instinct, this arithmetic of weight. His master's burnisher: two pounds, brass, irreplaceable. The box of Chinese ink sticks: one pound, and worth more than the whole building. The atlas—

He turned back to the table.

The atlas lay open before him, and he made himself look at it plainly, the way one looks at a debt. Forty-one plates of the finest cartographic work Vellamare had seen in a generation, and beneath the beauty of it, folded into the margins in his master's cramped cipher, something else entirely. But that was for daylight. That was for later. For now, in this last private hour before the city demanded things of him, he allowed himself simply to see the work as work—to feel the plain pride of it, which was the only kind of pride Master Aurel had ever permitted.

*Any fool can make a thing impressive,* Aurel said. *A craftsman makes a thing true.*

The coastline was true. He checked it against the field notes one more time anyway—the little oilskin notebooks in Aurel's hand, filled with soundings taken from hired fishing boats, bearings shot from clifftops, depths plumbed at slack tide by a man in his sixties who should have been retired twenty years. The Gull's Tooth matched. The shoal marks matched. The depth figures, written in the neat descending numerals that apprentices in three countries had tried and failed to imitate, matched.

Elodie cleaned the pen. He dried the nib with linen, capped the ink, and rubbed the last traces from under his fingernails—which, he noted with the detached observation of exhaustion, were permanently stained now, the right hand especially, the brown-black shadow of iron-gall ink worked so deep into the quick that no amount of pumice or lye would ever fully lift it. Marta next door said his hands looked like a printer's. His mother, had she lived to see them, would have said they looked like his father's, though his father had been a cooper and stained with oak tannin instead. Stained hands ran in found families too, he supposed. Aurel had taken him in at eleven, an orphan with a foreign name and nowhere else to go, and had never once—not once in nine years—made him feel that the taking-in was charity rather than arrangement. *You will earn your place,* the old man had said on the first day, *and I will earn yours. That is the whole of our contract.* Then he had put a graver in the boy's hand and shown him how to cut a map roller, and that had been that.

Nine years. Three months since the fall.

They said he had fallen. That was the official phrase, repeated in the fish-market and the customs queue and the pews of the harbour chapel: *the old mapmaker fell.* Down the stair from this very room, on a wet morning, a misstep, an accident of age and slick stone. The coroner—a brisk man with ink-stained fingers of a different sort—had signed the paper without troubling to climb the stairs himself. Accidental death. Case closed. The city had moved on with the smooth efficiency of water closing over a stone.

And perhaps it was true. Wet mornings were dangerous. Old men fell. Elodie had told himself both of those things many times, usually at two in the morning, staring at the ceiling of the room below this one where Aurel had slept for thirty years and now would never sleep again.

But grief is a poor liar's audience. Grief checks everything.

He blew out the lamp. Grey daylight was filling the room now, honest light, the kind that showed flaws. It showed the workshop as it was: the long trestle table scarred by thirty years of blades and compasses; the wall of shallow drawers, each labelled in Aurel's hand—*SOUNDS / SERANE COAST / TIDES W. / TIDES E.*—holding a decade of the master's labour; the great press in the corner; the rollers wrapped in oiled cloth; and everywhere, everywhere, paper. Maps in every state of being—blank vellum, sketched vellum, inked vellum, printed sheets drying on lines strung across the ceiling like laundry. A workshop drowning gently in its own product.

And here Elodie performed the ritual he had performed every morning for ninety-odd days now, the ritual that had no name because naming it would make it real.

He took the satchel from its hook.

It was a good satchel, waxed canvas with a leather strap, bought by Aurel in some port city or other years before Elodie existed to be carried on anyone's back. Empty, it weighed almost nothing. Into it, in strict order of precedence, went: the atlas plates, wrapped in oilskin, forty-one leaves of vellum that together weighed less than four pounds and were worth—what? More than money could say, and precisely that much trouble. The field notebooks. The cipher key, a scrap no larger than his palm, which he kept sewn into the satchel's lining. Aurel's letters, eleven of them, tied with string.

Four pounds. Perhaps five with everything. Five pounds, and it contained a dead man's nine years of obsession, an apprentice's three months of devotion, and—if the margins meant what Elodie feared they meant—engether enough truth to hang men.

Five pounds. And yet some mornings it felt as if the thing weighed as much as the bridge itself.

He did not pack it now. He only lifted it, tested the strap, hung it back on the hook by the door—by the door, always by the door, positioned so that a man leaving in haste need take only one step and one motion. Then he allowed himself the day's first honest smile, thin as it was, because the coastline was finished and the finishing of things deserved acknowledgment even when the acknowledging had to be done alone, quietly, by lamplight, by an apprentice whom the city had never bothered to learn the name of.

*Elodie Nakamura.* Half Japanese through his mother, half nothing-knowable through his father, wholly invisible as far as officialdom was concerned. He had been a shadow in this workshop for nine years, and shadows, he had learned, had certain advantages. No one watched a shadow. No one asked a shadow for papers. No one counted a shadow among a household's souls when the census came.

He had thought, until recently, that invisibility was safety.

Now he understood it might also mean that when the trouble came—as he was increasingly certain it would come—no one would notice it had arrived until it had already passed through him and taken everything.

* * *

The morning assembled itself around him in its usual pieces. Marta knocked at seven with bread and news in equal measure, and left both on the doorstep without quite meeting his eye—a new habit of hers, or perhaps not hers; perhaps it was the whole street's habit lately, and she was simply the closest door. Her two boys waved at him from the dyer's yard below, the younger one extravagantly, the elder with a furtive glance up and down Rope Walk first, as if waving at Elodie were an act that required reconnaissance.

That was new, too. Or new-ish. New the way weather turns.

By eight he had washed, eaten standing at the counter, and covered the finished plate with clean rag paper to protect it. By half past he had begun the day's legitimate work—the commission maps, the harbour survey for the Pilots' Guild, the dull honest jobs that kept the workshop's face presentable while the other work hid beneath the floorboards of attention. He worked steadily. Steadiness was a kind of prayer. Steadiness was what Aurel had left him instead of money.

At noon the light through the south window turned gold, and Elodie unlocked the deep drawer.

Not the hidden drawer—anyone could find a hidden drawer eventually; Aurel had taught him that locks and concealment were courtesies to the impatient, delays rather than protections. No, the deep drawer, the bottom one of the tall case, the one that held the map rollers. He slid it out, lifted the topmost rollers aside, and drew out the one that was heavier than it should have been.

Inside its hollow core, rolled tight, lay the marginalia pages.

He carried them to the window and unrolled the first of them, and there it all was again, in his dead master's hand: the routes. Not the published channels, not the safe roads of commerce, but the other roads—the night roads. Coves with depth-soundings precise enough for a hull to run cargo at low water. Dates. Initials. Sums. A web of small, patient annotations spun across five years of margins, innocent as spider silk, and just as purposeful.

Master Aurel, celebrated cartographer, pillar of the Guild, had spent the last years of his life documenting the smuggling trade of Vellamare with the same rigour he brought to coastlines—and had died with the work unfinished, and the work named names.

Elodie stood in the gold light with the pages in his hands and felt the old argument resume inside him, the one that never reached a verdict. *Burn it*, said the voice of survival, which sounded, increasingly, like his own. *Burn the margins, keep the maps, finish the beautiful harmless atlas, and live.*

*Finish it*, said the other voice, which sounded exactly like Aurel, down to the dry patience in it. *You will earn your place, and I will earn yours. That is the whole of our contract.*

He was still holding the pages when the knock came at the street door below—not Marta's knock, which had a rhythm to it, a domestic informality, but three slow raps of someone accustomed to having doors opened for him.

Elodie's hands went still. Below, he heard the younger Marta boy stop mid-shout in the yard. The whole street seemed to hold its breath for the length of one wave.

Three raps again. Patient. Certain of the answer.

Elodie slid the marginalia back into the roller, the roller back into the drawer, and put his hand flat on the banister to steady himself as he started down the stairs—the same stairs, he could not help noticing, the same worn treads and the same iron rail, where an old man had supposedly slipped on a wet morning three months ago.

The letter, when he took it through the cracked door, was ordinary in every respect: cheap paper, a courier's boy already ambling away, his name written on the front in a clerk's indifferent hand.

Only the seal, when he turned it over, was not ordinary at all.

Pressed deep into the red wax, crisp and unmistakable, was the crest of the magistrate of Vellamare.
