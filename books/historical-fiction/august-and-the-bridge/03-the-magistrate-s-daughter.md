## 3. The Magistrate's Daughter

Sera Voss had been auditing her father's ledgers since she was eleven years old, and in all those years she had learned one truth above the others: that numbers, like people, lied most convincingly when they were trying very hard to be believed.

She sat now in the magistrate's antechamber with a leather folio across her knees, waiting for Clerk Hedstrom to finish stamping the morning's papers. Through the window behind her, Vellamare's harbor spread itself out in the grey-gold light of early spring — fishing boats coming in with the tide, gulls wheeling over the fish market, and beyond it all, on the far side of the water, the low crooked roofs of the workshop quarter huddled against the hillside like sheep afraid of weather. The old iron bridge crossed the harbor between the two worlds, its lattice of riveted steel dark against the bright water. She had crossed it perhaps four hundred times in her life and had never once thought about it as anything other than the way between here and there.

"Sera." Her father's voice from the doorway of his study. Magistrate Edvard Voss was not a large man, but he filled rooms the way cold fills a cellar — quietly, thoroughly, without asking permission. "You have the inventory schedule?"

"The surveyor's office confirmed yesterday. The workshop assessments for the harbor district begin this morning." She rose and smoothed her skirt. "I've prepared the forms."

He took the folio from her hands and turned its pages with his thumb, the way he turned everything — slowly, deliberately, missing nothing. He had taught her to do exactly this. *Read the document twice before you trust it once.* It was the first lesson of her life, and she sometimes suspected it would be the last thing left of him when everything else was gone.

"You'll take Hedstrom," he said. "Not alone."

"Father, I've conducted forty-one of these assessments—"

"Forty-two." He closed the folio and handed it back. His eyes went briefly to the window, toward the bridge, toward the quarter beyond it, and something passed across his face — not worry, exactly. Calculation. "The Nakamura workshop first. Master Aurel's place. There are... irregularities in the customs records touching that address. Nothing you need trouble yourself with. Count what is there, record what is counted, and bring the figures back by evening."

"And if I find something worth troubling myself with?"

The look he gave her then lasted half a second too long. "Then you will bring it to me," he said, "before you write it anywhere."

* * *

The bridge sang under their boots.

That was the thing about the iron crossing that no one who lived on the city side ever mentioned: it hummed. Low and constant, a note struck somewhere in the trusses and held there for years, so that Sera felt it in her teeth before they were halfway across. Below, the harbor moved in long green swells, and the fishing boats came through the arches with their sails slack, and the whole structure shivered faintly at every footfall, alive, patient, bearing weight.

Clerk Hedstrom walked beside her carrying the document case, complaining about the damp, about his boots, about the smell of the dye-works that reached even here. He was a narrow man with ink-stained cuffs and the particular pallor of someone who had spent thirty years indoors being important to no one.

"Master Aurel's death, they say it was an accident," he offered, as though continuing a conversation they hadn't been having. "Fell down his own stair. Terrible thing. Old men and stairs."

"So the coroner ruled."

"Ruled is ruled," said Hedstrom comfortably. "Your father says count and record. Best we count and record."

Sera said nothing. But she marked, in the small private ledger she kept inside her own head, that Hedstrom knew the contents of her assignment before she had opened the folio, and that he spoke of Aurel Nakamura's death with the ease of a man reciting lines he had rehearsed.

The workshop quarter received them the way it received all officials: with shutters closing. Not slammed — Vellamare's poor had learned that slamming invited attention — but drawn, softly, one after another along the lane, so that by the time Sera reached the mapmaker's door, the street had the feeling of a held breath. A dyer's shop exhaled steam and mordant-smelling air into the alley; through its window Sera glimpsed a broad woman with forearms stained blue to the elbow, watching them with an expression that was not hostile but was certainly not welcoming. Somewhere a child was counting in a language Sera didn't know, and stopped mid-number when their footsteps passed.

Hedstrom knocked with the brass head of his cane, three official raps.

For a long moment, nothing. Then footsteps, quick and light, and the door opened.

The young man standing in the doorway was perhaps twenty. He had his master's surname and nothing else of him — where the dead mapmaker had been round and silver-bearded and slow-moving as deep water, this one was lean and dark-haired, with the watchful stillness of a bird that has decided not to fly away only because flying would confirm it should. His eyes went from Hedstrom's cane to Sera's folio to the seal on the document case, cataloguing each in the time it took most people to notice any of them.

"Apprentice Nakamura?" Hedstrom produced his papers. "Clerk Hedstrom, office of the magistrate. This is the magistrate's daughter, Mistress Sera Voss. We're here to conduct a routine inventory of the premises."

"Routine," the young man repeated. The word came out flat as a coin placed on a counter. Then he stepped back and opened the door wider. "You'll want light. Come through to the workroom."

* * *

Sera had seen workshops before. She had inventoried chandlers and rope-walks, cooperages and the great sail-loft by the breakwater, and she believed she understood what a room made for working looked like: benches scarred and cluttered, tools in drifts, the honest chaos of labor.

Aurel Nakamura's workroom was none of these things, and neither was the boy who worked in it.

The room was dim and smelled of size and linseed and something faintly mineral, like rain on slate. Every surface had a purpose and performed it. The drawing boards stood in a rack by the north-facing windows because, Sera realized after a moment's thought, north light doesn't move — it is the same at nine and at noon and at four, and a man who draws coastlines cannot afford shadows that wander. The inks sat in a locked cabinet, labeled not by color but by date of grinding. Along one wall, map rollers stood upright in numbered slots like soldiers or organ pipes, each wrapped in linen.

And on the great table under the window lay the atlas.

It was open — which struck her as wrong immediately, because no craftsman leaves an open work exposed — and covered hastily, she guessed, with the linen cloth now folded back at one corner. What showed beneath was a single chart, and Sera, who could not draw a straight line without a rule and whose sense of geography ended reliably at the harbor mouth, felt something in her chest go quiet and attentive, the way a room goes quiet when someone begins to play an instrument well.

The coastline ran across the vellum in a line so fine it seemed less drawn than grown, inked with a steadiness that did not waver from the paper's edge to its center. Depth soundings clustered off the headlands like seeds. Current arrows curved with a grace that suggested the draftsman had not merely recorded the water but understood it, loved it, argued with it. In the corner, a compass rose bloomed in eight points of gold leaf, and around its rim ran lettering so small and even that Sera had to lean close to read it, and when she leaned close she caught the smell of the ink — fresh, hours old at most.

"This plate is unfinished," she said.

The apprentice — Elodie, the door registry would say, though everyone in the quarter called him the Japanese boy behind his back and nothing at all to his face — watched her with those careful eyes. "It is nearly finished. The final coastline. My master began it. I am completing it according to his notes."

"According to his notes." Sera straightened. "May I?"

"It's your inventory," he said, and stepped aside, and something in the way he said it told her he had already calculated what she might find and how much of it mattered.

Hedstrom, meanwhile, had begun the true business of such visits: opening drawers, weighing rolls of vellum, running his finger along shelf edges to collect dust as evidence of hidden stock. He worked with the thoroughness of a man who had done this two hundred times and found something twice. Sera let him. She had her own method, and it was slower, and it was hers.

She examined the chart.

An apprentice completing a master's work copies. That was how it went in every trade — the hand follows the model until the model becomes instinct, and even the finest apprentices leave fingerprints: a heavier stroke here, a hesitation there, the tremor of a wrist still learning to trust itself. Sera knew nothing of cartography, but she knew documents, and documents were simply drawings made of words, and she had spent ten years learning to tell a forged signature from a tired one.

She looked for the seams.

There were none.

Where the master's hand ended and the apprentice's began, she could not find the join. The line of the coast ran unbroken in weight, in confidence, in that quality she had no technical name for and recognized anyway, the way one recognizes good singing without knowing music: authority. Two hands had touched this vellum, and both of them had been certain. Except — she bent closer, tilting the plate against the window's grey light — except in one respect. The older line was beautiful the way a river is beautiful, easy and assured. The newer line was beautiful the way a wire is beautiful, pulled taut, carrying current, every millimeter paid for. The apprentice drew better than his master, and he drew like a man who could not afford to be wrong.

"Mistress Voss." Hedstrom's voice, from across the room, with an edge of impatience in it. "The register, if you please. We haven't all day."

She noted the dimensions of the plate. She noted the number of rollers (thirty-one), the stock of vellum (four skins, Turkish, fine), the pigments (gold leaf, two boxes; ultramarine, one jar; lampblack, verdigris, sepia, in quantities consistent with a working studio). She wrote *one apprentice, E. Nakamura, resident,* in her neat columnar hand, and beside the entry for the atlas itself she paused.

*One atlas, unfinished, master's commission.* That was what the form wanted. Her pen hovered.

"What is the atlas for?" she asked.

Elodie Nakamura looked at her for a moment longer than politeness strictly allowed. "My master spent nine years on it," he said. "It was to be the complete hydrography of the northern coast. Every harbor. Every sound. He died before it was done. I intend to finish it."

"For whom? A patron? The Admiralty?"

"For him," the apprentice said. "For the promise."

It was not an answer, and it was delivered with the mild, unarguable finality of a door closing gently. Sera wrote: *Atlas, unfinished, purpose stated as memorial.* And beneath it, in the margin, in the tiny private shorthand her father had never been permitted to see, she added a note to herself that was not an inventory item at all:

*The boy draws better than the master. Ask why.*

* * *

They were nearly done — Hedstrom had found nothing and was visibly disappointed about it — when Sera saw the waste bin.

It stood beside the drawing board, a plain wooden pail, and in it lay the day's discarded practice sheets. Any apprentice produces them; she had expected scribbles, failed flourishes, ruined experiments. She fished one out with two fingers because it was her habit to check refuse against registers — smugglers, her father liked to say, were undone by their rubbish more often than their records — and the sheet she lifted was not a failure at all.

It was a perfect drawing.

A small chart of the harbor mouth, no larger than her two hands, complete with soundings, tidal annotations, and a delicately rendered buoy. It was flawless. And it had been crumpled and thrown away — not because it was wrong, Sera understood with a strange lurch, but because it had been right. Practiced. Discarded the way a musician discards a scale. The boy threw away perfection the way other men throw away scraps, and kept, under lock, whatever it was he considered worth keeping.

She became aware that he was watching her hold it. Watching with an expression she could not entirely read — wariness, yes, but underneath it something that startled her: recognition. The look of someone seeing another person notice the thing that everyone else has agreed not to see.

"You draw this and throw it away," she said. It was not a question.

"Warm-up," he said. "Hands before work. Like a pianist."

"Do you know many pianists?"

"No," he said, and almost smiled. Almost. It was the first thing on his face all morning that had not been calculated, and precisely because of that it told her the least.

She put the sheet back in the pail. On the walk to the door she paused at the wall of map rollers, and her eye caught — she would swear afterward it was chance, though some quieter part of her suspected otherwise — a thin line of gold showing at the seam of roller seventeen, where the wrapping had slipped. Gold leaf. In a workshop whose declared commissions, per the registry, included two parish boundary surveys and a wine merchant's estate plan. Nothing requiring gold.

"Roller seventeen," she said lightly. "What does it hold?"

"A mistake," said Elodie Nakamura. "From my master's last year. We keep our mistakes. They teach."

Hedstrom cleared his throat by the door. Outside, the gulls were loud over the water, and somewhere in the lane the dyer's woman was calling her sons home with a voice like a bell.

At the threshold Sera turned back. The apprentice stood in the middle of his ordered room with the linen cloth half-draped over the atlas, and the north light came down on him and the work together, and he looked — she searched for the word and disliked every candidate — *sequestered*. Walled. A man keeping vigil over something.

"Your master's death," she said. "I'm sorry. Was it sudden?"

Something moved behind his eyes then, quick and submerged, a fish turning under ice. "All deaths are sudden," he said, "if you weren't expecting them."

"Yes," said Sera Voss. "I imagine they are."

* * *

The bridge sang again on the return crossing, and Hedstrom talked the whole way — about the weather, about the cost of boots, about a cousin in the customs house — and Sera answered in the automatic syllables that kept such talkers satisfied while her mind worked elsewhere.

Because here was the thing she could not put down, the splinter under the skin of the afternoon: her father had sent her to that workshop knowing what she would find there. Or knowing what she would *not* find, which amounted to the same thing in a man who missed nothing. Irregularities in the customs records, he had said. Bring me what you find before you write it anywhere. And there had been nothing to find — no contraband, no hidden stock, no second set of books — unless what he'd wanted her to find was the boy himself. The apprentice. The too-precise hand. Her father sent auditors to places where he expected arithmetic to fail. He had sent his daughter to a place where the only anomaly was excellence.

She copied the day's figures into the fair ledger that evening in her father's study, by lamplight, while he read correspondence across the room. When she reached the atlas entry she wrote the sanctioned words — *unfinished; completion undertaken by the apprentice* — and her pen slowed.

"Father," she said, not looking up. "The Nakamura workshop. Why is it on the assessment list at all? It's solvent. The registry shows no debts."

"Solvent enterprises hide the most," he said, without lowering his letter.

"That's not an answer."

Now he did lower it. He regarded her across the lamplit desk with the expression she had known all her life, the one that was fondness and caution braided together into something she had never been able to untangle. "No," he agreed mildly. "It isn't. Some answers are load-bearing, Sera. Pull one out and see what the house does."

"I found nothing irregular," she said. "If that was the finding you wanted, you have it."

"I know," said Magistrate Voss, and returned to his letters, and the interview was over.

But later, undressing in her room with the harbor lights moving on the ceiling, she found herself thinking not of her father's evasions but of the waste bin. Of the perfect little chart of the harbor mouth, crumpled among the scraps. Of a young man who threw away flawless work every morning the way other people threw away eggshells, and who had looked at her — one moment, only one — as if she were the first person in nineteen years to pick it up and understand what it was.

*The boy draws better than the master. Ask why.*

She fell asleep with the question unanswered, and dreamed of an iron bridge singing under her feet, and woke before dawn with the irrational certainty that she had left something unfinished in that dim north-lit room — and that it was not the coastline.

Three days later, a courier in magistrate's livery carried a sealed letter across the bridge to the workshop quarter, and Sera Voss, reviewing the outgoing post as part of her ordinary duties, saw the crest pressed into the wax and felt the floor of all her certainties tilt one degree, like a ship taking water.

The seal was her father's. The addressee was the apprentice.
