## 4. A Sealed Letter

The courier came at the hour when the light through the workshop's south window turned everything the color of weak tea.

Elodie noticed this because he had been noticing light all morning—how it fell across the drafting table, how it caught in the suspension of gum arabic, how it made the wet ink of a coastline gleam like something alive. Master Aurel had taught him that a mapmaker must be a servant of light, that every shadow cast by a lamp could distort a bearing, and so Elodie had learned to read the sun's position the way other men read clocks. It was half past three. The gulls were quarreling over the fish market leavings. Somewhere below, Marta's boys were shouting about a ball that had gone over the wall into the alley.

And a man in a gray coat was climbing the stairs with his boots striking each tread exactly as hard as the last.

That was the first wrongness. Visitors to the workshop quarter did not climb with even steps; they climbed wearily, or drunkenly, or with the shuffling apology of people who had gotten lost among the crooked lanes and wanted only to be pointed back toward the city. Even the rent collector hurried, as though the quarter's steepness offended him. This man climbed like a soldier on parade, or like someone who wished it known that he was not afraid of what waited at the top.

Elodie set down his pen. He capped the inkwell out of habit—Aurel's voice in his head: *Never leave your ink open, boy; ink is time and time is money*—and stood, wiping his palms on his apron without looking down at them, which meant the black stains came away on the cloth and left his hands still marked.

The knock came before the man had finished the last stair, three blows struck with the heel of a hand, precise as a signature.

"We're closed for commissions," Elodie called, which was true, and also a lie, since he was alone now and took whatever work came up the stairs.

"Delivery for the workshop of Aurel Vasseur." The voice was flat, unhurried. "Signature required."

Elodie crossed the room and opened the door.

The courier was young, perhaps twenty-five, with the kind of face designed to be forgotten—a medium everything, a gray coat brushed clean, a leather satchel slung across his chest the way Elodie wore his own map satchel. He held out a board with a paper clipped to it and a pen already uncapped, and behind the paper, cradled in his other hand like something breakable or dangerous, was an envelope.

It was an ordinary envelope. That was the second wrongness, though Elodie would not understand why until later. It was heavy cream stock, the kind sold by stationers along the Corso for ten coppers a sheet, the kind any clerk might buy to send his mother a birthday greeting. It had no address written upon it beyond *The Workshop of A. Vasseur, Bridge Quarter, Vellamare*, in a hand so regular it might have been printed. And pressed into the flap of it, in wax the deep red of arterial blood, was a seal.

Elodie knew the seal. Everyone in Vellamare knew the seal. A bridge above crossed keys—the city's old emblem, adopted by the magistrate's office two generations ago and stamped on every summons, every writ, every condemnation notice that had come down on the lower quarters like weather. Elodie had seen it on the notice that repossessed the chandlery two doors down. He had seen it on the papers Marta's husband had carried the year the harbor dues doubled. He had seen it once, from a distance, on the document a bailiff nailed to the customs house door announcing the seizure of a ship whose name Elodie had never learned.

He had never seen it addressed to this doorway.

"Sign," said the courier.

Elodie signed. His hand wrote *E. Nakamura* in the small careful script he used for marginalia, and he watched it happen as if from slightly outside himself, the way one watches one's fingers during a long day of hatching and cross-hatching when the hand seems to know its work better than the mind does.

"The master is out," Elodie said. "If you'd like me to—"

"Delivered to the workshop. Signed for by an occupant." The courier recapped the pen, tucked the board under his arm, and was already turning down the stairs. "Good afternoon."

His boots went down the way they had come up, each tread identical. At the bottom, Elodie heard him pause—not long enough to mean anything, perhaps—and then the street door closed and the footsteps faded toward the Corso.

Elodie stood in the doorway holding the letter.

* * *

He did not open it at first. This was not caution, or not only caution. It was a strange, superstitious reluctance, the feeling one has standing at the edge of cold water, knowing that once committed there is no un-committing. As long as the seal stayed whole, the letter was just paper. Cream stock, red wax, a crest he had seen a hundred times. Paper could sit on a table. Paper could wait.

But paper, Aurel had always said, is never only paper. *Every document is a weapon or a shield, boy. Learn which before you pick it up.*

Elodie carried the letter inside and set it on the drafting table, between the half-finished chart of the northern coast and the jar of pens, and then he walked around it and looked at it from the other side, which made him feel foolish, and then he sat down on the stool and looked at it some more.

The wax seal had been pressed cleanly, evenly, with a signet that bore no cracks. Official seals were applied by clerks with proper sealing presses; this had been done by hand, but a practiced hand. Someone accustomed to closing important letters. Someone who wanted it understood at a glance whose authority this correspondence flowed from.

Magistrate Aldric Voss's office sent letters to ships' masters and guild halls and landowners. It did not send letters to workshops in the bridge quarter. Nothing that happened here required the magistrate's personal attention; that was precisely what made the quarter a place where a mapmaker could keep certain charts and ask no questions. The law's attention was like the tide—it had its channels, and this narrow stair was not one of them.

Until now.

Elodie picked up the letter knife—a thin bone blade Aurel had bought in a junk shop years ago, worn smooth as a shell—and slid it under the flap.

Inside were two sheets.

The first was written in the same printed-regular hand as the address, in iron-gall ink gone brownish at the edges where it pooled. Elodie read it once quickly, the way one reads anything terrifying—taking in shapes rather than meanings, the way the eye takes in the shape of a wave before it understands the wave will break over you—and then he made himself go back to the beginning and read it properly, word by word, because he had been trained to read documents properly, trained by a man who believed that precision was a form of respect owed to the world.

*To the occupants of the workshop of Aurel Vasseur.*

*Whereas information has been received and duly recorded naming the said workshop, its late proprietor, and the persons residing and laboring therein, as parties to the conveyance of contraband goods by sea and river along the northern approaches to this port; and whereas such information is of a character requiring formal investigation under the statutes governing smuggling and conspiracy;*

*You are hereby notified that an inquiry has been opened. Further communication will follow. You are advised to make no alteration to premises, records, or instruments in your keeping, any such alteration being itself evidence of guilt.*

*By order of the Magistracy of Vellamare.*

There was no signature. Only the crest again, stamped small and perfect at the foot of the page, and beneath it a date.

Eleven days ago.

The second sheet was shorter. It was written in a different hand entirely—a quick, slanted, private hand, the loops compressed as though the writer had been in haste or fear—and it said only this:

*They have names. Yours is among them. Whether you put it there or someone else did, I cannot say. Come to no official place. Answer nothing until you understand who wants what.*

No signature. No crest. The paper was cheaper than the envelope's cream stock; it looked torn from a common notebook.

Elodie read both sheets three times more. Then he laid them side by side on the drafting table and weighted their corners with the brass objects that came to hand—a paperweight, a divider, the little bronze bell Aurel had kept for calling him in from the balcony—and he sat very still and listened to his own heart working, quick and shallow, like a bird trapped under glass.

*Its late proprietor.*

They knew Aurel was dead, of course. Everyone knew; the death had been ruled an accident, a fall on the wet stairs of the Customs House annex, and there had been a brief notice in the gazette, two lines, misspelling his surname. But the phrase still landed strangely. *Late proprietor.* Written eleven days ago, it meant the inquiry had opened before Aurel died—or within hours of it. Which meant the information had been *received* while Aurel still lived. Which meant—

Elodie got up and went to the cabinet.

The cabinet was Aurel's pride and his secret both: a tall walnut piece with false-backed drawers that Elodie had discovered in his third month of apprenticeship and never admitted to discovering, and which Aurel had finally shown him openly in his last autumn, drawing out the hidden tray with the air of a man handing over a debt. Inside lay the master's private papers, the working notes, the lists of clients who paid in coin that didn't ask questions, and beneath all of it, wrapped in oilcloth, the atlas—the forbidden atlas, the great work, the reason Elodie rose before dawn and worked past midnight and told the neighbors nothing.

He did not open the hidden drawer. Instead he searched the ordinary ones, the ones any inspector would search, and found what he was looking for: the ledger of legitimate commissions, the receipts, the correspondence with the harbor trust about the coastal survey. Honest paper. Shield paper.

And then he sat back down across from the letter and forced himself to think the thought he had been circling.

Someone had named this household to the magistracy as smugglers. Not as witnesses, not as persons of interest—*as parties*. The language of the notice was not the language of an opening inquiry; it was the language of a conclusion wearing inquiry's clothes. Information *has been received*. An inquiry *has been opened*. Eleven days ago. Before the fall on the stairs. Before the coroner's verdict. Before Elodie had buried his master in the pauper's row of the hillside cemetery because no family came forward and the parish would not pay for stone.

Had Aurel known? Had the letter come for him too—had this same gray courier climbed these stairs with this same cream envelope while the master still lived? Elodie searched the memory of their last weeks and found nothing, no visitor, no delivery, no change in Aurel's habits except a new watchfulness Elodie had attributed to the strain of finishing the atlas. But then, Aurel had kept secrets the way other men kept matches, close and casually, and had died with most of them intact.

*Whether you put it there or someone else did, I cannot say.*

Who wrote the second sheet? The hand was educated but hurried. Whoever it was knew the contents of the inquiry file—knew that names had been entered, knew that Elodie's name was among them—and yet warned him against the official channels, as though the writer feared the inquiry less than whoever moved behind it. A friend in the machinery. Or an enemy setting a trap within a trap, baiting him toward some error that remained invisible from here.

Elodie realized he had begun to shiver, though the workshop was warm. The stove ticked. Down in the alley, Marta's boys had recovered their ball and moved their game to the corner by the seawall, and their shouting rose faintly through the floorboards, ordinary and bright, belonging to a city in which letters like this one did not arrive.

*Make no alteration to premises, records, or instruments in your keeping.*

Which meant, of course, that he must alter everything, immediately, invisibly—or that he must alter nothing, if the warning letter spoke truly and the fastest road to ruin was any motion at all. He thought of the atlas lying wrapped in its oilcloth eight feet from where he sat. Routes. Soundings. Names and dates in Aurel's cramped marginal hand, the true commerce of the northern coast rendered in ink more honest than any ledger the magistracy possessed. If the inquiry was real, the atlas was either his salvation—proof that others ran the contraband, proof of who profited—or his gallows, depending on whose eyes saw it first.

He thought of the magistrate's daughter, Sera Voss, who had come twice to audit the workshop's licensing and stood too long over his draft charts, her finger hovering above a contour line without touching it, saying, *This is very fine work, Mr. Nakamura. Unusually fine.* He had taken it for suspicion then. Now he wondered what she would make of a letter bearing her father's seal.

Because that was the thing that would not stop turning over in him, smooth and terrible as sea-worn glass: the seal. The magistrate's own crest, hand-pressed, flawless. Letters under that seal were logged, copied, filed. Every writ that left the office existed in triplicate somewhere in the Palazzo's record rooms. So either this letter was entered in the rolls—an official inquiry, findable, provable, real—or it was not, and a seal meant to bind the law's honor had been lifted like a stamp from a drawer and pressed onto private malice.

Elodie did not know which possibility frightened him more.

The light was failing. Without deciding to, he found he had risen and crossed to the window, and he stood looking out at the harbor, where the evening lamps were coming on one by one along the quay and the old iron bridge hung above the water between the workshop quarter and the city, its lamps strung like beads on its spine, carrying home the last carts and the last clerks. From here it looked so solid. Everything looked solid until it wasn't—stairs, verdicts, empires of cream-colored paper.

Somewhere across that bridge, in the Palazzo, a man had sealed this envelope. Elodie imagined him at a desk, pressing the signet into the wax with steady fingers, and understood, with a cold clarity that felt like the beginning of a map taking shape on blank vellum, that he could not simply wait. *Further communication will follow.* The next visit might not bring a letter.

He returned to the table. He folded the two sheets together, then reconsidered, separated them, and hid them apart—the official notice flat behind the drawer-bottom of the plan chest, the unsigned warning rolled tight into the hollow of a broken map roller that no longer held anything anyone would want. Then he banked the stove, trimmed the lamp, and pulled the atlas from the cabinet after all.

Not to destroy. Not tonight. Tonight only to look—to unroll the first plate across the table and study the margins as he had never quite dared to study them before, reading not as an apprentice reads his master's corrections but as a hunted man reads a document that may contain, somewhere in its crowded edges, the names of the people who killed Aurel Vasseur and meant to hang his household for it.

The lamp hissed softly. Outside, the tide was turning.

And it was there, in the fourth plate, in the margin beside a sounding mark off Gull Point, that Elodie found the first date—and recognized, in Aurel's tiny ciphered shorthand, the initials of a man whose name appeared, eleven days ago, beneath the magistrate's own seal.
