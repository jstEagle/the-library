## 5. What the Atlas Shows

The lamp guttered once before Elodie trimmed the wick, and in that brief dimming the atlas on his bench seemed to breathe—forty-one plates of vellum lying flat beneath weighted glass, pale as a body prepared for burial. He had been working over them for six hours, and his eyes had begun to play the tricks that exhaustion plays: coastlines shifting, soundings swimming, the little printed numbers of depth crawling like insects across the harbor floor.

He should have slept. Marta had told him to sleep, pressing a heel of yesterday's bread into his hands at the door as though feeding him were a duty she had assigned herself and would not be argued with. *Eat, boy. The dead do not need you awake at three in the morning.* But he had eaten standing up, all of it, tasting nothing, and then he had come back down the narrow stair to the workshop because sleep was a country he could no longer reach, and work was the only road left open.

Master Aurel was eleven days dead. The workshop still smelled of him—tobacco cut with clove, the sour-sweetness of the fish glue he heated too fast, always impatient, always certain the kettle would not boil unless watched. Elodie kept expecting the smell to fade. It did not fade. If anything it thickened at night, when the tide turned and the harbor wind pushed up through the shutters, carrying the master back into the room as if he had merely stepped out to buy ink and would return complaining about the price.

Elodie sat down on the low stool and pulled the first plate toward him across the scarred oak. Plate One: The Outer Reaches, from the Saltstone Light to the mouth of the Vell. It was beautiful. He allowed himself to acknowledge this the way one allows oneself to look at a beautiful thing at a funeral—with guilt, and quickly. The coastline ran sure and unbroken under Aurel's hand, every promontory named in the master's small severe capitals, every shoal stippled in graduated densities so that the shallows seemed literally to shallow beneath the eye, sand giving way to rock giving way to deep water drawn in fine cross-hatching like rain falling upward out of the sea.

He had traced this coast himself twice as practice. He knew its every curve better than he knew his own face in a mirror. Which was why, at some hour past four, with the lamp burning steady now and the workshop utterly silent except for the tick of the copper roof cooling, Elodie noticed something he had never noticed before.

The stippling was wrong.

Not wrong the way an apprentice's work is wrong—wobbly, uneven, timid. Wrong the way a sentence is wrong when it means two things at once. He bent close, close enough that his breath fogged the glass weight, and looked at the shoal off Gull's Teeth where no honest chart needed a shoal. The dots were not random. Aurel had taught him that stippling must fall like sediment, denser where the water shallowed, thinning toward depth—and Elodie had filled three practice sheets learning to make his hand obedient to that law. But these dots clustered in groups. Three, then five, then two. Three-five-two. And further along the coast, in a stretch of water Aurel had marked *unsoundable*, another cluster: seven, one, four.

He sat back. His heart was doing something slow and heavy, each beat arriving like a stone dropped down a well.

Numbers. They were numbers.

He got up, took the key from around his neck—he wore it there now, had worn it since the morning they buried the master and Elodie pocketed it from the dead man's desk drawer without letting himself think about whether he was stealing—and unlocked the long cabinet where Aurel kept his reference works. The pilot books came out. The tide almanacs for the last six years. The harbor master's published soundings, which Aurel had helped correct and therefore distrusted absolutely.

It took him until dawn to confirm what some cold, quiet place inside him already understood. The clusters matched dates. Three-five-two: the third of May, 1911—the night the revenue cutter *Sparrowhawk* recorded, in its public log, a chase it abandoned off the Teeth in fog. Seven-one-four: July the fourteenth, the seizure at Quay Nine that everyone in the quarter still talked about, when customs men found a hold full of untaxed silk and not one man who would say whose it was. Every dense patch of stippling along the outer coast sat beside a date rendered in dots, and every date marked a night when something had slipped past or failed to slip past the law.

And the depths themselves—the soundings, those authoritative little numerals—did not match the harbor master's charts anywhere the smugglers' routes would run. Off Gull's Teeth the true depth was nine fathoms; Aurel's atlas said twelve. A stranger trusting this chart would ground his ship on the Teeth and drown. But a man who knew the code—who knew the atlas lied precisely where the law's own charts told truth—would find in that lie a corridor no revenue cutter would ever dare to follow.

*Unsoundable*, Aurel had written. Elodie read the word again and heard, now, not hydrography but laughter. Dry, private, wicked. His master laughing at the magistrate's men from inside his own grave.

* * *

He went through the plates the way a doctor goes through a body, looking for what killed it.

Plate Four: the inner harbor, exquisite, the quays drawn with such loving precision that he could count the bollards. In the margin, in what appeared to be ordinary annotation—a compass rose, a scale bar, the decorative sea-serpent Aurel allowed himself exactly once per plate—there were initials. Small ones. *T.V.* tucked into the serpent's coil. *R.M.* hidden in the flourish of the scale. He knew some of them from the workshop's business ledger, names that had ordered charts and paid promptly in cash: Tadeo Voss, who owned three coastal brigs. Remy Marchetti of the chandlery on Rope Walk. Names, he realized with a sensation like stepping into cold water, that belonged to respectable men. Men who attended the regatta banquet. Men whose wives bought ribbon from the same shop as the magistrate's daughter.

Plate Eleven: the river approach. Here the marginalia grew richer, almost reckless. Along the southern bank, where a careful eye would see only marsh-grass rendered in fine dry-brush green, the grass grew in rows. Not natural rows. Surveyed rows. Elodie held the plate sideways to the lamp and saw that the grasses' bends spelled, in a shorthand of his master's own devising—the same shorthand Aurel had taught him for field notes, swearing him to secrecy as a joke—*landing at slack water only; watchman paid thru Michaelmas*. And beneath the marsh, in the white space between the river's edge and the plate border, written so faintly it might have been ghost-impression from a page pressed against it: a list of nights. Nineteen of them. Each with a small mark beside it, a check or a cross. Sixteen checks. Three crosses.

Three failures. Elodie found himself mourning those three failures as if they were men. Perhaps they were.

By the time gray light began to seep through the shutters he had reached Plate Seventeen, and Plate Seventeen stopped him entirely.

It was the workshop quarter itself—the tangle of lanes below the cliff, the copper roofs, the stairs, and crossing it all, drawn in ink so fine it seemed spun rather than ruled, the old iron bridge. Elodie had walked that bridge ten thousand times. He had never seen it drawn like this: every rivet accounted for, every expansion joint, the tide races beneath it mapped with a tenderness that bordered on devotion. Aurel had loved the bridge. He used to stand in the middle of it in bad weather, coat whipping, saying a mapmaker ought to know his city's pulse from the places where it beat hardest.

In the lower corner of the plate, where the cartouche would ordinarily carry the title in Aurel's severe capitals, there was instead a single line of the field-shorthand, pressed so lightly Elodie had to wet his fingertip and touch the vellum to raise it:

*If I am stopped, the rest is with the one who finishes.*

The rest. The lamp flame stood perfectly still and yet the light seemed to sway. *The one who finishes.* Not *my apprentice*. Not *the boy*. The one who finishes—as if the atlas had never been addressed to any reader but him, as if every plate were a letter waiting eleven years to arrive.

He put his head in his hands. Outside, the gulls had begun their arguments; somewhere above, Marta's household stirred, a child's footsteps running the length of the floor overhead. The city was waking up around a secret it did not know it kept.

Because that was the thing—this was the thing he kept circling and circling like a moth at glass—this was not art. He had told himself for eleven days that he was preserving a masterpiece, completing a great cartographic labor, keeping faith with craft. That had been bearable. Craft asked only skill and patience, and he had both. But no artist encodes revenue-chase dates in shoal-stippling. No artist hides paymaster's initials in a compass rose. This was testimony. Every plate was sworn evidence wearing the costume of beauty, and the names scattered through the margins were not decorations but accomplices—or victims, or witnesses, he could no longer tell which—and somewhere among them, surely, was the answer to the question he had carried since the funeral, since the moment at the graveside when the magistrate's clerk had offered condolences with a smile that did not reach past his teeth: *who did this to you, old man?*

Aurel had known he might be stopped. Had built the atlas to survive him. Had left the most dangerous knowledge—the routes, the names, whatever *the rest* meant—to be completed by the one who finished, meaning that the final plates, the ones Elodie had not yet begun, were not missing chapters but sealed ones. The master had trusted him with this without asking him. Had chained him to it from beyond the grave with nothing but a line of ghost-pressures in damp vellum.

Elodie got up and crossed to the window and opened the shutter a hand's width. The harbor lay lead-colored and calm, masts swaying, smoke rising from the bakery chimney on Rope Walk. Ordinary. Lawful-looking. And underneath, according to nineteen nights and sixteen checks, running like a second city beneath the first, the other Vellamare—the one that moved at slack water and paid its watchmen through Michaelmas and drowned strangers on false soundings.

He understood, finally and completely, why Master Aurel was dead.

And he understood, in the same cold instant, that the atlas in front of him was no longer an inheritance. An inheritance is a gift; you may keep it, sell it, refuse it. This was a burden, and burdens do not ask permission. Either he finished it—completed the coded plates, preserved the testimony, made himself the keeper of everything that had killed his master—or he burned it, and let the other Vellamare go on drowning its truths in false soundings forever. There was no third door. He had spent eleven days believing in a third door, the door where he simply finished a beautiful book in a quiet workshop and stayed invisible. That door had closed while he slept, and he had not even heard the latch.

His hands, when he looked at them, had ink under the fingernails—his own ink now, layered over traces of the master's, indistinguishable. He thought of the coroner's verdict. *Accidental fall upon the stairs.* He thought of the ink stains on the wrong hand, and pushed the thought away, because if he followed it tonight he would go mad, and madness finished nothing.

He began, very carefully, to stack the plates—not by number, as they would be bound, but by danger, the way a man stacks papers he may need to destroy in a hurry. Shoals and dates on top. Initials beneath. The bridge plate last, face up, where he could cover it fastest.

Then he heard, through the open window, the sound of boots on the lane below. Two pairs, unhurried, stopping outside the workshop door.

One of them knocked.
