## 6. Neighbors Who Watch

Marta Venn had been dyeing cloth in the Shadowmere quarter for twenty-two years, and in that time she had learned to read a neighborhood the way she read a vat of madder—by color, by temperature, by the small shifts that meant something underneath had gone wrong.

The color of Shadowmere was changing.

She noticed it first at the fish stall on Wednesday morning, when Old Pieto, who for eleven years had set aside a mackerel for her boys because Tam once pulled his grandson from the harbor water, wrapped her purchase in silence and would not meet her eyes. Not hostility. Something worse. *Embarrassment.* He handled the coin as though it might accuse him of something, and when she wished him good morning he mumbled into his apron and turned to the next customer before the word was fully out of her mouth.

By Thursday, the dyer's own courtyard had gone quiet in a way that had nothing to do with weather. The women who usually leaned over the shared wall to trade gossip and salt for bluing—the Corvi sisters, Widow Ansa, fat Luccia whose husband worked the customs sheds—all of them had errands elsewhere. Their voices still carried from three streets over, warm and busy and ordinary, but not one of them came near her gate.

Marta stood in her courtyard with her hands stained blue to the wrist, indigo worked so deep into the creases of her knuckles that no lye would ever fetch it out, and listened to the silence, and understood that someone had been talking about them.

About her. About the boy.

* * *

She had known Aurel Nakamura for fifteen years, back when he was only the mapmaker with the foreign wife and the ink-stained collar, and she had loved him in the plain, unsentimental way one loves a neighbor who has never once asked for anything. When his wife died, Marta had sent soup for a month. When the boy came to him—not his blood, though he carried the name and half the face—Marta had watched Aurel teach him to grind pigments on the back step, patient as a saint, and had thought: *there is a household that will keep.*

Then Aurel went into the harbor in February and drowned, and the magistrates called it an accident, and the soup stopped because there was no one left in that workshop to send it for except a boy who thanked her too politely and ate alone.

Marta had seen the body. That was the thing nobody knew, the thing she kept folded up inside her like a length of cloth dyed the wrong color and hidden at the bottom of the chest. She had seen Aurel on the slab, or near enough—one glimpse through the door of the coroner's yard before they shooed her off—and she had noticed what everyone else was too grieving or too indifferent to notice: the ink.

A mapmaker's right hand lives in the inkwell. Aurel's right hand had been clean. It was the *left*—the hand that held the pen's partner, the weighting hand, the hand that steadied the ruler—that was stained black to the second knuckle, fresh stains, the kind that come off on cloth and never quite come off skin.

She had told herself it meant nothing. Hands are strange; grief makes strangers of us all; perhaps he had changed his habits in his last years. She had four sons to feed and a dye business that survived winter by a margin thinner than her best muslin, and Shadowmere survived by not seeing things. That was the neighborhood's oldest craft, older than her vats: not seeing.

But now the neighbors were seeing too much, or pretending to, and the silence they were building around the workshop and around her was the same silence that comes before a door is kicked in.

* * *

It was Luccia's husband, of all people, who finally told her—or half-told her, sideways, in the way men spoke dangerous things when they'd had enough wine to be careless and not enough to be brave.

He caught her at the communal pump Friday evening, while her youngest hauled the yoke across the cobbles behind her, and he glanced up the street and down it before he said anything at all, which told her more than his words did.

"Marta." His voice dropped. "There's been a man going door to door. Three days now."

"What sort of man?"

"Respectable." He said the word like it tasted bad. "Gray coat, city manners, boots too clean for this quarter. Says he's from the assessor's office, checking property claims along the harbor row. But he's not asking about property, Marta. He's asking about *people*. Who visits the workshop. Whether the boy goes out at night. Whether—" he hesitated, wiped his mouth "—whether the Japanese boy has visitors. Foreign ones."

The pump handle dripped between them. Down the lane, a lamp was being lit in a window, then another, the small warm ritual that happened every evening at this hour without anyone willing it, and tonight it looked to Marta like a string of eyes opening.

"Did you tell him anything?" she asked.

"I told him I work customs sheds till dark and see nothing but gulls." He shifted his weight. "But the Corvis talked. And Ansa. You know how it goes. A man with a notebook and a patient manner gets more than a magistrate with a warrant." He looked at her then, finally, and there was real misery in his face, which she hadn't expected. "Marta, they're asking whether you feed him. Whether your boys run messages for him. I'm telling you because—because when the questions stop being about the workshop and start being about the street, it means somebody's building a case, and cases need witnesses, and I'd rather you heard it from me than found out when the papers come."

He left before she could thank him, walking fast, not looking back, and Marta understood that the kindness had cost him something and that he would not be able to afford it again.

* * *

Saturday morning she sent her eldest, Dario, nineteen and broad as a door, down to the bakery on the pretext of bread, purely to see whether the gray-coated man would appear. Dario came back with loaves and a pale face.

"Saw him," he reported, setting the bread down too hard. "Talking to the rope-seller at the corner of Tannery Row. Didn't like the look of him, Ma. He stands like—" Dario frowned, searching for it. "Like his weight's on one side. Like a man who spent years braced against a ship's roll. And he had one of those folding cameras under his arm. New kind, brass fittings. Nobody takes pictures of tanneries."

"Did he speak to you?"

"He asked my name." Dario turned his hat in his hands, a habit from childhood she thought he'd outgrown. "I gave it. Seemed worse not to. Then he asked if I knew the apprentice at the mapmaker's. I said everyone knows everyone here, which isn't answering, and he smiled like he liked that answer better than any other he'd gotten all week, and wrote it down anyway."

*The Japanese boy.* That was the phrase that had traveled door to door ahead of him, Marta realized. Not Elodie, not the mapmaker's lad, not even the boy's proper name spoken whole. *The Japanese boy*, as if that half of him were the whole of him, as if it made him a stranger to be catalogued rather than a neighbor to be protected. She had watched this quarter swallow Venetians and Genoese and a Lascar sailor's widow without a burp of trouble, and she had flattered herself that tolerance was the same as loyalty, and now she was learning the difference at fifty-one years of age: tolerance lasts exactly as long as it costs nothing.

The price had just gone up.

* * *

That afternoon she crossed the alley to the workshop. It was a crossing she had made a hundred times with eggs, with mordant advice, with leftover festival cake, and she felt the strangeness of her own caution like a wet coat. She knocked. The boy opened the door almost at once, as if he'd been standing near it—which, she thought, looking at him, he probably had.

Elodie Nakamura was seventeen and had his dead master's long fingers and his dead mother's fine bones, and he had grown thin since February in the specific way of someone who forgets meals not from sorrow but from absorption. Ink on his cheekbone. A smear of gold leaf at his wrist. He looked at her with those careful dark eyes and said, "Signora Venn," and stepped back to admit her, and she saw his gaze go past her shoulder to scan the alley before the door closed.

"You've heard," she said.

"Someone looked through the windows last night." He said it flatly, without drama, and that frightened her more than trembling would have. "I thought it was children. It may have been children."

"It wasn't children." She took the room in as she always did—the drying racks hung with maps like laundry, the smell of size and gum arabic and the sharp mineral breath of pigments, the master's chair empty by the great table. On the table, covered by a linen cloth, lay something long and rectangular that he moved slightly as he passed, unconsciously, the way a man shields his ribs. "There's a man asking at every door. Gray coat. Questions about you, about who feeds you, who talks to you. Luccia's husband warned me. He wanted me to know he hadn't answered."

Elodie was silent for a moment. Then: "Why wouldn't he answer? Answering is safer than not. That's how this works, isn't it?" He said it without bitterness, more like a student reciting a lesson he'd rather not have learned. "They don't need everyone to talk. They need enough."

"Your master's death was ruled an accident," Marta said, and heard her own voice do something odd around the words. "Accidents don't send men door to door."

Something flickered across the boy's face—too quick to name, but she thought it might have been relief. The relief of a person who has been carrying a suspicion alone in the dark and hears another voice confirm it exists.

"You saw the body," he said quietly. "You were at the yard in February. I remember you being there."

"I saw enough."

"Enough for what?"

And there it was, the question she had been dreading for five months, standing in the middle of the workshop among the drying maps with its hand out. Because *enough* meant testimony, and testimony meant her name in papers, and her name in papers meant the customs shed would find reasons to slow Luccia's husband's manifests and the dyers' guild would discover irregularities in her licensing and Dario would lose the dock work he was waiting on—all the quiet machinery by which this city punished women who spoke. She had four sons. Two of them not yet grown.

"Enough to worry," she said, which was cowardice, and she saw him recognize it as such, and forgive her, which was somehow worse.

"They'll come for the workshop soon," Elodie said, turning to the window, to the sliver of alley visible between the shutters. "Paperwork first, probably. Some seizure order. Master Aurel always said the atlas we're making was worth more than the building—it's why he kept it quiet, why some pages never left this room." He paused. "When they come, Signora Venn, stay away from this door. Whatever they ask about you—"

"Stop." She said it harder than she meant to. "I've been dying cloth in this quarter longer than you've been alive, boy, and I'll decide who I stand beside. But I'll tell you what I will do and won't." She pointed her blue-stained finger at him, the gesture she used on her sons when the matter was final. "I won't lie for you. Lies rot; I've seen whole families ruined faster by the cover than the crime. But I'll remember things. Remembering is different. Remembering is mine to give or withhold, and I haven't decided yet what I remember."

He studied her, and after a moment he nodded slowly, as if she'd offered him something more valuable than protection—as if, in this city, in this season, honest uncertainty was rarer and worth more.

"The man with the gray coat," he said. "Did anyone mention how he walks?"

"Limping," Dario had said. *Weight on one side.* "They say he favors one leg. Sea legs gone wrong."

"Yes," Marta said. "A limp. Naval, someone guessed."

Elodie turned back to the window. For a long moment he didn't move at all, and the light through the shutter slats striped him like a prisoner, and when he finally spoke his voice had gone very soft and very steady, the way her late husband's used to go just before he did something irrevocable.

"Thank you, signora. Go home the long way, past the fish stall. Let them see you came here and let them see you weren't afraid." A pause. "And check your cellar latch tonight. All the houses on this side share old foundations. If anyone comes asking again—anyone at all—there's a knock I'll teach your boys. Three slow, two fast."

"And what happens if someone knocks three slow, two fast?"

He looked at her then, and for the first time since February the careful apprentice mask slipped, and she saw underneath it something that made her think of Aurel in his last year: a man carrying a weight he refused to set down because setting it down would mean admitting how heavy it was.

"Then whatever you've hidden in your cellar," he said, "won't be hidden anymore."

* * *

She walked home the long way, past the fish stall, chin level, and let them look. Old Pieto didn't meet her eyes. The Corvi sisters' shutters were cracked open a finger's width, and closed when she passed. At the corner of Tannery Row, where the rope-seller's awning threw its afternoon shadow, a gray coat and a brass-fitted camera waited in the shade of a doorway, and the man attached to them watched her pass with the mild professional interest of a man ticking a box—*the dyer, visited the workshop, Saturday, sixteen hundred hours*—and she met his gaze full and held it until he looked away first.

Small victory. She held it all the way home, and it kept her warm through the evening, through supper, through the boys' noise and the banked fires.

It stopped keeping her warm at midnight, when she went down to the cellar for the jar of pickled quinces she'd forgotten at dinner, and lifted the lamp, and saw, plainly visible against the far wall where yesterday there had been nothing, the outline of a door she had never noticed in thirty years of living in that house—a seam in the stone, old and skillfully fitted, running floor to ceiling, wide enough for a man to walk through.

Wide enough, she thought, standing very still with the lamp shaking slightly in her hand, for a man to walk through in either direction.

From the other side, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of footsteps.
