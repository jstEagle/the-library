## 7. Tea with Sera

Sera Voss told herself it was an inspection.

The paperwork said so, or nearly. She had copied the workshop's entry from the registry of licensed premises into her own notebook, in her own hand, on her own initiative, and the word *audit* sat at the top of the page like a small, insufficient shield. Her father had not ordered this visit. Her father did not know of it. Those two facts she carried through the streets of Vellamare like coins in a torn pocket, jingling with every step, threatening to fall out and expose her at any moment.

She wore her plainest coat. She had left the magistrate's offices by the side stair that smelled of tallow and old petitions, and she had taken the long way, down through the fish market where the awnings dripped mid-morning condensation onto her shoulders, so that no one who mattered would see her cross out of the city proper and into the workshop quarter—the crooked, salt-stained neighborhood that clung to the harbor's western edge like a barnacle to a hull. The quarter was connected to the rest of Vellamare by a single iron bridge, and Sera felt the crossing in her teeth, as she always did: the low thrum of the tide through the lattice, the sense of walking into a place that was not quite her father's jurisdiction and not quite outside it.

On the far side, the air changed. It always changed. The city smelled of stone dust and ink and the beeswax the clerks used on their desks; the quarter smelled of dye vats and tar and frying onions and, beneath all of it, the mineral breath of the sea coming up through gaps between buildings. Laundry hung between rooftops on lines strung like rigging. A dog with one folded ear watched her pass from a doorway without lifting its head. Somewhere behind the dyer's house, a woman was scolding children in a voice that carried across three courtyards.

Sera had grown up believing that power flowed outward from the magistrate's hall the way light flows from a lamp—that wherever she stood was lit, and the rest of the world existed in gradations of shadow. Crossing the bridge, she felt the metaphor reverse itself. Here, *she* was the shadow. Here, people watched her from windows without appearing to, and the watching had weight.

The mapmaker's workshop stood at the end of Copper Roof Row, its name drawn from the way the light struck the sheet-metal roofs at evening until the whole lane glowed like something smelted. The workshop's own roof had gone green at the seams with verdigris, and rain had streaked the plaster below the upper window in long pale tears. The sign—*AUREL DANE, CARTOGRAPHER & ENGRAVER*—had faded to a ghost of gilt. Someone had begun repainting the frame of the door; the work stopped halfway along the lintel, as if the painter had been called away mid-thought.

As indeed someone had.

She knocked before she could talk herself out of it.

Footsteps inside, uneven, hurried—and then a pause she could almost hear, the silence of a person deciding whether to exist. Then the bolt drew back.

Elodie Nakamura stood in the doorway holding a pen. Not a rag, not an apron—a pen, still wet at the nib, a thread of blue-black ink gathering at its tip with the patience of something that would eventually drip. He looked younger than he had during the official audit, she thought, and more tired, and his right hand, the one not holding the pen, was stained to the second knuckle as though he had thrust it into a squid. There was ink under his fingernails too, worked deep, the kind that never comes out. She knew that stain. She had it herself, in miniature, from years of copying depositions. It was the mark of a person who lived at a desk.

"Mistress Voss." His face did something complicated—surprise flattened quickly into courtesy, courtesy stretched thin over wariness. "I wasn't told to expect the office today."

"The office isn't here," Sera said. "I am."

He understood at once; she saw it land behind his eyes, the recalculation, the re-sorting of her from function to person. He stepped back from the doorway—not wide enough to be an invitation, not narrow enough to be refusal—and after a moment she took the space offered and went in.

* * *

Elodie's heart had not slowed since the courier came.

Four days now, and the sealed letter still lay where he had hidden it, wrapped in oilcloth inside the false bottom of the paper chest, and every hour of every day he was aware of it the way one is aware of a splinter lodged too deep to draw. The magistrate's crest. On ordinary paper. In an ordinary hand. Naming this household—naming Marta, naming her sons, naming him—in a conspiracy Elodie could not begin to imagine, let alone prove they hadn't joined.

And now the magistrate's daughter stood in the workshop, uninvited, off the books, looking at everything.

"Your master kept the light well," she said. She had drifted to the north window, where Aurel's drafting table still held its great adjustable lamp, its brass arm frozen at the angle of a man who would never again reach up to move it. "Most engravers ruin their eyes working toward the glass. He angled away from it. Clever."

"He said glare was for amateurs," Elodie said. "He said the work should come to your eye like something remembered, not something stared at."

Something crossed her face at that—something quick and private—and she turned away from the lamp as though it had shown her more than she wanted. "You quote him."

"I quote everyone. It's an apprentice disease."

"You're not an apprentice anymore, though. Are you." It was not quite a question. She let it sit in the room like weather.

Elodie busied himself with the kettle. This was the truth he had been circling for four days, the truth the letter made dangerous: he was not an apprentice anymore, and there was no master to stand between his hands and the world's judgment. Whatever the workshop contained, whatever the atlas contained, it was his now—his guilt, his inheritance, his evidence. If the magistrate's men walked through that door, the law would see only a half-Japanese boy alone among expensive papers and a dead man's debts, and Elodie had lived long enough in Vellamare to know exactly how that arithmetic resolved.

He set water on the brazier and took down two cups instead of one. He did not ask if she wanted tea. Asking would make it hospitality, and hospitality would make it social, and social was a country neither of them could afford to be seen visiting. Instead he simply placed the second cup on the workbench nearest her, the way one leaves a saucer of milk for something wild—not expecting it to stay, only hoping it will drink while it believes itself unobserved.

She noticed. Of course she noticed; she noticed everything, he was beginning to understand. Her eyes moved over the workshop the way her father's men moved over ledgers—but where theirs skimmed, hers *read*. She read the racks of drying maps. She read the rollers standing in their cabinet like rolled-up seasons. And once—only once, briefly, when she thought he wasn't looking—she read him.

"What are you auditing today?" he asked, to break it.

"I told you. The office isn't here." She took the cup when he poured, and held it without drinking. Steam climbed between them. Through the roof above, gulls argued over something. "I find that some things are better examined privately first. Before they become public examinations. Public examinations have... procedures. Momentum. They're like a tide—you can argue with a tide, but you do it wet."

"That's very nearly advice."

"It's very nearly observation." She sipped at last, and he watched something in her shoulders loosen by a degree, as if the tea were permission she'd been waiting to grant herself. "My father says the quarter is a sieve. He says everything that comes into this harbor leaks through Copper Roof Row before it reaches a customs table. He says it the way a man says something he's proud of knowing."

"And what do you say?"

"I say sieves are also how you separate what's worth keeping from what isn't." The words came fast, then seemed to alarm her by having come at all. She set the cup down. "Master Nakamura—"

"Elodie."

"*Master* Nakamura." Firmly, deliberately, and he felt the strangeness of it, the title landing on him like a coat cut for a bigger man. "There are inquiries being prepared against certain premises in this quarter. I don't know the schedule. I don't know the grounds. I know that when they come, they will not be gentle, because the gentleman directing them believes gentleness is a currency wasted on this side of the bridge."

Elodie kept his hands flat on the workbench, because if he didn't they would shake, and shaking hands tell truths. "The gentleman with the limp."

Her cup stopped halfway to her mouth.

"I've seen him," Elodie said quietly. "Twice now. Once photographing the front of the workshop from the dyer's steps. Once asking after 'the Japanese boy' at the chandlery. He walks like the sea gave him back reluctantly."

For a long moment Sera said nothing, and in that silence Elodie heard the whole shape of the thing they were building between them—two people laying down words like planks over a gap, each plank tested before weight is put upon it, neither willing to step fully across. She could lose her post for being here. He could lose everything for telling her what he knew. And neither of them knew yet whether the other was the trap.

"If such a man reports to anyone," she said at last, carefully, each word placed like a stone in shallow water, "it is not to my father directly. There are channels. Deputies. The machinery has many gears, and not all of them turn where the light reaches." She met his eyes then, and her voice dropped to something almost too quiet for the room. "Whatever you are finishing in this workshop—I would advise you, as a matter of general professional observation, to finish it soon. And to know precisely which parts of it can survive being read aloud in a courtroom, and which parts cannot."

"And if the parts that can't survive it are the true ones?"

The question hung between them. She looked at him, and he watched her weigh answering honestly against answering safely, and he understood suddenly, intimately, that she performed that weighing a hundred times a day—that it was the constant arithmetic of being the magistrate's daughter, dutiful and clear-eyed in a house built on a foundation she was only beginning to suspect had sand in it.

"Then I suppose," she said, "one hopes the courtroom is worthy of the truth. Or that the truth finds a better court."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Outside, Marta's voice rose and fell in the courtyard, scolding someone about indigo, and the sound was so ordinary that it hurt.

"Why did you really come?" Elodie asked.

Sera looked down into her cup for a long time, as if the answer might be floating there among the leaves. When she spoke, she did not look up.

"When I audited this workshop officially, I found coastlines in the margins of your practice sheets that a journeyman couldn't draw. I found a hand that corrects its master's projections in pencil and lets the correction stand uninked, out of respect." Now she looked up. "I came because precision like yours doesn't occur in isolation. It occurs under instruction. And I wanted to see the instruction. And—" the smallest hitch, quickly mastered— "I wanted to know whether the person making those lines was afraid."

"Was I?"

"Not then." She gathered her coat around her, set her untouched-by-more-than-two-sips tea aside with the exactness of someone returning a borrowed blade. At the door she paused, her hand on the bolt, and spoke without turning around. "The bridge will have work done on it soon. Inspection crews, scaffolding, notices. When you see the notices, Master Nakamura, remember what I said about tides. About arguing with them wet."

Then she was gone, and the door closed, and Elodie stood alone in the green-lit quiet of the workshop with two cups of tea going cold—one drunk like a debt paid, one barely touched—and the terrible, vertiginous understanding that she had just warned him about the bridge without saying why, about the seizure without saying when, about everything without saying anything at all.

He washed both cups slowly, thoroughly, the way his master had taught him to clean brushes: as if the work of the hands could settle the work of the heart.

It didn't. Because underneath the fear, underneath even the gratitude, one thought kept surfacing, cold and bright as a coin at the bottom of a well:

She had asked whether he was afraid. But she had flinched—at the lamp, at the mention of her father's pride—and Elodie knew the look of a person carrying evidence they hadn't yet admitted to finding.

Whatever Sera Voss had seen in her father's offices, it was sitting behind her eyes the way the sealed letter sat in his paper chest.

And one of them, sooner or later, would have to open first.

* * *

That night, Sera sat at her own desk in the house on Cathedral Square and copied nothing.

This was unusual. Her evenings were for copying—depositions, schedules, the fair hands her father required. Tonight the page before her stayed blank, and her palm, she realized, kept curling shut around nothing, as if it already held a date she had not yet written.

She thought of the second cup of tea, barely touched, and whether leaving it had been cruelty or kindness. She thought of a young man with ink to his knuckles saying *was I afraid?* in a voice that already knew the answer had changed since she last asked. She thought of her father at supper, laughing at the deputy's joke a half-second too readily, like a man paying protection.

She opened her notebook to the page headed *audit*, looked at the word for a long moment, and then—very carefully, in the small hand she used for things she did not intend to show anyone—crossed it out.

In its place she wrote nothing at all. But beneath the crossed-out word, without deciding to, her pen drew a single line: a coastline, precise and unhurried, curving off the edge of the page into blankness.

She recognized the hand she was imitating. That frightened her most of all.
