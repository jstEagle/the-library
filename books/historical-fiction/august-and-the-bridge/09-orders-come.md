## 9. Orders Come

The magistrate's office smelled of sealing wax and old decisions, and Sera Voss had learned to read both the way other people read weather.

She stood at the window of her father's private chamber while he worked through the morning's documents, and she watched the harbor light move across Vellamare in long silver bars. From this height the city arranged itself into something almost honest: the tiled roofs of the lower quarter, the masts nodding in the basin, the iron bridge spanning the channel between the workshop district and the city proper like a stitch holding two fabrics together. Somewhere down there, past the fish market and the rope walks, was a workshop with a copper roof that rang like a struck bell when it rained. Somewhere down there, a young man with ink under his fingernails drew coastlines more precisely than any cartographer she had ever audited.

Her father dipped his pen.

Sera had come to the office early, as she did every morning now, carrying her ledger of workshop assessments under one arm like a shield. The audit of Nakamura's establishment had been her assignment—routine, she'd been told, part of the magistrate's office's seasonal review of licensed trades—and she had performed it with the thoroughness her position demanded. She had measured the windows against the property rolls. She had counted the reams of paper and weighed the vials of pigment. She had asked her questions in her flattest official voice while Elodie Nakamura stood among his drying maps with his hands folded behind him, answering everything and revealing nothing, his dark eyes following her with an attention that had made her feel, for the first time in years, genuinely observed.

*Too precise,* she had written in her report, and then crossed it out, because *too precise* was not a finding, it was a confession of her own fascination.

"Seraphine." Her father's voice pulled her back into the room. "You're hovering."

"I'm waiting, Father. There's a difference." She turned from the window. Magistrate Aldous Voss sat behind his desk the way he sat behind everything—a broad, deliberate man with silver at his temples and hands that moved with the unhurried certainty of someone who had never once been contradicted by circumstance. He wore his authority the way other men wore their coats: fitted to him so long ago that he no longer felt its weight.

"Then wait sitting down. You make me nervous standing there, all your mother's angles and none of her patience."

"I have plenty of patience." She took the chair across from him anyway. "I've been patient through three audits, two inventories, and a customs review that produced nothing but a complaint about the price of Prussian blue."

"And yet." He set down his pen and regarded her over the desk. "The file on the mapmaker's shop remains open."

"The file remains open because the evidence remains inconsistent." She kept her voice level, professional, the voice she used in chambers. "Aurel Nakamura's death was ruled accidental. Drowned at the low quay after dark. But the tide tables don't support it—he fell where the water barely reaches a man's knees, and the coroner's own notes place the bruising above the waterline. Someone should ask why we closed it so quickly."

"Someone did ask. I asked. And the answer came back that old men slip on wet stone, Seraphine, even careful ones." He said it mildly, but she heard the door closing inside the words, the way she always heard it. "Is that why you keep returning to the workshop? Grief for the master? Or curiosity about the apprentice?"

"Professional diligence."

"Ah." He smiled, and it was the smile he'd used since she was small enough to sit on the arm of his chair—the smile that said he saw through her and found her transparency charming rather than inconvenient. "Diligence. Is that what they're calling it when you take tea in the quarter twice in one week?"

Sera felt heat climb her throat and despised it. "You have me watched?"

"I have the city watched. It's the entire function of my office. You'd be astonished what people tell a clerk with a coin purse." He opened a drawer—not the shallow one with the pens, but the deep one on the left, the one she had never seen him open in her presence—and withdrew a document already drafted, already engrossed in the fine secretary's hand, waiting only for signature. "Which brings us to business. Yours, as it happens."

He turned the paper toward her but kept two fingers resting on its top edge, and Sera read upside down the way every child of an official household learns to read: *Writ of Seizure and Forfeiture.* The name beneath it was *Nakamura, Aurel, deceased; premises and effects, Mapmakers' Row, third house from the bridge stair.*

The words seemed to darken as she looked at them.

"On what grounds?" she asked, and was proud of how steady it came out.

"Unlicensed trade in restricted materials. Conspiracy to defraud the customs revenue. Association with persons engaged in smuggling along the northern coast." He recited it like a grocery list. "The evidence has been developing for some months. The letter we received last week confirmed the household's involvement. Names, dates, routes. Very specific."

*The letter.* She had heard whispers of it in the clerks' room—a sealed communication bearing the magistrate's own crest, delivered by courier, naming names. She had assumed it was one more thread in the endless tapestry of harbor gossip. Now it sat underneath a writ with her father's fingers on it.

"Confirmed by whom? A letter arrives accusing a dead man's household and we don't examine the hand that wrote it? We don't verify the seal's provenance? Father, anyone can purchase a press of the Voss crest from any engraver on Cordwainer Street—"

"Seraphine." Just her name, but with the full weight of thirty years of magistracy behind it. "You are auditing workshops, not conducting my investigations. This order is signed, sealed, and scheduled. Thirty days from today, my men will cross the bridge and take possession of the premises, the stock, and all papers therein. The apprentice will be detained for questioning and released if he's clean—which, between us, I suspect he is. Boys that age are ballast, not cargo. It's the adults around him who concern me."

*Released if he's clean.* She held onto those four words like a railing over a drop.

"And the workshop itself? The maps? Master Aurel spent forty years—" She stopped herself. Careful. Too much knowledge was its own kind of confession. "The city loses a working atelier, and for what? A letter nobody can source?"

"For law and order, which is not sentimental work." Her father signed then—three strokes of the pen, date, flourish, wax—and Sera watched the seal press down into the red like a small wound being cauterized. He blotted it, squared the page, and set it in the dispatch tray with the same care he gave to dock fees and drainage petitions. "Thirty days. The quarter will be given notice through the crier. There'll be no surprises, no violence. I run a clean office, whatever you think of me."

"I don't think anything of you," Sera said. "That's the problem. I can't get a single thought of yours to hold still long enough to examine it."

He laughed—genuinely, warmly—and rose to kiss the top of her head as he passed, smelling of tobacco and the lemon oil he used on his desk. "You are your mother's daughter. Go home, or go to your tea, or do whatever it is you do. And Seraphine—" he paused at the door, not turning around, "—stay off Mapmakers' Row until this is finished. It would grieve me to have your name appear in a file."

Then he was gone, and she was alone with the writ.

* * *

She didn't mean to copy the date. Her hand simply did it.

She had stayed in his chamber long after he left, telling herself she was reviewing her audit notes, and her pen had drifted to the margin of her own ledger, and there it was in her own neat secretary's script: the thirtieth day, counted forward from today, written in the same hand that recorded tide heights and customs valuations. A date like any other date. Except that it was the day Elodie Nakamura's world would end, and she had helped measure it out.

She looked at the numerals for a long time.

Then, because she could not explain to herself why, she wet her thumb at the inkwell, pressed it briefly to the wet figures, and transferred them—faint, reversed, a ghost of numbers—to the heel of her left palm, where they sat in the creases like a small brand. A secret written on the body. She curled her fingers over it as though it might smudge away the fact of it, knowing full well that ink on skin was nothing, that the true mark was somewhere deeper, in the place where duty lived, which had begun, very quietly, to taste of betrayal.

She thought of the tea. Two weeks ago, sitting in the workshop among the drying maps while rain ticked on the copper roof, watching Elodie grind pigment with a patience that seemed less like skill than like devotion. He had shown her a coastline—just a coastline, the northern shore rendered in lines so fine they seemed breathed rather than drawn—and she had felt something shift in her chest that she had refused, then and since, to name. He had told her, without telling her, that the atlas mattered. That finishing it mattered. That some promises outlive the people who receive them.

And now she carried the number of days remaining against her own skin.

"You're being ridiculous," she said aloud to the empty chamber. The word *ridiculous* echoed off the ledgers and came back unconvinced.

Because it wasn't ridiculous, was the trouble. Because her father had signed an order on evidence he refused to let her see, sourced from a letter he refused to let her trace, closing a death he refused to let her question. Three refusals, laid end to end, made a shape, and the shape looked like concealment. She had grown up believing her father's office was the straightedge against which the crooked city was measured. She had built herself out of that belief, plank by plank, the way a ship is built—her commission, her precision, her incorruptible little self—all of it fitted to a keel she had never once thought to inspect.

Now she had inspected it. And the wood sounded hollow.

Sera rose, gathered her ledger, and went out through the clerks' room, where Deputy Corvane sat at his high desk sorting papers with his soft white hands. He glanced up as she passed, and smiled with perfect courtesy, and said, "Busy morning, Miss Voss," and something in the smoothness of it—the way his eyes went first to the ledger under her arm, cataloguing it—made her skin prickle. She had known the man for six years and had never once seen him tired, or hurried, or surprised. Men without friction, her mother used to say, are either very good or very bad, and it takes years to learn which.

"Very," Sera said, and kept walking.

In the corridor outside, she stopped by the tall window that faced the harbor and uncurled her fist. The date sat on her palm, faint and blue-black, already beginning to fade at the edges the way all ink fades, all evidence fades, all certainties fade if you handle them enough.

Thirty days.

She thought of Elodie among his maps, unaware, grinding his pigments, keeping his promise to a dead man. She thought of the bridge at dusk, iron and rivets, the fragile stitch between his world and hers. In thirty days her father's men would cross it to take everything he had, and she—she was the office. She was the seal. She had sat in the chair and watched the wax close over the wound and said nothing.

Unless.

The thought arrived whole, fully armed, before she could stop it: *unless she warned him.* Not with words—words were traceable, words were treason—but the way warnings actually traveled in a port city, sideways and wordless, in glances and delays and misplaced files. She was, after all, the most meticulous auditor in the building. She knew exactly how a file could be slowed. How a survey could be delayed. How thirty days could quietly become forty-five, if a certain clerk's schedules happened to conflict.

It would be a small corruption. Her first. The kind that starts all the others.

Sera closed her hand over the fading date and walked down the stairs into the noise of the morning, past the crier just setting out with his bell and his bundle of public notices, past the racks of sealed dispatches bound for every corner of the city—including, she noted with a lurch she couldn't suppress, a fat envelope addressed in the secretary's hand to *the Premises of Nakamura, Mapmakers' Row.*

Notice of seizure. Already moving. Already faster than any delay she could engineer.

She stood in the doorway a moment, the harbor wind pulling at her coat, the date dissolving on her palm, and understood that she had perhaps a day—two at most—before the paper outran her conscience.

She turned toward the bridge.
