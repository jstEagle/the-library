## 10. Burning the Marginalia

The candle had burned down to its last thumb's width before Elodie finally admitted what he was doing.

He was not working. He had told himself he was working—had laid out the fresh vellum for the Ligurian coast, had uncapped the ink, had trimmed three pens to a fine enough point to draw a hairline harbor wall—all of it the liturgy of craft, performed so that he would not have to look at the thing that sat at the edge of the table like a guest who had overstayed his welcome.

The thing was a stack of loose pages.

They were Aurel's marginalia. Elodie had cut them free that afternoon with the bone-handled knife his master had used for trimming quills, one clean stroke at a time, because if they were separate from the plates—if they were only paper among paper—they could not be read as part of anything. That had been the theory. In practice, all he had done was make the atlas honest and the pages dangerous. Now the danger lay in a stack four fingers high on the workbench: routes drawn in red where red should never appear, names written in Aurel's cramped left-slanting hand, dates circled twice when they were certain and once when they were guessed. Thirty years of the master's secret accounting, cut loose and waiting to be dealt with.

Elodie picked up the topmost sheet and held it to the light.

It was the western approaches to Vellamare itself, though you would not know it unless you knew it. There was no coastline here, no soundings, no compass rose. Only a webwork of lines threading between marks that might have been rocks or might have been initials, and along the lower margin, in ink gone brown with age: *Voss — quarterly — see ledger third — not the father.*

*Not the father.* As if the father were the obvious suspect and needed ruling out. As if Aurel had sat at this very bench years ago, pen in hand, and thought carefully about which Voss it was, and decided it mattered to be precise.

Elodie set the page down. His hand was not quite steady.

Outside, the workshop quarter was settling into its night noises—the groan of the drying racks in Marta's yard next door, a dog somewhere near the fish market complaining about the tide, the far-off iron voice of the bridge letting a late cart cross, its planks booming softly under the wheels. He knew these sounds the way he knew his own pulse. For eleven years they had been the sound of safety, or something close to it: the sound of a place where a half-Japanese boy with no surname anyone respected could learn to draw a coastline so true that grown men went quiet looking at it.

Now every sound seemed to arrive pre-emptively, as evidence.

He made himself say it aloud, because Aurel had always said that a fear named in your own voice shrank by half. "I should burn them."

The words fell into the lamplight and lay there. The candle flame leaned, as if listening.

It would be simple. The copper brazier in the corner still held last night's coals, banked under ash, warm enough to catch paper if he fed it slowly. Four fingers of pages. Perhaps twenty minutes' work. And then the workshop contained nothing but an apprentice finishing his dead master's atlas—a sad, admirable, harmless labor. The seizure order, when Sera's copy of it arrived—and it would arrive; she had warned him without warning him, over tea neither of them had drunk half of—would find maps and maps and more maps, beautiful and blameless. They could take the whole room. They would find nothing to hang anyone with.

And Marta's sons would stay free. And the household would stand. And Elodie Nakamura would live to be old, drawing charts for merchants, telling no one about the years when he had carried a murder in a satchel.

It was the reasonable thing. It was, he suspected, the thing Aurel himself would have counseled if Aurel had ever been able to counsel anything without first making a joke of it.

But the master's last wish—

Elodie pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes.

Completion. The word had been given to him not once but twice: spoken hoarse in the hospital room with the harbor light sliding across the ceiling, and then written, after, in the sealed letter he had found folded inside the atlas's false back board, in that same left-slanting hand, steadier than a dying man's should have been. *Finish it, boy. All of it. Every line I drew, keep true. What is in the margins, keep too. A map that hides its own testimony is half a map. Half a map is a lie.*

*Keep too.* The old man had known exactly what he was asking. He had spent thirty years writing down what he saw—the ships that ran dark, the clerks who signed their passes twice, the payments that moved through the magistrate's office like water through gravel—and he had known it would kill him eventually, and he had chosen, with open eyes, to leave the knowing to his apprentice rather than let it die with him.

And now the apprentice was sitting up at midnight with a brazier six feet away, seriously weighing whether to answer that trust with ash.

"No," Elodie said to the empty room. It came out smaller than he wanted. He said it again, firmer. "No."

Because there was another arithmetic underneath the first one, and he could no longer pretend he hadn't seen it. If he burned the margins, the conspiracy survived. Not just survived—flourished, confirmed, safe forever behind a curtain of clean vellum. The man who had bent Aurel's arm behind him—or whatever had actually happened on that wet dock in the dark, the accident the magistrate's men had been so briskly done investigating—would go on signing his passes twice. Marta's sons would be safe from this particular noose, yes, but the rope itself would remain, coiled and patient, and someday it would find another neck, perhaps theirs, perhaps Sera's, perhaps his own. Burning the pages did not buy safety. It bought time, purchased with silence, and silence was the currency the other side traded in. He would simply be paying their toll.

Aurel had taught him that maps were arguments. Every chart took a position: this coast is here, this passage is safe, this world deserves to be known truly. Burn the margins and the atlas became a lie so complete it would never even know itself as one.

Elodie stood, crossed to the brazier, and raked the ash off the coals—not to feed them, but to smother them properly, pressing the shovel down until the orange glow died. Then he closed the grate, as if shutting a door on a temptation that might otherwise wander back in the night.

"All of it," he said quietly. "Every line. Keep true."

* * *

The problem, having refused the easy answer, was that the hard one remained: twenty dangerous pages, and a search coming, and nowhere in a mapmaker's workshop that a searcher wouldn't look.

He walked the room the way he imagined the searchers would walk it, wearing their eyes. They would take the flat files first—the plan chests against the north wall, drawers labeled in Aurel's tidy capitals, COASTS / HARBORS / INLAND / MISC.—and they would go through them thoroughly, because paper was what they had come for. They would shake out the rollers on the rack above the benches. They would leaf through the atlas itself, plate by plate, and if they were halfway clever they would hold the vellum up to a strong light to check for erasures and palimpsests. They would open the jars of gum arabic and look under the lids. They would tip the stool, look under the ink shelf, run a finger along the window frame.

What they would probably not do—and here Elodie stopped, mid-stride, in the middle of his own floor—was examine the rollers themselves.

Map rollers hung on the rack like fat grey sausages, a dozen of them, each a wooden dowel wrapped in layered felt and canvas, used to press a freshly glued chart flat and smooth. Old ones especially were dense things, wound tight through years of reuse, hardened with dried paste, heavy in the hand. Heavy. Elodie picked one up and turned it slowly. The felt had cracked into ridges like bark. You could wind a great deal of thin paper around a dowel before the diameter grew enough to notice—especially if the paper was trimmed close, and who in the world was better at trimming paper close than a mapmaker's apprentice?

He set the roller down and allowed himself, for the first time all night, a small and private smile.

Then he got to work, because sentiment was a luxury and precision was not, and the difference between hiding and being caught was going to be measured in millimeters.

First, the pages had to come apart. Some of the marginalia sheets were pasted onto backing boards—Aurel's habit, to keep the flimsy stock from cockling—and these Elodie steamed loose over the kettle, one at a time, holding each with tongs while the old paste softened and released its grip with a sigh. The steam smelled of flour and age and faintly, underneath everything, of the master himself, whose hands had touched every one of these papers thousands of times. Elodie kept his face very still while he worked. Grief was a door best kept shut during business hours.

Next, trimming. Each sheet went under the blade against the self-heating board, cut down to strips narrow enough to wrap and thin enough to disappear—names and dates preserved, decorative borders sacrificed, every stroke of Aurel's hand kept wherever keeping it did not cost concealment. He worked slowly. A slip here could not be mended; there was no second original. Twice he stopped entirely, hands hovering, because a passage needed reading before cutting, and reading meant understanding, and understanding meant the weight settled back onto his chest where he had tried to set it down.

That was how he learned, around two in the morning, that the atlas's marginalia recorded three separate payments routed through the harbor trust in the year before Aurel's death, each authorized by the same clerk's countersignature, and that Aurel had noted beside the third one, with terrible dry economy: *Hale again. He knows I know.*

Hale. Deputy Magistrate Corvin Hale, who had signed the notice of investigation into Aurel's death within a day of the body being found, and who had pronounced the drowning accidental within three, and whose name Elodie now read in his murdered master's handwriting, circled twice.

The candle had long since been replaced by its neighbor. Elodie sat back on the stool and looked at the word for a long time.

So it was not only smugglers' routes he was guarding. It was the reason the workshop was being seized at all, the reason the questions were moving door to door through the quarter, the reason a letter with a magistrate's crest had arrived naming innocent people. Hale did not want the atlas found. Hale wanted it buried—and everyone who had ever touched it buried with it, tidily, under legitimate process. Which meant that burning the margins would not merely betray Aurel. It would do the deputy's work for him, gratis, in the dark, like a good little accomplice.

"Well," Elodie said to the roller in his hands. "Aren't you popular."

The roller, being a roller, declined to comment. But it accepted its new contents.

He began winding. Strips of Aurel's testimony, edge to edge, snug around the dowel, each layer separated from the next by a turn of plain canvas scrap so the ink faces could not transfer or smear. The paper disappeared into the felt the way a secret disappears into a routine: completely, if the routine is dull enough. When a roller reached its proper girth—he checked each against an unwound twin on the rack, calipers in his head, apprentice's eye trained by ten thousand hours of exactness—he bound it with its cord, dusted it lightly with the pounce that aged everything agreeably, and hung it back among its brothers.

One. Two. Three. The fourth roller took the thinnest stock, the earliest pages, brittle as autumn; he handled those as if they were made of moth wings, which in a sense they were—the wings of a version of his life in which this was all only ever going to be art.

By the fifth, dawn was a grey seam under the shutters, and Elodie's eyes were sandpaper and his back had filed a formal complaint. Five rollers on the rack, hanging in their ordinary row, holding thirty years of murder and money in their bellies. Anyone searching the room would see exactly what the room had always shown: tools, tired and true.

He banked the fire, capped the ink, washed his hands—and stopped, watching brown clouds of it swirl off his fingers and down the drain. Ink under the fingernails, in the whorls, in the creases of his knuckles. Aurel's hands had looked like this. Aurel's left hand, the coroner's report had said, though a man who wrote as much as Aurel wrote carried his stains on the right, and Elodie had noticed, and had said nothing, and the saying-nothing had been sitting in him ever since like a swallowed hook.

Not tonight. Not anymore. Tonight he had chosen: the pages lived, and therefore the truth had a heartbeat, and therefore somebody, sometime, was going to have to carry it across some bridge into daylight. Probably at a cost. Possibly soon.

He dried his hands, pulled the atlas toward him across the bench, and unrolled the Ligurian coast he had abandoned at dusk. The pen waited. The ink was still wet-capped. Somewhere below the window, the quarter was waking—Marta's shutters clattering open, the first gulls arguing over the harbor, and beneath it all the low iron murmur of the bridge taking the morning's first carts, faithful as breath, carrying whatever was put upon it from one shore to the other.

Elodie dipped his pen, set the nib to the vellum at the exact point where he had left off, and began to draw.

He was two inches of coastline into the day when boots rang on the cobbles outside—too many boots, too fast, in step—and a fist hammered on the workshop door with a sound like a verdict beginning.
