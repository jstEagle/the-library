## 11. The Man with the Limp

The morning came in grey and salt, the kind of light that made everything in Vellamare look like a half-finished sketch—outlines present, colors withheld. Elodie had been awake since before dawn, hunched over the third plate of the atlas with his sleeves rolled to the elbow, when he heard the footsteps below.

He knew the footsteps of the quarter the way he knew the grain of the workshop floorboards. Marta's wooden clogs, flat and certain, at half past six. The fishwives' hurrying slippers at seven. Old Benoît's dragging heel at seven-thirty, no earlier, never earlier, because Benoît held that punctuality was a vice of clerks. This was none of these. This was a measured tread with a hitch in it—a step, a drag, a step—a rhythm like a heart with one damaged valve.

Elodie set down his pen. Ink pooled at the nib, darkening, patient.

He did not go to the window. He had learned that much from Master Aurel, who used to say that a mapmaker's first instinct—to look—was also his greatest liability. *We are men who stare at things for a living, boy. Learn to stare without turning your head.* So Elodie stood very still beside the shutter and listened, and through the gap where the wood had warped away from the frame he watched the street below.

The stranger stood in the middle of the lane with his back half-turned, and the first thing Elodie noticed was not his face but his boots. They were good boots—naval issue once, by the brass eyelets and the peculiar square toe—and they had been resoled twice, badly, the second time by someone who had tried to accommodate a leg that would not bend quite right. The man shifted his weight onto the good leg as he raised something to his eye.

A camera. A folding plate camera on a leather strap, black and glossy as a beetle.

The shutter clicked. A small dry sound, like a beetle tapping glass.

Elodie felt the click somewhere behind his sternum. The man was photographing the workshop—the whole façade of it, the copper roof gone green with weather, the signless door, the windows with their panes of old glass that turned the morning grey to the color of weak tea. There was nothing remarkable to photograph. The workshop had stood on that corner for forty years and looked, if anything, poorer each year, its stone streaked with soot from the dye-house next door. Nobody photographed buildings like this. Buildings like this were photographed only when somebody wanted to remember them—or wanted to find them again.

*Step, drag, step.* The man moved along the frontage, unhurried, and took another plate. He angled the camera upward toward the upper window—Elodie's window, the workroom window—and for one long moment the lens seemed to hold him in its small round gaze. Elodie stayed motionless in the shadow of the shutter, though no rational part of him believed the camera could see him through warped wood and old glass. Irrational parts of him believed other things entirely.

The stranger lowered the camera and consulted a notebook. Even at this distance Elodie could see the economy of his movements: a man accustomed to recording, to noting, to filing. Not a policeman. Policemen in Vellamare wore their authority on their belts and in their voices. This man wore his in his patience.

Then Marta came out of the dyehouse with a basket of wet wool on her hip, saw the stranger, and stopped as though she had walked into a wall she hadn't known was there.

Elodie watched her shoulders rise. Watched her set the basket down slowly, deliberately, the way she did when she wanted her hands to be free. Marta the dyer was a broad woman with forearms like mooring rope and hair going iron-grey at the temples, and she had lived in the quarter longer than any three families, and she was afraid. That was the thing that turned Elodie's mouth dry. Not the camera. Not the limp. Marta, afraid, in her own doorway, in her own lane, where she had buried a husband and raised two sons and out-argued every tax collector the city had ever sent.

The stranger approached her. His voice carried up through the thin air, pitched low and courteous, and Elodie could catch only pieces of it—fragments riding the updraft off the harbor.

*—forgive the intrusion, madame—*

*—doing a survey. Architectural. For an insurance concern—*

Marta said something short. Her arms were folded now, the basket abandoned between her feet.

*—the mapmaker's establishment. Such a shame, what happened to the master.* A pause. *You knew him well?*

Another pause, longer. Then the stranger said the sentence that Elodie would carry for the rest of his days, that he would hear again in a hundred different rooms and a hundred different voices, always with the same cold clarity:

*And the apprentice. The Japanese boy. Is he still here?*

Elodie's hand found the edge of the worktable without his asking it to. He gripped the wood until the grain printed itself into his palm.

Half-Japanese, he might have corrected, if correction were a thing one could shout through a window at a stranger with a camera. Half, and born four streets from where the man stood, and apprenticed eleven years, and christened in the harbor church because Aurel had insisted that a boy ought to have a godfather even if he had no country. But the man had not said *half*. Men like this never said *half*. The word *half* acknowledged the other parent, and the other parent, and the other half of Elodie's life—the quarter where he had been *gaijin* to the fishermen's children and *the foreigner's pickling* to the sailors—was precisely the half that such men preferred to amputate with a syllable.

Below, Marta answered. Elodie couldn't hear the words, but he heard the shape of them: flat, unhelpful, final. The stranger inclined his head as though she had given him something valuable anyway. He produced a card from his breast pocket and offered it. Marta did not take it. After a moment the man placed it on top of her basket, stepped back, and stood regarding the workshop once more—not the building now, but the windows, all of them, one after another, in sequence, the way a man counts doors in a corridor he intends to walk down later.

Then he turned, and Elodie saw his face.

It was an ordinary face, which was somehow worse. Fifty perhaps, sun-browned, with a short greying beard trimmed with naval precision and eyes the pale washed-out blue of a chart of deep water. Nothing cruel in it. Nothing kind either. It was a face that had been emptied of expression the way one empties a drawer before moving house. And beneath all of it, unmistakable now that he moved, the limp: left leg stiff, swinging slightly outward, the signature of an old injury taken below decks where the beams were low and the sea gave no warning.

Naval. Definitely naval, or once. Elodie filed it away as his master had taught him to file everything—by observation, by inference, by the patient assembly of details into coastlines.

*Step, drag, step.* The stranger went down the lane toward the harbor road, and the sound of him diminished until it was only another morning noise, indistinguishable from cart wheels and gulls, and then gone.

* * *

Marta came up the stairs twenty minutes later. She did not knock—she never knocked—but she paused on the threshold of the workroom, and that pause told Elodie everything before she spoke.

"You heard."

"Most of it." He was still standing by the window, though the lane below was empty now except for a cat investigating the place where the camera case had rested. "Insurance survey," he said. "For a building nobody insures."

"For a building nobody *photographs*." Marta crossed the room and picked up the card where Elodie had retrieved it from her basket and set it on the table. It was heavy stock, cream-colored, blank on one side. On the other, in a small clerk's hand: **M. HALLORAN & ASSOCIATES, MARINE SURVEYORS**. An address in the harbor district. No given name. No seal.

"M. Halloran," Elodie read aloud.

"That's what it says."

"That isn't who he is."

"No," Marta agreed. She lowered herself onto the stool by the drying rack, which creaked under her, and for a moment she looked older than Elodie had ever seen her, older than the quarter itself. "I've been thinking since he turned the corner. Thinking hard. And I'll tell you what I'm ashamed of, Elodie Nakamura, so listen. I'm ashamed because I lied to him smooth as silk—I told him the apprentice ran off to the fishing fleets last spring, that the workshop's been shut since the master died—and I lied well, I've had practice lying to men like that—and the whole time I was doing it, some part of me was remembering that I didn't lie for Aurel. When they asked me about Aurel, I said what everyone said. Accident. Slip on the wet stones. I said it because it was easier, and because my boys—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "Because I have reasons. I've always had reasons."

Elodie sat down across from her. Between them lay the atlas, closed, hidden under a linen cloth as it always was now, as though cloth could make a thing invisible rather than merely unseen.

"He asked about me specifically," Elodie said. "Not the workshop. Me."

"Yes."

"'The Japanese boy.' As if you'd know where to find me. As if I were an item in an inventory." He turned the card over, then over again. Cream stock, clerk's hand. He made himself put it down. "Marta. Insurance men don't ask after apprentices. Surveyors don't care who works in a building, only what state the roof is in. Whoever sent him wants to know two things. Whether the workshop is watched, and whether I can be found."

"And which answer did we give him?"

"The wrong ones, probably. I've been up here all morning with a lamp lit. If he looked at the right minute, he saw the light under the shutters."

Marta swore, quietly, comprehensively, in the language of the fish market.

"There's more," she said finally. "He wasn't the first. Two weeks ago—no, three—a woman came asking at the doors. Said she was collecting for a seamen's charity. Polite, well-spoken, took notes. Fen at the chandlery told her what she wanted to hear and thought nothing of it. But yesterday Fen mentioned it to me, and I asked around, and there is no seamen's charity registered in Vellamare under that name. Someone has been walking this quarter, door by door, making a map of us." She smiled without any humor in it. "Making a map, do you see. Of the people who might notice things. Who might talk. You're not the only one being counted, boy. We all are. We just haven't seen the ledger yet."

Elodie looked at the covered shape of the atlas on the table. Three weeks ago it had been a grief and a duty. Two weeks ago it had become, in the lamplight of those terrible marginalia, something else—routes, dates, initials, payments recorded in a cipher of Aurel's own devising, evidence pressed flat and inked. Now the evidence had begun to walk. It had grown a naval limp and a cream calling card and a polite woman with a fictional charity, and it was moving through the quarter like tide through a harbor, silent, filling every channel.

"I should go," he said. It came out smaller than he intended. "There are ships out of the east quay. By tonight I could—"

"Finish that sentence and I'll box your ears," Marta said, with such ferocity that a gull outside startled off the roof. "Eleven years I've watched you grow up in that window, Elodie. Eleven years you've mended my fence and taught my Tomas his letters and sat at my table every Sunday like a son I didn't have to birth. Aurel didn't die so you could run. And more to the point"—her voice dropped, roughened—*"you don't run from men who photograph houses. Running confirms the inventory. You stay, and you let them count you, and you watch them back."*

It was the same doctrine, Elodie thought absurdly, that Aurel had preached about fieldwork. *Never flee a coastline you haven't finished surveying.* He almost laughed. Grief did that, arrived at strange doors wearing strange clothes.

"Watch them back," he repeated.

"That's what neighbors are for. That's what we've always been for." Marta rose, took up her basket, and paused at the door. Something in her face shifted—the guilt from before, banked but not extinguished, glowing under the ash. "If you need hiding, the cellar connects. You know that. Whatever comes through that door, it comes to all of us or none of us. Remember that, whatever else happens."

She went down the stairs. *Clump, clump, clump,* sure and flat, the most comforting sound in the world.

* * *

He worked through the afternoon because work was the only prayer he knew. The fourth plate demanded the outer shoals, and he built them line by line, stipple by stipple, letting the craft quiet the alarm in his chest the way sand quiets water. Twice he caught himself listening for the limp on the lane below. Both times it was only Benoît, or the wind worrying a loose sign.

At dusk the light came sideways and gold, the hour Aurel had called *the honest hour*, when shadows confessed true heights and every coastline looked like itself and nothing else. Elodie opened the shutter to check the sky, and that was when he saw the second man.

Not Halloran—a younger man, dressed plainly, leaning against the harbor wall across the lane with a newspaper he was not reading. He had been there, Elodie realized, since at least midday; he had the particular stillness of hired watching, the studied idleness of a man paid by the day to be furniture. Their eyes met for half a second across the golden air. The man did not look away. He raised his newspaper, unhurried, and turned a page.

*Being seen.* Elodie stood in the open shutter and let himself be seen, and understood, with the calm of deep exhaustion, that this was the first time in eleven years anyone in power had considered him worth seeing at all. The invisible apprentice—the half-blood, the foreigner's son, the boy who mixed inks and carried parcels and signed nothing. For years he had treasured that invisibility the way other men treasured gold. Now he saw its price. Invisible men could be erased without ceremony. Invisible men could slip on wet stones, and the whole city would nod and say *accident*, because no one had ever bothered to learn his face.

Well. They knew his face now. Or his category, at least: *the Japanese boy.*

Let them look, then. Let them come with cameras and charities and newspapers they didn't read. He had something they wanted badly enough to spend money and patience on, and a thing desired is a thing feared, and a thing feared—

A thing feared has power. Aurel had written that once, in the margin of an old hydrographic chart, in the cipher Elodie had spent three sleepless nights breaking. He hadn't understood it then. He understood it now, standing in the honest hour with the watcher across the lane and the atlas warm under its cloth at his back.

He closed the shutter. Lit the lamp. Sat down.

And it was only then, trimming the wick, that he remembered the detail he had missed all day, the small wrongness that had brushed past him that morning like a fish under a boat. The stranger's notebook. When the man had consulted it, before speaking to Marta, he had not been checking directions or descriptions.

He had been checking a list of names. And the name at the top of the page, glimpsed for less than a heartbeat in the slanting light—written not in a clerk's hand but in the tight, downward-slanting script of official correspondence—was a name Elodzie knew. A name that appeared in the atlas margins fourteen times, always beside the largest payments.

The name began with a V.
