## 12. Sera's Inheritance

The magistrate's house kept its accounts the way a church keeps relics: behind locked doors, in ordered rows, touched only by hands deemed worthy. Sera Voss had been deemed worthy since she was nineteen, when her father first set the household ledgers before her and said, with the offhand generosity of a man granting a small kingdom, *You have an eye for figures, my dove. Better than mine.* It had been the proudest moment of her life—prouder than her commission, prouder than the medal she kept in a drawer because displaying it felt like boasting. Her father trusted her with the true arithmetic of their lives. Not the performed arithmetic of dinners and carriages and the quarterly donations to the Cathedral Restoration Fund, but the real sums: what the house cost, what the servants were owed, what remained beneath the floorboards of their respectability.

For six years she had balanced those books without ever once doubting them.

Now she sat in her father's study on a Tuesday evening in late October, with the lamp turned low and the ledgers open before her, and the numbers would not sit still.

It had begun as diligence, nothing more. The workshop audit—Elodie Nakamura's audit—had been formally reassigned to her after her father signed the seizure order, which meant that in thirty days she would stand in that ink-smelling room above the harbor and catalog every map, every roller, every pot of pigment belonging to a dead man's estate. She wanted to do it properly. More than properly; she wanted to do it *perfectly*, with a precision that would make her father say again, *You have an eye, my dove.* And so she had spent three evenings cross-referencing Aurel Vaas's known commissions against port records, tide tables, customs receipts—the dry sediment of a cartographer's legitimate business.

She had found nothing. That was what troubled her first. A master mapmaker with Vaas's reputation should have left a richer trail of sales: charts sold to shipping houses, coastal surveys commissioned by the Admiralty, decorative maps for the parlors of merchant families. Instead the record was thin, almost ascetic, as though the man had earned his living from air. Either his business ledger had never existed or it had been removed. Neither possibility sat comfortably.

So last night she had done something she had never done before. She had taken the household ledgers—not the ones she was given, but the older ones, the ones from the years before her mother died, bound in cracked green leather and stored in the bottom drawer of her father's desk, under a false bottom she had discovered at eleven years old while hunting for birthday presents—and she had carried them to her own rooms like contraband pressed against her ribs.

She told herself it was procedure. Cross-referencing. An auditor's thoroughness.

She did not quite believe herself, and had not believed herself since the day she stood in Elodie Nakamura's workshop and watched him draw a coastline freehand, without measurement, from memory, and felt something turn over in her chest that she had no honest name for.

* * *

The study smelled of pipe smoke and lemon oil, even though her father was at the Assembly until midnight. Sera had let herself in with her own key, lit two candles instead of the gas, because candlelight made fewer demands and forgave more, and spread the old ledgers across the blotter in chronological order. 1904 through 1911. Eight volumes. Each entry in her father's small, upright hand—except where it wasn't.

That was the second thing that troubled her. Beginning in 1907, certain entries appeared in a different script: narrower, more slanted, the numerals cramped. Not her father's writing. Not the clerk Ferren's either—she knew Ferren's loops the way she knew her own reflection. This third hand appeared perhaps six times a year, always on the debit side, always in round sums, always with the same laconic notation: *harbor maintenance*. Or *channel survey*. Or *pilotage fees*.

Harbor maintenance. Her father was not the harbormaster. He had no more responsibility for channel dredging than she had for the moon. Those costs belonged to the Port Authority, itemized and published, and the Port Authority had never once billed the magistrate's office for them—she checked, that very afternoon, pulling three years of published schedules from the municipal archive and running her finger down columns until the lamplight blurred. No billings. No transfers. No line anywhere in the public record corresponding to the private one.

Which meant the entries were lies. Small, tidy, repeated lies, entered in a stranger's hand, paid out of her father's accounts, for seven years.

Sera sat very still and listened to the house. The clock in the hall. The settling of pipes. Cook moving somewhere below, singing half a hymn under her breath. Everything ordinary. Everything continuing as though the world had not quietly changed shape around her.

She took a fresh sheet of paper and began, with hands that wanted to shake and were forbidden to, to copy out every anomalous entry: date, amount, notation. When she finished there were forty-one of them. Forty-one payments between 1907 and the spring of this year, totaling a sum that made her eyes sting if she thought about it too long—more than her father's official salary for those same years, more than the house, more than her education and her brother's commission combined.

Then she did what she was trained to do. She looked for pattern.

Amounts first: they clustered. Three hundred florins most often, sometimes four-fifty, occasionally six. Never odd figures, never haggled-looking sums. Tariffs, she thought. Rates. As though someone were paying by the shipment.

Dates next—and here her breath actually stopped, here she had to set down the pen and press her palms flat against the desk until the trembling ran itself out—because the dates were not random either. They came in runs of three or four within a fortnight, then silence for months. Runs in March and October mostly. Spring and autumn. Seasons of favorable tides, seasons when revenue cutters were rotated to the northern stations and the southern approaches went thin-patrolled. She knew that because she had just spent three evenings reading tide tables and patrol schedules for a murdered mapmaker's audit, and the knowledge lay in her head like loaded shot.

Spring and autumn. Three hundred florins a run. Harbor maintenance that did not exist.

And then—one entry, dated the twelfth of March, 1912, in the cramped third hand, the notation not *harbor maintenance* this time but something worse, something that seemed to look up at her from the page with her father's authority behind it:

*Vaas — settled.*

Settled. Paid. Closed. Six weeks before Aurel Vaas was pulled dead from the water beneath the iron bridge with his skull broken on a piling and the coroner's verdict of accidental fall delivered in less time than it took to write it.

* * *

She did not remember leaving the study, but she must have walked, because she found herself at the kitchen door with her coat still unbuttoned and Cook staring at her with a wooden spoon in one hand.

"Miss Sera? You've gone white as flour, child."

"I'm fine," Sera heard herself say, in a voice that sounded borrowed. "A headache. The lamps."

Cook, who had known her since she was born and who was not fooled by magistrates' daughters any more than by weather, poured her a cup of chicory coffee and set it down without comment, and Sera sat at the scrubbed kitchen table among the domestic smells of bread and onions and tried to think the unthinkable in orderly increments, because order was all she had.

Possibility the first: her father was being blackmailed. Someone had evidence of some old sin and was bleeding him in installments disguised as harbor fees. It was almost comforting. But blackmailers did not wait seven years, and blackmailers did not write *Vaas — settled* in their victim's ledger, and her father—who feared scandal more than drowning, who polished his reputation the way other men polished boots—would have confessed rather than pay, or fought rather than confess. She could not picture him cornered. She could picture him calculating.

Possibility the second: the entries were clerical. Someone else used his accounts, forged his hand, and he never noticed. Seven years. Forty-one entries. Her father noticed when the wine cellar was short two bottles. No.

Possibility the third arrived without permission and simply stayed, the way the worst things do: her father knew. Her father had been paying. Payments timed to smuggling seasons, routed through channels he controlled, recorded in a code of blandness so ordinary no auditor had ever thought to question it—no auditor except the daughter he himself had trained to have an eye for figures. And when a mapmaker drew coastlines too carefully, marked routes too precisely, kept an atlas that could name dates and sums and landing coves—when that mapmaker became a threat worth *settling*—

Her father had signed the seizure order for the workshop twelve days ago. Effective in thirty days. She had copied the date onto her palm that morning like a girl copying a sweetheart's initials, and now the ink ghost of it rose on her skin as though the number itself were ashamed.

He wasn't seizing the atlas to find evidence. She understood it suddenly, completely, the way one understands a wave has broken only when it is already past. He was seizing it to *bury* evidence. To catalogue it, box it, stamp it closed, and shelve it in the archive annex where no one would read marginalia for fifty years. And she—his eye for figures, his dove—was to conduct the inventory herself. He had assigned her. Of course he had. Who else could be trusted to see exactly what needed seeing and nothing more?

She put her face in her hands and breathed the smell of onions and bread and let herself, for exactly one minute, be a daughter grieving a father who might still be innocent. Then the minute ended, because she had trained herself since girlhood that grief without action was just self-indulgence wearing better clothes, and she went back upstairs and did the thing she would later recognize as the moment her old life ended.

She compared the dates against the map.

Not the true atlas—she had no access to it, not yet—but her audit notes. Three evenings of cross-referencing had left her with a list of Aurel Vaas's documented coastal surveys: coves charted in March, soundings taken in October, anchorages measured and named in his neat italic hand. She laid her transcription of the ledger entries beside that list and matched them, one by one, like a woman dealing cards to an invisible opponent.

Survey at Gullwater Cove, March 1909. Ledger payment, March 1909.
Soundings off the Salt Quarries, October 1910. Payment, October 1910.
Anchorage survey at the Teeth, spring 1911. Payment. Payment. Payment.

Every route the mapmaker had drawn, her father's money had followed within weeks. He hadn't been mapping smugglers' roads for the law. He'd been drawing invoices. And somewhere in that workshop above the harbor, in margins and rollers and gold-leafed plates, every transaction of seven years was waiting in ink for a reader brave enough—or ruined enough—to look.

* * *

Dawn found her still at the desk, the candles burned to pools, the ledgers closed and stacked in their original order because whatever she did next, she would not leave disorder behind her as evidence of her passage. That much of her upbringing held. She was still a Voss. She suspected she would be a Voss for the rest of her life, the way one is scarred for it.

She thought about going to him. Waking him, laying the pages on his breakfast table, saying *Explain this to me, Papa, tell me I'm wrong, tell me there is a word for these sums that isn't what I think it is.* She rehearsed it standing at the window while the harbor turned grey-gold below, gulls working the morning water, the iron bridge catching first light along its spine like a blade laid flat. In the rehearsal he explained everything. In the rehearsal she believed him. In the rehearsal they wept and were restored, because she loved him, because she had loved him for twenty-four years and love does not release its grip merely because the floor gives way—it claws at the wreckage, it digs, it insists on survivors.

But the rehearsal kept failing at one detail. She could not make his face answer.

And underneath the failure, quiet as silt, another understanding settled: if she confronted him, she would warn him. Whatever else was true, her father was meticulous, and meticulous men who feel the ground move respond by moving faster. If she showed him his own ledgers, anything not yet hidden would be hidden by nightfall. Any witness not yet handled would be handled. Perhaps even a certain workshop, a certain apprentice boy with ink to his knuckles and a dead master's promise in his hands—

She stopped that thought and examined it, because it had arrived suspiciously fast, suspiciously warm. Was she protecting the investigation, or protecting the investigator? Did it matter? Could it be both, the way a bridge is both sides at once?

The clock struck five. Somewhere across the harbor, in the workshop quarter, a chimney would be breathing its first smoke. She imagined him at his table already—she had learned in one afternoon that he was the sort who worked before the world woke—bent over vellum in candlelight, unaware that the daughter of the man who had signed away his home had spent the night discovering that her inheritance was built on the same crime his master had died recording.

They were entangled, then. Her family and his. Not by conspiracy—she refused, yet, to go that far—but by money, by dates, by the terrible arithmetic of matched columns. His atlas could ruin her father. Her father's office could erase him. And she stood precisely at the hinge between them, holding keys to both doors.

Sera dressed in her commission uniform because it was the only armor she owned, pinned her collar straight, and gathered the transcribed pages into her document case—forty-one entries, seven years, one word in her father's hand's stead that she had copied three times before she could make her pen form it calmly: *settled*.

There was one place the truth might be read plainly, and it was not in her father's study. There was a workshop above the dyer's yard where a young man drew coastlines from memory, and an atlas that was not art but testimony, and she was, after all, the officer assigned to audit it. Thirty days until seizure. She saw no reason the audit could not begin unofficially. She saw, in fact, a dozen urgent reasons why it must.

She paused once at the front door, her hand on the latch, and looked back at the stairs leading up to her father's room, where he slept the sleep of a man whose ledgers balanced.

"Papa," she said softly, to no one, a word that was half farewell and half warning.

Then she stepped out into the October morning, crossed the garden wet with the season's first real rain, and set her course toward the bridge—the old iron span that linked the city to the quarter where everything she had believed was coming apart, one rivet, one entry, one impossible date at a time.

She did not yet know that the man with the limp had been asking after the Japanese boy at the dyer's door. She did not know that the workshop had already been photographed, measured, marked. She knew only that the floor of her life had opened beneath her, and that the only solid thing left to stand on was paper—and she was walking toward it.
