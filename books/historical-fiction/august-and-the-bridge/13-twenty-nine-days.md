## 13. Twenty-Nine Days

The candle had burned down to its last thumb's width when Elodie Nakamura finally admitted to himself that his hand was shaking.

He had been working since the bells rang sext, nineteen hours past, and the workshop around him had long since stopped being a room and become a pool of amber light with walls he never looked at. The great table before him was Master Aurel's table, scarred with thirty years of pins and pen-knives, its oak gone soft as bread at the edges where generations of palms had leaned. On it lay the atlas—not the decoy, not yet; that would come later, when his hands were steadier or his desperation greater—but the true one, the one that was killing him by inches, opened to the northern approaches of the harbor.

The coastline there was wrong.

He had known it for perhaps an hour. Known it the way a man knows a splinter has gone in past the point of pulling—first as an itch of wrongness, then as certainty, then as shame. The shore running from the salt-works to the old powder jetty should have curved like a drawn bow, a long concave sweep that Master Aurel had walked him along twice in better days, pointing out where the mud gave way to shingle and where the smugglers' boats could lie up against the ebb. Instead, under his tired eyes and tireder hand, it bowed outward. It bulged like a cheek with a stone in it. It was, he realized with a horror that arrived slowly and then all at once, the coastline of some other place entirely—a coast he had invented because his body was still drawing while his mind had gone home to sleep hours ago.

He put down the pen.

The nib clicked against the inkwell's rim, a small sound enormous in the silence, and Elodie sat very still and let the truth of it settle over him the way damp settles over stone. Nineteen days of work on this plate alone. Nineteen days of survey notes cross-referenced against Aurel's marginalia, of soundings copied from the master's own ledgers, of headlands built up in washes of sepia so fine they seemed to breathe. And he had ruined it in a single evening because he had told himself, as he always told himself now, that speed mattered more than beauty, that the seizure order was coming like weather, that finished and flawed would save them where unfinished and perfect would not.

His master's voice came back to him across the year since the funeral, dry as the dust in the rollers' cradles: *A map that lies is worse than no map, boy. A map that lies gets people drowned.*

"I know," Elodie said aloud, to the empty workshop, and heard how young his voice sounded. "I know."

* * *

He did not fix it. That was the first discipline, and the hardest. He wanted to—he wanted desperately to scrape the error away with a blade, to work the vellum back to white and pretend the night had not happened—but scraping left ghosts, and ghosts under ink showed through in raking light like bruises under skin. Anyone holding the plate to a window would see the mend, and anyone who saw the mend would wonder what else had been mended, and the whole atlas would begin to whisper *forgery, forgery* in the ears of the men who would one day hold it up as testimony. If it was to be evidence—if it was to stand in whatever hall they brought it to and speak for Master Aurel and for Marta and her sons and for Elodie himself—it could bear no whispers.

So he cut the plate free of its guards with the bone-handled knife, and set it aside face-down, and did not look at it again, and began instead to stretch fresh calfskin.

The skin had been soaking in the stone trough since morning, and it came out cool and heavy as a drowned thing. He stretched it over the frame with the practiced cruelty of long habit—wetted, thonged, tightened, pounced smooth with pumice until it took the candlelight like the inside of a shell—and while he worked he let himself think, for the first time in days, about time.

Twenty-nine days. He had copied the number onto the corner of his drafting slate three nights ago, and beneath it the date of the seizure, and beneath that the date the bridge was condemned to fall, one day after, so that the whole arithmetic of his life now hung there like a merchant's tally. Twenty-nine days to finish forty-one plates. Even working as he had been working—which was to say, wrongly, ruinously, at the pace of a man being chased—it was not possible. Working as he ought, at the pace of a craftsman, it was laughably impossible. And yet the impossibility changed nothing, because the alternative was to stop, and stopping had a shape he could not bear: the atlas seized half-made, Aurel's last years carted off in an evidence box, the marginalia read by clerks who would understand none of it, or worse, understood all of it and buried it.

He thought of Master Aurel's hands. He thought of them more often than he meant to, and always the same way—the way they had looked on the slab at the deadhouse, laid palm-upward, patient, waiting to be claimed. Ink stains on the left hand. Only the left. A right-handed man does not stain only his left hand unless someone else held his wrist, and Elodie had carried that knowledge for a year now the way he carried everything: silently, close to the chest, folded small enough to fit in a satchel.

Twenty-nine days. He pulled the first thong tight.

* * *

Dawn came into the workshop the way it always did, grudgingly, through windows fogged with salt haze, turning the copper roof-plates above into a dull rose glow before the light ever reached the tables. By that hour Elodie had the new skin stretched and sized, and had transferred the graticule—every line of it checked twice against the master's field books, because if the frame was true the drawing might follow it home—and he allowed himself the extravagance of washing his face in the yard cistern and eating half a loaf Marta had left on the sill, stale but real, with a smear of her good blackberry preserve gone slightly sugary at the edges. He ate standing up, looking at the bridge.

From the yard you could see just its spine above the warehouse roofs: the iron arch of it, black with a century of coal smoke, carrying the quarter's whole life across the harbor mouth to the city proper. Men were already crossing it, small and early, carts and heads and shoulders moving in both directions. In twenty-nine days it would be rubble on the seabed, and the workshop quarter would be an island of suspects with no way off except the ferry, and the ferry would be watched. He had done the sums on the slate for that too. The sums said: carry everything. The sums also said: carry nothing heavy. Everything and nothing, and one pair of shoulders between them. He chewed the stale bread and made himself look at the bridge until he had looked at it enough, which was never.

Then he went back inside and began the coastline again.

* * *

This time he did it properly, and doing it properly was its own peculiar torment, because every careful stroke cost him minutes he could feel draining away like the candle he refused to light in daylight. He worked from Aurel's field notes first, copying the master's own measurements without interpretation—let Aurel draw this coast, even posthumously, even through Elodie's borrowed hand. The bow-curve of the shore came back slowly, point by fixed point: the salt-works slipway, the leaning elm that served as a daymark, the shingle bar that showed at half-tide. He inked the low-water line in a hair-fine stroke, breathing out as the pen traveled, the way Aurel had taught him in his first month, when he had been eleven and furious at being taught to breathe instead of draw. *The line knows when you're afraid,* the old man had said. *Ink is honest. It's the only honest thing in Vellamare.*

By late afternoon the coast stood clean and true, and something in Elodie's chest unclenched by a fraction, and in that unclenching he found the exhaustion waiting for him, patient as a creditor. It sat down inside him and began to collect. His eyes kept trying to double the lines. The sounds of the quarter came to him wrapped in wool—the dyer's mallets next door, gulls, a child shouting—and he could not always say how long each sound had been going on.

He permitted himself one indulgence. He turned to the margin.

Aurel's marginalia ran along the plate's lower edge in a cipher of the master's own devising, innocent-looking to any clerk who might seize it—little flourishes, tide-tables, a Latin motto about the faithfulness of seas—but underneath the flourish, decoded, it named names. Tonight, checking his work against the notes, Elodie's eye snagged on a symbol he had seen a hundred times and never truly looked at: a small anchor followed by three strokes and the abbreviated word *cust.* Custos. Keeper. Warden. He had assumed it marked a customs station, and mostly it did. But this anchor sat beside the powder jetty, where there was no customs station and never had been, and the three strokes matched the three payments he had found flagged elsewhere in the ledgers—payments that climbed the coast like stepping stones toward the city and its magistracy.

He wrote the symbol down on a scrap, then looked at the scrap with distaste—at himself for needing to write it down, at the weakness it confessed. His memory had been the one thing about himself he had always trusted; memory was what you carried when you could carry nothing else. But lately the numbers crowded so thick, the dates and sums and tides, that he feared one slipping loose and taking others with it.

He burned the scrap in the candle flame and watched the curl of ash settle on the copper tray.

* * *

Night fell, and with it the fog off the harbor, and Elodie lit two candles instead of one, which was profligacy, and positioned them in the reflector frames Aurel had built from polished tin, which was thrift, and the compromise between the two felt like the most honest accounting he had done all day.

He worked. The second coastline grew under his hand in the small bright world the reflectors made, and because he was working properly now, the work itself began to do what work does at its best: it carried him. The pen moved and the shore existed. He washed the sea in graded blues, laying the shallow water pale against the land and deepening it seaward until the harbor floor seemed to slope away beneath the paper's surface. He drew the powder jetty with its pilings green with weed. He lettered the soundings in the master's italic hand, which he had stolen years ago and made his own, so that when the atlas was finished the two of them would be indistinguishable in it, which was the point, which had always been the point. *Signed with both masters' names*, he had promised himself once, in a moment of sentimentality he usually disallowed. He disallowed it again now. Sentiment slowed the pen.

Somewhere past midnight, his shoulder seized.

It happened without drama—a hot wire of pain drawn suddenly tight from his neck to his elbow, and the pen skidded a quarter-inch through the wash he was laying over the shingle bar, and the wash puddled, and the puddle dried darker than its surroundings, and there it was: a shadow on the water, exactly the size and shape of a tired man's failure.

Elodie sat back and looked at it for a long time.

He did not swear. Swearing took breath, and breath was needed for other things. He simply looked at the blot and felt the whole weight of the twenty-nine days press down on him at once—the impossibility of the schedule, the ruined plate lying face-down like a corpse he'd hidden under the floorboards, his shaking hand, his aching eye, the letter upstairs in its hiding place with the magistrate's crest on it, cold as a coin left out overnight. And beneath all of it, quieter than all of it, the question he had been refusing to ask because asking it was the same as answering it: *what if I am not good enough, or fast enough, or strong enough, and it all burns because of me?*

He got up, and his knees cracked like kindling, and he went and stood at the window a while in the dark.

The fog had thinned. Across the black water the city showed its lights, warm and many, the lamplit streets climbing the hill to the citadel where justice was administered by men who signed letters with their own crests and murdered mapmakers with other men's hands. Somewhere up there Sera Voss was keeping whatever vigil she kept, and he made himself not think about her, and failed, and turned back to the table.

He scraped the blot. There was no help for it—scraping a wash is different from scraping a line; done delicately, in layers, with the blade nearly flat and the pumice dust dampened, it can be done invisibly, and Elodie Nakamura at four in the morning, with his hands steadied by despair into perfect calm, was as delicate as he would ever be. He rebuilt the wash in five passes instead of two, letting each dry, and when it was done the shingle bar lay under clean blue water and no clerk alive would find the seam.

But he had lost two hours. He entered it in his mental ledger—two hours, plus the nineteen of the ruined night, plus the day itself—twenty-nine days minus everything, and the sum came up short, and it would come up shorter every morning from now until the seizure, and no craft in the world could make the arithmetic kind.

He blew out one candle and kept working by the other, and did not sleep, and did not consider sleeping, because sleep was a country he no longer held a passport to.

* * *

Gray light again. The loaf's other half. The cistern, colder than yesterday, or his hands hotter.

He was laying the title cartouche for the plate—the plain, official-seeming frame that would fool a seizing clerk into cataloguing it as mere coastal survey, harboring the coded margin like a knife wrapped in a napkin—when he heard the door.

Not Marta's knock, which was three quick raps and a pause, like a wren on a rail. Not Sera's, which hardly existed, a knuckle's touch, apologetic. This was a single knock, slow, deliberate, delivered with the confidence of a man who expected doors to open, and it fell into the workshop's morning silence with a weight that made Elodie's scalp tighten before his mind had formed a single thought.

He stood, and slid the true atlas into the map roller behind the false wall panel in one motion worn smooth by rehearsal, and left the decoy pages visible on the table where they belonged, and crossed the floor, and opened the door.

A man stood in the fog. Broad through the shoulders, gray at the temples, dressed with the expensive plainness of someone who did not wish to be remembered. He leaned his weight on a walking stick of dark wood—not a stick for fashion; a leg beneath it that did not trust itself, a sailor's leg, a naval leg—and he looked at Elodie with pale, unhurried eyes, and smiled the way winter smiles at a late harvest.

"Good morning," the man said pleasantly. "You must be the Japanese boy."
