## 14. An Offer of Passage

The knock came at half past nine, when the fog off the harbor had thickened enough to soften every sound in the workshop quarter and Elodie had convinced himself that no one would come at all that night.

He knew before he opened the door who it would be. There was a rhythm to the knock—two raps, a pause long enough to seem polite, then a third—that belonged to no neighbor, no client, no courier. It was the knock of a man who had practiced not seeming impatient.

Elodie set down his pen. The line he had been drawing—the northern lip of the Serrat Channel, its final curve—sat unfinished on the drafting table, wet ink gleaming like a vein under lamplight. He looked at it for a moment longer than necessary. Then he crossed the room, lifted the bar, and opened the door onto the fog.

The man with the limp stood on the threshold with his hat in his hands, as though he had come on a social call. He was older than Elodie had first judged in the street—fifty, perhaps older still, with the weathered brown of a face that had spent years under foreign suns, and eyes the pale grey of gunmetal that never quite settled on any one thing. His left leg bore the weight; the right swung forward a half-beat behind it, stiff at the knee, the particular gait of an old break badly set or a joint shattered by something larger than a fall.

"Master Nakamura," the man said. His Vellamare accent was nearly perfect. Nearly. A vowel here and there sat a fraction too far back in the throat, learned rather than grown. "Forgive the hour."

"You have me at a disadvantage," Elodie said, keeping his voice even. "You know my name. I don't know yours."

"Captain is what people called me, once." He said it lightly, the way a man sets down a heavy object and pretends it weighed nothing. "May I come in? The fog is damp, and I've climbed more stairs than my leg appreciates."

Every instinct Elodie had—the ones Master Aurel had sharpened, the ones the last months had honed to a fine edge—told him to close the door. But the man had already seen the workshop. He had photographed it from the street ten days ago with a camera on a wooden tripod while Marta stood in her doorway wringing her apron. Refusing him entry now would only confirm whatever he already knew, and would tell Elodie nothing in return.

So Elodie stepped aside.

* * *

The man limped across the floorboards with the unhurried ease of someone cataloguing everything he passed: the drying racks hung with maps in various states of completion, the smell of gum arabic and lamp oil, the great drafting table beneath its green-shaded lamp, the closed cabinet where the atlas slept wrapped in oilcloth inside map rollers disguised as shipping tubes. His gaze touched each thing and moved on without lingering. That was what frightened Elodie most—not the looking, but the discipline of the not-lingering.

"You work late," the man observed.

"The work doesn't keep daylight hours."

"No," the man agreed. "It wouldn't." He lowered himself into the visitor's chair by the stove, right leg extended stiffly before him, and set his hat on his knee. Up close, Elodie could see the faint white seam of an old scar running from beneath his collar to his jawline, as if someone had once tried to open his throat and changed their mind halfway. "I'll be brief, since I can see you're mid-line. The Serrat Channel, isn't it? The northern approach."

The cold that moved through Elodie then had nothing to do with the fog. Only three people alive had seen which coastline lay open on that table, and one of them was dead.

"I don't discuss commissions," he said.

"No. Your master didn't either. Very proper, both of you." The man reached into his coat—an unhurried motion, deliberately slow so that Elodie could see his hands were empty of anything but paper—and produced a leather folio, which he laid flat on his knees without opening it. "I'll come to the point, because I respect your time and you strike me as a man who counts his hours. You have perhaps twenty-six days before the magistrate's men walk through that door. Fewer, if they decide to stop being courteous. When they do, they will take every sheet of paper in this room, and they will take you as well—not to trial, mind you. Trials are for matters that need justifying. You'd simply go, and after a while there would be very little left to prove anything about anyone."

"You're well informed."

"I'm paid to be." The man's mouth curved, but his eyes stayed flat. "Here is what I'm offering, and I'll say it once. Papers. Genuine ones—not forgeries, whatever the word going around the quarter may be. A sailor's protection pass under a name of your choosing, a billet on a ship out of Vellamare, signed articles, a berth, wages advanced. A brig called the *Meridian Rose*, bound for the southern ports, leaving on the morning tide eleven days from now. Clean escape. No questions at any port between here and the equator. By the time anyone thinks to look for you, you'll be a name on a crew list that no longer exists."

Elodie said nothing. The stove ticked. Somewhere out in the fog a ship's bell rang twice, muffled, patient.

"And in exchange," Elodie said finally.

"In exchange, you leave the atlas exactly as it is. Unfinished. You board that ship with your satchel, your clothes, and whatever sentimental trifles a young man carries, and you let the work stay here to be seized with everything else. Unfinished. Incomplete. Worthless as evidence and worthless as art."

The word *unfinished* landed in the room like a stone dropped into still water, and Elodie watched its rings spread outward through everything it touched: Aurel's dying instruction, given in a voice already half gone; the fourteen months of nights; the gold leaf ordered special from the foil-maker's on Tanner's Row; the final plate that existed so far only as a pencil ghost on calfskin. *Unfinished.* The man had chosen the word precisely. They always did.

"Why?" Elodie asked. "If the magistrate wants the workshop seized, why would anyone pay to send me away with my life? And if someone else wants the atlas destroyed, why not simply burn the workshop down some night? It's happened to less important buildings."

For the first time, something moved behind the grey eyes—something that might have been respect, or might have been recalculation.

"Burning draws inquiry," the man said. "Inquiries turn over stones. And the magistrate's office, whatever else it is, does genuinely want this quarter quiet, not charred. As for why spare you—" he shrugged, and the shrug cost him something, because his right shoulder moved stiffly, as though the damage had traveled further than the leg, "—there are people who prefer problems to sail away. It's tidier. A dead apprentice becomes a martyr, and martyrs get songs sung about them. A vanished apprentice becomes a rumor, and rumors starve."

"And the atlas?"

"Paper burns slowly when men are careful. Careless fires are suspicious. But incomplete work—" he tapped the folio on his knee, once, "—incomplete work is merely disappointing. An atlas without its key plates proves nothing except that a dead man had ambitions. Let them seize it. Let it sit in an evidence vault until the ink fades. No names, no routes, no testimony. Just beautiful, useless geography."

* * *

Elodie found, to his own surprise, that part of him was listening. Not the part that loved the work—that part had gone very quiet and very cold—but the older, simpler part, the animal part, the one that wanted to live. Twenty-six days. Eleven of them bought outright. A ship. A name that no longer existed by the time anyone came asking. He thought of Marta's sons, of her eldest Tam's laugh carrying over the courtyard wall, of the dyer's steam rising pink and violet into the mornings. He thought of Sera Voss's face across a teacup, and made himself stop thinking of it.

"You've done this before," he said aloud. "Offered passages."

"Once or twice."

"What happened to them? The others you offered it to?"

The man considered the question with what appeared to be genuine attention. "Most took it," he said. "One didn't." He did not elaborate, and the silence where the elaboration should have been was more eloquent than anything he might have said.

Elodie rose and walked to the window. The fog pressed against the glass like a hand. Beyond it, invisible, the harbor lay somewhere below with its ships and its tides and its eleven-day departures, and beyond the harbor the city, and spanning the black water between the two worlds the old iron bridge with its ten thousand rivets, each one a day he had counted, each day now suddenly negotiable.

"If I refuse," Elodie said, without turning, "what happens?"

"Nothing happens tonight. Nothing tomorrow. I'm not a threat, Master Nakamura—I'm a door. Doors don't chase people." A pause, and then, quieter, with the first note of something almost human all evening: "But doors close. This one closes on the morning tide eleven days from now. After that, the offer doesn't exist, and neither, functionally, do you. The men who come after won't be paid to be tidy. They'll be paid to be thorough."

Elodie turned back. "And if I accept—if I walk out and leave the atlas—who guarantees your principals keep their word? Who guarantees I reach the *Meridian Rose* at all? A man who knows about forged papers knows about accidents at sea."

"Fair questions." The man opened the folio at last. Inside lay documents arranged with a draftsman's neatness that Elodie resented recognizing as kin to his own: a sailor's protection pass, blank in the name field; a letter of introduction on shipping-agent's paper; a bank draft drawn on a house in a southern port. "Half now, half on the quay. And this—" he held up the pass between two fingers, "—you keep from tonight, whether you sail or not. Consider it proof that the offer was real, and a small apology for the camera on the street. My employers felt I was conspicuous. They were correct."

He rose, limped to the table, and laid the pass down beside the unfinished Serrat Channel—deliberately, Elodie saw, deliberately within arm's reach of the wet ink, so that the choice would sit physically in the workspace, contaminating it.

"One more thing," the man said, retrieving his hat. At the door he paused, and for a moment the practiced lightness fell away entirely, and Elodie saw underneath it a tired man with a ruined leg and a scarred throat who had carried other people's escapes in his pockets for too many years. "Your master was offered this same door. Four years ago, after the business with the Margrave Run. He declined it. Said a map abandoned halfway wasn't a map at all, just a lie with borders." The pale eyes held Elodie's. "I thought you should know what refusing costs. It cost him, in the end, rather more than he expected."

Then he was gone, his uneven footsteps fading down the stair, into the fog, toward whatever ship or office or grave such men returned to.

* * *

Elodie stood for a long time without moving.

Then he crossed to the table and looked at the pass lying beside the map. Heavy stock, watermark true, the magistrate's own counterstamp flawlessly rendered—he turned it to the lamp and could not find the forgery in it, though he knew it was there the way he knew a coastline was wrong before he could say why. Someone had made this beautifully. Someone had made this to be believed.

*Eleven days.*

He picked it up. He meant only to read it again, to memorize the ship's name, the tide, the details of the trap so precisely baited—and then he became aware that he was holding it over the flame of the lamp, and that the corner had begun to brown and curl, and that part of him, the animal part, the part that wanted to live, was watching his own hand with something like horror and something like hope.

He pulled it back before it caught. The corner stayed scorched, a small brown crescent like a bite mark. He could still sail on it. Probably. A harbor official would have to look closely, care more than was safe, want trouble more than coin.

He sat down at the drafting table, laid the damaged pass face-down beneath the blotter where he could feel its edge against his wrist like a pulse, and took up his pen.

The Serrat Channel waited, its final curve still open, still wet, still demanding to be finished. Aurel had refused the same door. *A map abandoned halfway is just a lie with borders.* The master had known the price when he named it. Perhaps he had even known which currency it would be paid in.

Elodie drew the line. Slowly. Beautifully. The way a man writes his own refusal.

Outside, the fog thickened further, and somewhere in it, he was certain, the man with the limp was reporting back to whoever held his leash: *he hasn't decided.* And Elodie understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that the next visitor through that door would not come with papers.

It would come with the eleven days shortened—or with Sera Voss's name spoken in a voice that made threats sound like courtesies.

He dipped his pen, and worked faster than care allowed.
