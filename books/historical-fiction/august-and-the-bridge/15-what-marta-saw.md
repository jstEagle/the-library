## 15. What Marta Saw

The dye vats taught Marta Yarrow everything she knew about waiting. Indigo took its own time; madder root could not be hurried; a cloth pulled too soon from its bath came out pale and ruined, and there was no undoing it. She had raised two sons on the patience of dyes, and buried a husband with it, and kept her house standing through the lean winters when the harbor froze and the dyers' quarter smelled of cold ash instead of color. Waiting was her craft as much as dipping cloth. She had believed herself a master of it.

She had been wrong for eleven days now, and the wrongness sat in her chest like a stone swallowed whole.

It began—no. It did not begin eleven days ago. It began on the night Aurel Nakamura died, and everything since had only been the stone moving slowly down through her, finding new places to lodge.

* * *

She had been coming home late from the Kestrel's Anchor, where she sometimes mended sails for coin that didn't smell of dye. That was her first lie to herself, that night: that the sail-work was the reason she'd been out past dark, when in truth she'd lingered over ale and the company of a widow from the rope-walk, both of them laughing too loudly at nothing, glad of an hour when they were women and not widows and mothers. So it was nearly midnight when she turned up Copper Roof Lane with her lantern guttering, and saw light still burning in the mapmaker's workshop window, and thought nothing of it. Aurel kept mad hours. All the best craftsmen did.

Then she heard voices, and the voices were wrong.

Not angry—not yet. Low. The kind of low that meant men were being careful, which meant men were doing something that needed care. She had lived thirty-nine years in Vellamare's working quarters; she knew the sound of careful men the way sailors knew weather. She put out her lantern without deciding to. Her body decided for her, and pressed her into the recessed doorway of the cooper's shop, three doors down from the workshop, where the shadow was deep and the smell of oak shavings covered even hers.

Two figures crossed the workshop's window-light. One was Aurel—she knew his stoop, the way he carried his left shoulder lower, a cartographer's hunch earned over decades bent above drafting tables. He moved quickly for a man his age, agitated, his hands carving the air. The other figure stood very still, and stillness like that cost money: a tall man in a clerk's dark coat, the kind of coat that belonged to no trade and therefore belonged to the magistrate's offices, where a man's work was paper and his clothing needed only to say *respectable*.

"—the third plate is not finished," she heard Aurel say, his voice rising through the glass, cracked with something worse than anger. "You think I don't know what you want? You want it never finished. You've wanted that since the day he commissioned it."

The clerk said something she couldn't catch. Low, reasonable, the voice of a man reading terms aloud.

"Then take it up with your master," Aurel said, and laughed, and the laugh frightened her more than anything else, because it was the laugh of a man who has stopped calculating consequences. "Take it to the magistrate himself, if you can find him sober between sessions. Tell him the atlas will be completed as agreed. Every route. Every name. My hand or no one's."

The clerk moved then. Not toward Aurel—toward the door.

Marta should have gone home. She knew that then and knew it more bitterly every day since. She should have walked the long way round by the fish market, come in through her own yard, and let whatever happened behind that workshop door happen in a world she had not witnessed. Her sons slept upstairs. Tomas, fourteen, all elbows and questions. Little Piet, nine, who still crept into her bed during storms. Everything she had left in the world was sleeping under her roof, and the men of Vellamare who wore respectable coats had a way, she knew, of making examples. Her husband had learned it at a dockworkers' meeting he'd attended out of curiosity and come home from quieter than when he'd left, and quieter ever after, until the quiet became illness and the illness became a grave.

But her feet stayed in the cooper's doorway, because Aurel Nakamura had bought indigo from her for eleven years and always asked after the boys, and because some part of her—the part that had laughed too loud at the Kestrel's Anchor—wanted to know what a respectable coat wanted with a mapmaker at midnight.

The workshop door opened. The clerk stepped halfway out, looked up and down the lane—and here was the thing she would carry to her grave, the thing that woke her at three in the morning with her heart hammering: he looked directly at the cooper's doorway where she stood. Directly. The lantern she had extinguished hung dead in her hand; her grey shawl blended with the cooper's shadowed wall; and still his eyes passed across her and paused there, half a heartbeat, the way a man pauses when he sees movement at the edge of vision and must decide whether to trust it.

Then Aurel spoke again from inside, louder—"And tell Deputy Corvane that I keep accounts too"—and the clerk's attention snapped back to the door like a dog to a whistle, and he went in, and closed it softly, so softly, behind him.

*Deputy Corvane.* She turned the name over in the dark. She knew it the way everyone in Vellamare knew it: the magistrate's chief deputy, the smooth one, the one who administered oaths and seizures and the paperwork of other people's ruin. She had seen him twice from a distance, at the quayside ceremonies, silver-tipped cane, immaculate gloves. A man who never touched anything himself.

What happened next took less time than the telling of it would. There was no crash. No scream. That was the obscenity of it—that a life like Aurel's, thick with ink and salt air and the boy he'd brought across the sea to raise, could end with almost no sound at all. She heard a thud like a heavy book falling from a shelf. She heard a chair scrape. She heard—one more time, faintly, terribly—a voice that was not Aurel's say, clearly enough to reach her through old glass: *"Hold him still."*

Then nothing. Minutes of nothing. Her own breath so loud she pressed her shawl against her mouth.

The door opened. The clerk came out alone, unhurried, adjusting his cuffs. He carried nothing. Behind him, through the window, she saw the lamp swing as someone moved—it must have been another man; there had been a second set of footsteps leaving, heavier—and then the light steadied and she made herself look, because she would need to know, later, whether what she'd seen was real.

Aurel Nakamura lay on his workshop floor among his beautiful maps, one arm flung out, and from this distance and through this glass he might have been sleeping, except that men do not sleep with their faces turned at that angle against the boards.

The clerk paused once more on the threshold. He tipped his head back and looked up—at the copper roof, at the upper windows, at the lane. And then, unhurried as a man closing a ledger, he drew from his pocket a small flask, uncorked it, went back inside, and did not come out again for the better part of ten minutes.

When he finally left, walking toward the city end of the lane with his coat hanging respectably straight, Marta counted to two hundred before she let herself breathe, and to five hundred before she moved. She told herself she was giving him time to get far away. She was also giving herself time to decide.

She never went in. She never touched Aurel, never closed his eyes, never confirmed with her hands what her eyes already knew. By morning the watch had been summoned by the bakery boy, and the word going round the quarter was *accident*—the old man, the drink, the fall against the drafting table, a strike of the temple on the iron corner-strap of the map press. The flask was found near his hand. Everyone agreed it was sad but unsurprising; mapmakers were dreamers, dreamers drank, drink killed. Even the physician who signed the paper sounded bored by it.

Marta stood in the small crowd outside the funeral and said nothing, and the stone settled deeper.

* * *

There was one detail she had never spoken aloud, not to the priest, not to her sister in the rope-walk, not to God in the dark before sleep took her—if sleep could be said to take anyone anywhere anymore, rather than simply failing to arrive. On the morning after, when the watchmen carried Aurel out feet first on a shutter and the neighbors stood in their doorways murmuring, Marta had found herself looking at the dead man's hands. A dyer looks at hands. Hands were her trade's whole testimony; she could read a week of a person's labor in their stains.

Aurel Nakamura's right hand was clean.

His left hand was black to the wrist with ink, worked deep into the whorls of the fingerprints, days-old layers of it, the hand of a man who had been drawing furiously, endlessly, right up to the end. But a man draws with his dominant hand. Elodie, the boy, dipped his right hand in the inkwell a hundred times a day; the whole quarter knew the apprentice's stained fingers. And Aurel had been right-handed—she had watched him write his ledgers at her kitchen table while she invoiced his indigo, and he had held the pen in his right hand, always.

Yet the right hand was clean, and the left was drowned in ink, and there was a bruise—she had leaned close, close enough that a watchman frowned at her—circling the right wrist like a cuff. Like a place where fingers had gripped.

*Hold him still.*

She had told herself it meant nothing. Ink transferred; a struggling man smears; bruises happen when old men fall. She had told herself this while kneading bread, while dipping cloth, while watching Elodie in the weeks after the funeral walk past her door with his master's satchel over his shoulder and his chin set in that stubborn young line, carrying on the work that had, if she was right about any of this, killed the only father he had.

That was the arithmetic that kept her silent, and it was honest arithmetic. If she spoke, she spoke against a deputy of the magistrate. Against a clerk whose eyes had found her shadow in a doorway at midnight and filed it away. What happened to witnesses in Vellamare? They drowned crossing harbors they'd crossed a thousand times. They fell from ladders. Their sons were arrested for crimes that dissolved upon investigation, after the lesson had been taught. She had Tomas to think of, and Piet, and a widow had no protection but invisibility. Silence wasn't cowardice, she told the ceiling at night. Silence was armor. Silence was the only shield a poor woman got to choose.

She had almost convinced herself. Eleven days of almost convincing herself.

* * *

Then came Thursday, and the stranger with the limp.

She noticed him first because limps in the dyers' quarter usually meant dockwork injuries, and this limp was naval—the stiff, measured roll of a man whose leg had been broken and reset by a ship's surgeon in a hurry. He wore plain clothes, good cloth badly chosen, the disguise of a man who had read about ordinary people but never been one. He stopped across the lane from her dye-yard and made a sketch. Of the workshop. Of Elodie's windows, where lamplight burned late every night now, later every night, the boy killing himself by inches over those maps.

"Good morning," the man said, tipping an imaginary cap. "You'd be the dyer. Marta, is it?"

"I don't give my name to men who already have it."

A smile, thin as paper. "Just gathering particulars. The workshop—young fellow runs it alone now, does he? Half-Japanese, I'm told. The Japanese boy." He said it the way a man labels a crate. "Keeps odd hours. Has visitors?"

"No visitors."

"Come now. A young man alone in a city like this always has visitors. Ships' captains. Old friends of his master." He produced something from his pocket—a photograph, small, glossy, new. Elodie, taken without his knowing, crossing Copper Roof Lane with the satchel. Someone had photographed her neighbor's orphan son as though he were a criminal already convicted. "You'd tell me, I'm sure, if he seemed frightened lately. Frightened men run. Running men are easier to find before they run than after."

"He's not frightened," Marta said. "He's grieving. There's a difference, though I wouldn't expect a man who photographs children to know it."

The smile didn't waver, which was worse than anger. He gave her a card with no name on it, only a number, and rolled away on his bad leg toward the bridge, and Marta stood in her dye-yard with her hands blue to the elbow and understood that the clock she had been pretending didn't exist was ticking after all.

* * *

That evening she sent Piet to bed early with a lie about needing quiet for the mordant bath, and sat Tomas down at the kitchen table, and looked at her eldest son's face—the same bones as his father's, the same cowlick—and felt the stone finally move all the way down and find its voice.

"Tomas. If I asked you to be brave, would you understand it wouldn't be the shouting kind?"

He put down his spoon. Fourteen years old, and he studied her face the way she studied cloth, for flaws and fasts and what would hold. "Is this about the mapmaker?"

Her chest went tight. "Why would you ask that?"

"Because you stopped humming after he died. You hum when you dip the blues. You haven't hummed in eleven days." He said it without accusation, which somehow made it unbearable. "Ma. What did you see?"

And there it was—the moment she had dodged for eleven days, delivered to her by a boy who knew her by her humming. She could lie to deputies and priests and the dark ceiling above her bed, but lying to that steady young face across her own table was a different weight entirely.

So she told him. Not everything—not yet, not the name, not the flask, not the wrist—but enough. That Aurel hadn't died alone. That a man in a respectable coat had been there. That his death had been ruled wrongly, and she had known, and said nothing, and why: *because of you, because of Piet, because a widow's word against the magistrate's office is a candle against the tide.* She watched Tomas absorb it, watched his jaw set in a line she recognized from mirrors and funerals, and when she finished he was quiet a long moment.

"You could talk to the boy," he said at last. "Elodie. If anyone else saw something, it'd be him. And if the men in coats are asking about him—" Tomas met her eyes. "Then he needs friends who'll stand in doorways for him, Ma. Not just hide in them."

She lay awake long after, listening to the tide turn beneath the cliff, and the guilt that had been a stone found its voice at last, and the voice did not whisper *be safe*. It whispered the question she'd spent eleven days refusing to ask:

If Aurel's murder had been ruled an accident, what was planned for the boy who inherited his work?

Across the lane, through her window, she could see the workshop lamp still burning. And below it, faint in the lamplight, Elodie's shadow bent at his table—writing, she would learn soon enough, a letter he had no business writing, to a sender he had no business answering, bluffing bravely with a dead man's knowledge while the noose he couldn't see drew quietly tighter around the whole quarter.

Marta rose in the dark, dressed, and took down her good shawl. Tomorrow she would knock on the workshop door. Whatever came of it—whatever the respectable coats did afterward—she had watched a man die in silence once already, and she would not do it twice.
