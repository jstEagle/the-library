## 16. The Letter Answered

The letter had been sitting in the drawer for three days, and Elodie had learned that a sealed thing left unopened long enough begins to feel less like paper and more like a stone—something with weight, with temperature, with a patience of its own. He had placed it beneath the tray of burnishing tools, face down, as if the wax seal bearing the magistrate's crest might forget itself if it could not see the room. It did not forget. Every time he opened the drawer for a burnisher, a knife, a scrap of pumice, there it was: red wax, the Voss arms pressed deep, the paper inside holding whatever cold sentence had been composed for him.

He had read it eleven times. He knew that was a foolish number to admit to, even to himself, but he counted things; it was how Master Aurel had trained him. Count the rivets on the bridge, count the tide-marks on the harbor stairs, count the strokes of the pen when inking a coastline so that the hand learns what a mile feels like. So he had counted his readings, and on the eleventh he had finally understood what the letter was not.

It was not an accusation. That was the strange, terrible elegance of it. It was an *invitation*—polite, almost courtly, written in a clerk's careful hand, informing the workshop household that certain irregularities had come to the magistrate's attention, and that a person of good sense might wish to make certain *arrangements* before the office's interest became formal. There was no demand. There was no threat spelled out. There was only the shape of a threat, the way a coastline implies the land behind it, and the sender had trusted—correctly—that Elodie would fill in the interior himself, with all the terrors his imagination could supply.

Which meant, Elodie realized on the eleventh reading, sitting very still in the grey light of the workshop while the harbor gulls argued over something drowned, that the sender did not know what Elodie knew.

Or did not know how much he knew. Or did not know whether he knew anything at all.

The letter was a fishing line. And somewhere behind the magistrate's crest, behind the careful clerk's hand, someone was holding the rod and waiting for a tug.

* * *

He thought about it for two more days while his hands went on working, because hands that stopped working invited questions, and questions were the one currency he could not afford. He inked the northern channels. He ground pigments. He stretched a fresh skin of vellum and let it dry under weights, listening to the paper breathe. And all the while, in the back room of his mind, the letter turned over and over like a stone in a current.

The problem was this: he could not answer honestly, because honesty was death. If he wrote *I know nothing, I have seen nothing, the atlas is only maps* — then he was either lying, which they would prove, or truthful, which meant the atlas held nothing and Master Aurel had died for paper. If he wrote *I know everything* — then he made himself worth killing tonight rather than in thirty days.

But there was a third road. He had begun to see it on the twelfth reading, which he did not count, and it was this: the sender had written to him *blind*. Whoever composed that letter did not know what the apprentice had found, what the apprentice had read, what the apprentice might have hidden, copied, or sent away. The sender was guessing. And a man who guesses can be made to believe he has already lost.

His master had a saying, one of the hundred sayings Aurel Voss—no, not Voss, that was the magistrate's name; Aurel *Kestrel*, dead these many weeks, ruled drowned by his own ink—had kept like coins in his coat. *The mapmaker's advantage*, the old man used to say, tapping the table with one ink-stained finger, *is that he has already been everywhere the other man is only going.* You drew the coast before the admiral sailed it. You marked the shoal before the captain struck it. You knew the shape of the land while everyone else was still lost in it.

Elodie looked at his own hands—small, stained at the cuticles, the nails rimmed with a black that no pumice would ever fully lift—and thought: I have been invisible for eleven years. The boy who grinds the pigment. The boy who stretches the vellum. The half-Japanese apprentice whom the quarter calls *the master's shadow* because he walks half a step behind and half a tone too quiet. No one sees a shadow. No one counts a shadow.

But a shadow, he thought, has been watching everything the light has touched.

* * *

He chose the paper carefully, the way he chose everything now. Not workshop stock—too traceable, the watermark a signature. Not new paper at all—too clean, too eager. He took instead a sheet from the rag basket, one of Aurel's old drafting offcuts, a piece that had been folded and refolded, that carried the ghost-creases of a hundred abandoned sketches. Paper that looked like it had been handled by a frightened man in a hurry. Paper that told a story before a single word was written on it.

He wrote the first draft at the kitchen table after midnight, by the light of a single candle turned low, with Marta's dog asleep across the doorway like a drawn bolt. He wrote in a hand that was not quite his own—larger, more hurried, the descenders ragged. Eleven years of copying his master's letters for clients had given him a dozen hands the way a singer keeps a dozen songs, and he chose the one he had invented for Aurel's impatient correspondence, the hand of a man who thought faster than his pen could follow.

*You write to me of arrangements,* the draft began. *I will write to you of evidence.*

He stopped. He read it back. His heart was going hard, the way it went when he stood too near the bridge rail with the tide running black below.

*Evidence* was the word. Not *the atlas*—never name the thing you are protecting, that was the first rule of hiding anything, you named the room and not the treasure. Not *documents*, which invited a search for documents. *Evidence*—a word broad as the sea, and underneath it, implied, the whole drowned weight of whatever the reader feared most.

He let the candle gutter and rewrote it clean the next evening, and this is what he sent, or near enough, for he would carry the exact words in his memory the rest of his life the way a man carries a scar:

*You write to me of arrangements. I will write to you of evidence. My master kept accounts. Not of money — of routes, dates, and names, and of the hands that paid for them. He had a habit, as you may recall, of writing everything down. It was the habit that killed him, though not in the way your office has decided it did.*

*You have thirty days, your letter says. I have had eleven years to learn patience, and I have had, since his death, a great deal of time to read what he left behind. I am one person in a workshop full of paper. You cannot search what you cannot find, and you cannot find what I have already scattered. Burn the workshop and the ash will still testify.*

*I do not want money. I do not want passage. I want what my master wanted, which is for the truth to arrive at its destination. Tell me who gave the order for the letter under your seal, and I will tell you which page of his accounts bears your name.*

*You have until the tide turns on Thursday. After that, I stop negotiating and start distributing.*

He read it four times. On the fourth reading he found the flaw—*thirty days, your letter says* — the letter had never named thirty days; only the seizure order Sera had whispered of had done that. He struck the sentence and replaced it with something vaguer and more menacing: *You have your schedule. I have mine.* A bluff, he thought with a wild, cold exhilaration, should be mostly silence. Let the other man's fear write the details in.

The signature he agonized over longest. He could not sign his own name—Elodie Nakamura was a boy, a shadow, a nothing, and a nothing could be made to disappear. He could not sign Aurel's name—the man was dead and the office knew it. So he signed, in a small, deliberate, infuriating hand, the way a mapmaker signs a plate he intends to be read for a century:

*The Apprentice.*

* * *

Sealing it was its own ceremony. He had no crest, so he pressed the wax with the handle of Aurel's burnishing tool, which left a ring like a tiny compass rose—accidental, anonymous, and somehow more frightening for it. A seal that could belong to anyone. A seal that said: *we are many.*

Delivering it was the part that made his palms sweat for a full day beforehand. He could not use the courier who had brought the first letter; that man was watched, or was a watcher. He could not post it; the magistrate's office would have fingers in the postal house, surely. He considered the harbor, the fishing boats, the dyers' carts that went up to the city twice a week with Marta's bolts of indigo and madder—Marta, who had begun to look at him with a new careful kindness that he could not yet read, as if she were carrying something heavy and deciding whether to set it down.

In the end he chose the simplest, stupidest, most invisible method in Vellamare: he became a delivery boy. On Thursday morning he put on his oldest coat, tucked the letter into a baker's box with two day-old loaves, and walked it up through the workshop quarter, across the old iron bridge—counting the rivets as he always did, *one, two, three*, each one a day, each one a small iron promise—into the city, and stood outside the magistrate's offices on the Corso until he saw a junior clerk emerge for his midday bread. Then he approached, bowed the small bow of one servant to another, and said, in his flattest, most forgettable voice, that the baker had sent a box for the office, compliments of a well-wisher, no name required.

The clerk took it without looking at him. Nobody looked at him. Eleven years of being unseen, and for the first time in his life Elodie Nakamura walked away grateful for it.

* * *

The answer, if it came, would come the way the first letter had come—by courier, by ordinary paper, by cold fear arriving in daylight. So he worked, because working was the only prayer he knew. He inked the eastern shoals. He cut a pen until the nib sang. And on Saturday, when the courier's knock came at the workshop door, Elodie's hands were steady and his heart was not.

The letter was short. Shorter than his. The clerk's careful hand had gone hurried in places, and there was a blot near the middle where the pen had been pressed too hard and held too long, as if the writer had stopped to think and the ink had run out of patience for him.

*Your terms are received. No page bearing any name of ours will be distributed. In exchange, the workshop will be left in peace and the matter closed. A token of good faith is enclosed.*

The token was a banknote, folded once, of a denomination that would have kept the workshop in vellum and candles for a season.

And beneath the signature—unsigned, of course, merely a flourish, a curl of ink that meant nothing and everything—was a single line that Elodie read three times before its meaning finished arriving, cold, from the soles of his feet upward:

*The master's accounts are of interest to more parties than one. Burn what you cannot carry. Trust no one who asks to see them, however kindly they ask.*

* * *

He stood in the middle of the workshop for a long time, the banknote in one hand and the letter in the other, while the light moved across the drying maps and the harbor sounds came faintly through the shutters—gulls, rigging, the far iron knock of the bridge under some early cart.

They had answered. They had *negotiated*. Which meant they believed him, or feared him, or both—and fear was a currency he had never held before, and it was heavier than he had imagined, and it did not spend itself gently. He had moved a piece. He, the shadow, the apprentice, the invisible boy, had sat down at a board where magistrates and smugglers played, and had pushed a letter across it, and the board had trembled.

But the last line would not stop turning in his mind. *Trust no one who asks to see them, however kindly they ask.* It was meant, he understood, to isolate him—to cut him off from allies, from help, from the one person in Vellamare who had already stood in this workshop and looked at his work too long and too precisely. It was a threat dressed as advice.

And yet. And yet. He thought of Sera Voss's grey eyes, of the way she had held a finished chart to the light as though it might confess. He thought of her father's crest on the first letter's wax. He thought of the deputy whose name he could not yet read in the marginalia, the cipher that resisted him night after night, Aurel's little jokes of numbers and tides.

*More parties than one.*

He folded the letter small and hid it inside a map roller, behind the plate of the northern coast, and he stood a moment with his hand flat against the roller as if taking its pulse. Somewhere in this city, more than one party wanted the atlas, and only one of them had written to him, and neither of them knew that the apprentice had just learned the most dangerous thing a mapmaker can learn:

Not where the rocks are. But that the chart itself is worth a war.

That evening, a knock came at the workshop door—soft, unhurried, three knocks he did not know—and through the frosted pane of the upper glass he could see, backlit by the streetlamp, the silhouette of a woman in a magistrate's cloak, standing in the rain, waiting to be let in.
