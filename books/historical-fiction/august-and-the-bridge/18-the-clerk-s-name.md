## 18. The Clerk's Name

The rain had stopped sometime before midnight, and the workshop smelled of wet wool and cooling copper. Elodie had lit three candles—one for the desk, one for the map rollers, and one he had wedged into the mouth of the ink bottle Aurel had kept for forty years, its glass stained black at the lip like the rim of an old man's mouth. He told himself the third candle was for light. It was not. It was for company.

The marginalia had defeated him for six nights running.

He had tried every approach a mapmaker's apprentice could devise. He had held the master's notes to the window glass, hunting watermarks. He had read them aloud in the cadences Aurel used when dictating, as if the dead man's rhythm might shake loose the sense. He had copied the marginal strokes onto fresh paper, then onto vellum, then onto the back of an old tide chart, hoping a change of surface might change the cipher's temper. Nothing. The notes remained what they had first appeared to be: the fussy annotations of a meticulous craftsman. *Tide runs early here. Soundings unreliable after storm. Ink from the southern supplier, no other.*

Ink from the southern supplier, no other.

Elodie sat very still.

He had read that line a hundred times. It sat in the margin of the harbor chart, beside the mouth of the Serel, in Aurel's smallest and most careful hand—the hand the master reserved for things he did not want noticed. And it had never once made sense, because Aurel had bought his ink from the chandler on Copper Lane for as long as Elodie had been in the workshop, and Copper Lane was north of everything.

Unless it was not about ink.

He pulled the harbor chart toward him and looked at the line again. *Ink from the southern supplier, no other.* The letters were small, uniform, unhurried. Aurel's cipher-hands, Elodie realized now, had always been his ordinary hands slowed down—slowed the way a man walks when he is carrying something fragile through a crowded room. The disguise was not in the letters. The disguise was in the pace.

He took a clean sheet and began to write out the marginal lines that had never fit the charts they adorned. Not the ones about tides—those were true, or true enough. The others. The ones that named suppliers Aurel had never used. The ones that complained of clerks and customs and the temper of particular harbormasters, in a city where Aurel had no dealings with harbormasters at all. He wrote them out one beneath another, and when he had eleven of them, he set the list beside the harbor chart and did what he should have done six nights ago.

He read them the way a smuggler reads a coast.

*Ink from the southern supplier, no other.* The southern supplier. South of the Serel mouth there was exactly one supplier of anything: the warehouse of a man named Corvane, who traded in rope and salt and, if the atlas's hidden routes were honest, in rather more than rope and salt. The line did not sit beside the Serel mouth by accident. It was a name wearing the clothes of a complaint.

Elodie's candle flames leaned in the draft from under the door, and he leaned with them, his nose nearly touching the paper.

*Soundings unreliable after storm.* Beside the northern channel. There was no storm in the record for the year the note was dated—Elodie knew the weather of Vellamare the way he knew his own hands, because Aurel had made him log it daily for six years. But there had been a customs seizure in the northern channel that autumn, a boat run aground on purpose, its cargo "lost" before the surveyors came. Unreliable after storm. After the seizure. The soundings—the cargo—were unreliable because a clerk had made them disappear.

He wrote the translation beneath each line, his own hand shaking now, the letters coming out crooked. *Corvane. The northern seizure. The surveyor who looked away.* Line by line, the margins of a dead man's charts stopped being marginalia and became a ledger of the living.

* * *

Toward the second hour, the workshop grew cold enough that he worked with his coat on and his breath faintly visible, and he did not notice either thing. He had moved to the earliest charts now, the ones Aurel had drawn before Elodie was born, and the cipher was looser there, younger, the disguise thinner. A young man's caution, Elodie thought. The old master had grown more careful with age, the way a scar grows thicker over a wound that never quite closed.

And then, on a chart of the eastern quays dated nineteen years back, he found the line that stopped his heart.

*The clerk Grell takes his portion at the third warehouse; the rest is honest.*

Grell.

The name meant nothing and everything. Elodie sat back and let the candle smoke drift across his face. Grell. There had been a clerk named Grell in the customs house—Elodie knew the name the way every child of the workshop quarter knew it, from the older men who muttered it over their pipes, from Marta, who spat it once when a surveyor had valued her dyed cloth at a tenth of its worth. Deputy Grell. Chief deputy to the magistrate himself. The man whose seal now rode on documents that crossed Elodie's desk. The man whose office had ruled Aurel's death an accident—a fall from the loft ladder, a blow to the temple, a tragedy, a burial, a closed file.

The clerk Grell takes his portion.

Nineteen years. Nineteen years of portions, if the marginalia were true—and Elodie no longer had any doubt that they were true, because Aurel had died for them, or near enough that the distinction had stopped mattering. Nineteen years of a clerk skimming the smuggling routes the atlas mapped, taking his cut at the third warehouse, growing fat and careful and powerful, rising from clerk to surveyor to deputy while the man who documented his thefts drew coastlines in the quiet of a workshop that smelled of linseed and rain.

And when the mapmaker had finally—finally—begun to assemble the atlas whole, every route, every name, every date in one binding, the clerk had come to the workshop.

Elodie remembered the ladder. He had been the one who found it, lying along the wall beneath the loft, set square and orderly, exactly as a careful man would leave it. He had thought, in the first raw days, *how like Aurel, to fall and still leave his tools in order.* He had thought it was grief talking, sentiment dressing itself as observation. Now he understood: the ladder had been set square because a man who was not Aurel had set it there. A tidy man. A clerk's man. A man who took his portion at the third warehouse and left no loose ends, except the one he had not found—the marginalia, hidden in plain sight on the walls of the atlas he could not read, because the cipher was not in the letters but in the pace, and pace cannot be seized.

Elodie put his head in his hands. The candles guttered and steadied.

* * *

He did not sleep. He worked until the windows went from black to the color of wet slate, and then he worked through the gray, because the list was growing and each new line made the shape of the thing clearer and more terrible. It was not one clerk's greed. The margins named others—surveyors who looked away, a harbormaster whose "soundings" were always conveniently wrong, payments that moved through the third warehouse like water through a channel cut for the purpose. And at the head of the channel, receiving, directing, taking the largest portion of all: Grell. Not Grell the clerk anymore. Grell the chief deputy. The right hand of the magistrate's justice. The man who would sign the seizure order when it came. The man whose men would carry the workshop's contents away in crates and call it law.

The conspiracy did not merely reach into the seat of justice, Elodie thought, staring at the list until the letters swam. It sat in the seat. It wore the seat's robes. It ruled on the honesty of the very routes it fed from, and it had killed the only man who ever drew its portrait.

He thought of the sealed letter in its drawer, the magistrate's crest pressed into red wax, naming the workshop household in a smuggling plot they had never touched. He had wondered, all these days, who had written it—whether it was Grell's office acting for the magistrate, or Grell acting in the magistrate's name, or Grell acting for himself with the magistrate's seal lifted like a key from a hook. Now the question answered itself with a horrible simplicity. The letter was Grell. It had always been Grell. The frame was not a scheme devised against the workshop; it was housekeeping. The atlas was nearly finished, and the last witness to nineteen years of theft was a half-Japanese apprentice no one had ever thought to fear, and so the workshop would be seized, the atlas would be buried in an evidence room or burned in a courtyard, and the household would carry the crime that Grell had committed for two decades. The clerk takes his portion. And when the ledger grows dangerous, the clerk takes the ledger too, and the hands that kept it.

The third candle had burned down into the ink bottle's neck and drowned there with a small hiss. Elodie watched the smoke rise and thought of Aurel's hands—the right one stained to the wrist on the day they found him, when a man writing marginalia all his life would have stained the left. He had noticed it in the first hour and let the official story smooth it away, because he had been twenty and alone and the alternative was unbearable. The alternative was not unbearable. The alternative was this: a name, a date, a portion at the third warehouse, and a dead man's careful hand saying it all along, waiting six years for an apprentice to learn to read.

"I see him," Elodie said aloud, to the empty workshop, to the rollers, to the charts on their walls like a congregation of quiet witnesses. "Master, I see him. Grell."

Saying it made it real, and real was a weight he felt settle onto his shoulders like the satchel he had already begun to measure the world by. Because a name was not enough. A name in a dead man's cipher was worth nothing before a magistrate whose chief deputy was the man named. The marginalia would need to be read aloud by someone the court could not dismiss—and Elodie could already hear how it would go. *The foreign boy says the charts accuse my deputy. The foreign boy, whose household stands accused. The foreign boy, who had every reason to forge.* The atlas was evidence only to someone who already believed, and no one in power believed, because power was the thing accused.

Unless.

He thought of Sera Voss then, and hated himself a little for it, and thought of her anyway. She had audited the workshop with a magistrate's seal and a magistrate's eyes, and she had looked at his coastlines and seen—what? He had watched her not-say a dozen things over tea, her hands steady around the cup, her warnings wrapped in talk of weather and tides and the quality of the light for close work. *Careful work wants steady hands,* she had said, and he had heard the sentence beneath it. She circled the truth the way he circled the cipher, and neither of them could land.

If anyone in the magistrate's orbit could be made to read the margins, it was her. And if Grell learned that she had read them—

Elodie pressed his palms flat against the desk, against the harbor chart, against the line that had started everything. *Ink from the southern supplier, no other.* The ink was dry. It had been dry for years. But ink, he thought, was never really finished; it only waited for the right light.

* * *

He spent the last hours of the night doing what Aurel had trained him to do with every dangerous thing in the workshop: he made it ordinary. He copied the translated list in his smallest hand onto a slip of paper no larger than a customs docket, folded it into the spine of an old practice chart of the northern channel—a chart so bad Aurel had kept it purely to mock—and slid the chart back into the rack among its betters. The original margins stayed where they had always been, in plain sight on the atlas walls, disguised in their slow hand. Two copies of the truth now existed: one for the world to overlook, and one for him to carry.

He banked the fire. He washed the brushes, because Aurel had believed a man's character showed in his brushes, and because the ritual steadied him. Through the window, the workshop quarter was waking in its gray, ordinary way—Marta's shutters opening below, the dyer's steam beginning to rise, a cart on the cobbles, a gull arguing with the morning. All of it unknowing. All of it named, now, in a dead man's hand: the third warehouse, the northern channel, the routes that ran beneath their feet like water beneath ice.

And across the harbor, visible from the window if he leaned, the magistrate's offices stood with their green shutters and their brass fittings, and on the second floor, behind the window with the eagle carved above it, the chief deputy's rooms. Elodie looked at those windows for a long time. Somewhere behind the glass, a man who took his portion at the third warehouse was drinking his morning coffee, signing his morning papers, ruling on other men's honesty. Nineteen years of it. And this morning, for the first time, someone across the water knew his name and what he had done, and the knowing changed nothing yet, and would change everything, or would kill him too.

The knock came at the door just as the light turned gold—two soft raps, the rhythm of a neighbor, and then a pause too long to be a neighbor's. Through the gap under the door, a shadow stood without moving.

"Boy," said a voice he did not know, low and even, with the particular flatness of a man who has rehearsed his sentences. "I've a message from an interested party. About your master's unfinished work. It's worth your while to hear how it ends."

Elodie stood very still among the charts, the translated name folded in its hiding place behind him, the true atlas sleeping in its rollers above, and the shadow under the door waited, patient as a tide.
