## 20. Father and Daughter

The Voss house stood on the Calle d'Oro the way a verdict stands on a page—imposing, deliberate, and built to be read from a distance. Sera had walked its marble entry hall ten thousand times, but tonight the floor seemed to tilt beneath her, each polished tile a ledger line she could no longer unsee.

She had spent three days with her father's accounts. Not the public ledgers—the ones bound in blue calf and displayed to the assessor like trophies of honest stewardship—but the older volumes, the ones kept in the locked drawer of his study, wrapped in oilcloth against the harbor damp. She had learned where he hid the key when she was nine years old, watching him from the doorway of the nursery as he pressed it into the seam of the windowsill and told himself, in the confident voice he used for small self-deceptions, that no child would ever think to look. Children remember everything they are not supposed to. It was the only inheritance he had given her without knowing it.

The payments began eleven years ago. Small at first—sums that could be excused as harbor fees, dredging costs, the ordinary grease of municipal administration. But they followed a rhythm that had nothing to do with tides or seasons of trade, and everything to do with a pattern Sera now knew by heart: the curve of a coastline drawn in a dead man's ink, the deepwater run past the salt flats, the sheltered anchorage beneath the cliff at Torra Point where no legitimate ship had reason to lie quiet in the dark.

She had copied nothing. That was the first discipline of an auditor—never leave your own hand where it can be found. Instead she had memorized dates, amounts, initials. A column of numbers living behind her eyes like a splinter working toward the heart.

Her father was in the study when she came down after supper. Magistrate Corin Voss sat behind the great desk their grandfather had bought from a ruined Venetian family, a man diminished by furniture too large for him, though he would never have admitted it. He was reading correspondence by lamplight, his spectacles low on his nose, and for one treacherous moment he looked exactly like what she had always believed him to be: a tired public servant, gray at the temples, faithful to the city that had honored him.

"Father."

"Sera." He did not look up. "You missed port wine with us. Your mother noticed."

"Mother noticed. Or you instructed her to notice."

That made him lift his head. He removed his spectacles with the unhurried care he used for everything, folding them, setting them beside the lamp. "That is a strange greeting for a daughter who has always been precise with her courtesies."

"I've been precise with other things as well." She closed the study door behind her and heard the click of the latch the way one hears a cell lock from inside. "Eleven years of entries in the second drawer. The ones in your own hand, under the initial *V.C.* I've been precise about those."

Something moved across his face—not guilt, which she might have forgiven, and not surprise, which she might have exploited. It was calculation. Her father was calculating how much she knew, and how much of what she knew could hurt him, and that single act of arithmetic did more damage than any confession could have done.

"You've been in my private accounts." His voice stayed level. He rose, crossed to the sideboard, and poured himself a measure of brandy he did not offer to share. "I taught you better than to read documents you weren't assigned."

"You taught me that every entry tells a story. You said that when I was fourteen. You said a ledger is more honest than a witness, because paper doesn't know how to lie convincingly." She took a step toward the desk. "So tell me the story, Father. Tell me what the payments to the pilot at Torra Point were for. Tell me why the sums increase every year on the anniversary of the customs reform, like a debt being serviced. Tell me why the largest single disbursement is dated four days after Master Aurel the mapmaker drowned in two feet of calm water."

The brandy glass stopped halfway to his mouth.

For a long moment there was no sound in the room but the harbor wind testing the shutters and the tick of the mantel clock, which their mother wound on Sundays and which had never once been allowed to run slow. Sera realized she was holding her breath, and that some part of her—some last stubborn garrison of the daughter she had been—was still hoping he would say: *It's a misunderstanding. Sit down, and I will explain everything, and you will believe me because you will want to.*

Corin Voss drank, set down the glass, and said nothing at all.

* * *

He walked to the window instead. It was a habit she knew—he arranged conversations so that people had to look at his back and be reminded of his rank—but tonight the gesture read differently. Tonight it looked like a man who could not face what he had raised.

"Do you know," he said, "what this city was before men like me held it together? Smugglers' wharf. A place where every third crate lied about its contents and every magistrate before me either took his share or drowned taking notes." He spoke to the glass, to the dark harbor beyond it where lamplight shivered on black water. "I inherited rot, Sera. I contained it. That is not a crime."

"The mapmaker is dead."

"The mapmaker was careless. Men who write things down near this harbor are careless." He turned then, and his eyes found hers, and for the first time all evening he looked old. "You are twenty-three years old and you audit shipping manifests for a living. Do you imagine you understand what you've found? Entries are not confessions. Initials can be borrowed. Pages can be misread by daughters who have decided, in advance, what they want them to say."

"Then let me misread them aloud." Her voice shook, and she hated it, and she kept going. "The payment dated the ninth of October, four years ago, marked *for the survey*. The same week Aurel's workshop was searched by clerks carrying your warrant. The payment dated last spring, marked *continuation*, the same month the bridge inspection was ordered. Father—I have matched these entries against routes. Real routes. Routes drawn in an atlas I have seen with my own eyes. Your money moves along those routes like blood in veins."

She had said too much. She saw it land—the slight stilling of his frame, the way his gaze sharpened and went distant, filing her words somewhere for later use. *An atlas I have seen.* He would turn that over. He would wonder which atlas, whose hands had held it, whether his daughter's fascination with the Nakamura boy's workshop was professional or something else entirely.

"Whose atlas?" he asked quietly.

"A dead man's."

"Dead men don't draw."

"Their apprentices finish what they start."

Silence stretched between them, taut as a hawser. Her father studied her the way she had watched him study witnesses—not cruelly, never cruelly, but completely, cataloguing every tremor for later reference. And she understood, with a cold settling in her stomach, that she had just become a problem to him. Not a daughter who had stumbled onto something. A variable. Something to be managed.

"Give me the numbers you memorized," he said.

"I haven't memorized anything."

"Sera." Almost gently. "You copy dates onto your palm. You have done it since you were a girl studying for examinations. Show me your hand."

And that—that more than anything—was the moment filial love curdled. Because he knew her. Of course he knew her; he had made her, lesson by lesson, into the instrument standing before him. Every discipline he had drilled into her was now aimed at his throat: her precision, her memory, her refusal to accept a story that did not reconcile. He had built the thing that would destroy him and called it fatherhood.

She opened her right hand. The palm was clean; she had scrubbed the ink away that morning, knowing this conversation was coming. What she showed him instead was the faint ghost of the date she had written there days ago—the seizure date, thirty days hence, copied in her own careful script and half-erased.

"You're seizing the workshop," she said. "In thirty days. On your own signature. And four days after Aurel died, you paid someone *for the survey*. Father, if you are hiding, I can protect you—we can find a way. But if you are covering for the man who killed him, then I need to know now, tonight, while there is still a version of you I can save."

He came back to the desk. He did not answer her. He unlocked the second drawer—the drawer she had already violated—and drew out the oldest ledger, the one bound in cracked green leather, and set it on the blotter between them like a card played face up.

"You want a story," he said. "Here is the only one I will give you. This city runs on arrangements older than both of us, and the alternative to arrangements is chaos, and chaos kills more people than corruption ever has. Someday, when you hold my office, you will understand the difference between what should be true and what must be managed. I have spent thirty years managing." He opened the ledger to a ribbon-marked page. "What you will not do—you will not carry your schoolgirl conscience into rooms where grown men decide how the harbor stays open and the quarter stays fed. Is that understood?"

"No," Sera said. "It isn't."

He looked down at the open page. Whatever he saw there, whatever name or figure, his expression did not change—only his hands did, very slightly, tightening on the ledger's edge. Then he lifted the page free along its binding seam, crossed to the hearth where embers still shifted from the evening fire, and dropped it in.

Sera did not move. She would remember afterward that she did not move, did not cry out, did not beg him to stop, because some colder and older part of her understood instantly that this was the point. He was not destroying evidence. He was showing her what happened to evidence. He was answering her question in the only language left to them, and the language was flame.

The page curled. Ink darkened and flaked to ash. She caught one glimpse of figures before the heat ate them—a column of sums, an initial—and then it was black, and then it was nothing, and her father stood watching it burn with his back to her, his shoulders at last betraying something, though whether grief or relief she could not tell.

"There," he said softly. "Now it is managed."

"Father—"

"Go to bed, Sera." He did not turn. The firelight made a red rim of his gray hair. "You are my daughter, and I love you, and for that reason alone I am going to pretend this conversation happened in a dream, where all impossible things belong. But hear me once. If you carry what you think you know outside this house—if you take it to the inquiry board, to a rival magistrate, to anyone—there are men in this city to whom you will be a loose thread rather than a Voss. And loose threads get cut. I could not manage that. Do you understand me? I could not survive managing that."

The threat and the love were so braided together she could not pull them apart, and perhaps neither could he. That was the unbearable thing. He meant both sentences. He was warning her and shielding her in the same breath, and the door he thought he was closing was the one she had already walked through.

"Goodnight, Father," she heard herself say, in the courteous voice he had praised her for. "Sleep well."

At the threshold she stopped, one hand on the doorframe, and almost—almost—turned and asked him plainly: *Did you kill Aurel? Did you order it? Am I the daughter of a murderer or the daughter of a coward?* The question stood fully formed in her throat like a stone.

She swallowed it. There would be a better time, and a worse weapon, and she intended to find both.

* * *

She did not go to bed. She walked the long gallery above the entry hall where the Voss portraits hung, generations of upright faces rendered in oils, and she looked at none of them. In the tall window at the gallery's end, the harbor lay spread below like an open ledger—masts, lanterns, the dark line of the workshop quarter across the water, and beyond it the iron span of the old bridge, strung with its small persistent lights, connecting the city to the place where the truth lived in a satchel in a boy's keeping.

Thirty days. Then the seizure, and after the seizure the demolition, and after the demolition a gap of black water where a crossing had been for sixty years. Her father had signed the first order with the same hand that had burned the page. Whoever guided that hand—whoever the deputy was, whoever the payments served—had arranged matters so that her father's signature would be the murder weapon, leaving his own prints nowhere at all.

That was the shape of it now. She could see the whole design the way one sees a coastline from the sea: not the details, but the certainty that the details exist.

She pressed her palm flat against the cold window glass, over the tiny glitter of the bridge, and made her decision the way she made all of them—precisely, in ink that could not be erased. Tomorrow she would request the assignment formally. Tomorrow she would put herself inside the seizure, inside the raid, inside the room where the atlas would be found or hidden or taken, because it was the only vantage from which she could act, and because a loose thread, her father had neglected to mention, can also be pulled until the whole seam gives way.

Down in the harbor, far across the black water, a light in the workshop quarter guttered and steadied—someone awake past midnight, working by candle over paper, unaware of what was coming toward him wearing her face and her father's name.

Sera turned from the window and went to sharpen her pencils.
