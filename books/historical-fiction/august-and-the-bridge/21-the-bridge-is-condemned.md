## 21. The Bridge Is Condemned

The notice went up on a Tuesday, nailed to the customs house door with four brass tacks, and by noon half the quarter had pressed itself against the railing of the Salt Quay to read what none of them wanted spoken aloud.

Elodie heard it before he saw it — the particular hush that falls over a crowd when bad news is being passed mouth to mouth, each whisper softer than the last, as if volume might make it truer. He was carrying a tray of washed brushes out to dry in the pale sun when Marta came up the workshop stairs faster than her hips allowed, her dye-stained apron flapping, her face the color of unprimed linen.

"They've posted it," she said. "The bridge. Come and see."

He set the tray down. He would remember, afterward, that he had set it down carefully, square to the table's edge, because his hands needed something small and orderly to do while the rest of him understood what was coming.

The crowd parted for them without parting — the way water moves around a stone it has already decided to keep. The notice was a broadsheet in official black, the magistrate's crest stamped fat and red at the top like a wax seal on a corpse's mouth. Elodie read it twice, then a third time, because the words refused to arrange themselves into anything but their worst meaning:

*By order of the Office of the Magistracy of Vellamare, the Iron Bridge spanning the Narrows is hereby declared structurally condemned. Demolition shall commence on the morning following the execution of outstanding writs against properties within the Bridge Quarter, said demolition to be carried out by the Office of Works under supervision of the Deputy Magistrate's office. All crossings after that hour are prohibited. By order, etc.*

*The morning following.*

He did the arithmetic standing there, in the smell of tar and wet rope, while around him old men muttered and women pulled their shawls tighter as though the wind off the harbor had turned cold on purpose. The seizure order was thirty days from its signing. Sera had copied the date onto her palm; he had seen it there once, faintly inked below her thumb, and had thought at the time it was a note about tea.

Thirty days for the quarter. Thirty-one days for the bridge.

They were not going to leave him even one day of overlap. Or rather — they were leaving exactly one day, and one day only, and that single day was the whole design of it. The seizure first. Then the fall of the span. Anyone the magistrate's men decided to keep inside the quarter when the writs were executed would find the road behind them cut, the iron dropped into the Narrows, the city withdrawn like a hand from a glove. No appeal could outrun a wrecking charge. No petition could cross open water on foot.

"Condemned," Marta said beside him, tasting the word. "That bridge has stood since my grandmother was a girl. My boys used to dare each other to spit off the middle of it." Her voice stayed flat, practical, but Elodie had known her three years now and could hear the fear underneath, the way you hear the sea under a floorboard-flat calm. "It isn't rust they're condemning, Elodie."

"No," he said. "It's us."

* * *

He walked the length of it that evening, because he could not bear not to.

The Iron Bridge crossed the Narrows in a single shallow arc, four hundred feet of riveted steel and timber decking, built in the days when Vellamare had believed itself a city of the future. It had gone brown-black with a century of salt, and gulls stood along its upper chords like notes on a staff, and at its center, where the two halves of the arc met over deep water, the deck hummed very faintly underfoot — a vibration so old and constant that no one who lived with it heard it anymore, any more than they heard their own hearts.

Elodie heard it now. Now that he knew it was numbered.

He counted as he walked. Not rivets this time — he had counted those before, eleven thousand and some, on nights when sleep would not take him — but steps. Four hundred and twelve from the quarter's end to the far abutment, if you walked like a man with somewhere to be. Five hundred if you walked like a man memorizing.

The lamplighter was working his way along the city side, and the lamps came on one by one across the water, little suns rising in reverse order, and beyond them Vellamare proper climbed its hill in tiers of amber windows: the opera houses, the law courts, the wide clean streets where no one had ever been asked for papers at a doorway. He had lived in the shadow of that city all his life and never felt the distance between them more than a five-minute walk. Now he measured it differently. Now it was the distance between a man and the gallows, and it was going to be measured in falling steel.

A pair of dockhands passed him going the other way, and one of them — a big fellow whose face he half-knew from the fish market — slowed, looked at him, looked away, and said something low to his companion. Elodie caught only the shape of the words, not their substance, and told himself he had not heard the word *atlas* in them. He kept walking. His satchel strap lay empty against his hip and felt heavier for it.

At the midpoint he stopped. Below, the tide was turning, the water in the Narrows running green-black and fast, carrying out toward the harbor mouth a litter of orange peel, a broken oar, a paper boat some child had launched and abandoned. Everything the Narrows received, it carried away. That was the whole virtue of the place, Master Aurel used to say — the best hiding spot in Vellamare is moving water, because moving water keeps no inventory.

Aurel had died with his left hand stained and his right hand clean, and the coroner had written *accident*, and every route out of the Bridge Quarter now ran through a bridge scheduled to die the day after the magistrate's men came through the doors. Elodie stood in the humming center of the span and let himself think, for the first time plainly, the thought he had been circling for weeks the way a fly circles glass:

Someone had planned this. Someone had planned it long before the notice was printed. The condemned report was dated — he had noticed the date, because dates were his trade — and the date was older than the seizure order. The bridge had been murdered before it was killed. The paperwork, like everything else in this affair, had simply been waiting for the right morning to be posted.

He turned back toward the quarter. Behind him the city glowed, warm and lawful and closed.

* * *

Sera found the notice before she was meant to.

She had come down to the customs house on an errand for her father's clerks — copying schedules of the seized-properties docket, dull work assigned deliberately, she suspected, to keep her hands full and her eyes elsewhere — and she saw the broadsheet pasted beside the door, still glossy with paste, and read it standing in the gray light of early afternoon with her gloves on and her heart going strangely.

*Demolition shall commence on the morning following.*

She had copied the seizure date onto her palm ten days ago and had watched it fade since, letter by letter, as she washed her hands, until now only the month remained legible, a ghost of ink below her thumb. She had told herself the fading meant nothing. Orders changed; schedules slipped; the machinery of the magistracy ground slow.

But this notice did not slip. This notice fit. She held her palm open, looked at the ghost-date, and then at the demolition date on the board, and saw how neatly the two interlocked, tooth into gear, and understood that nothing here was slipping. Something was being assembled.

Her father had signed the seizure order. Had her father signed this?

She asked the duty clerk, casually, pen still in hand, whether the condemnation order had come through the ordinary channels. The clerk — young, freckled, terrified of her in the way junior clerks were terrified of the magistrate's daughter — consulted his ledger with trembling fingers.

"No, mademoiselle. It came direct from the Deputy's office. Deputy Corven's office. We weren't — we weren't consulted on the engineering assessment, mademoiselle, if that's what you mean. The Works office hasn't filed one either, yet. The condemnation cites an assessment that hasn't been lodged." He realized, visibly, what he had just said, and went the color of his own ledger paper. "I'm sure it will be lodged, mademoiselle. In due course. They always lodge it after, when—"

"When it can't be disputed," Sera finished quietly.

The clerk said nothing. The silence said it for him.

She walked home the long way, along the quay, and stood where the crowd had stood that morning, though the crowd was gone now and only the notice remained, patient as a trap. Across the Narrows the Bridge Quarter sat low and dark against the water — the crooked rooftops, the dyers' lines strung between buildings like signal flags, and above them all the copper roof of Aurel's old workshop, gone green with years, catching what light there was.

She thought of Elodie's hands, ink-stained to the second knuckle, moving over vellum with a precision she had spent three years in her father's offices learning to recognize and never once seen matched there. She thought of tea cooling between them while they talked around a truth shaped like a knife. She thought of her father at his desk, and of a page of figures burning in the grate while he watched her watch it.

*The morning following.* If the writs were executed on schedule, the people of the quarter would have one day's warning and no means of using it. One day to carry out whatever could be carried. One day, and then four hundred feet of open water where a road had been.

And she had copied the date onto her palm like a schoolgirl keeping an appointment, when she should have copied it onto a protest, or a resignation, or a letter to the Crown assizes.

"Sera Voss," she said aloud, to the empty quay, and the name sounded suddenly like someone else's — someone she had inherited rather than chosen, a garment fitted to her at birth and buttoned ever since.

She took out her notebook, and beneath a column of property schedules she wrote the demolition date, and then, after a moment, drew a small careful line beneath it, the way a surveyor marks a boundary. Then she closed the book, and put on the face she wore in her father's offices, and walked home past the sentries at the gate who saluted her because they did not know yet what she was becoming.

* * *

In the workshop, Elodie worked until the candles failed him, which was later than sense allowed.

He had hung the notice's logic on the wall of his mind like one of the master's charts: *seizure, then span.* Thirty days minus however many had already bled away. Against it he set the atlas — nine plates finished, three in progress, the marginalia hidden in rollers like letters in a soldier's boot — and against the atlas, the question that had no honest answer: *carry it out when? Across what?*

He spread the chart of the Narrows under the lamp and looked at it as he had never looked at it before: not as a mapmaker, but as a fugitive. The bridge was drawn there in fine black line, elegant, permanent. Beneath it he wrote, in the smallest fair hand he owned, the demolition date. Then the seizure date. Then he sat back and looked at the gap between them — one day, a sliver of white vellum no wider than a fingernail — and understood that his entire future now lived inside that sliver.

One day. One crossing. One chance to move an atlas, a household's papers, Marta's sons' futures, and the truth about a murdered man out of a quarter that was about to become an island.

He thought of Sera's palm, the faded ink. She had given him the seizure date once, wordlessly, holding her hand open over the teacups as though showing him a wound. Would she give him this one too? Could she? Every such gift moved her further out onto a ledge above her father's world, and he hated himself a little for already hoping she would.

Outside, the copper roof ticked as the night cooled. Down in the street, someone was walking past the shuttered workshops slowly — too slowly — and pausing, once, outside the door. Elodie blew out the candle and stood in the dark until the footsteps moved on.

On the wall chart, the bridge ran thin and black across the water, drawn in a hand that trusted the world to keep its promises.

He dipped his pen, and beneath the demolition date, added a single word in gold — the color he reserved for things worth saving:

*Before.*

* * *

Marta heard the news from the fishwives and carried it home like a stone in her apron pocket.

She gathered her boys in the kitchen — Jakob scowling at sixteen, Tomas at eleven still soft enough to cry and trying not to — and told them plainly, because lies were a currency her family had never been able to afford. "When the writs come," she said, "you go where I tell you, when I tell you, and you don't ask why. And Tomas—" she caught his chin, made him look at her, "—if the soldiers come and Mama isn't home, you run to the cellar and you stay there until Elodie Nakamura comes for you. Do you hear me? Him and no one else."

"Why him?" Jakob asked.

"Because he's the only one in this quarter," Marta said, "with a map of every way out."

After the boys slept she sat a long while by the banked fire, doing her own arithmetic. Thirty days. One day's grace. And somewhere in the magistrate's offices, a man with smooth hands and a deputy's chain had arranged it all so neatly that even the paperwork would arrive after the fact, tidy as a confession dictated to the victim.

She had seen a man die in that workshop. She had kept her mouth shut for her sons' sake, and the keeping-shut had grown roots, and now the roots were strangling her. She looked at the sleeping loft where her boys breathed in the dark, and at the door she'd need only forty steps to reach, and across those forty steps to the workshop beyond, where a boy with ink in his blood was racing a calendar he didn't fully understand.

*One day,* she thought. *They've left everyone one day. Unless someone gives them another.*

She put another stick on the fire, and began, very quietly, to plan how a woman breaks twenty months of silence.
