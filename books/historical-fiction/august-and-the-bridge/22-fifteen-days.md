## 22. Fifteen Days

The tide table was the first thing Elodie copied into the back of his notebook, in a hand smaller than any he used for coastlines. He had taken it from the harbormaster's office window on the Passeig, where the day's chart was posted behind glass for the benefit of fishermen who could not afford almanacs, and he had stood there long enough that a boy selling roasted chestnuts had asked him twice if he wanted any. He did not want chestnuts. He wanted to know, to the minute, when the harbor would be at its emptiest beneath the old iron bridge, and what the water would do while he was on it.

Fifteen days. The seizure order was signed; Sera had told him the date in the only way she could, pressing her copy of it — the words written on her palm in her own hand, ink gone faintly violet against her skin — against his when they had parted under the awning of the chandlery, an embrace disguised as a handshake, or a handshake that wished it were an embrace. Fifteen days until the magistrate's men came up Copper Roof Lane with their inventory ledgers and their wax seals and their boots, and fifteen days after that — no, one day after that, which was worse than after, which was *before anything could be done about it* — the bridge would fall.

He had begun to think of time not as days but as crossings.

* * *

The first test he made with nothing in his hands at all. It was a little past two in the morning, the hour when even Vellamare's insomniac quarter went quiet, when the last lamp in Marta's dye-house guttered out and the only light on the water came from the harbor beacon turning its slow white eye across the swell. Elodie left by the workshop's rear door, took the alley behind the tallow-chandler's, and came up onto the bridge from the quarter side the way a man approaches a horse he does not yet trust.

The Ponte Ferro had carried cart traffic and foot traffic for sixty years, and its bones showed it. The iron was rust-brown and black, furred at the joints with orange blooms of corrosion, and every third rivet along the near rail had been painted over so many times it looked like a candle stub frozen mid-drip. At its center the span rose in a shallow arch, high enough for masts of modest height to pass beneath, and from that arch hung the lampposts — four of them, gaslit, though the city had let two go dark years ago and never relit them. Two lamps burning. It was, Elodie thought, exactly the kind of arithmetic a city performs when it has already decided a thing is dead.

He walked the length of it slowly, and he listened.

A bridge talks. Master Aurel had taught him that, years ago, on the one evening walk they had ever taken together — a rare indulgence, Aurel in his good coat, pointing with his stick at the harbor and saying, *Draw me this from memory tomorrow, boy, and I will know if you looked or merely saw.* The bridge had creaked and ticked around them then, expanding off the day's heat, and Aurel had said: everything made by hands keeps talking after the hands are gone. You only have to learn the language. Now Elodie walked and listened and heard the Ponte Ferro's vocabulary: the low groan at the second pier where the current scoured, the metallic shiver in the railing when the wind came off the open sea, the small ringing footsteps of his own boots on the deck plates, each one distinct in the night like a coin dropped in an empty room.

Halfway across he stopped, over the deepest channel, and looked down. The water was black and moving and forty feet below, and the tide was nearly full, the harbor holding all it could hold. In fifteen days there would be charges set into these piers. He tried to imagine the iron coming apart, tried to make his mind hold the picture, and found he could not. His mind kept sliding off it, the way a blade slides off a nail head. Some things the hand refuses to draw before the eye consents to see them.

He turned and walked back. Four hundred paces, give or take, at an old man's stroll. He counted without meaning to; counting was what his hands did when they were empty.

* * *

The second test, three nights later, he made with the satchel half-loaded.

Loading it was itself a discipline he had not anticipated. The atlas — the true atlas, the one whose plates lived inside map rollers by day and came out only when he worked — weighed more than it looked, because paper lies about its weight the way stone doesn't. Nine finished plates of heavy rag paper, each mounted on linen, each interleaved with oiled silk; the folio of marginalia transcriptions, which he had recopied in cipher onto thinner stock to save weight; the gold leaf, which weighed almost nothing and was therefore the most dangerous thing in the bag, because a thing that weighs nothing is easy to crush. Then the tools he could not leave behind if the worst happened and he had to begin again somewhere: two pens, the burnisher, the shell of gum arabic wrapped in waxed cloth, a folding rule. He laid everything out on the workbench under the single shaded lamp and weighed each item in his palm like a man pricing his own life by the ounce.

The satchel was Master Aurel's — cracked black leather, brass buckles green at the edges, a strap worn to a shine by a shoulder broader than Elodie's. When he slung it, it settled against his hip with the familiarity of an old dog, and he felt the way he always felt wearing it: that he was borrowing a shape his master had made in the world and had not yet grown into it.

Two in the morning again. The tide would turn at 2:41; he had checked the glass twice before leaving. Half-loaded, the satchel pulled differently than empty leather, and he wanted to know how it changed his feet.

The bridge received him without comment. He walked faster this time — a working pace, the pace of a man late for something that mattered — and at the crown of the arch the wind came up off the water and pushed at him sideways, and he felt the load swing, a small pendulum at his hip trying to conduct him like music. He shortened his stride until the swing died. That was lesson one, learned and filed: short steps, the strap gripped in the left hand, the right hand free. A man crossing with something precious crosses like a waiter carrying soup.

At the center he stopped again and looked down, and this time the water gave him something to use. The tide had begun to turn; he could see it, or rather see the boats below — two fishing luggers moored in the channel, and they had swung, gently, both bows now pointing toward the sea instead of the town. The whole harbor quietly reorienting itself in the dark, thousands of tons of water changing its mind, and no sound to mark it but the soft knock of hulls against mooring buoys.

*Everything made by hands keeps talking.* The tide talked too. And the tide, unlike the bridge, was on a schedule posted in glass for anyone to read.

2:41. Slack water, more or less. Low water would follow roughly six hours on — no. He stopped himself, standing in the wind with his lips moving over arithmetic, and laughed once, a small startled breath of a laugh, because he realized what he was doing: he was rehearsing. Not the crossing. The *explanation*. As if some examining voice would demand why the tide mattered, and he must have his reasoning ready. Nobody would ask. The tide mattered for one reason only, and the reason was this: at low water, the drop from the deck to the harbor bed was shallower by eleven feet, and eleven feet of black water was the difference between a wet man climbing out on the mud and a drowned one pinned under falling iron. If the charges fired while he was mid-span — and he had to plan as if they might, because men like the deputy did not keep appointments — then low water was his margin. His one margin. Everything else he would engineer down to the rivet. The rest he would leave to eleven feet of luck.

He walked on. Three hundred and ten paces loaded, at working speed. Better than he'd guessed.

* * *

By the fifth night the tests had become ritual, and ritual, he knew, was just fear that had learned to organize itself.

He varied the hours — 1:20, 2:05, 3:00 — walking the span with the satchel fuller each time, and he kept a page in the notebook that looked, to anyone who might ever steal it, like nothing at all: columns of times, tide heights, weather notes. *Wind NE light. Rail damp — grip w/ glove. Lamp 3 lit again, city lamplighter drunk or diligent.* Underneath, in cipher, the real ledger: *Full load, 4 min 10 sec quarter-to-city. Full load against wind, 5 min dead. Deck plate loose at pier 3, count 14 from seaward rail — report? To whom?*

That question he wrote and crossed out and wrote again. *To whom?* There was no office in Vellamare to which one reported a loose plate on a condemned bridge. The city had already spoken its mind about the Ponte Ferro; a loose rivet was simply the bridge dying ahead of schedule.

On the fifth night he made the full-load crossing, and it nearly broke him, and that too was information.

He had packed the satchel as he would pack it on the true day: atlas complete, master's letters, the gold leaf, the cipher folio, and — because he could no longer separate the atlas's survival from the household's — the small oilcloth packet of documents Sera had risked copying for him, the payment schedules that matched the routes in the margins, the evidence that reached upward into the magistrate's own house. Heavy. All of it heavier than the sum of its papers, the way grief is heavier than the body it sits in. He came up onto the deck at 2:15 with the tide three hours off low, and the wind was stronger than forecast, a nor'easterly with rain in it somewhere out over the dark, and by the time he reached the arch's crown his shoulders were burning and his breath came in the shallow sips of a man climbing stairs.

He stopped at the top. Set his hand on the cold rail. Made himself look down through the cross-bracing at the water, far below now with the tide dropped, showing ribbed mud at the channel edges like the exposed ribs of some enormous animal.

And the thought arrived, unbidden and precise as a ruled line: *I cannot run.*

Not *I am not strong enough.* Something cleaner and more terrible than that. The atlas could not run. Paper does not sprint. Whatever happened on the day — seizure, demolition, the deputy's men, fire — the plan could not be flight. Flight was for things with legs. For fifteen days he had been framing this as an escape, a smuggler's problem, weight and route and tide, and the whole frame was wrong. He was not carrying the atlas *away*. He was carrying it *across*. Across to the city, across to the inquiry Sera had forced into being, across into daylight, where a document becomes testimony instead of contraband. The distance was four hundred paces. The destination was not the far shore. The destination was a room full of people obliged, finally, to look.

His breathing steadied. His shoulders, absurdly, eased. He stood at the crown of the old bridge in the wind with forty pounds of truth dragging at his hip, and he felt, for the first time since the letter with the magistrate's crest had come through the workshop door, something adjacent to calm.

Then he turned and saw the light.

It was small and yellow and it moved — not on the bridge, but below and beyond it, on the mudflats exposed by the ebb, a lantern traveling fast along a path only its bearer knew. Elodie flattened himself against the rail without deciding to, an apprentice's reflex, the instinct of a man whose work has taught him that being seen is being lost. The light picked its way out toward the bridge's second pier, stopped, and rose and fell twice — deliberately, unmistakably, a signal.

From the city side, after a long moment, another light answered. Twice.

Elodie stood pressed to the freezing iron with his heart going like a hammer on sheet metal, and made himself watch, and made himself think. Not smugglers — the atlas had shown him the smugglers' ways, and none ran here, under the eyes of two quarters. Not the deputy's men; they moved by day, with paper, in boots that wanted to be heard. This was something else. Someone meeting someone, on the flats beneath the very bridge he meant to save himself across, twelve nights before the whole span was scheduled to come down — and men do not signal to each other on empty mudflats at three in the morning about anything innocent.

The two lights converged at the base of the pier. They burned side by side, steady now, close enough that he could see, when the wind tore a gap in the cloud, two dark shapes standing close together, heads bent — one tall, one carrying itself with the unmistakable forward-leaning roll of a man favoring one leg.

The limp. The naval roll he had seen crossing Copper Roof Lane, camera under arm, asking Marta about *the Japanese boy*.

Elodie did not breathe. Below him, the limping man raised his lantern, and — slowly, deliberately, the gesture of a showman or a threat — swept its beam once along the underside of the bridge, tracing the piers, the cross-bracings, the places a man would look if he were choosing where to set charges. Or where he had already set them.

The lights went out. The shapes moved off across the mud, and the tide, patient as arithmetic, began to come back in over every trace of their path.
