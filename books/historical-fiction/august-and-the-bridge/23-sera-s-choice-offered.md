## 23. Sera's Choice Offered

The deputy's office smelled of lamp oil and old decisions.

Sera Voss had been summoned there at half past seven in the morning, before the clerks had finished lighting the lamps in the corridor, before the harbor fog had burned off the windows, and she understood—before he said a single word—that something in the geometry of her life had shifted. Deputy Corvane did not summon people early unless he intended to hand them something heavy. She had watched him do it to others: the young surveyor he'd sent to condemn the fish-market stalls, the harbor clerk assigned to inventory the drowned cargo of the *Meridian*. He gave weight to people whose hands he wanted to test, or whose hands he wanted to dirty. There was rarely a difference, in his arithmetic.

"Sit, Voss."

She sat. The chair was low; his desk was high; the arrangement was not accidental. Behind him, mounted on the wall between a chart of the harbor approaches and a framed commendation signed by her father, hung a sabre that had not been worn since the border war. It was a room built to remind a visitor of permanence. Sera had learned, in four years of auditing for the magistrate's office, that the men who decorated with permanence were usually the least certain of it.

Corvane turned a paper toward her with one finger. The limp was worse this season, she noticed—the drag of his left heel audible now even across carpet, a soft percussion under everything he said, like a metronome set to an unhurried tempo.

"The seizure order for the mapmaker's workshop," he said. "Thirty days from signature. Your father signed it on Tuesday." He let that sit. "It executes in nineteen days."

"I know the file," Sera said. Her voice was level. She had practiced levelness the way other women practiced piano.

"You know the file better than anyone in this building. You audited the workshop twice. You inventoried the plates. You interviewed the apprentice—" a thin pause, the paper of his mouth folding, "—Nakamura. Twice, I believe."

"The second interview was a follow-up on the plate count discrepancy."

"Was it." Not a question. He folded his hands atop the order, and she saw that beneath them the document bore her father's seal, wax-red and intact, and beside it a line where a second signature belonged. Hers.

"You're assigning me the seizure," she said.

"I'm offering you the honor of it." Corvane rose, limped to the window, and stood with his back to her, watching the fog peel off the harbor. "The quarter will be sealed the day after. The bridge comes down. Whatever that workshop contains—plates, ledgers, correspondence—it must be catalogued by someone whose judgment this office trusts absolutely." He turned. The light through the fog made his face the color of wet stone. "There is no one in this building I trust more than the magistrate's own daughter."

Flattery, delivered like a verdict. Sera kept her eyes on the order, on the empty line awaiting her name. Nineteen days. She thought of the key plate she had read by candlelight three nights ago—the routes traced in Aurel's hidden marginalia, the dates, the payments that matched her father's ledgers entry for entry—and felt the floor of her certainties tilt again, the way it had tilted the night she first found those numbers, the way it tilted every time she let herself remember that Elodie Nakamura's hands had drawn coastlines more honest than any ledger in this building.

*Say no*, said something in her that sounded like candlelight on copper roofing, like rain, like a voice saying *you shouldn't be here* and meaning *stay.*

But she knew what refusal meant. Refusal was a flag planted. Corvane did not suspect idly—he suspected precisely, the way he did everything—and a refusal would confirm what the suspicion already knew: that she had read things she should not have read, that her questions about the plate discrepancies were not diligence but devotion, that somewhere between her first audit of the workshop and this cold morning she had stopped being the magistrate's daughter and become something the deputy would need to handle.

And if she refused, he would simply assign the seizure to someone blunter. Someone who would break the rollers. Someone who would burn first and catalogue never.

"There are irregularities in the file," she said carefully.

"There were irregularities. They've been resolved." Corvane returned to the desk and sat, and the chair received him with a creak like a ship taking load. "The atlas recovered from the premises has been authenticated. Master Aurel's final work. Smugglers' routes, as suspected. Case substantially closed pending seizure and formal deposition."

*The false atlas.* Elodie's decoy, with its doctored tides and its invented coves, sitting in the evidence vault wearing the dead man's name. She had seen it once, through the vault grille, and marveled again at the craft of the lie—how lovingly wrong every coastline was, how the errors were themselves a signature only two living people could read. She had reported none of it. That silence was already a crime. This assignment would make the crime official.

"If you doubt the file," Corvane said, and here his voice changed, softened into something almost paternal, which was infinitely worse than the metronome voice, "then all the more reason you should lead the seizure. Doubt is resolved by contact with evidence, Voss. Not by brooding." He slid the paper across the desk and set a pen beside it. "Your father signed it without hesitation. I'd expected better of you than hesitation."

There it was. Not duty offered—duty *weaponized*, loaded with her love for her father, aimed at the one place she could not armor. If she signed, she raided the man she loved with her own hands and made her betrayal legible to everyone, including herself. If she refused, she told Corvane that the magistrate's daughter had chosen a half-Japanese apprentice over the office, and the machinery that had swallowed Master Aurel would turn toward her with all its patience.

Either pen lifted her hand, some part of her was lost.

She thought, absurdly, of Marta the dyer, whom she'd passed in the lane below the workshop weeks ago—how the woman's hands were permanently stained blue to the wrist, how she had looked at Sera with neither fear nor deference but a kind of weary assessment, as if measuring how much truth a person could carry before it stained them too.

Sera picked up the pen.

She wrote her name in the small, exact hand her tutors had praised, the hand that had audited harbors and grain tariffs and the drowning accounts of a shipping concern her father might have owned shares in, and when she finished she found she had pressed hard enough that the nib had nearly cut the paper. The signature looked like a wound in the document.

"Nineteen days," Corvane said, taking the order back. He examined her signature with the satisfaction of a man testing a knot. "You'll have six men. Warrant executed at dawn. Inventory completed by noon. And Voss—" he paused at the door of his own office, hand on the frame, not looking at her, "—the apprentice is to be present throughout. His cooperation is noted in the file. Should it become... less noted, you'll inform me personally. Before anyone else."

*Personally.* She heard the word's second edge. He wasn't just giving her the raid. He was giving her a test with a witness attached.

"Understood," she said, and walked out through the corridor of waking lamps with her spine straight and her heart going like a trapped bird against her ribs, and she did not let her breath shake until she reached the stairwell, where the fog-light came gray through the windows and no one could see her put her hand flat against the cold stone wall and stand there, breathing, counting, the way she had counted the date onto her palm weeks ago.

Nineteen days. Now they were hers.

* * *

She went to the bridge that evening because she could not go to the workshop.

That was the new rule she was writing for herself: after the signature, contact was confession. If Corvane had watchers—and of course he had watchers, the man with the naval limp did not assign raids without first mapping the territory around them—then every visit to the workshop quarter was a thread he could pull. So she stood instead at the city end of the old iron bridge as dusk came down over the harbor, and she looked across at the far shore where the workshop quarter huddled under its copper roofs going green-black in the wet air, and she tried to see it as the law saw it: a district to be sealed, a span to be dropped, a list of names to be filed under conspiracy.

She could not do it. The quarter refused to be an abstraction. It was Marta's dye-works exhaling steam that smelled of madder and iron; it was the chandler's boy running the bridge planks with a lantern; it was the sound, faint across the water, of someone practicing a fiddle badly and cheerfully. It was a workshop window glowing amber where a man she loved was probably working by candlelight, hunched over vellum, ink to his knuckles, doing with terrible patience whatever the plan on the bridge had required of him.

They had drafted it here, eleven days ago, in the wind: expose the deputy at the seizure itself, using the atlas as testimony, using the public ritual of the law to hang the law's own corruption in front of witnesses. It had seemed clean then. Possible. She had even believed, in the flush of it, that she might come through with her commission intact—that justice, properly handled, would reward rather than devour her.

Now the seizure had a face, and the face was hers.

*He knew,* she thought, staring at the amber window. *He knew when he handed me the pen.* Corvane's suspicion needed confirmation, and what surer confirmation than to watch the magistrate's daughter lead a raid and note exactly what she catalogued gently and what she overlooked? Or—worse, cleverer—to watch her fail to find things that had already been moved, proving she'd been warned? Every choice she made inside that workshop would be read. She was not being given the raid. She was being placed inside it like a instrument designed to measure betrayal, and whatever needle reading resulted, Corvane would act upon.

Unless she gave him nothing to measure. Unless she performed the perfect seizure—thorough, impartial, ruthless—and trusted that the true atlas was already beyond his reach, hidden in map rollers or cellar stones or carried in pieces through Marta's dyehouse, and that on the morning of the raid she would "discover" precisely the discrepancies that would force the inquiry into the open. Play the huntress so well that the trap she sprang was his.

It required telling Elodie. Which required breaking the rule she had made an hour ago. Which required the bridge.

She stepped onto the ironwork. The planks rang softly under her heels, that familiar hollow music, and below her the tide ran dark between the pilings, carrying lamplight in broken ribbons. Halfway across she stopped, out of old habit, at the point where the city lights fell behind and the quarter's lights hadn't yet arrived—a pocket of dusk suspended between jurisdictions, hers alone.

Footsteps behind her. The drag of a heel.

Her whole body went still. Then the steps resolved—not the deputy's measured limp but a lighter gait, uneven in a different way, hurried, and a voice she knew better than her own said, "You shouldn't be standing here."

Elodie came up beside her out of the gloom, collar up, a satchel strap crossed over his chest like a bandolier, and the sight of him undid her composure more thoroughly than the deputy's entire performance. He looked thinner. Candlelit weeks had carved him.

"You can't be here either," she whispered. "If you're seen—"

"I wasn't followed. Marta watches the lanes; she'd have whistled." He glanced past her toward the far shore, then back, and his eyes went to her hands, as if expecting to find them already holding a warrant. "Something's happened."

How did he always know. She looked at the black water rather than his face. "He gave me the seizure, Elodie. Corvane. Nineteen days. Dawn execution. Six men, full inventory, and—" her voice caught on the last of it, and she made herself finish, because he deserved the whole blade, "—and my signature on the order. I signed it. In his office. With him watching."

Silence, except the tide. When she finally made herself look at him, she expected hurt, and there was hurt—but beneath it something else, a quick calculation, the craftsman assessing damaged material to see what could still be built from it.

"He wants to watch you raid me," Elodie said slowly. "To see what your hands do."

"Yes."

"Then we let him watch." A grim light kindled in his face, and God help her, she loved him for it—that his answer to a trap was not retreat but redesign. "You'll conduct the most meticulous seizure in the history of this office. You'll catalogue every plate of the false atlas. You'll be flawless. And on that morning, in front of your six men and whoever else he stations to observe, you'll find what the false atlas cannot hide—the paper stock that doesn't match Aurel's stock, the tide tables that contradict his own notation, the dates that fall after his death." He was speaking faster now, leaning close so the words wouldn't carry off the bridge. "You won't accuse anyone. Accusation is what he's waiting for. You'll simply *report discrepancies*, in writing, through channels, so it becomes a matter of record that cannot be buried without more records."

"And when the inquiry convenes—"

"Then the true atlas surfaces where it will do the most damage. With witnesses. On the record." He straightened. Under the distant lamplight his face was half shadow, and she saw how much this cost him—not the danger to himself, she realized, but the danger the plan would inevitably pull across her. "Sera. If it goes wrong, the deputy won't distinguish between us. You need to understand that what you signed today puts you inside the blast radius. I would spare you if I could, and I can't, and I'm sorry."

She reached out and took his hand—the right one, the ink-stained one, the fingers rough with pen calluses and fine as an instrument—and held it over the black running water between the rails.

"My father signed the original order without hesitation," she said quietly. "Corvane told me so today. As though it were a virtue." She turned Elodie's hand over, tracing the stain at the edge of his thumbnail, violet-dark, permanent-looking. "I hesitated for the length of a pen stroke. That's all the distance between my family and everything I now believe. One stroke of a pen." She looked up at him. "So no. No sparing. We do this together or not at all."

For a moment he said nothing, and the bridge hummed faintly under their feet with the passage of some late cart on the far shore, and the tide turned below them with a sigh like the building letting go of a long-held breath.

"Together," he said.

They parted at the midpoint, as they always did—he back toward the quarter and the amber window, she back toward the city and the tall gray house where her father ate dinner behind closed doors and burned ledger pages in hearths. At the crest of the span she stopped once and looked back. He was already a dark shape dissolving into the far shore, into the maze of lanes that in nineteen days would be sealed, in twenty-one days cut loose entirely when the charges dropped this beautiful doomed span into the harbor.

Nineteen days to perform perfection. Nineteen days to learn, line by line, the script of her own treachery—treachery against the office, or against the man who had weaponized her duty; she no longer knew which word applied to which side, and suspected the deputy didn't either, and suspected further that his not-knowing was the entire architecture of the trap.

At home, she washed, dressed for dinner, and descended to find her father's study door ajar, lamplight leaking through, her father's voice low within it—and a second voice answering. Measured. Unhurried. Accompanied, faintly, by the soft drag of a heel on carpet.

"—the daughter leads it, then," the second voice was saying. "Good. Watch what she touches."

Sera stood in the corridor with her hand on the banister, her heartbeat loud enough to fill the house, and understood that the rehearsal for her raid had just begun—and that the first scene was being performed whether she joined the cast or not.
