## 25. Marta's Sons

The knock came at the hour when the dye vats were cooling and the whole street smelled of wet wool and mordant, and Marta knew before she opened the door that it was not a knock meant for her.

Three of them stood in the fading light: two constables in the magistrate's grey-and-blue, and between them a clerk with a ledger under his arm who did not meet her eyes. She had lived in Vellamare long enough—thirty-one years, married here, widowed here, buried a husband beneath the cypress on the hill—to know the difference between a knock that came for debts and a knock that came for blood. This was the second kind. It had a politeness to it, a rehearsed gentleness, like the hands of a man preparing to take something from a shelf he did not want to wake.

"Mother of Tamás Yllir?" the elder constable asked, though she had seen him buy thread from her stall at the market and knew his wife's name and the name of his wife's sister and the color of the ribbon his daughter had worn on Naming Day last spring. That was the way of the quarter. Everyone knew everyone, which was why it cut so strangely now, how quickly the knowing had stopped.

"I am," she said.

"We have questions regarding your son's movements on the night of the ninth."

"The ninth of what month?" she asked, though her hands had already gone cold inside her sleeves. The ninth. Everyone in the workshop quarter knew what the ninth meant. It was the night Master Aurel had been found at the foot of his own stairs with his neck broken and his spectacles folded neatly—folded neatly, as if he had stopped on his way down to tidy them—on the landing beside him.

The constable consulted his ledger-keeper, who turned a page with a damp thumb. "The ninth past."

"My Tamás was home." Her voice came out flat and even, and she marveled at it, the way one marvels at ice holding when a cart rolls over it. "He ate supper with me and his brother. He went to bed. He has not been out past dark since the winter he was fourteen, when I thrashed him for it, and he remembers."

"Nevertheless." The younger constable shifted his weight, and behind him, down the lane, she saw doors closing one after another like eyes shutting—the Kessler shutters, the dyer's boy pulling the shop door inward, old Fen at the cooperage turning his back mid-sentence. The whole street performing its own disappearance. "We'll need him to come answer some questions at the magistrate's office. Just questions. He's not charged with anything."

*Not charged with anything.* She had heard those words before, years ago, spoken about a fishwife whose husband had run afoul of a harbor official. The woman had repeated them like a prayer for six weeks, right up until they released what was left of him.

"He works at dawn," Marta said. "He needs his sleep."

"Madam." The clerk spoke for the first time, and his voice was soft, almost kind, which frightened her more than anything else. "You may bring him yourself, tomorrow morning, if you prefer. Or we may bring him tonight."

There it was. The scale laid out plain, with her son in one pan.

She went inside without asking their leave, climbed the narrow stair, and stood in the doorway of the room her sons shared. Tamás was mending a net by candlelight, his great clumsy hands moving with the surprising delicacy he reserved for knots, and Jozsef lay asleep in the cot beneath the window, one arm flung over his face, nineteen years old and still sleeping like a child thrown down by a summer afternoon.

Tamás looked up. He saw her face, and set down the net.

"They've come for you," she said quietly. "Questions about the ninth."

Her son did not look surprised. That was the thing she would carry afterward, through all the nights that followed—that he did not look surprised. He looked like a man who had heard weather coming for days and finally felt the first drop land.

"Did you do something?" she whispered.

"No, Mother." And then, after a silence in which the candle guttered: "But I saw something. The night the mapmaker died. I was coming back late from the pier—there was herring money nobody had claimed, and I—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "I saw a man come out of the workshop door. Not Aurel. A gentleman. Clean boots. He walked toward the bridge, and he walked wrong on one leg, like the sea had given him a limp he never paid for. And the light was on upstairs in the workshop after, and then it wasn't."

Marta stood very still. Below her feet, below the floorboards and the cellar where she kept her dyes, below the packed earth of Vellamare itself, something seemed to shift—a settling, like a house deciding which way it would fall.

"You said nothing," she said.

"To whom? To the constables?" Tamás gave a short, terrible laugh. "A gentleman with clean boots, Mother. Who do you think their questions are for?"

She could not argue with him. She had spent twenty-two years teaching her sons which silences kept them alive, and now the lesson sat in the room with them like a third person, and she hated every hour she had ever spent on it.

"They'll keep me overnight," Tamás said, already folding the net with the calm of a man packing for a trip he'd expected. "Ask your questions, give me bread and water, let me go tomorrow with an apology. That's how it goes when they want to frighten, not to hang."

"And if they want to hang?"

He didn't answer. He crossed to the cot and looked down at his brother for a long moment, and then he kissed his own fingers and touched them to Jozsef's hair, the way their father used to, and Marta understood that her eldest son had already written whatever needed writing and left it somewhere she would find it later.

At the door, before he stepped out into the lane where the constables waited, he put his hand briefly on her shoulder. His hand was enormous. When had her boys grown hands like that?

"Don't do anything foolish, Mother," he said. "Whatever you're thinking. Don't."

And then he was gone, walking between the grey coats with his head up, and the lane swallowed him, and every shutter on the street stayed closed.

* * *

Jozsef woke near midnight asking where his brother was, and Marta told him, and watched the color leave her younger son's face by candle-inch. He was the tender one, Jozsef; he had wept when their father died and wept again at the funeral of a dog three years later, and he wore his heart where other men wore watches, easy to read and easy to break.

"They took Tamás because of the mapmaker's boy," he said. "Everyone says so. Everyone says the workshop is cursed now."

"Everyone should learn to stop saying things," Marta snapped, sharper than she meant, and Jozsef flinched, and immediately she wanted to gather him up the way she had when he was small enough to gather. Instead she sat down across from him at the table, where the dye stains had soaked so deep into the wood grain that no amount of scrubbing would ever lift them—blue at the edges, rust-red in the center, thirty years of color ground into the grain of things—and she made herself speak slowly.

"Listen to me. Your brother will come home. But if anyone asks you anything—if a man comes to this door with questions—you tell them nothing. You know nothing. You were home. You are always home. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"Yes, Mother." Then, wretchedly: "Is that true, though? Are we always home?"

She looked at her youngest son and saw the question underneath the question, the one children begin asking when they are old enough to see their parents whole: *What else are you hiding?*

"There are kinds of truth," Marta said, "that are heavier than a family can carry. You'll understand when you're older."

"When Father died you said the same thing."

"And you were older when you stopped asking, weren't you?" She reached across the table and took his hand. It was still soft. Tamás's hands had gone hard at twelve on the piers; Jozsef's had stayed soft helping her with the fine work, the silk, the delicate baths. "Sleep now."

But she did not sleep. She sat by the banked fire while the house ticked and settled around her, and she thought about Aurel Nakamura—not the mapmaker, not the master, just Aurel, the quiet foreigner who had bought saffron from her twice and once repaired, unasked and unpaid, an old brass astrolabe her husband had treasured, saying only, *It was a good instrument. It deserved better than a drawer.*

She thought about the night he died, and about what she had seen from her window.

* * *

It had been raining that night. She remembered because the gutters had been singing, that low wooden thrum they made in hard rain, and because she had gotten up to move a bolt of indigo-dyed wool away from the wall where the roof leaked. It was past eleven. From the small window over her drying loft, if she leaned, she could see the workshop's upper story, its copper roof green-black with rain, its one lit window throwing a rectangle of gold onto the wet cobbles of the yard between the buildings.

She had seen the carriage first. No lamps. A fine carriage, too fine for the quarter, standing silent in the rain with its horse steaming, and she had thought: *smuggler*, because everyone knew the workshop sat atop smugglers' routes, everyone had always known, and it had never mattered to her because Aurel was kind and Elodie was polite and the routes ran under the quarter like groundwater, touching everything and harming no one who minded their own business.

Then the gentleman had come out of the workshop door, and stood a moment in the rectangle of lamplight, and she had seen him clearly despite the rain: broad, silvering at the temples, a coat worth more than her house, and when he turned to signal the carriage, the turn had begun in a stiff hip and rolled wrong down his leg. A naval walk. A sea-legs limp.

And then he had gone *back inside*, and that was the part she had never told anyone, the part that had sat in her chest for months like a stone she'd swallowed. Why go back? She had leaned closer to the glass, the leak forgotten, and the lamp in the upstairs window had swayed—swayed, as if struck, as if there had been a struggle up there—and then gone out. And perhaps a minute after that, no longer, the front door had opened again and the gentleman had descended the stairs unhurried, wiping his hands on a handkerchief in the lamplight spilling from the doorway, folding the handkerchief, putting it away, and stepping into the carriage, which rolled off toward the bridge without lamps and without sound but for hooves on wet stone.

And she had closed her curtain. That was the sum of it. That was what her silence weighed, measured out: she had seen a man wipe his hands after murder, fold the cloth, and leave, and she had closed her curtain and gone back to bed and told herself in the morning, when the shouting started and the constables came and the word *accident* went up like a flag over the quarter, that she might be wrong. That it might have been illness. That gentlemen sometimes called late and wiped rain from their hands. That her sons were young and fatherless and needed her careful.

That last part was true. It had always been true. It was true the morning she found Tamás's net mended and Jozsef humming at the vats and the world going on as if the curtain had never closed, and it was true now, with her eldest sitting in a grey room answering questions shaped like a noose.

They had taken him as leverage. She knew it the way she knew the weight of wet wool—by feel, by thirty years in the trade. Nobody arrested Tamás Yllir for questions about a night he hadn't been awake for unless they wanted something from someone who loved him. And there were only two people in the world who might know what the magistrate's men feared the workshop knew: the apprentice, Elodie—and herself.

The fire ticked. The rain, starting again, sang low in the gutters, and it sounded the way it had sounded that night, and Marta Yllir sat with her hands flat on the table among the permanent stains, and understood that the stone she had swallowed was going to have to come back up through her teeth.

If she spoke—if she went to the inquiry everyone whispered was coming, or to the magistrate's daughter, whom the quarter spoke of carefully, half fear and half hope—and described the gentleman, the limp, the handkerchief, the lamp that swayed and died, then Tamás walked free and Elodie walked free and every soul in the workshop quarter breathed easier.

And then her family was no longer invisible. Then the Yllir name was attached, publicly, irrevocably, to the murder of Aurel Nakamura and to whatever war was being fought in high rooms over maps and routes and gold. Gentlemen with carriages and clean boots did not forgive witnesses. They did not forget mothers. Jozsef was nineteen. Jozsef had soft hands and walked to the harbor alone every Thursday.

She pressed her palms against her eyes until sparks bloomed in the dark.

*Aurel repaired the astrolabe. He said it deserved better than a drawer.*

Toward dawn, hooves on wet stone stopped outside her door, and a knuckle rapped twice—not the constables' triple knock, but something lighter, familiar, urgent. Marta rose, lifted the latch, and found the Nakamura boy standing in the grey light with rain on his shoulders and terror held carefully behind his eyes, the way one carries a full cup across a moving floor.

"Marta-obasan," Elodie said, low. "They've taken Tamás. I know. I need to know what you saw that night—I think you saw something—and I need to know before the sun is up, because the man who killed my master knows exactly where we all live."

She stood in the doorway with the cold coming in around her hem, thirty years of silence pressing down on her tongue like a coin laid over a dead man's eye—and above the boy's head, in the east, the sky had begun, thin and merciless, to lighten.

"Come in out of the rain, child," she heard herself say. "I'll make tea. And then I am going to tell you something I should have told the whole city months ago."

She closed the door behind him, and the latch fell with a sound like a decision.
