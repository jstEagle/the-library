## 26. Ten Days

The knock came at dusk, three raps soft as knuckles on a coffin lid, and Elodie knew before he opened the door that it was not Marta, not a neighbor, not anyone whose business was honest.

He had been expecting it, in the way one expects rain after days of copper-colored sky. The limping man had come once before—photographing the workshop from across the lane, asking Marta questions in a voice like oiled wood—and Elodie had spent every night since listening to his own heartbeat against the pillow, waiting for the second visit. Waiting men aged faster. He opened the door with the lamp held high, so that the light would fall full on the stranger's face and the stranger would know he was seen.

There he stood, broad-shouldered under an oilcloth cape beaded with harbor mist, his left boot striking the doorstep with the deliberate heaviness of a man who had learned to make a prosthesis sound like confidence. Naval, Elodie thought, as he had thought before. A deck officer who had lost something below the knee to rope or shot or rot, and had spent every year since making the world pay for it.

"Master Nakamura." The man smiled without warmth, the way magistrates smile at confessions. "You keep late hours."

"You keep finding my door," Elodie said. "That's a longer habit than mine."

"May I?"

Elodie stepped aside. It was not courtesy; it was arithmetic. A refusal would only confirm what the man already suspected, and there were things to be learned by letting a wolf into the room and watching what it sniffed.

The limping man entered the workshop the way other men enter churches or banks—with an appraiser's eye. His gaze moved along the drying racks where maps hung like laundry, across the long cedar table scarred by thirty years of Aurel's compass points, up to the ceiling beams where rollers of finished work were stored in their canvas sleeves. Elodie watched him count the rollers. Watched him linger on the seventh, the one that weighed slightly wrong, and felt his own pulse slow itself deliberately, the way Master Aurel had taught him to steady his hand before a hairline coast: *fear is a tremor, boy, and tremors ruin lines*.

"Aurel Voss's bench," the man said, resting two fingers on the table as if greeting a dead colleague. "I sat at this table once, years ago. Before your time."

"He never mentioned you."

"He wouldn't have. We weren't introduced. I was sent to ask him for something he refused to draw." The man turned, and the lamp caught the flat gray of his eyes. "He was stubborn about what went into his atlas. You strike me as less sentimental."

"I'm more sentimental," Elodie said. "I just hide it better than he did."

Something flickered across the visitor's face—not amusement, exactly, but its distant cousin. He lowered himself onto the stool near the stove, favoring the good leg, and produced from inside his cape a packet wrapped in oilskin and tied with waxed cord. He set it on the table between them like a card played face down.

"Papers," he said. "Passage aboard the *Cormorant's Wake*, out of the north quay, ten days from tonight. Cabin passage. Under a name that will raise no eyebrows in any port from here to Nagasaki. And—" he tapped the packet "—a letter of introduction to a shipping house in Yokohama that owes me favors. A life, Master Nakamura. Assembled while you wait."

"And in exchange you get nothing."

"In exchange you leave the atlas here. Unfinished. Locked, sealed, surrendered to no one. When the seizure comes, the magistrate's men find what they find, and you are on open water learning to taste salt again instead of hanging from the gibbet they're building for your household."

"The gibbet isn't built yet."

"It's always built," the man said mildly. "They build it before the trial now. Efficiency."

Elodie did not touch the packet. He kept his hands folded on the table, apprentice hands, ink-stained at every nail bed, and made himself look at the oilskin wrapping the way he looked at a suspect chart—at its projections, its distortions, the places where the truth of the coastline had been bent to serve someone's purpose.

Ten days. The number settled into him like a stone into still water, and he watched the ripples spread outward through everything he knew.

* * *

After the man left—he rose, inclined his head, and let himself out with the same terrible courtesy he had entered by—Elodie stood at the window and watched the mist swallow him at the corner by Marta's dye-house. Then he took down the seventh roller, carried it to the table, and unrolled the true atlas under the lamp with hands that were almost steady.

Almost.

He needed to think the way the atlas thought. That was the trick of it, the thing Aurel had understood and died for: a map was not a picture of the world, it was an argument about the world, and every argument had an author, and every author had a purpose. So—who wanted this atlas unfinished? Not destroyed. The limping man had said *surrendered to no one*, and Elodie turned the phrase over until its seams showed. If the deputy merely wanted the evidence gone, burning the workshop would be simpler. Cheaper. Fires happened in port cities all the time; insurance men shrugged and magistrates wrote *cause unknown*. No—the atlas had to survive, intact, seized officially, entered into the record and then buried beneath procedure so deep it would never surface again. Seized paper could be misfiled. Misfiled paper could be forgotten. But paper in the hands of a fugitive, paper that might appear at an inquiry, paper that might fall into the possession of some inconvenient advocate—that paper had to be chased, feared, hunted.

Which meant the deputy did not want the atlas destroyed. He wanted it owned.

And which meant, Elodie realized with a coldness that started in his spine and spread outward to his fingertips, that the offer of passage was not mercy. It was retrieval. The forged papers were bait, the ship was the trap's jaw, and somewhere between the north quay and open water the *Cormorant's Wake* would meet a boat, and the atlas would change hands, and Elodie Nakamura would go into the sea wearing a name no one would bother to look for. A half-caste mapmaker with no family and no country, drowned off a foreign hull—no inquiry, no grave marker, not even a rumor. The cleanest kind of disappearance. The kind that didn't stain anyone's ledger.

He sat down on Aurel's stool and let the understanding finish itself, because understanding was like inking: once begun, a line had to be drawn to its end or spoiled entirely.

The deputy had killed Aurel—or ordered it done—to stop this atlas from being completed. Now, ten days from a seizure he himself had scheduled, he was offering to let it be completed, provided it completed itself into his custody. Why the change? Because the inquiry Sera had forced was coming. Because discrepancies had been reported publicly, in writing, by the magistrate's own daughter, and a public inquiry could not be misfiled. Because somewhere in the marginalia of these pages lay names and dates and payments that would hang him, and the only safe atlas was an atlas delivered quietly into the evidence room of a court whose chief deputy controlled what evidence was read aloud.

He wants it to bury, not to burn, Elodie thought. He wants my master's life's work to become his alibi.

And the threat—

There had been no threat yet. That was what troubled him most as he rolled the atlas closed and slid it back into its sleeve. The limping man was too skilled to waste a threat early. Threats were currency, and you spent them when purchase was required.

* * *

It rained that night, hard, the kind of autumn rain that came off the harbor sideways and made the copper roof of the workshop ring like a struck bowl. Elodie worked anyway, because ten days meant forty working hours at most and the final plate—the bridge plate, the gold-leaf plate—demanded a steadiness he did not currently possess and would have to manufacture from discipline alone. He laid the gilding size in hairline strokes along the span of the old iron bridge, and as he worked he found his mind refusing the work, dragging itself again and again to the same place:

Sera.

She had not come to the workshop since the storm night, since the almost-confessions passed between them over drying maps with the rain hammering the copper above them like applause from a vast indifferent audience. She had gone back to her father's office, back into the machinery of the law, carrying whatever she had decided to carry, and Elodie had told himself that distance was safety, that her absence was her armor and his too. But the limping man knew about the workshop's visitors. Marta had said men had been asking door to door. A girl from the magistrate's office drinking tea with the accused mapmaker was not a secret a whole street of frightened neighbors could keep.

At two in the morning he set down the gilding brush, wiped his hands on the rag that smelled of Aurel's tobacco and thirty years of linseed, and admitted the thing he had been circling all night:

He could protect the atlas with craft. He had decoys, hidden rollers, a neighborhood willing to pass satchels through cellars. Craft was his native language; in it he was fluent, maybe more fluent than anyone alive. But he could not craft-protect a person. People were not vellum. They tore along lines no artist chose.

And the deputy knew it. Of course the deputy knew it. You did not threaten the mapmaker, who had nothing to lose and therefore nothing to fear. You threatened what he loved, and you did it softly, obliquely, with a name spoken in passing, and the mapmaker handed over everything he had.

The knock, when it came again, came at four in the morning, and this time it was only the wind rattling the shutters. Elodie sat with his hand pressed flat over his own thundering heart and understood that he had crossed some line in the night. Fear had been living in him for weeks like a lodger. This was different. This was colder and heavier and it did not shake.

This was resolve.

* * *

Dawn came gray and thin over the harbor, and with it Marta, bringing bread and her usual talent for reading him like a tide table. She set the loaf on the table beside the oilskin packet, which Elodie had left in plain sight deliberately—if the workshop was searched, let them find the bait where bait ought to be.

"You didn't sleep," she said.

"I slept enough."

"Liar." She looked older in the morning light, her dyer's hands stained blue to the wrist, and he thought of what she carried unspoken—the thing she had witnessed the night Aurel died, the silence she kept to keep her sons whole. "The man came back."

"Not a man. An invoice." Elodie turned the packet over but did not untie it. "Ten days. Papers, passage, a new name—in exchange for the atlas, delivered unfinished."

"And if you refuse?"

"Then the offer expires." He met her eyes. "Marta. What do you know about Miss Voss's movements? Has anyone asked about her? Door to door, I mean—the way they asked about me."

Marta went very still, the stillness of a woman doing rapid and painful arithmetic. "Two nights ago. A clerk, polite as you please, asking whether the magistrate's daughter visited the workshop quarter regular. Tam said she'd seen her twice. I said I'd seen her never." Her jaw tightened. "I lied to a magistrate's man, Elodie. At my age. For you."

"For all of us," Elodie said quietly, and hated how much it sounded like a plea.

He explained it then, all of it, in the flat careful voice he used for reading coordinates: the deputy's true purpose, the trap dressed as mercy, and the probability—the certainty, he corrected himself, the mathematical certainty—that the next visit from the limping man would include not a threat but a demonstration, and that the demonstration would involve Sera Voss, because she was the loose thread the deputy could not misfile and the pressure point the mapmaker could not armor.

When he finished, Marta was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, in the voice of a woman who has buried one husband and outlived two sons' childhood fevers: "Then you know what you have to do."

"I know what I want to do. I want to put the atlas on a fishing boat tonight and be done with it. I want to take the papers and run and let the whole rotten city eat itself." He looked at his hands. "But if I run, she stands alone in front of her father's machine with nothing but her word against a deputy, a magistrate, and twenty years of filed paper. And if I surrender the atlas, Aurel dies a second time—a worse death, the kind where even the truth of how he died gets buried with the proof." He drew a breath. "So there's a third thing. There always is. It's just harder to draw."

"And that is?"

"I finish it," Elodie said. "All of it. Ten days. And I don't run, and I don't surrender, and when the seizure comes the atlas they take will testify better than any witness they've ever sworn in. Every route, every payment, every date—inked fair and legible for any judge in Vellamare to read. Let him seize it. Let him *want* it. Wanting it is his mistake. A man who needs a thing to bury it has already confessed he can't afford to have it found."

Marta studied him for a while, and then, unexpectedly, she smiled—a small, fierce, blue-handed smile. "Aurel used to talk like that. Right before he did something that scared me half to death." She picked up the oilskin packet of forged papers, weighed it once in her palm like a stone she was considering throwing, and dropped it into the stove.

The waxed cord caught, flared green, and was gone.

"You'll want breakfast in you, if you're going to frighten people," she said. "And Elodie—" she paused at the door, mist and morning light behind her, "—if the girl comes again, you tell her the truth. All of it. Women like her decide faster than men like you, and better."

* * *

He worked until the light failed, and then past it, and the workshop filled with the small sounds of industry that had been Aurel's religion: the sigh of vellum stretched on its frame, the whisper of the pen, the tick of the brass scale against paper edges. By lamplight he inked the last approaches to the harbor mouth, and in the margin, in the cipher only he and a dead man had shared, he added a final notation—not a route, not a payment, but a sentence, plain once decoded, that he had been composing in his head all day:

*The bearer of this atlas did not flee. Remember that when you weigh his testimony.*

At midnight he heard footsteps on the lane outside—two sets, unhurried, passing—and he blew out the lamp and stood in the dark with the finished plates against his chest and counted the seconds until the footsteps faded toward the bridge.

Ten days. Nine, now, and shrinking like a tide going out.

Tomorrow he would send word to Sera—not a note, notes could be intercepted, but a message in the language of maps, a chalk mark on the third piling of the bridge that meant *meet me where we planned, bring nothing you cannot deny*. Tomorrow he would begin teaching the atlas to defend itself.

Tonight he stood at the window and watched the dark shape of the old iron bridge against the harbor lights, the thin spine connecting his world to hers, and made it a promise: *Hold*, he told it silently. *Nine more days. Hold.*

Far off across the water, at the far span, a lantern flared once—someone walking the bridge at midnight, pausing at the midpoint, looking back toward the workshop quarter.

Then the lantern went out, and the figure was gone, and Elodie's resolve, hardened though it was, cracked down its center like vellum bent too fast.
