## 29. Seven Days

Seven days. The number had stopped being an abstraction sometime in the night and become a weight Elodie could feel in his wrists, the way a mason feels the mass of a stone before he lifts it. He had written it on a scrap of paper and pinned it above the workbench—*VII* in Master Aurel's old shorthand, the numeral the master used for deadlines that mattered—and now it watched him from the wall while he prepared the last plate.

The copper roof ticked overhead as the morning sun warmed it. Below, the workshop smelled of size and rabbit-skin glue and the particular sharpness of gold leaf, which Elodie had come to believe had a smell at all, though Marta insisted metal could not smell and he lacked the words to argue otherwise except by holding the thin booklet of leaf under her nose and watching her eyebrows climb. That had been weeks ago. It felt like another life, one in which the atlas was still only an atlas.

He cleared the great table of everything but what the final plate required: the burnisher, the agate stone, the gilder's cushion with its faded suede surface and its little wall of parchment to keep the leaf from sliding off, the knife with the blade worn to a crescent, a dish of garlic water for the size—for gold clings where breath has been, an old gilder's trick Aurel had taught him with the gravity of scripture—and the plate itself, vellum, stretched and scraped until it held light like the skin of a drum.

This was the plate the atlas had been building toward, though Elodie had not known it when he began. Forty-one plates of coastline and channel, cove and customs house; forty-one plates of routes that smugglers walked at night and magistrates pretended not to see; and now the forty-second, the last, which would hold them all together the way a keel holds a hull. Not a coast this time. A bridge.

*Draw me the bridge,* Aurel had told him, eleven months ago, in the last conversation they ever had. *Not the way it looks. The way it means. Every bridge is a promise, boy—that two shores belong to each other. Draw me the promise.*

Elodie had thought then that it was sentiment, the old man's habit of turning geography into philosophy. Now he understood it was instruction. The bridge was the atlas's true subject after all—not the routes men took to escape the law, but the connections the law could never quite sever. Cut the bridge, and you did not cut the quarter loose; you only admitted, in iron and dynamite, that the quarter had never truly been governed at all.

He breathed on the vellum where the first pier would rise. The garlic water caught. He lifted a square of gold from the cushion with the flat of the knife—a motion so practiced now that his hand did it without consulting him—and laid it down, and the leaf spread across the breath-moistened line like sunrise spreading across water.

Gold behaved like nothing else. Ink forgave; you could lift it, correct it, work back into it. Graphite was patient. But gold leaf was thinner than a lie and just as fragile, and once it touched the prepared ground it belonged there forever or nowhere. You could not hesitate over gold. You could only know.

Seven days, and he had never felt less certain of anything, and so he did not permit himself to feel anything at all. He worked the way the tide works, without opinion.

* * *

By midday the first pier stood complete, a pillar of beaten light rising from the blank vellum, and Elodie allowed himself the luxury of sitting back and looking at what the morning had made. The workshop door opened behind him—he knew the sound of Marta's step, the slight drag of her left heel, the way she always pushed the door with her shoulder because her hands were rarely free—and she came in carrying bread wrapped in cloth and an expression she had not managed to disguise since her eldest son's arrest.

"Tomas's hearing is set," she said, without preamble, setting the bread down beside the gold dish and then moving it carefully out of range of any stray breath. "Day after the inquiry convenes. Convenient, isn't it. They've learned arithmetic."

"Marta." He did not know how to finish the sentence. *I'm sorry* was too small. *I'll fix it* was a lie, or worse than a lie, a thing he had no right to promise and every intention of dying before breaking. He said instead: "Sit. Look at this."

She came around the table and stood over the plate, flour on her knuckles, and for a long moment she said nothing. The gold pier threw no shadow—it was too shallow for shadow—but it seemed to throw something anyway, some quality of attention, and Marta bent closer as though drawn by it.

"That's the north pier," she said. "You've got the rust streaks under the waterline. How do you do rust in gold?"

"You don't do rust. You leave the gold unpolished there, and burnish the rest, and the eye invents the difference." He picked up the agate burnisher, rubbed it warm in his palm out of habit. "My master taught me that. He said gold is honest only if you tell it where to be humble."

Marta made a sound that was almost a laugh and mostly wasn't. Then her eyes moved along the span of the unfinished bridge, the long empty stretch between the first pier and the second, and her voice changed entirely.

"What are you going to put there? In the middle. Along the rail."

"Names," Elodie said.

Her head came up sharply.

"The whole quarter," he went on, quietly, because saying it aloud in daylight made it real in a way that planning it at night never had. "Every household. Dyer, chandler, ropewalk, fishwife, the widow Osei, your boys. Master Aurel. Inscribed along the span, small enough that a stranger would take it for texture, close enough that anyone who lives there will find themselves." He looked down at the pier he had gilded, at the honest humble gold. "An atlas records what exists. This plate is going to record who. If they seize everything else—if they burn the workshop, if they take the decoy and declare the case closed—there will still exist one piece of paper on which this quarter was not a problem to be demolished but a place full of people with names."

Marta sat down slowly on the stool Aurel used to occupy, and pressed the heel of her hand against her mouth, and stayed like that for a while. When she lowered it her eyes were wet and furious, which was, Elodie had learned, how Marta wept.

"My mother's name was Sefa," she said. "Nobody's called her that in thirty years. She came here from the coast road with nothing and dyed wool until her fingers were blue to the wrist." She pointed at the empty middle span. "Put her down. Sefa Yilmaz, dyer. Put her where the sun would fall if bridges got sun."

"I will," Elodie said. "In gold."

"She'd have hated that. She'd have said gold shows every fingerprint." Marta stood, wiped her hands on her apron out of pure habit, and at the door she paused with her back to him. "Elodie. If it comes to it—if Thursday goes wrong—you carry those birth records I gave you like they're your own blood, you hear me? Paper outlives us all. Make sure it outlives us *well*."

Then she was gone, and the bread sat on the bench smelling of caraway, and the numeral *VII* hung on the wall above it all, and Elodie picked up the gilder's knife and went back to work.

* * *

The afternoon went to the second pier, and the evening to the water beneath both—the harbor rendered in a wash so deep a green it read almost black, pricked here and there with minute points of gold where the tide carried lamplight. He ate standing up. He had learned, in these months, to eat standing up and sleep sitting and measure time not in hours but in drying stages: the size tacky at half past three, the mordant ready by five, the wash dry enough to lay leaf over by eight. His whole life had become the tempo of materials. There was a strange mercy in it. Materials did not count days. Materials only asked whether you were paying attention.

He was laying a line of leaf along the underside of the second pier when the light failed past usefulness, and he lit the candles, and kept working by their small stubborn flames. This was the hour when the workshop spoke to him most clearly—the hour when Aurel had always worked, the hour the master had called *the ink's own time*, and Elodie had inherited the habit along with the tools along with the debt. The shadows of the map rollers climbed the walls like organ pipes. Somewhere below, the neighborhood settled: a dog, a shutter, Marta's boys' footsteps on the stair before he remembered that only one set of footsteps would come tonight, and his chest tightened around the fact like a fist around wet paper.

Tomas. Arrested as leverage, held as leverage, and the inquiry still four days out. Everything the deputy did now was aimed at Thursday: frighten witnesses, scatter names, make the price of truth higher than the price of silence. And it was working on everyone, Elode thought, except perhaps on Marta, whose silence had already broken once and who knew now the taste of speaking. Fear worked best on people who still had something left to lose quietly.

He blew a stray filament of gold off the plate—too hard; three squares lifted at the edge and he swore under his breath and repaired them, breath by careful breath. *Slow. Aurel's voice, in memory: Slow is fast, boy. Fast is slow and ruined.*

At ten o'clock there came a knock on the door in the rhythm he knew—two, pause, three—and his heart did what it always did now, a single hard strike against the ribs like a hammer against a chisel. But it was the right rhythm. Only three people alive knew it.

Sera slipped in with the fog, hood up, and she was carrying something wrapped in oilcloth under one arm, and her face in the candlelight had the particular stillness she wore when the news was bad and she had spent the walk over deciding how much of it to say.

* * *

"They moved the demolition notice," she said, before she had even taken off her coat. "It's posted tonight. New date."

Elodie set down the burnisher very carefully, the way one sets down anything when one's hands want to shake. "When?"

"Dawn. Day after tomorrow." She unwound her scarf, and her hair came loose, and she pushed it back from her face with a gesture he had seen a hundred times and that still undid him a little each time, and he hated that it undid him, because tenderness was a leak in a hull and he could not afford leaks. "They're calling it a structural emergency. A cable snapped on the south tower—they've even cut one, I saw the frayed end myself. It's theater. My father—" she stopped. Started again, flatter. "The deputy's signature is on it. Mine should have been. I'm meant to countersign the seizure inventory Thursday morning, which means I'm meant to stand in this workshop with a warrant and a clerk and take an accounting of everything you own while men with crowbars go through the cellar."

"And you came to warn me about the bridge."

"I came to warn you about everything." She pulled the oilcloth bundle onto the table, next to the gold dish, an act of such casual violation of the sanctity of the workbench that he almost laughed. Inside were papers: the demolition order, a copy of the seizure warrant, and something else—a survey drawing of the bridge with the charge positions marked in red, each little red cross sitting exactly where a rivet-line ran, exactly where the structure lived. "Dawn, day after tomorrow, and then the span is gone, and Thursday morning this side of the harbor is an island with one rowboat landing, and whatever happens at the inquiry happens to people who can't easily be reached by it." She looked up at him. "He's cutting the quarter adrift before the truth can walk across. It's elegant. I hate that it's elegant."

Elodie looked at the red crosses for a long time. Then he looked at his plate—at the two piers of finished gold, the empty middle span waiting for names—and he found, somewhat to his own surprise, that the fear had organized itself into something colder and more useful.

"It doesn't change the plan," he said. "It moves it. If the bridge falls Wednesday dawn, the atlas crosses Tuesday night. I carry it over the night before, and Thursday I walk into the inquiry from the city side like any other citizen, and the deputy can burn this workshop to the waterline and it buys him nothing."

"Tuesday night. With charges already wired into the ironwork." Sera's voice was level but her hands, he noticed, were folded very tightly. "Elodie. If a charge misfires early. If a sentry gets nervous. You'd be carrying forty pounds of paper across a bridge rigged to kill."

"I'd be carrying forty pounds of *testimony* across a bridge rigged to kill," he said, "which is the same weight but pulls differently." And then, because her face did not soften at all: "Sera. Seven days ago this was abstract. Now it has a dawn attached to it. I need to work. Help me or watch me, but either way, hand me the garlic water."

Something in her broke then—not the bad kind of breaking; the kind ice does in spring, when the river underneath insists on itself. She laughed, one short exhale, and took off her coat entirely, and rolled her sleeves, and said, "Where do you want the names written? I have the steadiest hand in the magistrate's office and you know it."

* * *

They worked until the candles were pools and the window showed grey. Sera ruled hairline guides down the middle span in silverpoint—her lines were indeed better than his, and he told her so, and she accepted it with the grace of someone who had never once been permitted to be praised honestly in her father's offices. Then Elodie began the names.

It could not be done carelessly. Gold leaf does not take a pen; the letters had to be built the reverse of writing—laid in tiny beads of mordant with a fine brush, one letter at a time, leaf pressed over, excess swept away—so that each name emerged from the span the way a face emerges from fog. It was slow as prayer and demanded the same total attention. Sera read the list aloud, softly, name by name, household by household, and Elodie laid each one into the bridge: *the widow Osei and her daughters; Pell the chandler; Anselm of the ropewalk; Sefa Yilmaz, dyer; Marta's sons, Tomas and Ivo,* and here his brush hesitated one heartbeat over Ivo, thirteen years old, who had run messages for him all winter without ever asking what was in the envelopes, and then he laid the name in gold because that was the whole point of gold—it did not distinguish between the brave and the frightened, it made everyone equally permanent—

*Aurel Vasseur, cartographer, master of this house.*

And then, because Sera was watching and because the plate deserved it and because seven days was enough time to be brave at least once, he added a final line beneath the span, small, in ink rather than gold, in a hand disguised as annotation:

*and E. Nakamura, apprentice, who carried it across.*

Sera read it upside down from her side of the table. She said nothing. But she reached across the gold dish and the knives and the candle stubs and put her hand flat over his wrist for a moment, a pressure, a seal, and then withdrew it and dipped her own brush and asked, "What's next?" as though nothing had happened, which was, between the two of them, its own kind of vow.

* * *

Near dawn she slept in Aurel's chair, wrapped in her scarf, and Elodie worked alone in the grey light with the last names, and this was when the flaw showed itself.

He saw it the way one sees a wrong note in music heard often: not by looking for it but by feeling it. The middle span. The names were right, the ruling was right, the gold was behaving—but the *span* was wrong. He had drawn the bridge from memory and from the survey Sera brought, and the survey was accurate, and his memory was accurate, and yet the curve of the main span sat a fraction too proud, too triumphant, the arc of a monument rather than a working bridge. He measured it three times against the survey with dividers, and three times the dividers told him what his eye already knew: he had unconsciously raised the crown of the arch by the width of a hair, idealizing it, making the bridge more beautiful than true.

An atlas that lied by a hair was still a lie. Aurel had fired an apprentice in Marseille for less—had said, in the man's hearing, *A map is a promise made to strangers.*

Elodie sat back and looked at the plate for a long time. Two nights remained. The plate had nine days of work in it and two nights to hold them, and the correction would mean scraping the entire crown of the span—names included, gold and all—and beginning the middle third again.

He thought about leaving it. No one else would ever see the error. The inquiry would not bring dividers; they would bring warrants.

He thought about Aurel's right hand, and the ink on the wrong one, and the first crack in the official story, and how everything—all of it, the atlas, the deaths, the bridge, Sera asleep in the master's chair—had begun with someone refusing to overlook a detail the size of a hair.

He reached for the scraper.

* * *

By the time the sun cleared the copper roof opposite, the crown of the span was bare vellum again, smooth as the day the skin was made, and Elodie's arms ached from the burnisher, and the gold dish held its little book of leaf like a hymnal, and he had begun again—pier three, breath and leaf, sunrise spreading across water.

He was reaching for the mordant brush when he heard footsteps on the exterior stair. Not Marta's drag. Not Sera's quick tread. Not the coded knock.

Light steps. Light and unevenly eager, the steps of someone young trying to walk quietly and thinking about it too much. Elodie's body went still before his mind did, the craftsman's reflex, every sense pouring into his ears.

The knock came. One knock, then a shuffle, then nothing—the knock of a person who had not planned what to say after knocking.

He crossed the room and opened the door, and Ivo stood there on the landing, thirteen years old, grey-faced, hat in both hands, having clearly been awake all night.

"Master Elodie," the boy whispered, and his voice cracked on the second word. "You have to hide the maps. I did something. I did a terrible thing."
