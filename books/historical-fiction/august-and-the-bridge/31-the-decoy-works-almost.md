## 31. The Decoy Works, Almost

The seizure began, as all official things in Vellamare began, with the sound of boots on cobblestone.

Elodie heard them from the workshop loft, where he sat at the long table with his hands folded in front of him like a man at his own funeral. He had rehearsed this morning for eleven days—the posture of it, the expression of it, the particular quality of defeated stillness an apprentice ought to wear when the law came to take everything his master had built. His heart hammered against his ribs anyway, a traitor organ that had not read the script.

*Let them find the false atlas,* he told himself, for the hundredth time since dawn. *Let them carry it away in its brass-bound case like a captured flag. Let Deputy Corvel declare his victory to the harbor.*

Below, Marta's voice rose through the floorboards—sharp, indignant, pitched perfectly by a woman who had spent thirty years arguing with dyers and dockhands and knew exactly how loud grief was allowed to be. She was playing her part too. They had agreed on it over the cellar hatch three nights ago: she would protest, loudly, because a household that protested was a household with nothing worse to hide than its temper.

The door splintered anyway. It had not been locked.

Four men entered first, then Corvel himself, ducking beneath the lintel with the careful economy of a man who had learned to move around ships. His limp was almost invisible indoors—a small hitch in the left stride, a slight roll of the shoulder—and Elodie understood suddenly how easily he had missed it before, how deliberately it could be hidden or shown. Today it was hidden. Today Corvel wanted to look like justice itself: upright, unhurried, inevitable.

"By order of Magistrate Voss," Corvel said, and his voice filled the workshop the way water fills a hull, "all maps, charts, papers, instruments, and correspondence belonging to the estate of Aurel Marchetti are hereby seized as evidence in the matter of conspiracy to defraud the customs of the port."

*Conspiracy to defraud.* Not smuggling. Not murder. Something dry and administrative, chosen to bore the newspapers. Elodie filed that away even as his mouth went dry.

The men moved through the workshop with the rough tenderness of people who did not care what they broke. Drawers came out whole. The drying rack went over with a crash that made Marta cry out on the stairs. Someone's boot found the jar of cut quills and ground them to feather and black spit.

And there, exactly where Elodie had left it—visible, prominent, angled toward the door like a thing forgotten in haste—sat the false atlas.

It had cost him nine days of his life. Nine days stolen from the true atlas sleeping in its rollers above the ceiling plaster, nine days of candle stubs and aching wrist, and every hour of it spent on a masterpiece of lies. The decoy atlas had Aurel's routes drawn correctly for the first half of its length—correct enough to survive a casual comparison with anything Corvel's clerks might already possess—and then, page by page, so gradually that only someone who knew the coastline as intimately as a lover would ever notice, it went wrong. A channel widened where it should narrow. A cove shifted two miles south. A landing place that had seen forty smuggled cargoes appeared on paper as a stretch of unbroken cliff. And threaded through the margins, in ink mixed to match Aurel's hand but in letters Elodie had practiced until his own hand forgot itself, ran the names of men who had never touched the trade—or rather, the names of men who had touched something far worse and laundered it through the customs houses. Names Corvel would be very relieved to see. Names that would make him stop looking.

He watched the deputy cross the room to it. Watched him open the brass clasps with gloved fingers. Watched him turn the pages slowly at first, then faster, then hardly at all.

Elodie counted his own breaths. Four in. Six out. The way Aurel had taught him to steady his hand before gold leaf.

"Is this it?" Corvel asked, and the question was aimed not at Elodie but at the man beside him, a thin clerk with ink-stained cuffs who held a list of comparison charts borrowed from the harbormaster's office.

"That's the one they described, sir. The big one. The masterwork."

Corvel closed the atlas. For one terrible moment his eyes lifted and found Elodie across the ruined workshop, and Elodie saw in them something he had not prepared for: not suspicion, not triumph, but *satisfaction*—the deep, animal satisfaction of a man who has found under his boot the thing he has been treading carefully around for months. The deputy wanted this atlas buried, Sera had warned him. The deputy wanted it found and buried in the same motion, seized and shelved and forgotten in an evidence room where no clerk would ever be curious enough to measure its coastlines against the truth.

"This," Corvel said softly, lifting the brass-bound case as though it were a relic, "concludes the matter."

He turned to Elodie then, and his voice took on the warm, public register of a man performing magnanimity for an audience of soldiers.

"You're the apprentice. Nakamura." He pronounced it carefully wrong. "You'll face no charges. The magistrate is satisfied that a boy of your years was used by older and cleverer men. You may thank your fortune that Master Marchetti died before he could see what his obsession became."

Every word was a hook baited with mercy. Elodie bowed his head. "I only ever did what my master asked, sir."

"I know," said Corvel, and smiled. "That's precisely why you're safe."

They carried the atlas out into the grey morning light, and Marta stood in the doorway with her fists on her hips and called them thieves in three languages until the last soldier was gone, and then the workshop was very quiet, and Elodie sat down on the floor amid the broken quills and laughed once, a short ugly sound that frightened him more than the boots had.

It had worked.

God help him, it had worked.

* * *

Sera heard the news from her father's own study, delivered by her father's own voice, and the strangeness of that was the first thing wrong.

"The case is concluded," Magistrate Voss said. He stood at the window with his hands clasped behind him, watching the harbor, and he did not turn when he spoke. "Deputy Corvel recovered the Marchetti atlas this morning. The evidence of the smuggling network is complete. There will be no trial—the ringleaders are beyond our reach, the principals are dead or dispersed, and the magistrate's office considers the matter closed with honor." Now he turned, and his face was composed into the expression he wore for pronouncements, the one she had trusted all her life. "You may inform the registry."

"Inform the registry," Sera repeated. Her voice came out flat.

"That the file is sealed."

She stood very straight. She had been standing straight for eleven days, ever since she had copied the seizure date onto her palm and felt the ink burn there like a brand; the date had washed off days ago and she could still feel it. "Father. The atlas was recovered from a workshop staffed by one apprentice and a laundress-dyer. Who, precisely, is being charged?"

"No one of consequence. That is the point of a conclusion, Sera—it ends."

"And the murder?" The words were out before she could weigh them, and she watched her father's shoulders change, a tension no one but a daughter would read. "Aurel Marchetti fell from his own roof, we are told. Into a courtyard he had no reason to enter, at an hour he was never abroad, with ink stains—" *on the wrong hand,* she did not say, because she had learned that from Elodie and could not reveal that she had learned it from anyone— "with anomalies the coroner noted and dismissed in the same sentence. Does the conclusion cover that too?"

Her father looked at her for a long moment. In the window behind him the gulls wheeled over the masts, and the light made his face unreadable.

"You have been spending your diligence unwisely," he said at last. "I have let it pass, because you were your mother's daughter and grief makes strange industries. But it passes no longer. The file is sealed. Corvel's report is accepted. You will inform the registry, and then you will take the fortnight's leave you are owed, and you will rest."

It was dismissal dressed as care, and they both knew it, and neither of them said so.

"Yes, Father," Sera said.

She informed the registry. She signed the sealing order with a hand that did not shake, because her hands had learned their trade too well. And then she walked—not home, not to the leave her father had granted, but to the records annex off Customs Square, where she had spent enough months auditing ledgers to know which clerks were honest, which were lazy, and which could be moved by a well-placed question about pension arrears.

For four hours she compared. It was the work she was made for, and she gave herself to it with the cold joy of a woman finally permitted to use the whole of her mind: Corvel's inventory of the seized atlas against the harbormaster's pilot charts; the pilot charts against the tide tables; the tide tables against the customs seizure logs of the past six years. The decoy was beautiful. That was the trouble. It was beautiful the way a forged signature is beautiful—the line too confident, the flourishes in love with themselves. The false channels in the Marchetti atlas showed depths that no vessel drawing less than nine feet could have used, yet the customs logs recorded seizures at those exact coordinates, year after year, of vessels drawing twelve. Either the smugglers had invented ships that sailed on gravel, or the map lied.

And there was more. There was the matter she had been circling for weeks, the payments in her father's second ledger, the ones that marched along the same dates as certain landings, the ones she had hoped and hoped against hope were something innocent—rent for a warehouse, bribes to no one important, the ordinary grease of port administration. She laid them beside the atlas's doctored margins now and found they matched the *false* routes. Matched them perfectly. Whoever had paid those sums had been paying against a map that did not describe the real coast—which meant whoever drew the false map had known, or guessed, or been told, exactly which figures needed covering.

Someone had built the lie to fit the ledger. Or the ledger to fit the lie.

Her father's name was not on any of it. Not directly. It never would be; he was too careful, and she loved him, and both of these things were now wounds.

At dusk she wrote her report. She wrote it in the plain, ruthless prose her office prized, without adjectives, without accusation, stating only discrepancies of measurement, only contradictions between seized evidence and independent record, only questions a competent inquiry could not refuse to ask. She cited the coroner's anomalies in the Marchetti death. She requested, in the final line, in words so mild they were almost apologetic, that the matter be reopened for public examination before the demolition of the bridge rendered the workshop quarter inaccessible to investigators.

Then she signed it, sealed it, walked it to the night box of the Chief Assessor—who owed her father nothing and hated Corvel for reasons dating back a decade—and stood a moment on the steps of the annex with the harbor wind pulling at her hair, feeling simultaneously like the most loyal daughter in Vellamare and the most treacherous.

There was no way to be both. She had known that for weeks. What she had not known until this moment, reading the ghost of her own signature through the seal, was that she had stopped trying to be.

* * *

Marta brought the news up the cellar ladder two days later, and she brought it the way she brought everything, at full volume, half-drowned in dye.

"They've reopened it!" She filled the loft doorway, dripping rain, her gray hair escaping its scarf in triumph. "Public inquiry! Posted on the customs house door this noon where every fishwife in Vellamare could read it—the Marchetti matter, reopened, public sessions, beginning Thursday next in the assize hall. Reopened, boy! Your deputy's little victory lasted three days!"

Elodie set down his pen. On the table before him the true atlas lay open to its penultimate plate—the estuary, silvered with mica dust, waiting for gold—and he had been staring at it for an hour without seeing it, because since the seizure he had been unable to shake the sensation of a trap that had closed on empty air.

"Who forced it?" he asked. "Inquiries don't reopen themselves. Corvel owns half the bench."

"A petition for review." Marta grinned like a woman watching her sons' team win at bowls. "Filed by a clerk of the magistrate's own office. No name given in the posting, but Greta's cousin works the registry, and she says—" Marta dropped her voice with theatrical reverence, which for Marta meant merely conversational volume— "she says the handwriting on the petition belongs to the magistrate's daughter."

The rain filled the pause, drumming on the copper roof.

"She reported discrepancies," Marta went on, softer now. "Measurements. Tide tables against the seized map. She made them put it in writing, Elodie, in language so dry old Assessor Brenner choked on his lunch reading it. That girl burned her own house's timber to make a fire hot enough to cook the truth."

Elodie looked down at the atlas, at the estuary glittering under the lamplight, and felt hope rise in his chest the way the tide rose under the bridge—vast, patient, and utterly indifferent to what it might drown.

Three days. The deputy's victory had lasted three days, which meant Corvel knew by now, or would know within hours, that the map he had paraded through the streets was flawed—that the apprentice he had patted on the head and pronounced *safe* had handed him a forgery with a smile. Elodie tried to imagine the moment of Corvel's understanding: the clerk's report, the tide tables spread on his desk, the slow closing of the limping man's fist. A man who had killed once to bury a secret does not respond to exposure with resignation.

"Thursday," Elodie said. "How many days is that?"

"Four. And the bridge—" Marta's grin faded; the other news was already in the quarter, passed door to door like a sickness. "Demolition's set for Friday morning. Day after the first session. They mean to have the whole business concluded before the quarter is an island."

Four days of inquiry. One bridge. A deputy who now knew he had been played, and a magistrate's daughter who had just made herself the most dangerous witness in Vellamare by putting her suspicions into an official document with her own hand.

Elodie thought of Sera—of her steady voice over tea, of the storm-lit almost-confessions on the copper roof, of her walking alone to the annex with the wind pulling her hair—and fear arrived in him, cold and specific, fear not for himself or the atlas or even the promise rotting in the marginalia, but for her. She had struck Corvel in the only place he could not afford a wound, publicly, procedurally, irrevocably. Men like Corvel did not answer such blows with lawyers.

"Thursday," he repeated quietly, and reached for the gold leaf. "Then we'd best make sure that whatever they examine on Thursday is the truth, the whole of it, down to the last rivet."

Above the loft, the rain kept time on the copper roof like a countdown, and somewhere across the dark harbor, in a house with a lamp still burning in its study, the deputy was reading the petition for the third time and learning, line by measured line, exactly whose handwriting it was.
