## 32. Three Days

The notice went up at low tide, nailed to the customs house door while the fog was still peeling off the harbor.

Elodie heard of it the way he heard of most things now—through Marta's voice, pitched high and wrong, calling up the stairwell of the workshop before his second candle had burned through. He came down with ink still on his fingers, a smear of Prussian blue along his knuckle that he would remember afterward, absurdly, because it was the last ordinary thing.

"They've moved it," she said. She was holding a copy of the notice someone had torn from the door and carried through the quarter like a live coal. Her hands shook, and the paper rattled. "The bridge. They're not waiting for the seizure anymore. It's dawn, Elodie. Dawn tomorrow."

He took the paper from her because she could not seem to hold it still enough to read. The magistrate's seal sat fat and black at the bottom, and above it, in the flat bureaucratic hand he had come to hate, the sentence was written out as plainly as a tide table:

*By order of the Office of the Magistrate of Vellamare, the iron crossing known as the Long Span is condemned and shall be demolished by explosive charge at first light on the morning of the ninth day of August. All persons are advised to clear the structure and its approaches after nightfall.*

First light. Not the day after the seizure, as the original order had said—not even the pretense of sequence remaining. The inquiry convened at midday tomorrow. Sera had secured it only days ago, forcing the discrepancies in the seized decoy atlas into the open where the deputy could not quietly bury them, and Elodie had allowed himself three nights of something dangerously close to hope.

Now the arithmetic rearranged itself with terrible clarity. The deputy did not need to wait for the law to finish. He needed only the water. Demolish the bridge before dawn, before the inquiry could convene, before any witness could be brought across from the workshop quarter—and the quarter would wake as an island. No testimony could walk to the courthouse. No atlas could cross. And when the seizure parties finally came by boat, they would come to a quarter already sealed, already condemned, its people scattered or drowned in paperwork, and whatever the deputy chose to find there would be found.

*The trap snapping shut.* That was the phrase that arrived in Elodie's mind, uninvited, in Aurel's voice, in the dry tone the old man reserved for bad weather and worse clients. He had spent weeks thinking of the demolition as a wall closing slowly, a door swinging shut over days. He had not understood until this moment that it was a snare—spring-loaded, waiting only for the weight to be right.

"Three days," Marta whispered. "They said three days. It was posted for three days past the seizure. That's how these things are done. There's always—" She stopped. There wasn't always. There had never been a rule except the ones powerful men wrote for themselves.

Elodie read the notice twice more, then folded it into quarters, then eighths, until it was a small hard square he could have fit beneath a fingernail. His hands were steady. He noticed that too, with a kind of distant curiosity—the steadiness of hands that had spent years doing fine work under poor light, that had learned to be calm precisely when the work mattered most.

"I have to see the bridge," he said.

* * *

He walked the length of the quarter to the head of the span, and the crowd thinned around him the way water parts around a piling. The Long Span stretched out over the harbor in the gray afternoon light, its iron ribs dripping, gulls standing sentry along the rails. He had counted its rivets once, in another life it seemed, back when the bridge was merely the fragile thread between two worlds. Now he counted what remained: one evening, one night, one dawn.

Workmen were already at the far abutment. Even from this distance he could read them the way he read coastlines—the furtive angle of their shoulders, the crates stacked and covered with tarpaulin, the way none of them looked at the water. Those were not engineers' crates. Engineers worked in daylight and talked loudly about loads and stresses. These men moved like smugglers, which meant the charges were already being placed, tonight perhaps, in the dark, in the pilings below the tideline where no curious eye would wander.

And the tide. He made himself think about the tide, because thinking about the tide was better than thinking about the men with the crates. Spring tides this week; he had charted them himself for the atlas's harbor plate. Low water near four in the morning, turning just before dawn. Which meant that at first light, when the charges fired, the current would be slack—no debris swept out to sea, no confusion, everything dropping neat and tidy into the harbor mouth. A clean demolition. A demonstration. *Look what happens to quarters that shelter criminals.*

Except he was not going to be here to watch it.

He stood at the rail midway across, where the planks hummed faintly underfoot, and he let himself feel the whole weight of the thing at once, because there was no time left to feel it in pieces. Three days of margin had become one night. The true atlas lay hidden in map rollers in the workshop—thirty plates of vellum and gold leaf, every smugglers' route, every name, every date, the murder of Master Aurel encoded in marginalia that Sera had read by candlelight and understood. The inquiry would convene at midday. The atlas had to be there. The atlas *was* the testimony; without it, Sera stood alone before her father's colleagues with nothing but discrepancies and conviction.

And between him and the courthouse lay four hundred feet of condemned iron and a man with a naval limp who would be standing at the far end.

He had known it would come to this since the night on this same bridge when he and Sera had drafted the plan on a sheet of vellum spread against the rail—her hand steadying the corner, his pencil moving fast, the two of them building a strategy the way he built a coastline, line by patient line. But plans drawn at dusk are drawn by optimists. The plan had assumed days. The plan had assumed the deputy would play by the calendar because the calendar was how respectable men hid. Elodie gripped the cold rail and admitted what he should have admitted sooner: the deputy had stopped hiding. A cornered man does not keep appointments. He burns the appointment book.

He turned back toward the quarter. Behind him, the workmen loaded their crates onto a barge and pushed off from the abutment, and the sound of the small engine carried flat across the water like a cough in church.

* * *

Marta's kitchen smelled of lamp oil and boiled coffee, and it held everyone the quarter had left: Marta herself, hands wrapped around a cup she never drank from; her younger son Tomas, sixteen and unable to meet anyone's eyes since the night they had all learned what he had traded for his brother's freedom; old Piet the rigger, who had helped raise half the roofs in the district; the widow Anselm and her two girls. Word had traveled faster than the notice had. The quarter knew.

"The boats," Piet was saying as Elodie came in. "There are fishing boats. My cousin keeps two at the north slip—"

"And your cousin will be arrested for harboring before his lines are wet," Marta said. "They'll watch every slip tonight. You know they will."

"They can't watch everything."

"They don't have to watch everything." Elodie said it quietly, and the room quieted to hear him, which still astonished him some days—the invisible apprentice, the Japanese boy, finding his voice in other people's kitchens. "They only have to watch the bridge and the slips. One man with a lantern at each. We'd be carrying the one thing in Vellamare they cannot afford to let cross."

Tomas looked up then, stricken. "Because of me. If I hadn't told them where—"

"Stop." Elodie crossed the room and put a hand on the boy's shoulder, and felt him trembling. "You were frightened for your brother. Any man in this room would have done the same. I would have." He said it firmly enough that he almost believed it, which he had learned was how such things worked. "What matters now is not what was taken. The deputy thinks he has the atlas, and what he has is paper I fed him. He thinks the case is closed, and tomorrow noon it opens in front of the whole city. He is frightened, Tomas. Frightened men move early and make mistakes. This notice is his mistake."

It was true, and it was also not the whole truth, and Elodie let the difference sit in his chest where he kept such things. The mistake was real—but so was the trap. The deputy had not merely moved the demolition up. He had moved it to *dawn*, hours before the inquiry, and Elodie did not believe for a moment the timing was only administrative. A bridge that falls at first light makes witnesses of no one on the far side. And if a certain apprentice happened to be caught upon the span when the charges fired—if the atlas and the boy who carried it dropped together into the harbor, unfindable, unprovable, a tragedy blamed on the condemned structure itself—then the inquiry would convene at noon to discuss discrepancies, and there would be nothing at its heart but absence.

"He knows," Elodie said, before he decided to say it. Every face turned to him. "About the inquiry, about Sera's report, about what the true plates show. And he knows the atlas has to cross the bridge to reach the courthouse. He isn't demolishing the bridge despite the inquiry. He's demolishing it *for* it. The bridge falls, the evidence drowns, and the quarter wakes up cut off, guilty by geography." He looked around the kitchen, at the faces he had lived beside for years without truly seeing until the raid scare, the cellar night, the long chain of small kindnesses that had turned neighbors into accomplices. "So we do not give him a drowning. We give him a courier he cannot catch."

Piet squinted at him. "And how does a boy outrun an explosion?"

"He doesn't outrun it," Elodie said. "He starts early."

* * *

Sera came at moonrise, hood up, entering through the dyer's yard the way she had learned to, and found him in the workshop with everything he owned spread across the floor in a pool of candlelight.

She stood in the doorway a moment, and he saw her take inventory the way he had taught her to read a map—satchel, oilcloth, the rollers with their false ends, the bundle of Aurel's letters tied in sailcloth, the small tin of tools, the gold leaf in its tissue paper. Everything measured against what could be carried.

"You heard," he said.

"I signed the acknowledgment of the amended order myself." Her voice was flat, which he knew was how she sounded when the feeling underneath was largest. "My father's office processed it. The deputy brought the amendment at nine o'clock this morning with the ink still wet and told the clerks it was a routine correction of scheduling. Routine." She laughed once, without anything in it. "I stood there and initialed my own ambush."

"Your father?"

"Gone to the country estate until the inquiry. Convenient, isn't it, how often he's away when the worst things happen." She came into the room and knelt beside the spread of gear, and her hand found the satchel strap and rested there, as if testing the weight of the next several years. "Elodie. Tell me you're not crossing at dawn."

"Tell me why I shouldn't."

"Because he wants you on that bridge. Because there will be men watching both approaches, and the charges might be fired early—fired while you're on it, do you understand? Not at first light. Whenever a figure is seen halfway across with a heavy bag. That's what I'd order, if I were him, and I am sickened to know how easily I can think like him now."

"I know." He said it gently, and kept sorting. "I've thought of it. It's why I'm not crossing at dawn. I'm crossing at three o'clock, at dead low tide, four hours before the charges, in the dark, when the watchers are cold and stupid and expecting nothing until the sky goes gray. The atlas goes to Piet's cousin's boathouse on the city side. Then at noon it walks into the inquiry in Sera Voss's hands, entered openly, witnessed, so that no one can disappear it. That was always the plan. The deputy has only changed our schedule, not our route."

She was silent for a moment. In the candlelight her face was the map he knew best in the world, and he watched duty and love fight across it the way weather fronts fight over the harbor.

"If you're taken," she said, "they won't just take the atlas. They'll take you, and no one will ever hear the word *apprentice* again. You'll be a smuggler's boy who died running, and the story will be written before your body cools."

"Then I won't be taken."

"That is not a plan."

"It's the only kind of plan there's time for." He set down the gold leaf and took her hands, ink-stained and clerk-inked, his blue against her black. "Sera. Listen to me. Three years I have been invisible—a half-blood apprentice, a shadow behind a dead man's door. I have been passed on the street by people who have bought maps from me. I built my whole life on not being seen. And now one frightened, vicious man has arranged an entire demolition, an entire frame, an entire war, around the possibility that I might cross a bridge with a bag." He smiled, and felt it crooked and strange on his face. "Do you know what that is? That is the finest compliment anyone has ever paid my work."

"It is not funny."

"No," he agreed. "But it is true, and truth is the only cargo I'm carrying tomorrow."

She bowed her head until her forehead nearly touched his hands, and for a long moment neither of them spoke, and the candle flame leaned and righted itself in the draft from the harbor. When she lifted her face she was again the magistrate's daughter, spine straight, jaw set—the woman who had chosen, three nights ago on the bridge, that truth would define her rather than duty, and who had been paying for the choice daily since.

"Then we finish it," she said. "The inquiry convenes at noon. I will hold the floor until you arrive or until the hour turns—I will read your master's marginalia aloud myself if I must, decoding it in front of the whole bench, and let them arrest me for impertinence if they can stomach it. But Elodie—" her grip tightened, "—you give yourself four hours. If you are not across by seven, you turn back. Promise me. A drowned atlas proves nothing and a living witness with the routes in his memory proves nearly as much. If the bridge refuses you, you let it."

He promised. He was not sure, later, whether either of them believed the promise, but she made him say the words, and saying them put a shape on the fear that let him breathe around it.

Before she left she stood a moment at the door with the hood half-raised, silhouetted against the dyer's dark yard, and said, without turning, "My father signed the amendment, Elodie. Whatever he is, whatever he's been—he signed the paper that kills your bridge. Remember that when it falls. Remember it when you decide what to forgive."

Then she was gone, and the workshop was very quiet, and Elodie sat down among his belongings and began, methodically, to pack.

* * *

He slept two hours, badly, and woke at one to work.

The final preparations were simple, and simplicity was its own terror. Oilcloth folded around the atlas, corners double-turned and waxed. The rollers with their hidden pages left behind—they were too risky now, too clever by half; the atlas would travel as one solid block, wrapped like a bale, indistinguishable in the dark from bolt goods. Aurel's letters went inside his shirt, against his chest. Marta's sons' birth records, folded soft as cloth, went in the satchel's inner pocket beside the folded copy of the condemnation notice—the irony of carrying the deputy's own order across the bridge he was destroying did not escape him, and he held onto it like a coin for luck.

At two, Marta came to the workshop door without knocking. She wore her shawl and her oldest boots, and behind her in the yard stood Piet, Tomas, the widow Anselm's grown son, and six others he had known all his life—faces he had mapped a hundred times without knowing he was mapping them, drawn now in the lamplight with the particular steadiness of people who have decided something together.

"We're walking you to the bridge," Marta said. It was not an offer. "All the way to the head of the span. And then we're standing there, all of us, in plain sight, until you're across. Let the deputy's watchers count *that*. Let them write it in their report—that the whole quarter came out of its houses at three in the morning to watch one boy cross, and dared them to stop him."

"Marta—" Elodie began, and found his throat had closed.

"Hush." She pressed something into his hands: a small square of dyed cloth, madder-red, the color she had been boiling into wool since before he came to the quarter. "For wrapping the atlas if the oilcloth fails. Red shows in water. If it goes in the harbor, we'll know where to look, and we'll look, do you hear me? Nothing of ours sinks unwatched."

He looked at them a long moment—at the dyer and the rigger, the boys he had taught to draw letters, the women who had fed him for years without once asking what he was—and understood, with a fullness that hurt, that he had finished the atlas at last. Every name inscribed along the golden span of the final plate, and here they all were, alive, standing in a dyer's yard at two in the morning to carry a stranger's promise to the water's edge.

"To the bridge, then," he said.

They went down through the sleeping lanes in silence, twenty shadows moving between the houses, and ahead of them the Long Span waited in the starlight with its charges packed in its pilings like a heart gone wrong, and beyond it, across the black water, the city lamps burned over the courthouse where, in nine hours, the truth would be summoned to appear.

Elodie shifted the satchel strap on his shoulder and stepped out of the shadow of the last house onto the open approach, and somewhere on the far shore, in the darkness where the lamplight did not reach, he heard the unmistakable sound of a man shifting his weight from an unhealed wound—the limp, the waiting, the trap already sprung around the empty middle of the bridge.

Four hours until low tide. Four hours until the inquiry. One crossing left in the history of the Long Span.

Elodie Nakamura set his foot on the first plank and began to count.
