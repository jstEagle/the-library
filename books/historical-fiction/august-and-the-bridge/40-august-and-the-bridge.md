## 40. August and the Bridge

The new workshop smelled of cedar shavings and salt, and Elodie Nakamura stood in the middle of it with ink on his fingers and nowhere in particular to be, which was, he had learned over the past year, a kind of wealth.

It was August again. The same month that had once meant only heat and the stink of the harbor at low tide now meant something else entirely—meant open shutters, meant Sera's laughter drifting up from the street below, meant the quarter of Saltrow waking from its long year of grief like a sleeper who could not quite remember what had frightened her.

He ran his hand along the new drafting table. He had built it himself in June, from plans Master Aurel had left folded in a drawer—plans for a table with a shallow drawer beneath the surface, deep enough for rollers, shallow enough that no one searching would think to look. The old man had designed half his furniture as hiding places. It occurred to Elodie, not for the first time, that he had apprenticed to a smuggler who only ever smuggled truth.

Outside, the sound of the city came through the window in layers: gulls first, then the fishwives crying the morning catch, then—beneath it all, steady as a heartbeat—the hammers and cranes of the new crossing going up across the harbor. They were calling it the Voss Bridge already, though Sera protested every time she heard it, and though the magistrate himself, her father, had refused to attend the groundbreaking and sat instead in his study with the ledgers he was still, month by month, setting right.

Elodie had watched the first pylon go into the water in April. He had stood on the Saltrow shore with Marta and her sons and half the neighborhood behind him, and when the stone sank into the green harbor and sent up its single ringed wave, old Joren the cooper had wept openly and blamed the wind, and nobody had contradicted him.

A bridge, Elodie had thought then. You do not understand until you lose one how much a bridge is. It is not iron or stone. It is the fact of your neighbor being reachable. It is the difference between a quarter and an island.

* * *

Sera arrived at nine, as she did every morning now, carrying two paper cones of roasted chestnuts even though it was far too warm for chestnuts and they both knew she bought them purely because the vendor on the corner had been the first shopkeeper in the rebuilt row to greet her by name.

"You have ink on your face," she said, by way of good morning.

"You have chestnut grease on yours."

"One of us has an excuse." She set the cones on the windowsill, where the gulls would begin their negotiations within the hour, and crossed to the great flat cabinet against the north wall. "Is it done? The printer's boy came by twice yesterday."

"It's done." Elodie said it quietly, the way one says such things. "The last proof went back to Halloran & Sons on Tuesday. Four hundred copies, first printing. Aurel Nakamura and Elodie Nakamura, cartographers." Hearing the names together still did something strange to his chest—not pain, exactly. More like a joint being flexed after a long splint.

"Two Nakamuras," Sera said, "and neither of them related to the other."

"The master chose me the name. It seemed poor thanks to let it die with him."

She turned from the cabinet and looked at him—a long look, unhurried, the kind of look the old Sera Voss would never have permitted herself, trained as she had been to audit everything and dwell on nothing. A year ago she had walked into this quarter with a clipboard and her father's seal and the belief that precision was the same as justice. Now she wore plain linen instead of the magistrate's office gray, and her hands, which had held only ledgers and her father's disappointment, were calloused at the palms from learning the graver.

"And the marginalia?" she asked. "All of it? The routes, the names, the dates?"

"Everything. Halloran printed the plates exactly as drawn. The deputy's payments are in the margins of plate nineteen in gold leaf you can read across a room. The clerk's name is coded in plate seven, and the key is plate twenty-nine." He smiled without much humor. "We put the bridge on plate twenty-nine. Every name of the quarter inscribed along the span. Anyone looking for the key will find four hundred people who watched everything happen."

"They'll try to buy the plates," Sera said. "Someone always tries to buy the evidence."

"Let them try. Halloran keeps the originals under lock, and I keep—" he touched his breast pocket, where a single folded vellum sheet lived now, the master's final coastline, carried so long it had worn soft as cloth, "—I keep what can be carried."

* * *

They opened the doors at ten.

There was no sign, not yet—Sera had painted one, a compass rose on weathered blue, but it was drying in the back room and Elodie secretly hoped she would hang it crooked, the way Aurel had hung everything, the way a man hangs things when he trusts his neighbors to forgive him his angles.

But they came anyway. Of course they came. Marta arrived first, because Marta had been first at everything this year—first to testify, first to stand in the inquiry hall with her sons' birth records held up like a torch, first to forgive Elodie for the weeks her younger boy had spent unable to meet his eyes after the betrayal that no one, in the end, had punished, because the inquiry had understood what fear does to a fourteen-year-old with a brother in a cell. Marta brought bread and a jar of dye-blue so vivid it looked like a piece of sky somebody had bottled, and she hung it from the rafters "for luck," and if her voice broke when she said the workshop looked like Aurel's, only Sera noticed, and Sera said nothing, which was its own kind of gift.

Then Joren, with a barrel of his best for the shelves. Then the Widow Kessler, who had avoided Elodie for three years of watching and now would not leave, apparently, until she had inspected every drawer. Then children, dozens of them, Saltrow children who had never been inside a mapmaker's shop, pressing their noses to the display copy of the atlas that lay open on the front table to plate twenty-nine, the golden bridge, the names marching along its span in a cartographer's hand so fine the schoolmaster had already declared he would use it to teach penmanship.

Tomas Marta's-eldest—that was how people said it now, Tomas-of-Marta's, the way islanders name things—stood at the back in his dockworker's clothes and did not come all the way in. Elodie caught his eye across the room. The boy—he was not really a boy anymore; the year had put a man's shoulders on him—lifted his chin, and Elodie lifted his, and that was the whole of the apology and the whole of its acceptance, and both of them found it sufficient.

By noon the little shop was too full for commerce, so there was no commerce, only talk and bread and the blue jar of luck swaying gently overhead, and Sera standing beside Elodie at the counter with her shoulder just touching his, and the afternoon sun coming through the west window and lying across the atlas like a hand laid flat in blessing.

* * *

In the quiet after closing—they had to push the last of the Widow Kessler out with the promise of tea next week—Sera found the letter.

It had come with the morning post and Elodie had forgotten it among the congratulations, a plain envelope with the Halloran & Sons seal. He slit it expecting a shipping date.

*Dear Mr. Nakamura,* it began. *We write regarding the second printing, and a matter of some delicacy.*

"What matter?" Sera asked, reading over his shoulder, her chin nearly resting on it, an intimacy that still startled him daily.

"*Requests have reached us from certain parties in the capital for the original plates. We are, of course, contractually bound to refuse. But we are a small firm, Mr. Nakamura, and refusal is easier when the plates are not ours to refuse. We propose returning them to your custody at your earliest convenience, and would note only that the gentleman making inquiries was described to us as having served in the navy.*"

Elodie read it twice.

The limping man. In the capital, making inquiries of printers. The deputy had gone to prison in March; the inquiry had taken him and two clerks and a customs officer, and the trial was set for autumn, and men like the limping man were the reason trials needed witnesses alive.

"He knows the plates exist," Sera said. Her voice had changed—the auditor's voice, flat and quick. "If he can't stop the book, he'll want the originals destroyed. Evidence burned is evidence denied."

"Halloran won't give them up."

"Halloran is a small firm," Sera repeated, "and a firm with daughters, probably, and debts, certainly." She took the letter from his hand and read it a third time, and Elodie watched her do what she did—the thing he had loved first and feared most, the precise mind moving along a problem like light along a coastline, finding every inlet. "They're asking you to take custody. Which means whoever comes for the plates next will come to you."

"To the workshop," Elodie said. "To the bridge quarter. Where there is currently no bridge."

Below the window, out across the bright evening water, the pylons of the new crossing stood in their half-finished ranks, and the cranes swung against the amber sky, and the workmen's lamps were beginning to bloom one by one along the unfinished deck like a string of lights being strung between two shores that had spent a year learning the distance between them.

"We should tell someone," Sera said.

"Yes," Elodie agreed. And then, because he was his master's apprentice, and his master had taught him that a map is a plan and a plan is a promise: "After we decide where to hide them."

* * *

That night they carried the plates home from Halloran & Sons themselves, in the false bottom of a fish cart Marta's boys provided without asking questions, and stored them in the shallow drawer beneath the new drafting table, in a piece of furniture built from a dead man's blueprints expressly for the keeping of dangerous truths.

Elodie locked the drawer and sat a while in the dark workshop, listening to the harbor. The night tide came in soft against the pilings. Somewhere out on the water the workmen on the new bridge were singing—one of the long haul-songs, the kind with a hundred verses—and their voices carried across the black water the way voices only carry when there is finally something close enough to carry to.

He thought about the limping man, and about the trial in autumn, and about the plain arithmetic of the thing: a book of four hundred copies could be burned in an hour, but a plate engraved in gold could outlive everyone who feared it, and the man with the naval limp knew it too.

He thought, also, about the letter still in his pocket—because he had read it a fourth time in the lamplight and noticed what Sera, reading for threats, had read past. The last line. Small courtesy, printers' politeness:

*We remain, sir, your obedient servants, and would add only that the gentleman inquired not after the routes but after the names.*

Not the routes. Not the smuggling, not the payments, not the murder.

The names.

Every name of the quarter, inscribed along the span of the bridge in gold.

Elodie rose and lit a candle and unlocked the shallow drawer, and drew out plate twenty-nine, and laid it on the table under the flame's small circle of light. The golden bridge gleamed up at him out of the dark—Marta and her sons, Joren, the Widow Kessler, Tomas-of-Marta's, Sera herself hidden in the lettering where only he knew to look—four hundred witnesses engraved forever in a metal that does not burn.

And he understood, with the cold clarity of a man reading a coastline by lightning, that the limping man was not coming to destroy the evidence.

He was coming for the list.
