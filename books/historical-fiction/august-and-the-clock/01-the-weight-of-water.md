## 1. The Weight of Water

The ledgers of the Halloway Trading Company were kept in a room with no windows, and Silas Ravensworth had long ago decided this was deliberate. Numbers, like divers, could survive in the dark. Men could not.

He arrived at half past six, an hour before anyone whose opinion mattered, and unlocked the counting room with the key that hung on a nail behind his own ribs — not literally, though some mornings it felt that way. The key was brass, worn smooth, and it opened the archive where eleven years of his life sat shelved in calfskin spines. He hung up his coat, lit the lamp, and began.

The morning's work was pearl. Three lots from the northern beds, shipped down on the *Corvane*, each crate supposed to hold forty sacks and each sack supposed to hold what its manifest swore it held. Silas weighed, counted, sampled, and recorded. His pen moved in small, economical strokes. He did not daydream. Daydreaming was how clerks made errors, and errors were how clerks became noticeable, and being noticed at Halloway's was a thing you survived rather than enjoyed.

By eight he had found two discrepancies. The first was trivial — a transposed digit in the gross weight of lot seventeen, the kind of mistake any tired man might make, and he corrected it in the margin with the initials-and-date ritual the company demanded. The second was not trivial. Lot twenty-three came up four ounces light against the manifest, and four ounces of pearls was more money than Silas would earn in four years.

He sat with the figure for a while, listening to the building wake up around him. Boots on the stairs. The kitchen boy arguing with the coal scuttle. Somewhere above, Director Halloway's window being cranked open to let in the smell of the harbor.

Then he did what he always did with discrepancies: he wrote them down exactly, in his own hand, in his own private book, and then entered the official figure into the official ledger with a note flagging the variance for review. Whether anyone reviewed it was not his affair. That was the arrangement he had made with the company in his own head, a treaty never spoken aloud: *I will see everything and say nothing, and in exchange you will forget I am here.*

It had held for eleven years. He was thirty-one years old and he trusted it the way a diver trusts old rope.

* * *

Mr. Quill — overseer of the counting floor, a man built like a hat rack and about as warm — arrived at nine and made his circuit. He paused at Silas's desk the way he always did, not because there was anything to find but because stopping was a way of reminding Silas that he could stop.

"Ravensworth." Quill pronounced it as if the name had personally inconvenienced him. "The Corvane lots. Done?"

"Two variances flagged. Lot twenty-three is short."

"Short." Quill picked up the ledger page and held it at arm's length, as though distance improved his reading. "Short is a word with consequences, Ravensworth. You understand that. A variance goes upstairs, upstairs sends someone down, someone down asks questions, questions cost hours, hours cost money. All over four ounces."

"Four ounces of pearl," Silas said mildly.

"I know what an ounce of pearl is. I also know what an afternoon of Director Halloway's time is worth, and it's more." Quill set the page down and tapped it once with a forefinger, a little punctuation of contempt. "Sometimes a sack settles in transit. Sometimes a wet weight was taken dry or a dry weight wet. The sea is imprecise, Ravensworth. Your figures needn't make a parade of it."

"The ledger requires—"

"The ledger requires what the company requires. Which this month is a quiet shipment and a clean report." Quill's eyes were pale and patient, the eyes of a man who had won every argument of his life by outlasting people. "Amend the entry. Note the difference as transit loss. Standard practice."

Standard practice. Silas had heard the phrase applied to so many things — missing cargo, missing wages, missing men — that it had stopped meaning anything and become instead a kind of weather, something that simply happened over Vellamare and could not be argued with.

He amended the entry. He noted the difference as transit loss. And in his private book, in his own hand, he recorded both versions, because that was the one habit he permitted himself that was not invisible: he kept the truth somewhere, even if only in a locked drawer, even if only for himself. A man who saw everything and wrote none of it down eventually stopped believing what he'd seen.

Quill moved on. Silas flexed his writing hand and thought, not for the first time, that his father had died holding his breath for the company and that Silas had spent his whole adult life doing a slower version of the same thing.

* * *

His father had been a diver. That sentence contained most of what mattered about Silas Ravensworth, though no one at Halloway's knew it, because he had put the name Ravensworth on his indenture papers without explanation and let the clerks assume what clerks assumed — that it was a dock name, a nothing name, a name attached to no one who counted. His father had dived for eleven years for the company before the sea took him, and the company's ledger recorded the death in a single line, entered in another clerk's neat hand: *J. Ravensworth, diver, lost at sea, April.* No depth given. No bed named. No question asked. Silas had been nine. The company had offered his mother a condolence payment of one month's wages and, when she died two winters later of the fever, had offered him an indenture in place of a workhouse, which everyone agreed was generous.

Generous. Eleven years of auditing pearls in a windowless room for the company that had sent his father down too deep, on a day the water was wrong, because a director wanted a season's numbers to look a certain way — Silas didn't know all of that for certain. He knew pieces. Dock boys knew pieces; that was the tax on growing up at the docks. You assembled the shape of a thing from fragments the way you assembled a net, and what you got held water well enough.

He took his lunch at noon and walked down to the harbor front, past the fish stalls and the rope walks and the chandlery with its barrels of tar, down to the stretch of sagging warehouses and lean-to houses where he had been raised by nobody in particular, which is to say by everybody.

Maren's house was third from the waterline, a low stone thing with a roof patched in three colors of tin. Maren Okoye — no, that was another family; Maren Ede, retired diver, sixty-some years, hands like driftwood — was outside mending a pot with a hammer and the total absence of hurry that only very old people and very old cats possess.

"There he is," she said, without looking up. "The company's ghost."

"Maren."

"They feeding you up there, or do you still eat like a heron?"

"I eat fine."

"You eat like a man who checks the weight of his bread." She hammered the pot flat, inspected it, and set it aside. "Sit. Kettle's on. It's the cheap stuff, so don't make that face."

He sat on her step. The harbor spread below them, brown-green and glittering, dotted with the masts of the pearling fleet. Out past the point, barely visible, the diving boats rode at anchor over the northern beds. Silas looked at them the way you look at a scar.

"My father dove off that point," he said. He hadn't meant to say it. It came out the way water comes through a wall — find a crack, take the crack.

"Off that point, and off the shoal, and off the deep beds past the kelp line when they told him it was shallow there." Maren poured the tea without ceremony. "I dove with him nine years. I was in the boat the day they lost him." She said it plainly, the way old divers said everything, having used up their capacity for drama somewhere around the fifth funeral. "You don't remember that."

"I remember my mother getting the paper."

"You remember the important things, then." She handed him the cup. "You come down here every week, sit on my step, drink my bad tea, and talk about weights and measures. One of these days you're going to tell me why."

Silas watched the fleet. A launch was putting out from the company wharf, and even at this distance he recognized the silhouette of the man at the tiller — Beddoe, the beds master, the one who certified waters safe. Certificates, certificates. The whole coast was papered with them.

"I audit pearls," he said. "I'm good at it. It doesn't sound like much, but being good at something the company needs is a kind of shelter. You'd be surprised how long a man can live under a number."

"I wouldn't be surprised by anything this company does under a number." Maren drank her tea and squinted at him sideways. "Your father was the same. Quiet man. Kept everything down where the light don't reach. It's a way to survive." She paused. "It is not a way to live."

"It's worked so far."

"So has the seawall. Until the storm." She took his empty cup and stood, joints cracking like rigging. "Tomas Okonkwo's wife is asking after you, by the way. She says the tall clerk with the sad face hasn't been by in three weeks. Go see her before she takes it personal. And take her some of the dried fish from Kessler's — she's got the little ones and no man's wage coming in now."

"Tomas is gone three months," Silas said quietly. "And Riva Okonkwo still—"

"Still sets his bowl out. Yes." Maren's face did something complicated and settled back into granite. "The sea keeps what it takes. We keep the rest. Go on, now, I've got pots to ruin."

* * *

Riva Okonkwo lived two streets back from the water in a house that leaned companionably against its neighbor. There were children everywhere, as always — hers and borrowed ones — and the smallest came shrieking to wrap itself around Silas's leg the moment he appeared, which was the custom, and which he endured with the stiff patience of a man being embraced by furniture.

"Uncle Silas! Uncle Silas, Nnamdi says a whale ate the pier—"

"A whale did not eat the pier."

"How do you know? Were you there?"

This was, he reflected, the entire problem with children: they asked the correct questions. "I read the pier reports," he said gravely. "No whales logged."

She considered this, found it acceptable, and released him. Riva was in the doorway with flour on her hands and an expression that softened when she saw the parcel of dried fish under his arm, then sharpened, because Riva missed nothing and had never once pretended otherwise.

"Three weeks," she said.

"Work."

"There's always work. There's always work the way there's always water." She ushered him in, put him in the good chair, which was an honor and a trap, because the good chair meant you stayed. The little ones orbited. The kettle went on. And then Riva sat across from him, folded her floury hands, and looked at him with those diver's-family eyes that saw straight down through the surface.

"Tomas dove the new bed in March," she said, without preamble. "They certified the water safe. Beddoe signed it himself, everyone says. Forty fathoms, maybe more, and the safety line says thirty-five, and my husband went down because the company told him the numbers were wrong and the water was fine." Her voice stayed level. It was the levelness that frightened him, the terrible practiced flatness of a woman who had done her screaming in private. "Three boats. Six divers. Two came up. My Tomas did not."

"I know," Silas said. "I'm sorry."

"Sorry." She tested the word and set it aside. "The company paid me the widow's portion. Half a year's wage. They had me sign a paper saying the death was weather. Weather." For the first time something moved under the flatness. "There wasn't weather, Silas. There was blue calm water and a company certificate. But I signed, because there are five children in this house and signing was the only door out of that room." She leaned forward. "Now. Why does a clerk who audits pearls come to my street, drink my tea, bring my fish, and look at me like a man doing sums he can't finish?"

The kettle screamed. One of the children fetched it down. The room waited.

Silas looked at his hands. He thought about his private book in its locked drawer, both versions of every figure, eleven years of discrepancies in his own careful hand. He thought about his father's single ledger line. *Lost at sea, April.* No depth. No bed. No question.

He thought about the treaty he had made with the company in his own head, and how treaties only held until one side stopped needing them.

"I don't know yet," he said honestly. "But when I do, you'll be the second person I tell."

Riva studied him a long moment. Then she nodded slowly, the nod of a woman banking a fire rather than putting it out. "Who's the first?"

"A woman I haven't met yet," Silas said, "or nobody."

He left before the good chair could claim another hour. The sun was lowering over the harbor, turning the water to beaten copper, and the diving boats were coming home across it, low in the water, heavy with the season's take. On the company wharf, lamps were being lit one by one, and clerks were streaming out of the counting house like ants from a disturbed nest — all except one desk, which stayed lit late every night, and would stay lit tonight.

Because it was closing time, and the last thing on his list was locking the archive, and Silas Ravensworth was always the last to lock the archive.

He came up the company steps at dusk with his collar turned against the sea wind, keys in hand — and stopped.

On the broad stone step of the Halloway Trading Company, in the shadow of the brass-lettered door, something small was crying.
