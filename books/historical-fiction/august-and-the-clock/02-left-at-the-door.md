## 2. Left at the Door

The Halloway Trading Company closed its books at six o'clock, and Silas Ravensworth closed himself with them.

He had learned long ago that the last hour of the day was the most dangerous. Morning belonged to procedure — ledgers opened, seals broken, clerks arriving in their proper order like teeth in a comb. But closing time was when men lingered. When Overseer Brant liked to make his rounds and find something wrong, because a man who found nothing wrong was a man who could be spared. Silas had watched clerks lose their positions over a misplaced decimal at ten minutes to six. He had watched a warehouse boy dismissed for locking the door eleven seconds late. So he worked the last hour slower than any other, checking everything twice, making himself so unremarkable that even his absence would be noted only as routine.

That Tuesday in early spring, he balanced the final column of the pearl manifest at seventeen minutes past five, checked it again at twenty past, and was blotting the page when the light through the tall front windows began to fail.

Vellamare's spring came in sideways off the water — a gray wind that carried salt and rope-fiber and the low constant groan of ships at anchor. The counting house windows rattled in their frames. Somewhere above, the great clock in the company tower struck the quarter hour, its tone flattened by damp air, and Silas felt, as he always did, the small mechanical satisfaction of a day ending exactly as intended.

He squared the manifest, tied it with the green company string, and set it in the out-tray. He washed his nib. He capped his ink. Around him the other clerks were already gone — released in order of seniority, lowest first, so that Silas was always nearly the last, a fact he had never decided whether to resent or appreciate. The lamps were turned down to embers. The vast room smelled of paper dust and cold brass and, beneath it all, faintly, of the sea, which got into everything in Vellamare the way grief gets into a family: unnoticed until you moved away, impossible to remove once noticed.

Silas took his coat from the peg. He wound the regulator clock on the wall — one of his small duties, unwritten but expected; the clock must never drift, because the company's entire existence ran on the difference between tides measured to the minute — and he let himself out through the side door into the receiving yard, then around to the front steps, where he would lock the main doors and drop the key through the watchman's slot.

The front of the building faced the harbor road. In daylight it presented a facade of fluted columns and dark green doors, meant to tell visiting merchants that Halloway money was older and quieter than theirs. At dusk it told a different story: the columns went the color of old bone, and the wide stone steps descended into shadow, and the whole building seemed less like a place of business than like something beached, waiting for the tide to take it back.

Silas came down the steps with his keys in hand.

He stopped on the third step.

At first he thought it was a bundle of rags — the harbor collected them, along with crates, rope-ends, and once, memorably, a sea chest containing a dead man's letters. His eyes had been trained by seven years of ledgers to sort things instantly into categories, and *debris* was the correct category for anything left on company property after hours. Debris could be reported to the yard master in the morning. Debris was not his problem.

But debris did not breathe.

He stood very still. The wind moved his coat-hem against his legs. Down the road a cart rattled past, lamplight swinging, and in that brief wash of yellow he saw what the bundle actually was: an infant, wrapped tight in cloth, laid in the lee of the door where the wind couldn't reach it. Small. Terribly small. Its face was slack with the deep sleep of exhaustion or cold, lips slightly blue at their edges, lashes wet.

Silas looked up the street. Empty. Looked back toward the yard. The gates were chained; he'd heard the yardmen leave at half past four. The night watchman, Pell, would not come on duty until the bell — and Pell, in Silas's experience, saw nothing ever, on principle, because seeing things led to being asked questions about them.

There was a knot of something at the base of his throat that he recognized, distantly, as panic. He made himself breathe the way he did before opening a ledger that might contain bad news: slow, through the nose, eyes steady.

Then he crouched and picked the child up.

The weight of her shocked him. She weighed almost nothing and also far too much — the way a full ledger weighs, the way a decision weighs. Her warmth came through the wrappings faintly, a small failing fire. She did not wake. One tiny fist had worked free of the cloth and lay open against his forearm, fingers curled halfway shut, as if she'd been holding something and let go.

There was a note pinned at the swaddling's edge.

He almost didn't read it there — every instinct said take it inside, get out of the street — but his hands were already moving, tilting the paper toward the last gray light.

Five words, in a fine clerk's hand, the ink still sharp:

**Forget this face. That is all.**

Six words. He counted them twice, the way he counted everything. No signature. No name for the child. No date. Just the instruction, written in a hand that belonged in a counting house — educated, unhurried, the *t*'s crossed with the same economical stroke Silas used himself.

And below the note, where his thumb held the fold, he saw the mark woven into the cloth itself.

Later he would not be able to explain what happened in his chest at that moment. It was not recognition, exactly. It was older than recognition — the body knowing before the mind, the way a diver's body knows depth before the gauge confirms it. A pattern in indigo thread on undyed linen: three interlocking crescents, horns outward, around a central bar.

He knew it. He had seen it on a boat once, painted on a gunwale, when he was a boy on the docks. He had seen it since, too — in the company's own records, in the column headed *next of kin*, in the little system of clan-marks the diving families used instead of signatures because half of them wrote no letters. The clerks called them "native marks" and filed them under initials they invented themselves.

Silas stood on the steps of the Halloway Trading Company holding a child whose family mark he knew, under a note commanding him to forget her face, and understood with perfect clarity that he was standing at a fork in his life, and that one path was paved and lit and led nowhere good for anyone but him.

* * *

Pell arrived at the bell, as predicted, carrying his supper wrapped in newspaper and seeing nothing, as promised by nature.

"Lamp's out on the north gate again," he said, unlocking the watch box without looking up. "Third time this month. You'd think the chandlers could spare a wick that actually—"

"Pell."

"Hm?"

Silas had rehearsed nothing. That was unlike him, and he noted the fact with detached alarm, the way he'd note an unbalanced column. "Quiet evening?"

"Dead quiet." Pell bit into his bread. "Cart came through at half five. Nothing else. Why?"

"No reason. Manifests are filed. Doors are locked." He heard his own voice come out flat and ordinary and blessed whatever instinct had built it. "Cold tonight."

"Colder tomorrow." Pell waved him off with the bread and settled into his chair, and within a minute the sound of his snoring joined the wind.

Silas walked home the long way, which was to say the way that passed no windows belonging to the company, with the child inside his coat, against his chest, wrapped in his scarf over her swaddling. She had woken briefly in the shelter of the yard — he had ducked between stacked crates, heart hammering, absurdly certain that someone would see him and know, though know what? — and she had looked at him with dark, unfocused eyes and made a single small sound, not quite a cry, more like a complaint filed with the wrong office. Then she had slept again, one hand finding the fabric of his shirt and gripping it with a strength that seemed impossible for something that size.

His rooms were two flights up over a sailmaker's shop on Cordage Lane, in the district where the dock families lived — the divers' families, the ropemakers, the coopers, everyone whose labor fed the company's ledgers without ever appearing in them as names. He climbed the stairs quietly, though there was no one to hear, and locked his door behind him, and then stood in the middle of his single narrow room with the lamp unlit, listening to the child breathe.

This was the moment, he knew, when a sensible man would go to the parish. Or the constable. Or, God help him, the company itself — there were procedures for foundlings, he'd transcribed the relevant deposition once; the company took charge of such cases, entered them in a register, and the register, in Silas's experience, was where things went to stop existing.

He thought about the register.

Seven years in the Halloway counting house had taught Silas Ravensworth many things, but the deepest lesson had come in his first year, when he was sixteen, sent to reorganize the dead-file in the cellar. The dead-file was where completed contracts went, and disputes, and complaints, and inquiries. He had spent three weeks among those boxes and had emerged understanding something that could not be unlearned: the company did not destroy inconvenient documents. It kept them. Perfectly. Forever. It simply arranged matters so that they could never be found by anyone who might want them. A complaint from a widow about a dive certified safe would be answered, filed, cross-referenced, and buried under nine feet of shipping manifests. The paper trail existed. It simply led nowhere.

The company treated people the same way.

He thought of his father, who had drowned when Silas was nine, in waters the company had certified safe, and whose death appeared in the ledgers as *equipment loss, incidental*. Not even a weather loss. His father had been reduced to a footnote in a column about rope.

He lit the lamp at last and set it low, and unwrapped the child enough to see her properly. She was perhaps two months old, a girl — he checked, clumsily, blushing alone in his own room like a fool — with a dusting of black hair and the particular brown skin common to the diving families of the eastern clans. The Okonkwo coast families. He was fairly sure of it now, looking at the mark again in the lamplight: three crescents around a bar. He'd have to ask someone who knew the marks better than he did, someone old, someone safe — Maren, maybe, if Maren was sober, and Maren was always sober now, that being the one cruelty of age, that it takes away your reasons for drinking before it takes away the habit.

She woke while he was rewrapping her and regarded him with that total, unblinking seriousness infants have, as if he were a problem she had assigned herself.

"I don't know anything about children," he told her. His voice sounded strange in his own ears — it had been weeks since he'd spoken aloud in his own rooms. Possibly months. "I don't know anything about you. I don't know why anyone left you on those steps, and I don't know who wrote that note, and I am, if we're being honest, probably the worst person in Vellamare to be holding you right now."

She blinked. A bubble of spit formed at her lip and popped.

"But," Silas said, and here his voice did something he hadn't authorized; it roughened, "I know what happens to things the company finds inconvenient. I've filed them."

The note lay on his table where he'd set it. **Forget this face. That is all.** Someone with a clerk's hand and access to the company's front step had wanted this child erased — not harmed, necessarily, not killed, just *erased*, which in Vellamare, in 1897, under the green doors of the Halloway Trading Company, amounted to nearly the same thing and cost considerably less trouble.

If he reported her, she would enter the register. If he left her anywhere findable, whoever left the note would learn of it — and someone who could reach the company's doorstep at closing time could reach a parish nursery. If he tried to forget her face, as instructed, he would spend the rest of his life as he had spent the last seven years: competent, invisible, and quietly complicit, balancing columns over graves.

Silas looked at the child. The child looked at Silas.

"All right," he said. It came out barely above a whisper. He cleared his throat and said it again, louder, the way he imagined a man ought to say a thing that mattered. "All right. Then we don't report you. We don't register you. As of tonight, you were never on those steps."

It occurred to him that he had just, in plain terms, committed theft of company process, obstruction of whatever design that note represented, and — if the child's family was whose he suspected — the beginning of an inquiry the company had spent a year making sure no one would conduct. He was an indentured man. His contract ran another six years. They owned his labor, his lodging allowance, and, per clause fourteen, his "good behavior." Men had been transported over clause fourteen.

He found, examining himself carefully the way he'd examine a suspect ledger, that he felt not afraid. Or rather — he was afraid, thoroughly, down to his hands, which were not entirely steady as he folded the note and hid it inside the false back of his writing case. But underneath the fear was something with structure. Something with columns and entries and a balance point. Fear sharpening itself into resolve, the way you sharpened a nib: pressure, angle, purpose.

Tomorrow he would take the swaddling cloth to Maren. Tomorrow he would begin learning who this child had been, before she became a bundle on a step. Tonight—

Tonight she began to cry, a thin furious sound that filled the whole room, and Silas Ravensworth, who had audited forty thousand pearls and never once dropped one, discovered that he had no idea whatsoever what to do next.

He held her anyway. Out the window, across the rooftops, the company clock struck ten — patient, indifferent, keeping the time that belonged to Halloway — and Silus rocked a stolen child in the lamplight and began, for the first time in seven years, keeping a different kind of count.
