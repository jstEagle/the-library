## 3. The Clan-Mark

The cloth would not let me sleep.

I had done everything right the night before. I had fed the child condensed milk thinned with water from Mrs. Okoye's recipe — she'd raised four sons on it between shrimp seasons. I had wrapped her in my own coat and put her in the laundry basket behind the boiler room, where the watchman's rounds never took him until dawn. I had told myself it was temporary. One night. Until I could think.

But it was the cloth I kept returning to, laid flat across my kitchen table under the single lamp, while the baby slept in a drawer I'd lined with sacking. A square of indigo cotton, soft as an old man's hands, dyed that deep blue they get from the vats down on Serpent Street. And stitched into one corner, in thread gone pale with salt and washing, a mark.

Three lines rising like waves. A circle beneath them, open at the bottom, like a hull seen from underneath.

I knew what it was. That was the problem. Any clerk in the Halloway Trading Company could look at a clan-mark and tell you which family it belonged to, because we kept records of them — not out of respect, but for debt. Divers' families signed with their marks when they couldn't write their names, and those marks lived in the ledgers beside every advance, every equipment loan, every funeral cost deducted from a dead man's final pay. I had transcribed hundreds of them. I had been trained to see them as ink, nothing more.

This one I had transcribed myself, eleven months ago, into the ledger marked *Divers — Casualties — Spring Season*.

I sat with the lamp turned low so the light wouldn't reach the window, and I looked at the mark until my eyes burned, and then I folded it into my shirt, against my chest, where no one could see it, and I went to find Maren.

* * *

Maren Osei lived at the far end of Coaling Row, in a house she had built herself from ship's timber and stubbornness, with a porch that leaned toward the water like a woman listening for a boat that isn't coming home. She was sixty-one years old and had been diving since she was nine, back when the company still pretended women couldn't hold their breath, and she had retired only when her ears gave out for good — twenty-two years of pressure packed into a head that now rang like a harbor bell in bad weather.

She was shelling peas on the porch when I came up the path, and she didn't look up.

"You walk like a man carrying stolen goods," she said.

"Morning to you too."

"It's nearly noon." She flicked a pod into the bowl. "And you've got that face."

"What face?"

"The one your father used to make when he'd found something on the bottom he wished he hadn't." She looked up then. Her eyes were the gray of old shell, and they missed nothing, because twenty years of surfacing into bad news will teach you to read a person before they read you. "Sit down. Say it plain."

I sat on the step below her, which was habit — you didn't stand over Maren unless you wanted a lesson in manners — and I took out the cloth.

"I need you to tell me whose this is," I said. "And I need you to tell me quietly."

She set down the peas. She took the cloth in both hands, and I watched her thumbs find the stitching the way a blind man reads, and I watched her face do something complicated: first recognition, then denial, then a grief that arrived slowly, like water filling a boot.

"Where did you get this?" she said.

"Maren—"

"Where."

"On the company step," I said. "Last night. With a baby inside it."

For a long moment she didn't say anything at all. Down on the water a gull screamed and another answered it, and somewhere along the row a hammer was knocking planks, ordinary sounds, the sounds of a morning that believed itself innocent.

Then Maren folded the cloth — carefully, the way you fold a flag — and handed it back.

"That's the Okonkwo mark," she said. "Three lines for the three currents off the Kessu shelf. Open circle for the boat that watches over the diver. Every Okonkwo for six generations has worn it on their diving cloth, stitched by the mothers, because—" her voice caught, just slightly, and she steadied it the way divers steady themselves on a line, "—because if the sea takes you, the mark tells it who to answer to."

"And the ledger says—"

"I know what the ledger says." She picked up a pea pod and crushed it in her fist without noticing she'd done it. "Weather losses. All three. Tomas and his brother Emeka and the boy, Daniel — nineteen years old, first season certified. Weather losses." She spat the words like fish bones. "There wasn't weather, Silas. There was a clear spring sky you could have drunk from, and there were waters nobody dives twice, and there was a company man on that boat telling them the bed below was rich enough to retire on."

My mouth had gone dry. "You were there?"

"I was on the dock when the boat came back," she said. "Short two men and short a story. They said the squall came up sudden. I stood on those planks and watched the company men say it, and the sky behind them was so blue it hurt, and every widow on that dock knew they were lying and none of us could prove a thing, because dead men don't testify and the survivors sign what they're told to sign." She brushed the pea shells from her lap with great care. "That was eleven months ago."

Eleven months. I did the arithmetic without wanting to. Eleven months, and the child in my drawer was perhaps five weeks old.

Which meant someone had carried her for half a year. Hidden her, fed her, kept her alive — a mother or a grandmother or a sister, some woman with the Okonkwo mark in her household, waiting through a pregnancy that must have begun before the dive, before the lies, holding a piece of a murdered family under her heart while the company wrote *weather losses* into the permanent record.

And then, last night, she had run out of ways to keep hiding. Or she had been found. Or she had decided the child's only chance was a door she couldn't knock on, only leave her at.

With a note that said *Forget this face.*

"Maren," I said. "Who else is left? Of the family."

"You know who's left. You keep the books better than anyone on that dock." Her voice softened on it, which somehow made it worse. "Adaeze, the wife. And she vanished off the Row three weeks ago. Company moved to collect the family debt after the funerals, and when the collectors came, the house was empty. People said she ran to her people upcoast." Maren looked at me, and her gray eyes were terrible with understanding. "People said a lot of things."

"She didn't run," I said. "She was running *from* something. And she brought the baby here. To the company door. To *my* door." I heard how it sounded even as I said it, and hated the logic of it, and couldn't deny it either. "She didn't leave her at a church or a charity house. She left her at Halloway Trading. Either she'd lost her mind, or—"

"Or she knew the company owed her a debt it would never admit in money," Maren said, "so she delivered the proof of it to the doorstep and dared them to look at it." She was quiet a moment. "Or she knew a Ravensworth worked the ledgers there. Your father dove with Tomas for two years, before the sea took him. Adaeze would remember that. She'd remember there was one name in that building with a reason to count bodies honestly."

The lamp of my whole careful life flickered. I had spent thirty-one years being nobody — the quiet auditor, the boy who kept his head down and his pen clean, invisible as good bookkeeping. And it had never once occurred to me that invisibility might be exactly what made me useful. That a woman with nowhere left might look at the Halloway Trading Company and see, in all its floors of clerks and overseers and directors, one person she could trust with the shape of her whole grief.

I had been chosen by being overlooked. It felt like being seen for the first time.

"There'll be an inquiry eventually," Maren said. "There always is, when the insurers push. Spring at the latest. And when it comes, Crane's people will already have the record sewn shut — the safety reports, the depositions, the histories. Paper drowns faster than divers, and it stays drowned." She reached over and closed my fingers around the folded cloth. "You're asking me whose mark this is. I'm telling you. But what you're really asking is whether you're going to do something about it, and that question I can't answer for you. Only this." She nodded toward my shirt, where the cloth lay against my chest. "Your father carried that same look. The day before his last dive, he came to me and said, *Maren, the water's wrong and the pay's too high, and I can't afford to be right.* Then he dove anyway, because the contract said he must." Her jaw tightened. "You're not under contract to be silent. Not yet. Remember that difference."

* * *

I walked back along the Row slower than I needed to, past the net-menders and the bait sheds, past the shrine where the dock families nailed small wooden plaques for their lost — names and dates and little carved waves, dozens of them, generations of the sea's arithmetic. My father's plaque was third from the top, sun-bleached almost blank. *Elias Ravensworth, lost 1874.* The company had listed him as a weather loss too. There had been no weather.

At the mouth of the Row, a company runner passed me going the other way — one of Director Halloway's personal boys, quick and clean in his gray uniform, carrying a sealed envelope. He didn't glance at me. Why would he? I was furniture.

But I watched him climb the steps of the company house and disappear inside, and I thought about envelopes, and about how fast paper traveled when the powerful wanted it to, and about a note that had traveled no farther than a doorstep before someone tried to make it vanish.

Back at the company lodging, I checked the boiler room first. The basket was undisturbed; the child slept with one fist curled against her cheek, utterly unaware that she was the most dangerous object in Vellamare. I lifted her and she made a small sound against my shoulder, and I stood there in the coal-smelling dark feeling something crack open in me that I had caulked shut for thirty-one years.

Because I had opened the casualty ledger in my head, there in Maren's porch chair, and read the entry I myself had copied last April, in my own neat hand, believing it the way I believed everything printed above my signature:

*Okonkwo, T.; Okonkwo, E.; Okonkwo, D. — loss attributed to weather event, Kessu Shelf, 14 April. Final wages applied against outstanding debt.*

Weather losses. Three names reduced to an adjustment column.

And a fourth Okonkwo asleep in a laundry basket, breathing against my collarbone, whom the official record said should not exist.

They would come for her. That certainty settled into me with the cold patience of ballast. Not today, maybe not this week — but the company did not tolerate loose threads, and a surviving Okonkwo was a thread pulled halfway out of the weave. Whoever wrote *forget this face* had known it too. Sooner or later, someone upstairs would connect the abandoned child to the drowned family, and then the tidy solution would present itself: one more infant, one more unrecorded departure, one more line in no ledger at all.

I could prevent that precisely one way. I could make her visible — documented, witnessed, entered into record where deletion leaves marks. But entering her into record meant explaining her, and explaining her meant opening the very file the company had sealed with three dead men's names.

The past wasn't staying buried. It had washed up on the company step, weighing nine pounds, and it had my father's eyes — no, that was foolishness, sentiment — it had *my* attention, which amounted to the same thing.

I put her back in the basket, and I took a clean sheet of paper, and at the bottom of it I drew, slowly and exactly, the mark from the corner of the swaddling cloth: three lines rising, a circle open beneath. Then beneath the mark I wrote the only honest sentence I had produced in eleven years of employment:

*This family was not lost to weather.*

I looked at it for a long time. Then I folded it away with the cloth.

If I was going to commit the rest of my life to this, I would need more than a scrap of embroidery and a dead man's daughter's memory. I would need procedure. Testimony. Evidence that could survive cross-examination. I would need to learn, discreetly, how an admiralty inquiry actually worked — who took the records, who swore the witnesses, and whether there existed anywhere in Vellamare a person whose entire profession was putting truth into the transcript.

It came to me then, the way answers come up from deep water, slow and certain: the deposition room. Third floor. Where the stenographers sat.

There was a woman there with quick hands and careful eyes. I'd noticed her the way I noticed everything — silently, and assuming it didn't matter.

Tomorrow, I decided, it might.
