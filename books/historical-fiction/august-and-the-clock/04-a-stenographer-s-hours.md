## 4. A Stenographer's Hours

The deposition room smelled of ink and old varnish, and Odile Marchetti had long ago decided that the smell was the truest thing in the building.

Everything else in the Halloway Trading Company's offices was polished, arranged, curated. The mahogany paneling gleamed. The clerks' collars were starched to knife-edges. Even the silence had been maintained, like a garden — pruned of anything that might grow wild. But the ink smelled honest. Ink remembered what it touched.

She sat at her stenograph machine with her fingers resting on the keys, listening.

"—and I state for the record that the waters were calm and the depth well within safe limits," said the witness, a harbor pilot named Grelling whose hands, Odile noticed, were folded too carefully on his knee, as though he'd rehearsed their position.

"State that again, please," said the company's counsel, a soft-spoken man named Voss who never raised his voice because he'd never needed to.

"The waters were calm. The depth was well within safe limits."

Odile's keys clicked. She transcribed everything. That was the job — not to understand it, not to weigh it, only to catch it whole, like a net catching fish she wasn't permitted to eat. In eleven years at the company's service she had transcribed cargo disputes, salvage claims, insurance depositions, and once, memorably, an argument between two captains over a stolen figurehead. She was good at her work. She was so good at her work that people forgot she was in the room, which was precisely how she liked it. A stenographer who was noticed was a stenographer who got reassigned.

But lately the fish in her net had begun to look wrong.

"Thank you, Mr. Grelling." Voss closed his folder. "Next week we'll hear from the dredge foreman."

The session ended. Grelling stood, shook hands with Voss with the enthusiasm of a man shaking hands with a doctor who has just given him bad news gently, and left. Odile rolled the paper from her machine and squared its edges.

"Excellent testimony," Voss said to no one in particular. "Very clear."

*Very clear.* Odile carried the transcript back to her desk in the records annex, a narrow room lined with folio boxes that smelled of dust and camphor, and there she did what she always did: she read what she had written.

And there it was again.

*The waters were calm and the depth well within safe limits.*

Word for word. Not similar — identical. The same nine words, in the same order, that she had transcribed from a fisherman named Aubert three weeks ago, and from a dock clerk named Prewitt before that, and from a boatman whose name she'd already forgotten. Four witnesses. Four depositions taken on different days, in different rooms, before different minor officials. And every one of them had used the same sentence, complete, unbroken, grammatically identical down to the conjunction.

People did not testify like that. People testified in fragments. They said *it was rough-ish, maybe*, or *I couldn't say exactly*, or they contradicted themselves and corrected themselves and wandered. Human memory was a messy instrument. Odile knew this better than anyone alive, because her entire profession consisted of writing down what messy instruments produced.

These men had not been remembering. They had been reciting.

She pulled the older transcripts from the boxes. Aubert's deposition, taken April 14th. Prewitt's, April 9th. The boatman's, March 30th. All of them concerning, in one way or another, the spring diving season — the season when, according to the company's own casualty ledger, three divers of the Okonkwo family had been lost to weather. *Weather losses,* the ledger said. As if the sea had acted alone. As if the sea kept books.

Odile sat very still in the annex, one hand flat on the papers, and let herself think the thought she had been refusing to think for two weeks:

Someone had written the sentence first, and then the witnesses had learned it.

She was not a naive woman. She was the daughter of a court stenographer and had grown up understanding that testimony was a currency, and currencies could be counterfeited. But there was a difference between knowing a thing in the abstract and holding four pages of counterfeit in your own two hands, in your own careful shorthand, with your own initials certifying each page as a true and accurate record.

Her initials. Her certification. If this ever came apart — if anyone ever asked why the court's own stenographer hadn't flagged identical testimony — where would she stand? Beside the truth, or beside the company?

She locked the transcripts in her personal drawer, which was against regulations, and went home, and slept badly.

* * *

The trouble with noticing things, Odile thought the next morning as she walked to work along the harbor road, is that you cannot stop.

The gulls were screaming over the fish market. The morning light came off the water in long hammered sheets, and the divers' boats were going out — she counted them without meaning to, five boats, eleven men, the same arithmetic she performed every day now whether she wanted to or not. Eleven men going down into water that four separate witnesses had sworn, in identical language, was perfectly safe.

At the records annex she found a memo waiting on her desk, typed on the director's stationery:

*Mme. Marchetti — Be advised that the admiralty inquiry into the spring season casualties will convene within the quarter. All relevant depositions are to be prepared for submission. Accuracy and completeness are expected. — A.C., by direction of Director Halloway.*

An admiralty inquiry. So it was official now. The company would present its record, the record would be accepted, the deaths would be entered into history as weather, and the matter would be closed forever inside a bound volume in a government archive. She had watched the machinery work before, on smaller matters. It worked beautifully. It was designed to.

She was still reading the memo a second time when she heard the knock.

Not a knock, really — a knuckle against the doorframe, light, apologetic, the sound of a man who had calculated exactly how much noise he was permitted to make. She looked up.

He was youngish, narrow, with the careful neatness of someone whose clothes were good but not too good, worn by someone who understood that attention was a debt. A company man by his cut — junior ledger rank, maybe, from the ink stain on his right middle finger. He stood in the doorway of the annex the way a man stands at the edge of deep water.

"Mme. Marchetti?" His voice was quiet. "Forgive the interruption. I'm Silas Ravensworth. Company ledgers."

"I know who you are." Everyone in the offices knew of Ravensworth — the diver's son who'd become the company's most reliable auditor, the man who never made errors and never made friends among the clerks either. "What do you want, Mr. Ravensworth?"

"A question." He glanced down the corridor behind him, an instinctive flick, quick enough that most people wouldn't have caught it. Odile caught it. "A procedural question. Nothing more."

"Procedural questions go to the registrar."

"The registrar reports to Director Halloway." He said it evenly, without accusation, the way one states the weather. "My question is one I'd prefer not to travel through the director's office."

Odile set down her pen. This was the moment — she recognized it with the clarity of long practice — where caution said to send him away. Caution had served her well for eleven years. Caution had a voice, and the voice said: *you don't know this man, you don't know what he wants, you don't know who sent him, and the safest thing in this building is to be a woman typing in a corner whom nobody remembers.*

But caution had also been sitting in the back of her mind all morning, next to the four identical sentences, growing quieter and quieter. Because there was another kind of caution, she was beginning to suspect, and it was the kind that watched for the wrong kind of attention — and this man, standing in her doorway with his careful questions and his edge-of-the-water stance, had the look of someone else who had been watching what shouldn't be watched.

"Close the door," she said.

He did, softly.

"Now ask your question."

He didn't ask it immediately. He looked at her stenograph machine, at the stacks of folio boxes, at the memo on her desk — she saw his eyes pass over it and pause, just barely, on the words *admiralty inquiry* — and when he spoke again his voice was lower.

"When the inquiry convenes. Testimony that's gathered privately — statements, accounts, taken outside the formal sessions. Can such testimony be entered into evidence? Or does it have to come through the company's own record?"

"That depends on who enters it."

"On a private party. An interested party."

"An interested party can petition to submit evidence," Odile said. "It must be witnessed. Dated. It should be certified — ideally by a notary, or—" she stopped herself, but not quickly enough.

"Or a court stenographer," Silas finished quietly.

The annex was very silent. Somewhere down the corridor a clerk laughed at something; the sound came through the door and died.

"You've been thinking about this a while," Odile said.

"For about as long as you've been noticing that four witnesses use the same sentence, Mme. Marchetti."

She felt the floor tilt slightly, the way it does on a ship when a swell comes under you. "I don't know what you mean."

"I audit shipments." He said it mildly, almost apologetically. "Numbers that repeat exactly are called errors. Words that repeat exactly are called something else. And you're the one who writes the words down." He held up a hand before she could speak. "I haven't read your transcripts. I couldn't. I'm telling you what I'd conclude, sitting where you sit. That's all."

"Why come to me?"

"Because you're the only person in this building whose whole profession is making sure the record is true." He paused. "And because I think you've already asked yourself the question I came to ask. Whether you're part of the record — or part of the cover over it."

There it was, laid out on the desk between them like a document neither had signed yet. Odile looked at this ledger man with his ink-stained finger and his quiet voice, and she ran through everything caution told her, and then she ran through the other arithmetic — eleven men on five boats, three names in a ledger marked *weather*, four identical sentences wearing her certification at the bottom.

"If I answer your procedural question," she said slowly, "that's all I've done. Answered a question. You understand that."

"I understand that completely."

"And if I ever learn that you've involved me in anything—"

"You won't learn it," he said. "You'll decide it. There's a difference, and I think you already know the difference, or you'd have called the corridor guard ten seconds ago."

God help her, he was right. She had had ten seconds, and she had spent them closing the door.

"Private testimony," she said, businesslike now, because procedure was solid ground and she needed to stand somewhere. "Can be submitted by any interested party. It must be original, signed by the witness, dated, and its chain of custody must be demonstrable — who took the statement, who held it, who transferred it. Break the chain and opposing counsel will break the testimony. The company's counsel will be looking for exactly that. Voss is thorough."

"Chain of custody," Silas repeated, and she watched the words land in him, watched him begin immediately to build with them, the way certain minds do — quietly, structurally, brick by brick. "Each statement signed. Each page dated. Each transfer recorded."

"You've done this before."

"No," he said. "But my father went down for this company, and the sea keeps no ledger, so somebody has to." He straightened. "Thank you, Mme. Marchetti. That's all I needed."

He turned toward the door, and Odile heard herself speak before caution could stop her.

"Mr. Ravensworth."

He stopped.

"The inquiry convenes within the quarter. The company has retained a historian — Aldous Crane, the chronicler. He arrives this week." She picked up the memo and held it out to him. "They are preparing their version of the record right now. Whatever you're planning to prepare — you should know they started first."

Silas took the memo. He read it once, and something moved behind his eyes — not fear exactly, and not surprise. Recognition. The look of a man who has just learned the shape of the race he is already running.

"Then I'll need to be faster," he said.

He left the way he'd come, quietly, without disturbing anything. Odile sat alone in the annex with her locked drawer full of counterfeit sentences, and she understood that she had just done the one thing eleven years of caution had forbidden: she had chosen a side in advance, simply by telling him what Crane's arrival meant.

She unlocked the drawer. She drew out a fresh pad of her private shorthand — the dense, idiosyncratic system her father had taught her, unreadable to any other soul in Vellamare — and she sharpened her pencil, and waited for whatever came next.

Down at the harbor, faintly, she could hear the gulls, and beneath them the hammers of the dockworkers, and beneath everything, the sea itself, keeping its own accounts.
