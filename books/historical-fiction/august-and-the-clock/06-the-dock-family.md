## 6. The Dock Family

Maren Okafor had buried four husbands, two of them by the sea, and she had learned this: grief is a debt you pay in installments, and the sea always extends the credit.

She was sixty-one years old and her hands had spent forty years in salt water. They were hands like knotted rope now, the fingers too stiff for the fine work of braiding hair or threading needles, but they could still gut a fish faster than any woman on the Vellamare docks, and they could still hold a child steady. That second skill she had thought retired, like her diving gear, hung up and dried out and good only for remembering.

Then Silas Ravensworth came up the plank road at dusk with a bundle against his chest, walking the way men walk when they are carrying something that could break — or something that could ruin them — and Maren set down her filleting knife and knew, before he said a word, that the sea was extending credit again.

* * *

The dock families lived in the lean-tos and stilt houses below the company warehouse district, where the tide left its smell on everything and the stairs went up to nowhere because the city fathers never bothered to pave past the customs house. It was not a neighborhood so much as an argument the sea was having with the land, and the people who lived there had chosen to stand in the middle of it. Divers mostly, and their widows, and the children of divers who would themselves dive if the company ever decided their lungs were worth the price of air.

Silas had been one of those children. Maren remembered his father — Obi Ravensworth, a man who could hold his breath longer than anyone and who used the extra time, always, to make other people laugh underwater, gesturing grandly at nothing, miming operas in the green dark while the pearls waited. The company sent him down into water that killed him, and the company wrote *weather loss* in a ledger, and Maren had taken Silas in along with six other dock women taking turns, because a boy needs feeding whether or not anyone's books say so.

So when he stood at her door with that look on his face — the careful face, the face he wore at the company offices, smooth as a harbor before weather — she didn't ask what happened. She held out her arms.

"Give her here."

Silas blinked. "How did you know it was a—"

"Boy, I've known you since you were wet behind the ears twice over. Give her here before you drop her from shaking."

The baby was small but not starving, which meant someone had fed her recently, which meant whoever left her at the Halloway door had cared enough to do one thing right. Her eyes were open. Dark, wide, unbothered by the whole weight of the world pressing in on her swaddling. She looked at Maren with the serious appraisal of the very young, deciding, apparently, that this knotted old woman would do.

"She's got an Okonkwo mark on the cloth," Silas said quietly. "I'm nearly sure of it."

Maren went still. Outside, the tide knocked against the pilings, patient as a creditor.

"You're sure."

"I brought it for you to look at."

He unwrapped the corner carefully, the way you'd unwrap something that might still be alive underneath — and in a sense it was, because a clan-mark is alive long after the family that carries it stops breathing. Maren looked at the stitched pattern: three interlocking spirals around a central bar, worked in indigo thread gone soft with washing.

Okonkwo. Tomas Okonkwo had worn that mark tattooed over his heart, where every diver wore theirs, so the sea would know whose it was when it took them. Tomas, and his brother Emeka, and his wife Adaeze — three divers on one rope team last spring, sent down into the deep beds off Corvo Point in waters the company had certified safe, and none of them had come up, and the company had written *weather losses* in a ledger, all three, though there had been no weather worth the name. Flat calm, the survivors said. Flat calm and a bottom that swallowed them.

"Where did you get her?" Maren asked.

"The company step. There was a note." He handed it over: *Forget this face.* "The watchman saw nothing. Or says he saw nothing."

"Or was paid to see nothing."

"Or was paid to see nothing," Silas agreed.

Maren looked down at the baby, who had grasped her thumb with one small hand, an instinct older than ledgers, older than companies. Hold on. That was the first instruction any diver's child ever learned, before words, before names: *hold on.*

"Somebody ran," Maren said slowly. "Somebody from the family — a cousin, a grandmother, somebody who knew the company would come for the rest. And they couldn't keep her, and they couldn't leave her anywhere ordinary, because ordinary places, the parish foundlings, the workhouse—" She stopped. "They left her where the mark would be seen. Left her where someone who knew how to read it would find her. And the company put you at that door, Silas Ravensworth, because God has a sense of humor even about ledgers."

"I can't keep her." He said it fast, the way you say things you're ashamed of. "My room, my contract — if they find out I hid her instead of reporting—"

"You did right not to report her." Maren's voice came out flat and hard, the voice she used on young divers who tried to skip their surface intervals. "You've seen what happens to inconvenient things at that company. You've written the entries yourself."

Silas looked away, toward the water. He had his father's shoulders and none of his father's ease, and Maren had watched him build that carefulness brick by brick since the day the company boat came back without Obi. A boy learns to be invisible when visibility kills people.

"But I can't hide her either," he said. "Not alone."

"No," Maren said. "You can't. But you're not alone. You never were. You just forgot." She shifted the baby to her shoulder, where the child sighed and settled as if she had been waiting all day for exactly this shoulder. "Bring her inside. We'll need milk, and blankets, and someone to sit up nights, and someone to lie beautifully if the wrong person asks questions. Fortunately, I have never in my life told the truth to anyone I didn't respect."

For the first time all evening, Silas smiled. It was small and crooked and it made him eleven years old again, and Maren thought: *there you are.*

* * *

By morning the whole lower dock knew, which is to say the whole lower dock had decided together not to know.

That is a thing outsiders never understood about the dock families. The city thought them lawless because they ignored the ordinances about fish weights and wharf fees. In truth they governed themselves with laws stricter than any statute, and the strictest was this: no child of a diver goes nameless. Not while one dock woman still had breath to argue.

Delphine Aubert arrived first, before the sun was properly up, with goat's milk warmed in a copper pot and opinions already fully formed. Delphine had lost her own daughter to the deep twenty years ago — not drowned, worse, born and then taken by fever — and she had hands that knew exactly what to do with a newborn, and she took one look at the baby and announced, "She'll be sickly. Small bones. We'll feed her every two hours and she'll be fine," in the tone of a woman forbidding the future to disagree.

The Okoye brothers came next, both of them ex-divers with burst eardrums and permanent half-deafness, and they stood around the cradle — an orange crate lined with wool, because nobody had anything better and nobody apologized for it — and studied the clan-mark on the cloth with the gravity of scholars.

"That's Okonkwo work, the stitching," Rufus Okoye said. "Adaeze did that pattern. My mother copied it off her at a wedding, years back. Three spirals for the three rivers, the center bar for the rope."

"The rope holds everything together," his brother Emmanuel said, "and the rivers pull it apart. It's not a hopeful design, if you want the truth."

"It's a true design," Maren said. "That's better than hopeful."

They argued the name through most of the morning. Delphine wanted Marie, after her mother. The Okoyes proposed Amara, and then Nkiru, and then a long string of names each more beautiful than the last, and Maren let them run on because the arguing itself was the ceremony — a name given easily is a name given lightly, and this child deserved the full weight of deliberation.

It was little Colette Aubert, Delphine's granddaughter, age nine, who ended it. She had been crouched at the window counting ships, and she turned around and said, "She came in August. Call her August. Nobody else is called August."

A silence. Then Emmanuel laughed until he had to sit down, which for a man with one working eardrum and a bad heart was saying something.

"August," Maren repeated, testing it. The baby, hearing herself named, did nothing whatsoever, which everyone agreed was a dignified response.

August. The month the pearl fleets went out heaviest. The month the water was warmest and the drowning was easiest. The month Obi Ravensworth died, though Maren kept that to herself — the child would have enough ghosts without inheriting Silas's.

"We'll need a story," Delphine said, practical as ever. "For the registrar, if it comes to that. Whose child is she?"

"Nobody's," Maren said. "And everybody's. She washed up with the tide. Half this street has raised children that washed up one way or another; let the registrar come down here and sort through all of them if he wants a paper trail."

But she said it lightly, and her mind was not light at all, because she had already understood what Silas was too young and too frightened to say aloud: this child was evidence. A living, breathing piece of evidence that the Okonkwo family had not ended neatly in the spring, the way the ledger said. Somebody had survived long enough to bear a child, or to carry one, and had believed — correctly, thanks to Silas and his soft heart under all that armor — that the mark would be read. Which meant somebody out there knew things. Which meant the company, when it learned of this child's existence, would want her erased as thoroughly as it had erased her family.

*Well,* Maren thought, watching Delphine feed her with the practiced tenderness of a woman getting a second chance at her own lost months, *the company has erased a great many things. Let it try this one.*

* * *

In the afternoon, when the baby slept and the others had dispersed to their work, Maren sat alone with the swaddling cloth spread across her knees and let herself do the arithmetic she had been avoiding for forty years.

Tomas Okonkwo. Emeka Okonkwo. Adaeze Okonkwo. Spring, this year. *Weather losses.*

Obi Ravensworth. Eleven years ago, August. *Weather loss.*

Delphine's husband Pieter, thirty years back, and before him a whole liturgy of names Maren carried the way other women carried rosaries: Kofi Mensah, taken in '71. Sela Bright, '74, the only woman diver the company ever certified, and they'd certified her because she was cheaper than a man and mourned her less. The Ananias brothers, both on one rope, '79 — the company changed its rules after that, briefly, and then changed them back when the rules cost money. Old Mama Tibeau, who hadn't even been diving, just standing on the wharf when the company crane dropped a cargo net, and whose death had been entered in the books as *equipment loss*, the equipment being, presumably, Mama Tibeau.

Forty years of it. Forty years of *losses*, a word that made it sound like misplacing a glove. The sea took some, that was true and always true; the sea was honest in its hunger. But the company had learned it could write the sea's signature on its own crimes, and once a lie gets into a ledger, it breeds. Every falsified depth reading begat a falsified report begat a falsified history, and at the end of it all stood respectable men in good coats signing their names to documents that said the drowned had drowned themselves.

Maren had spent her life telling herself the same comforting thing every dock family told itself: we know the truth, whatever the paper says. The dead live in our mouths. As long as we say their names, the ledgers don't matter.

But Silas had come up that plank road with a baby in his arms and fear all over him, and she had looked at him and understood, finally, the flaw in the comfort. We say the names to each other. We say them in lean-tos the city won't pave, in a language the clerks don't speak. And the company's historians write their version in ink that lasts centuries, and someday we'll be gone, all of us, and the paper will be the only voice left, and the paper will say the Okonkwos were careless and the sea is dangerous and the Halloway Trading Company is respectable.

She looked at the sleeping child. August, nine days old maybe, breathing with the fierce concentration of the very small, one fist escaped from the blanket and curled against her cheek.

Every diver the sea had taken, Maren could accept, in her worst and wisest hours, as the price of the trade — the sea was owed, and divers had always known it, and gone down anyway. That was the bargain and she would not cheapen the dead by pretending they hadn't known it. But every diver the *company* had taken — those were not the sea's debts. Those were thefts dressed up as weather. And the difference between the two had never once appeared in a Halloway ledger.

"They will not erase you," Maren said to the baby, quietly, because some vows are too heavy for a full voice. "You hear me? There's people in this world whose whole job is turning lives into lines in books, lines they can cross out when crossing out is convenient. Your family was a line once. Crossed out. Well." She touched the tiny fist, and August gripped her finger in her sleep, holding on, holding on. "You're not a line. You're a whole page. And this street knows how to keep pages."

The tide turned outside, the way it did twice a day regardless of ledgers, and Maren sat with her vow until the light went long and gold across the water.

* * *

Silas came back at dusk, carrying tinned milk and a wooden rattle he clearly hadn't bought on purpose — Maren watched him realize he was holding a toy and saw the exact moment he decided not to be embarrassed about it, which was, she thought, the bravest thing she'd see all week.

"How is she?" he asked.

"Loud, hungry, and adored. Sit down. Hold her. You'll do it badly at first; that's how everyone does it."

He sat. He held. He did it badly — arms rigid, elbows locked, like a man defusing ordnance — and August screamed her opinion of his technique until Delphine repositioned them both, and then he looked down at the small red furious face settling against his chest and Maren watched something in him unlock that had been locked since he was seven years old.

"She needs papers eventually," Silas said, not looking up. "And a name on them. And the inquiry about the Okonkwo dive is coming — I heard the clerks talking today. If the company connects her to that family before anyone safe connects her first—"

"Then we connect her first," Maren said. "Tell me about this inquiry. Who takes the testimony? Where does it go?"

Silas finally looked up, and there was something new in his careful face — not hope exactly, hope was a younger man's currency, but the beginning of a plan, which in Silas Ravensworth amounted to the same thing.

"There's a court stenographer," he said. "Odile Marchetti. She transcribes the depositions. And Maren — I think she's noticed things. Things in the record that shouldn't be there."

Maren leaned back against the wall, listening to the tide knock its patient knuckles against the pilings, and felt, for the first time in forty years of installments, the balance of the debt begin to shift.

"Then tomorrow," she said, "you go and talk to her."
