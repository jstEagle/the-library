## 7. Questions in Safe Waters

The inventory route was mine by seniority of the unloved. Nobody at Halloway Trading Company wanted the salt-cured circuit — the row of low warehouses along the breakwater where the divers' gear was stored between seasons. It paid nothing in prestige and everything in solitude: three days of counting masks and weights, checking lacquer on air lines, signing forms nobody read. Overseer Bragg had handed me the manifest with the particular disgust he reserved for work beneath his dignity, which was most work.

Which meant that for three days, I could knock on doors.

I had rehearsed it on the walk out from the company offices, the way I rehearsed everything. An inventory check was an inventory check. A man with a ledger asking about gear is a bore, not a threat. Boredom opens more doors than honesty ever has. So I went warehouse to warehouse, hut to hut along the shanty rows behind the curing sheds, counting what they already knew I'd count, and then — leaning casual against a doorframe, pen still in hand — I asked my real questions.

*You were diving the Kessel shelf last spring, weren't you? Strange season for it. Cold spring.*

*Your partner took the deep runs that week? He always dive deep?*

*Funny thing — the requisition log shows extra line drawn that month. Heavy line. You recall who signed for it?*

Nine men. Nine doors. Eight of them gave me nothing but weather.

It was not cowardice, what I saw in their faces. I want to be fair to them, because within the year I would ask braver men than these to do a harder thing, and I would need to remember that fear is not a failure of character but a fact of the body, like hunger. What I saw was arithmetic. Each man looked at me — the quiet clerk with the clean ledgers, the diver's son gone soft indoors — and did the sum of what a conversation might cost, and closed the door gently, almost kindly, as if sparing me the trouble of hearing them lie.

One man, Ferro, who had a bad ear from a barotrauma years back, stood in his doorway a long moment after I asked about heavy line. His eyes went past my shoulder to the curing sheds, where two company watchmen leaned against a post sharing a pipe.

"Not here," he said, so low I nearly missed it. "Thursday. The net racks after dark. Bring your ledger." Then, louder, for the benefit of anyone listening: "Third row's short two masks. You'll want to note that."

I noted it.

* * *

Thursday came down grey and smelling of rain that wouldn't commit. The net racks stretched along the tideline behind the fourth warehouse — long wooden frames where the diving nets were hung to dry, forming corridors of rope and shadow that swallowed lamplight whole. A good place for conversations that didn't happen. Dock people knew every such place in Vellamare; we had been holding unsanctioned meetings in the city's blind spots for three generations.

Ferro was there before me, sitting on an upturned crate in the deepest corridor of nets, and beside him, hunched forward with his elbows on his knees, was a man I recognized only slowly, because grief had redrawn him.

Ezra Ndaya. Tomas Okonkwo's partner. Twelve years they had worked the same line, shared the same air tender, surfaced within arm's reach of each other so many hundred times that the other divers swore they could feel each other's thoughts through thirty feet of water. Tomas had died in March with his wife's brother and the boy who was to have married into the family — three of the four divers down on the Kessel shelf when the sea decided it was done waiting. Ezra had surfaced. Ezra had always surfaced. And I remembered now, from the funeral I'd watched from the edge of the crowd, that no one had stood beside him at the graveside. The clan had lost too much to spare comfort for the man who lived.

"Mr. Ravensworth." He said my name like a form he was filling out. "Ferro says you've been doing the rounds."

"Inventory," I said. "Routine."

He smiled without any humor in it at all. "In Vellamare, we say a thing twice when the first saying was a lie. Inventory."

I sat down on the crate opposite him. Between us hung a curtain of netting, diamond-shaped shadows crossing his face. I set my ledger on my knee and left it closed.

"Tomas's daughter is alive," I said.

I had planned to arrive there slower. I had planned, God help me, to be tactful. But he was watching me with the flat patience of a man measuring how much time I intended to waste, and I found I couldn't do it. Not with him.

"You've seen her?" His voice cracked on the second word.

"At the docks. She's with Maren Oduya's people. They're keeping her." I hesitated. "She has the clan-mark on her swaddling. That's how I knew whose she was. That's why I'm asking my questions."

For a long moment Ezra Ndaya put his face in his hands, and the nets above us creaked in the wind off the water, and I sat very still and let a grown man grieve in front of a stranger, because there was nothing else to do and nothing else worth doing.

When he lifted his head, something had changed in him. Not lightness. Resolution, maybe. Or surrender — with Ezra, that night, I honestly couldn't tell you which.

"They certified those waters safe," he said. "You know that. It's in the report. Depth soundings taken in February, safe bottom at forty fathoms, good visibility, no current risk. Company seal on it. We all read it, or had it read to us. Forty fathoms." He laughed once, a terrible sound. "Forty fathoms is a generous day's work. Forty fathoms we could dive till we were old men."

I waited. In the archives I had learned that the truth, once started, does not like to be interrupted.

"The new bed was deeper," Ezra said. "Charted in January by one of the company survey boats. Pearl bed like nothing anyone had seen — old shells, thick beds, the kind of take a man retires on. But it lay at sixty fathoms. Maybe more. And the water over it ran cold and fast, and every man who'd worked the outer shelf knew what that meant. The thermocline sits wrong out there. Your air goes strange. Your head goes strange."

"The report said forty," I said.

"The report said forty." He nodded slowly. "And Director Halloway's man came to us in February with the company seal and the soundings, and we believed it, because why would a company spend money certifying waters it meant to kill us in? There's no economy in that." His mouth twisted. "Except there is. Four dead divers costs the company four burial fees. A pearl bed like that one is worth more than this whole waterfront."

"You were on that dive," I said. Not a question. Ferro had told me enough to know.

"I was on the line with Tomas." Ezra's voice went very steady, the way a man's voice does when he is carrying something fragile across a long distance and must not shake it. "We drew the deep runs. We always did — we were the strongest pair, everyone knew it, and being strong is how they use you. Down past fifty the cold hit like a wall. Past fifty-five my lamp played tricks. And Tomas — Tomas kept going, because the order was down to the bed, and the order came from topside, and Tomas Okonkwo never disobeyed a topside order in his life. That was the joke about him. *Tomas would dive into hell if the tally sheet said hell.*"

He stopped. The wind moved through the nets. Somewhere behind the warehouses a dog barked twice and quit.

"He signaled me to go up," Ezra said finally. "At fifty-eight fathoms. My ears were bleeding — I could see it in my lamp, black threads in the water. He pointed up and made the sign we used, the one that means *go, don't argue.* And I went. Because I was hurt, and because he ordered it, and because some part of me already understood that the water down there was not water anymore. It was something else. Something with opinions."

"And when you surfaced?"

"There was a man from the company on the tender. Not the usual air tender. A clerk — I learned later he was from the director's office. He pulled me onto the boards, wrapped me in a blanket, and put his hand on my chest like this—" Ezra pressed his palm flat against his own sternum— "and he said, *The bed is at forty fathoms. Say forty fathoms.* And I said, *Tomas is still down,* and he said, *The bed is at forty fathoms.* "

The rain finally arrived, fine and hissing on the nets above us, threading down through the rope diamonds in silver lines.

"Three days later," Ezra said, "a woman brought an envelope to my door. Thirty pounds. More money than I'd seen in two seasons. No letter. No signature. Just the money and a card with the company crest, and I understood exactly what it was, because I'd already given my testimony to the inquiry clerk — *forty fathoms, rough water, his own line fouled* — the words they'd written for me before I spoke them." He looked up at me through the netting. "It wasn't payment for lying, Mr. Ravensworth. I'd already lied, for free, because I was afraid and because my lies cost me nothing that day. The money was for continuing to lie. Forever. It was rent on my silence, paid in advance, and I took it, and I have bought things with it, and my children have eaten from it, and every meal has tasted like the bottom of the sea."

I opened my ledger. Not to write — he flinched anyway — but because I needed my hands to hold something while I asked the next thing.

"Will you say this again? Where it counts? At the inquiry?"

The flinch became something worse. He stood, abruptly, and walked three paces down the corridor of nets, his back to me, shoulders rigid under his coat.

"You don't understand what you're asking. You keep books. You've never been on a line in your life. If I speak, I lose the money, I lose the work, my name goes on whatever list they keep, and my family—" He turned. "I watched them die, Mr. Ravensworth. I heard Tomas's last signal through the water. Do you know what I hear every night since March? I hear that signal. And I thought, if I stay quiet, if I take their money and keep my place, then at least his death bought something. At least the bed got worked. At least it *meant* something, all that cold water." His voice broke entirely now. "But they sealed the bed too. After the accident. Too much attention, too many questions. Three men and a boy dead and the company won't even harvest what killed them. So his death bought nothing. Bought *less* than nothing. And I still can't make my mouth open."

I rose and stood across from him in the dark, close enough to see the old scars on his hands, the diver's calluses that never soften.

"I'm not going to pretend I know what the bottom feels like," I said. "I was eight when my father died diving, and I remember the funeral and nothing else, so I won't insult you with grief comparisons. But I'll tell you what I do know. I know records. I know that right now, somewhere in the Halloway archive, there is a safety report with the true soundings on it, and I believe it's been altered, and I believe I can prove it. What I cannot manufacture is a witness. Paper lies beautifully. People are the only thing paper can't fake." I held his eyes. "I'm not asking tonight. I'm asking you to keep it possible. Don't sign anything else. Don't repeat their numbers again where a clerk can write them down. Just — leave the door unlocked in yourself. That's all. Leave it unlocked."

Ezra stared at me for a long time. Rain ran off the nets between us.

"Maren Oduya's people have the girl," he said at last.

"Yes."

"Then she'll eat well, at least." He bent and picked up his hat from the crate. "Inventory," he said, loud enough for the dark to hear. "You counted what there was to count. Third row's short two masks. Note it and go home."

I noted it.

* * *

Walking back along the breakwater with the rain easing to mist, I did what I always did with dangerous knowledge: I filed it. Ezra Ndaya, willing to talk, unwilling yet to testify. Ferro, willing to arrange, unwilling to be named. Eight refusals and one crack in the wall — and cracks, in my experience of ledgers and of companies, only ever widen under pressure or get patched over. The question was which would come first: the inquiry, or the patchers.

There was a third thing, too, that I carried home like a stone in my boot, and I made myself look at it squarely rather than limping around it. Ezra had taken the money. Ezra had repeated their numbers. And I had sat in the dark judging no one, feeling the great warm lift of *understanding*, and underneath the understanding something colder and less forgivable: relief. Relief that the worst witness — the one whose guilt ran deepest, whose voice would tremble, whose recanting, if it ever came, would cut hardest — was the one who hadn't said yes. Some part of me had already begun calculating which testimonies my case could survive losing.

That is what companies do to you even when you hate them. They teach you their arithmetic.

At the top of the dock stairs, Maren's lamp was burning in the window of the little room where August slept, and I stood a moment in the wet dark looking at it, letting it burn a hole through all my careful bookkeeping. One witness who might yet stand. One altered report I had to find. And a child ashore who would someday learn what her family had been worth to the Halloway Trading Company — thirty pieces, or sixty fathoms, depending on whose ledger you read.

Two days later, when I came off the morning tide-count, Odile Marchetti was waiting outside the company gates, ostensibly adjusting her umbrella, actually watching the door where a stranger in very good clothes was being escorted inside by two directors themselves.

"That," she said quietly, "is Aldous Crane. The historian. They've retained him to write the official history of the company before the inquiry sits." She looked at me, and her eyes were grim. "Silas — he doesn't record what happened. Whatever he writes is what everyone will *remember* happening. We have weeks, not months."
