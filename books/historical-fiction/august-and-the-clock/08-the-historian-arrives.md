## 8. The Historian Arrives

The summons came on a Tuesday, folded inside a deposition folio that Odile had been told to prioritize.

Not a request. A summons, on Halloway Trading Company letterhead, signed by the chief clerk's assistant — not even the chief clerk himself, because Odile Marchetti was the kind of employee one summoned through intermediaries, the way one summoned a plumber or a chimney sweep. *Stenographic services required. Committee Room B. Nine o'clock sharp. Bring your own pencils.*

She brought her own pencils. She always did. The company's pencils were the cheap cedar ones whose graphite smeared if you looked at them wrong, and Odile had built eleven years of professional reputation on the fact that nothing she transcribed ever smudged, never faded, never required a second reading to understand. Her shorthand was taught to no apprentices. Her folios were requested by name when accuracy mattered and avoided like plague when it didn't.

Committee Room B smelled of lemon polish and old arguments. Four men sat at the long table, three of whom she recognized from depositions past: Under-Director Pell, whose voice she could reproduce in her sleep; a lawyer named Greaves whose pauses she had learned to mark with a special symbol meaning *nothing is coming*; and a third man whose face she knew but couldn't place until he spoke and she remembered — a shipping assessor, once, in a case about drowned cargo.

And at the head of the table, in a chair that no one had assigned to him but which everyone had somehow already conceded, sat a stranger.

He was perhaps fifty-five, silver at the temples in a way that looked cultivated rather than earned, wearing a grey suit cut in the London fashion but softened just enough to suggest a man too important for fashion itself. His hands were folded on the table before him with the patience of a man who had never once had to reach for anything quickly. When the door closed behind her, he smiled — not at her, exactly, but in her general direction, the way sunlight falls on a piece of furniture.

"Gentlemen," he said, "shall we begin?"

Under-Director Pell cleared his throat. "Miss Marchetti. Thank you for coming. This is Mr. Aldous Crane."

The name landed in the room with weight, and Odile understood why the third man's face had been familiar: everyone at Vellamare knew Aldous Crane's face, because his face was on the frontispiece of his books, engraved, serious, faintly compassionate. Author of *The Taming of the Outer Shoals*. Author of *Empire of Salt and Silk*. Author of the two-volume history of the Northern Fisheries Compact that sat, leather-bound and unreadable, on every respectable shelf in the city, including — Odile happened to know — the shelf behind Director Halloway's desk, where it functioned less as reading material than as a declaration of allegiance.

"Miss Marchetti." Crane rose halfway, an economy of movement, and inclined his head. "I've read your work."

"My work, Mr. Crane?"

"Your transcripts." He resumed his seat. "A deposition is a transcript only when it's done badly. Done properly, it's testimony — a thing that stands on its own, that outlives everyone in the room. Yours stand. I noticed it years ago, actually, in the Meridian case. There's a page in your record of the second hearing — the fisherman who changed his story? You rendered his hesitation so precisely that I could hear it. I used it."

"Used it, Mr. Crane?"

"In my chapter on coastal testimony. I quoted you without knowing your name. I should very much like to know it now."

Odile set down her pencil case. Something in her — the part of her that had spent eleven years being summoned rather than invited — wanted to be pleased. She examined the wanting the way she examined a witness's phrasing: carefully, from all sides, suspicious of what lay underneath.

"That's flattering," she said. "I wasn't aware historians worked from transcripts."

"All good history works from transcripts." He opened the leather portfolio before him and drew out a single sheet, laying it flat as though setting a bird on a table. "Which is why I'm here. The company has engaged me to prepare its official history. Commemorative volume, naturally — the fiftieth anniversary approaches — but given the circumstances of the moment, the timing has acquired a certain urgency. The admiralty inquiry opens within the month, I understand. It would be unfortunate if the public's first encounter with this company's story were conducted entirely through the mouths of its accusers."

Greaves shifted. Pell studied his fingernails. The room had the particular silence of men who had agreed on something in advance and were now pretending they hadn't.

"You'll be taking dictation, Miss Marchetti," Pell said. "Mr. Crane composes aloud. It's faster than waiting for his manuscript pages. Everything you record will be returned to him for approval before filing."

"I see." She looked at Crane. "And what happens to what I record after Mr. Crane approves it?"

"It becomes history," he said pleasantly.

* * *

He began to compose at ten minutes past nine, and Odile discovered within four sentences that she was watching something she had never seen before in eleven years of transcription.

She had recorded liars. She had recorded men who wept convincingly, men who forgot conveniently, men whose memories rearranged themselves under pressure like furniture in a flooded house. Lying, she had learned, was a failure of nerve. Liars hesitated. They over-elaborated. They reached for details that collapsed when touched.

Aldous Crane lied the way other men breathed, and he did not hesitate, because — and this was the discovery that made her pencil hand go cold, that made her glad her shorthand was her own — he did not believe he was lying.

"The founding of the Halloway Trading Company," he dictated, pacing now, his hands moving as though conducting an orchestra only he could hear, "was an act of faith. Josiah Halloway arrived in Vellamare in 1847 with a single brig, a cargo manifest, and a conviction — mark this phrase, Miss Marchetti, I want it exact — *a conviction that the sea rewards preparation and punishes presumption*. From the first, safety was the company's religion. Every dive charted. Every depth measured twice. Every diver valued not as instrument but as artisan."

He paused, tasting his own sentence, and nodded as if it had passed inspection.

"Good. Continue."

"There is a legend," Crane went on, "that the early years were marked by losses. Every maritime enterprise has such legends; they are the barnacles on the hull of progress. But the record — the true record, the documented record — shows a company that learned faster than the sea could teach it. Where other firms buried their dead in unmarked graves and moved on, Halloway kept registers. Halloway commissioned inquiries. Halloway, gentlemen, *remembered*."

Odile wrote. Her hand was steady. Her hand was always steady; that was the whole point of her. But beneath the steadiness she felt something turning over, slow and cold, like something large rolling in deep water.

Because she had read the registers. Transcribing depositions for the company meant access, eventually, to everything the company filed, and Odile Marchetti forgot nothing she transcribed — that was the other half of her reputation. She remembered the 1861 register, where six names appeared under the heading *seasonal attrition*, with causes listed as *fever* for men who had, according to the widow's petition she'd typed two years later, come up bleeding from the ears. She remembered the compensation schedule of 1874, which paid out per lost diver a sum precisely calibrated to be cheaper than safety equipment. She remembered the inquiry Halloway had commissioned after the Sable Rock incident, and how its conclusions had been received into the record with four pages missing, pages she had personally been asked to renumber around.

Halloway remembered. That much was true. It simply remembered the way a man remembers a debt he intends never to pay.

"Miss Marchetti." Crane had stopped pacing and was looking at her with mild interest. "You've stopped writing."

"I haven't stopped, Mr. Crane. I'm keeping pace." She turned the folio toward him briefly, letting him see the dense, unreadable columns of her shorthand. "Dictation at your speed requires abbreviation. Unless you'd prefer I fall behind and ask you to repeat."

Something flickered across his face — assessment, brief and total, the look of a man checking a knot — and then the smile returned. "By all means. Keep pace."

But she had seen him check the knot, and she knew he would check it again, and she understood that from this morning forward she was a variable in Aldous Crane's calculations. The thought did not frighten her as much as it should have. What frightened her more was the sentence he had just dictated, which she had rendered faithfully in her private symbols, and which she now read back to herself silently:

*Where other firms buried their dead in unmarked graves and moved on, Halloway kept registers.*

It was a beautiful sentence. It was almost true. And that, she thought, was the whole architecture of the thing — not lies laid over the truth, but truth selected, sanded, oiled, and mounted in a display case with the sharp edges filed off. He was not going to deny the divers' deaths. Denial could be cross-examined. He was going to *contextualize* them, elegantly, irreproachably, until the deaths became weather, became cost, became the price of progress paid by men who had known the risks — and the inquiry, presented with so seamless and well-sourced a document, would find nothing to grip. You cannot subpoena fog. Crane was manufacturing fog, in committee rooms, on company time, and calling it remembrance.

* * *

They broke at noon, and Odile carried her folio to the clerks' room as instructed, where she found the transcription pool already transformed.

There were six clerks in the pool, five of them young men whose ambitions were proportionate to their salaries, and Crane had been among them for exactly two days. Already there was a tin of his personal coffee blend on the sideboard, offered around with the casual generosity of a man who never needed to buy loyalty because he collected it as a byproduct of existing. Already young Fenwick had asked him about London and received, apparently, forty minutes of anecdotes involving Disraeli's cat. Already someone had procured — she refused to learn how — a small vase of fresh rosemary for Crane's corner desk, because he had remarked once, in passing, that he thought better near the smell of it.

"He's writing our history," Fenwick said to her, glowing, as she hung her coat. "The actual book. Imagine — fifty years from now, people will read him and he'll have mentioned the archive room, and we'll be in it. The clerks. In the book."

"Fifty years from now," Odile said, hanging her coat on its hook, "we'll be dead, Fenwick."

"Well — yes. But remembered."

She looked at him — twenty-three, ink-stained, sincere — and felt the day's dread compress into something sharper. That was how it worked, then. Not through conspiracy, which required coordination and left traces, but through vanity, which required nothing but an audience. Crane would charm the archive into curating itself. The clerks would fetch him the documents he asked for and think themselves honored. Nobody would need to destroy inconvenient records if the official narrative grew up around them like ivy around a wall — thick enough that no one would ever again find the wall.

At two o'clock Crane summoned her again, and this time he had a folder of source materials arranged on the table with visible care, the way a magician arranges a deck.

"We come now," he said, "to the spring of this year. A sad season. Three lives lost — the Okonkwo family, divers of considerable local standing. I intend to treat it with dignity. Grief, honestly rendered, ennobles a history; evasion cheapens it. So let us look together at what the record shows."

He slid the first document across the table. Odile read it.

It was the site certification for the April dive — the waters off Greyfriar Bank — and it was clean. Immaculate. Depths noted, conditions assessed, hazards considered, all in a tidy administrative hand, concluding with the words *fit for commercial operation at stated depths* and a signature she recognized.

She recognized it because she had transcribed a different version.

In February, in this same building, in a deposition taken in the presence of counsel, a junior surveyor named Ellery Vance had testified about a preliminary report — a report with depth warnings, a report that flagged the cold current at thirty fathoms, a report that had been, in his careful lawyer-approved phrasing, *subject to subsequent revision.* Odile had rendered his hesitation so precisely that anyone reading it could hear the sound of a young man watching his career walk a plank. That deposition existed. It was filed. She knew the folio number.

The document on Crane's table made no mention of any preliminary report. It stood alone, complete, self-sufficient — a certificate with no birth certificate behind it. History, manufactured from whole cloth, dressed in the honest clothing of paperwork.

"This is the final certification," she said. Her voice sounded very far away to her own ears. "Was there not a surveyor's preliminary assessment?"

"There was," Crane agreed mildly, "and its findings were incorporated into the final document. That is what incorporation means, Miss Marchetti. The refinement of rough findings into authoritative form." He watched her across the table, hands folded, patient as furniture. "You seem troubled."

"I'm noting discrepancies."

"Between?"

"Between documents."

He smiled then — fully, warmly, the smile of the frontispiece engravings, the smile that Fenwick would describe tonight over drinks with genuine admiration — and said, with the gentle authority of a man correcting a student's arithmetic:

"Discrepancy implies two versions of equal standing. What exists here, Miss Marchetti, is the version. The other papers — drafts, preliminaries, the inevitable debris of process — those are not versions. They are noise. My work, and yours, is to leave the reader with signal." He gathered the certificate and squared its edges against the table. "Now. Shall we proceed to the question of the divers' experience levels? I have some lovely material about their fondness for risk. Very humanizing."

That evening she stayed late in the clerks' room, alone, and pulled the February deposition from the filing shelves — folio 4471, Ellery Vance, junior surveyor. She read her own transcript twice. Then she took a fresh sheet of paper and, in her private shorthand, the shorthand taught to no apprentice, she copied out the passage where Vance described the depth warnings in the preliminary report, and the current at thirty fathoms, and who had asked him to revise.

She folded the sheet small and slipped it between the pages of a completed deposition folio from an unrelated admiralty case, one of hundreds, gray and forgotten. Then she put on her coat and walked home through the dock district, past the taverns and the net-menders' sheds, and found herself slowing outside the wide window of a chandlery where a newspaper placard leaned against the glass.

LOCAL HISTORIAN TO CHRONICLE HALLOWAY'S GOLDEN AGE, it said. INQUIRY TIMING CALLED "COINCIDENCE."

She stood in the cold looking at the word *golden* and thinking about a man who had quoted her own transcription back to her as flattery, hours before asking her to sharpen the knife.

Fog, she thought. He was making fog, beautifully, and by the time the inquiry convened there would be so much of it that three dead divers would be invisible from the harbor wall.

Unless somebody kept a record the fog couldn't reach.

She turned up her collar and walked on, already composing in her head the list of witnesses whose depositions she had transcribed over the years — the ones whose hesitation she had preserved like insects in amber — and wondering, with a dread that tasted unpleasantly like hope, how one would go about finding a man who worked in the Halloway counting-house and asked quiet questions about inquiry procedure.

She had heard he'd been asking. The clerks talked. Clerks always talked.
