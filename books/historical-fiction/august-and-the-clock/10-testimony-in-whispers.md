## 10. Testimony in Whispers

Odile Marchetti had a theory about shorthand that she had never shared with anyone: that a system built for speed was also, by accident, a system built for secrecy.

The Pitman-style glyphs the court taught its stenographers were not letters. They were gestures — a hooked stroke for *h*, a downward slash for the *th* that ended words like *truth*. A stranger picking up her notebook would see weather, or birdsong, or the tracks of some small anxious animal. Only someone trained at the Vellamare Stenographic Institute, only one of perhaps forty living practitioners in the city, could read it back. And even among those forty, every hand drifted. Odile had developed private abbreviations years ago — little corruptions of the standard forms, invented to shave a syllable here and there — until her shorthand was, in effect, a language of one.

She had never thought much about this. It was simply how she worked, the way a locksmith thinks nothing of the hundred keys on his ring.

Then Silas Ravensworth had come to her office with a list of names, and now she thought about it constantly.

It was Thursday evening, and the Records Hall of the Admiralty Annex had emptied an hour ago. The gas lamps along the transcription gallery were turned low, per regulation, and Odile sat at the last desk by the window with a deposition folio open before her and a second document hidden beneath it — a loose sheet covered in her own quick strokes, transcribed from notes she had taken two nights before in Maren's kitchen, sitting near the stove while a retired diver named Elio Foss spoke in a voice like gravel settling.

*Ordered down,* the strokes said, if you knew how to read them. *Past the line on the gauge. Halloway's man on the boat said the bed was worth the risk and the risk was ours. I saw the gauge myself. I heard him say it.*

She read it back once, silently, the way she'd been taught — *never trust your memory of what you wrote; read the page* — and her stomach did what it always did lately, a slow cold turn, like something rolling over in deep water.

Forty people in the city could read this. Perhaps fewer who would.

She blotted the page, folded it into eighths, and opened the folio in front of her: Deposition 4471, the testimony of a harbormaster's clerk regarding disputed cargo weights, taken in June, certified, signed, shelved pending routine review. Nobody would ever look at it again. She slid her folded sheet into the hollow between the folio's sewn quires, where decades of handling had loosened the binding just enough to admit four thin pages, and pressed the cover flat.

To any inspector pulling the folio for audit, it would be exactly what it appeared: finished business, dead paper, weight without meaning.

Odile had been hiding things inside official records for eleven days now, and she had stopped sleeping properly, but her hands had never been steadier.

* * *

The problem, as she had explained to Silas that first careful conversation — conducted, at his insistence, walking the long circuit of the breakwater where the wind ate their words — was not transcription. Transcription was easy. The problem was proof.

"A page of shorthand proves nothing," she'd said. "Worse than nothing. If Crane's people find it, they'll say I invented it. They'll say you invented it together. A court doesn't weigh what a page says, Mr. Ravensworth. It weighs where the page came from."

"Then we tell it where it came from," Silas said. "Every statement witnessed. Every witness signing. Dated."

"You're describing chain of custody. That's evidence procedure. That's my job."

"Yes," he said, and there was something almost like a smile, gone as fast as it came. "I've been reading the inquiry rules. There wasn't much else to read in the archive."

They had worked out the method over three more meetings, each one more reckless than the last, because caution, Odile discovered, is a currency you spend down. The system they settled on was simple enough to survive nervous hands:

Each diver told his account to Odile, in whatever place he chose — a kitchen, a net loft, the lee side of a beached hull. She took it down in her corrupted shorthand, in the moment, the way she took depositions, so that the words were hers-by-craft but not hers-by-invention. Within a day she rendered it into fair English copy. The diver read it — those who could; those who couldn't had it read aloud twice — and then signed the foot of every page. Below the signature, Odile wrote the date, the place, and a single line: *Taken down from the mouth of the witness and read back to him entire.*

And then, because a signed page still needed to survive, the copies went into the folios. Finished depositions, certified and shelved, each one a small locked room with a false bottom. Odile kept a key-list in her head: 4471 held Elio Foss. 4388, a customs dispute from April, held the first statement of Joaquin Reyes. 4502 — she'd chosen that one with particular pleasure, an inquiry into a collapsed warehouse the company had settled quietly — held the pages that mattered most, the ones naming depth, and orders, and money paid for silence.

"The beautiful thing," she'd told Silas, "is that if they search my desk, my rooms, my person, they find nothing. The evidence is already filed. It's inside the company of its enemies' own paperwork, wearing its uniform."

"And if the annex burns?" Silas asked.

She'd had no answer for that yet. It was the flaw in the design, and both of them knew it, and neither of them said the obvious thing aloud: that men like Aldous Crane, when cornered, did not generally resort to arson — they resorted to something quieter. Recanting. Purchase. Threat.

Still. For now, the truth existed in eleven hiding places across two floors of the Admiralty Annex, signed and dated, and hope — fragile, absurd, handmade hope — had acquired paper and ink.

* * *

Friday brought Joaquin Reyes.

He came to the transcription gallery at the close of day, correctly dressed, correctly miserable, carrying a petition regarding a mooring fee — which was even genuine, Odile suspected, a real grievance serving as passport. She stamped his petition, made the correct bored noises, and then, per the arrangement, asked the question she asked everyone whose papers touched diving families: whether he had anything further to add regarding harbor conditions, for the record, the annex liked thoroughness.

What followed was the hardest hour of her professional life.

Reyes spoke badly. He spoke in circles. Twice he stood up as if to leave, and twice sat back down, and Odile took him down stroke by stroke, her stylus moving through the pauses as confidently as through the words, because the pauses were part of the testimony too — she had learned that from ten years of courtrooms, where the hesitations around a hard fact told you its exact shape.

He named the payment. Fourteen pounds, delivered by a company man whose name he gave reluctantly, in a voice dropped so low she had to watch his lips to catch it. He named the gauge. He named the night Tomas Okonkwo didn't come up, and the morning they told the widows it was weather.

When she read it back to him, entire, as the form required, he listened with his eyes closed like a man hearing his own funeral sermon.

"I signed nothing yet," he said afterward, staring at the fair copy. "If I sign —"

"Then it exists," Odile said. "That's all signing does. It makes a thing exist outside your head, where other people can reach it."

"That's what frightens me."

"I know." She set the pen down in front of him, gently, the way you'd set down something that might go off. "Mr. Reyes. I'm going to tell you the thing nobody told me. You've already signed, in every way that matters. You said it out loud. To me, a stenographer of the admiralty court, in the annex itself. The pen is just the echo."

He looked at the pen for a long time.

Then he signed all six pages, dated in his own uncertain hand beneath her printed certification, and left without saying goodbye, and Odile sat alone in the lamplit gallery listening to her own heartbeat, thinking: *eleven days, three witnesses, and I have just spent a man's fear like coin.*

She hid the pages in 4502, between the warehouse figures, and went home to not sleep.

* * *

Silas came Sunday, after the annex had been closed for the Sabbath and the night watchman — a cousin of a cousin of Maren's kitchen, chosen for exactly that reason — let them into the transcription gallery for one hour.

They worked by one shaded lamp, and the work was accounting.

Silas laid out his memorized inventory: what he knew from the ledgers, which reports had been altered, which dates conflicted, the hand he believed matched Director Halloway's own on the struck-out warnings. He could produce none of it on paper yet — the originals were beyond reach, and copying them outright would be theft and discovery both — but he could state it, and Odile could take it down, and here they hit the wall they'd known was coming.

"Your knowledge isn't testimony," she said. "You're not a diver. You're the company's own clerk. Everything you know comes from documents, and documents have to exist to be entered."

"Then what am I?"

"You're a map," Odile said. "You tell me where everything is buried, and when the inquiry opens, someone produces the originals and I can stand up and say *page such-and-such, ledger such-and-such, compare the hands.* But Silas—" she used his first name for the first time, and let it stand a moment, "—the documents are the case. Your testimony is only the string that ties it. If we can't get the originals, or copies, or a witness inside the company who will swear to the alteration—"

"There's me," Silas said quietly.

"You're indentured. Anything you say against Halloway, they'll bury under breach charges before you finish the sentence. They'll call it the vengeance of a dishonest servant, and Crane will write twelve beautiful pages about your ingratitude."

"Then I'd better make sure the string holds anyway." He was looking at her shorthand notebook, lying closed on the desk, that small animal-track language that kept three men's courage folded inside finished paperwork upstairs. "How many statements do we have?"

"Three. Reyes makes three. Two more divers have agreed. And one—" she hesitated. "One is wavering. The one who took the money. He signs this week or he doesn't."

"And if he doesn't?"

"Then the chain has a missing link, and Crane will find it, because finding missing links is what he's *for*." She rubbed her eyes. Eleven days of double work — the company's record by day, the true one by night — had built a fine constant headache behind her left eye. "You should understand what you're asking of these men, Silas. A signature on my pages isn't like signing a petition. It's a man writing down, in his own hand, that he watched his partners drown for a pearl bed, and took money about it after. Some of them will choose the money's silence forever rather than sign that. I'd have taken the fourteen pounds myself, maybe. I won't hate anyone who does."

"No," Silas said. "Neither will I. That's rather the point, isn't it? The company runs on everyone needing their wages. It doesn't run on cruelty. Cruelty is just the discount rate."

Odile looked at him — this careful, gray, competent man who had spent a lifetime being underestimated by overseers and had somehow arrived at sentences like *the discount rate* — and felt the wary alliance of the breakwater settle into something firmer and less nameable.

"We'll need a notary eventually," she said. "Someone who can certify the signatures if the witnesses can't appear. Someone outside the annex."

"I know a woman at the harbor office," Silas said. "Diver's widow. Hates the company with a theological intensity."

"Good. Bring me her name. And Silas—" she tapped the closed notebook. "There's one more thing. The pages hide well, but they don't travel well. If I'm ever searched, everything dies in those folios. We need a second set, somewhere outside government walls entirely. Somewhere Crane would never think to look, because it has no official power at all."

"Where?"

"You tell me," Odile said. "You're the one with the found family. Where do dockworkers keep things that matter?"

Silas was quiet for a moment, and then something shifted in his face — the loneliness she had noticed at their first meeting, the competence worn like armor, easing just slightly at the joints.

"Maren keeps everything," he said. "Baptismal records for children born on the water. Marriage lines for couples the church wouldn't take. Fifty years of it, in a biscuit tin under her floor. Nobody's ever audited Maren's kitchen, because nobody important knows it exists." He almost smiled. "The unofficial archive. The one with no authority whatsoever."

"Perfect," Odile said. "Authority is the first place they look."

They copied what could be copied — Silas reading the fair English drafts aloud from memory-adjacent notes while she re-transcribed a duplicate set in fresh shorthand, dated and marked *second true copy* — and by the time the watchman coughed politely at the door, the future of the case existed twice: once asleep in the annex folios, once folded flat inside Silas's coat, bound for a biscuit tin beneath a kitchen floor in the dock quarter.

* * *

Monday morning, Aldous Crane sent a memorandum to the Records Hall requesting a complete inventory of all depositions relating to maritime fatalities in the past eighteen months, "for the purposes of the forthcoming official history," to be conducted by his assistants, with the stenographic staff present during inspection.

The memorandum reached Odile's desk at nine.

She read it twice, sipping her tea, and noted with detached professional admiration that Crane had done the arithmetic exactly right: not a raid, nothing crude — an *inventory*, polite, procedural, impossible to refuse, sweeping every shelf her pages slept on.

Her hands, she observed with interest, remained perfectly steady. Ten years of courtrooms had taught her that steadiness was not the absence of fear but the decision to keep writing anyway.

She finished her tea, drew a fresh sheet toward her, and began, in swift sure strokes, the morning's ordinary work — while upstairs, behind her, four thin pages lay folded in the gutters of finished folios, signed, dated, and waiting, like seeds in winter ground, for the season to turn.

It would turn within the week. She could feel it coming the way sailors feel weather — and one of the signatures in her keeping, the frightened one, the paid one, was about to be tested first.
