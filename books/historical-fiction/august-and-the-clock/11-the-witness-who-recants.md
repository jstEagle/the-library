## 11. The Witness Who Recants

The note came folded inside a routine shipping manifest, which was how Silas knew it meant trouble. Nobody at Halloway Trading Company wasted a good hiding place on anything innocent.

*Your diver has been to see us. He will not be going to see you again.*

No signature. No need for one. The handwriting was small and precise, the kind of hand that belonged to a man paid by the letter, and Silas recognized it from the margin annotations on a hundred company documents — the same clerk who initialed the wage deductions, the same clerk who had once, without comment, copied out a list of divers who had asked questions about the Okonkwo dive.

Silas read the note twice, then fed it into the stove in the counting room and watched the paper curl and go gray. Around him the clerks scratched at their columns, oblivious or pretending to be, and outside the window the harbor lay flat and silver under an October sky that could not decide whether to rain.

Emrys Caul. That was the diver. Sixty-one years old, lungs like wet paper after thirty years of breath-hold work, a widower with one daughter, Prudence, who kept the accounts desk at the coal yard the company leased on the north quay. Emrys had been Tomas Okonkwo's partner for eleven seasons. He'd been down in the water the day the rope signals stopped coming up. And he had been paid forty pounds — Silas had found the entry himself, buried under "discretionary hazard compensation" — to say afterward that the water was fine, that the depth markers were honest, that no man had ordered anything beyond what any diver would freely attempt.

For six weeks he had said nothing. Then Silas had come to his door with Maren's identification of the clan-mark still warm in his mind, and Emrys had listened, and wept once, briefly, into his hands, and then talked. He had given Odile two hours of testimony in her careful shorthand: the order passed down through the dive master, *past the safe line, the bed's rich this season*, the depth gauge he had personally checked and personally watched get replaced with a false one before the company's surveyor arrived, the money handed over in an envelope by a man whose face he would not describe but whose boots he would never forget.

Signed. Dated. Witnessed. One link in a chain that already felt too thin, and now someone had gone and told Silas, politely, in advance, that the link was about to break.

He finished the manifest, initialed it, and put it in the outgoing tray. His hands were steady. He noticed that about himself sometimes — the steadiness arriving precisely when it shouldn't, as if some part of him had learned long ago that a man who trembled over ledgers did not survive long in this building.

Then he went to find Emrys Caul.

* * *

He didn't go straight away. That was the first lesson of the docks: never arrive looking like you were expected. He worked until the noon bell, took his bread and cheese standing at the seawall where the gulls argued over fish heads, and walked the long way around through the net-menders' lanes, letting his feet take the routes that had no witnesses worth worrying about.

Emrys lived in a lean-to room above a chandlery on Sable Row, a street so narrow the laundry lines overhead nearly touched. Silas climbed the outside stair and knocked, and heard, before any answer came, the particular silence of a man sitting very still behind a door, deciding whether to exist.

"Mr. Caul," Silas said quietly. "It's Ravensworth. From the company."

Another silence. Then: "You shouldn't have come here."

"I know."

The door opened anyway. Old habit, maybe. Or loneliness. Emrys Caul looked smaller than he had six weeks ago, the way men did when they'd spent those weeks not sleeping. His room held a cot, a sea chest, a chair, and a cold stove, and he sat Silas down on the chest because there was nowhere else, and stood by the window with his back half-turned, as if even now he couldn't bear to be looked at while he said what he'd come to say.

"They came to the coal yard Tuesday," Emrys said. "Two of them. Not rough men. Worse than rough men — polite ones. The kind that smile." He rubbed at his forearm, an old diver's gesture, working at something that wasn't there anymore. "They knew my daughter's name. They knew her wages. They knew she'd just been moved to the front desk, the one with the better light." He laughed, a dry sound with nothing in it. "Front desk. You know what that means at Halloway's? It means they finally decided she was worth watching. Worth keeping. Or worth taking away, whichever suits them come spring."

"They threatened her job."

"They didn't threaten anything." Emrys turned, and his eyes were red-rimmed and terribly clear. "That's the trick of it, lad. They offered. Said a man of my years shouldn't be troubled with legal matters, that memories get confused, that it would be a kindness to everyone — Prudence especially — if I set the record straight before some lawyer got hold of me and made me say things I'd regret. Kindness. Forty years I've known that word from that company, and every time it's had teeth in it."

"What did you tell them?"

"I told them I was tired." He said it simply, without excuse. "I told them I'd sign whatever they liked."

Silas felt it land somewhere under his ribs — not surprise, because he'd read the note, but hope dying officially, the way it only died when spoken aloud by the man carrying it. "And your testimony to Miss Marchetti—"

"Gone. I asked her for it back. She wouldn't give me the pages — clever woman, I'll say that — but she struck them from her chain, I saw her do it. Marked them withdrawn, dated it, witnessed it herself. By the book, so nobody can say she held a man's words against his will." He shook his head slowly. "She looked at me the whole time like she wanted to shake me and feed me both at once."

"Are you all right?" It was an absurd question and Silas knew it, but it came out anyway, because the old man looked like a hull with its seams opening.

"I'm alive," Emrys said. "That's more than Tom Okonkwo is. That's more than his brothers are. My girl keeps her desk and her light and her little room on Cooper Street, and next summer maybe she marries that ferryman she's sweet on, and none of it happens if I keep my mouth shut." He crossed to the stove and laid his palm flat against the dead iron, as if testing for a warmth that wasn't coming. "You want me to be sorry, boy. I can hear it in how quiet you're being. But I dived twenty-nine years and I learned one thing down there: you don't blame a man for surfacing."

"I don't," Silas said. And found, examining himself the way he examined a ledger line, checking twice for error, that it was true. He was angry — angry at the company, at the polite smiling men, at the whole machinery that made courage cost exactly what a poor man couldn't pay — but not at Emrys. Not really. What kind of fool would hate a drowning man for letting go of the rope?

What he hated was that the rope had been cut on purpose.

"You could leave," Silas said. "You and your daughter. There are ships—"

"To where?" Emrys smiled, and it was almost gentle. "I'm sixty-one. My chest sounds like a bellows with a split in it. And she'd spend her life looking over her shoulder in some foreign port, knowing why. No, lad. This is the trade. This is always the trade. Somebody stays behind and keeps breathing so somebody else can eat." He sat down heavily on the cot. "Go on, then. You've got others. Two, I heard. That's thin rope you're holding."

"It holds if it's tied right."

"Tied right." Emrys nodded at some private joke with the sea. "That's what Tom used to say about knots. Tied right, a bad knot holds; tied wrong, a good one slips." He looked up, and for a moment the diver surfaced through the frightened old man, clear-eyed and hard. "Listen to me. I can't testify. Understand that — I won't, don't ask again, don't waste your visits. But I haven't forgotten what happened down there, and I never will, and neither will the water. If it ever comes to it — if it comes to a courtroom and they drag me in and swear me — I won't lie under oath. I'll say I don't recall. I'll say it till they're sick of hearing it. But I won't lie. That much I've got left to give you, and it's not nothing."

It wasn't nothing. Silas understood exactly what it was: a man keeping one small sealed compartment dry inside a flooded hull. He stood, and put out his hand, and after a moment Emrys took it, his grip still a diver's grip, strong past all reason.

"I'm sorry," Emrys said as Silas reached the door. "Not for choosing my girl. For needing to choose at all."

Silas went down the outside stair into the gray afternoon and did not look back.

* * *

Odile was waiting in the reading room of the Athenaeum Library, at the corner table by the atlas cases, where two people could talk under cover of a spread-out map and the librarian's deafness. She had her stenography folio open in front of her, blank pages showing, and her pen capped, which Silas had learned meant she had nothing safe to write and everything unsafe to say.

"Emrys came to me Thursday night," she said, without greeting. "Brought the envelope back. All forty pounds. In coin. Can you imagine — he'd never spent a shilling of it. It sat in his sea chest like a stone." Her jaw tightened. "I wouldn't take it. Told him to buy Prudence something warm for winter. He cried. I pretended I hadn't seen, because a stenographer sees nothing, that's the whole profession."

"So we're down to two."

"We're down to two signed testimonies, plus whatever Maren knows firsthand from before the dive, plus your memory of the altered report — which is worthless in court without the page itself." She turned the atlas toward him, opened to some chart of southern waters, and tapped a coastline with one finger as though discussing currents. "Crane's people know we're down to two. That note you got — they wanted you to know. Fear travels faster when it's delivered."

"They're pruning us," Silas said. "One branch at a time. Quietly enough that each man convinces himself it's his own decision."

"And it *is* his own decision. That's the genius of it. They never force anyone. They just arrange the world so that silence is the only affordable thing." Odile closed the atlas with a soft slap. "I've been doing this job nine years, Silas. I transcribe lies for a living. Do you know what I've learned about them? A lie doesn't need to be believed forever. It only needs to survive until the inquiry closes and the record seals. After that, truth can shout all it likes — it's shouting at a locked book."

"Then we don't let the book seal."

"No," she agreed. "We don't." She looked at him across the table, and beneath the stenographer's composure he saw the same arithmetic running that ran in him: two witnesses, one forged report, one director's safe, and a clock ticking toward the admiralty inquiry with every clerk in Vellamare writing Crane's polished history into the official record page by page. "But understand what we're holding. If either of the remaining two breaks — and they'll be approached next, you know that as well as I do — we have nothing but your word against a director's handwriting and a celebrated historian's printed proof. Your word, from an indentured clerk. Which the examiner will weigh at approximately nothing."

"I know."

"And yet you're sitting there calm as Sunday."

Silas looked out the tall window at the harbor, at the masts swaying slightly against the pale sky, at the pearl barges riding low and heavy on their moorings. Somewhere under that water were three men the ledger called weather losses. Somewhere above it was a baby named August, learning to laugh in Maren's kitchen, wrapped at night in cloth bearing a mark the company had erased from every record it controlled.

"I'm not calm," he said. "I'm counting."

"Counting what?"

"Options." He turned back to her and lowered his voice below the librarian's range, below the map cases, below the world. "Because there's one document that beats every witness they can buy, and one witness they can't touch at all. The original safety report — the one with the depth warnings struck out in Director Halloway's own hand — is in his private safe. And paper doesn't recant."

Odile went very still. "Silas. Tell me you're not thinking what I think you're thinking."

"I'm thinking," he said, "that we should stop talking here."

Outside, the first rain of October came slanting off the sea, and in the counting rooms of the Halloway Trading Company the clerks bent over their clean columns, writing the year down exactly as they'd been told to.
