## 12. Crane's Draft

The summons came on a Tuesday, in the form of a cream-colored card slid under Odile's office door with the morning post.

*Mr. Aldous Crane requests the services of a stenographer of proven discretion for transcription work of considerable delicacy. Remuneration at double rate. Inquire at the Grand Meridian Hotel, Suite 4.*

She read it three times. *Proven discretion.* She knew what that meant in Halloway's vocabulary: someone who had sat through four years of depositions and never once raised her hand to object, even when the objections stacked up like cordwood. Someone who had learned to keep her face as blank as fresh paper.

Someone like her.

She should have refused. Every instinct she'd sharpened over four years in that deposition room told her that whatever Aldous Crane was writing, it was being written for one purpose only — and that purpose wore Director Halloway's face. Double rate was not payment; double rate was a leash made comfortable.

She went anyway.

Not because she wanted to see the manuscript. Because she needed to know exactly what shape the lie would take before the admiralty inquiry convened, and there was no better way to learn a weapon than to hold it in your own hands.

* * *

Aldous Crane received her in Suite 4 of the Grand Meridian with the ease of a man who had never once been kept waiting by anyone who mattered. He was smaller than his reputation — a compact, silver-haired figure in a dove-gray waistcoat — but his hands were remarkable: long, quick, always in motion, as if they were conducting an orchestra only he could hear.

"Mademoiselle Marchetti." He pronounced her name correctly, which unsettled her more than any flattery could have. "I've read your work."

"My work is verbatim transcript, Mr. Crane. It isn't read. It's filed."

"Everything is read by someone," Crane said, smiling. "That is rather the whole of my profession. Sit, please. You'll find the draft in the folio on the desk. I type poorly and write worse — my hand is a scandal my publishers have forgiven for thirty years out of loyalty. I need clean pages. Two copies. And I need them quickly; the inquiry opens in eleven days, and my history must be available to the commissioners beforehand. A record should precede judgment, don't you think? Otherwise people judge first and consult the record only when it agrees with them."

He said it lightly, as if it were an epigram, something to be quoted later. Odile recognized the technique. Crane built sentences like masons build arches — each stone placed so that removing any single one brought the whole thing down on your head. You couldn't argue with him without seeming to argue with reason itself.

"I'll need the raw material as well," she said. "Depositions, logs, correspondence. A stenographer can't transcribe accurately against nothing."

Something flickered behind his eyes — approval, or its close cousin, appraisal. "Naturally. I've had the company's archive assembled in the adjoining room. Mr. Ravensworth has been most cooperative in cataloguing it."

Silas. The name landed in her chest like a dropped coin. So Crane had already been inside the archive. Already been walked through it by the man who knew every shelf. Either Silas was playing a very deep game, or Crane believed he had won him — promotion, freedom, whatever price had been named. She thought of the way Silas had stood in the records room, careful as a man crossing ice, and decided she would not know which until she saw him again. If she saw him again.

"Very good," she said. "When do you want the first pages?"

"Yesterday, ideally. Today will do." He poured her coffee himself, unasked, and she noted that too — the charm deployed like a tool from a kit, precise and impersonal. "One condition, Mademoiselle. You transcribe what is written. Nothing more."

"Of course." Her voice was smooth. Inside, something cold and steady began to turn, like a lock taking a key.

* * *

Crane's manuscript ran to two hundred and forty pages in his scandalous handwriting, and Odile read all of it before typing a word, because she had learned long ago that you cannot transcribe honestly unless you first understand dishonestly — you must know where the author wants you to look so you can see what he wants you to miss.

The title was elegant. *The Pearl Coast: A History of Enterprise and Endeavor in Vellamare.* The prose was beautiful. That was the horror of it. She had expected clumsy propaganda, some clerk's crude whitewash. Instead she found a work of genuine artistry — measured, humane, generous even, full of sympathy for the hardships of divers and clerks alike, so reasonable in tone that disagreement felt like bad manners.

And threaded through the beauty, like hairline cracks through fine porcelain, were the lies.

Chapter fourteen concerned the spring diving season. She found the passage on the third page:

*"The tragedy of April last, in which three divers of the Okonkwo family perished, has been the subject of much sorrowful talk along the waterfront. It must be stated plainly: the waters in question were certified safe by the Company's surveyors, and the depths worked were well within established practice. What cannot be certified is judgment. The Okonkwos were known along the coast as men of exceptional ambition — divers who had twice exceeded prudent limits in pursuit of exceptional beds, and who on this occasion, regrettably, exceeded them once too often.*

*"It is a melancholy truth that the maritime peoples of this coast carry ancient superstitions regarding depth — beliefs in 'breath-curses' and 'the sea's jealousy' — which no amount of sound engineering has ever dislodged. Where a surveyor reads fathoms, the old divers read omens. The Company honors these traditions with respect; but respect is not evidence, and the inquiry owes the dead a truer accounting than folklore provides."*

Odile set down the page.

For a moment she simply breathed. Then she took out her own notebook — the small black one she kept for private thoughts, the one that never entered a deposition record — and wrote, in her personal shorthand, which no typist in Vellamare but herself could read:

*He makes them liars. He makes them reckless. He makes their mothers superstitious. And he does it in language so kind you'd thank him for it.*

Then, because fury without method was just noise, she did what she did best. She worked.

* * *

The raw materials were in the adjoining room, and she spent the afternoon among them with the manuscript open beside her like a prosecutor's brief run in reverse. Here was the surveyor's certification for the April grounds — signed, dated, thorough. Here were the depth logs, kept by the boat master, entries in a blunt seaman's hand.

And here — on page ninety-one of the log book, the entry for April 17th — she found what she had half hoped she would not find.

The line read: *Ordered down 40 ft. New bed charted by Co. survey. All lines out.*

Forty feet. Below the certified safe depth of twenty-five. Ordered — not chosen. Ordered, with the company's own abbreviation stamped after it, the same stamp that appeared on requisitions and wage dockets throughout the ledger system Silas had taught her to read, back when their alliance was still a pair of strangers circling each other in a records room.

Crane's manuscript said the depths were "well within established practice." The log said forty feet, ordered, on a bed the company itself had newly charted. Crane said the Okonkwos exceeded prudent limits. The log said the company had handed them the rope and counted the knots.

Her pen moved fast now.

*Discrepancy one:* Manuscript claims certified safe waters, "depths within practice." Log, April 17: forty feet, company-ordered. Certification covers twenty-five.

*Discrepancy two:* Manuscript calls the new bed a matter of rumor ("beds spoken of in taverns"). Survey chart in archive is dated six weeks *before* the dive — the company had planned it, mapped it, and sent men down to it.

*Discrepancy three:* Manuscript states the Okonkwos "had twice exceeded prudent limits" previously. She pulled the prior seasons' logs. Both earlier dives logged at nineteen and twenty-two feet. Within bounds. Crane had invented a pattern of recklessness out of whole cloth — or out of someone's memorandum — because a dead man's character is the easiest thing in the world to alter, requiring no forgery at all, only adjectives.

*Discrepancy four,* and this one made her hands go still: the wages. The final wage entry for Tomas Okonkwo showed a supplemental payment, initialed by an overseer, annotated in the margin: *hazard supplement, deep bed, per directive.* Hazard supplement. The company had paid them extra to go where it swore afterward they had gone on their own foolishness. They had died for a bonus, and the bonus was now the proof.

She sat back in the chair. The afternoon light came through the hotel windows thick and golden, gilding the dust motes, making everything look warm and settled and safe, and she understood suddenly why men like Crane existed — because the world desperately wanted to look like this room looked, golden and orderly, and was willing to pay double rates to anyone who could make it so.

Cold fury is a strange thing. Hot anger shouts, throws things, gets itself arrested in taverns and remembered badly. Cold fury sits very still in a wicker chair in a sunlit suite and writes in shorthand no one else can read, and it does not stop, and it does not forgive, and it keeps perfect minutes.

She filled nine pages of the black notebook before evening.

* * *

She typed for three days.

It was, she admitted to herself, the finest thing she had ever transcribed — every sentence polished, every transition seamless, the whole narrative moving with the terrible confidence of a verdict delivered before the trial. By the second day she could have recited passages in her sleep. By the third day she had stopped hating the prose and begun to study it, the way a locksmith studies a rival's pick. If she knew how the lie was built, she knew how it would come apart.

Because here was the thing about Aldous Crane's history, the thing its author had perhaps grown too celebrated to remember: it was built entirely on assertion. There was not a single document cited. Not one log, not one deposition, not one signature. The commissioners would receive two hundred and forty gorgeous pages and a footnote reading *from the Company's records, consulted in confidence.* Confidence. The word was doing more work than any diver on that boat ever had.

A history with no sources is a costume. And costumes tear.

On the third evening, Crane reviewed her typed pages with a gold pencil, marking nothing, nodding at intervals like a man listening to music he already knew. "Excellent," he said at last. "Clean, faithful, discreet. You have a gift, Mademoiselle Marchetti. Have you considered broader employment? A mind like yours is wasted on depositions."

"I find depositions honest work."

"Do you?" He smiled, and for the first time the smile had edges. "Four years of them, I'm told. One develops — habits. Ways of hearing. Tell me, when a witness says a thing twice in identical words across two years and two hundred miles, do you note it?" 

The room went very quiet. Outside, a gull cried over the harbor.

"I note what's on the page, Mr. Crane."

"Mm." He tapped the manuscript with the pencil, gently, twice. "So do I. It's why I'm careful what goes onto it." He rose, signaling the audience was over, and pressed an envelope into her hand — heavier than double rate warranted. "The remaining chapters Thursday. Good night."

She walked out through the marble corridor of the Grand Meridian with her spine straight and the envelope unopened in her glove, and she did not let her hands shake until she reached the street.

* * *

She found Silas where she had told him she might be found — the fish market at closing, by the ice stalls, where conversations between a stenographer buying supper and a company clerk checking invoices attracted less attention than anywhere else in Vellamare. He was pretending to examine mackerel with scholarly intensity.

"He offered me the manuscript," she said quietly. "Two hundred and forty pages. The Okonkwos drowned themselves, Silas. Ambition and superstition. The company certified safe waters. There isn't one citation in the entire text — not one log, not one name of a witness. Just 'records consulted in confidence.'"

Silas set down the fish. His face did the thing it always did, the careful flattening, but underneath it she could see the machinery turning. "He's had the archive. I catalogued it for him myself." A muscle moved in his jaw. "I gave him the shelves. I didn't give him the story."

"No. But he's built one anyway, and it's good, Silas. That's what frightens me. If those commissioners read his book first, the inquiry is over before it starts. People believe what they read at breakfast."

"Then we make sure they read something else first." He glanced at the ice, at the melting water running dark off the table into the drain — everything in this city, she thought, eventually flowing toward the sea. "You copied the discrepancies?"

She touched her bag, where the black notebook lay against her hip like a second heartbeat. "Nine pages. The forty-foot order. The hazard supplement. The invented recklessness. Dated, cross-referenced, in shorthand."

"Good." For the first time since she'd known him, Silas Ravensworth smiled without guarding it, and it changed his whole face — made him briefly young, briefly someone who had not been raised to be invisible. "Keep typing his book, Odile. Every chapter. Be indispensable."

"And then?"

His eyes went past her, toward the harbor lights coming on one by one across the darkening water.

"Then on the day the inquiry opens, we show the commissioners what the confidence was covering."
