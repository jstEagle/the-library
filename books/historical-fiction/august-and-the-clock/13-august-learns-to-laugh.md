## 13. August Learns to Laugh

Maren Okafor had buried two husbands, one son, and forty-one years of diving beneath the gray water of Vellamare harbor, and she had learned this: grief does not leave a body. It settles in the joints like salt. You learn to move with it, the way an old diver moves with the current, and after enough years you can no longer tell where the ache ends and you begin.

So when the baby laughed for the first time on a Tuesday morning in late September — a sudden, startling bark of delight, both fists jammed in her mouth, her whole small body shaking with it — Maren felt something shift loose in her own chest, some calcified thing cracking like ice in a thaw.

She was mending nets outside the net-loft when it happened, August propped against her knee on a folded sailcloth. The cause of the laughter was nothing. A gull, probably. Or Josiah Cobb's hat blowing off into the water for the third time that week, which had made even the crane operators stop and laugh. But August threw back her head and *laughed*, loud and unembarrassed, the way only babies and drunks and the very old manage, and Maren heard the sound travel down the dock like a bell rung in fog.

By noon, everyone knew. By evening, it was all anyone talked about.

"She's got Okonkwo lungs," said Tomas's old cousin Berta, who had come up from the sardine sheds to see. "His mother laughed just like that. Whole village could hear her."

They did not say *the dead woman's name*. They never did, not yet. Amara Okonkwo had been dead eight months and they still spoke of her the way sailors speak of men lost overboard — in circumlocution, in gesture, in the direction a silence points. But Berta had said *his mother*, and Maren had watched the word land on the gathering dockworkers like a small warm stone, and she thought: *not yet, but soon.* The baby would pull their grief out of them the way the tide pulls things out of sand. She was already doing it.

Maren looked down at August, who had tired of laughing and was now studying her own toes with the fierce concentration of a scholar confronting a difficult text.

"You," Maren told her quietly, "are going to be trouble. The good kind."

August grabbed Maren's thumb and held on with astonishing strength, and did not let go for a long time.

* * *

The rhythms of the dock had reorganized themselves around the child the way a river reorganizes around a new stone.

In the mornings, before the first shift went out, someone always brought her to the loft steps so the crews could see her before they saw the water. It was superstition dressed as sentiment, and everyone knew it: divers were a people who touched what they loved before touching the deep. Silas had started it without meaning to — carrying her out in the grey dawn, holding her up like an offering — and now it was law though no one had written it down. Old Ferro, who hadn't dived since his lungs burst in '84, sang to her in a language none of the young ones knew. The fish-gutters timed their shouting contests to her nap schedule and failed, every day, to keep quiet, because her waking cry had become the dock's second bell.

Even the company men noticed. Two clerks from Halloway's counting-house came down on a Thursday to audit the salvage ledgers, and one of them, a pale boy with ink-stained cuffs, asked whose child she was.

"Everybody's," said Maren. "Nobody's. Take your pick."

The clerk looked at her a long moment, and Maren saw him do the arithmetic — the swaddling cloth had been seen, the note had been whispered about, the dates did not escape a man who worked with numbers — and then he closed his ledger book and said only, "She's fat. Healthy." And he went back up the hill and reported nothing, or everything, or exactly as much as he chose to, and that was the dock's whole strategy in miniature: hide the truth in plain sight among people who owed each other everything.

Maren had raised children on this dock who were not hers by blood — a drowned Spaniard's boys, a runaway from the inland farms, and Silas himself, who had come to her at eleven years old with his father's knife and his father's silence and nothing else. She knew how a community absorbs a child. What surprised her was how completely the child absorbed the community. August did not merely have caretakers; she had *witnesses*. Every meal she ate was observed and narrated. Every new tooth was announced at the union meeting. When she rolled over for the first time — from belly to back, which Berta declared was the Okonkwo way, "they never could stand being face-down, even the babies" — three separate people claimed credit, and the argument lasted four days and involved a wager that Ferro won unfairly.

The company had erased thirty names from its rolls in twenty years. The dock answered by making one name impossible to forget.

* * *

Silas came less often than Maren wanted and more often than was safe, and he came wrong — she could see it, everyone could see it, though nobody but her would say it.

He arrived after dark, always. He sat near the door, always. He ate what was put in front of him, thanked whoever had cooked it, played his part in the ritual of holding August — she demanded it of him now, reaching for his collar with both hands, insulted by refusal — and then, at some internal bell only he could hear, he rose, and thanked them again, and went back up the hill to his cold room above the counting-house, alone.

"He sits like a man waiting to be arrested," Ferro said one night after Silas left. "In his own family's kitchen."

"He is waiting to be arrested," Maren said. "Just not today."

But it was more than fear, and she knew that too. She had watched Silas Ravensworth since he was a wet-eared boy doing sums on the pier for pennies, and she knew the architecture of him: a house built with the doors bricked over. His competence was a wall. His politeness was a wall. Even his loyalty was a wall — he loved them all fiercely and let none of them carry anything, because carrying was the only way he knew to be worth keeping.

And now look at what he carried. An altered report memorized page by page. A recanting witness and the shame of understanding why. A stenographer's shorthand pages and the knowledge of what happened to women who crossed Halloway. A baby's face he had been ordered to forget. He carried it all alone, in the dark, up and down the hill, and he called this *protecting* them.

It came to a head on a Sunday, in the rain, in the back room of the net loft where the smell of tar and hemp could drown any sorrow if you breathed deeply enough.

Silas had come in daylight for once — rare enough that Maren marked it — because Odile Marchetti had sent word that the transcriptions were finished and witnessed, and there was a chain-of-custody question that needed his signature. He sat at the trestle table with the folios in front of him, reading each page twice, initialing in his small precise hand, and August slept in a hammock slung between two nails overhead, swaying slightly, and the rain came down on the roof like applause.

Maren watched him work. She watched him check the door latch without seeming to. She watched him flinch — a small flinch, quickly mastered — when Ferro dropped a crate outside.

And when the last page was signed and Odile had gone out into the rain with the folios sewn into the lining of her coat, Maren sat down across from him and put both her hands flat on the table between them, palms down, the way she used to when her sons came home with wounds they were pretending weren't there.

"Tell me the plan," she said.

"There isn't—"

"Don't." Her voice stayed level. Forty years of commanding dive crews had given her many registers, and this was the one that stopped men mid-lie. "You think because I'm old and my chest is bad, I don't see it. You're building a case the way you build everything — silently, alone, so that if it collapses, it lands only on you. That's not a plan, Silas. That's a funeral with extra steps."

"Maren—"

"When your father died," she said, "you were eleven. And you decided, right there on the pier, with his knife in your hand, that love was a debt you had to pay back by never needing anyone again. And we let you. All these years, we let you, because we thought time would do what we couldn't. Well, time has done what it does. Now there's a child upstairs who laughs like her grandmother, and a court coming, and a company that erases people, and you are standing in the middle of it trying to be the only wall between them and us." She leaned forward. "You were never meant to carry this alone. None of it. Not the grief, not the risk, not the knowing. That's what the company understands that you don't, Silas — that a man alone is cheap to bury. It's the only economy they've ever practiced."

Silas looked at his hands. For a long moment the only sounds were the rain and, above them, the soft creak of the hammock and the small wet snuffle of a sleeping child.

"I watched Tomas recant," Silas said finally, very quietly. "I watched him choose his daughter. And I understood it, Maren. Completely. And then I walked out of that room and realized I was asking every witness to do what I've been too afraid to do myself — stand up and be counted, pay the price in the open." His jaw worked. "I keep thinking there's a version where nobody pays. There isn't. So I've been trying to arrange it so the only one who pays is me."

"And we've been letting you," Maren said. "Because it's easier. Grief makes cowards of the old, same as it makes martyrs of the young." She reached across the table and took his hand — his ink-stained, careful, clerk's hand that had once been a boy's hand gripping a dead man's knife. "But here is what I know, and I know it from forty years of water: the sea takes the solitary diver first. Always. Not because he's weakest. Because he's got no line to the surface."

Silas didn't answer. But he didn't take his hand back either, and Maren had raised enough stubborn men to know that this was surrender, or the beginning of one.

Above them, August woke — not crying, just awake, surveying the world with grave suspicion until she located Silas's face, at which point she produced a string of syllables that were not words yet but were unmistakably *addressed to him*, an opinion, delivered firmly, requiring a response.

Silas stood and lifted her out of the hammock, and the child pressed her face into his shoulder with the total trust of someone who has never been dropped, and Maren watched the last of the walls come down — not all at once, walls never do, but a brick loosening, a crack running through the mortar.

"We'll need the fishermen," Silas said, into August's hair. "For the inquiry. Boats, alibis, eyes on the company wharf. And Ferro knows the harbormaster's clerks. And you—" he turned, and his voice cracked slightly, embarrassingly, and he let it, "—I'll need you. If it goes wrong. For her."

"You had that already," Maren said. "You had all of it already. You only had to ask."

* * *

Later that night, after Silas had gone — later, this time, than usual, because he'd stayed for supper like a man and argued politics with Ferro like family — Maren sat by the stove with August asleep against her shoulder, feeling the small weight of her breathing, and let herself think about the days ahead instead of only guarding against them.

There would be an inquiry. Crane's polished lie was already being bound in leather somewhere. Warrants would come, eventually — she had lived long enough to smell a noose being braided, and Silas's recent boldness smelled exactly like that. There were hard waters ahead, and the company had proven, eight months ago, what it did with people it considered ballast.

But the child laughed now. That was new since the spring. In April, August had been a bundle of needs and silences; in September she laughed at falling hats, and the whole dock rearranged itself around the sound like iron filings around a magnet. Tenderness, Maren thought, was not the opposite of the fight. It was the fuel of it. You did not fight hardest for abstractions. You fought for a specific weight against your shoulder, a specific laugh, a specific name that would be carved somewhere someday or it would not.

Amara Okonkwo had laughed like that. Maren had heard her, across twenty years and a hundred harbor mornings. The sea had taken her and her husband and her brother, and the company had written *weather losses* in a clerk's hand, and that might have been the end of it — another erasure, smooth and permanent as water closing.

Except for the weight against Maren's shoulder, breathing.

August stirred, sighed, and gripped a fistful of Maren's shirt with the ferocity of someone holding a line to the surface.

Outside, boots on the wet planks — quick, uneven, urgent. A knock, hard and low, at the loft door, and Ferro's voice pitched wrong beneath it:

"Maren. Open up. There's a man from the admiralty office asking after the child — and he's already got Silas's name."
