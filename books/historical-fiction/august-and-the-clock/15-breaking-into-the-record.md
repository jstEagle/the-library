## 15. Breaking Into the Record

**SILAS**

The invitation arrived at the dock house in gold ink on cream stock, which meant Director Halloway wanted witnesses, not guests. *The Halloway Trading Company requests the pleasure of your company at a banquet commemorating twenty-five years of faithful enterprise.* Faithful enterprise. Somewhere in that phrase were three dead divers, folded flat like pressed flowers.

My name had been added by hand at the bottom: *Mr. S. Ravensworth, Keeper of Records.* A title I'd never been given before and understood immediately. They weren't inviting me to feed me. They were inviting me so that whatever happened tomorrow — whatever forged report appeared in the archive, whatever charge was laid against me — would happen in front of two hundred people who could swear they'd seen me drinking wine and smiling.

So I would go. And I would smile. And while they watched me smile, Odile Marchetti would be elsewhere in that house doing something far more dangerous than anything I'd planned.

We met one last time at noon, in the back of the net-mender's shed where Maren kept her winter twine. Odile stood with her back to the light, and I noticed she'd dressed plainly — no stenographer's neat collar tonight. She looked like a woman who delivered laundry.

"The banquet starts at eight," she said. "The speeches start at nine. Crane gives the address — twenty minutes, maybe thirty, he'll enjoy himself too much to be brief."

"That's my window."

"That's your window if my diversion works." She turned a spool of twine over in her hands as if it interested her. "The kitchen takes delivery of the wines at six. I've made arrangements for one case of the Madeira to be, shall we say, warmer than intended. By nine o'clock the cellar steward will be ill, loudly, in front of the catering staff, and there will be a dispute about who is responsible, and disputes travel upward in houses like that. The butler will go down to settle it. The footmen will follow. The service corridor to the director's study will be empty for as long as the shouting lasts."

"And if it doesn't last?"

"Then you come out the way you went in, empty-handed, and we're both ruined by Thursday." She said it without drama, the way she read testimony back into the record. "Silas. The plate."

I took the camera plate from its wrapping — borrowed from Herr Vogel, who photographed ship launches and asked no questions because he owed the docks more than he could ever repay. Twelve exposures, glass plates, each one a small rectangle of held breath. I'd practiced loading it blind, in the dark of my room, until my fingers could do it dreaming.

"Twelve shots," I said. "The original report is four pages. I'll shoot every page twice, then the ledger entry, then the register of the safe itself. If they've already swapped it—" I stopped. I wouldn't think that. "If it's there, we have it."

"If it's not there," Odile said, "we know they knew, and we change everything." She looked at me then, fully, and I saw what I'd seen the first day in the records office: a woman counting costs and paying them anyway. "Wear the white gloves," she said. "Nobody stops a man in company livery carrying gloves. They assume he's about to touch silver."

* * *

**ODILE**

I had transcribed eleven thousand pages of testimony in my career, and every page had taught me the same lesson: the truth does not fail because it is weak. It fails because someone gets tired, or frightened, or bought, at precisely the moment standing firm would have sufficed.

So I did not permit myself to be tired or frightened. Not at seven o'clock, when I entered the Halloway house through the tradesmen's entrance wearing the grey dress of a woman from the Marconi catering firm, carrying a crate of oysters whose provenance I had invented that afternoon. Not at half past eight, when I passed Aldous Crane in the corridor and he smiled his historian's smile at me — a man seeing a servant, nothing more, a woman beneath the dignity of his attention. I even dropped a small curtsy. It cost me something to do it. I decided to consider the cost an investment.

By nine the banquet hall glowed like the inside of a lantern. Two hundred candles, two hundred guests, and Halloway at the head table with his gray beard and his gray conscience, raising his glass to *faithful enterprise* while the word *Okonkwo* sat in my chest like a swallowed coal.

Crane rose to speak. His voice was exactly what I expected: warm, measured, built for lecture halls. He had the room inside a minute. He spoke of the sea as a partner, of the company as a family, of progress as a tide that asks sacrifices of those who ride it. Beautiful sentences. Every one of them a lie with good posture.

At nine-ten, the Madeira did its work.

The sound came up through the floor first — a raised voice, then breaking glass, then a woman's shriek from somewhere below. The cellar steward, I was told later, had performed his illness magnificently; there had been genuine dispute about responsibility, and it traveled upward exactly as disputes do. The butler left the hall at nine-fourteen. Three footmen followed. The corridor beside the director's study emptied.

Which meant that at nine-fourteen I needed every eye in that hall to remain exactly where they were.

I had chosen my moment the way I chose words in shorthand — nothing wasted. At nine-fifteen, as Crane reached the crescendo of his peroration — *"and history, ladies and gentlemen, will remember this house kindly"* — a tray of champagne flutes came off the edge of the serving table behind me.

I did not drop it. That distinction matters, and I have maintained it ever since, even under oath. The tray was nudged — by the press of the crowd, by fate, by the general unkindness of objects near edges — and two hundred crystal voices sang at once, and the crash was enormous, and every head in Vellamare's most powerful room turned toward the sound.

Toward me.

"Clumsy girl," said the magistrate's wife, and I flushed and stammered and knelt among the shards, cutting my thumb lightly for authenticity, apologizing to everyone within earshot. A footman hurried over — the last one, I noted with a lurch, the very last one between Silas and discovery — and together we swept up glass while Crane resumed, slightly annoyed, slightly diminished, and entirely unaware that history was being remembered at that very moment one floor below him, by lamplight, in silence.

* * *

**SILAS**

Halloway's study smelled of cigar smoke and leather polish. I gave myself ten seconds inside the door simply to breathe, because panic makes men fast and fast makes them stupid, and I could not afford either.

The safe sat behind the desk where Odile's floor plan said it would: a Chubb, forty years old, older than me. Old Chubbs had a temperament. They liked patience. My father used to say the sea rewards the diver who moves slowest, and I had spent my whole life learning what he meant.

The white gloves went on. I knelt. I put my ear to the cold steel door the way a doctor puts a stethoscope to a chest, and I began to listen.

First number. The wheel turned under my gloved fingers with a whisper of oiled metal. Too fast — back off. There. The faintest catch, a tooth brushing a tooth, like a heartbeat skipping. Twenty-two.

Second number. My own pulse interfered; I slowed my breathing until it barely existed. Somewhere above me, Crane was telling two hundred people that history would remember this house kindly. Let him talk. History was down here, kneeling on a Turkish rug, listening to a lock.

Forty-one. The catch again, softer.

Third number. This was the one I'd feared, because third numbers on old Chubbs could drift — decades of settling, a house that leaned slightly toward the harbor — and drift meant the number written down years ago might not be the number living in the mechanism now. Seven. Nothing. Six. Nothing. Five —

The tumblers fell into one another like a decision being made, and the handle turned, and the door swung open onto darkness.

Inside: papers. Deeds. A strongbox of coin. A leather folder stamped *INQUIRY — PREPARED MATERIALS.* And beneath it, thin and alone, a single sheet in a different hand, older, creased once down the middle.

I drew it out with both hands, reverent as a man handling relic bones. Even before the lamp found it I knew — by the weight of the paper, by the water stain in the corner — that it was the original. The true safety report. And there, in the margin, in ink I'd memorized from a hundred requisition slips: *Depths confirmed hazardous beyond equipment rating. Recommend refusal.* Struck through in Director Halloway's imperious hand, and beside the strike, the certification stamp applied like a signature on a death warrant.

Four pages. I shot each one twice, the magnesium flash hooded by the shield Vogel had rigged, each small pop of light feeling loud as cannon fire. Then the ledger entry. Then the open safe itself, its contents arranged as found. Eleven exposures. One plate remaining.

I hesitated only a moment. Then I photographed the strike-through close, filling the entire frame with Halloway's handwriting, so that no court in the empire could claim ambiguity about whose hand had crossed out the warning.

The pages went back exactly as they lay. The safe door closed. The wheel spun to zero.

And then, upstairs, the music stopped.

* * *

**ODILE**

The music stopping was not part of any plan.

I learned afterward that a guest — a shipping magnate with more money than sense — had demanded a song from the quartet that wasn't on their stands, and in the confusion of rearranging the musicians, the household's attention had scattered like startled birds. The butler returned early. The footmen returned. And a junior clerk named Pell, passing along the upper corridor on an errand, saw a sliver of lamplight under the director's study door where no lamp should have been burning.

I did not learn any of this until later. What I knew at the time was simpler and worse: footsteps on the stairs. Many feet. Moving with purpose.

I dropped the last shard of glass into the sweep pan and stood very still, listening past Crane's voice, past the clink of glasses, to the architecture of the house. Silas had perhaps ninety seconds. Perhaps less.

There was only one way to buy him more, and I took it, because I had already done the arithmetic and discovered I was willing.

I fainted.

Not gracefully — grace was not the point. I went down among the broken champagne glasses with my eyes rolled back, and the wife of the harbormaster screamed, and then the room did what rooms full of important people always do when a body hits the floor: it converged. Doctors materialized who were not doctors. Someone shouted for water. Someone else shouted for air, though the windows were already open. The chairman of the mercantile association insisted on holding my head, and did, awkwardly, while I lay with my cheek against the parquet watching two hundred shoes crowd around me and thinking, with a clarity that still shames and thrills me: *every one of you is looking here.*

Through the forest of ankles, I saw the study door at the top of the far stair open, silently, and a figure in company livery step out and pull it shut behind him. White gloves. Steady gait. He crossed the landing, nodded to no one, and vanished down the servants' passage.

Then, and only then, did I allow myself to recover — sitting up slowly, dazed, mortified, apologizing to the harbormaster's husband for the trouble, accepting a glass of water I did not want, declining a chair I badly needed.

"Overwrought," pronounced the self-appointed doctor. "These serving girls work such long hours."

"Too kind," I whispered, and let them help me toward the kitchens, away from the light, while behind me Aldous Crane cleared his throat and resumed his speech about how history would remember this house kindly.

He had no idea, none at all, how correct he was about to become.

* * *

**SILAS**

Odile found me at the coal chute, and I will carry the sight of her to my last day: grey dress black with dust, cut thumb wrapped in a scrap of napkin, sliding down into the dark after me with her jaw set like a woman descending into scripture.

"Go," she said. "I'll follow the chute. You take the yard."

"We go together or not at all." I braced at the chute's mouth. Below us the coal bin waited, deep and soft as a grave that hadn't decided yet. Above us — unmistakable now — bells. Someone had found the lamp still warm in the study, or the rug's crushed pile where I'd knelt, or simply remembered a serving girl with a tray and a clerk named Pell with a story, and the house had begun to understand what it had lost.

"I'm not leaving you," I said, and heard how it sounded, and did not take it back.

She didn't ask me to. She sat at the chute's lip, gathered her skirt, and pushed off, and I went after her, and the darkness took us both at speed. The coal caught us the way water catches a diver — hard, then gently, then all around. For one absurd moment, buried in another family's fuel in a stranger's dark, I laughed. I couldn't help it. Terror and triumph use the same lungs.

We clawed our way up the slope of it, spilled out through the bin's hatch into the freezing yard, and ran — not toward the street, which would be watched, but along the tideline where the dock families' lanterns hung like low stars. Maren was there, as promised, with the boat. August was there, bundled against her shoulder, asleep, oblivious, alive.

Behind us the Halloway house blazed with lights, window after window igniting, and a bell began tolling the alarm in earnest, and Odile gripped my sleeve and pointed at my breast pocket where a corner of oilcloth showed.

"You got it," she said. Not a question.

"Every page. Twice."

She closed her eyes for one breath, and in that breath I watched the terror leave her the way a tide leaves a harbor — completely, and leaving everything rearranged behind it.

"Then tomorrow," she said, opening her eyes, "we deliver it to the inquiry before they can deliver their forgery. Because I promise you, Silas — by morning, there will be warrants."

The boat pushed off into black water. Behind us the bell rang on, and ahead of us the city slept, not yet knowing that by sundown tomorrow, everything its historians had written would need to be written again.
