## 16. Hunted

The knock came at half past four in the morning, and it was not a knock so much as a blow.

Silas was already awake. He had been awake since two, lying on the cot in Maren's back room with August asleep against his shoulder, her breath warm and even against his collarbone, listening to the city turn over in its sleep. He had learned to sleep like a diver learns to hold his breath — shallowly, ready to surface at the first wrong sound.

The wrong sound came: boots on the boardwalk outside, three sets, moving without hurry. Men who expected to find what they came for did not hurry.

He stood, handed August through the curtain to Maren — she was already up, already dressed, because of course she was; the dock never slept easy when Halloway's name was spoken — and pulled open the door before they could break it.

Three men. Two constables in admiralty blue, and between them a company man Silas recognized from the third floor: Pell, a small neat creature who carried Director Halloway's displeasure the way other men carried watches, close and consulted often.

"Silas Ravensworth." Pell held up a folded paper as though displaying a specimen. "You are served."

* * *

There were three warrants. Silas read them standing in his own doorway while Pell watched him with the flat satisfaction of a man who has finally been permitted to do what he was hired to do.

Theft of company property — one page, original safety report, dive of the twelfth of March, value assessed at five shillings, which Silas thought was almost funny, the price they put on the truth. Breach of indenture — the contract itself quoted back at him in careful clerical language, *the said Ravensworth being bound to serve the Halloway Trading Company faithfully until the term of seven years shall expire.* And third, the one that made something go cold and quiet behind his ribs:

Kidnapping. An infant, female, approximately eleven months of age, last seen in the company of the accused.

"They think she's mine," Silas said.

"They think whatever a magistrate will believe," Pell said. "Come along quietly and none of this needs to be difficult."

Maren stepped forward into the doorway, and Silas felt the room rearrange itself around her the way water rearranges around a hull. She was sixty-eight years old and half a head shorter than the nearest constable, and she looked at Pell the way she had once looked at weather coming off the sea.

"Child was left on your company's doorstep," she said. "In front of witnesses. Ask your own night watchman. Or don't you keep records?"

"We keep all records, madam," Pell said smoothly. "Including records of an infant removed from company premises without authorization."

"Removed," Maren repeated. "Like a splinter."

"Maren." Silas touched her arm. He could feel the whole street listening — doors cracked open, lamps lit behind shutters, the dock families assembling themselves silently around the fact of him. That was worth more than he could say, but it would not stop a warrant. Nothing stopped a warrant except being somewhere else. "I'll go with them."

"The hell you will," said a voice from the crowd, and Brann the stevedore pushed through, still pulling on his coat. Then another voice, and another, and suddenly there were twenty dockworkers on Maren's steps and constables finding that the arithmetic of arrests had changed overnight.

Pell's composure flickered — just once, just enough. He was a man paid to take possession of things, and he understood viscerally when a thing could not be taken.

"There'll be another attempt," he said to Silas, low. "And the next time it won't be constables. The inquiry opens in six days. Think about where you want to be standing when it does."

"I know exactly where I want to be standing," Silas said.

* * *

They moved August within the hour.

It happened with a speed and coordination that would have astonished anyone who thought of the dock families as disorganized poor. Old Maren made the decisions and Brann carried them out, and by sunrise the child had passed through four households like a coin changing hands — the Ferreira widow first, then out by fish cart to the Callahans' net loft, then down to Josie Adeyemi's rooms above the chandlery, each keeper knowing only the next link in the chain and none knowing all of it. It was, Silas realized, exactly how the divers' testimony had traveled. They had built this machine without meaning to. Every loss the company had dealt them had taught them how to protect something.

He kissed August's forehead at the net loft door, memorizing the weight of her one more time, and she grabbed his nose with one small determined fist and laughed, and it took everything he had not to break right there among the drying nets.

"She'll be fine," said Callahan, gruff, not looking at him. "Better than fine. There's more love on this dock than your company ever counted. Go do what you have to do."

So he did the only thing left: he disappeared into the dock the way his father had taught him to disappear into water. No patterns. No fixed hours. A bed in three places, never two nights running. The warrants went up on the customs house wall by noon — THEFT, BREACH OF INDENTURE, KIDNAPPING — and Silas read his own name in fresh ink beside the tide tables and felt, strangely, lighter. There was a kind of freedom in having nothing left to lose publicly. His face was known now. His silence was worthless to them. All he had left was what he knew and what he dared.

He spent the afternoon in the loft above the Cooper's Arms, watching the harbor through a gap in the shutters, counting Halloway's boats.

* * *

Odile burned her shorthand at dusk.

She had done it properly, which is to say she had done it like a stenographer: every page of the divers' statements, transcribed in her private system over those long careful weeks, taken first to Notary Elwood Voss on Chandler Street. Voss was a narrow, asthmatic man whose practice survived because he notarized for everyone — company men, union men, whoever paid — and because no one had ever thought him important enough to corrupt or destroy. Odile had chosen him for precisely that unimportance.

She laid the pages on his desk one by one. "Witnessed copies," she said. "Each dated. Each bearing the witness's mark or signature, as recorded."

Voss examined them over his spectacles, stamped and signed a certification of authenticity for the complete set, and asked no questions beyond the ones required by law. When she left, the certified set stayed locked in his strongbox, and the originals rode home inside finished deposition folios from the Halloway depositions, hidden in plain sight among a hundred pages of corporate monotony.

Then, in her small room over the print shop, she fed them into the stove.

She had expected it to feel like destruction. It felt instead like a release — the way a diver must feel cutting loose a weight belt, everything rising at once. Each page caught and curled and brightened, Tomas Okonkwo's brother's statement going up in a bloom of orange, the depth figures she had checked four times dissolving into heat. The words were gone from paper and existed now in only two places: a notary's strongbox, and the minds of people who had spoken them aloud.

Let Crane hunt for documents that no longer existed. Let the company subpoena shadows.

Her hands smelled of smoke when she finished, and she sat for a long while watching the last embers, thinking about what she had spent. Eleven years at the stenographer's bench. Her license, most likely — the bar association took a dim view of its officers concealing evidence, however righteous the cause. Possibly her liberty; Pell's people had already been asking questions at the courts building, polite questions about Miss Marchetti and her late hours.

She had written her resignation that afternoon, undated, to be delivered whenever it would do the most damage. It sat in her drawer like a loaded weapon.

A knock at the street door, two short and one long. Silas.

She let him in and saw immediately what the week had done to him — the hollows under his eyes, the way he moved like a man keeping weight on a bad deck. She put tea in front of him without asking and sat across the table, and for a moment neither spoke. Two cautious people, past caution now, too tired for it.

"They burned nothing of yours?" he asked.

"Nothing that mattered. Everything that mattered is in Voss's strongbox and in my head." She turned her cup slowly. "Silas. If this goes wrong at the inquiry — if the examiner sides with them — you need to understand what I've put on the table. I did it freely. But you should carry the knowledge of it. That's part of the cost, knowing what others paid."

He looked at her then, really looked, and she watched him take the weight of it the way he took everything — carefully, fully, refusing to set any part of it down.

"Then here's mine," he said. "My indenture, my name, my record — every clean ledger line I've kept since I was twelve years old. If we win, I get them back. If we lose, I don't. And August grows up visiting me through bars, if she can find which prison they've put me in." He smiled, and it was a real smile, tired and unbreakable. "I find I don't mind. Isn't that strange? Ten years I kept my head down so nobody would ever look at me. Now my face is on the customs house wall and I've never felt more visible. More *here*."

"That's not strange," Odile said. "That's what it costs to matter."

* * *

The second attempt came the next night, and it was not constables.

Silas was crossing the coal yard behind the chandlery shortly after midnight — moving between safe houses, as he now did nightly — when he heard footsteps match his rhythm and then deliberately fall out of it. He turned. Two men in plain coats, big through the shoulders, moving with the unhurried confidence of professionals. One carried a canvas sack. Neither wore a constable's color, and neither, Silas noted with the cold clarity that fear sometimes granted him, seemed troubled by witnesses.

"You're wanted for a conversation," the nearer one said. "About returning what isn't yours."

"And if I decline?"

"Then the conversation happens anyway. Rougher."

Silas backed toward the coal stacks, calculating. He was not a fighter. He had a boat hook within reach and ten feet of darkness at his back, and he was weighing whether he could use either when the yard filled with whistles — three sharp notes, then three more, echoing off the warehouse walls from every direction at once. The dock signal. *Man in trouble. Come.*

The two professionals exchanged a glance that said plainly their fee had not covered this, and withdrew into the dark with dignity. Thirty seconds later Silas was surrounded by stevedores holding lanterns and cargo hooks, Brann at the front breathing like a bull, Josie Adeyemi on the fence with a whistle still at her lips.

"They won't try again with muscle," Brann said, when the yard had emptied. "Too public now. They'll go at you with paper instead. Paper's how that lot fights when it's cornered."

"Then we'd better make sure," Silas said, "that at the inquiry, our paper is better than theirs."

* * *

On the fifth day, with one day remaining before the inquiry opened, Odile came to the Cooper's Arms loft carrying a sealed envelope and an expression Silas had learned to dread — the expression of a woman bearing news that was precise and terrible.

"The anonymous package," she said. "The photographed report. It needs to reach the examiner tomorrow morning, before Crane finishes his presentation. One chance, one delivery, no second attempt."

"So we deliver it tonight."

"No." She set the envelope on the table between them. "We deliver it tomorrow, during the session, in front of witnesses — because if it arrives early, they'll intercept it, and if it arrives secretly, Crane will call it forgery. It has to appear *in the room*, where a dozen reporters can see it land. Which means someone has to be inside the inquiry hall with it. Someone they haven't thought to watch." She paused. "Or someone they've watched so thoroughly they've grown careless about the obvious."

Silas looked at the envelope, and then at the warrant list he knew by heart, and understood what she was not yet saying — that the safest courier was a man already charged with stealing the very document inside it. A man with nothing left to lose and a summons of his own waiting at that hall.

Outside, the harbor bell rang the hour. Six days of hiding ended tonight.

"One day," Silas said quietly. "Then we stop running."

Odile met his eyes. "One day," she agreed. "And Silas — when you walk in there tomorrow, walk in like a man who owns his own name. Whatever else happens in that room, from the moment you speak, no one at Halloway Trading Company gets to tell your history for you ever again."

He picked up the envelope and held it a moment, feeling its lightness, feeling the whole weight of the drowned beneath it.

Tomorrow, the inquiry.
