## 17. The Inquiry Opens

The admiralty court in Vellamare had been built to make ordinary people feel small. Odile Marchetti felt it the moment she stepped through the doors: a vaulted ceiling painted with the arms of the Crown, benches of dark polished oak, and a raised dais where the presiding examiner would sit beneath a portrait of some long-dead admiral whose eyes followed everyone who entered. The room smelled of beeswax and cold stone and, faintly, of the sea that came through the high windows on the harbor wind.

She took a seat near the back, among the clerks and the curious, her shorthand notebook closed on her knee like a sleeping bird. She was here in an official capacity — the inquiry required a stenographer of record, and she was one of three assigned by the courts. That was the beauty of it. She did not have to sneak in. She had been summoned, sworn, and seated, with a company lawyer's nod and everything. For once, the machinery that had spent months grinding against Silas Ravensworth was going to carry her exactly where she needed to be.

She found him with her eyes before she could stop herself. Silas sat at the far end of the public benches, between Maren and a dockworker named Brannigan, his wrists bare where the irons had been removed for transport. They had brought him in through a side door, flanked by two constables, and the courtroom had gone quiet in a way that was not sympathy but appetite. Vellamare loved a spectacle, and this one had cost a pearl diver's son everything he had.

He looked thinner than he had in the archive, when Crane had made his offer and Silas had refused it with a flatness that still frightened her a little. But his back was straight. His hands were folded. He looked, Odile thought, like a man who had already done the hardest thing and was merely waiting for the world to catch up.

The examiner entered at nine o'clock precisely. Sir Edmund Voss — she knew him by reputation, a man who had spent thirty years untangling maritime disputes and was said to possess the patience of a tide and the memory of a ledger. He was small, gray, precise, with spectacles that caught the window light so that his eyes were often invisible. He arranged his papers, adjusted his spectacles twice, and said, "This inquiry is opened," in a voice like a drawer closing.

Then Aldous Crane rose to speak.

Odile had watched Crane work the corridors of the Halloway Building for weeks. She had typed his manuscript with her own hands, page after page of seamless, confident prose, and she had told herself she was prepared for whatever he would do in open court. She was wrong. In the manuscript, Crane had been a historian. In the courtroom, he was something else entirely — a sculptor working in front of his subject, shaping the room's understanding with every pause, every gesture toward the exhibits, every gentle correction of a fact no one had yet gotten wrong.

"Examiner," he began, "the Halloway Trading Company has served this port for sixty-one years. It has employed four thousand souls. It has lost, in those six decades, one hundred and ninety-two men to the sea." He let the number sit. "This inquiry concerns three of them."

Three. As if they had been selected from a catalog. Odile wrote nothing; her official record would be transcribed by machine dictaphone and checked later. Her own notebook stayed shut. What she kept today was in her head, where no subpoena could reach it.

Crane walked the court through the company's history of the Okonkwo dive as though walking them through a garden he had planted himself. The bed off the Kestrel Shoals, newly charted in the spring of 1896. The survey vessel *Meridian*, its instruments, its soundings. The certification of safe depth at eleven fathoms. The dive order issued under standard terms. And then the deaths — Tomas Okonkwo, his brother Emeka, their cousin Daniel — lost not to any failure of the company but, Crane suggested with exquisite delicacy, to the sea itself, which "does not read our charts, Examiner, however carefully we draw them."

"The divers were experienced men," Crane continued. "Brave men. But experience breeds confidence, and confidence, in deep water, breeds error. The company's own investigation found evidence that the party exceeded the certified depth in pursuit of a richer bed below. Tragic. Preventable only by the divers themselves. The company mourns them still."

He produced the safety report. Odile recognized it instantly — the forged replacement, the clean copy the company had manufactured after discovering the original was missing, the document they intended to charge Silas with falsifying. It went into evidence without a ripple. A clerk read the relevant passage aloud: *Depth certified safe to eleven fathoms. No warnings recorded.*

"May I ask," Sir Edmund said, "who conducted the original survey?"

"Surveyor Whitcombe, Examiner. A man of twenty years' service. He has since taken a position abroad, but his report speaks for itself."

"Convenient," said Sir Edmund, in a tone Odile could not parse — dry enough to be nothing, pointed enough to be everything.

Crane smiled as if the examiner had paid him a compliment, and moved on.

For two hours, the seamless history unrolled. Odile watched it work on the room the way she imagined a tide works on sand — imperceptibly, irresistibly. The company's counsel asked soft questions. Company witnesses confirmed soft answers. A clerk testified that all records were maintained according to standard practice. A second clerk confirmed the first. By half past eleven, Odile could feel the verdict settling over the room like weather: a tragedy, an unfortunate one, no one's fault, the sea's fault, the dead men's fault, anyone's fault but the company's.

Sir Edmund asked few questions. He drank his water. He made small notes. Once, when Crane described the depth certification as "beyond reproach," the examiner looked up and held the historian in his invisible gaze for a long three seconds before returning to his papers. Odile wrote one word in her notebook, so lightly the pencil barely marked the page: *waiting?*

Or perhaps: *bored?* She honestly could not tell. That was the terror of men like Sir Edmund Voss. You could drown in the space between his sentences.

At noon the inquiry recessed until two. In the corridor outside, the crowd had thickened — dockworkers in their Sunday coats, company clerks pretending not to know each other, a knot of journalists from the *Vellamare Courier* comparing notes in low voices. Odile passed close to Silas's bench as the constables moved him. He did not look at her. He said, very quietly, to no one, "Afternoon session. Watch the examiner's left hand."

His left hand. She filed it away and did not ask.

* * *

The afternoon opened with more of the same. Crane called the company's marine superintendent, a heavy man named Gorse who sweated through his collar and swore that no order to exceed safe depth had ever been given in his tenure. He called the paymaster, who produced ledgers showing the divers had been paid in full for the fatal expedition — as though wages were absolution. He called, at last, to Odile's surprise, a diver: old Petros Vane, who had been paid for his silence and had recanted his statement when his daughter's position was threatened.

She watched Vane take the oath with his hat crushed in both hands. She watched him confirm, in a voice like gravel sliding, that the Okonkwos had gone down of their own accord, past the marks, chasing pearl. Every word was a stone dropped into her chest. She had transcribed his true statement herself, in her own shorthand, page by page, while his hands shook. She had watched him sign it. And now she sat ten feet away while he buried it alive in front of God and the admiralty.

But — and she caught it, because catching such things was her trade — Vane's eyes never once went to Crane. They went to the door. To the back of the room. To the place where the dockworkers sat. And when he finished, he sat down so fast he nearly missed the chair, and his hat brim came away wet where his forehead had pressed it.

Guilt, Odile thought. Guilt with a return ticket.

Sir Edmund adjourned briefly to consult his notes, and the room murmured like a shell held to the ear. Odile used the interval to look at the examiner properly for the first time. He was reviewing the forged safety report, turning it in his fingers, holding it up to the light. Reading, she realized, the way she read — not the words but the paper. The age of the ink. The lie of the margins.

At five minutes past two, the bailiff crossed to the examiner's bench carrying a package.

Odile saw it arrive the way one sees lightning — already there, already done, the thunder arriving after. A plain brown parcel, string-tied, no markings but her own name and the court's address in a hand she did not recognize. The bailiff whispered to Sir Edmund. Sir Edmund turned the parcel over once, twice. Then he said, in that drawer-closing voice:

"Before we continue with the company's witnesses, the court will receive an item delivered to this building within the hour. Bailiff, open it."

String cut. Paper folded back. Inside: a photographic plate, developed and mounted on card, enclosed in a protective sleeve — and beneath it, a single sheet of paper bearing a line of text.

The bailiff carried it to the bench. Sir Edmund put on his spectacles. And Odile Marchetti, who had spent weeks learning to read that gray face and had failed every time, finally saw what she had been waiting for.

His left hand stopped moving.

Silas had told her: watch the examiner's left hand. The right hand turned pages, adjusted spectacles, performed the theater of patience. The left hand was the honest one. And now the left hand had gone absolutely still on the edge of the bench, fingers spread, pressing down as though the paper might fly away.

"Mr. Crane," Sir Edmund said. "You have offered into evidence a safety report certifying the Kestrel Shoals dive at eleven fathoms, with no warnings recorded. Is that correct?"

"It is, Examiner."

"And you are familiar with the original documents from which that report derives?"

"I supervised their review personally."

"Then perhaps you can assist the court." Sir Edmund lifted the mounted photograph so that the whole room could see it — the image too small to read from the benches, but unmistakably a page, a real page, with lines struck through in a heavy hand. "This is a photograph of a document. I will read the legible portions into the record. 'Sounding at fourteen fathoms confirms unsafe conditions beyond the eleven-fathom mark. Strong current. Recommend no dive be ordered below certified depth.' The remainder has been struck out — struck out, Mr. Crane — in a hand which the court will compare against documents already in evidence. Do you recognize this page?"

The silence lasted four seconds. Odile counted them, because counting was what she did when the world came apart.

"I—" Crane's voice, for the first time in all the weeks she had listened to it, found no purchase. "I am not able to identify the document from a photograph, Examiner. If the original were produced—"

"The original," Sir Edmund said mildly, "was removed from your director's private safe three nights ago, according to a warrant application now before this court. The court notes that the company's response to its loss was to accuse a clerk of forgery — a clerk, I observe, who has been held in custody since, and who has not yet been permitted to testify." He set the photograph down with great care, the way a surgeon sets down an instrument. "The court further observes that the handwriting of the annotations on this photographed page appears, at first glance, consistent with correspondence from Director Halloway already entered into evidence this morning. Exhibit forty-one. The court will compare them tonight."

The room turned. Odile felt it turn — physically, a shifting of weight across the benches, a hundred spines straightening at once. The journalists were writing. The company counsel were conferring in a huddle tight enough to share a heartbeat. Old Maren, three rows up, had closed her eyes, and her lips were moving, and Odile would have bet her career it was the names of the dead.

And Silas — Silas sat with his hands still folded, watching Crane, and there was nothing triumphant in his face at all. Only a kind of terrible patience, the patience of a man standing at a wall with a crack running through it, knowing the wall must come down and knowing what it would fall on.

"One further matter," Sir Edmund said. "The note accompanying this item reads as follows, and the court will enter it verbatim." He read aloud, slowly: "'The original was altered before the company's copy was made. The hand that struck these warnings is the hand that ordered the dive. Let the record show what was removed.'" He lowered the paper. "Unsigned, naturally. The court does not credit anonymous accusations, Mr. Crane. But the court does credit documents. And tomorrow morning, this inquiry will hear from the man accused of forging the report the company itself entered into evidence — because it occurs to the court that a forger would hardly photograph the very document that convicts him."

Crane opened his mouth. Closed it. Opened it again. "Examiner, the company reserves—"

"The company," said Sir Edmund Voss, "reserves nothing. This court sits to find what happened to three men in the spring of 1896, and it will find it, Mr. Crane, if it must take this port apart plank by plank. We resume at nine o'clock. The prisoner Ravensworth will be produced. Adjourned."

The gavel fell, and the room erupted into motion — but Odile stayed in her seat, heart hammering, watching the bailiff seal the photograph into an evidence envelope. Someone had sent it. Not Silas; he'd been in custody. Not her; she'd been in this room since nine. Which meant someone else had the plate, someone else had moved, someone in the chain they had built so carefully had acted alone.

She thought of the coal chute, the borrowed camera, the flash of powder in the dark of the director's study. She thought of the list of people who had touched that plate between the safe and this bench.

There were only three names on it. And one of them, she realized, rising slowly as the crowd pushed past her, was a name she had never expected to see written in this story at all.

She had to find Silas before tomorrow morning. Because whoever had sent the package hadn't just cracked the wall.

They had started a clock running — and Odile was fairly sure none of them knew what happened when it finished.
