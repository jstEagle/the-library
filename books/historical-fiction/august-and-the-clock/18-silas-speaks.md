## 18. Silas Speaks

They came for him before dawn.

Silas heard the boots on the dock boards first — the particular cadence of men who wanted to be heard coming, who wanted the whole waterfront to know that Halloway Trading Company still had teeth. He had been awake since midnight anyway, sitting on Maren's good chair with August asleep against his chest, her small fist curled around his collar button, and he found that when the knock finally came he felt something adjacent to relief.

The waiting was over. Now there was only the doing.

"There's a warrant," said the man at the door. Silas knew him distantly — Braddock, a company enforcer who had once been a decent stevedore and had let the company sand that out of him. "Theft of company property. Breach of indenture. And the child."

"Her name is August," Silas said.

Braddock's jaw worked. Whatever lived behind his eyes was tired and old and did not enjoy its job. "You'll come quiet or you'll not come quiet. Makes no difference to me which."

"It never does," Silas said, and put the baby gently into Maren's arms.

Maren held his forearm hard enough to bruise, her fingers knotted like dock rope. "You say your piece," she told him. "We'll hold everything else."

He believed her. That was the strange mercy of it — thirty years of keeping himself separate, of being the ledger-man who belonged to no one because belonging meant losing, and now here were forty dockworkers between him and the constables, not blocking the way, just standing there, watching, so that every boot-step he took down the gangway would be witnessed.

August woke and cried once as the door closed. He did not look back. If he looked back, he understood, he might do something foolish, and he needed every part of himself intact for what came next.

* * *

They held him overnight in a cell beneath the admiralty house — a courtesy arrest, Odile would tell him later, arranged by the company's solicitors so that he could be produced directly before the inquiry in the morning, in irons, a living exhibit of what happened to men who meddled with Halloway history.

The irons were real. The performance was theirs.

Silas slept, which surprised him. He dreamed of his father, not drowning for once but surfacing, breaking the water with pearls in his fists, saying: *count these, boy, count them true.*

When they marched him up the stairs and into the inquiry chamber, the morning light came through the high windows in long gold bars, and the room was full — fuller than it had been the day before, word having spread along the docks faster than any constable could run. Dockworkers stood three deep at the back wall because they were not permitted seats. Maren was among them, and Odile sat at the press table with her shorthand pad open, and Director Halloway sat at the company bench wearing mourning clothes he had no right to, and beside him Aldous Crane looked like a man whose manuscript had begun writing back.

The presiding examiner, Vice-Admiral Corven, rapped for order.

"The court recognizes the prisoner," someone said, and the words *prisoner* settled over Silas like a coat he had been fitted for all his life.

He stood. The chains on his wrists made a sound like small bells, absurd and deliberate. He felt the whole room leaning toward him the way water leans toward a breach in a hull.

He had rehearsed nothing. Rehearsal was a kind of lying, and today he intended to be the most honest man in Vellamare.

"I am Silas Ravensworth," he said, "indentured diver's son, ledger-keeper, fourteen years bound to the Halloway Trading Company under contract." He drew from inside his coat — the guards had searched him for weapons and found none, because they did not know what a document could be — the folded paper that had ruled every hour of his life since he was eleven years old. His signature at the bottom. His father's death date entered in the margin as if it were an inventory line.

"What are you holding?" Corven asked.

"My leash," Silas said, and tore it in half.

The sound of the tear was very small and filled the entire chamber. He tore it again, lengthwise, and let the pieces fall to the floor.

"No man bound by Halloway can testify freely," he said. "Your own law holds that an indentured servant's words belong to his master — that anything I say here can be dismissed as the grievance of contracted labor. So I have ended the contract. I break my indenture publicly, before this court, and I accept whatever penalty your statutes attach to it. Fine me. Jail me. Add the years to my term. But you cannot now dismiss what I am about to say as a bound man's complaint, because I have just purchased my freedom to speak it — at the price of everything I owned, which was nothing but the debt itself."

Halloway was on his feet. "Examiner, this is theater—"

"Sit down, Director," Corven said quietly, "or lose your standing in my inquiry." He turned back to Silas, and his eyes had gone from cold to merely careful. "Proceed. And understand that perjury before an admiralty inquiry carries seven years."

"I have counted things for fourteen years, sir," Silas said. "I have never miscounted once."

* * *

He told it plainly, because plainness was all he had and it would be enough.

He told them about the spring dive. About the *Meridian's Bell*, chartered by the company to survey the newly charted bed off the Serpent Reefs, where the charts themselves carried depth warnings in three hands across twenty years of marginalia. He told them how Tomas Okonkwo and his two brothers-in-craft had been ordered down past safe depth — past the line where a diver's blood begins to boil with its own pressure, past the line their own fathers had died teaching them to respect — because the pearl bed below that line was richer, and the season's ledger demanded richness.

He named the numbers. He had memorized them; he had always been able to memorize. Forty fathoms certified safe. Sixty-two fathoms ordered. Three divers down. Two ropes recovered frayed. One body never recovered at all, though the sea gives back what it takes slowly, and a fisherman had found Tomas Okonkwo six weeks later and been paid four pounds by a company clerk to forget where.

"The safety report filed with the harbor office," Silas said, "was altered before filing. The depth warnings were struck through. I have seen the original page. It exists. It was kept — not destroyed, because Director Halloway does not destroy documents, he keeps them the way a blackmailer keeps letters — in the private safe in his study at the company house. I photographed that original page on the night of the director's banquet. The plate has already reached this court. You have seen it. Compare the hand that struck the warnings to the hand that signs Director Halloway's correspondence. It will match. It matched for me in the dark, by lamplight, in the space of ninety seconds."

"Objection," the company solicitor said. "The prisoner is not qualified to give opinion on handwriting."

"He isn't offering opinion," Odile said from the press table, rising. "He's offering comparison, and the court has the originals of both hands in evidence. Let the examiner compare them himself."

Corven compared them himself. There was a long silence while he held two papers side by side under the window light, and Silas watched the vice-admiral's face do what faces did when a man realized the floor under him had been painted on.

Then Silas told them about the money.

"One diver survived that dive," he said. "A man who was on the rope for Tomas Okonkwo, who felt the line go slack, who was ordered to cut it and surface and say nothing. He was paid thirty pounds for his silence — drawn from the discretionary fund, coded as salvage equipment. I know because I audited the entry myself, two months later, and asked why salvage equipment cost exactly the same sum every quarter, and was told to clean my ledgers and mind my station." His voice held steady, and he was grateful to it. "That man recanted his testimony last week, because the company threatened his daughter's position. I do not condemn him. Fear is honest arithmetic too; I did the same sums for fourteen years and called it prudence. But I tell this court where the payment is recorded, and I tell you that the man who received it signed a receipt, and receipts live longer than courage does."

He told them about the child. Not the child herself — he would not put her in this room, would not let Halloway's eyes anywhere near her — but the fact of the swaddling cloth, the clan-mark of the Okonkwo line, the note that ordered him to forget a face.

"The company wrote 'weather losses' in its ledger for three dead men," Silas said. "Someone outlived the lie long enough to leave a living witness on your doorstep. You have spent a year trying to make me forget her face. I have spent that year learning every other face this company hoped would stay drowned."

He stopped. The chains hung quiet. Somewhere behind him a dockworker began, very softly, to clap, and Corven let it go for three seconds before rapping it down.

* * *

Cross-examination was Crane's idea, and it was a mistake, and everyone in the room knew it within four questions.

Crane approached the stand the way he approached everything, beautifully — silver at his temples, voice pitched to the balconies of lecture halls. "Mr. Ravensworth. You've told us a story of villainy. Yet you served this company faithfully for fourteen years. Why would an honest man serve villains?"

"Because honest men need wages," Silas said, "and cowards need company. I was both for a long time. The company counts on that. It is, in fact, the company's entire business model — pearls above, silence below."

"You audited the books. You found this 'discretionary payment.' And instead of reporting it, you continued to serve. Isn't that complicity, Mr. Ravensworth?"

"Yes," Silas said.

The answer landed wrong-footed; Crane had prepared for denial. Silas leaned forward against the rail, chains clinking.

"Yes, Mr. Crane, it was complicity, and I have carried it every day since, and I am paying for it right now, in front of all of Vellamare, in the only coin I have. What are you paying? You are writing a book that says three dead divers killed themselves through superstition. When this inquiry ends — and it is ending, Mr. Crane, you watched the examiner compare those two hands this morning same as I did — your book will be reissued with corrections written by someone else. That is your wage for this morning's work. I'd have taken the fifth question more seriously than the first, in your place."

Corven's mouth twitched, which in a vice-admiral was a laugh.

Crane tried twice more and then the company solicitor rose to object to his own witness strategy, which told Silas everything, and Corven adjourned the session until the divers should be called.

As the guards took his arms, Silas looked across the chamber at Halloway. The director was not looking at him. The director was looking at the torn pieces of the indenture contract on the floor, and his face wore the expression of a man who has just discovered that a number he trusted has stopped adding up — and Silas understood, with the calm of a man past the reach of threats, that Halloway had lost before the divers ever took the stand, because Halloway had built everything on the certainty that bound men keep silent.

And then Halloway did look up, and meet his eyes, and smile.

It was a small smile. Private. Almost fond.

And it said, more clearly than any threat: *you have testified about the report, and the orders, and the payment. But you haven't yet said what's in the rest of the safe. And I burned it last night.*

Behind Silas, at the press table, Odile had gone white — because she alone in the chamber knew what the second document photographed that night had shown, the one Silas had not mentioned, the one that named a name far older and higher than Director Halloway.

She was already writing when the guards led him out.
