## 22. August and the Clock

The morning of August's birthday, Maren woke before dawn out of habit rather than need, and for the first time in a year there was nothing to get up for but a good thing. She lay in her narrow bed above the net-mender's shed and listened to the harbor breathe. Somewhere below, gulls argued over fish heads. Somewhere farther off, the tide came in the way it always did, patient and indifferent and older than every ledger ever written.

She was seventy-one years old. Her hands had spent fifty of those years pulling rope and hauling nets and holding drowning men's heads above water when the sea gave them back. They were not gentle hands. But they had learned to be careful around a baby, and that had been its own kind of education.

August slept in the basket beside her bed, one fist curled against her cheek, breathing with the small steam-engine rhythm Maren had come to love more than she had ever expected to love anything again. On the wall, the tide clock ticked. It was wrong, as it had been wrong since spring — it ran eleven minutes slow no matter how many times Maren corrected it — and she had stopped correcting it. Some clocks earned their errors.

*One year old,* she thought. *And every name on the memorial wall accounted for at last.*

She got up quietly, dressed, and lifted the child, who woke just enough to burrow against her shoulder with a sound like a contented seal. Maren carried her downstairs into the blue half-light and out onto the dock.

The dock community had been decorating since before sunup, because dock people did everything early — they worked before the wind changed, ate before the work called, mourned before the body was found. Bunting made from retired signal flags hung between the crane arm and the old customs house post. Long tables had been dragged out from the Fisherman's Rest, and already Tamsin Oduya was laying them with cloth that had clearly been borrowed from somebody's funeral, which nobody remarked upon, because dock people also understood that joy and mourning used the same fabrics.

By seven o'clock the whole waterfront knew about the party, and by eight the whole waterfront was pretending not to know, so they could arrive casually throughout the day and stay too long.

* * *

Silas came at midmorning, and Maren watched him cross the dock the way she had watched him cross things his whole life — carefully, like a man checking ice that had never once held him.

He looked different this year. Not bigger. Lighter, somehow, as though something had been subtracted from him rather than added. The ledgers were gone from under his arm; he carried instead a canvas satchel stamped with the words DIVERS' TESTIMONY ARCHIVE — EST. 1897, letters Odile had painted herself, slightly crooked, refusing all offers to redo them. The archive lived now in two rented rooms above the chandlery: forty sworn statements, the photographed original safety report, six years of depth logs Odile had subpoenaed through channels that still made Silas wince to discuss, and a waiting list of divers from three other ports who had heard, the way news travels on water, that there was finally somewhere their stories could be put down and kept.

"Look who's walking," Maren said, though August was doing less walking than drunken sailing, arms out, veering toward Silas with the unerring instinct children have for the person least equipped to receive them.

"I saw," Silas said. "From the end of the pier. She fell over twice."

"She got up twice."

"That's what frightened me."

But he crouched and opened his arms, and August sailed into them, and Maren saw her boy — because he was hers, whatever the papers said, whatever he said, the dock had decided it decades ago and the dock did not revisit its rulings — Maren saw him hold the child the way he had once held ledgers, with total attention, as if the whole world might be audited later and he wanted to be sure of every entry.

"You sleep?" Maren asked him.

"Some."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," he admitted. "Not really. There's a diver coming Thursday from Corvessa. Lost two brothers in '93. He wants to know if his testimony will matter." He paused. "I couldn't tell him yes. I could only tell him we'd keep it."

"Keeping is enough," Maren said. "It was always the keeping that failed first."

* * *

The party proper began at noon, when the sun warmed the planks and the smell of fried fish overcame the smell of tar, which everyone agreed was a victory. There was a cake shaped like a boat, baked by Tamsin and decorated with such ambition that it eventually had to be referred to as abstract art. There was a fiddler who played tunes no one had requested and everyone danced to anyway. Old Brannigan, who had lost a son to the sea in '89 and had not smiled in public since, was discovered laughing at his own joke, and pretended afterward it had been a coughing fit.

Odile arrived late, in a gray traveling dress, carrying a wrapped parcel and the particular exhaustion of a woman who had spent the week giving evidence in three separate proceedings. The Halloway directors' trial was grinding through its second month. She would not talk about it except to say, when pressed, that the wheels moved slowly and ground finely, and then she would drink her cider and let someone else change the subject.

She sat down next to Maren on the bench outside the Fisherman's Rest, where August was holding court in Maren's lap, solemnly accepting tribute: a wooden bird from Brannigan, a ribbon from Tamsin, a shell from a boy whose name nobody knew and whom nobody questioned, because that was how the dock worked.

"How is she today?" Odile asked. "A year old. What does that make her, in stenographer's terms?"

"Fully deposed," Maren said. "She's got opinions on everything and can't yet articulate any of them."

Odile laughed — a real laugh, head tipped back, and Maren noted, not for the first time, the way Silas looked up from across the dock when it happened, and the way he looked away again quickly, a man still learning that he was allowed to want things.

"And you?" Maren said. "You've given a year to this."

"A year," Odile agreed. She turned her cup in her hands. "When I started transcribing company depositions, I thought my job was to write down what people said. Then I learned my job was to write down only some of what people said, in an order approved by men who'd never met the people saying it." She shrugged. "Now I write down everything. It takes longer. The truth is inefficient. Nobody's ever told you that?"

"Everybody's told me that," Maren said. "I never listened."

* * *

In the middle of the afternoon, when the cake had been reduced to ruins and the fiddler to repetition, Silas stood on a crate by the crane arm and tapped a spoon against his cup until the noise settled.

"I'm bad at speeches," he said, to general agreement, shouted affectionately. "So I'll keep it short. A year ago I didn't know this child's name. A year ago none of us did. She came to us with nothing but a mark on a cloth and an order to forget her." He looked down at August, perched on Maren's hip. "The Halloway Company spent thirty years writing histories where people like her family didn't exist. Weather losses. Actuarial entries. Footnotes. And it worked, for thirty years, because the people who remembered stayed quiet and the people who wrote had no reason to stop."

He reached into the satchel.

"So this is her present. It isn't much. It's small, and it doesn't tell good time — Odile checked." A ripple of laughter; Odile raised her cup without denying it. "But it's honest, which is more than most clocks manage."

He brought it over and set it in Maren's hands so she could do the giving, because he understood, in the way he understood most things late but completely, that this gift was from all of them, and needed to come from the oldest of them.

It was a ship's clock, brass-cased, small enough to fit in a child's two hands. Its face was enameled white, its numerals crisp, and it ran — ticking steadily, absurdly — backward. The hands swept counterclockwise, unhurried, turning twelve into eleven, ten into nine, moving against every current a clock was supposed to obey.

"It runs backward," Silas told August, who grabbed it immediately and attempted to eat it. "Because time doesn't have to go only one direction. What happened can be un-happened, a little. Names that were erased can be carved back in. Lies written down can be written over. It takes longer than you want and costs more than anyone expects." He glanced at Maren, then at Odile, then at the crowd of weathered faces. "But it can be done. We're the proof. And someday, when someone tells her the record is closed, the books are balanced, nothing can be changed—" he took a breath, "—she can point at this clock and know better."

Maren looked down at the child, who had abandoned eating the clock in favor of watching its hands move the wrong way, transfixed, as if recognizing something kin.

"There now," Maren said softly, to August, or maybe to every diver the sea had taken, whose names stood carved at last on the memorial wall three piers over, including three the company had never bothered with. "See? Even the hours can turn around and come home."

* * *

Evening came the way it does on the water, all at once, the light going gold and then gone. Lanterns were lit along the tables. The fiddler switched to slow tunes. Children who should have been abed long ago slept in parents' laps while the adults talked in the low voices of people who have survived something together and are still deciding what it meant.

Silas found Maren at the rail, looking out at the black water.

"He'd be proud of you," she said. No need to say who. His father lived between them the way drowned men do, unnamed and everywhere.

Silas didn't answer right away. Below them, August slept in Odile's arms, the backward clock clutched against her chest even in sleep, its ticking a tiny stubborn heartbeat against the harbor's larger one.

"He dove waters he was told were safe," Silas said finally. "They weren't. That was the whole of it, and it took twenty years and a baby on a doorstep to say it out loud." He shook his head. "I used to think keeping my ledgers clean was the same as being clean. It wasn't. It was just quieter."

"Yes," Maren said. "That's exactly what it was."

He leaned on the rail beside her, shoulder touching hers, and for a long while they watched the lights of the fishing fleet come home.

"The archive takes statements Thursdays," he said at last, in a different voice, lighter, deliberately casual, the voice of a man building a bridge one plank at a time and pretending it's nothing. "Odile says we need a second transcriber. Says the shorthand's too much for one person, especially with the Corvessa man coming." A pause. "She may have mentioned my name."

"To whom?"

"To you."

Maren smiled at the dark water. "I'm seventy-one years old, boy."

"And you can read, and you remember everything, and you're the only living person who heard the original statements before Odile cleaned them up." He turned to look at her. "The archive shouldn't belong to two people anyway. Archives that belong to two people die with them. Archives that belong to a family—"

"—keep going," Maren finished.

Above them, in Odile's arms, August stirred, and laughed in her sleep — one small bright syllable, a sound none of them had taught her, arriving from wherever dreams come from, and the clock ticked on against her chest, turning backward, faithfully, toward morning.
