## 1. What Can Be Carried

The lamp on Ansel Amberdale's kitchen table had a cracked green shade, and under it every letter from Edith Marchmont turned the color of harbor water. Ansel had learned to read at that table as a child, and had learned, in the last two years, to read other people's words there as well — a different literacy entirely, one that required no schooling and could not be unlearned.

It was half past six on an October evening, and the letter was open.

Not torn open — that mattered. Tearing left a confession in the paper. Ansel used a thin knife, heated flat against the stove iron, lifting the flap with the patience of a man defusing something. The envelope would go down to the wax pot afterward, a drop of sealing wax rolled warm over the old seam, and by morning it would look like nothing more than what it pretended to be: correspondence from a suffragist to her sister in Harrogate, passed through the hands of her driver because her driver was reliable.

Reliable. Ansel turned the word over the way one turns a coin found on a pavement, checking both faces.

*Dear Winifred — The season closes around us like a hand. I have been asked to speak at the Guildhall on the fourteenth, and I mean to say the thing I said in Leeds, only harder...*

Ansel read it twice. That was the rule: twice for sense, once for anything that might matter later, though "later" was a country Ansel visited without ever quite believing in. The habit had started small, the way all corruptions do — a letter left open on the hall table at Cranbourne House, a name glimpsed, then a sentence, then whole paragraphs swallowed before conscience arrived too late with its lantern. Now the ritual was fixed. Steam, knife, read, reseal. Twenty minutes of theft committed so gently that even the stolen goods never knew they'd been gone.

The intimacy was the indecent part. Not the reading itself — half the country's post was being opened these days, men in grey offices with censors' stamps and no shame about it. No, the indecency was this: that Ansel knew Edith Marchmont better than anyone who claimed to love her. Knew that she wrote to her sister in a voice she used nowhere else, dry and tender at once, that she called the war *the noise* when she was tired and *the business* when she was furious, that she had written, eleven months ago, three pages about a stillborn niece and had never mentioned the child to a living soul since. Knew her jokes before they landed. Knew which causes she believed in absolutely and which she wore the way she wore her good coat — because the occasion required it.

You cannot unknow a person like that. You cannot hand them their umbrella in the morning, straight-faced, carrying all of it folded up inside you.

Ansel finished the second reading, marked nothing — never mark anything, memory is the only filing system that can't be seized — and held the envelope over the little spirit burner until the wax took. Green wax. She had chosen green years ago; it suited her, or she had decided it suited her, which amounted to the same thing. The seal came out slightly crooked. It always did. Ansel told himself that the crookedness was camouflage: nobody suspects a sloppy forgery of being one.

Outside, the street was doing its evening arithmetic — boots on cobbles, a milk cart, somewhere a wireless set murmuring bulletins to a family too poor to own one but too proud to admit whose window it leaked from. The news, when it came through the wall, was the usual litany. Another offensive with a name like a hymn. Another list. Ansel turned the lamp down and thought about pockets.

That was how the days were measured now, had been measured since spring: by what fit in a coat pocket. A pocket could hold a letter, a loaf, a tin of condensed milk, a folded blanket if the blanket was army-issue and thin as regret. It could hold a promise, provided the promise was made of practical things. It could not hold a person. Ansel had checked. Ansel had stood in front of the wardrobe mirror in the small hours, arms spread like a scarecrow, testing the give of the lining, and known with a driver's certainty that no amount of tailoring would make a coat large enough for what needed carrying out of this city.

The mirror had been honest. Mirrors usually are; it's people who lie professionally.

* * *

Morning came grey and smelling of coal smoke, and Ansel loaded the car outside Cranbourne House while the housekeeper watched from the step with the disapproval of a woman who considered motorcars a moral failing and drivers a venial one.

"Miss Marchmont says ten past nine," the housekeeper said. "She says don't be early."

"She says that every morning."

"And are you ever early?"

"Never," said Ansel, who was always early, who sat at the end of the lane with the engine off for six minutes exactly, learning to be punctual in reverse.

Edith Marchmont came down the steps at nine precisely, in a coat the color of wet slate, carrying her satchel and her face — which was to say, the public version of it, composed and faintly amused, the face of a woman who had been shouted at by crowds often enough to find crowds tedious. She was fifty-one and straight-backed and she got into the car the way she did everything, as if arriving were her idea.

"The Guildhall," she said. "And we'll go by the docks."

"Docks," Ansel repeated, and heard the word come out half a tone wrong.

Edith looked over. She missed very little, Edith Marchmont; it was one of the many things Ansel knew about her that she didn't know Ansel knew.

"There's a woman at the timber yard," Edith said. "Wife of a Latvian stoker whose ship was impounded. Three children. Her landlord has given her notice because the man hasn't been home in eight months and the authorities take that for desertion rather than unemployment. She wants help writing to the Board of Trade." A pause. "I want to see the state of the yard myself before I put her case in a speech. There's a difference between hearing about mud and standing in it."

"Yes, miss."

They went by the river road, and the city unspooled beside the water the way it did every morning — cranes standing against the sky like patient gallows, tugs grumbling at their moorings, the tide brown and busy. Bunting from some patriotic fête last spring still hung off a chandler's rail, faded to the pink of old scars. Posters on the hoardings, layered three deep: *YOUR COUNTRY NEEDS YOU*, and under that, newer, smaller, meaner, the ones that didn't shout but simply pointed. Ansel drove and did not look at the newest ones, and looked at them anyway.

"Amberdale." Edith, sorting papers in her lap. "The letters you take for me. Any trouble with the post?"

"No, miss. They seem to travel well."

"I imagine they do." She said it mildly, smoothing a page flat, and Ansel's hands made a small cold journey on the wheel. But she went on to something else — timings, a message to be taken to a printer — and the moment closed over like water over a dropped stone. Perhaps it had been nothing. With Edith, everything she said could be read twice, and Ansel had made a discipline of not reading her the way he read her letters, because a person could go mad doing that in the present tense.

At the timber yard the mud was indeed a thing to stand in, and the Latvian woman — Frau Ozolin, though she'd stopped correcting people years ago — met them in a doorway with a baby on her hip and two more children behind her skirts like a rear-guard action. Edith sat on an upturned crate without a flicker of concern for her coat and took dictation in a cheap exercise book, and Ansel stood by the car and watched the dockers watch them.

They watched Edith the way people watch weather. Curious, assessing. And then a group of them near the crane noticed something else, and the quality of the watching changed.

They were looking past the yard, down the track toward the old boathouse at the water's edge — the Marchmonts' boathouse, disused since the second winter of the war, its paint gone the grey of driftwood. Looking, and then not-looking, the quick sideways glance men use when there is something they've agreed not to see. One of them, older, with a face like a knuckle, spat into the mud and said something to another, too low to carry. The other laughed, but not warmly.

Ansel's heart performed a slow, deliberate turn, like a car taking a corner too fast.

Konrad. Konrad, who slept in the boathouse because in April a promise made in peacetime had come due, as promises sometimes do, wearing uniform and barbed wire and new laws with German words in them. Konrad, who was legally no longer a person but an enemy alien, registered, restricted, forbidden the coast — *forbidden the coast*, as if the sea itself could catch his accent. Konrad, whom Ansel had sworn passage to, back when the world was wide enough for such vows to cost nothing.

"He's not here," Ansel said to the nearest docker, pleasantly, following his gaze. "The boathouse? It's empty. Been empty two years. Rats, mostly."

The knuckle-faced man looked at Ansel for a long moment. Then he smiled, showing tobacco-stained teeth, and touched his cap in a way that was not respectful at all.

"'Course it is, sir," he said. "Empty as Sunday church."

* * *

On the drive back, Edith was quiet, composing, Ansel assumed — she composed in cars the way other women knitted. The city slid past. Somewhere ahead a newsboy was shouting, and the word he was shouting, repeated from mouth to mouth along the pavement like a spark down a fuse, was *peace*. Rumors again. They'd swept through twice already this autumn, false alarms, bells rung by mistake or mischief, strangers embracing in the streets and then going home embarrassed and hungover.

"They'll keep ringing the bells until the bells are true," Edith said at last, to the windscreen. "Or until someone starts a riot first. This city is a rope pulled tight, Amberdale. When it goes, it will go all at once, and not in the direction anyone hopes."

"Yes, miss."

She turned then and looked at him directly, and her eyes were the sharpest instruments Ansel had ever been on the wrong side of.

"You've been carrying something heavy lately," she said. "In your shoulders. I notice shoulders; it's a professional habit. I won't ask. But I'll tell you what I tell my sister when she writes to me at two in the morning worrying about things she won't name." She put her papers away. "Whatever you're carrying — decide soon whether you can carry it alone. Because things carried alone get heavier every week, and this country has stopped being kind to men with secrets. Do you understand me?"

"Yes, miss," Ansel said, and meant: *I understand you perfectly, and that is the problem.*

That evening, after the house was shut down and the lamps banked, Ansel walked the long way home along the water, past the chandler's and the impounded lighters, and stood where the mud flats began, looking at the boathouse. One shutter was open a hand's width. In the gap, briefly, a match flared and died — someone lighting a candle stub, someone who knew better than to let light show and couldn't quite help it.

Ansel waited until full dark, then crossed the flats, boots sinking, and put three things through the broken slat of the boathouse door: a heel of bread, a folded newspaper, and a note.

*Pell came to the house today,* the note said, which was true — a census man named Pell, clipboard and bowler and eyes like a man counting spoons, who had asked, politely, dreadfully, *and who sleeps under this roof?*

Ansel had lied to him. The lie had come out smooth enough, but Pell had written something down anyway, and smiled at whatever he wrote the way the docker had smiled, and now the bread and the newspaper seemed very small things to set against a clipboard.

Under the first line, Ansel added a second:

*We go before the week ends.*
