## 2. The Question Too Many

The census taker came on a Thursday, when the light was thin and the harbor smell had worked its way inland, and he arrived — as all the worst things in my life have arrived — politely, with a clipboard.

I was polishing the windscreen of the Marchmont motor when I saw him come up the drive: a small man in a good coat gone slightly shiny at the elbows, a leather board hanging from a strap around his neck like a peddler's tray, and on the board, papers. He paused at the gate to consult them, and I remember thinking, with the peculiar clarity that fear lends, *he is checking us against something*. As if we were already written down somewhere and he had only to confirm we still matched.

"Morning," he said. "Pell. Census." He said it the way other men say their rank, as though it were an office rather than an errand. He had a narrow face and eyes the color of dishwater, and he smiled with the bottom half of his face only.

"The housekeeper will see to you," I said, and turned back to the windscreen.

"It's the driver I'll want, actually." He consulted his papers again, unhurried. "Ansel Amberdale. Resides here?"

"On the premises, yes."

"Splendid. Then perhaps you can spare me the housekeeper's time and answer for the household. You'd know better than anyone who sleeps under this roof — eh?" Another half-smile. "Drivers see everything."

I should note here what I knew at that moment, because I did not know it honestly until later. In the boathouse at the foot of the Marchmont garden, forty yards of lawn and one stand of laurel away from where Mr. Pell stood licking his pencil, Konrad Reiss was sleeping off a night shift, wrapped in a tarpaulin among coiled rope and the smell of creosote, his name on a register in Whitehall under a heading I had read about in Edith's letters: *enemy alien*. And in my breast pocket, folded into eighths, was a letter in Edith Marchmont's hand that I had no right to carry and less right to have read.

"Go on then," Pell said, pencil poised. "Names. Everyone who slept in this house last night, yourself included."

"Mrs. Marchmont. Miss Alice Marchmont, her niece. Mrs. Tregenna, the cook. Ivy Dunn, housemaid. Myself." I recited it flatly, the way one recites a number plate.

Pell wrote it down slowly, enjoying the slowness. His pencil made small, dry sounds, like an insect in a wall. Then he looked up.

"And the boathouse?"

The word went into me like a splinter, clean and deep, before I even understood why it hurt. The boathouse was not part of any census question. Nobody counted boathouses. Boathouses held boats.

"What about it?" I said.

"Is it occupied?"

*Say no. Say it quickly.* A lie told fast is a fence; a lie told slow is a confession wearing a hat. I heard myself say, "It's not fit for occupation. Roof's gone at one corner. We keep junk in it."

"Mm." Pell wrote something. It was a short word; it took him two strokes, but he gave it three. "Junk," he repeated, pleasantly. "Funny thing, junk. It moves about. Comes and goes. I've had reports—" he tapped his clipboard— "of lights at odd hours along this stretch. Nothing official, you understand. Just neighbors being neighbors. Times like these, people like to be neighbors."

"There's no one in the boathouse," I said.

And there it was — my lie, out in the open air between us, and it landed wrong. Not wrong the way a dropped plate lands wrong, all at once. Wrong the way a key lands wrong in a lock: almost turning, catching, telling you by its resistance that the mechanism inside knows more than you do. Pell tilted his head. For a moment the pleasantness fell off him entirely and I saw the machinery underneath, which was not cruelty exactly but appetite — the modest, patient appetite of a small man given small power over large questions.

"I didn't say anyone was," he remarked. "I asked whether it was occupied. You've answered for the junk." He made another mark. "You're not local, Mr. Amberdale."

"From up-country."

"Name like yours. Amberdale. That's English enough." He said it as though conferring a favor. "There are names about that aren't, just now. You'd be surprised what turns up in boat-houses and coal sheds and lofts over stables. Whole families of them, practically. Folk who came here years ago and thought—" he smiled fully now, both halves— "thought they'd got away with it."

The wind came off the water and moved the laurel at the end of the lawn. Behind the house, down past the kitchen garden, the boathouse sat with its one sound corner and its one broken one, holding its breath as I was holding mine.

"You'll want to sign," Pell said, and turned the clipboard toward me.

I signed. My hand did it without consulting me, which is the mercy of hands. *Amberdale* came out in my ordinary scrawl, the same scrawl that signs for petrol and parcels, and I understood, watching the ink dry, that I had just sworn to something. That is the trick of paperwork: it never asks you to lie. It asks you to sign, and lets your own name do the lying afterward.

"Much obliged." Pell hung the clipboard back on its strap. At the gate he paused, and half-turned, and gave me the bottom-half smile once more. "I'll be round again before long. We do follow-ups, you know. First count's never the whole count. There's always someone sleeping where they shouldn't." He tipped his hat, actually tipped it, the courteous little monster. "Good morning to you."

I watched him all the way down the drive. He did not look back, and somehow that was worse than if he had. A man who looks back is wondering. A man who doesn't is certain he can return whenever he likes.

* * *

I did not go to the boathouse at once. That was deliberate, though my legs wanted otherwise. Instead I finished the windscreen, badly, leaving a haze at one corner; I carried a message up to the house for Mrs. Tregenna that could have waited a week; I stood in the hall while Ivy took my hat, and answered some remark of hers with a laugh I heard from outside myself, hollow as a knocked pipe. If anyone was watching from the road — and Pell had taught me in ten minutes to assume everyone was — let them see a driver about a driver's day.

Only when the light had gone gold and the streetlamps were being lit did I walk down through the garden, unhurried, whistling a tuneless thing, and let myself in through the gap in the laurel that Konrad had cut with his pocketknife in September and that we kept trimmed to look accidental.

He was awake. Of course he was awake; he always seemed to know when the world was speaking about him. He sat on the upturned dinghy with his coat buttoned to the throat, and the last light through the broken roof corner fell across his face in a bar, so that half of him was evening and half was shadow.

"Well?" he said. In his mouth, our language was still the old one, the peacetime one, the one we'd spoken across a garage counter in 1913 when he brought his motorbike in with a seized valve and stayed four hours talking while I worked.

"A census man came." I kept my voice low, though there was nothing alive nearer than the gulls. "Pell. He asked who slept here. I gave him the household. He asked about the boathouse."

Konrad didn't move, but something in him settled, the way ballast settles. "What did you tell him?"

"That it holds junk."

"Ja." He looked at his hands. They are dockworker's hands, broad as spades, and I have seen them gentle with a sparrow's wing. "And he believed you."

"He wrote it down."

"That is not the same thing." He said it without reproach, which made it heavier. Down at the harbor, faintly, a winch started up and quit again, the sound carrying over the wall the way sounds do at dusk. "In Germany, when I was a boy, there was a man who counted us. Every few years. He wore glasses and he apologized the whole time, and my mother fed him cake, and afterward we were counted, anyway. Cake does not stop the counting." He looked up. "Ansel. If he comes back—"

"He won't find you."

"If he comes back, I will go to the police myself and say I am here. Better one man registered than a family fined, deported—" he used the English word with care, tasting its ugliness— "or worse. I am not worth a household, Ansel."

"You promised to stop saying that."

"And you promised me passage out, if things ever turned." He said it mildly, an old coin produced from an old pocket. "Things have turned."

I crouched down level with him, on the cold boards, and smelled the tar and the salt-rot and the river in everything, and I thought: this is what a promise looks like when the world re-prices it. In 1913 it cost an afternoon and a favor owed to a second mate. Now it cost contraband passage, forged papers I couldn't make, a harbor full of men looking for exactly his face, and the gallows-math of what happened to drivers caught smuggling enemy aliens out of port towns. But a promise isn't priced by the promiser. That's the whole indecency of promises. You set their worth when you make them and pay whatever they cost when they're called in.

"He comes back," I said. "That's how they work, the follow-ups. He said so himself. So we don't wait for his schedule." I heard my own voice go flat and certain, and recognized it as Edith's voice, borrowed. "Before the week's out, Konrad. I'll get you to a ship."

He studied me for a long moment, half in light, half out of it, and then he nodded once, the way he accepted a repair bill — trusting arithmetic he couldn't check.

Afterward I walked back up through the darkening garden, and at the top, before the house, I stopped. Through the study window I could see Edith Marchmont at her desk, lamp lit, pen moving, drafting tomorrow's letters — letters I would fold into my coat pocket tonight, unfold under my attic skylight, and read.

And standing there in the cold, I understood that Mr. Pell had not really come for the household at all. He had asked his question too many, and watched which one of us flinched.

He would be back for the flinch.
