## 3. A Promise Made in Peacetime

The boathouse smelled of tar and old rope and the particular cold of water that has never once been warm. Ansel came down the plank path after dark, carrying a lantern in one hand and a paper sack in the other — bread, a heel of cheese, two apples, and, wrapped separately at the bottom where it couldn't be seen, a newspaper with the relevant column cut out.

Konrad was sitting on an upturned crate when the light found him, his long legs folded awkwardly, his hands open on his knees as though he had been waiting to be arrested and had decided, in the meantime, to be comfortable about it. He was a big man gone soft at the edges from weeks of idleness, with a face that had been weathered brown by twenty years of North Sea work and now, indoors, was slowly fading like a photograph left in a drawer.

"You cut it out," he said. Not a question. He could hear it in the way Ansel held the sack.

"I didn't want you reading it twice."

"Once is enough for any man." Konrad took the bread first. He always took the bread first; there was something in him that distrusted charity that arrived in the wrong order. "Read it to me anyway."

Ansel sat down on the second crate, set the lantern between them so their shadows leaned together against the boat slung overhead, and unfolded the scrap of newsprint as if it might go off.

"'Register of Enemy Aliens,'" he read. "'All persons of German or Austro-Hungarian birth resident within the borough are required to present themselves at the police station not later than the fourteenth instant, for the purposes of registration, enumeration, and such restrictions upon movement and employment as may be directed—'"

"Restrictions," Konrad said, tasting the word. "That's a polite word. In German we would say *Verbote*. Prohibitions. It is the same word they used for dogs." He tore the bread and did not eat it. "You know what happens after registration. They take your photograph. They give you a card. The card says where you may walk and when you may walk there and what you may not do, which is everything you have done your whole life. And then one day the card says come to the station again, and this time you don't come back."

"That's not happening here."

"It happened here before you were born, to people whose names nobody wrote down because writing it down was the crime." Konrad looked at him across the lantern light, and his eyes were patient in the way of men who have been patient all their lives and gotten nothing for it. "Ansel. You promised me passage. Five years ago, on a night very much like this one. Do you remember what you said?"

Ansel remembered. That was the trouble. He remembered everything; it was both his gift and his affliction, the same faculty that made him useful to Edith Marchmont and dangerous to himself.

* * *

It had been June of 1913, the summer the harbor smelled of paint and possibility, before anyone had thought to measure the water for what it might carry. Ansel was nineteen and driving for the Marchmonts already, and on that particular evening he had been sent down to the quay to collect a crate of books Mrs. Marchmont had ordered from Hamburg — she bought her German editions where they were cheapest, and no one had yet decided that buying a book in German was a small act of treason.

The stevedore who loaded the crate into the motor car was Konrad Vogel, then thirty-one, with forearms like mooring posts and a laugh that carried across three berths. He worked for the shipping line, lived in a room above a chandler's shop, and sent half his wages to a sister in Bremen whom he described, whenever asked, as "too stubborn to need it and too proud to say so."

They got to talking because the crate was heavy and the afternoon was hot, and by the time it was stowed Ansel had learned that Konrad had come to England at seventeen with nothing but a seaman's papers and his father's blessing, and had never once been back, and missed it the way you miss a tooth — not constantly, but sharply, at meals.

"And if it ever turns," Ansel had said — he couldn't remember why he'd said it, except that young men say things like that on hot afternoons, promises being free — "if things ever go wrong here for you. I'd drive you anywhere. To any port. Myself."

Konrad had laughed, that big harbor laugh. "And what will you drive, little clerk? A wheelbarrow?"

"A motor car. Mrs. Marchmont's. She lets me take it out on Sundays."

"Then I will hold you to your Sunday." Konrad had spat ceremonially into the water, seaman-fashion, and shaken on it, his hand swallowing Ansel's whole. "A promise made in peacetime. Those are the only ones worth anything, you know. Promises made in wartime are just tactics. But a man who promises you passage while the sun shines—" he tapped his own chest, twice, over the heart, "—he means it in the blood."

* * *

"Now," Konrad said, in the boathouse, five years older and five years poorer in every currency but memory, "the sun does not shine. And still you are here."

"Of course I'm here."

"Do not say 'of course.' Nothing is 'of course' anymore. Of course stopped existing in August of 1914." He ate some of the bread at last, methodically, a man fueling rather than dining. "I want to tell you something, and I want you to listen without arguing, which I know is difficult for you."

Ansel said nothing, which was his version of agreeing.

"The registration. I will not do it."

"Konrad—"

"No. Listen. If I register, I become a name on a list, and lists are how they feed you into the machine without spilling anything. If I do not register, I am a rumor, and rumors can be smuggled. You cannot smuggle a man who has signed his own name." He brushed crumbs from his knee with great care, as though tidiness were the last citizenship available to him. "So. Passage. You promised me a Sunday drive. I am asking you now to make it a longer Sunday than either of us planned."

"To where?"

"The harbor. Any neutral hull that will take a hand in exchange for a berth. There are tramp steamers still sailing under flags nobody hates yet. A man who can work cargo can buy his way aboard with his back." He smiled, and it was the old smile, or nearly — worn thin at the creases, but the same cloth. "I have been thinking about it for weeks. I have had nothing else to think about. It is remarkable how much planning a man can do in a boathouse."

"They're watching the docks. Pell told me — told Mrs. Marchmont — there are men at every gate now, checking papers."

"Then we will not use the gates." Konrad said it the way he said everything, as though the difficulty were merely weather. "There is a mudflat below the coal yard, east of the dry dock. At low tide a man can cross it, if he doesn't mind being filthy and afraid. I have crossed worse flats in worse weather for less reason."

"And if the tide comes in?"

"Then I will be filthy, afraid, and drowned, and you will be spared further trouble." He said it lightly, and then, seeing Ansel's face, put his big hand briefly on Ansel's shoulder — the same shoulder he'd gripped in 1913, though neither of them remarked on it. "Listen to me. What I did for you, in the old days — carrying your letters, teaching you the knots, telling you which foremen were honest — I did it because you were a boy who listened. Now you are a man who remembers. Between those two things, we will find a ship."

Ansel wanted to say yes immediately, cleanly, the way promises are made in peacetime. But he was twenty-four now, and he had spent a year reading Edith Marchmont's correspondence by lamplight, and he knew what he had learned there: that every yes costs something, and the cost is never paid by the person saying it alone.

"Give me until Friday," he said. "I need to learn the tide tables. And I need to know whether Pell's men watch the coal yard at night or only by day."

"Pell." Konrad pronounced the name like something scraped off a boot. "Your census man."

"He's more than a census man now. He enjoys it. That's what frightens me — not the questions, the enjoyment."

"Yes." Konrad nodded slowly. "I have met his kind in every country. The small man who finally holds a stamp. Be careful of him, Ansel. Men like that don't want to catch you. They want to catch you *trying*. It proves them right about the world."

* * *

Ansel walked back up the plank path with the lantern doused and the empty sack folded flat inside his coat, and stood a moment in the dark looking back at the boathouse — a black shape among black shapes, indistinguishable from the sheds and the gorse and the slope of the headland, which was precisely its virtue. Somewhere behind that wall a man was sleeping on a sail locker cushion, declared by Act of Parliament to be an enemy, and known to Ansel as the man who had taught him to tie a bowline one hot afternoon in 1913 because the boy kept getting it wrong and laughing at himself.

Five years ago the promise had cost nothing. It had been a coin tossed in a fountain, a wish wearing the costume of a vow. Now the same coin, turned over, showed its other face: *aiding the enemy*, *harboring*, *concealment* — words with numbers attached, numbers with cells attached. Kindness, it turned out, was subject to revaluation like any other currency, and the exchange rate since 1914 had been ruinous.

He thought of Edith's letters upstairs in his coat lining, and of the lie he had told Pell at the door — *no one else sleeps under this roof, sir* — and how the lie had landed wrong, the way a stone lands wrong on ice, and how Pell's pen had paused, just for a moment, before writing it down.

Pell would come back. Men like Pell always came back; it was the coming back that was the job.

Friday, then. Tide tables tomorrow. And between now and Friday, Ansel Amberdale would have to do the thing he was best at and most afraid of: memorize exactly what mattered, and decide what he was willing to lose.

When he let himself into the Marchmont house through the kitchen door, the hall clock was striking eleven, and on the newel post, folded once and weighted with a brass dish, lay a note in Edith Marchmont's upright hand:

*Mr. Amberdale — Come to my study before you retire. There is a matter concerning the census that we must discuss.*

She knew. Or she suspected. Either way, the arithmetic was about to begin.
