## 4. Edith's Arithmetic

Edith Marchmont had learned long ago that a house keeps accounts of its own.

The Marchmont house on Wren Street had been her grandmother's, then her mother's, and now hers — three generations of women who had each, in their turn, believed the walls were neutral. Edith knew better. The house remembered everything: which floorboard sighed under weight, which window stuck in damp weather, which lamp in the study burned with a slightly greener light than the others because of some flaw in its glass that no glazier had ever been able to name. It was under that greenish lamp that she did her real work, and it was that lamp she came downstairs to extinguish one Tuesday night in October when she found the letters out of order.

Not opened. Nothing so crude as that. But the stack on her desk — correspondence awaiting her signature before tomorrow's delivery run — had been squared differently than she'd left it. She was precise about such things; precision was the whole architecture of her life. A woman who could not keep her papers straight could not keep a movement straight, and a movement that could not keep itself straight would be folded up by Parliament like a bad argument. She had left the stack with the thickest envelope third from the top. It now sat fifth. Someone had gone through them and put them back with care.

She stood in the doorway a long moment, coat still buttoned, gloves still on, and felt the particular cold that comes not from weather but from understanding.

Ansel.

She had hired the driver in June because Ansel Amberdale drove beautifully and asked no questions — or appeared to. Quiet hands on the wheel, quiet eyes on the road, a way of being present without imposing presence, which was rarer than driving skill and worth more. The movement needed people who could be trusted to carry things: leaflets, funds, letters, sometimes women past police lines. Trust, in Edith's experience, was not a feeling but a system. You built it out of small verifications, redundancies, the assumption that everyone might fail you eventually and the design of your operations around that assumption. She had designed around Ansel's failure too. She had simply never expected the failure to look like this — not theft, not betrayal, but *reading*.

She crossed the room and lifted the top letter. Her own hand, addressed to Mrs. Calloway in Leeds. Sealed. She turned it over. The flap had been steamed and re-stuck; there was a faint waviness to the paper that only someone who handled two hundred envelopes a month would notice, and Edith noticed everything, which was both her gift and, she suspected, the reason she slept badly.

So. For how long? Weeks, certainly. Perhaps since summer.

She should have been furious. She catalogued the fury, found it present but thin, and beneath it something more troubling: a reluctant, professional respect. To steam an envelope open and reseal it well enough to fool her required patience, steady hands, and an intimate knowledge of her habits. Ansel had studied her the way she studied the Home Office. There was a mirror in that, and mirrors were never comfortable.

*What did you read?* she thought. *And what did you do with it?*

Because that was the real question. Reading was vanity — the intimacy of other people's words, the little thrill of knowing what Mrs. Calloway really thought of the Yorkshire committee. Edith could almost forgive vanity; she had plenty of her own. But if Ansel had read the letters, Ansel knew things. Names. Routes. The safe houses in Hull. The fact that "the milliner" meant a magistrate's clerk who sold information, and that "the shipment of hats" meant forty pounds in banknotes sewn into a coat lining. If any of that left this house — if it were extracted, or sold, or simply let slip in a moment of fear — the movement would not survive the winter. People would go to prison. Not the famous ones, whose trials made banners; the small ones, the typists and treasurers, the women with children, whose names never made the papers and whose sentences were always longer.

Edith sat down at her desk and did what she always did with a problem: she reduced it to terms.

One: Ansel had access and had abused it, but had apparently not yet betrayed it.

Two: dismissal solved nothing. A dismissed driver who knew the routes was a loose cannon rolling around the deck; a kept driver was a known quantity under observation. This was arithmetic, not sentiment.

Three — and here her pen paused above the blotter — there was the matter of the boathouse.

She had known about Konrad for eleven days.

She had discovered it the way she discovered most things: by noticing what didn't add up. The coal consumption at Wren Street had risen. Ansel had begun taking the long way home along the river on certain evenings, a route that served no delivery purpose. And once, passing the boathouse at the end of the lane below the garden, she had seen smoke where no smoke should be — a thin thread rising from the chimney of a structure that was supposed to be empty, in a city where an unexplained fire was an accusation waiting to be made.

She had gone down herself, alone, at dusk, and knocked.

The man who opened the door was tall and stooped at once, as though he had spent years trying to take up less space than his body required. German-born — she saw it immediately, in the set of the mouth, the careful English assembled word by word like furniture from a flat crate. He had said, "I am sorry, I will go," before she had said anything at all, and the *sorry* had been so worn smooth with use that it must have been the first word of his life for five years running.

Enemy alien. Registered, she assumed, or not registered, which was worse. Dockworker. And hidden — fed, warmed, sheltered — by her own driver, on her own property, without her knowledge, in the autumn of 1918, when a neighbor's suspicion was all it took to bring a mob or a magistrate.

She had looked at him for perhaps ten seconds. He had looked back the way animals look at you from inside a trap: not pleading, exactly. Braced.

"I haven't decided what I've seen," she had told him, and walked away.

That had been eleven days ago, and she had spent those eleven days doing the thing she was best at in the world, which was counting.

Here is what Edith counted, that night, under the greenish lamp, with the tampered letters squared neatly in front of her:

The movement had perhaps four hundred active members across the north. Of those, perhaps sixty were load-bearing — the ones whose arrest would collapse a section, the ones who knew the others. Edith was one. Ansel, unknowingly, had become one, by virtue of knowing everything a driver knows. Sixty lives, four hundred dependents, a cause that had already buried two of its best women in prison cells and would bury more before the war ground itself out. Against this: one man in a boathouse, whose name Edith didn't even know until she'd asked Ansel point-blank last week and received a lie so transparent it was almost touching. *A cousin,* Ansel had said. *Visiting.* With a face like that, visiting, in October, from nowhere.

One against sixty. One against four hundred. Any honest accountant would close the ledger.

But Edith had been counting lives selectively for so long that she had begun, lately, to hear the sound the selection made — a small dry click, like a lock turning, each time she set someone outside the count. The women they couldn't afford to bail out. The witnesses they couldn't afford to protect. The working-class girls whose arrests were useful, actually, *useful*, because juries softened when the defendant looked young and poor, whereas a middle-aged widow's imprisonment was merely tragic and moved no one. She had made those calculations in committee rooms with tea going cold, and she had told herself every time that the cause was larger than the person, and every time the click had sounded, and every time she had chosen not to listen to it.

Righteousness, she thought, is just power that has learned better manners. It still counts. It still selects. It simply does its arithmetic in the language of justice, which makes the sums easier to sign.

Konrad was outside the count. That was the honest entry in the ledger. His safety was not load-bearing for anything except — and here her pen stopped again — except the promise of a driver who had once, in peacetime, told a friend *if things ever turn, I'll get you out*, and who had apparently intended to honor it even after the world re-priced kindness as contraband.

There was something in that promise that Edith recognized and envied. Her own commitments had all been made to abstractions. *The Cause. The Vote. Women Yet Unborn.* Abstractions were wonderful debtors; they never aged, never got sick, never stood in a doorway looking braced. Konrad was not an abstraction. Konrad was a man who said *I am sorry, I will go* to a woman holding all the power in the exchange, and Edith had walked away carrying that sentence ever since, the way you carry a stone in your shoe that you refuse to stop and remove because removing it would mean admitting you'd been limping.

She blew out the lamp. The study went the color of deep water.

Her options arranged themselves with terrible clarity. Report Ansel and the lodger both: clean, defensible, fatal to two people and possibly to the movement anyway, since Pell — the census man, the one with the enjoying-himself eyes — had already come sniffing twice, and paperwork, once started, did not stop politely. Dismiss Ansel quietly and evict the lodger: marginally cleaner, equally fatal, and it would put a desperate man and a wounded driver out into a city full of mobs with nothing left to lose. Or —

Or she could fold them into the count.

Not all of them. She was not a sentimentalist; the movement could not carry every fugitive in England. But these two — these specific two, already inside her walls, already holding her secrets — these she could shelter, provided she controlled the terms. Provided the risk was managed, contained, converted from accident into policy. That was the difference between recklessness and strategy: paperwork. Even mercy needed a filing system.

It would cost her. She could already see the shape of the cost. If it surfaced — when it surfaced — every enemy of the suffrage bill would have her: *the woman who harbored an enemy alien while demanding the franchise.* They would make her the story instead of the argument. Thirty years of work, weighed against one dockworker and one driver, and the scales were not kind.

Edith sat in the dark and understood, finally, what she had understood dimly for years: that the movement had taught her to count, and that the only question left was whether she could still, on occasion, refuse to.

She heard the front door below — Ansel, bringing the motor round for the evening collection, punctual as ever, guilty as sin, loyal beyond sense. Footsteps in the hall. The pause outside the study door, the held breath of someone deciding whether to knock.

Edith rose, smoothed her skirt, and composed her face into the expression she used for cabinet ministers and bishops: pleasant, unreadable, faintly amused, as though she already knew how the conversation would end and was simply waiting for them to catch up.

"Come in, Ansel," she called, before the knock could land. "We need to talk about your cousin."

* * *

The door opened slowly, the way doors open when the person behind them is calculating whether the hallway offers a better future than the room.

Ansel stood in the frame with cap in hand — a formality Edith had never demanded and which therefore meant more, not less. In the lamplight Ansel's face went through its whole small theater: blankness, assessment, the flicker toward denial, the collapse of it.

"You knew," Ansel said.

"I know most things that happen under my roof. It's the roof's one talent." Edith gestured to the chair across the desk. "Sit down. I'm not going to ask whether he's your cousin, because we'd both waste time pretending to believe the answer. What I am going to ask is how much coal he's using, how often Pell's men pass the lane, and whether you understand that the boathouse chimney is visible from the garden path on a clear evening."

Ansel sat. The shoulders came down half an inch — not relief, Edith noted, but the lowering of a guard that would go back up the moment she said the wrong thing.

"You're not dismissing me," Ansel said. It wasn't a question either. It was a man checking the floor for traps.

"I am considering approximately nine courses of action, and I have not yet finished my arithmetic." She laced her fingers on the blotter. "But you should know that I have known about him for eleven days, and in eleven days I have reported nothing, and you may draw from that whatever conclusion lets you sleep. Now." She pulled a sheet of paper toward her and uncapped her pen. "Tell me about the harbor. Which piers, which masters, which ships are still flying neutral colors. Because if we're going to commit a crime, Ansel, we are going to do it properly — and I want to know why Mr. Pell has taken such a sudden interest in my coal bills."

Outside, the wind off the river carried the smell of coming rain, and somewhere down the street a newsboy was shouting about peace — shouting it the way boys shout things they don't believe, hopeful and hoarse and already apologizing for it.
