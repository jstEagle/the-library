## 5. The Census Taker Calls Again

He came back on a Thursday, when the light went thin early and the gulls had begun to claim the river for the night.

Ansel saw him first from the kitchen window — Mr. Pell, crossing the street at his unhurried clerk's pace, umbrella furled though there was no rain yet, only the promise of it. He wore the same gray coat as before and carried the same leather folder, and he paused at the gate to consult something inside it, though Ansel would have bet a week's wages he already knew what it said. Pell was a man who consulted papers the way priests consulted scripture: not for information, but for the pleasure of being seen consulting.

"Miss Marchmont's house," Pell said at the door, though it wasn't a question, and Ansel thought: *he knows the door now. Last time he asked which it was.*

"Miss Marchmont is out. A meeting."

"So she is." Pell smiled at that, small and satisfied, as if her absence were itself an entry in his ledger. "I shan't keep you long, then. I find these things go more smoothly with the household help, anyway. Less — " he weighed the word delicately — "*arrangement*."

*Arrangement.* The word sat in Ansel's stomach like a swallowed coin.

They stood in the front hall because Pell did not remove his hat and did not seem to expect to be invited further. He opened the folder on the hall table with two hands, reverently, and smoothed the census schedule flat.

"Now. When I called in September, I recorded four persons sleeping under this roof. Miss Edith Marchmont, proprietor. Her sister, Mrs. Havel. Yourself — Ansel Amberdale, chauffeur and general man. And the cook, Mrs. Dunmore." He looked up. His eyes were pale and patient, the eyes of a man who could wait out weather. "That remains correct?"

"That remains correct."

"Mm." Pell drew a pencil from behind his ear, made no mark, and set it down again, point toward himself, like a knife laid aside but not gone. "Curious thing about schedules. They accumulate annotations. I've been doing the rounds of this district nine years, Mr. Amberdale, and I'll tell you what September taught me." He tapped the paper once. "Every house has its arithmetic. Four in the schedule, five hats on the pegs. Three names, six cups drying. You'd be astonished how often the crockery gives people away."

Ansel kept his face still. Behind his eyes, unbidden: the boathouse, the tin plate washed and turned upside down on the sill, Konrad's boots standing toes-out by the door like a schoolboy's. Small habits. Kindnesses leaving fingerprints everywhere.

"There are six cups," Ansel said carefully, "because Mrs. Dunmore drops them."

Pell laughed. It was a genuine laugh, warm even, and somehow worse for that. "I'm sure she does. Now here's my difficulty." He turned the schedule around so Ansel could read it, which felt obscene — seeing your own life rendered in another man's pencil. "Regulation 14B. Enemy aliens must register; those who shelter them, knowingly or through *negligence*, become liable under DORA. And the harbor district, Mr. Amberdale — this district — has more German-born men per street than anywhere north of London. Dockworkers. Bakers. Men who've been here twenty years and whose boys are in French mud wearing our uniform." His voice softened into something almost like regret. "Paperwork doesn't care about any of that. That's the beauty of paperwork, if you think about it. It doesn't need proof. It needs only a question left unanswered."

"I answered all your questions."

"You did. Very promptly." Pell closed the folder. "Which is why I've come back. In my experience, Mr. Amberdale, prompt answers are like clean boots — worth looking underneath."

The clock in the hall ticked. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard settled. Ansel heard every sound in the house at once, the way you do when you realize the house is listening too.

"Is there anything else?" he said. "I have the car to see to before dark."

"Of course, of course." Pell tucked the folder under his arm and moved to the door, then paused on the threshold as if remembering a courtesy. "One thing more. Purely social." He glanced back, and for a moment the clerkish mildness fell away entirely, and Ansel saw the thing beneath it — not malice exactly, but appetite. The plain animal satisfaction of a man who is good at his work and knows it. "The boathouses along the Marchmont waterline. Whose are they, precisely? Deeds and such. Only I've been walking the foreshore of an evening — doctor's orders, fresh air — and I notice things. A light, once. A boat that shifts its mooring. Nothing." He raised a hand, dismissing his own suspicion like a conjurer making a card vanish while daring you to say where. "Probably nothing. But I find nothing is very rarely nothing. Good evening, Mr. Amberdale."

The gate clicked behind him. Ansel watched from the window until Pell turned the corner, unhurried, umbrella swinging, a man with all the time in the world because the world worked for him now — not guns, not proof, just forms and evenings and patience.

Then Ansel went to the scullery and stood with his hands braced on the sink until they stopped shaking.

* * *

Edith came home at seven, smelling of coal smoke and other people's arguments, and found Ansel waiting in the study with the lamp lit low. She took one look at him and put down her bag.

"Pell."

"How did you — "

"You've got the face of a man who's been read his rights." She unpinned her hat and sat, gesturing for him to do the same, though he stayed standing; standing was easier when you might have to run. "Tell me everything. Exactly."

He gave it to her straight: the schedule, Regulation 14B, the crockery, the foreshore walks, *a light, once*. Edith listened without interrupting, fingers laced, and Ansel watched her do what she always did — convert fear into data, data into plan — and hated how much comfort it gave him, and needed it anyway.

"He's building a file," she said at last. "Not a case. Cases need evidence. Files need only time and ink. He'll come back with a warrant or he'll come back with nothing, and either way he enjoys coming back." She said it flatly, without drama, and that flatness frightened Ansel more than shouting would have. "How long since Konrad's been in the boathouse?"

"Since the registration order. Eleven weeks."

"And the steamer situation?"

"Dead. The *Vesna* was requisitioned three weeks ago — I told you. There's nothing sailing neutral out of this harbor till spring, maybe longer."

Edith was quiet. Rain had finally started against the study glass, soft and steady, erasing the garden. When she spoke again her voice had changed — lower, stripped of platform polish, the voice she used perhaps once a month and never in public.

"Ansel. If Pell finds him, he doesn't only take Konrad. He takes this house. My correspondence, my lists, forty women's names, everything — because a house that harbored an enemy alien will be searched to its foundations, and no magistrate in this county will lose sleep over it. Do you understand what I'm saying?"

"I understand you're counting."

"Yes." No flinch. "I am counting. It's what I'm *for*. And the count says one man's safety against everything we've built for ten years." She held his gaze. "But the count also says this: Konrad Fischer mended my father's boats before your mother was born, and I am not the woman who hands a man to Pell to save my filing cabinets. So don't ask me whether we keep him. Ask me instead — " and here her mouth curved, tired and sharp — "how quickly we can make him someone else's problem. Someone farther away."

Something loosened in Ansel's chest, and immediately re-tightened into something heavier. Not relief. Assignment.

"Before the week ends," he said.

"Before the week ends," she agreed. "And Ansel — the car stays in the garage at night now. Walk him. Move him in rain, in fog, when the streets are empty. And whatever you're planning—" she rose, took up the lamp, and for a moment her face floated above him, half-lit, resolute, "—don't tell me the details. If I don't know it, I can't be made to say it."

She paused at the door.

"And burn the letters you shouldn't have, dear. I know about those too."

* * *

That night Ansel walked down through the orchard in the dark, taking the long way, checking the lane twice, feeling the whole city rearrange itself around him into a map of watchers. Every lit window was a witness. Every footstep behind him was Pell, or worse, Pell's superior, Pell's colleague, the great patient machinery of forms and files grinding on regardless of guilt or innocence, needing no proof, wanting none — proof was slow; paperwork was *forever*.

The boathouse door gave its usual groan. Inside, by the shielded flame of a hurricane lamp, Konrad sat mending a net he could no longer legally use, his big hands moving with the unconscious grace of thirty years at the water. He looked up. He'd been expecting bad news; Ansel could see it in the way his shoulders didn't drop.

"Well," Konrad said. "Say it."

"We move you. Before Sunday. Away from the water first — the quarry road, the old bothy above Harrowgate — then on from there. Overland. It'll be cold and it'll be slow and I won't lie to you about the odds."

Konrad considered the net in his lap, turning a knot between his fingers. Outside, the rain thickened, drumming on the boat below them like fingers on a desk.

"Eleven weeks I've sat in this box watching the tide do my job for me," he said quietly. "You should have moved me in September, boy."

"I know."

"No. You didn't. Now you do." He began, methodically, to coil the net. "Sunday, then. Tell me what to bring."

What to bring. Ansel thought of coat pockets, of everything a life could be reduced to when it had to fit through a doorway fast. One bag. Papers that lied. A name that wasn't his.

Everything, Ansel realized, that could be carried.

Down the hill behind him, faint through the rain, a bell was ringing — the church, the harbor chapel, some hour he'd lost count of — and it sounded less like a call to prayer than like a countdown already begun.
