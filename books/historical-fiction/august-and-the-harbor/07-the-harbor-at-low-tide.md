## 7. The Harbor at Low Tide

The tide was going out when we came down to the harbor, and it took the light with it.

We had walked from the Marchmont house in the blue hour before dawn, Konrad and I, moving through streets still wet from the night's rain, and he had carried nothing but the coat on his back and a canvas bag I'd packed for him — spare shirt, bread, a tin of sardines, Edith's forty pounds sewn into the lining where no customs man would think to pat. He walked like a man who had practiced being invisible for years and had only now been told he must be good at it. Shoulders drawn in. Eyes down. A big man making himself small, which is a thing that shames them, I learned. Konrad Voss could not walk past his own reflection without apologizing to it.

"The *Elsinore Star*," I said, for the third time, because saying it made it true. "Danish flag. She takes on coal Thursday and sails with Friday's tide. The second mate owes a favor to a man who owes a favor to Mr. Whitlock at the dock office, and Mr. Whitlock does not ask what favors are for."

"And if he asks anyway?"

"Then you're a Dane going home to Copenhagen, and your Danish is very poor because you've been away so long."

Konrad made a sound that was almost a laugh. "My Danish is very poor because it does not exist."

"That's what 'away so long' means."

We came out onto the quays where the gulls were already arguing over whatever the night tide had left them, and I looked for her — the tramp steamer, black-sided, salt-scabbed, flying the colors of a country small enough to mind its own business — and she was not there.

I told myself, first, that I had the wrong berth. That is what the mind does; it reaches for the smallest lie and holds it up like a lamp. I counted berths. I counted cranes. I stood there with my hands in my pockets doing arithmetic against the whole gray harbor, as though the ship would be embarrassed into appearing by the correctness of my sums.

"She isn't here," Konrad said. He said it quietly, the way he said most things, as though even his voice might be requisitioned.

"She'll be along."

"Ansel." He touched my arm, once, lightly. He was not a man who touched. "Look at the berth."

I looked. There was a collier in her place — a fat, ugly British vessel with naval grey paint slapped over her merchant black and a white ensign drooping at her stern, and men aboard her who moved with the particular hurry of men working under orders rather than wages. A board nailed to the bollard by the gangway said, in a clerk's careful hand: **REQUISITIONED BY ORDER OF THE ADMIRALTY**.

The date on the notice was six days old.

Six days. All week I had been carrying this morning in my pocket like a coin, taking it out to touch whenever Pell's visit got too loud in my head, and the coin had been counterfeit the entire time. Somewhere in an office I would never see, a man with better handwriting than mine had signed a paper and a Danish tramp steamer had become His Majesty's coal-hauler, and nobody had thought to tell the harbor master's clerk, and nobody had thought to tell me.

"I'm sorry," I said. It was a stupid thing to say. Sorry is what you say when you've spilled tea.

Konrad was looking at the collier with an expression I hadn't seen on him before. Not despair. Something more patient and more terrible. "She flies the white ensign," he said. "That is a warship's flag, yes? On a coal ship."

"Yes."

"Then they are taking everything." He said it without drama, the way you'd note the weather. "Ships. Men. Names." He looked down at his own hands, those great dockworker's hands, scarred from twenty years of other people's cargo. "In 1913 I carried sacks on these quays and nobody asked what was inside me."

I wanted to tell him we would find another ship. There were always other ships; that was practically the definition of a harbor. But the words wouldn't come out clean, because while I'd been staring at the requisition notice, some part of me that never sleeps had been counting the men on the quay. Loafers by the fish shed who weren't loafing. Two by the customs shed. One walking, unhurried, toward the far end of the quay and then stopping, and turning, and walking back — the pace of a man on a beat, a man being paid by the hour to have nowhere in particular to go.

They were too well-fed for stevedores and too bored for policemen, and one of them wore a collar I recognized, because I had seen it across my own doorstep with a clipboard in its hands.

Pell's men.

"Pell knows about the docks," I said. "He's watching them. All of them, maybe. Or maybe just watching, and hoping something walks into his net so he can write it down."

Konrad turned slowly, following my eyes, and I watched him do the same arithmetic I had done. He was better at it than me. He had been counting exits since 1914.

"So," he said. "No ship."

"Not today."

"And men watching the water." He nodded slowly. "Then I will go to them."

It took me a moment to understand what he meant, and when I did, the cold went into me not like winter but like water — sudden, total, filling the boots.

"No."

"It is simple, Ansel." He said my name carefully, the way he said all English names, as though translating on the fly. "I go to the customs shed. I say: I am Konrad Voss, enemy alien, late of Bremen, and I am tired of hiding in a boathouse like a rat. They put me in a camp on the Isle of Man, perhaps. There is barbed wire and there is bad tea and there are other Germans, and I wait until the war ends, and then—" He spread his hands, presenting the future like a tray. "And then. If I am still—"

"They interned the men at Knockaloe in tents, Konrad. Through the winter. And the mobs outside the camps—"

"I know what the mobs do. I read the papers you bring me." For a moment something flared in him, the old fire, the man who had argued politics on the quay in 1913 with coal dust in his lungs. Then it guttered. "But it is not only me, Ansel. You see that. Every day I am in that boathouse, you are carrying me. In your coat. Under your name. That woman upstairs in your house — the Missus — she sees you going out at night with covered pots, and she says nothing, but one day she will say something, or Pell will knock on *her* door with his little questions, and then everyone falls who has ever been kind to either of us. I did not escape drowning to pull other people under with me."

"You didn't escape anything," I said. "You were born in Bremen. That's not a crime, it's an accident of geography, and this country has gone mad enough to punish accidents—"

"This country," Konrad said, gently, horribly reasonable, "is at war. My country started it. I understand why they are afraid. Fear makes people count, and I am the kind of thing that gets counted." He looked out at the mud. The tide was far out now, and the harbor bottom lay exposed — black stinking ooze, old bottles, a bicycle frame, the ribs of something that had once floated. "You know what I hate about low tide? Everything the water hides, it shows you. What the harbor really is. Mud and rust and things people threw away."

"We owe each other," I said. "That's what's under the water. Don't dress it up as rust."

He looked at me then, really looked, and I saw him weigh it — weigh me — the way he'd weighed sacks for twenty years, by feel, by trust in his own two hands.

"You promised me passage," he said. "In 1913. On this quay, near enough. You were a boy who drove a motorcar for rich ladies and you said, *if things ever turn, Konrad, I'll get you out, I know the harbors.* Do you remember?"

"I remember."

"And I laughed at you. Because things were good. Because Bremen and Hamburg were just places where beer came from." He shook his head. "Now things have turned, and you came. You packed a bag and sewed money into it and you walked beside me all this way at dawn, and the ship is gone, and it is not your fault, Ansel. Ships get taken. Tides go out. Promises break against things bigger than promises." He took a breath. "Let me keep yours unbroken by ending it myself. I'll walk to that shed and give them a name they can file, and you drive home, and in five years—"

"No," I said again, and this time it came out with everything behind it, all of it — the letters I'd read, the secrets I'd hoarded, Edith's voice saying *counting lives selectively*, Pell's clipboard, the mud, the bells that kept not ringing — everything I had. "Not like that. Not surrendered to *him*. Konrad, listen to me. Pell doesn't want you filed. Pell wants you *found*, by him, with witnesses, and he wants the people who hid you found with you, and he'll take us apart politely, page by page, because he enjoys it. You walking into his shed isn't mercy. It's handing him the whole ledger."

Konrad was quiet for a long moment. Down the quay, the loafer by the fish shed lit a cigarette, and the match flame was the brightest thing on the water.

"Then what?" he asked finally. "There is no ship."

"There's no ship *today*."

"Ansel—"

"There are other neutrals coming through. There's a Dutch coaster, there are Swedish boats, there is always, always another ship, and Pell can't watch every berth forever because Pell answers to a department and departments move their men around like furniture. We go back. You stay in the boathouse three more days, four, a week—"

"Every day—"

"I *know* every day. I'm the one carrying you, remember? In my coat." My voice cracked and I let it. Let him see what it cost; it was the only argument I had that couldn't be answered. "You don't get to make this sacrifice for me. That's not how we owe each other. We owe each other *alive*."

The gulls screamed. The collier's winches began their morning grinding somewhere above us, indifferent as weather. And Konrad Voss, enemy alien, twenty years a dockworker, stood on the exposed mud-smelling quay with his one canvas bag and did not move, and did not surrender, and I understood that I was seeing him decide — that his whole enormous patience was tipping, gram by gram, between the shed and me.

"Three days," he said at last. "Then I choose the shed."

"Three days."

We turned back toward the town, and I felt the watcher's eyes slide across our backs like a hand checking pockets, and I kept my pace even and my face empty and my heart hammering out the only sum that mattered: three days, no ship, one letter in Edith Marchmont's desk drawer that could ruin her, and a census taker who liked his work.

Behind us, the tide had begun, almost invisibly, to turn.

* * *

Edith was waiting in the hall when I came in, before I'd even gotten my coat off. She read my face the way she read a crowd.

"Well?" she said.

"The ship's gone. Requisitioned six days ago. Pell's men are on every quay." I set my hat down on the table, and my hands were not steady, and I didn't try to hide it. "Edith, I need to tell you something else. About a letter. About what I'll have to do if this goes wrong."

She went very still, and then she said, in a voice I had never heard her use:

"Sit down, Ansel."
