## 8. Rumors of Peace

Edith Marchmont had spent thirty years learning to read a crowd the way other women read a letter — by what it left out, by the pressure beneath the ink. So when she stood at her study window on the fourth of November and saw the lamplighter running down Harbor Row with his pole still in his hand, shouting before he'd finished lighting a single lamp, she knew before he reached the corner that something had broken loose in the city.

She opened the window. Cold air came in carrying the smell of coal smoke and river mud.

"Madame!" The lamplighter's face was lit from below by the one lamp he'd managed. "They've signed it! The Germans have signed! It's over!"

"Where did you hear that?"

"The *Courier* boy — extras — everyone's saying—" He was already running again, shouting to the next street, the words trailing behind him like sparks.

Edith closed the window and stood very still.

It wasn't true. She knew it wasn't true, or at least she didn't know it was true, which amounted in practice to the same thing. The armistice negotiations were stalled — she had it from Mrs. Fetterley, whose brother sat in some ministry anteroom counting telegrams. But knowing a rumor was false had never once stopped a rumor from doing its work. Edith had built a career on that fact. Half her movement ran on sentences people wanted so badly to believe they believed them into existence.

The difference now was what the believing would cost, and who would pay it.

She thought immediately — before bells, before flags, before any of the pageantry her mind began unspooling — of Konrad Voss in the boathouse, and of Ansel Amberdale, who had been gone since morning with the motorcar and had not said where. That absence had a shape she recognized. It was the shape of a plan she hadn't been told about.

"They'll be at the harbor," she said aloud, to no one, and went for her coat.

* * *

The city came apart joyfully first, which was the cruel trick of it.

By the time the motorcar — Ansel had returned it to the mews, keys left under the seat, a note saying only *gone to see about the boat* — could not have helped her anyway, Edith was walking, because every street between Marchmont House and the water had become a river of people moving in one direction. Shopgirls with their aprons still on. Men from the munitions works, their faces black with the shift's grime, weeping openly without shame. A woman carried a cage with a canary in it, holding it up like a lantern. Someone had dragged a piano onto the corner of Wexford Street and a man was playing *Tipperary* badly while four hundred voices drowned him out.

Edith moved against the current, hatless, her gray hair coming loose from its pins, and no one looked at her twice because no one looked at anything twice tonight. Joy made the city generous. She felt it tug at her too — the animal relief of it, four years of counted dead suddenly suspended in air like dust in a shaft of light — and she hated herself a little for feeling it, because she knew how quickly this particular weather changed.

She had seen it change before. In '14, outside the recruiting offices: the same crowds, the same tears, and by August the same mouths calling a baker's son a coward because his mother had pulled him back by the collar. Crowds did not hold two feelings well. They held one, and whatever it was, it needed somewhere to go.

She reached the top of Harborside Hill as the weather turned.

* * *

It happened the way she later described it to Ansel, and the way she privately knew she had failed to prevent: gradually, then all at once.

The celebration was still going at the harbor gates — flares now, someone's bonfire of packing crates — when a knot of men near the customs house stopped singing. Edith watched it happen from fifty yards off, watched the music die in one patch of the crowd like frost crossing a windowpane. A man in a dockmaster's coat was saying something to a group of stevedores, pointing down toward the dry docks. Then another voice took it up, and then it wasn't speech anymore but a sound, low and rhythmic, the oldest sound crowds make.

She couldn't hear the word at first over the singing elsewhere. Then she could.

*"Foreigners."*

Then: *"Where do they sleep?"*

A woman near her clutched her arm. "Is it starting? Is it starting again?" — meaning nothing, meaning everything; the woman's eyes were wet with happiness that hadn't yet decided what it was.

Edith did not answer her. She was already calculating.

The mob — it could be called nothing else now, though half of them were still holding flags — moved along the quay away from the boathouse, toward the lodging houses on Cordwainer Lane where the German families had lived openly for a generation, the ones too poor or too stubborn to hide. There would be windows broken there tonight, Edith thought, and worse, and there was nothing she could do about Cordwainer Lane except be ashamed of the arithmetic she was performing: *not our problem, not our risk, not Konrad.* The movement had taught her arithmetic. She hated how fluent she'd become.

But mobs are weather, and weather can be turned, and Edith Marchmont had spent three decades turning things larger than herself with nothing but her voice and the strange authority of a woman nobody had ever quite dared to interrupt.

She stepped up onto the plinth of the sailors' monument, planted her feet in the manner she used on platform, and waited for the head of the crowd to reach her.

* * *

"Gentlemen!"

Her voice cut the night the way it always did — trained, pitched, carrying over noise the way a lighthouse beam carries over fog. Heads turned. In the flare light she must have looked like what she was: a tall woman of sixty-one in a good coat, standing above them on stone with the absolute composure of someone who has never once been shouted down.

"You're looking for Germans," she called. "I'll save you the trouble."

A roar of assent. Somewhere a bottle shattered. The dockmaster's-coat man pushed forward, red-faced, triumphant — a man being handed exactly the evening he'd wanted.

"There's one at the old Marchmont boathouse," Edith said, clear as a bell. "Been there since spring. I've fed him myself."

The roar turned toward her, hungry and delighted. And here was the trick, the whole trick, the thing she would lie awake examining for weeks afterward: she let them have their fury for exactly three seconds — long enough to taste it, long enough to trust her — and then she raised one hand, palm out, and the hand alone quieted forty people, because power is a habit and hers was thirty years deep.

"But you won't find him there," she went on, "because an hour ago he was taken. Constable Pell and two of his men walked him out the north road to the internment camp at Frith." She let that land. "You want to celebrate your victory by dragging a prisoner out of a police cart? Go ahead. But you'll want witnesses, and the *Courier* man is down at Cordwainer Lane right now, photographing. If you're going to be in tomorrow's paper, gentlemen, decide which picture you'd rather be in."

She had invented Pell's involvement in a single breath, using the name of the one official in the district whose diligence everyone knew, wrapping her lie in the state's own uniform. She watched the arithmetic work behind their eyes — the fear of the paper, the fear of Pell, the deflating discovery that their prey was already government property and therefore no fun at all. Mobs wanted transgression, not procedure.

"Frith camp," someone repeated uncertainly.

"North road, past the tollhouse," Edith confirmed, and pointed, grandly, wrongly, in the opposite direction from the boathouse. Forty men peeled away into the dark toward a camp that lay nine miles east, and a *Courier* photographer who did not exist, and a constable who would have been astonished to learn of his own efficiency.

The woman with the canary squeezed Edith's arm. "You're a wonder, ma'am. You're a national treasure."

"I'm going home," Edith said, "to lock my doors," and descended from the plinth with her heart hammering so hard she had to pause at the monument's base and pretend to adjust her boot.

Because she had done it — she had saved him, probably, tonight — and she understood even as her pulse slowed that she had spent something irreplaceable. Her name had been the instrument. She had stood in public, in flare light, identified, and directed a mob, and lied to a crowd as easily as breathing, and if one man in that forty recognized her tomorrow — and someone would; someone always did — the story would travel by breakfast. *Marchmont points the patriots at the camps.* It would be twisted, inevitably, into its ugliest shape: *Marchmont shelters enemies.* The suffrage campaign, six months from its most critical vote, would carry her scandal like ballast. Mrs. Fetterley would call within a day. The committee would convene. She had bent her power toward mercy for one hidden man, and the price was that the cause itself now hung from a thread she herself had frayed.

That was the lesson of power nobody taught you: it was not a thing you spent sparingly because you were wise. It was a thing you spent because mercy arrived at inconvenient hours, wearing someone else's face.

* * *

She found Ansel at the boathouse just after midnight, sitting on the slipway stones with Konrad's shadow beside the door, both of them listening to the last of the distant singing. When she came down the path, Ansel scrambled up, stricken.

"You heard—"

"I told them you'd been taken to Frith," Edith said flatly. "Both of you. Congratulations — you're officially contraband the Crown already owns. It buys perhaps two days before anyone checks." She looked at the dark water, at the tramp steamer's riding lights far out in the roads. "Whatever you're planning, Mr. Voss, whatever arrangement my driver has made without consulting me — it sails soon. Before this peace turns real enough for paperwork. Do you understand me?"

Konrad said something low in German that she didn't catch, but his bow was deep enough to translate.

"And Ansel." She turned to her driver, and found she couldn't manage severity, only exhaustion. "Tomorrow morning, my study. There is a great deal we need to discuss. Including what you've been reading."

Ansel went white in the flare light.

"Yes," Edith said. "I know. All of it. Come at eight o'clock, and bring the letters you think I don't know about — we may yet find a use for them."

She climbed the path back toward the house, alone, her name already spending itself in the dark behind her.
