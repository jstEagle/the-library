## 9. The Last Manifest

The collier was called the *Mercy of Leeds*, which struck me as either a joke or a prayer, and she was taking on coal under a sky the color of wet ash. I watched her from behind the customs shed, my breath coming out in rags, my hands so cold they had stopped belonging to me. Somewhere on her deck or in her hold or in the ledger of some indifferent clerk, there was going to be a space for one more man — if I could buy it.

Konrad waited in the coal merchant's yard two streets back, folded into the shadow between a wagon and a wall, patient as furniture. He had not argued when I told him to stay. He had only looked at me the way he looked at heavy things before lifting them, measuring whether I would hold.

I checked the letter a fourth time.

It lived inside my shirt now, against my sternum, where I had carried it since Edith pressed it into my hands three nights ago with an expression I had never seen on her — not fear exactly, and not resolve, but the flat arithmetic of a woman who has done sums all her life and finally added up the wrong column. *If it comes to choosing*, she had said, *then spend it.* The letter was dated August of 1914, written to a man in Glasgow whose name appeared nowhere else in her correspondence, and it contained four sentences that would have put her in a cell and her movement in the ground. I knew them by heart. I had read that letter eleven times over five years, and every time I felt like a thief, and every time I kept reading.

Now it was currency. That was all it had ever been, maybe. All any of us were to somebody: letters waiting to be opened.

* * *

Pell found me at half past ten.

I should say he cornered me, because that is the truth of it, though at first he only drifted out from between two coal trucks like weather, hands in his overcoat pockets, hat brim dripping. He looked smaller than I remembered from the Marchmont parlor. Suspicion had been a large thing in him; here at the water's edge, with gulls screaming over the collier's derricks, he was just a tired man in a bad coat who had been given a job nobody thanked anyone for doing.

"Mr. Amberdale." He didn't smile. That frightened me more than a smile would have. "You're a difficult man to catch at your work."

"I deliver letters."

"You do." He produced the manifest without hurry, unfolding it the way other men unfold a newspaper on a Sunday. "Funny thing about paper, Mr. Amberdale. Paper remembers. Now this here" — his finger came down on a line — "says the *Mercy of Leeds* carries eight hundred tons of house coal, twelve crew, no passengers. Signed by her master this morning." His eyes came up. "And this other piece of paper says a Konrad Brandt, German national, dockhand, declared enemy alien, failed to report to the police at Holbeck on the fourteenth. Two papers, Mr. Amberdale. Only room in the world for one of them to be telling the truth."

The wind came off the harbor and cut through everything I wore. Behind Pell, the collier's winch groaned and swung a skip of coal out over the quay, and I watched it hang there, turning slightly, deciding nothing.

"I don't know a Konrad Brandt," I said.

"No," Pell agreed mildly. "I expect you'd say that standing next to him." He refolded the manifest along its crease, carefully, a man who respected paperwork even when it lied for him. "Here's what happens now. I walk to that gangway. I show my papers to the mate. Every soul aboard gets counted against the list, and any soul not on it gets counted twice, if you follow me. And then I go and find a young woman named Edith Marchmont, who has made herself very popular lately with very important people, and I ask her some questions with my hat in my hands, polite as you please." He paused. "Unless something changes my mind about where my afternoon is best spent."

He wanted me to bargain. He had left the door open on purpose; a man who enjoyed suspicion as much as Pell did did not want it settled, he wanted it fed. And I understood, standing there in the cold with my heart going like a loose valve, that this was the moment the whole autumn had been driving toward — not the armistice rumors, not the crowds, not the bells everyone kept swearing they could hear in their teeth. This. A tired man with a list and a driver with a letter, each holding the only paper that mattered to the other.

I thought about Edith's voice: *spend it*. I thought about how I had measured my whole adult life by what fit in a coat pocket, and how I had never once asked myself what it would cost to take everything out.

"There's a berth missing from that ship," I said.

Pell's eyebrows went up, just slightly. Interest, not surprise. He had known. He had probably walked down to this quay knowing, the way a fisherman walks to the river knowing.

"There's a berth missing," I repeated, "and there's a letter missing from the world, if you take my meaning. One goes away tonight, the other goes away tonight. Nobody counts anything twice."

"And the letter," Pell said slowly, tasting it, "would be worth more to certain parties than one German dockhand is worth less to anybody."

"It would end a movement. Yours. The one you've spent two years trying to find the door into." I pulled the envelope from my shirt. My fingers shook, and I let him see it; there was no dignity left to protect, so I spent the trembling instead. "I'm not clever enough to trick you, Mr. Pell, and you're not cruel enough to hang a man for driving a motorcar. We're both just men carrying paper. Trade fair."

* * *

He took it with two fingers, like something hot.

For a long moment he stood there reading, and I watched four sentences do what four sentences do — watched the color move in his face, watched him understand the size of the thing he was holding, watched the small terrible pleasure of a man realizing he has caught something too big for his hands. Gulls fought over something behind the coal shed. The winch groaned. The tide was going out and taking the light with it.

"This would ruin her," he said quietly. Not gloating. Almost reverent. "This would finish it, all of it, the whole—"

"Yes."

"Then why—"

"Because she told me to give it to you," I said, "and because Brandt is on that ship in twenty minutes, and because I am done pretending that arithmetic is honest when the numbers are other people." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "One life against a cause, Mr. Pell. You count yours however you like. I've done mine."

Pell looked at the letter a while longer. Then, deliberately, he tore it — once, lengthwise — and again, and dropped the pieces into the black water between the quay and the collier's hull, where they spread apart like small pale birds and were gone under.

"The manifest stands as wrote," he said. "Eight hundred tons, twelve crew, no passengers." He put his hands back in his pockets and turned toward the gangway, then stopped. "I never liked this work, Mr. Amberdale. Everyone assumes a man in my trade enjoys it." He adjusted his hat against the wind. "Tell your friend the sea doesn't ask where you were born. That's more than most countries can say."

And he walked off down the quay with his empty pockets and his satisfied paperwork, and I leaned against the coal shed and was sick into the harbor, and felt enormously, stupidly better afterward, the way you do when a fever breaks.

* * *

I brought Konrad down through the coal yard at a quarter past eleven, walking not fast and not slow, two men going about the dull business of the hour. The mate of the *Mercy of Leeds* took a fold of notes from Konrad's hand and a name from Konrad's mouth — a Yorkshire name, flat and plausible — and jerked his thumb at the gangway, and that was all. Five years of hiding reduced to a nod and a plank of wood.

At the top Konrad stopped. He turned around and looked at me across the widening gap of air and water, and he said nothing, because we had never needed many words, but he touched his chest — right where the letter had lain against mine — and then held out his hand as if pressing something into it. Giving me something invisible to carry. I nodded that I had it. I would keep it.

Then he went down into the ship, and the hawsers came off, and the *Mercy of Leeds* — eight hundred tons of house coal, twelve crew, no passengers — leaned out on the ebb tide and took him with her.

I stood on the quay until her funnel smoke went soft in the dark, and then I put my hand in my coat pocket, where for five years I had kept other people's words, and found it empty, and found, strangely, that it still weighed something.

It was past midnight when I got the motorcar back to the Marchmont house. There was a lamp burning in the study window, and behind it Edith's shadow, moving slowly, the way a person moves when they are feeding papers to a fire.

I went up the steps and knocked, and the door opened before my hand fell the second time.
