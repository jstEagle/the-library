## 1. The Weight of Sorts

There were exactly three ways to measure a thing in the Sandoval print shop, and Felix Sandoval — who had been born Felicidad, and who measured her words more carefully than anything else these days — was fluent in all of them.

The first was type. A point was one seventy-second of an inch, and an em was the width of the letter m at whatever size you were casting, and if you said *ten-point Caslon* to any printer between here and the capital, he would know precisely what you meant. Type was honest that way. Type did not care whether the hand setting it belonged to a man or a woman; it only cared whether your fingers were clean.

The second measure was flour. The miller on Copper Lane sold by the sack, but he trusted the Sandovals, so Felix paid by weight and took what she could carry, which this morning was eleven pounds wrapped in paper that had already printed one side of its life. She carried it home through streets still smelling of last night's fog and this morning's fish, and she thought, as she always did, about how eleven pounds of flour weighed almost exactly as much as a full case of ten-point body type. This was the sort of thought that came to her unbidden and that nobody else would find interesting, and she treasured it privately, the way other people treasured lockets.

The third measure was the one she was inventing for herself: what can be carried.

She was not yet sure why she needed it. But a person who lived one lie deep learned to keep accounts the way sailors kept accounts of the sea — not because they expected the storm that night, but because the habit of counting was the only thing that made the waiting bearable.

* * *

The Sandoval print shop stood on Tintero Street like a man standing very straight at his own funeral: narrow, dignified, older than everything around it and pretending not to notice. Downstairs was the shop — two presses, the good Heidelberg and old Mateo, the flatbed her grandfather had bought secondhand from a man who'd stolen it from somebody else — and the composing stone, and the cabinets of type, forty cases of letters in every size from the great fat fourteen-point used for proclamations down to the six-point reserved for legal notices nobody read. Upstairs, reached by stairs that complained in E-flat, was the apartment where three people slept and one of them was legally a ghost.

Felix set the flour on the counter and called up the stairs.

"Teo! If you've fallen asleep over the proofs again, I'm letting the rats have you."

"No rats," came Teo's voice, papery and pleased with itself, "would have me. Too much ink in the blood." And there he was at the top of the stairs, old Teo Brandt, proofreader of forty years, holding his magnifying glass like a bishop holds a crozier. His eyes had gone soft as old apples, but put a page of galleys in front of him and he could find a turned letter at forty paces. "You're late. The boy from the chandler came."

"I'm never late."

"He waited eleven minutes. I counted. It was the most excitement I've had since Lent."

Felix counted the coins out of her father's — no. Out of *her* purse, though her hand always hesitated there, half a heartbeat, on the word. Her father's purse. Aurelio Sandoval had been dead eleven months now, carried off in six weeks by the fever that had come up from the ships, and in those six weeks he had done three things: he had taught her the last of what he knew, he had made her promise not to be stupid, and he had made her swear the oath that governed her whole life now.

*The license dies with me,* he had said, gripping her wrist with the strength that the fever hadn't gotten to yet. *They'll close the shop. They'll give it to some cousin of the tax assessor and you'll be out on the street. You're my son, do you hear me? You've been my son since the day you were born; the paperwork just caught up late.*

So Felix Sandoval, daughter of Aurelio and Lucia, buried her father, put on his spare coat, and became Félix Sandoval, son and heir, licensed printer of Tintero Street. Lucia had died years before, so there was no wife to explain, no awkward questions at the door — only Teo, who had looked at her once, the morning after the funeral, when she'd cut her hair badly with the sewing scissors, and had said only: "You missed a spot behind the left ear," and fetched the good scissors.

That was the whole of the conversation, ever after. Some families were made by blood. Hers had been made by a proofreader's refusal to comment.

* * *

The morning's work was honest work, the kind that kept the license respectable: five hundred handbills for Ferrante's Dry Goods, ALL WOOLENS REDUCED, in twelve-point, and two hundred invoices for the harbor office, which paid promptly and asked nothing. Felix set the handbills herself because her fingers knew the case blind, left to right, letter by letter, the way a musician knows a scale. Most people thought typesetting was reading backward. It wasn't. It was something better than reading — the words went into your hands and stopped being words at all, became weights and positions, *e* here, *s* there, spaces thin and thick, until the line locked up solid and true and you could lift it and it held.

Her father used to say a well-set line of type was the closest a workingman got to prayer. Felix, who had been a workingwoman pretending to be a workingman all her adult life, thought he might have undersold it.

"You're smiling at the woolens," Teo observed from his corner, where he was reading the morning's gazette with his glass and his lips moving slightly. "Only you could smile at Ferrante's woolens."

"It's a good f. I recut it in spring."

"Mm." Teo turned the page. Then he didn't turn the next page for rather a long time.

Felix noticed, because noticing was her trade too. Teo read the gazette the way he read everything — fast, ruthless, hunting errors the way a hawk hunts mice. But now he sat over the third page like a man picking through rubble.

"What is it?"

"Official account," Teo said. "Of Salt Quay." He said it the way you'd say the name of a grave. Everyone in Valdorra had spent two weeks saying it that way. "Riot, they call it. Says the stevedores armed themselves first. Says nine dead." He lowered the paper. "Marisol Reyes was down there. Marisol Reyes says forty."

"Marisol Reyes talks," said Felix, and then hated herself for it, because Marisol Reyes had hauled rope beside her father for twenty years and had once walked four miles in the rain to bring them a broken press-part she'd found on the quay, on the mere chance it might fit old Mateo. But fear made a person say ugly little things, and the trick — the whole trick, the whole impossible trick of Felix's life — was to say them and keep moving before they could take root.

"The gazette sets it in type," Teo said quietly. "Type doesn't make it true, girl. We both know that better than anyone living."

"We know it. Nobody else does." Felix lifted the locked chase of the handbill form and carried it to the press. "And nobody else is going to, either, so kindly stop looking at page three like it owes you money."

Teo said nothing. That was worse than an argument. Teo's silences were annotated.

* * *

Midday. Felix measured out the afternoon in small economies. She swept the shop, because a clean floor was the cheapest advertisement in the world. She checked the stock of paper against her ledger, and the ledger against the truth, and trimmed the difference toward honesty because the harbor clerk who inspected ledgers was lazy but not blind. She watered the single plant on the windowsill, a geranium her mother had planted, stubbornly red in a city going gray.

And she measured what could be carried.

This was the private arithmetic, the one she told no one. It went like this: the shop could not be carried. Forty cases of type could not be carried. The Heidelberg certainly could not be carried, and old Mateo would die where he stood, and thinking about that felt like planning her own funeral, so she didn't. What could be carried was: one case — she'd choose the ten-point, the workhorse, the alphabet worth more than its weight in silver to anyone who knew how to use it. One valise of clothing, hers cut for a man, Teo's smelling of pipe smoke. The ledger, if there were time. Bread. Water. The geranium, probably not, though her hand wanted to write it on the list anyway and she let it, because a list should contain one impossible item, just to remind you lists weren't prayers.

She did not write down: the locked drawer under the composing stone. Because the locked drawer contained nothing, officially. Unofficially it contained three pamphlets she had not printed and two she had — quietly, at night, pull-and-wipe, pull-and-wipe, burning each proof in the stove while the wax still softened — pamphlets about the grain tariff, about the press-gangs, about the men the harbor police had beaten outside the governor's house in October. Small truths, set in small type, distributed by hands she never saw to readers she never met, for coin left in a crack in the tannery wall. It was treason, probably. She preferred to think of it as literacy.

Her father had known about the drawer. Had helped her build it, in fact, with a false bottom that even the tax assessor's cousin would admire. *A printer prints,* he'd said. *Everything else is commentary.* And then, because he was her father and could not leave a sentence unblessed: *But be careful, my son. My brave idiot son.*

She was careful. She was so careful it sometimes frightened her how good she'd gotten at it — the lowered voice, the rolled sleeves, the way she'd taught herself to sit like a man, knees wide, elbows on stone, taking up space without apology. Eleven months of being someone else, and the terrible discovery of this year was that the someone else had grown comfortable inside her skin, like a good shoe. Some nights she wasn't sure which of them was the disguise anymore.

The bell over the door rang.

Felix's hands did the arithmetic before her mind did: handbills drying, invoices stacked, drawer locked, coat on the peg, voice — she dropped it half a tone before she turned around. "Closed till two," she said. "Can I help you?"

The man in the doorway was neither soldier nor clerk nor customer, which made him immediately suspicious, and he was dressed with the particular neatness of a person who owned exactly four good garments and maintained all of them. Gray coat, gray hat, gray eyes behind round spectacles, and under his arm a leather folio stamped with the seal of the Ministry of Interior.

"Census," he said. Not unkindly. He consulted a card. "Sandoval. Printers. Licensed premises, one resident above shop, one employee." He looked up, and his gaze went past her to the stairs, to the sound of Teo humming up there among the proofs. "That would be... yourself, and the proofreader?"

"That's right," said Felix, in her father's voice, wearing her father's name, measuring the distance to the door the way she measured everything now — in points, in ems, in what could be carried. "Anything else?"

The census taker opened his folio. "One question," he said. "Perhaps two."
