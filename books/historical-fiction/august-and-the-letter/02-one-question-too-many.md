## 2. One Question Too Many

The census taker came on a Thursday, which was the worst possible day, because Thursdays were when Frau Hettich from the corner house sent her boy over for flour, and flour required measuring, and measuring flour required both of Felix's hands and none of her nerves.

She had just leveled the scoop when the bell above the shop door rang — not the customer's bell on the left frame, which had a cheerful little trill, but the heavier bell on the right, the one her father had hung for official callers because he believed, in his words, that a man should hear the difference between a blessing and a bill before he turned around.

Felix turned around.

The man in the doorway was narrow as a composing rule and dressed with the kind of precision that suggested he ironed his own collar twice. He carried a leather portfolio under one arm and an umbrella he clearly did not need, since the morning was dry and had been dry for a week. He consulted a small notebook before speaking, which Felix found either reassuring or alarming; she could not immediately decide which.

"Good morning," he said. "I am Aurelio Voss, census office, third district." He produced a card. The card was typeset — actually typeset, she noted, not lithographed — in a tidy eight-point Caslon. "I am conducting household verification for the Sandoval printing establishment."

"We're printed and verified," Felix said, which was not a sentence.

Voss looked at her with mild, patient eyes behind spectacles that sat slightly crooked, as though even his glasses could not quite agree with one another. "I beg your pardon?"

"Registered. We're registered."

"Of course you are." He stepped inside without quite being invited, the way officials do, and set his portfolio on the counter beside the flour, then reconsidered and moved it away from the flour, which raised him considerably in Felix's estimation. "You are Felix Sandoval, proprietor. Son of the late Emilio Sandoval."

"I am."

"Your father established the shop in 1874."

"He did."

"You have operated it alone since his death."

"With my apprentice," Felix said, and the moment the word left her mouth she felt it land wrong, like a letter dropped into the wrong case. *Apprentice.* Why had she said apprentice? Nobody had said apprentice. He hadn't asked about anyone.

But Voss's pen was already moving. "Ah yes." He flipped a page in the portfolio. "The apprentice. That is, in fact, why I am here, beyond routine verification." He adjusted his spectacles with one finger, and the finger trembled very slightly, and Felix would think about that tremble later, at length, in the dark. "There is a discrepancy."

* * *

Here is what Felix knew about discrepancies: they were how shops died.

Her father had taught her that much between lessons on kerning and ink viscosity. A license, he said, was a story the government agreed to believe about you, and every story has a page count. You keep the pages aligned, you keep the margins tight, and no inspector ever reads past the colophon. But introduce a discrepancy — an extra sack of paper stock, a press run the ledgers can't account for, an apprentice who does not exist — and some careful soul will pull a thread, and the whole signature comes apart in his hands.

So she had been careful for three years. Careful the way only a woman pretending to be her own brother can be careful: binding her chest before dawn, lowering her voice into the gravel register her father had never used, walking with her heels planted like a man who owns the ground under him. Careful in the shop's books, where *F. Sandoval & Son* appeared in her best italic hand. Careful everywhere except, apparently, wherever it counted.

"The discrepancy," Voss repeated, and laid two documents side by side on the counter like a man laying out type specimens for comparison. "The first is your father's death registration. The second is this year's household census form." He tapped the second document. It was her own handwriting looking back at her. "You have listed one resident besides yourself. An apprentice, name of—"

"Firmin," said Felix, whose mind, offered a full second to prepare, had produced *Firmin.*

"Firmin," Voss agreed pleasantly. "And yet." He produced a third document. "The parish records of San Almaden, which cover every birth registered in this district since 1860, contain no Firmin whatsoever. Not Firmin Sandoval, not Firmin anything. I checked the whole diocese." He said it almost apologetically, as if he were sorry to be thorough. "It took me four days. My wife says I enjoy suffering."

Somewhere in Felix's chest, her heart performed a maneuver she had previously only read about in serialized novels.

"There are parishes outside the district," she said.

"There are," Voss conceded. "Rural parishes. Mountain parishes. Parishes that lost their records in the floods of '91, certainly." He removed his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, and without them his face went oddly young and unguarded. "Mr. Sandoval, I am going to ask you a question, and I want you to understand that I am asking it as a clerk, not as an inquisitor. Clerks hate loose ends. It is our entire temperament. Where is Firmin's birth record?"

And Felix — who had rehearsed lies for three years against precisely this day, who had a whole drawer of prepared answers about cousins up-country and papers lost in transit and the general slipperiness of rural registrars — heard herself say:

"He doesn't have one."

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight. It sat down between them on the counter next to the flour and made itself comfortable.

Voss put his spectacles back on. "He doesn't have one."

"Some people fall through," Felix said. Her voice was doing something unfortunate, climbing toward its natural register and having to be hauled back down mid-word. "The registrar in his village drank. There was a fire. There was—" She stopped, because she had now given the boy three separate catastrophes in eleven seconds and any moment she might add a flood and a wolf attack.

"A drinking, burning village," Voss said gravely. "Yes. I've heard they suffer."

Felix stared at the counter. On the wall behind her, the clock her father had bought off a sinking merchant ship ticked with its permanent slight limp, and she thought, absurdly, that she had never loved anything so much as that clock for making sound that wasn't hers.

"Mr. Sandoval." Voss gathered his documents with small precise movements, squaring their edges against the countertop the way she squared a forme before locking the chase. "The census bureau requires me to file a correction within ten days regarding any unverifiable resident. It is form nine-C. It is a dull form. I have filed many." He tucked the portfolio under his arm. "I will tell you that my own father came to this country on a boat with no papers at all, and the priest who baptized him invented a birthdate out of kindness, and I have spent thirty years being officially four years younger than God knows I am. So." He settled his hat. "I dislike forms that pretend the world is tidier than it is. I will consider carefully what form nine-C ought to say."

Then he was gone, the heavy bell swinging behind him, and Felix stood among her cases of type — thousands upon thousands of letters, every word ever needed, and not one of them useful — and realized she had never in her life lied worse.

* * *

That night she did not sleep.

She banked the stove, checked the lock, checked it again, and lay in the loft above the pressroom listening to Valdorra breathe — the gulls arguing over the harbor, a drunk singing two streets over, boots. At half past midnight there were boots, a patrol's unhurried rhythm passing the shuttered windows, and she held still until they faded, counting her heartbeats the way she counted ems.

It was near two when she gave up on sleep and came down the ladder for warm milk and self-reproach, and that was when she saw it.

A card on the floorboards, just inside the door. White, small, slid neatly under the gap at the threshold — she could see the scuff line in the dust where it had traveled.

She picked it up by habit before her fear caught up with her hands.

It was Voss's card. Same typeset name, same census office, same tidy Caslon. But below the printed name, someone had added a single line of text — set in metal type, inked, and pressed onto the card by hand. She knew this because she could see the impression, the slight bite of the letters into the paper stock, and because no handpress in this city would touch such a line except illegally, secretly, in the dark.

The line read:

*The truth needs no license to circulate.*

Felix stood in her dark shop holding a census taker's card like it was a live coal.

She knew the sentence. Every printer in Valdorra knew the sentence, or had once; it was the closing line of *The Common Voice*, banned five years ago, its author jailed, its plates seized and melted — melted, officially, in front of witnesses. And yet here it was, freshly set, freshly printed, pressed onto the calling card of the one man in the empire who had spent four days searching the parish records for a boy who did not exist.

*He knows,* she thought. Not about Firmin — there was no Firmin to know about, which was rather the point. He knew about *her.* About the chest bindings and the lowered voice and the ledger written in her own careful italic, and he had walked out of her shop carrying all of it, and instead of form nine-C he had come back in the night to leave her a line of forbidden type.

Which meant one of two things. Either Aurelio Voss was the most dangerous man she had ever met, or he was something else entirely — something she didn't have a word for yet.

She turned the card over. On the reverse, in pencil, small and hurried, as if written standing up:

*They are asking questions at Salt Quay. Someone should be ready to answer.*

Outside, far off toward the harbor, a whistle blew once and stopped.

Felix did not know yet what had happened at Salt Quay. She would know by tomorrow midnight, when the knocking came — three quick raps and one slow, the old printers' signal — and a dockworker with salt dried white on her coat would stand in the doorway and say words that would break the century open.

But that night, all she knew was the card in her hand, and the whistling wind off the water, and the strange, terrifying feeling of a door she had kept locked for three years quietly opening from the other side.
