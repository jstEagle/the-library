## 3. The Massacre at Salt Quay

Felix Sandoval had learned to read a knock the way other people read weather.

Three sharp raps meant the rent collector. A soft, apologetic tapping meant old Mrs. Ferreiro from the milliner's, wanting wedding invitations on credit. The heavy, official pounding of soldiers or inspectors meant she was to become, instantly and convincingly, a young man — shoulders squared, voice dropped, father's cap pulled low.

But the knock that came at midnight on the third of October was none of these. It was quiet. Almost shy. And it did not stop for eleven minutes.

She knew because she counted, lying in the cot behind the composing stone, watching the ceiling crack branch like a river delta in the lamplight of her imagination. She counted because counting kept her from answering, and not answering kept her safe, and safety was the whole architecture of her life these days: measured out like type, locked in cases, hidden in plain sight.

The knock came again. Twelfth minute.

"Print shop," said a woman's voice, low and rough as rope. "I know you're in there. I can see the lamp."

There was no lamp. Felix had blown it out an hour ago. Which meant either the woman was lying, or there was a lamp somewhere Felix didn't know about, which was impossible, or —

Or the woman was simply the kind of person who assumed lamps would be lit where work needed doing, and had never once been wrong about it.

Felix got up. She did not put on the cap. It was midnight, and whoever stood on the other side of that door already knew what they knew.

* * *

Marisol Reyes had rehearsed the whole thing on the walk over.

Not the walking part — she could walk anywhere in Valdorra blind drunk and bleeding and still arrive by the shortest route; it was a professional skill among dockworkers. What she'd rehearsed was the speech. *I am Marisol Reyes, stevedore, second shift, Salt Quay. I saw what happened on Tuesday morning and I will say it plainly and I will sign my name to it.* Fourteen words of preamble, then the account, then the name. She'd practiced until the words sat smooth as river stones.

Then the door opened on a slip of a person in a nightshirt — a boy, surely, all elbows and dark eyes, though something about the face wouldn't settle into *boy* no matter how Marisel looked at it — and every rehearsed word fell through a hole in her chest and was gone.

"You're Sandoval," she said instead.

"I'm — yes." The boy hesitated in a way boys didn't. "The shop is closed."

"The shop," Marisol said, "is always closed when it matters."

She heard herself say it and winced. That wasn't in the speech. That was just true, and truth arrived badly dressed sometimes. But the young Sandoval didn't slam the door. He looked at her hands instead — Marisol realized, following his gaze, that her knuckles were split and her palms were still faintly stained with rust from the quay railings, the rust she'd gripped when the first volley came — and something in his expression shifted, like a man reading fine print he'd hoped not to find.

"Come in," he said. "Before someone sees you."

* * *

Felix lit the lamp properly this time and set out the good stool, the one without the wobble, because whatever this woman wanted, she had walked through the tannery district at midnight alone to want it, and you owed courtesy to that kind of courage.

Up close, she was perhaps forty, built like something designed to hold up heavier things, with salt in her cropped hair and a healing cut above one eyebrow. She sat on the stool like it might be taken away from her at any moment. Her eyes went everywhere in the shop — the cases, the press, the galley trays — with an assessment Felix recognized. It was the way her father had looked at machinery: *can this be trusted with weight?*

"You're the printer," the woman said. "Sandoval and Son."

"My father died two years ago," Felix said. Careful. "I keep the license."

"And do you also keep the opinions?"

Felix's heart performed a small, disciplined maneuver. "I keep type. Type holds whatever's set in it. I don't ask the letters their politics."

"No," the woman agreed. "But somebody's been asking yours. There was a pamphlet last spring — *The Grain Ledger*, about the export quotas while people starved. My cousin had a copy. Paper like yours." She tapped the counter. "Same stock. Same ink smell. Same little crooked *g* in the middle words — your twelfth case, the one nearest the window, has a damaged bowl on the lowercase g. I've seen three pamphlets with that same crooked g."

Silence. The lamp hissed.

"I could be wrong," Marisol Reyes added, in the tone of a woman who had never in her life been wrong about cargo counts and did not intend to start a habit.

"You could be arrested," Felix said quietly. "Saying that to me. If I reported you."

"If you were going to report me," Marisol said, "you'd have done it while I was standing in the street making a speech to your door."

It was, Felix reflected, extremely inconvenient when people were perceptive. She thought of Voss's card upstairs in the flour tin. She thought of her father's rule — *we print what pays and we stay alive* — and then she thought of how her father had also kept, locked in the bottom drawer, forty copies of a pamphlet about the cholera cover-up that had never paid a centavo and had very nearly gotten him hanged. She inherited things she didn't always understand until later.

"What do you want printed?" she asked.

* * *

Marisol had expected the question. She had prepared for it. And now that it came, she found she couldn't do it sitting down.

She stood and walked to the window instead, where the shutter gaps showed a slice of empty street, and she told it the way she'd been telling herself in the dark so she wouldn't lose it: Tuesday morning, six o'clock, four hundred stevedores at Salt Quay refusing to unload the *Andaluz* until the wage cut was rescinded. Peaceful. Boring, even — men passing bread, women singing the unloading songs wrong on purpose to make each other laugh. She'd brought her niece's boy along to see what solidarity looked like.

Then the column of soldiers came down Quay Road, and an officer she never got the name of read something off a paper, and nobody heard it over four hundred people not being quiet, and then —

"They fired twice," she said. Her voice stayed level. She was proud of that and ashamed of it in equal measure. "First over heads. Then not. Eleven dead that I counted myself, and I count for a living. Two of them were boys under fifteen. One was old Tomás Ocampo, who worked the quay forty years and once threw a foreman into the harbor for cheating a widow, God rest him." Her hands had made fists without consulting her. "My niece's boy lost two fingers to a bullet and stood up afterward and helped carry bodies, because he's twelve years old and braver than every man in this city."

Behind her, she heard it: the small, precise sounds of someone working. When she turned, the young Sandoval was at the cases, fingers moving, setting letters into a composing stick as she spoke — not words, she realized, just measuring. Counting her sentences in ems. Estimating.

"The gazette says we started it," Marisol said. "Says we had weapons. Says nine dead, all ours, and no soldiers charged. I watched a sergeant's boot print in Thomas Ocampo's blood, sir, and I read that gazette, and I understood something." She crossed back to the counter and put both ruined hands flat on it. "They're not just killing us. They're unmaking us. A man who dies is dead. A man who dies and was never even *there* — that's something worse. That's God losing the paperwork."

Something flickered across the young printer's face — grief, recognition, fear, hard to say which order.

"You want your account set in type," Sandoval said.

"I want it *set*. Properly. Names spelled right. Dates right. Number of dead right." Marisol leaned closer. "I know what it costs. I'm not asking you to be brave. I'm telling you I'll stand in front of whatever comes of it and say I made you. My name goes on it. *Testimony of Marisol Reyes, stevedore.* You print the truth and hide behind me if you have to."

"That's backwards," Felix said. "That's entirely backwards."

"It's arithmetic," Marisol said. "You've got a life left to lose. I've mostly got the truth."

* * *

Later — much later, when the lamp had burned low and the woman had gone home through streets that were, tonight, mercifully empty of patrols — Felix stood at the composing stone with the flour tin in her hands.

Voss's card lay inside, where it had lived for a week like a splinter too deep to dig out. Census taker. Meticulous man. A week ago she'd have said her greatest danger was a form with a wrong line on it.

Now she knew better. Now the danger had a body count, and the government held the only pen allowed to write it down.

Except pens weren't the only things that wrote.

Felix took out a fresh sheet and began, in her best book hand, the headings for tomorrow night's work. *Testimony of Marisol Reyes.* Below it, room for the names. All eleven. Spelled right.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed them shaking the way you notice rain starting — distantly, factually — and then she noticed something else: the shaking wasn't only fear. Terror and purpose, it turned out, lived close enough together in the body that you could mistake one for the other right up until you tried to set type, and found your hands steadier than they'd been in two years.

Somewhere across the city, Aurelio Voss was asleep, or writing reports, or doing whatever census takers did with the shape of a household. Somewhere the presses of the official gazette were rolling out tomorrow's lie in ten thousand tidy copies.

Felix banked the lamp, checked the lock on the door twice, and went back to bed.

At four in the morning, boots passed the shop on Quay-side patrol — slow, deliberate, stopping once, right outside. She lay rigid in the dark and listened to them listen to her silence.

When they moved on, she whispered into the rafters, to her father, to the shop itself:

"We're going to print it anyway."

The type cases, being lead, said nothing. But they had never felt heavier, or more ready.
