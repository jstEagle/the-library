## 4. Composing Stick

Teo Villanueva had proofread for the *Sandoval & Son* press for thirty-one years, and in all that time he had developed exactly one superstition: never set a dangerous sentence before midnight. Dangerous sentences set before midnight attracted attention. Dangerous sentences set after midnight were simply work, and work was holy, and no soldier alive could tell the difference between holy work and an invoice.

So when Marisol Reyes laid her testimony on the composing stone at half past eleven — four pages of dockside horror written in a hand that shook only on the third page, where the names of the dead began — Teo looked at the clock, looked at the ceiling as though consulting someone recently deceased, and said, "We begin at twelve."

"We begin now," Felix said.

"Twelve," Teo repeated, with the serenity of a man who had outlived three governments and intended to outlive this one by being unreasonable at strategic moments. "It is a rule. Like kerning. You do not argue with kerning."

Felix, who had been arguing with kerning since she was nine years old, let it pass. There was no time. There was never any time anymore, but tonight the absence of it had a shape and a weight, like a case of type carried wrong.

* * *

Marisol stood at the window with the lamp shuttered behind her, watching the street. She was a stevedore's daughter and a stevedore herself when the ships came in, and she had the shoulders of both trades. Her hands, which had held the pen that wrote the account, now hung at her sides as if they didn't know what to do without it.

"You're sure you want your name on it," Felix said. Not a question, really. She asked anyway, because asking was a kind of door you offered people, and some people needed to walk through it aloud.

"Nine men," Marisol said, not turning around. "I knew six of them by their first names. The gazette says 'a disturbance instigated by foreign agitators.' There weren't any foreign agitators. There were men asking to be paid for the hours they'd worked." She turned then, and her face in the lamplight was very steady. "If I don't put my name under what happened, then it's just more paper. Paper floats. Names sink into people. Put my name."

Teo, sorting sorts by feel in the near-dark — he claimed he could read a type case blind, and had once proved it to a skeptical apprentice by setting an entire shipping notice with his eyes shut, including a typo, which rather undercut the demonstration but nobody mentioned it — said quietly, "Galley's ready."

And so they began.

* * *

The shop at night was a different country from the shop by day. By day it smelled of ink and oil and the river damp that got into everything; by night it smelled of those things plus candle wax and fear, which Teo insisted was not a smell, and which everyone else insisted absolutely was.

They divided the labor the way a crew divides a deck in weather. Marisol read the testimony aloud, slowly, twice through each paragraph, so the sentences could be heard before they were handled. Teo composed — his fingers walking the case with the unhurried certainty of a man crossing his own kitchen in the dark. And Felix set the headings and the smaller items: the date, the place, the line that would run beneath the title like a keel under a hull —

*Set down by an eyewitness, and printed so that it cannot be unsaid.*

"That's a good line," Marisol admitted, on the fourth reading of it.

"I stole it from my father," Felix said. "He used to say it about wedding invitations."

"He had a sense of humor, your father?"

"He had a sense of *timing*. Which is funnier."

Outside, boots went past on the cobbles — six pairs, unhurried, the patrol that crossed Calle Herreros at ten minutes past midnight every night since the quay. Everyone stopped. The press did not tick. The candles were cupped by hands that had learned to move quickly. Marisol watched them go through the gap in the shutter, counting, and reported, "Six. Rifles. One of them is limping."

"Poor devil," said Teo, still setting. "Standing around in the cold at his age, protecting a government from a sentence."

The boots faded. The candles came back up. The type went on growing in the galley, letter by letter, the massacre assembling itself in reverse — not committed again, but *set right*, each word locked into place where the official version had left everything loose and sliding.

That was the thing about printing, Felix thought, tightening the quoins with a key that had been her father's hand in hers twenty years ago. Typesetting was the only honest argument. Every letter had to stand next to every other letter and bear its share of the weight or the whole line collapsed. A lie couldn't hold a form. It always pulled something crooked.

* * *

Around two in the morning, Teo called for the proof pull and found, in his own galley, an error that made him sit down heavily on the paper cabinet.

"I've set *stevedores* as *stevendoes*," he announced, as though reporting a death in the family.

"Fix it," said Felix.

"At my age you don't fix a thing like that, you *mourn* it, and then you fix it." But he was already standing, already reaching for the correcting tool, and Marisol laughed — the first laugh anyone had managed since the quay — and the sound of it startled them all a little, the way a match startles a dark room. It showed them how much room there was, and what was in it.

What was in it, Felix realized, looking around the lamplit shop at half past two, was a family. Not the kind you're issued. The kind you compose — set piece by piece into a chase and locked up tight. An old man mourning a misspelling. A dockworker keeping watch with a shuttered lamp and a steady voice. Herself, living under her father's name like a sort kept in the wrong compartment because the right one would close the shop, close the press, close the whole small door through which anything true might someday come out.

Which reminded her.

"Census," Felix said, and the word dropped into the room like a lead slug.

Marisol looked over. "The man? Voss?"

"He came Tuesday. He asked about—" Felix turned the composing stick over in her hands. "There's no birth record for 'Felix Sandoval, son of Andrés Sandoval,' because there's no such person. He saw the register. He saw *me*. He asked one question too many, and I lied badly, and he wrote something down, and I've been sick about it for three days."

"And tonight you remembered," Teo said gently, "because we are about to print treason, and a census form is a small thing to hang us all with."

"It could bury us," Felix said. "One line in an archive. *Household record inconsistent. Resident unverified.* That's all it takes. They come, they ask for papers, and I have none that are true." She set the stick down. "But—"

"But," Marisol said.

"But he filed the form *before* he found the discrepancy. If the record stands as he first wrote it — son of Andrés, resident, apprentice printer — then the archive says I exist. The state's own paper protects me. It all depends on whether he corrected it."

Silence, except for the river ticking against the pilings somewhere below the hill, and the candle doing its small patient work.

"So we are hanging our lives," Teo summarized, "on the penmanship of a civil servant."

"Yes."

"Do we know anything about him?"

"He wore his coat buttoned wrong," Marisol offered. "I saw him leave. Buttoned wrong, and he knew it, and he didn't fix it because fixing it meant stopping in the street and admitting the mistake in front of God and everyone."

Teo nodded slowly, as if this were intelligence of great military value. "A man who won't admit a mistake in public," he said, "will sometimes refuse to make one in private. It's the same pride wearing different clothes. He may yet leave the record alone — not for us. For himself."

"Or he'll correct it tomorrow," Felix said, "and we'll be dead by Friday."

"Also possible," Teo conceded. "That's the trouble with the future. It keeps both doors open and charges rent on either side." He returned to the case. "In the meantime, there is a massacre that requires the correct spelling of *stevedores*, and I intend to provide it."

* * *

They finished the last signature as the sky over the harbor went the gray of old newsprint. Marisol slept two hours on the paper-stacked bench with her boots on, and woke instantly and completely when Felix touched her shoulder, which told Felix something about the nights Marisol had lived through before this one.

By five, the forms were locked in the chase, ready for the press at first honest darkness — except there was no honest darkness coming, only the thin dishonest dark before dawn, when patrols doubled.

Felix stood over the locked-up forme and ran her thumb along the edge of it. Nine names. One testimony. One witness's signature, brave as a lit window.

"The census form," she said quietly, mostly to herself. "I need to know what he wrote. Before the soldiers need to know it. Whichever comes first decides everything."

Teo, banking the coals of the stove, glanced at her.

"Then tomorrow," he said, "you had better find out what a careful man does with a mistake."

Somewhere across the city, in a filing room smelling of dust and official purpose, a form lay in a drawer with Felix Sandoval's name on it — and beneath that name, in a clerk's careful hand, either a shield or a sentence. Neither Felix nor Teo nor Marisol knew which. But by morning, the man who held the pen was about to find out that his neat columns had been read by more eyes than his own.

And Aurelio Voss, as it happened, had not slept well either.
