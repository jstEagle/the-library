## 5. What the Census Knows

Aurelio Voss had always believed that the truth lived in ledgers. Not in speeches, which lied by their nature, and not in newspapers, which lied by their profession, but in the humble columns of a well-kept record: names, dates, occupations, the number of persons residing under one roof. A census was an act of faith. You wrote down what was, and thereby you said: *this happened, these people existed, and no one may unwrite them.*

He had believed this for thirty-one years, which was to say, his whole life, most of it spent in rooms that smelled of dust and sealing wax, counting other people so that the Republic could pretend it knew them.

On the morning after the Feast of the Ascension — he remembered because he had eaten two slices of almond cake at his sister's table and regretted both — Voss was summoned to the third floor of the Hall of Records, where the light came through frosted glass and everyone spoke as though the building were full of sleeping infants.

Deputy Registrar Ondine Kessler did not ask him to sit.

"Voss." She tapped a form on her desk with one lacquered nail. "The Sandoval printing establishment. Calle de los Plomos, number eleven. Household of four, per your own hand." Tap. "And yet the tax rolls list one resident proprietor. Deceased eleven months." Tap tap. "Explain."

"The proprietor's son remains," Voss said. "Felix Sandoval. Apprentice. The license transfers through the male line; I verified it against the guild register."

"I don't doubt your arithmetic." Kessler's voice was not unkind, which frightened him more than its opposite would have. She was a woman who sharpened her kindness into instruments. "I doubt your diligence. There is no birth record for Felix Sandoval *the son*. There is a baptismal entry — 1877, Parish of Santa Aurelia — for a daughter."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, a clerk dropped a drawer and swore with the mild fluency of a man who had done it many times before.

"Names are misrecorded," Voss offered. "Priests are careless. Parents are ambitious."

"Parents are ambitious," Kessler agreed. "Which is why the correction is simple. You will revisit the household, confirm the sex of the resident, amend your form, and file it within the week. The amended copy goes to the Provost's office, as all corrections do now." She slid the form across the desk. "You understand why we are careful, Voss. A print shop with a hidden woman is a small thing. But small errors are how large lies begin."

*Yes,* thought Voss. *Or the other way around.*

He took the form. His hand did not shake, which surprised him; he had imagined, in the long walk up three flights, that it might.

* * *

He did not go to the Sandoval shop at once. He went instead to the archive annex, where the birth registers were kept, and asked to see the parish transcripts for 1877, and there she was in faded iron-gall ink: *Felix Sandoval, hija de Tomás Sandoval y Mercedes Ruiz.* Daughter. He read the word four times, as if it might change under attention, the way words in this city had lately begun to do.

Here was the thing about Aurelio Voss that his colleagues never guessed, because they took his fussiness for ambition: he loved the records the way other men loved their children or their dogs or the national team. Every entry was a person insisting on existing. And the state, in its wisdom, had given him a pen.

Now the state wanted the pen back for one stroke.

If he filed the correction — *daughter, residing unlawfully under a male identity, holding a trade license by fraud* — then Felix Sandoval would lose the shop inside a month, and probably her liberty soon after, and probably, given what the Provost's men did with printers these days, considerably more than that. If he refused, he would be removed from the census division, perhaps investigated, and someone less sympathetic would make the house call instead.

There was a third option. It occurred to him while he stood in the annex pretending to check the 1854 plague-year registers, which no one had consulted since the plague. It was a small forgery, really. Almost a courtesy.

A daughter living as a son was fraud. But a *clerical error* — a transcriber's slip, a son recorded where a daughter should be, corrected now in good faith — that was merely bureaucracy doing its slow, imperfect work. Corrections were filed with the Provost's office. But amendments of obvious clerical origin, those went to the dead-letter shelf, pending review, pending review, pending review. He had seen forms sit there for nine years. Some had outlived their subjects.

All he had to do was write down the truth in a way designed never to be read.

His father had been a customs officer who once waved through a crate of banned novels because, he told young Aurelio, "the manifest said hymnals, and who am I to argue with a manifest?" Voss had thought it a joke. He was beginning to suspect it had been an inheritance.

* * *

The bell over the Sandoval shop door still announced him like an accusation. Inside, the smell of ink and oil and hot metal wrapped around him; the press sat in the back like a patient animal, and the apprentice — the son, the daughter, the person whose existence Voss held between finger and thumb — was sorting type at the composing stone, hands quick and sure.

"You," Felix Sandoval said, and set down the tweezers with exaggerated care, the way one sets down a knife before an argument.

"Me," Voss agreed. "I've come to correct my error."

"We haven't any errors here."

"No," he said. "That's rather the point. May I sit? My knees are fifty-one years old and this morning they are sixty."

Felix pulled out a stool without a word. From the back room came the clatter of Teo the proofreader, arguing with a dictionary; from upstairs, footsteps that belonged to nobody officially resident. The shop was full of people the ledger said did not exist. It occurred to Voss that he had spent his life cataloguing the visible world, and that everything that mattered had been happening just off the page.

"Your form," he began, and stopped, and started again. "When I filed your household, I listed you as your father's son. That was noticed. I have been instructed to correct it."

Felix went very still. Not the stillness of guilt — Voss had interviewed enough liars to know guilt's particular flavor of motionlessness — but the stillness of a person calculating doors and windows.

"And if you correct it?" Felix asked quietly.

"Then the license reverts to the Crown, and you are charged with fraud, and I am commended for my thoroughness." He laid his hat on his knee. "So I have decided instead to discover that my original entry was accurate, and that the parish transcript contains a transposition error made by a curate with failing eyesight, which I am formally amending in your favor. The paperwork will rest on the dead-letter shelf in the annex, where paperwork goes when the state wishes it had never been born. It will be reviewed eventually. Nothing in this country is reviewed eventually except graves."

For a long moment Felix simply looked at him. Then, slowly, the tension went out of those capable hands.

"You're risking your post," Felix said. "For us. Why?"

Because a census taker had once been a boy who watched his father wave through contraband novels with hymnals written on the manifest, and had understood, only twenty years too late, what his father had actually been doing with his small dull life. Because the gazette's account of Salt Quay had used the phrase *dispersed without serious incident*, and Voss had counted the casualty intake at the Harbor Infirmary himself, in another capacity, in another life, and knew the number was not zero. Because someone had to keep a true record somewhere.

"Because I take censuses," he said. "It is my business to write down what people are, not what they ought to have been. Your father had a daughter. She keeps his press. That is what is. I refuse, professionally, to enter anything else."

Teo appeared in the doorway with a proof sheet in one hand and spectacles pushed up on his forehead, looking between them like a man arriving late to a play and deciding not to ask.

"There's more," Voss said, and lowered his voice although no patrol could hear him through these walls yet. "Corrected forms are copied to the Provost's office. So are *un*corrected ones, eventually, when the shelf is audited. And the audit schedule has changed. There's an order posted in the annex — internal, sealed. Within the week, possibly days, the archive will be seized. Every register. Every household form. They are going to take the paper record of this city into custody, Mr. Sandoval. Do you understand what that means?"

Felix understood; Voss watched the understanding arrive like weather.

"They're going to unwrite people," Felix said.

"They are going to try. Registers can be burned, but—" he nodded at the press, at the cases of type gleaming dully in the lamplight, "—you people were invented for exactly this. If there is anything true in this shop that exists only on our paper, it should soon exist on yours."

From upstairs, faintly, a floorboard creaked. Teo exchanged a glance with Felix that contained an entire committee meeting.

"One more thing," Voss said, standing, retrieving his hat. "The correction I filed today lists your household truthfully — four residents, one of whom I have described, due to curatorial oversight, in terms that protect you. When the seizure comes, they will look for discrepancies. There will be one. Mine." He settled the hat brim low, an old man's gesture, or a young conspirator's. "I find I don't mind being a discrepancy. It's the first honest error of my career."

At the door he paused, hand on the bell frame.

"Mr. Sandoval. When the soldiers come — and they will come — carry only what prints."

The bell rang him out into the grey street, and behind him he heard, unmistakably, the first brisk movements of a household that had just been given its orders: drawers opening, boots found, a case of type sliding onto a table with the heavy, deliberate sound of history changing hands.

Three days later, Voss learned, the seizure order moved up. The soldiers would come not next week but tomorrow, before dawn.

And in the tannery district, in a cellar that smelled of hides and lamp oil, a press was already waiting.
