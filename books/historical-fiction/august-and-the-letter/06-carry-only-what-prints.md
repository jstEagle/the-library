## 6. Carry Only What Prints

The knock came at a quarter past four in the morning, and it was not the knock of a neighbor.

Felix knew the difference the way she knew the difference between a well-inked roller and a dry one — by feel, by instinct, by the particular dread that settled in her stomach before her mind had finished the arithmetic. Neighbors knocked twice, apologetically, with the flat of the hand. This was fists. Three of them, in rhythm, on the shutters below.

"Up," she said to the dark room, and then, because panic is contagious and someone in this family had to be the antidote, "Up, but quietly. We are going to be calm about this."

From the cot by the stove came the sound of Marisol Reyes sitting up and hitting her head on the low beam, which she did every morning, without exception, and without complaint. "I'm calm," Marisol said, rubbing her skull. "I've been calm since Tuesday."

"That was you shouting at the bread seller."

"He was cheating old women. Calmly."

Below, the fists again. Then a voice, muffled by oak: "Sandoval! Open in the name of the census bureau!"

*Census*, Felix thought, and felt something loosen in her chest even as another part of her went cold. Not the gendarmerie. Not yet. But census men did not travel with soldiers unless the census had decided to stop pretending to be about counting.

She dressed as her father's son — trousers, collar, the binding she had worn so long it had become less a disguise than a posture — and lit no lamp. In the dark she could find anything in this shop. Twenty-two years of living among cases and cabinets had printed their locations into her hands. The locked portfolio with the massacre account: under the floorboard by the hearth. Marisol's sworn testimony: inside it. Voss's card with its single banned line of type: in her breast pocket, where it had lived for three weeks like a coal she couldn't put down.

*They know,* she told herself firmly. *Voss altered the record. He told us we had days.*

Soldiers, apparently, did not consider three days to be a meaningful unit of time.

* * *

Teo was already awake. Teo, aged somewhere between sixty and geology, was always awake at hours no honest man kept, a habit from forty years of proofreading that had permanently rearranged his relationship with sleep. He stood at the foot of the stairs holding a candle he had not lit, listening to the pounding with his head cocked like an old dog deciding whether the postman was friend enough to bark at.

"The back stair," Felix whispered.

"The back stair creaks like a scandalized widow."

"Then we go quickly and let it gossip."

He didn't move. He looked at her — really looked, the way he looked at a proof, hunting the error everyone else had read past — and said, "The proofs are still in the composing room. Sixty pages. Salt Quay, names, dates, Marisol's account. If they find them, we're not fugitives, Felix. We're evidence."

"I'll burn them—"

"You'll do nothing of the kind. You'll take the girl and the case and go." He said it mildly, the way he corrected a comma. "You can't carry everything. That's the whole lesson of this trade, child. You choose what prints and you let the rest go to the hell-box." He smiled, and it was the same smile he gave when a young compositor set a line perfectly for the first time. "Besides. Nobody arrests an old man for tidying up. I'm going to tidy up."

"Teo—"

"They'll want someone to arrest regardless. Better an old proofreader with slow legs than the only pair of hands in Valdorra that knows where the press is hidden." He took her chin in his papery fingers, tilted it up. "Your father pulled me out of a debtor's cell in '81 and never once mentioned it afterward. I have been waiting twenty years to settle the account. Don't rob me of the transaction now."

The front door shook. Wood splintered somewhere below, or perhaps that was only Felix's chest.

"Go out the back, along the tanner's wall, third arch," Teo recited, brisk as ever. "The tannery people will hide you. They owe half the district money and the other half pamphlets." He pressed something into her hand — the small brass composing stick, worn smooth, older than any of them. "Carry only what prints."

Then he turned and went *down*, candle unlit, humming an old quay song off-key on purpose, loud enough to draw every eye and ear in the building toward himself and away from the creaking back stair.

Felix stood one heartbeat longer at the top of those stairs than she should have. Marisol caught her sleeve.

"Felix."

"I know."

"Now."

* * *

They went out through the bindery window into the alley, where the cold hit like a bucket of harbor water, and they carried what they carried.

It was not much, and it was everything. One type case — the job case, the good one, fourteen-point body text and the title sorts and the ampersand Felix had recut herself at sixteen because her father had refused on principle to print anything with an ugly ampersand. It weighed as much as a sleeping child and had to be held against the chest, both arms, which meant Marisol carried the portfolio and Felix carried the lead, and neither of them said aloud what both of them understood: that they were fleeing a city with the entire Sandoval family fortune slung over one shoulder, and it consisted of several hundred pieces of metal arranged in no order anyone else could read.

Behind them, light bloomed yellow in the shop windows. Voices. The crash of a cabinet being opened by hands that did not love it.

"They're tossing your father's drawers," Marisol said grimly.

"My father is past caring." Felix shifted the case higher. "Though if they damage the italic fount, I will haunt them, and I will be *meticulous* about it."

Marisol laughed — a short, startled sound, immediately strangled by fear, but real. That was Marisol all over. She had watched soldiers fire into a crowd of striking dockworkers from ten yards away, had seen men she'd shared bread with fall on the salt-stained stones, had walked to a stranger's print shop at midnight to make the government's lie answerable — and she still laughed when a joke arrived. Felix had decided, somewhere in the long nights of setting the massacre account, that this was the bravest thing she had ever witnessed: not the testimony, but the laugh surviving it.

They moved along the tanner's wall, boots silent on wet cobbles, the stink of hides thick enough to lean on. Behind them the noise of the search rose and fell. Once, distinctly, Teo's voice — indignant, theatrical, *far too loud* — protesting that a man of his age could not possibly lift a press stone and would they kindly stop breaking his shelving, some of it was original to the premises.

Then a new sound. Not shouting. The specific silence of handcuffs closing.

Felix stopped walking. The case nearly slipped; Marisol caught its edge with one hand and held Felix's arm with the other, and for a moment the two of them stood in the dark between the tannery arches, holding each other up and a tray of letters, while somewhere behind them an old man was being walked away in irons for the crime of having read too carefully his whole life long.

"He knew," Marisol said quietly. "He planned it. That's different."

"It isn't fair, though."

"No." Marisol took a breath. "But you know what he'd say if we stood here crying on a tanner's wall."

"He'd say my grief has a misplaced modifier."

"He'd say we haven't got time to mourn what isn't lost yet." She tugged. "Come on, printer. Third arch."

* * *

The third arch was a rusted door behind a curtain of drying hides, and behind it was a steep stone stair going down into the smell of the earth itself, and at the bottom of the stair was a lantern held by a woman Felix had never seen before, broad-shouldered, ink stains to the elbow — real ink, printer's ink, the black that never entirely leaves.

"Sandoval?" the woman said.

"Yes."

"Voss sent word you'd come tonight. Earlier than he hoped." Her eyes went to the case in Felix's arms, and something in her face changed — hunger, recognition, the look of a starving woman shown bread. "Is that—"

"One case," Felix said. "The best one."

The woman stepped aside. "It's not much down here. Two presses, three families, and a rat we've named after the Minister of the Interior. But the presses run."

Felix descended into the dark, lead against her heart, portfolio against Marisol's, Teo's brass stick warm in her fist where she'd gripped it so hard the edges had printed themselves into her palm. Grief came with her — it would come everywhere now, she understood that, it had joined the household and would carry its share of the luggage — but underneath the grief, steady as a forme locked in the chase, was the account. Sixty pages. Names. Dates. The truth about Salt Quay, typeset by hand by people who loved each other, waiting in a locked case like a seed.

Above them the city was still asleep, dreaming the government's dream.

Felix set the case down on a trestle table and began, with shaking fingers, to unlock it.
