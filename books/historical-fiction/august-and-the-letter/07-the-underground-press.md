## 7. The Underground Press

I had always thought a tannery smelled like the end of the world. Descending into the cellars beneath the Herrera & Sons works, I learned that beneath the end of the world there is another world entirely, and it smells like ink, tallow, and men who have not seen the sun since spring.

Felix went first down the ladder, the type case strapped to her back like a coffin lid, and I went second, because if the ladder broke I wanted it on record that I had died ahead of her and therefore could not be blamed for anything. The rungs were slick. Somewhere below, water dripped in a rhythm so regular it might have been typeset.

"Ladder's honest," called a voice from the dark, in the tone of a man who has tested many ladders against his own weight and been betrayed by several. "Third rung from the top lies about itself, but the rest are true."

The cellar opened up like the inside of a loaf of bread — low vaulted rooms, one leading to another, stone walls hung with drying hides overhead and, below the hides, the strangest congregation Valdorra had ever produced: three hand presses in various states of undress, a paper stock piled under oilcloth, a stereotype casting box glowing sullen orange at the far end like a hearth that had opinions, and perhaps a dozen people moving among them with the hushed economy of surgeons.

A woman came forward wiping her hands on an apron gone grey with lead dust. She was sixty if she was a day, built like a dockside crane, with spectacles pushed up into wild white hair.

"You're Reyes," she said. Not a question. "The one from Salt Quay."

"Marisol Reyes," I said. "And this—"

"I know who this is." The woman's eyes went past me to Felix and narrowed. "Sandoval. The son."

There was a pause I could have set type in. Felix, to her everlasting credit, did not flinch, did not look at me, did not do any of the dozen things that would have confirmed whatever the woman thought she knew or didn't. She only said, "And you're Berta Ocaña. Your shop on Candileja Street printed hymnals until '94."

Berta's mouth twitched. "Hymnals with certain pages left blank, yes. A printer learns to leave room for God's later revisions." She stuck out a hand like a boat cleat. Felix took it. Something was decided in that handshake, though I couldn't have told you what.

* * *

What was not decided — what took all night and most of my patience — was everything else.

They sat us at a scarred table under a lamp with a tin shade, and the whole network gathered around at what they probably imagined was a casual distance. There was Berta; her nephew Emilio, who ran the casting box and had the nervous habit of polishing every piece of metal within reach; a pair of brothers named Quintana who had carried a press disassembled through four checkpoints in a fish cart and told the story often enough that the fish cart had become a character; a young woman called Pilar who said almost nothing but watched Felix the way a cat watches a cupboard where the mice have gotten clever.

"Teo isn't here?" Felix asked, scanning faces.

"He was here until the raids started," Berta said. "Then he went to ground. Half our people are in ground of one sort or another." She folded her arms. "So. You bring us a case of type and a story. Everyone brings a story. Half the stories are bait. The prefecture pays by the head now, and pays better than composition ever did."

"The story isn't mine," I said. "It's mine and forty other people's. I stood on the quay when they fired. I carried Tomás Iriarte to the chapel steps and he died telling me the name of his mother, which was Carmen, and which the gazette will never print because the gazette says there were no deaths, only a disturbance, ably suppressed."

"Ably," repeated Emilio, with bitterness, buffing a brass rule to a shine it would never use again.

"Anyone can say they saw a massacre," Pilar said quietly. It was the first thing she'd said, and the room went still around it. "Informer or witness, the words sound the same in this cellar."

"That's fair," I said, "and I've got no way to prove my eyes. But she doesn't need proof for hers." I nodded at Felix. "Look at her hands."

Felix held them out, palms up, over the lamplight. Ink in every crease, worked in past washing, the permanent grey of someone who has handled type daily for years. You can't fake those hands. Believe me — I'd spent two days trying to keep my own clean enough to pass a magistrate's inspection and failing gloriously.

Berta looked at the hands for a long moment. Then she reached behind the lamp, produced something wrapped in a rag, and unwrapped it on the table.

An "e." Lowercase, twenty-four point, cast in soft metal and ruined — the counter punched through, the bowl caved as though some soldier's boot had found it interesting.

"Our fount of twenty-four point lost an argument with a gendarme's rifle butt in March," Berta said. "We've been composing around it ever since. Every page we set avoids that letter like a debt collector. Do you know how hard it is to write about liberty without the word *serpe* in it? Without *esperanza*? Try it sometime."

"What do you want," Felix said, "a new matrix?"

"We haven't the punch to cut one, and no time for casting trials. But there's another way, if you're the kind of compositor I've heard you are. Recut the letterform by hand. Wood, gutta-percha, whatever your memory gives us." Berta's eyes were sharp above a smile that wasn't quite a smile. "From memory, Sandoval. No specimen book. If your hands know their letters, we'll know whose side you're on."

Around the table, nobody moved. This was the examination, then. This was the whole cellar holding its breath.

"And if I can't?" Felix asked.

"Then you're welcome to sit here and feed paper, same as anyone. We're not monsters." Berta shrugged. "But we won't put our necks in a press for a stranger's testimony either. Down here, trust is a currency, and we're all of us poor."

* * *

They gave her a bench by the single good lamp, a block of seasoned pearwood, a graver, and a silence so thick I could hear the hides dripping upstairs.

Felix turned the broken "e" over and over in her fingers like a jeweler appraising a fake. Then she closed her eyes.

I have known Felix Sandoval since we were both children stealing figs off the cathedral wall, and I thought I knew all her faces — the shopkeeper's face, the son-of-the-house face, the terrified face she wore the night she came to open the door to my knocking at midnight. This was a new one. Her face went somewhere private and settled there, and her hands began to work without consulting her.

She drew the letter on the wood block first — freehand, no measuring tools, though she checked herself twice against the broken cast as if interrogating a hostile witness. Then the graver. The sound it made was small and exact, a whisper of shavings curling away, and the letter began to surface out of the pearwood like something rising through harbor water.

The network pretended not to watch. They watched magnificently badly. Emilio drifted past four times to polish things that did not need polishing. One of the Quintana brothers narrated, sotto voce, to the other: "Now the bowl. Mind the bowl. She's minding the bowl." Pilar did not drift, did not pretend; she simply stood at the edge of the lamplight with her arms crossed, and after a while her arms came uncrossed, which from her I gathered was applause.

"She cut her first form at six years old," I said into the quiet, because someone had to fill it and I have never once in my life declined the job. "Her father caught her setting her own name upside down in the case and made her print it anyway. It's still pinned inside the cabinet in the shop. Was. In the shop." The word *was* landed harder than I meant it to. Nobody remarked on it. Kindness, down here, meant letting things lie in the forme.

Felix worked for two hours. At some point Berta brought her coffee that could have stripped paint off a press bed, and Felix drank it without looking up, which is the highest compliment available between printers.

When she blew the last shaving from the bowl and held the block to the light, the cellar leaned in as one body.

It was beautiful. That's the only word. The letter was rounder than the original, warmer somehow, with a small irregularity in the terminal where her graver had chosen character over perfection — the kind of flaw that makes type look alive instead of manufactured. Berta took it, examined it against the ruined cast, tilted it, squinted through her spectacles and then over them.

"Well," she said at last. "Our old 'e' was machine-cut. Yours has a limp. But it's an honest limp." She pressed the block back into Felix's hands. "Set me a line with it. Right now. Prove it prints."

Emilio fetched the proofing press, and Felix locked up a line in half a minute — *la esperanza*, hope, the very word they'd gone without since March — and pulled the proof onto a scrap of blotting paper. The letters came up crisp and black and certain.

Pilar stepped forward and picked up the proof sheet. She read it, and read it again, and then she did something I suspect she did rarely: she smiled, briefly, like sun through fog.

"It's a good letter," she said. "It looks like it's been through something."

"So have we all," Berta said. Then she raised her voice to the cellar at large. "Right. The Sandoval shop prints with us. Anyone who doubts it can doubt it on their own time." And that was that. Twelve suspicious, exhausted, half-buried people became, in the space of a woodcut vowel, something I recognized: a family. I'd have known the shape anywhere. We'd assembled one ourselves out of a dead man's license and an orphaned apprentice and a dockworker with more nerve than sense.

We worked till near dawn planning it — the pamphlet, the full account, Marisol Reyes's testimony word for word, the names of the dead read out and set line by line. Berta estimated paper, Emilio estimated metal, the Quintana brothers argued cheerfully about fish carts. It was nearly four in the morning when Felix finally sat back, ink to the wrists, and laughed — actually laughed, tired and bright — at something Emilio said about gendarmes and hydraulics.

That was when the ladder creaked.

Every lamp but one went dark before my heart finished its next beat. Figures came up out of the shadows along the walls with the ease of habit; Berta put herself between the stairs and the presses, a boat cleat of a woman braced for weather. On the bottom rung, silhouetted against the grate of light from the street above, stood a man in a civil coat, raising both empty hands.

"Don't shoot," he said, in the careful voice of someone who has rehearsed this. "My name is Aurelio Voss. I'm the census taker." He paused, and I heard him choose his next words like a man choosing type from a case. "I've come to tell you the archive will be seized within days. And I've brought you something worth printing — if you'll let me live long enough to hand it over."
