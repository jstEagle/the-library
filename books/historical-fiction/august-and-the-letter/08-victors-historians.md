## 8. Victors' Historians

Aurelio Voss had always believed that an archive was a promise. You wrote a thing down, you dated it, you signed it, and somewhere in the vast gray machinery of the state a clerk filed your truth against the day someone might need it. It was, he had told his mother once, the closest thing to immortality a modest man could buy.

His mother had said that was the saddest sentence she'd ever heard from a boy who could whistle.

He thought of her often now, in the third week of his appointment to the Ministry's Historical Reconciliation Section, which occupied the top floor of the old customs house and smelled of sealing wax, cold coffee, and — Voss was increasingly certain — fear. The work was simple. The work was to unwrite.

On his first morning he had been given a desk by the window, a pot of glue the consistency of porridge, and eleven bound volumes of the *Gazette* covering October through December. His instructions, delivered by a supervisor named Colonel Brandt whose mustache entered rooms slightly before he did, were expressed in the ministry's usual language of gentle murder: *"Anomalies to be reconciled. Redundant coverage to be excised. Continuity of record paramount."*

Redundant coverage meant Salt Quay. Excised meant gone.

Voss spent that first morning with a razor blade and the glue pot, slicing columns out of the public record as neatly as a surgeon taking a tumor. The strike of the stevedores became "a brief labor interruption." The barricades of crates on Salt Quay became "unauthorized obstructions." And the afternoon of the fourteenth of November — when the soldiers formed their line and the drummer boy, God help them all, had actually beaten time before they fired — became nothing at all. White paper where a massacre used to be. He glued it down smooth. It held.

That was the thing about memory made official, Voss thought, pressing his thumb along the seam. It wasn't strong. It didn't need to be. Paper remembered whatever the last hand to touch it wanted remembered, and everyone simply agreed to have forgotten the difference.

* * *

By the second week he had been promoted, which he understood to be a threat. Brandt clapped him on the shoulder in the corridor — a blow that would have felled a smaller man — and said, "Voss, you've a tidy hand. Tidy mind. I've recommended you for the casualty reconciliation."

The casualty ledger lived in the vault below, in a room kept at a temperature suitable for neither paper nor people. It was the true ledger, the hospital one, the one compiled in the days after the firing by two overworked physicians and a nun with magnificent handwriting who had recorded, alongside each name, small clinical annotations that would haunt a stone statue. *Fractured skull. Age unknown. Carried in by his brother, who would not let go of the ankle.*

One hundred and nine names. The gazette, after reconciliation, would say seven, all trampled in a panic of the workers' own making, regrettable, closed matter.

Voss copied the ledger. He was a census taker; copying was his native art. He sat in that cold room with his coat buttoned to the throat and transcribed all one hundred and nine names in his tidy hand onto slips of paper thin enough to fold into the lining of his sleeve, and he did it with the peculiar calm of a man watching himself do something unforgivable from a great distance and finding, to his surprise, no objection.

There was a moment — he would remember this later, in worse moments — when he reached the forty-first entry and found a familiar name. *Reyes, Tomás. Dockhand. Died holding the door of the fish cellar. Survived by daughter, M.* Marisol's father. He sat with that for a long while, until the clerk monitoring the vault coughed meaningfully, and then he went on writing, because the dead deserved a tidy hand even if they were about to be unwritten.

* * *

"You look," said Inés Corvo, the archivist, "like a man committing a crime."

"I am reconciling anomalies," Voss said.

"Ah." Inés was seventy, half-blind, and had run the vault since before Brandt was weaned, and she said things like *ah* in a tone that suggested she had already reconciled him entirely. She was shelving folios beside him, unhurried. "You know what I did in the war of succession? Nothing. I shelved. Whatever they told me to shelve, wherever they told me to shelve it. Forty years, and do you know what I've learned?"

"That shelving is its own quiet heroism?"

"That paper outlives conviction." She slid a folio home with her palm. "They burn a pamphlet, another is printed. They burn a man, his brother remembers. But rewrite a ledger, file it correctly, stamp it — that lasts. That's the dangerous thing. Not the killing. The filing."

"Inés—"

"I'm shelving," she said, and moved off down the row, and if her route happened to take her past the guard desk, and if the guard happened to be called away to inspect a leaking pipe for eleven minutes, well. Inés Corvo had been shelving for forty years. She knew exactly how long a shelf took to fill.

* * *

He took the original ledger on a Thursday, because Thursday the vault closed early for the clerks' choir practice, a fact Voss had confirmed with three weeks of patient, census-takerly observation and which he still found absurd. He wore his good coat, the one that made him look like every other gray functionary in the building, because looking like every other functionary was the whole of the disguise anyone needed. The ledger went into his document case between a genuine memorandum on ink requisitions and a folded copy of the reconciliation order bearing Brandt's seal, which Voss had decided constituted either irony or armor.

At the door, Inés looked up from her desk, looked at the case, looked at him, and returned to her crossword.

"One across," she said. "Seven letters. *What remains when everything else is taken.*"

Voss stood in the doorway like a fool.

"Don't know it?" Inés asked mildly, filling in a square.

"...Testimony," Voss said.

"Mm," said Inés, who had already written it.

* * *

The tannery district after dark was a country with its own laws, none of them written, all of them enforced. Voss had never been south of the canal in his life except once, lost, at nineteen, and he navigated now by the instructions folded in his pocket in a hand that was not Felix Sandoval's — he'd learned her hand by now, the way it leaned — but Teo's, an old proofreader's script, precise as type: *Third tannery past the burned chandler's. Knock twice, wait, knock once. If asked, you are the man from the census. Do not apologize. Apologizing marks you.*

*I am the man from the census*, Voss thought, knocking twice on a door that smelled of lime and rendered fat. It was, he reflected, the truest thing he had said in months, and also the confession he could never make aloud: he had counted them. He had put his pen to their household and started all of this, or at least stood close enough to the start to feel the heat of it.

A slot opened. An eye. Then Marisol Reyes opened the door all the way, and behind her, in a cellar lit by two lamps and crowded with press frames, type cabinets, and the faces of perhaps a dozen strangers pretending not to listen, stood the underground press of Valdorra.

"The man from the census," Marisol said. Her voice was flat. Her father's name was forty-first in a ledger under his arm, though she couldn't know that yet, and something about the set of her shoulders suggested she suspected every possible version of why he'd come.

"I've brought—" Voss began, and stopped, because there were too many sentences available and all of them were wrong. *I've brought your father's death. I've brought the only honest paperwork the government produced this winter. I've brought the end of my life as previously scheduled.*

He set the document case on the composing table instead and opened it, and lifted out the ledger with both hands, the way he had seen Felix Sandoval lift the tray of type she carried out of a burning chapter of her life.

"The original," he said. "Casualties at Salt Quay. One hundred and nine names, with the physicians' notes. What the gazette will say instead is seven, and by spring there will be no copy of this left anywhere in Valdorra except—" he looked around at the presses, the lamps, the wary faces — "wherever you manage to print it."

Silence. Then a thin old man Voss recognized from wanted-poster descriptions as Teo stepped forward, took the ledger, angled it toward the lamp, and read. His lips moved. Somewhere around the sixtieth name he stopped moving them and simply held the book, and Voss watched an entire cellar of suspicious, armed, exhausted people become, in the space of a page turn, something with a single heartbeat.

"They're going to erase them," Teo said quietly. "All of them. Every name."

"That is my department's stated objective, yes," Voss said. "I resigned from it approximately ten minutes ago. Formally, I suppose, just now, by burglary."

Someone laughed — a short, startled bark from the back — and the tension cracked like ice on the canal. Marisol came forward and touched the ledger's cover, lightly, the way you touch something that might be a ghost.

"Why?" she said. "Why bring this? You'll hang."

"Because I spent three weeks discovering," Voss said, "that the state can kill a hundred and nine people, but it cannot bear to *count* them. Counting is my profession. Someone ought to do it honestly."

Felix Sandoval emerged from behind the press then, ink to the wrists, and looked at him for a long moment — at the gray coat, the tidy case, the census taker's face — and Voss braced for the accusation he deserved.

Instead she said only, "Can you set type?"

"...No."

"Then tonight," she said, turning back to the press, pulling the lamp closer, "you'll hold pages. Everyone holds pages."

And above them, faint through the tannery floorboards, came the sound Voss had been listening for all evening without admitting it: boots. Many of them, moving south along the canal, toward the district, in no particular hurry — the unhurried tread of men who knew exactly where they were going and had been given all night to get there.

Teo looked up from the ledger. "How long do we have?"
