## 9. Setting the Final Page

The last day of the century smelled of tallow, ink, and the particular metallic dread of a pressroom running out of time.

Felix had learned to tell time by the press. In her father's shop she could have stood blindfolded in the composing room and named the hour by which job was on the stone — the morning invoices at eight, the funeral broadsides at noon, the wedding invitations that made Teo sigh and say *love pays worse than grief but smells sweeter*. Here in the cellar beneath the tannery district there was no window to read, no clock worth trusting, only the rhythm of the Vanderberg proof press that Marisol's cousin had hauled down three flights of stairs on his back and refused ever afterward to discuss.

By that rhythm, it was late afternoon on the thirty-first of December, 1900, and they were four signatures behind.

"Hand me the quoins," Felix said, and Marisol put them in her palm without either of them looking — the way you hand someone a candle in the dark, or a knife.

Above them, muffled through layers of brick and cured hides and the ordinary commerce of the tannery district, the city was preparing to celebrate. Bells would ring at midnight. The harbor guns would fire. People were buying sweet breads and paper hats and cheap noisemakers, and the government had declared a holiday from truth that was going very well indeed, thank you, with posters on every wall announcing the *tragic accident* at Salt Quay, regrettable, unavoidable, closed.

In the cellar, forty reams of thin paper waited like a held breath.

"Read me the ledger again," Felix said. "Page six."

Teo's voice came from the corner where he sat with the stolen casualty ledger open on his knees, reading by a lamp turned so low it was nearly a rumor. He was older than the century and intended, he had told them, to outlive it out of spite. His hands shook now — they had shaken since the soldiers took him in April and gave him back in August with frostbitten fingers and a limp he described as "a souvenir" — but his voice did not shake at all.

"Reyes, Tomás, stevedore, age thirty-one. Reyes, Lucia, fishwife, age twenty-eight." His own name for the woman he'd never met. "Okafor, Samuel, coal-heaver—"

"Stop." Felix lifted the galley to the light and checked the line. *Okafor, Samuel.* The e was slightly proud of its neighbors. She had cut that letterform herself in October, in the first week, when the underground's only supply of e's had been dropped down a stairwell by a nervous courier. She touched it now with her ink-blackened thumb the way other people touched saints' feet in churches.

"It'll do," she said.

"It always does," Marisol said. "You say 'it'll do' like the letter might argue."

"Letters argue. That's the whole trade." Felix locked the chase, tightened the quins, slid the forme onto the bed of the press. "My father used to say every sort is a small stubborn person who wants to be somewhere else. Your job is to convince them that where they are is exactly where they meant to be all along."

"And yours succeeded?"

"My father's did. Mine we'll find out at midnight."

* * *

They had been printing since dawn and would print until the bells, and then — this was the plan, such as it was, drawn in charcoal on a hide stretched over barrels — they would print one hour more, while the whole city stood in the plazas counting down and the streets filled with the one crowd all year that no one could disperse without scandal. Twelve thousand copies of the true account of Salt Quay. The massacre as Marisol had seen it. The ledger Voss had carried out of the archive in his coat, knowing — he had said it plainly, standing in the doorway with the book against his chest like a man carrying his own heart into surgery — that it ended his life as he had lived it.

Fourteen names. Fourteen dead stevedores and their wives and one boy of fifteen whom the gazette had not mentioned because the gazette had not mentioned any of them, having reported instead a riot, begun by workers, resolved by the firm hand of lawful authority.

"You're smiling," Marisol said. "Nobody smiles while inking a roller. It's unnatural."

"I'm thinking about Voss. He used to correct my father's punctuation when he came to count us. He once stood in our shop and informed Felix Sandoval, junior, that the license renewal form contained a comma splice." She rolled ink across the type. "He risked being shot to fix a document's grammar, and now he's committed treason, and I would bet the press itself that his last thought before he ran was that the casualty ledger's column headings weren't aligned."

"They weren't," came Teo's voice from the corner. "I checked."

Felix laughed — really laughed, a short helpless sound that bounced off the low ceiling — and Marisol caught it, and for a moment the cellar was just a workshop full of people who loved each other, doing the thing they were best at, on the most important night of their lives.

Then the floor above them creaked in boot-time, and nobody smiled anymore.

* * *

The tannery district had its own weather. The vats breathed out a fog that lay in the streets till noon, and the soldiers sweeping toward them moved through it like slow shadows, door to door, list in hand. Twice already a runner had come down the stairs — the tanner's daughter, eleven years old, ferociously serious about her duties — to report positions. The second time she had added, with the flat delivery of a child repeating something she did not understand: "The officer says anyone found with printed matter after midnight will be shot before the new year. He said it like it was a good bargain."

"He would," said Teo. "Soldiers always think they get to keep the old century and shoot the new one in its crib. They're wrong both times."

"Keep reading," Felix said. "Page nine."

But the arithmetic was merciless and everyone in the room was doing it. Twelve thousand copies. The press ran perhaps three hundred an hour when the formes were ready and the boys — Marisol's cousins, quick and silent as cats — kept the paper coming. They needed every minute until well past the bells.

And the press knew it. Felix was certain of this. Machines were like sorts; they were small stubborn persons who wanted to be somewhere else.

It jammed at half past eleven, on the last signature, the final sheet of the fourteen names, with the sound of a large animal deciding against cooperation.

"No," Felix said, in the reasonable tone she used on inanimate objects, which fooled no one, including the objects. "No, no, *no*—"

She had it apart in ninety seconds, sleeves shoved up, arms elbow-deep in the mechanism, Marisol holding the lamp, Teo reading names aloud over them steady as a tide because he had decided that the names should not stop being spoken until they were printed, and the cousins feeding paper by hand to keep anything moving at all. The trouble was a twisted delivery tape and beneath it, worse, a shifted piece in the forme itself — the lock-up had loosened with ten hours of pounding, and a line of type had walked, and pieing it back together would cost them twenty minutes they did not have.

"Pull it," Felix said. "Give me the whole line."

Boots on the stairs.

Not the daughter's light feet. Not the cousins'. Boots, plural, deliberate, descending the three flights from the street door with the unhurried confidence of men who had done this many times tonight and been rewarded each time.

"Out the vat tunnel," Marisol whispered. "Now. You can take the forme through the—"

"Set the final lines by hand," Felix said. Her voice came out very quiet and very steady, and she was almost surprised to hear it, the way you're surprised by your own signature appearing under your hand. "There's no time to reset the forme. Hand-set the last two lines directly on the stone, print from the stone, and pull the sheets while I hold the frame. Go. Teo, dictate."

"*Sandoval,*" Teo said — and she understood, in one cold bright instant, what he was doing. The last lines of the pamphlet were the imprint. Not the government's lie. Their own colophon, set by law in every honest publication since printing began: the account of the Salt Quay massacre, printed by the Sandoval press, Valdorra, this last day of the year 1900.

Her name. Her father's name. On the record. No more sons borrowed from the registry, no more licenses held by the dead. Just the truth and who had set it.

Marisol was already composing, her dockworker's fingers — fingers that had tied knots in storm rigging and pulled drowning men from black water — moving among the sorts fast and sure, picking letters by feel in the lamplight the way Felix had taught her over three months of nights. *"Printed by the Sandoval press—"*

The door at the top of the stairs opened.

Felix put her shoulder to the press frame and pulled the first sheet, and the impression came up clean, every letter sharp, *Okafor, Samuel*, her own recut e sitting proudly in its place, and boots were on the landing now, and Teo's voice never wavered:

*"Valdorra. This thirty-first day of December, in the year one thousand nine hundred."*

"Incoming!" shouted one of the cousins from the stairhead, and glass broke somewhere above, and Marisol slammed the last sort — the final period, a full stop, the smallest and most stubborn person in the case — onto the stone, and Felix pulled the second sheet, and the third, feeding them behind her to the boys who stuffed them into satchels and vanished down the vat tunnel one by one into the dark.

The door burst open.

Felix did not turn around. There is a moment in every printer's life — her father had told her this, laughing, the week before he died — when everything else in the world stops existing except the sheet coming up off the type, and whether the impression is true.

Hers was true.

She drew the last sheet clear, held it up to the lamplight — fourteen names, one testimony, one imprint, complete — and heard Marisol say, pleasantly, dangerously, to the men with rifles in the doorway:

"Gentlemen. Careful where you step. That's history drying."

And from the top of the stairs, cutting through the noise of splintered wood and shouted orders, came a voice Felix knew — precise, weary, punctilious to the last — saying, "Not those men. Those are mine. I'll need everyone downstairs to remain exactly where they are while I inspect these documents. All of them." A pause. "Starting with the comma splice."
