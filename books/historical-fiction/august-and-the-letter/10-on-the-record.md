## 10. On the Record

I have never trusted a crowd before that night. I had been one of them, after all — at Salt Quay, shoulder to shoulder with men who smelled of tar and herring, and I had watched that same shoulder-to-shoulderness become the thing the soldiers used as an excuse. A crowd, I had learned, is what governments call people when they want permission to fire.

But on New Year's Eve, 1900, standing in the mouth of Copperlane with two hundred pamphlets in a fish crate against my hip, I looked out at the midnight crowd of Valdorra and thought: *all right. You'll do.*

The bells were still ringing when Felix handed me the first stack. Her fingers were black to the second knuckle with ink, and there was a smear of it across her cheekbone like a dueling scar, and I have never — before or since — seen anyone look so much like they had earned their own face.

"Read one first," she said. "Before you hand them out. If you're going to be arrested for sedition, you should at least know the sentence structure."

"I know the sentence structure," I said. "You read it to me four times while you corrected the commas."

"Fifth time's the charm."

The crowd came down from the cathedral square where the official New Year had been celebrated with fireworks and a band playing something martial, and it flowed into Copperlane the way water finds a gutter, and we began handing out paper like it was bread in a famine. Which, if you think about it, it was.

Most people took a pamphlet the way you take anything from a stranger at night — suspiciously, with the corners pinched, ready to drop it and swear innocence. Then they'd read the first line. *On the fourteenth of October, at Salt Quay, soldiers of the Republic fired upon unarmed stevedores, and forty-one citizens died.* And their faces changed. Not all at once. It moved across a face like weather — first confusion (this was not what the gazette had said), then fear (someone will see me reading this), then something harder underneath the fear, something that made people fold the pamphlets small and put them inside coats, which was exactly wrong for us but exactly right for them, because a folded pamphlet inside a coat gets carried home, and a thing carried home gets read twice, and a thing read twice gets talked about over supper, and no government on earth has ever figured out how to police a supper table.

An old woman stopped in front of me with her pamphlet shaking in her hands. She wore mourning black gone grey at the seams, the kind of black that has been worn so long it has become simply clothes.

"My son," she said. "Emiliano Ruiz. Is he — is he in here? In the names?"

"He's in the names," I said. "Page three. All forty-one."

She pressed the paper flat against her chest the way you'd press a hand to a wound, and she looked at me, and she said, "They told me he was a rioter. They made me sign a paper saying so, for the compensation, which was nothing." Her jaw worked. "He couldn't even swim. Why would a man who can't swim start a fight on a quay?"

I had no answer for that. There isn't one. So I put another pamphlet in her hands — "for your sister, for whoever needs to see his name spelled right" — and she went off into the crowd upright, like a woman who had been given back something the state had confiscated, and I turned to find Felix watching me with tears cutting clean lines through the ink on her face.

"Don't," I said. "Don't cry yet. We still have sixty copies and the harbor side."

"I'm not crying," she said, crying. "It's the lead dust. Occupational hazard."

By two in the morning the harbor district had them. By dawn they were on ships — this part I did not see myself, but Teo told it later, and Teo never embellished except about himself. A purser on the *Cormorant* out of Galdes took twenty copies sewn into a tarpaulin of sailcloth. A Norwegian second mate who could barely read Spanish took ten anyway, saying in his blunt way that his sister translated for him and that his sister was better than any government. Two copies went into the diplomatic mail pouch of a nation whose ambassador was, at that moment, toasting the Republic's continued stability at an official ball. History has a sense of humor. It just takes decades to land the joke.

* * *

Teo came home in June.

We knew the date of his release weeks before it happened, because the whole tannery district somehow knew, the way districts know things — through wives and cousins and a boy who delivered ice and saw everything. On the appointed day Felix scrubbed the front step of our new rooms until the stones showed their original color, and I cooked the meal Teo had dreamed aloud about during his first week underground, which was white beans with sausage and too much garlic, and we waited.

He came up the street at midday carrying his carpetbag and wearing a prison suit that fit him worse than his own clothes ever had, and he had aged — of course he had aged; eight months in San Miguelo puts eight years on a man's shoulders — but his eyes went straight past us to the press in the corner, our secondhand beauty, bought with smuggled funds and blessed by everyone in the cellar network in turn.

"Well," he said. "Somebody's been letting my spacing go slack."

"There is nothing wrong with your spacing," Felix said.

"There is always something wrong with the spacing. That's what proofreading *is*." He set down the bag, looked around the room slowly — at Felix, at me, at the press, at the window with its view of laundry lines and chimney smoke — and then he sat down heavily on our best chair and put his face in his hands and stayed that way for a long moment while we stood there not knowing whether to touch him.

When he lifted his head he was smiling, wet-eyed and crooked. "The beans," he said, "are burning."

"The beans are *caramelizing*," I said. "Sit down. You're home."

He ate three servings. He told us almost nothing about prison, in the way of men who have decided what to tell and will get to it in installments over years. What he did say, apropos of nothing, fork halfway to his mouth, was: "They asked me, at the trial. Who printed the pamphlets. I told them a printing press prints. Do you blame the hammer for the nail?" He shrugged. "The judge didn't laugh. But the court stenographer did. I heard her. That's how I knew we'd won something."

* * *

Voss's letter came in the autumn of 1902.

It arrived with no return address, postmarked from a port city in a country whose name I had to look up, addressed in a careful clerk's hand to *the Sandoval print shop, or wherever it has removed itself to.* Inside was a single sheet, and when I unfolded it Felix made a sound like she'd been struck, because the letter was typeset. Printed. Set in lead.

And we knew the type. God help us, we knew every nick and every worn shoulder of it, because it was ours — the tray we had carried out of the shop on the night of the raid, the type that had fled with us through the tannery cellars and printed the massacre account and the ledger and the forty-one names. Voss had acquired a page of it somewhere, somehow — probably from one of the network's safe houses, probably paid for, probably at absurd risk to a man who had already spent his career and his country for less — and he had set his letter by hand, letter by letter, the way you would speak someone else's language to prove you had learned it.

The text itself was short. I have it in front of me now, in the frame on the wall, and I can transcribe it exactly:

*I have taken a position as a registrar in a small port town where nobody's history is interesting, including mine. I am content. The census here records bakers as bakers and daughters as daughters, and no one asks a question too many. I read the account of Salt Quay in a foreign newspaper eleven months after setting the correction that made its printing possible, and I confess I wept in a public reading room, which caused comment. I wish to state for the record, since you of all people understand records: I filed the form falsely. Aurelia Sandoval exists. She always did. I merely wrote down what the empire refused to. Yours in good standing with no institution whatsoever — A. Voss.*

Felix read it four times. Then she went to the case, chose a piece of type at random, and held it up to the light like a jeweler checking a stone.

"He set 'falsely' with an em-dash before it," she said. "That's correct usage. The man was born to be a printer and got lost on the way."

"Write him back," I said.

"With what address? 'Dear Aurelio, somewhere abroad'?" But she was smiling, and later that year, whenever one of our pamphlets went out on a ship, she took to slipping one copy aside, marked *for the registrar*, and handing it to whichever crew seemed most likely to lose it in the right direction. Whether any of them reached him we never learned. Some letters you send on faith.

* * *

Years pass. This is the part nobody warns you about in the middle of a revolution: afterward, there are years, and they contain dishes.

The Republic fell in 1906 — quietly, embarrassingly, the way rotten things fall, more from its own weight than our pamphleteering, though I have never once let a listener believe that order of causation. The new government was imperfect, being made of people, but it commissioned an inquiry into Salt Quay, and the inquiry wanted documents.

They came to us because there were no other documents to come to. The old archive had been seized and scattered; the gazettes had been rewritten; the official casualty ledger had been doctored until it recorded a riot with three deaths, all of them unfortunate accidents involving the soldiers' own falling rifles. What remained — what survived — was the Sandoval printing: Marisol Reyes, eyewitness, verified by name; the true ledger, stolen by a census officer whose testimony accompanied it; the forty-one names, each one spelled correctly, because Felix Sandoval checked spelling the way other people check for fires.

A historian came to the shop — we had a proper shop again by then, on a street with trees, and FELIX SANDOVAL & COMPANY, PRINTERS painted on the glass without apology or alias. She was a young woman from the university with ink-stained cuffs of her own, and she handled our surviving first edition of the massacre account with white gloves, turning pages as if they might wake up.

"It's extraordinary," she kept saying. "Every other record contradicts every other record. This is the only source that agrees with itself. How did you — how does a document survive like this?"

Felix considered the question with the seriousness she gave all questions concerning type.

"You print enough copies," she said, "and you put them in enough hands, and you spell the dead correctly. After that, the truth is very hard to burn. Believe me. People tried."

"And the others? The ones who helped? Teodorin the proofreader, the census officer—"

"Teo is in the back arguing with the comma placement on a wedding invitation. The census officer sends us a typeset card every New Year. This year's said, 'Still no questions worth asking here.'" She smiled. "History isn't only what historians keep, madam. It's what printers refuse to lose."

The historian wrote it all down. Later, in her book, there is a footnote — historians love footnotes the way Felix loves em-dashes — and the footnote reads, in full: *"All citations herein to the events of 14 October 1899 refer to the Sandoval printing, the sole surviving contemporary record."*

Sole surviving. Teo proofread that phrase three times and declared it, for once, unimprovable.

As for me — I am Marisol Reyes, dockworker, eyewitness, and these days co-owner of a print shop, and I will tell you what I learned between one century and the next. For years I lived as a name nobody would write down, and Felix lived as a name that wasn't hers, and Teo lived as a man the state decided to forget. We hid. We lied well and badly by turns. And then one night we stopped hiding and started printing, and I understood that identity is not something you defend behind locked doors.

It is something you set in lead. It is something you hand to strangers in the dark. And if you spell it right, it holds.

Last month a girl came to the shop asking for an apprenticeship. Seventeen, quick hands, ink already under her nails from some illicit press of her own devising. Felix interviewed her with great gravity while pretending to sort type.

"Why do you want to print?" Felix asked her.

The girl thought about it honestly, which is rarer than talent. "Because somebody has to write down what actually happened," she said.

Felix looked at me over the girl's head. I shrugged: *your call.*

"Start Monday," Felix said. "First lesson: we begin with the type cases. There are forty-one names in that tray over there, and before you touch anything else, you're going to learn to set every single one of them correctly."

The girl looked at the tray — at the worn, beloved letters that had crossed a city in a fleeing man's arms and come out the other side of a century still legible — and nodded solemnly.

"And after that?" she asked.

"After that," Felix said, "we find out what happened this year that nobody's writing down. There's always something. Bring your hands."
