## 1. The Counting Season

The trouble with keeping a lighthouse alone, Ines Sandoval had decided by her third winter of it, was not the loneliness. Loneliness was a room you could learn to live in, the way you learned to live with a drafty window — you stuffed it with rags and got on with things. No, the trouble was arithmetic.

Everything on a light station came down to counting. Count the wicks. Count the oil drums in the storehouse — eleven and a half, and the mail boat would not come again for nine days. Count the turns of the clockwork that rotated the lens, four hundred and forty to the full cycle, and pray the weight descended evenly through the night as it was meant to, because if it jammed at three in the morning you went up those stairs yourself, in your nightgown, cursing in two languages.

And now, this winter, she counted something new: heartbeats under her floorboards.

Four of them, when there ought officially to be one.

She stood at the kitchen window that first Tuesday of December, watching the river do its slow gray work below the point, and performed her morning inventory out loud, because saying things aloud kept them true. "One keeper," she said. "One lamp. One kettle." The kettle sat on the stove, sulking steam. "One bed."

The bed was the problem. There were, in fact, three beds in the station — hers upstairs, and the big iron-framed one in the keeper's cottage proper where she had slept with Mateo for six years before the river took him, and then the cot in the cellar room, which had not been in any inventory ever filed with the Lighthouse Board, and which held, most nights, a family of three who did not exist according to the government of this country.

"One bed too many," she murmured, and drank her coffee, which had gone bitter, which was fitting.

Outside, the gulls were arguing over something dead near the boat slip, their voices like rusty hinges. The sky had the flat pewter look she had learned to read the way other women read faces. Storm coming, that sky said. Not today. Not tomorrow. But soon, and seriously. The old fishermen called December through February the counting season — count your wood, count your salt fish, count your luck — because the great storms came off the water without invitation and stayed as long as they pleased, and what you hadn't laid in by the time the ice broke, you did without until spring.

Ines had counted. She had counted very well indeed. It was the one thing she could be proud of this winter, and she took her pride where she found it.

* * *

The cellar room had been Mateo's idea, back when it was merely a place for pickles.

He'd walled off the northwest corner of the cellar the autumn before his brother Rafael's second attempt at crossing, put up studs and plaster, run a stovepipe through the coal chute's old flue, and told the supply merchant it was a root cellar improvement, which was even technically true, because the shelves still held jars. He'd done it grinning, hammer behind his ear, saying, *A lighthouse keeper should have somewhere dark to keep things, no? Everyone expects it.* Mateo had joked his way through life the way water finds its way downhill — effortlessly, inevitably — and it had occurred to Ines more than once since his drowning that he must have known, somehow, that the room would matter. He built shelters the way other men built debts: ahead of need.

Now the room held a mattress, a small iron stove, a curtain across the doorway that pretended to be a wall, and Marisol and Rafael and Tomás, who had come in October on a moonless night, poling across from the far bank in a leaky skiff with everything they owned in two pillowcases and a birdcage with no bird in it.

"The cage was Tomás's idea," Marisol had explained, deadpan, the first night, when Ines raised an eyebrow at it. "We might catch a bird later."

They might. Ines had lived long enough among desperate people to know you didn't argue with a child's birdcage.

Rafael was worse than he had been in spring — she could hear it in his breathing through the floor seams on cold mornings, a wet whistle where his lungs ought to have been quiet — but he insisted on working. Every day he mended things that did not need mending: the spare davit blocks, the hinges on the oil house, a chair that had been perfectly sound since 1882. A man cannot hide in a cellar and remain a man, he said. He needed to fix something real each day or he would go quietly mad, and so Ines found him tasks, and Marisol rolled her eyes at the repaired hinges, and everyone agreed this arrangement kept the whole household sane, more or less, depending on the day.

Tomás, seven years old and made entirely of questions, was the hard part.

Children are not built for cellars. Tomás had been born in a courtyard in a city where the winters smelled of chestnuts, and now he lived in a room the size of a hay wagon with two adults and a birdcage, and every day the boy pressed himself to the one high window — a foot of glass at ground level, screened by the lilac hedge — and watched the frozen river with an expression Ines recognized and dreaded. It was not boredom. Boredom would have been fine. It was *calculation*. He was measuring the ice. He was deciding whether it would hold him.

"Tomás," she said one morning, finding him at the high window with his mittens already on, "what do we say about the window?"

"It's a boring window."

"And what do we do about boring windows?"

"We don't look at them." He said it with the weary patience of a child humoring a foolish adult. "But Tía Ines. If the ice is thick enough, we wouldn't need a ferry at all. We could just *walk*."

"You could just walk," she agreed, crouching to his level, "directly into the river, which is a thing even the fish avoid doing in January. Do you know why?"

"Because they'd freeze."

"Because they're *smart*, and they have good sense about ice, and I expect the same of boys." She straightened his hat, which was her own husband's, too big, brim folded twice. "Spring comes early some years. The ferry runs again. And until then—" she tapped the tip of his nose— "boring windows."

"Boring windows," he sighed, and went off to help his father ruin a perfectly good chair.

Ines watched him go and felt the familiar arithmetic turn over in her chest. Two berths on the ferry north. Three of them. She had done the sums every night for a week, lying awake beside the cold space where Mateo used to sleep, and the sums refused to come out right no matter how she arranged them, and she had begun, guiltily, shamefully, to hope that someone else would solve the problem for her — the marshal, the weather, God, anyone. Then she hated herself for the hoping, and got up, and trimmed the wicks.

* * *

At four o'clock the light needed preparing, and preparing the light was the only hour of the day when Ines felt entirely certain of herself.

She climbed the tower the way she always did, unhurried, one hand on the rail, ninety-two steps and she could have done it blind, had nearly done it blind once in the October gale when the lantern's oil had guttered and the wind had torn the words out of her mouth before they became screams. At the top, in the lantern room, the great lens waited in its cage of brass, patient as a cathedral, and beyond it the river lay all the way to the horizon gray-blue and glittering with its first skin of ice at the edges.

She cleaned the glass first — inside and out, though the outside would only get dirty again, which was the whole of housekeeping in a sentence — then filled the reservoir, trimmed the five wicks to exactly the height the manual demanded and her own judgment slightly improved upon, and wound the clockwork. Four hundred and forty turns. The counterweight descended through the night at its own dignified pace, rotating the lens so the beam swept the water once every minute, saying the same single word over and over to the darkness: *here, here, here.*

Mateo had loved that about the light. *It never says anything clever,* he'd told her once, turning the crank beside her, his sleeves rolled up over his forearms. *Just here. But it's the one word everybody out there is praying for.* He had said it lightly, because he said everything lightly, but she had thought about it every single night since the night the river declined to answer his prayers.

She lit the wicks at dusk, watched them take, adjusted the flame to the clean white color that meant it would burn steady, and stood a moment with her hand flat against the warm brass.

Below, through the tower's ribs, she could see the cottage windows glowing. Marisol cooking — the smell of onions and the last of the good paprika climbing the stairwell. Rafael coughing, and trying to cough quietly, which was somehow worse to hear than loud. Tomás, no doubt, at his boring window.

Here, said the light, sweeping the river. Here. Here.

It was almost enough to make her believe the word applied to all of them.

* * *

The mail boat came Thursday, late, its hull rimed with spray that had frozen into a kind of armor, and Ines went down the cliff path to meet it with her list in her mitten and her face arranged into the expression she thought of as her public self: competent, unhurried, mildly bored. A widow-keeper, neat as a pin, nothing under her roof but lamps and grief.

"Papers for you," called the pilot, a boy named Ellery whose voice kept breaking in inconvenient octaves. "And a passenger, Mrs. Sandoval. Government fella. Doing the census."

The word landed on the ice of her mind and sat there, thin and sharp as a skate blade.

"A census," she repeated. Her voice came out perfectly level. She was almost proud of it. "In December?"

"Winter enumeration for the light stations," said the passenger himself, stepping carefully onto the slip. He was young — thirty, perhaps — with a ledger under his arm and the particular posture of a man whose collar has never once been comfortable. He removed his hat politely. His hands, she noted, were red with cold, and he noted her noting them, and something quick moved behind his eyes, taking stock of her the way she was taking stock of him. "August Reyes. Census office. I'll need an afternoon of your time, Mrs.—"

"Sandoval. Keeper Sandoval." She shook his gloved hand. His grip was careful, neither firm nor weak, a handshake belonging to a man who measured things professionally. "An afternoon. Well. The light doesn't keep itself, Mr. Reyes, but the storm season hasn't properly started. You've come in the gap between weathers. Lucky."

"Yes," he said, looking past her shoulder, up the cliff, at the station. At the cottage. At, she realized with a small cold drop of her stomach, the smoke rising from the chimney — one chimney, one fire, but burning generous and constant, because there were four souls in the house and fires knew how to count. "Lucky," he agreed, and opened his ledger against his forearm, and wrote something down.

Behind her, up in the cottage, faint on the wind, a child laughed.

Tomás, laughing at some joke of his father's, the sound carrying the way children's laughter does across cold air, bright and clear and utterly unaccountable for.

Ines smiled at August Reyes with everything she had — the smile of a woman with one bed and no secrets — and began composing, even then, even as she led him up the path, the smooth and simple truth-shaped lie she would tell him about who wintered at Point Alder Light.

*She was very good at this,* she reminded herself, climbing. *She had been keeping more than a light lit for a long time now.*

She only wished she believed it quite as firmly as she needed to.
