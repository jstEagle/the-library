## 2. A Man with a Ledger

The mail boat *Puffin* had no business being out on the water in the first week of December, but Captain Hollis had never met a schedule he respected more than his own stubbornness, and so August Reyes stood in the bow with his collar up and his census case clutched to his chest like a hot brick he was unwilling to share.

"Reyes," Hollis had said at the dock that morning, spitting into the wind out of pure ceremony, "nobody does a winter count. The government knows that. You know that."

"The government," August had replied, "does not know anything except what I write down for it."

Which was true, and which was also the whole trouble with the job. August Reyes had been a census taker for six years, and in six years he had learned that the ledger was a kind of lie told very slowly, one name at a time. People were not households. People were not occupations and ages and whether they could read and write. People were the smell of bread through a door, or the way a child's boots sat by a hearth, too small to belong to anyone on the form. But the form did not care, and the form paid eleven dollars a month plus mileage, and mileage in Point Alder meant standing in the bow of a boat while the river threw handfuls of its own ice at him like a beggar insulted by the size of the coin.

"There she is," Hollis said, nodding ahead. "The widow's light."

The Point Alder station rose off the promontory the way a lighthouse always does — suddenly, as if it had been hiding behind the fog all morning just to see your face when it appeared. White tower, black gallery, keeper's cottage tucked against its flank like a child against a mother's hip. Smoke rose from the cottage chimney in a straight, disciplined line, which struck August as somehow characteristic of a place he'd never been. Even the smoke here kept its accounts in order.

"She keeps a good light," Hollis said. "Best on the river since her husband went under. Better, some say." He said it the way men say things they have decided not to explain further, and steered for the landing.

The berth was iced over. Hollis's boy threw a line, missed, and August — city-bred, ledger-handed, a man whose relationship with rope was strictly professional — caught it on the second try and made fast without thinking about it, which surprised them all including himself.

"You'll want to mind the walk up," Hollis said. "Ice took a man's hip last February."

"I'll take the hill seriously."

"Hill's not the problem." Hollis looked up at the tower, then back at August, and whatever he meant, he folded it away and lit his pipe instead. "Mind how you go."

* * *

The walk from the landing to the station took twelve minutes and cost August both his dignity and the feeling in his ears. The path switchbacked up through bare birches, each one wearing frost like a borrowed white coat, and twice he went down on one knee in a way he would later describe to himself as *deliberate*. At the top, the wind found him properly for the first time — a big flat-shouldered wind coming down the river valley with nothing to slow it — and he understood, in a general way, why someone would build a light here, and in a much more specific way, why someone would need paying to live in it.

The cottage door opened before he knocked.

She had seen him coming, obviously. From the gallery rail you could watch the whole path; a keeper who didn't watch her approach was a keeper asking to be robbed, or worse. So August was prepared for a woman who had already measured him from two hundred yards, and still the measurement, when it landed, felt like something done to him rather than by him.

Ines Sandoval was not tall. She wore a man's oilskin coat over a grey dress, sleeves rolled to the elbow as though she had just come in from work, which she almost certainly had. Her hair was dark, shot through early with silver at the temples, pinned back with the practical violence of a woman who has decided hair is an administrative matter. She held the door half-shut behind her — not rude, exactly. Economical. A door is warmth, and warmth is fuel, and fuel is counted.

"Mr. Reyes," she said. Not a question.

"Mrs. Sandoval." He got his hat off. His gloves stayed on, because his hands were past the point where pride could help them. "Bureau of the Census. I'm sent to take the winter enumeration of the light station and surrounding district. I wrote ahead — "

"You wrote. I read. It's cold, Mr. Reyes, and you look like a man the river's already tried once today." She stepped back and opened the door fully, and the warmth came out and wrapped him like a debt being forgiven. "Come in before the heat gets out arguing with you."

He liked her immediately and distrusted himself for it. Six years of this work had taught him that liking a subject was the first way a count went wrong.

* * *

The inside of the cottage was small, clean, and organized the way ships are organized: nothing decorative, everything necessary, and every necessary thing in the place where a stranger's hand would look for it last. A stove that had been fed recently enough to tick. A table scrubbed pale. On the wall, a framed print of a saint August didn't recognize, holding a lantern instead of the usual prop, which struck him as either devotion or irony.

"Sit there," she said, indicating the chair nearest the stove. "That one wobbles, so don't sit there and then blame my floor." She set a kettle on without asking whether he wanted tea, because in this part of the country tea was not a question but a condition of hospitality, and refusing it would have been a declaration of war.

August unpacked his ledger, his ink, his pen, and the printed schedules, arranging them square on the table. It was theater, all of it — he knew these forms by heart — but the ritual mattered. People trusted a man who treated their names as though the ink might stain.

"You'll find me quick work," Mrs. Sandoval said, sitting opposite. She dried her hands on her apron and folded them, and waited. Not nervously. Waited the way a bank teller waits.

"That's what I'm hoping." He uncapped the ink. "Standard questions, standard order. Name of head of household."

"Ines Marisol Sandoval."

He wrote it. Then he heard it again in his own head — *Ines Marisol Sandoval* — and paused with the pen above the paper.

"Your middle name," he said carefully, "would be Marisol?"

"It would."

"And your sister-in-law — the wife of your husband's brother — her name, according to the parish record I cross-referenced in Alderport—" he had not, in fact, cross-referenced any such record; he was improvising, testing, the way you press a floorboard to hear if it's loose— "is also Marisol."

The kettle ticked. Somewhere upstairs — the tower stair, he thought, must run through the house — a board settled with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

"Small parish," Mrs. Sandoval said. "Names go around. My husband's mother named her boys' wives out of the same book of saints, I expect, to save herself the trouble of learning new ones." She smiled. It was a good smile. It reached her eyes and warmed them and asked for absolutely nothing back. "Is the middle name important to the Bureau?"

"No," August admitted. "It isn't. It's important to me, which is worse." He wrote *Marisol* in the space provided and moved on. "Occupation of head of household."

"Keeper of the Point Alder Light, United States Lighthouse Establishment, appointed—" she gave the date without hesitation, to the day— "upon the death of my husband, Tomás Sandoval, formerly assistant keeper."

Tomás. The name went into the ledger and something behind August's sternum noted it separately, in a different hand, in a book he did not keep for the government.

"My condolences," he said. "I understand he was drowned. Two winters back?"

"Spring ice. The tender went down off Gull Rock." She said it the way people say things they have said so many times the words have worn smooth, like river stones. "You may write 'widow.' The Bureau likes that word. It explains a woman neatly."

"The Bureau," August said, "likes most words that end an inquiry."

For the first time, something moved behind her eyes — not warmth, exactly, but recognition. The look of a person discovering, with mild annoyance, that the clerk across the table is also a person, and possibly a dangerous one.

* * *

They worked through the schedule. Age: thirty-six. Born: across the border, naturalized — papers in order, she confirmed, and produced them from a drawer before he finished asking, which was either admirable efficiency or a rehearsal, and August filed it under both. Able to read and write: yes, three languages, though the form had room for a check mark only. Value of personal estate: she laughed at that one, briefly and without much humor, and he wrote the figure she gave him because there was no column for dignity.

Then the household questions, and the room changed.

It did not change in any way a court would accept as evidence. The kettle went on ticking. The stove breathed. Mrs. Sandoval poured the tea — strong, dark, sweet enough to be medicine — and her hands never trembled and her voice never shifted. But August Reyes had been raised by a liar. A gentle one, a brilliant one, a woman who could look a landlord in the eye and swear the rent was coming Thursday with such conviction that August, age nine, had believed it too, right up until the eviction notice. He had learned, at that table long ago, that lies do not live in the face or the voice. Lies live in timing. In the half-second of stillness before an answer that should have been instant. In the answer that arrives *too* complete, every gap pre-filled, every follow-up anticipated — because only a rehearsed truth is ever that well built.

"How many persons," he read, "were resident at this dwelling on the official date of enumeration?"

"One," said Mrs. Sandoval.

And it had come instantly. That was the tell. Truth takes a breath; it looks inward, consults, counts. *One* arrived like a coin already in hand, polished, presented face-up.

He wrote *1*. He blotted it. He turned the page with a sound like a small sail going up.

"Number of dwellings on the station grounds," he continued, mild as milk. "I see the cottage, the tower, and — there's a building below the rise, isn't there? Stone. I passed it on the path."

"The old powder store, from when they blasted the channel. Door's been chained since before I came here. You can see the chain yourself if the Bureau requires."

"No requirement." He made his note. "Dwelling occupied or unoccupied?"

"Unoccupied."

"Any other persons residing within the boundaries of the station — lodgers, employees, visitors staying as of the enumeration date?"

"No."

There. Half a heartbeat. Barely a seam in the cloth — but August had spent his childhood running his thumb along seams, checking where his mother's stories had been sewn, so they wouldn't split in public. He lifted his teacup and looked at her over the rim, and she looked back, and for a moment the two of them sat perfectly still on either side of a small wooden table, measuring each other with instruments too fine to show on any dial.

She thinks I know, he thought. And I don't know. But she doesn't know that I don't know, and I can't tell her that I don't, because if I'm wrong —

If he was wrong, then a widow was drinking tea with a government man and wishing him gone, and there was an end to it.

If he was right — and the stone building with the chained door, and the woodpile stacked for five, and the smoke rising straight and disciplined from a chimney that fed a one-woman house — if he was right, then somewhere on this frozen point there were people the census was not supposed to see, and the difference between those two worlds was a signature on a page in his case, and his signature was worth eleven dollars a month.

"More tea?" Mrs. Sandoval asked.

"Thank you, no. I ought to finish before the light goes." He turned to the final section. The pen moved. "Condition of the light apparatus, for the Lighthouse Establishment's supplement. Burning apparatus type."

"Fresnel, fourth order. Clockwork rotation, eight-hour train. I wind it at dusk, midnight, and four."

"Wicks?"

"Kept spare. Trimmed weekly." She answered faster now, easier, back on home ground. This was her ledger, and in it she was flawless. "You can inspect the lantern if you've the stomach for a hundred and twelve steps. The Bureau usually sends someone heartier than you in January, though. Old man Corbett used to do it. He fell off your side of the river and broke nothing but his patience."

"I'll leave the tower to Corbett's successor," August said, and closed the ledger, and squared it, and thanked her, and put on his hat, and did all the correct things in the correct order while the other book behind his sternum wrote its quiet entry:

*Point Alder station. One soul declared. More than one shadow.*

At the door, she stopped him with nothing but stillness.

"Mr. Reyes. When will your figures be final?"

"End of the month. Then they're sealed and sent to the capital, and after that not even God amends them, though He's welcome to try."

"A month is a long time to be exact," she said. It sounded like small talk. It was not small talk. "I hope the river treats you gently between here and the village. Ice comes early this year, they're saying. Early ice, early trouble."

"So I've been told." He stepped out into the wind, which took his breath and his hat brim simultaneously, and turned back once on the path. She stood in the doorway with the lamplight behind her, one hand on the frame — a woman guarding a door, in every sense the word would bear.

He walked down toward the village, where he had a bed at the inn, a week's work among farm families who lied to him only about their incomes, and a growing, unwelcome conviction that he had just drunk tea with the best liar he had ever met.

Or the worst-treated truthful woman on the river, said a quieter voice, the one that sounded like his mother.

Behind him the light came on above the point, right at dusk, exactly as promised, and swept the water once, twice, steady as a heartbeat.

August pulled his collar higher and began composing, in his head, the list of old station records he intended to request from the district office. Just routine. Just thoroughness.

Just one bed too many, if he was wrong.

Just four souls too few, if he wasn't.
