## 3. One Bed Too Many

The records room at the Point Alder light station smelled of lamp oil, old paper, and — faintly but unmistakably — of onions. August Reyes noticed the onion smell immediately and decided he did not mind it. He had spent eleven years as a census man in river towns, and he had long ago formed the opinion that a government office without the smell of somebody's cooking was a government office where nobody actually lived.

He had been given the keeper's small front room to work in: a desk with one wobbly leg shimmed by what appeared to be a folded copy of *Harper's Weekly*, a chair that creaked reproachfully whenever he shifted his weight, and a window looking out over the gray river. On the desk Mrs. Sandoval had left him a tin of water, a cup, and — he checked twice to be sure — a plate of shortbread wrapped in a clean cloth.

"Help yourself," she had said, in the tone of a woman who would be mildly offended if he didn't. "The logs are in the cedar box. The station registers go back to '71. Mind the one from '84; the mice got at it before I got at the mice."

She had said it perfectly deadpan, and then gone back out into the wind to scrape rust off the gallery rail, leaving August alone with her paperwork and her shortbread and the growing suspicion that Ines Sandoval was not a woman who needed much looking after, whatever the Bureau of Lighthouses seemed to think.

He was here, officially, for two reasons. The first was the census — the winter enumeration, which required him to verify the roster of every light station on this stretch of the river, since light stations were federal property and their keepers were counted separately from the townsfolk. The second was the audit-prep, though nobody had used that word yet: a quiet tidy-up of station records ahead of the new year, making sure the ledgers agreed with reality, or at least with each other.

He opened the cedar box and began at the beginning, which is where all trouble starts.

* * *

The current register — 1889, the year just ending — was kept in a firm, slanted hand that sloped slightly leftward, as if the writer were leaning into a wind even while sitting down. August read through it with professional pleasure. It was, frankly, the neatest station log he had seen outside of a district office. Entries were dated, timed, initialed. Oil consumed, wicks trimmed, weather noted in a shorthand that managed to convey both precision and personality:

> *Nov. 3 — Fog thick enough to slice. Burned all night. The Hendersons' boy came by to sell eggs; charged me full price and called it charity.*

August found himself smiling at the page. Then he turned to the front of the register, to the printed form every keeper was required to complete: **Persons residing at this station.**

One name. **Ines M. Sandoval, Keeper. Widow. Age 34.**

That was all. One bed occupied, so to speak, in the official arithmetic of Point Alder.

He went back to the previous year's register, 1888, and turned to the same form.

Four names.

**Mateo R. Sandoval, Keeper.** His brother-in-law's husband, presumably — no, August corrected himself, reading again: Mateo Sandoval, the late keeper himself, drowned in April of this year, which August already knew because everyone within fifty miles of water knew it. **Mateo R. Sandoval, Keeper. Ines M. Sandoval, Assistant Keeper. Rafael Sandoval, Brother of Keeper, laborer. Marisol Sandoval, wife of Rafael.**

Four residents. And now, eight months after the drowning, one.

Well. That was not itself suspicious. Drownings emptied households; grief scattered families; a brother and his wife might well have gone back to wherever they'd come from once the keeper they'd come to help was gone. August wrote possibilities in his head the way other men wrote in diaries, and the head-list looked reasonable enough. Rafael and Marisol Sandoval had returned to — where? He flipped back through earlier years. The 1887 register listed only Mateo and Ines. The 1886 register likewise. So Rafael and Marisol had arrived in 1888 and departed in 1888. A visit. A helping hand during an illness August suspected had begun before anyone admitted it. Perfectly ordinary.

Except.

He sat back in the creaking chair and let the exception unfold.

Except that when he had taken Mrs. Sandoval's census statement yesterday evening, standing in her kitchen with the kettle muttering on the stove, he had asked — routinely, the way he asked everyone — whether anyone besides herself resided at the station. And she had said no, sir, just myself and the cat, and God help the mice. And she had smiled when she said it, and he had written down *one resident*, because her answer was confident and her manner was open and because eleven years of this work taught you to trust people who made jokes about their vermin.

But there had been things. Small things. Things he hadn't thought to weigh until now, sitting here among her immaculate logs.

There were four chairs at her kitchen table. Not two, not three — four, and none of them matching, the way chairs match when a household has grown by accretion rather than purchase. There had been a coat on the peg by the door that was far too big for her and cut for a man broader than the late keeper, judging by the photograph on the mantel. There had been boots by the stove, two pairs, and one pair looked recently worn, salt-stained and still damp-dried at the seams.

And there had been the cellar door, which she had crossed the kitchen to stand near — not in front of, exactly, nothing so obvious as that — while answering his questions. She had stood near it the way a hostess stands near the dish she'd rather you didn't ask to try.

August ate a piece of shortbread and considered.

It could be nothing. Widows kept their husbands' brothers' boots; widows kept extra chairs; widows stood where they liked in their own kitchens. He had learned, over eleven years, that the great majority of oddities dissolved under gentle questioning like sugar in warm water. Most people were not hiding anything. Most people were merely untidy, or sentimental, or strange.

But he had also learned — and here the shortbread turned to sawdust in his mouth, because this learning had cost him more than the rest — that a person who lied well was more dangerous to a ledger than a person who lied badly, and Ines Sandoval lied beautifully. She hadn't said *no one else lives here* with the careful over-insistence of a rehearsed liar. She had said it with the bored fluency of a woman stating the weather. Just myself and the cat, and God help the mice.

He wrote nothing down. He closed the registers, aligned them neatly in the cedar box, and looked out the window at the river.

*Say nothing yet,* he told himself, in the voice of his first supervisor, a dry old buzzard named Pell who had taught him everything except how to stop caring. *A ledger is a story, Reyes, and your job is to make sure it's the true one. You don't accuse a book of lying until you've read it twice.*

* * *

The village of Point Alder consisted, as far as August could determine, of one street, two churches that disagreed about something nobody could remember anymore, a general store, a smithy, and a tavern called the Split Rail whose sign depicted neither split rails nor anything else recognizable. It also contained approximately ninety souls, every one of whom knew every other one's business with the loving thoroughness of scholars annotating a sacred text.

He took a room at the Split Rail — the mail boat wouldn't return for six days — and presented himself to the village in the traditional guise of his profession: a harmless, faintly ridiculous functionary with a leather satchel and cold hands, to be fed, questioned about the city, and dismissed. This disguise had served him well for a decade. People forgot he was listening. People talked.

Mrs. Okafor at the general store talked first, and best.

"The keeper?" She weighed his tea, his coffee, a sack of nails, and half his dignity on the same brass scale, all without pausing. "Ines Sandoval runs that light better than her husband did, if you want my honest opinion, and I know you're not supposed to have opinions, Mr. Census Man, so I'll charge you for this one too. She hasn't missed a lighting since the funeral. I'd miss my own mother's funeral before I'd miss a lighting, and I think she feels the same way."

"She keeps good records," August offered.

"She keeps good *everything*. Eggs, oil, accounts." Mrs. Okafor squinted at him. "You're checking up on her?"

"Routine verification. Station rosters, prior years' entries. Standard."

"Mm." The scale bobbed. "Her brother-in-law was here last year. Rafael. Big fellow, quiet, hands like shovels. Did the heavy work Mateo couldn't do anymore, toward the end. His wife too — Marisol. Sweet girl, hardly spoke above a whisper, sewed like the devil. Made Mrs. Pell a quilt in two weeks that should've taken a month."

"And they've gone back home, I understand."

"Have they?" said Mrs. Okafor, in a tone that contained, delicately balanced, no information whatsoever.

"That's what the record implies."

"The record," said Mrs. Okafor, "isn't from around here." And she rang up his nails and refused to explain herself, and hummed while she did it.

August walked out into the gray afternoon with his satchel heavier than it had any right to be.

At the smithy he fared better, or worse, depending on how you counted. Tom Brandt was a young man with a burn-scarred forearm and the conversational subtlety of his own anvil.

"Sandovals? Good family. Mateo taught me half my knot-work. The brother, Rafael — strongest back on the river. You want to move a forge, you want Rafael." Tom beat something flat with something heavy, thoughtfully. "Haven't seen him since spring, though."

"Not since the funeral?"

Tom paused, hammer raised. The pause lasted exactly one beat too long.

"Nope," he said. "Not since the funeral."

"Anyone seen them? Since spring?"

"Why?" Tom set the hammer down and looked at August with the frank, unlovely directness of a man who has decided to stop pretending. "You counting them? Because if you're counting them, I'd want to know why it matters whether you count four or one. They never took a cent off this town. They gave more than they took. So either they're here or they're gone, and either way, seems to me, the number that matters most is zero — as in how much harm they ever did anybody."

"I'm not accusing anyone of anything," August said mildly. "I count. That's all I do. I put down what's true and let the paper do the rest."

"Yeah, well." Tom picked the hammer back up. "Paper's done a lot of resting on its laurels around here. Paper said the Hendersons' fence line was theirs for forty years and it wasn't. Paper said my father owed the bank money he didn't owe. Paper's fine, Mr. Reyes, right up until someone reads it wrong on purpose."

August thanked him for his time and got, for reply, the sound of iron being punished.

He walked the length of the street and back again, hat collar up against the wind coming off the water, and made a survey of himself. This was a habit he'd developed young, growing up in a house where feelings were stored like gunpowder — dry, dark, and stacked high: *What do you actually know, Augusto, and what do you merely suspect?*

He knew the register said four, and the census said one, and widows sometimes simplified their paperwork out of grief or laziness or a dozen innocent reasons.

He knew there were four chairs and damp boots and a cellar door a capable woman orbited like a moon.

He knew Mrs. Okafor knew something and was choosing, with great care, to know it privately.

And he knew that Tom Brandt — honest, blunt Tom Brandt, who wouldn't have known a lie if it shook his hand — had hesitated for exactly one beat too long, which meant that more people than one were keeping a secret together, and that the secret had weight, because secrets with no weight don't need sharing around.

*People hide from debts,* his father used to say. *People hide from shame. But nobody organizes a whole village to hide something unless somebody's life depends on it.*

His father had known whereof he spoke, though that was another ledger entirely, one August kept locked in a drawer in his chest that he opened perhaps once a year, at night, briefly, and then shut hard.

* * *

That evening the tavern filled with rivermen escaping the wind, and August bought rounds strategically and listened the way he was paid to. The talk was ice and ferry schedules and the price of coal, and then — because a stranger asking mild questions is a lamp drawn across a dark room — the talk turned, briefly, to the lighthouse.

"Fine woman," said a bargeman whose name August hadn't caught. "Ran that light single-handed through the equinox gale. Should've had an assistant's wage all autumn and the Bureau knows it."

"They'll get to her," someone said comfortably. "Bureau gets to everybody eventually. Usually about ten years too late."

"Any family out there now?" August asked, casually, into his cup.

The table considered this. An older man with a face like a dried cod finally answered, slowly, watching August as he answered, which was interesting in itself.

"Family," he said. "Now there's a question. Used to be family. Whether there still is—" he shrugged elaborately, "—depends on who's doing the counting, I expect."

Laughter, but thin laughter, the kind that covers a held breath. And August laughed with them, and bought the next round, and understood with sudden, chilly clarity that he had just announced himself — politely, inadvertently, in the way of his whole harmless ridiculous profession — as the man in the room asking about hidden people. And that every soul at that table had heard the question, and every soul had decided, in the space of a heartbeat, what he was going to be told.

Which was nothing. Which was, in its own way, confirmation of everything.

He climbed the stairs to his narrow room afterward and stood at the window a long while, watching the point itself, black against the blacker water. Every twelve seconds, precisely, the light swept past — a wheel of white rolling over the waves, steady as a heartbeat.

Somebody was keeping that light. One woman, according to the paper. Four chairs, according to the kitchen. Something in between, according to a village that had closed ranks around a widow faster and more completely than any village had ever closed ranks around August's questions in eleven years of asking them.

He got out his notebook and, beneath the neat entry *Sandoval, Ines M. — resident, sole*, he did not write a word. He simply left a small pencil dot in the margin, his private mark, the one Pell had taught him: *return to this page.*

Then he blew out the lamp and lay down, and the light swept past the window every twelve seconds, and he thought — absurdly, uncomfortably, in the space between waking and sleep — of four chairs around a kitchen table, and of a coat too big for anyone he'd met, and of the smell of onions in a records room where officially only one person lived and cooked and grieved and kept a perfect log.

Tomorrow, he decided, he would walk out to the point again. Officially, to verify the fog signal apparatus against the inventory.

Unofficially, to see whether the keeper's kitchen had acquired, overnight, one fewer chair.

The light swept past. Twelve seconds. Swept past again.

Somewhere out there in the dark, the river was already thinking about ice.
