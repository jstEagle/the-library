## 4. Salt and Secrets

The supply boat came on the second Tuesday of December, which was late, and late meant trouble riding in the hold along with the flour.

Ines saw it first as a smudge against the gray water, and she did what she always did: she pulled the little brass bell cord that ran down through the tower's heart into the cellar room, three short tugs and one long. *Company. Stay down.* Then she tied her scarf over her hair, straightened her apron, and became, in the space of a breath, a woman with nothing in her house but herself and her grief and an unreasonable quantity of lamp oil.

The bell had been Emiliano's idea, back when the hiding had begun. "You ring it like you're calling me to dinner," he'd said, laughing his quiet laugh, the one that never reached all the way up. "That way if anyone ever asks about the sound, it's true. You *are* calling someone to dinner."

He'd been dead four months by then, and still solving problems for her from beyond the grave. It was becoming a habit of his.

* * *

The boat was piloted by Henrik Dahl, who was seventy if he was a day and who steered like a man arguing with the river rather than traveling upon it. He brought the skiff alongside the landing with the grace of a dropped sack of potatoes and began hauling crates up before Ines had even reached the bottom stair.

"You're late," she called.

"The river's late," Henrik said, as though the river were a hired hand who'd been out drinking. "Ice is thinking about forming up past the narrows. Another week, maybe two, and nothing comes north till spring. You want to make your list now, Mrs. Sandoval, because after this there's just me and God and whatever's in your pantry."

"Then I'll take double the coffee."

"Double the coffee." He nodded with deep approval, as if this confirmed everything he believed about the moral character of lighthouse keepers. "A woman who understands priorities."

Ines signed for the delivery — flour, salt pork, lamp oil, a bolt of flannel, and, wrapped separately at the bottom, the parcel of books Marisol had asked for, smuggled past the village postmaster under Henrik's coat. She counted the crates. Six. All accounted for. Her hands did not shake, because she had trained them not to, the way one trains any useful animal.

It was going perfectly, which should have been her first warning.

She was halfway through directing Henrik's boy — no, not Henrik's boy. Henrik had come alone. The second figure hauling crates up the landing was small, quick, and wearing a cap two sizes too large, and Ines knew that walk anywhere in the world, that particular bounce on the left foot, because she had watched it come down her own cellar stairs every night for five weeks.

Tomás.

He had heard the boat. Of course he had heard the boat; the child could hear a mouse changing its mind three rooms away. And he had come up through the coal chute, the little idiot, the brave, wonderful, catastrophic little idiot, because he was eight years old and eight-year-old boys are constitutionally incapable of letting a boat arrive without seeing it.

"*Henrik*," Ines said, loud enough to startle gulls off the breakwater, and she crossed the landing in six strides and put herself between the boy and the old man's view. Tomás froze mid-step, one crate in his arms, his eyes enormous above the too-large cap. "Your other boy didn't come?"

Henrik squinted at her over the rail. "My what?"

"Your boy. Last time you brought that great strapping nephew of yours to haul the heavy crates." She said it smoothly, warmly, and inside her own skull a voice was screaming. There was no strapping nephew. There had never been a strapping nephew. But Henrik was old, and old men's memories were soft things, easily pressed into new shapes, and she watched him actually consider it.

"Nephew," he said slowly. "Big fellow. Bad knee."

"That's the one."

"Hm. Didn't come this trip." Henrik shrugged and went back to his rope. "Just me and my back, complaining the whole way."

"Then let's not make your back do the work twice." Ines took the crate from Tomás's arms — gently, so gently, while pretending to snatch it — and set it down. "Go on back to the skiff, lad, and mind you don't slip on those boards. They're rotten on the north side."

Tomás looked up at her, and for one terrible, endless moment she thought he might say something. Eight-year-olds were built entirely of the impulse to say things. But then he nodded, solemn as a deacon, turned, and walked — bounced, that left-foot bounce, God help her — back toward the skiff, where he sat down on the gunwale and began, with apparent fascination, to study the river.

Henrik glanced over. "Whose boy is that?"

"The ferryman's sister's boy. Helping out for the crossing money." The lie came out level and easy, and Ines hated how good she had gotten at it. There had been a time, not so long ago, when lying made her ears hot. Now it was like pouring water. "They're short-handed since the ice came early upriver."

"Mm." Henrik hauled another crate. "River's full of boys looking for coin this year. Half the valley's on the move, if you ask me. Warrants and hard winters." He said it without malice, without even much interest, the way a man comments on weather, and Ines felt the words go through her like a fishhook finding flesh. "Marshal's office posted new papers in the square last week. Bounty notices. A man could nearly make a living off other people's troubles, if he had the stomach for it."

"Could he?" Ines lifted the last crate. "Sounds like lonely work."

"Most work that pays well is." Henrik wiped his hands on his coat and surveyed the stack. "That's the lot. I'll be back before the ice closes if the river lets me, but don't count on it. Make your list."

"I'll make it long," Ines said, and walked him back down to the skiff, and stood on the landing waving until the boat was a smudge again, with one small figure in the stern who did not look back, because he had been told never to look back, and he was, for once in his short life, obeying.

Then she went inside, sat down on the bottom stair, and put her face in her hands.

* * *

The coal chute would have to be nailed shut. No — boarded, but boardable from outside, so it looked like winterizing and not imprisonment. She'd tell Tomás it was for his own safety, which was even true, which was somehow worse. Every rule she made for that child was true and necessary and a small brick in the wall of his childhood, and she laid them anyway, one by one, because the alternative was watching him hang from a marshal's wagon.

She found him in the kitchen, already scrubbed and penitent, sitting at the table with his hands folded like a small man awaiting sentencing. Marisol stood at the stove with her back very straight. Rafael was mending a net that would never see water again, his fingers moving over the mesh the way they must once have moved over fields.

"He heard the bell," Marisol said, before Ines could speak. "I told him to stay. I told him."

"I know you did."

"He *never* listens—"

"Mama." Tomás's voice cracked on it. "I only wanted to see the boat. I haven't seen anything but this house since—" His jaw set, and Ines saw Emiliano in it, suddenly and painfully, the same stubborn line, the same refusal to be small. "Since forever. I wanted to see the *river*."

And there it was. Not disobedience. Hunger. Five weeks in a cellar room with a grate for a window, and the boy hadn't been naughty; he'd been starving for the world.

Ines pulled out a chair and sat across from him.

"Tomás. Do you know why the rules matter?" He shrugged, aggrieved. "Look at me." He looked. "There are men who would take you away from your mother. Not bad men, necessarily. Men doing their work, men who've never met you, who'd put you in a wagon and carry you south and never think of you again by supper. That's what's out there. Not monsters. Just work." She held his eyes. "The rules aren't because you're bad. They're because the world doesn't know yet that you're good."

His chin wobbled. He mastered it, the way his whole family mastered things, and nodded once.

"Now," Ines said, "because you scared ten years off my life, you owe me labor. Tomorrow you're going to learn the lamp. Real work, keeper's work, up the tower stairs. If you're going to live in a lighthouse, you'll earn your keep."

The transformation was total. The sullenness fell off him like a dropped coat. "The *lamp*? The real one? Can I light it?"

"You can polish it. Lighting comes later, if you prove you can clean glass without leaving thumbprints."

Marisol made a sound that was half laugh, half sob, and turned back to her stove, and Rafael's fingers kept moving on the net, but slower now, and Ines understood that she had just bought them all another week of peace with the promise of a tower staircase. Children could bear almost anything, it seemed, except boredom. It was the adults who bore the other thing — the waiting, the listening-for-boots — and bore it badly.

* * *

That night, after the lamp was lit and turning, Ines climbed down from the lantern room and did not go to bed. She went instead to the window at the turn of the second-floor stair, the one that faced the river, and she sat on the cold sill with her knees drawn up, the way she had sat the night Emiliano drowned, though that window faced east and this one faced north and grief does not care about compass points.

They had found his boat snagged in the willows below the point, upright and empty, oars shipped neatly. That detail had undone her more than the rest — that he had shipped his oars. Emiliano, careful as tide tables, tidy as a hymn, had rowed out into the October squall to tend a fouled mooring buoy, and somewhere between the point and the willows the river had taken him, and he had had time, perhaps, to ship his oars. To be neat about it. To die the way he lived, apologizing for the inconvenience.

Rafael had come within the month. Had crossed the border in the dark with his wife and son and a warrant behind him and a debt in front of him, and had stood in Ines's kitchen dripping river water that wasn't even the right river, and had said, "You don't have to. Say it and we'll go. Say it and we're strangers."

Emiliano had written to her from his brother's last safe address, months before the drowning. *If it goes wrong for us, Rafa will run north. He won't ask you. He's too proud. So I'm asking you. Keep the light, Ines. Keep it for all of us.*

She had laughed at the letter when it came. *Keep the light,* as if it were a figure of speech, as if he weren't asking her to hide three human beings inside a federal station, inside a job that inspected and counted and recorded, inside a life already narrow as the tower stairs. She had written back something fond and evasive. She had meant to think about it properly. There had been time, she'd thought. There was always time, until the river decided otherwise.

And then Rafael had stood in her kitchen with wet boots and his son asleep against his shoulder, and she had heard herself say, "The cellar room's dry. Mind the third step, it complains," and that had been that. No decision at all, really. Just a letter finally answered, four months late, to a man who couldn't read it anymore.

Guilt, she thought, watching the ice floes knock together in the dark like slow applause, was the one inheritance that passed sideways. Emiliano had left her no money, no children, no property but a lighthouse and its loneliness. What he'd left her was his brother's survival, weighed and measured and owing. And she paid on it daily, in small coins: a nailed chute, a rehearsed lie, a boy's laughter kept underground.

Below her, faint through the floorboards, she could hear Marisol singing to Tomás — a lullaby in the old language, half-voice, careful, the way you sing when singing itself is contraband. Ines closed her eyes.

The river boomed softly against the point. Ice season. Closing season. Whatever was going to happen would happen soon, and she was no closer to an answer than the day Rafael dripped on her kitchen floor.

She was still sitting there when she heard it: a sound from the lane below the station, unhurried, deliberate. Boots on frozen gravel. One set. Stopping.

Through the dark glass she could just make out a figure standing at the property line, hatless despite the cold, looking up at the light. Not approaching. Just standing. Counting windows, maybe. Counting shadows.

Watching, the way census men watch.

Ines did not move. She sat very still in the dark of the stairwell, her heart going like the lamp mechanism, steady and mechanical and counting, counting, counting — and the figure below turned up its collar at last and walked on toward the village, and she told herself it was nothing, it was Henrik, it was a drunk, it was nobody.

But she stayed on the cold sill a long while after, listening, until the river boomed again — louder now, she thought, or maybe only closer — and the ice spoke in its grinding voice of everything that was coming.
