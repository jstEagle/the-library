## 5. The Question Too Many

The mail boat had come and gone on Tuesday, which meant August Reyes was now a fixture of Point Alder whether Point Alder wanted him or not. He had taken a room at the widow Brenner's boarding house — four dollars a week, a bed that sagged in the middle like a hammock with ambitions, and a breakfast of fried pork that arrived at seven sharp and stayed with a man until well past noon. The village had decided, after two days of observation, that he was harmless. He encouraged this. Harmless men were told things.

But there was one place in Point Alder he had not yet been invited to linger, and that was the keeper's cottage at the light station, and it was there, on Thursday afternoon, that he found himself sitting at Ines Sandoval's own table with a cup of tea in his cold hands and his official ledger closed and face-down beside his elbow like a card player who has folded and doesn't want anyone to see how badly.

The tea was a kindness. He hadn't asked for it. She had simply set it in front of him when he came in out of the wind, stamping snow off his boots, saying something official about verifying the station's resident count against the Light-House Board records, and she had said, "Sit. You look like a man the river's already half-drowned," and put the cup down, and that had been the end of the official part for a while.

He should have drunk and gone. Instead he drank and stayed.

The cottage was small and ferociously clean. A stove ticking with heat. A shelf of books he suspected had been read rather than displayed. Mended things everywhere — mended curtains, mended chair seats, a mended window frame still faintly smelling of fresh putty — the particular furniture of a household that had decided, quietly and without complaint, to outlast everything. On the wall hung a photograph of a man with a beard and a confident squint, whom August understood to be the late keeper, Henrik Sandoval, drowned the spring before not fifty yards from the door he'd lived behind. The river did not care how well you knew it. That was the first thing Point Alder had taught him, and it repeated the lesson as often as necessary.

"More tea?" Ines asked. She stood at the stove with her back to him, doing something to a pot that involved no urgency whatsoever. She was a woman who could make standing still look like industry.

"Thank you, no." He warmed his hands on the cup anyway. "I appreciate this. Most folks on my route give me five minutes on the doorstep and a form to sign."

"I know what it is to be cold," she said, without turning. "The light doesn't keep itself warm."

* * *

They talked about nothing for a while, and August let them, because nothing was often the fastest road to something. She told him about the ice — how it boomed at night now, how the old-timers could read the river's temper by the sound of it, how the ferry would run until the channel pinched shut and then everyone north of Point Alder would be an island until spring. He told her about census work in a way designed to be dull: the routes, the forms, the arithmetic of households. He mentioned, idly, that most people lied to him. Not maliciously, mostly. They lied about ages, about who slept under which roof, about the boarder nobody officially had. Everybody rounded their lives down to whatever shape suited them.

"And you just write down what they tell you?" she asked.

"I write down what I can prove." He smiled at his tea. "Mostly they're the same thing."

"Mostly," said Ines Sandoval, and turned back to her pot.

It was, he would think later, the exact moment he should have packed up his ledger and left. There are conversations that are doors and conversations that are traps, and you can generally tell which is which only by stepping through. But he had spent three days reading old station logs by lamplight in the widow Brenner's parlor, and the arithmetic of this house sat in his chest like a swallowed stone: last winter's log, in Henrik Sandoval's blunt sailor's hand, listing four souls at the station — Henrik; his wife; his brother Andrés; Andrés's wife. And this winter, one name declared on the rolls, one plate at the table as far as any villager could say, and yet the woodpile going down faster than one woman could account for, and — he was nearly sure — a second set of small boot prints in yesterday's snow along the seaward path, half-swept but not swept in time.

So instead of leaving, he said, casually, into the warm and ticking quiet of the kitchen:

"Do you winter out here all alone, Mrs. Sandoval?"

The question was nothing. It was the kind of question a census man asks a hundred times a season, the kind you ask to fill silence, the kind that deserves a one-word answer and a nod.

Ines took the pot off the stove. She set it down. She wiped her hands on her apron, unhurried, and when she turned around her face was open and mild and slightly amused, the face of a woman being asked whether she minded the weather.

"Alone as the light is alone," she said. "Which is to say, yes, but with company." She nodded at the photograph on the wall. "Henrik's still about, if you ask me. He never could leave a thing half-done, and the light was his great unfinished business. Some nights I hear him on the stairs and I don't even get up anymore. It's only him checking his work."

She laughed — softly, at herself, at ghosts, at the whole ridiculous situation of a woman living at the edge of a frozen river talking to her dead husband — and the laugh did what good lies always do: it made him want to stop asking.

That was the tell.

Not her voice. Her voice was steady as the lamp mechanism. Not her eyes; her eyes met his without flinching or holding too long. It was the laugh. It was the speed of the answer. Nobody answers *are you alone out here* with a polished little speech about ghosts unless they have answered it before — rehearsed it, tried it out against the dark, worn the edges smooth. A truthful person stumbles. A truthful person says *yes, of course,* and looks faintly puzzled, and resents the question a little. A truthful person does not hand you warmth and a story shaped exactly to your sympathies before the kettle has finished settling.

August knew, because he had been raised by one.

His mother, God rest her and God forgive him for thinking ill of her, had been the finest liar in three counties, though she'd have called it survival and been right. She could look a landlady in the eye and swear there were only two at the table when there were five, and she'd do it while passing the bread, and she'd add a detail — *my sister's boy, he eats like a bird, you won't notice him* — because the detail was what made it true. She taught him, without meaning to, without ever once saying so, that a lie is known by its comfort. Lies are comfortable. That's why people tell them. The truth is usually awkward, usually too much or too little, usually arrives with its collar buttoned wrong.

And Mrs. Sandoval's answer fit him like a coat made to measure.

He looked down at his tea and thought: *there it is.* And felt, to his considerable annoyance, not triumph but something adjacent to grief.

"Your husband's brother," he said, keeping his voice in the same idle register, the register of a man making conversation about weather and roads. "Andrés, wasn't it? He was here last winter, according to the station log. Did the family move on?"

The pause, when it came, was perfect. Half a heartbeat — long enough to remember, short enough to seem natural. His mother would have applauded.

"He went north," she said. "Him and Marisol both. Henrik's death changed things. The station's a hard place for a big family in winter, and there was work for them upriver. They wrote when the mails ran." A small shrug, rueful. "Mails run thin out here. You'd know better than I."

"I would," August agreed. "Thin as the ice, some weeks."

"Mm," said Ines Sandoval, and refilled his cup without asking, which was either hospitality or a way to have her hands busy, and he honestly could not tell which, and that frightened him more than anything else she'd done.

* * *

He stayed another hour. He couldn't have said precisely why, except that leaving immediately after the question would have been an admission, and he wasn't ready to show his hand, not even to himself.

They talked about the storm season. She explained the lamp to him — the clockwork that turned it, the weights that had to be cranked every few hours through the night, the way the tower hummed in a hard blow like a struck bell. She spoke about it the way other people spoke about a spouse: with exasperation and devotion in the same breath. "It wants attention every four hours, all night, in the worst weather especially, and it never once says thank you." She said it dryly enough that he laughed, and the laugh surprised him, because he had come out to this station prepared to dislike its keeper — prepared, if he was honest with himself, to find a cold woman running a cold house with something hidden in it.

What he found instead was a woman who had set out tea for a government man she had every reason to distrust, and kept his cup full, and told ghost stories about her dead husband, and watched him the entire time from behind those mild dark eyes with more patience than any liar he'd ever met. His mother included.

When he finally rose to go, dusk already pooling blue in the windows, she walked him to the door and stood in it with the cold pouring past her.

"You'll be in the village a while yet?" she asked. Pleasantly. Making conversation.

"A few days. Records to reconcile. Then the ferry north, if it's still running."

"The ice decides that, not the ferryman." She paused, and then added, in exactly the same pleasant tone: "Point Alder looks after its own, Mr. Reyes. Whatever the papers say about who belongs here. I hope your work goes gently."

It was a warning. Or a blessing. With this woman, he suspected, the two came from the same shelf and were dispensed in identical doses.

He walked the seaward path back toward the village in the falling dark, snow squeaking under his boots, the great light waking above him — he heard the clockwork catch, heard the lens begin its slow rotation, throwing its long arm out over the black water once every eight seconds like a lighthouse-sized blink. Eight seconds. He counted them the whole way down the hill. It gave a man something to do besides think.

He thought anyway.

* * *

At the boarding house, the widow Brenner had left the lamp lit in the parlor and a plate of cold pork covered with a cloth, and August sat down across from his ledger and opened it and looked, for a long time, at the page for Point Alder Light Station.

One line. One name. *Ines Sandoval, keeper, widowed.*

And in his notebook, in the cramped private shorthand he used for things that were not yet facts: *woodpile low. boots, small, seaward path. tea for two cups? no — one cup, washed, still wet on the rack.* He had noticed that. He noticed everything; it was his curse and his trade. One cup, washed, upside down on the drying rack, still beaded with water at two in the afternoon, in a house where the declared population was one woman who had already had her midday tea with him.

He closed the book.

Here was the trouble, and he turned it over now the way you turn a stone to see what's underneath: he could report a discrepancy. That was the procedure. Discrepancy noted, marshal informed, inquiry conducted, and whatever was found in that cellar or spare room or loft would be found by men with sleds and warrants and no interest in tea. Or he could write down what he could not disprove — a widow, alone, keeping her husband's light — and let the arithmetic stay unbalanced, and carry whatever that cost him.

Or — third road, the muddy one, the one his boots kept drifting toward despite himself — he could find out first. Before any paper moved. Find out who was in that house and why, and what had become of Andrés Sandoval and his wife, and whether the word *warrant* applied, and to whom, and for what.

Because here was the thing he had not written in any notebook: his mother had lied to landladies and clerks and once, memorably, to a sheriff, and every single lie she had ever told had been in service of the same plain fact — that there were people at her table who had nowhere else to be. He had grown up eating bread passed by those hands. He had been one of the shadows being counted wrong.

He banked the parlor stove, took up the lamp, and stood a moment at the window looking out toward the point, where the light swung its arm across the dark — once, twice, patient as breathing — over a river that boomed and groaned in the night like something large turning over in its sleep.

Tomorrow, he decided, he would call on Father Anselmo. Priests, in his experience, knew every soul in a parish twice over: once as the law counted them, and once as God did. And somewhere between the two countings was the truth about the keeper of Point Alder light — and, if he was very careful, perhaps a way through this that did not end with sleds and warrants and a woman alone at the edge of the ice.

The light blinked. He counted eight seconds, and went up to bed.
