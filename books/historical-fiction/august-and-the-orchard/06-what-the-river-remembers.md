## 6. What the River Remembers

The morning Ines decided to walk to the village, the tide had left the flats gleaming like a coin rubbed clean, and she told herself she was going for lamp oil.

It was not entirely a lie. The oil drum was down to its last finger's width, and winter did not forgive a keeper who ran dry — the light would gutter in March like a candle at a wake, and Point Alder would go dark over the very water where ships went to die. So yes. Lamp oil. That was the reason, written plainly on her shopping list in her own sensible hand: *oil, salt, flour, thread.*

She wrote *thread* because it seemed harmless. A woman buying thread was nobody's business but her own. A woman asking questions about warrants was another creature entirely, and so she left that item off the list, though she carried it in her chest the whole four miles along the cliff path, where the wind came off the river with the smell of iron and coming cold.

Behind her, the lighthouse stood white against the gray sky, patient as a held breath. Inside it, under her floorboards and down three steps into the old coal cellar, slept a family of three who did not officially exist. She had gotten good at not-existing them. She'd gotten good at a great many things since Rafael drowned — good at hauling oil up ninety-one stairs, good at trimming wicks by feel, good at answering questions without ever quite lying. It was this last skill that worried her. Competence at lying, she suspected, was like competence at anything: the more you practiced, the more you needed to.

* * *

Father Anselmo was in the churchyard when she arrived, wrestling a dead rosebush out of frozen ground with the grim determination of a man who had buried half his congregation and intended to outlast the rest.

"Señora Sandoval." He straightened, trowel in hand, soil on his cassock. "You've come to confess."

"I've come for lamp oil."

"The church doesn't sell lamp oil. The church sells forgiveness, absolution, and — on Thursdays — soup." He squinted at her. "But you're welcome to try the confessional anyway. I find people often come for one thing and need the other."

"I need nothing," said Ines, which was the kind of sentence that, spoken aloud in a churchyard, immediately proved itself false.

Anselmo set down the trowel. He was sixty if he was fifty, a small round man with hands like knotted rope and eyes that had watched smugglers' boats slide past this coast for thirty years and remembered every one of them. It was Anselmo who had rowed Diego Sandoval across from the fishing camp two winters ago, wrapped in a tarp among the herring barrels, while the boy's wife wept on the far shore. It was Anselmo who had told her afterward, mildly, that God kept no census. She had liked him enormously for that.

Now he looked at her a long moment, and whatever he saw in her face made him say, quietly, "Come inside. The wind has teeth today."

* * *

The church smelled of wax and cold stone. Anselmo lit the stove in the little side room where he took his tea, poured two cups without asking, and sat across from her with the patience of a man who had learned that silence pulls truth faster than questions do.

Ines held out for nearly a minute.

"Diego," she said. "His family. Is there any word? Any change?"

The priest's cup paused halfway to his mouth. Then it completed its journey, unhurried, and he drank, and set it down, and looked into the middle distance the way men do when they are deciding how much of the truth a person can carry.

"You've heard something," Ines said.

"I've heard everything," said Anselmo. "That is my profession. I hear everything and repeat almost none of it." He turned the cup slowly on its saucer. "There is a new marshal. Came up from the city in October. Young. Ambitious. The old marshal was content to lose paperwork; this one files it twice."

"Files what?"

"Warrants." He said it flatly, the way you'd say *weather*. "Diego's warrant was renewed in November. Marisol's name has been added. And now—" he hesitated, and Ines felt her stomach drop before he even finished, "—there is a bounty. Twenty dollars for information leading to the apprehension of undocumented persons. Fifty if it leads to a whole household."

Twenty dollars. Ines turned the number over in her mind the way you'd turn a stone found on the beach, wondering what lived underneath it. Twenty dollars was two months of a fisherman's wage. Twenty dollars was a new coat, a repaired roof, a winter's worth of flour and pork. Twenty dollars was what hunger looked like when it put on respectable clothes.

"Fifty," she repeated.

"For a household." Anselmo's voice gentled. "I'm telling you because you deserve to hear it from a friend rather than discover it from a stranger. There are people in this village who know or suspect what's under your roof, Ines. Most of them love you. But love is not bread, and this has been a hard year. The sardines moved north early. Boats sit idle. A man with an empty smokehouse and a full winter ahead of him looks at twenty dollars differently than he did in June."

"Who?" she asked. "Who would—"

"No." He raised a hand. "I will not hand you names, because I don't have names, only weather signs. And because if I gave you a name, you would spend your nights watching that door instead of sleeping, and a keeper who doesn't sleep lets her light fail, and then we all drown." He leaned forward. "What I will tell you is this: the window is closing. The ferry makes its last run north before the ice breaks — you know this. After the ice goes out, the river belongs to the patrol boats until spring thaw settles. If that family is going to cross, it must be soon, and it must be quiet, and it cannot be half-measures."

"They know," Ines said. "We've talked about the ferry."

"And?"

"And the ferryman wants payment in full, and berths are scarce, and I am a widow living on a government salary that assumes my household contains one stubborn woman and no one else." She heard the bitterness come out of her mouth and didn't call it back.

Anselmo was quiet a moment. Then he opened the drawer of his little desk and counted out coins onto the wood — not many, but not nothing, small silver pieces pushed across with the flat of his hand as though he were embarrassed by them.

"The poor box," he said. "Which, I remind you, exists precisely for the persecuted, and I'll thank you not to argue theology with me in my own church."

"Father—"

"Ines." He fixed her with a look that had probably quelled better rebels than her. "I helped row Diego across that river once already. I'm too old to row again. Let me do the next best thing." His voice dropped. "But hear me, and hear me clearly. The money buys berths. It does not buy safety. A bounty changes people — not the wicked ones, who were always for sale, but the tired ones. The decent ones. Decent people do terrible arithmetic in February." He tapped the table once, softly. "Count your days. Don't count your friends."

* * *

She bought her lamp oil from Okafor's store, and her salt, and her flour, and her thread — a spool of brown thread she did not need, purchased purely for its power to make her presence in the village unremarkable. Okafor himself rang her up, cheerful as ever, and asked after the light, and asked whether she was keeping warm out there all alone, and Ines smiled and said the tower kept her warmer than most husbands had, which got the laugh she wanted and moved the conversation safely along.

It was on the way out, passing the stove where the village women clustered in winter like moths, that she caught the fragment.

"—marshal's offering real money, Rosa was saying, real silver, just for word of where they're hiding—"

"—well, somebody's got to be feeding them, someone's cellar, someone's attic—"

"Not here. Nobody here would—"

"Well, *somebody*, Marta, or why would he still be looking?"

The women's voices dropped as Ines passed; nods, smiles, the ordinary courtesies of neighbors. One of the speakers was Rosa Pell, wrapped in a good wool shawl that Ines had never seen before — a fine shawl, city-fine, the kind of thing a woman bought when money had recently come to hand. Or was about to. Ines made herself nod back, made her smile reach her eyes through sheer craftsmanship, and walked out into the cold with her parcel under her arm and her heart going like a gull in a gale.

*Rosa.* No. Surely not. Rosa had held Tomás on her knee at the Midsummer dance two years ago. Rosa had wept at Rafael's funeral harder than Ines herself had managed to.

And yet Anselmo's voice followed her up the cliff path like the tide: *Decent people do terrible arithmetic in February. Count your days. Don't count your friends.*

* * *

The house was warm when she returned, smelling of onions and the small brown loaf Marisol baked every day because kneading dough kept her hands busy and her mind from drowning. Tomás was at the table doing sums on a slate — the boy was seven and ferociously proud of carrying numbers — and Diego was mending a net he had no lawful right to mend, his big hands moving with the unconscious grace of a man whose whole life had been rope and water.

Marisol read Ines's face the moment the door shut behind her. She always could. She set down her knife, wiped her hands, and waited until Ines had hung her coat and stood a moment in the warmth, letting the cold leave her bones.

"Well?" Marisol said quietly.

"There's a bounty now," Ines said. There was no gentle way, and Marisol had never once thanked her for gentleness; she was a woman who wanted the wave entire, not a description of the foam. "Twenty dollars for a person. Fifty for a household. The new marshal means business. And Father Anselmo says the window closes when the ice breaks — after that, patrol boats until spring."

Silence. The fire ticked. On his slate, Tomás carefully wrote *7 + 8 = 15* and frowned at it, sensing without understanding that the room's temperature had changed around him.

"Fifty dollars," said Diego at last. He said it the way other men might say *hurricane.* "For us. We're worth fifty dollars to somebody."

"To anybody," Marisol corrected softly. "That's what a bounty means, Diego. It isn't that someone hates us. It's that anyone can." She looked at Ines, and her dark eyes were steady and terribly clear. "How long?"

"Until the ice breaks. Weeks. Maybe less, if the cold comes hard. Anselmo thinks less."

Marisol nodded slowly, once, twice, a woman taking the measure of a thing. Then she turned back to her onions, and her knife came down, and came down again, even and unhurried, and Ines understood that the decision was already forming somewhere behind that calm — forming the way ice forms on the river, silently, invisibly, until one morning the whole surface has changed and nothing can cross it the old way again.

From the table, Tomás looked up from his slate. "Mamá, is fifteen right?"

Marisol crossed the room and bent over the slate, and kissed the top of his head, and held the kiss a moment longer than sums required.

"It's right, mi amor," she said. "Everything adds up, in the end. You just have to decide what you're willing to carry to make it balance."

Ines stood in her own kitchen, in her own house, and felt the words land on her like snowfall — soft, and cold, and promising to get deeper.

Outside, far off across the gray water, she could hear it if she listened: the river muttering to itself in the dark, remembering everything, keeping its own count.
