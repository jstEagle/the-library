## 7. Ice on the Windows

Tomás had decided that winter was a color before it was a cold.

He had thought this was a very clever thing to know, and he had told his mother so, and she had said *sí, mi amor* in the voice that meant she was listening to the soup and not to him, and so he had carried the idea down to Uncle Emilio instead, who was whittling a spoon that would never be finished because he kept starting over whenever he found a flaw.

"A winter is gray," Tomás said. "But not all one gray. A hundred grays."

Uncle Emilio turned the spoon in the lamplight. "A hundred grays," he agreed gravely. "You should write that down."

"I can't write yet."

"Then remember it. Remembering is writing for later."

That was the best thing about Uncle Emilio, Tomás thought. He never said a thing was silly. Papá used to say things were silly — not meanly, but quickly, like swatting a fly — but Papá had been gone since before the boat, since before the cellar room with its smell of onions and lamp oil, and now Papá existed mostly as a hole shaped like a man, and everyone walked carefully around it.

The cellar room had become the world. Tomás knew it the way other boys knew their streets: by heart and by feel. He could walk from the cot to the stove with his eyes shut — eleven steps, turn at the shelf of preserves, mind the third floorboard which creaked even if you breathed on it. He knew which jars were peaches (the good ones, near the back, because Tía Ines said good things keep better when you have to reach for them) and which were pickled something-or-other that Mamá pretended to like. He knew the sound of Tía Ines's boots on the stairs above, and he knew the difference between her *all-clear* knock — two quick, one slow — and the *stay-quiet* knock, which was no knock at all, just the absence of one, stretching on too long while somebody's footsteps crossed the kitchen overhead.

For most of December the absence-of-knock had been rare. But lately it came more often. Lately there were footsteps Tomás didn't know. Heavy ones. And once — this had frightened him worse than anything, though he'd never say so — a woman's laugh, close to the kitchen door, and Tía Ines laughing back, and Tomás pressed against the cold stone wall with his hands over his ears thinking *she will forget us, she is laughing and she has forgotten us.*

She hadn't forgotten them. She always came down after, with bread or beans or a story about the village, and her face did the thing where it smiled at the top and worried underneath, like a house with pretty curtains in a window that needed paint.

Tonight the stairs creaked and two knocks came, quick-quick, then one slow, and Tomás shot up from the cot before Mamá could say anything, because going up first meant he got the good chair by the window, and also — though he'd never admit this either — because he couldn't stand another hour of the cellar room's ceiling, which he had memorized down to every water stain, including the big one shaped like a rabbit being chased by a smaller, angrier rabbit.

* * *

The kitchen of the keeper's cottage was warm in the way only winter kitchens are warm, warm like an apology. The stove ticked. A pot of something with cabbage in it sent up steam that fogged the window over the basin, and that was the whole reason Tomás loved this room, though he would have died before saying so in front of Mamá, who would have gotten the look that meant *we are guests here, we are hidden here, wanting things is dangerous.*

Because the window was the treasure. It faced the river.

Tía Ines let him sit on the stool by it as long as he promised to be "a lamp himself — still and quiet." He was very still and very quiet, and he wiped a porthole in the fog with his sleeve, and there it was: the river, frozen white-gray-silver from bank to bank, running away north until it went thin as thread and disappeared into the dark.

It was the biggest thing Tomás had ever seen. Bigger than the harbor at home. Bigger than the sea, even, because the sea moved and argued, but the frozen river just lay there, patient, like a road someone had built overnight out of moonlight.

"The ice is thick enough now," Tía Ines said behind him. She was mending a sock that had given up hope long ago; Tomás had watched her attack it three evenings running. "Emilio says he can hear it settling at night. That means it's taking weight."

"Whose weight?"

"Anybody's." She bit the thread. "A fox. A sled. A fool."

"And people," Tomás said. "People cross it."

The mending stopped.

In the corner by the stove, Papá-hole shifted. Mamá looked up from her letter — the one she read four times a day although it said the same five things — and Uncle Emilio's knife went quiet in the wood.

"People cross it," Tía Ines said carefully, "when they shouldn't. Ice lies, Tomás. It looks strong and it isn't. There are places where springs feed under, and the ice goes thin as window glass, and a man steps out and the river takes him before he finishes shouting."

"How do you know where the thin places are?"

"You don't. That's the trick of it."

"That's a terrible trick."

"It is," she agreed, and something in her voice made him turn around. She wasn't smiling at the top anymore. She was all worry underneath, like a curtain with nothing pretty behind it. Then she caught herself and made the smile come back, and Tomás felt a strange grown-up feeling go through him, the feeling of seeing a magic trick from behind — knowing how it was done and wishing you didn't.

"When do we cross?" he asked.

Nobody answered. The stove ticked. The sock sat in Tía Ines's lap like a small dead animal.

"To the free side," Tomás went on, because nobody had stopped him, and stopping was clearly not going to happen. "Marisol — Mamá says when the ice breaks, the ferry runs again, and the ferry goes north, past the checkpoint, to where nobody asks for papers because nobody has papers, everybody's just people. When? Is it soon? Is it after Christmas? I'm asking because I need to know whether to leave the peach jars alone or—"

"Tomás." Mamá's voice. Not sharp. Worse than sharp. Soft. "Come here."

He went. He sat between her and Uncle Emilio on the bench, and Mamá put her arm around him, and for a minute the only sound was the stove and the wind trying the door latch like a polite burglar.

"You remember what we agreed," Mamá said. "We don't talk about crossing. We don't talk about the ferry, or the marshal, or names. We especially don't talk about names."

"I didn't say any names."

"You almost said mine."

"It's your name," Tomás protested. "I've met you."

Uncle Emilio laughed — a short bark, quickly swallowed — and Mamá gave him the wife-look, the one that could travel around corners, and Tía Ines set down the sock and came to sit across from them, elbows on knees, the way she did when she was about to say something true.

"Tomás," she said. "Do you know why we don't talk about it?"

"Because talking is loud?"

"Because—" She paused. Tomás watched her decide, actually watched the decision happen on her face, like watching someone choose a jar off a high shelf. "Because there is a man whose job is counting people. Writing names in a book. And some people — bad men, men who get paid — want to know who is in this book and who isn't. And if they find out we're here, they take us away. Back across the border. Or worse."

"Worse how?"

"Tomás," Mamá said.

"He asked," Tía Ines said mildly. "I won't lie to a boy who asks. I'll leave things out, but I won't lie. Lies are heavy and children end up carrying them." She held his eyes. Hers were dark brown, nearly black, with little lights in them like the lamp glass. "So. Worse means jail. Worse means being sent somewhere cold and far from your mamá. Do you understand?"

Tomás understood. He understood it the way you understand the sea — you understand it perfectly right up until you're in it.

"So when we cross," he said slowly, working it through, "we'll be safe. On the free side."

"When we cross," Mamá began, and then stopped, and Tomás saw her look at Uncle Emilio, and Uncle Emilio look at Tía Ines, and Tía Ines look at the window, at the great white road lying patient under the stars, and nobody finished the sentence, and the sentence hung there unfinished like the spoon that would never be done.

*If,* Tomás thought. They hadn't said *when.* They'd started to say *when* and it had turned into *if* in their mouths, quietly, like water turning to ice.

He looked back at the window. His breath-fog porthole had closed over again. He wiped it open with his sleeve, harder this time, and stared north along the frozen river to where it thinned into darkness, and he made himself a promise the size of the river, which was the biggest promise he knew how to make.

* * *

He dreamed about the crossing that night, down in the cellar room with the angry rabbit stain overhead.

In the dream the ice sang. That was the strange part — Uncle Emilio had told him once that the river sings when it freezes hard, a low whale-song groaning through the night, and Tomás had heard it sometimes through the floor, a long mournful note like God clearing His throat — and in the dream the song got louder and louder until it was words, and the words were *come on, come on, come on,* and they were walking out onto the white road, all four of them plus Tía Ines, and the stars were below them in the ice as well as above, so they walked inside the sky. And Tomás looked down at the star he was stepping on and the star opened its eye, and the eye was the river's eye, and it blinked—

He woke with his heart banging. Above him, faintly, the real river sang its real note, long and low and patient. *Come on,* it seemed to say. Or maybe it said *wait.* With rivers you couldn't tell.

He lay awake a long time after that, doing sums, because sums were calming, Papá had taught him sums. One ferry. Three berths — he'd heard Tía Ines say it to Mamá through the floor last week, *three berths, no more, and the price went up again, the old vulture.* Four of them. Four souls, three berths. Even he could see the trouble with that arithmetic, and he was nine.

Four minus one. Which one?

He turned the question over the way you turn a stone over, and then wished immediately he hadn't, because underneath were all the crawling things. If Mamá stayed — no. If Papá-hole— no, Papá wasn't a soul to count, Papá was already subtracted, that was the whole grief of it. If Uncle Emilio stayed, Mamá would cry for years, Tomás knew it in his bones. If Mamá stayed, Tomás would jump off the ferry and walk back across the lying ice himself, thin places and all. And Tía Ines — well, Tía Ines belonged to the lighthouse the way the flame belonged to the lamp. You couldn't move a flame. Everybody knew that.

Which left him.

Tomás lay in the dark and considered himself. He was small, which took up less berth. He was light, which cost the ferryman less in oats. He was, he decided, objectively the correct answer, and the correctness of it settled over him with a terrible calm, and then, beneath the calm, fear rose like floodwater, and he did the bravest thing he had ever done in his short life, which was to lie there and not cry, because crying was loud, and loud was dangerous, and he was going to be brave the way Tía Ines was brave — quietly, competently, without anybody noticing until afterward.

He fell asleep doing sums. Four minus one. One carried forward.

* * *

Morning came down the stairs as two quick knocks and one slow, and Tomás climbed up into the warm kitchen, and the day proceeded the way days proceeded: porridge, dishes, lessons — Tía Ines was teaching him letters from a newspaper dated two years back, which she claimed was fine because "spelling hasn't changed" — and Tomás behaved normally, ate normally, laughed at Uncle Emilio's impression of the census man (who had come and gone weeks ago but lived on in family legend as a pair of cold red hands attached to a ledger), and told absolutely no one about the arithmetic he'd done in the night.

He was being so normal about it that it nearly worked.

It fell apart at the window, in the late afternoon, when the light went gold and long across the frozen river and turned it into a road of hammered brass, and Tomás forgot himself entirely, which was his special gift and his family's recurring problem.

"When the ice breaks," he announced to the glass, to the river, to the room, "I'll be the one who goes. Because I'm smallest. I don't eat much. And then when I'm safe on the free side, I'll send money, and you can all follow on the next ferry, and—"

"Tomás." Mamá's voice cracked clean through the middle of his speech.

"—and I'll learn a trade, maybe lamps, since I know about lamps now—"

"*Tomás.*"

He turned. His mother had both hands pressed flat on the table as if the room were tilting. Uncle Emilio had gone very still. And Tía Ines stood in the doorway with a bucket in one hand and an expression Tomás had never seen on her face before — not the smile-over-worry, not the curtain-with-nothing-behind-it, but something raw and stricken, as if the boy had reached into her chest and read the sums written on the back of her ribs.

Because that's what they were, he realized. The same sums. Four minus one. She'd been carrying them longer than he had, and hers had calluses on them.

"Who put that in your head?" Mamá whispered. "Who told you—"

"Nobody. It's just arithmetic." He said it gently, the way adults talked to him, and saw it land, and saw that it landed badly.

Mamá came across the room and gathered him up — all of him, though he was nearly too big to be gathered, and would soon be too big altogether, and some part of him noted that this might be among the last times — and she held him so tight the buttons of her dress printed themselves into his cheek, and she was shaking, and she said into his hair, over and over, in Spanish, in the old language, in a mix of both, *no, no, my love, no, that will never happen, I would sooner the river take me—*

"Don't say that," Tía Ines said sharply. "Don't hand the river ideas."

And that broke the terrible moment open into something almost funny, the way things broke in that house, and Mamá laughed wetly into Tomás's hair, and Uncle Emilio said the river had enough ideas already, it was always singing about them at night, and Tomás laughed too, shaky, still held fast.

But Tía Ines didn't laugh. She set the bucket down — it sloshed; she was carrying water in from the cistern, always working, always — and she looked at the window, at the brass road running north, and Tomás watched the arithmetic finish itself behind her eyes.

"We'll need more than three berths," she said quietly, to no one, to everyone, to the river. "Or we'll find another way over. Whatever it costs."

She picked the bucket back up. At the door she paused, and looked back at Tomás sitting in the circle of his mother's arms, and something passed between them — not a promise exactly, because promises could break, but an understanding, which was heavier and held better.

"Finish your letters," she said. "All of them. A boy who's going to send money home ought to spell properly."

Then she was out into the blue cold with her bucket, and the door shut, and Tomás sat with his mother's heartbeat against his ear and thought: *whatever it costs.* He was nine, and he knew about costs now. Costs were what you paid when the sum came out wrong.

Down the path, toward the village where the ferryman kept his ledgers and his vulture's prices, Tía Ines's boots crunched on the snow, steady as a heartbeat, going to make the arithmetic come out right.
