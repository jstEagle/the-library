## 8. The Ferryman's Price

The ferryman's name was Hollis Brandt, and he smelled permanently of tar and black pepper, a combination Ines had never gotten used to in eleven years of knowing him. He received her in the boathouse where he wintered his vessel, a flat-bottomed craft named—with the grim humor of a river man—the *Maybe Someday*, which sat propped on blocks like a patient animal waiting out a long illness.

"You'll want tea," Hollis said, before she had said a word. "Nobody comes to see me in January wanting anything but tea first."

"I want berths."

"Tea first." He was already ladling water from the stove kettle into a dented pot. "Berths are bitter medicine. You take them sweet or you don't take them at all."

Ines unwound her scarf and sat on the crate he kicked toward her. The boathouse was warm enough that frost was weeping down the nail heads in the walls, and somewhere overhead a squirrel was rearranging its winter stores with the fussiness of an old maid tidying a parlor. She let him fuss over the tea because it gave her a moment to arrange her face into something ordinary. She had become, these past months, a woman who arranged her face the way other women arranged their hair.

Hollis poured two tin cups and sat down across from her on an upturned rowboat, knees nearly to his ears. He was sixty if he was a day, with hands like knotted rope and eyes the pale gray of old ice.

"Three berths," Ines said. "North side, before the break-up."

Hollis blew on his tea. He did not look at her, which meant he had been expecting this conversation, which meant someone in the village had talked, which meant the arithmetic of Point Alder was already being done without her.

"River breaks early this year," he said. "Ice is thin above the narrows. I heard it groaning Tuesday night like an old man getting out of bed. When it goes, it goes all at once, and no boat crosses until spring thaw proper."

"Then we should be quick about terms."

"We should." He sipped. "I can give you three. Not four. The *Maybe Someday* runs dark, no lights, no manifest, and three souls I can hide under herring barrels. Four and the boat sits too low for the shallows above the mill race. Three is what the river allows, and I don't argue with the river. Argued with it once in '81 and it took my brother for the privilege."

"Three," Ines repeated. Her heart did a small, traitorous leap of relief before she could remind it that relief was premature. "And the price?"

"Full fare, paid before sailing. Twelve dollars a soul. Thirty-six total." He said it plainly, without apology, because Hollis Brandt had never once apologized for the economics of survival. "I know what you're thinking. You're thinking that in summer, when the regular ferry ran, a crossing cost two bits and a smile. But the regular ferry is docked, the marshal is watching the north berth, and every night I run, I risk my boat, my license, and my neck. Twelve dollars is what my neck is worth to me. It's not negotiable, Paloma—"

"Ines," she said automatically.

"Ines," he corrected himself, unbothered. "It's not negotiable, is what I'm saying. Half now, half later? No. If the marshal catches me mid-crossing with your people and your money in my pocket, I'd like to at least have all of it."

* * *

She walked home along the shore road doing sums, because sums were safer than thoughts.

Thirty-six dollars. In the coffee tin beneath the loose floorboard in the pantry—beneath the flour sack, beneath the false bottom Tomás's father Raul had fitted so cleverly two years ago when the family first arrived—she had twenty-three dollars and forty cents. Twenty-three forty. She turned the figure over and over as she walked, the way one turns a stone in the pocket, feeling for some hidden facet that would make it more than it was.

Twelve dollars short and change. Nearly a third of what was needed. And where did a lighthouse keeper find twelve dollars in January, when the lamp oil was government-issued, the vegetables were pickled in the cellar, and the nearest bank was a day's journey south?

She could sell the cow. The thought arrived and she examined it. The cow was less an asset than a personality; Bessie had opinions about fences and a lowing voice that carried across the point like a fog signal. Selling Bessie would buy perhaps eight dollars from the Henderson farm, and then there would be no milk, and milk was the difference between Tomás looking thin and Tomás looking hollow.

She could write to her late husband Elias's people in the southern counties. She composed the letter in her head as she crunched through the snow: *Dear family, I need money, and by the way the sons you mourned are harboring fugitives.* No.

She could ask the priest. Father Mateo kept a poor box, but the poor box had been feeding three hidden mouths since October, and asking him to fund the escape of the very people he'd smuggled in felt like asking the arsonist to pay for the fire brigade.

By the time she reached the station, she had exhausted respectable arithmetic and begun considering the kind that kept her awake at night. There was the emergency fund in the keeper's locker—government money, technically, held against lamp repairs. Eleven dollars. Taking it would be theft from the Lighthouse Establishment, a federal offense, the sort of thing that got keepers dismissed and prosecuted and written up in the annual reports as cautionary examples. She imagined the entry: *Sandoval, Mrs. E., relieved of post for embezzlement of $11.00.* They wouldn't even spell her name right.

*But it would make thirty-four,* whispered the part of her brain that had learned, over the hard years, to do cruel math fluently. *And Marisol has two dollars saved from mending work.*

Thirty-six exactly.

She stood in the kitchen doorway with snow melting off her boots, and found Marisol already there, kneading bread with the fierce concentration of a woman who had decided that if the world would not hold still, at least the dough would rise on schedule. Tomás sat on the counter beside her, aged seven and charged with the solemn duty of counting raisins into the dough, though from the bulge in his cheek he was conducting a parallel audit.

"Well?" Marisol said, not looking up. "What did the tar man say?"

"Three berths. Thirty-six dollars. Paid in full, before sailing."

Marisol's hands stopped. Flour dust hung in the lamplight between them, turning slowly, like the river ice must turn when it finally lets go.

"And how much do we have?"

"Enough," Ines said, which was the first lie she had ever told Marisol Sandoval, and they both knew it, because Marisol set down the dough, wiped her hands, and said, "Ines. How much."

"Twenty-three forty."

Tomás stopped chewing.

* * *

That evening the household gathered around the kitchen table—not the whole truth of the household, because Raul was below in the cellar room, keeping out of sight as always, but Marisol brought his questions down the stairs and carried his answers back up, so that the table talk was really a conversation in relay, a letter written sentence by sentence between floors.

"He can't take four?" Raul's voice came up through the floorboards, muffled by wood but not by much; the cellar room had been built against inspection, not against eavesdropping.

"He says the boat sits too low," Ines called back, feeling absurd, shouting domestic negotiation through her own kitchen floor. "He says three or none."

"Tell him four or I swim."

"Papa," Marisol said sharply, to the floor. "You swim in January and we bury you in April when the ground thaws. That helps nobody."

There was a silence from below. Then, quieter: "Then tell me which three."

And there it was. The question laid out on the kitchen table among the bread and the lamp and the salt cellar, wearing an apron of ordinariness, as if it were any other household matter—as if it were whether to butcher the pig before or after Christmas.

"Nobody's choosing anything tonight," Ines said firmly. "I'm short twelve dollars. Until I have thirty-six, the question doesn't exist."

"That's not—" Marisol began.

"It doesn't exist," Ines repeated, with the voice she used on ships that tried to dispute right-of-way with a lighthouse. "We have days yet. The ice hasn't broken. I will find the money."

She found, after Marisol went below to bring Raul his supper, that Tomás was still sitting at the table, ostensibly doing sums on a slate but in fact watching her with the grave brown eyes of his father's family, eyes that missed nothing and forgave most things and understood, even at seven, more than any child should be asked to carry.

"Aunt Ines," he said carefully. "If you're short money, I have fifty cents."

And there it was—a boy offering his life savings, fifty cents in a tobacco tin, probably earned shoveling the walk of some villager who never asked his name. Ines laughed before she could stop herself, and the laugh cracked open into something dangerously close to weeping, so she pulled him against her shoulder and held him among the smells of flour and lamp oil and pretended it was only affection, only an aunt being fond.

"Fifty cents," she said into his hair. "You drive a hard bargain, but I'll tell you what: you keep your fifty cents, and when we're all safe on the other side, you'll buy me a cup of coffee with it. Deal?"

"Deal," he said into her collarbone. Then, muffled: "Aunt Ines, why does the ice breaking matter? Is the river angry?"

"The river isn't angry, cariño. The river is just honest. It does what it does, and we arrange ourselves around it." She said it lightly, and only afterward heard what she'd said, and how it applied to everything—to the warrants, the marshal, the census taker with his careful ledger, to herself arranging her face each morning around the shape of what she couldn't say aloud.

She put Tomás to bed in the loft—he slept up there with Marisol now, while Raul kept the cellar—and went down the tower stairs to check the lamp, because checking the lamp was the one hour of the day when her mind agreed to be quiet. Brass to polish, wick to trim, oil to measure. The light swept out over the black river, and far downstream she could see, by its reflected glimmer, the pale seam of the ice road running north—the same ice that would soon crack and heave and become a barrier instead of a bridge, taking the last ferry berth with it.

Days yet, she had told Marisol. But how many depended on weather, and weather answered to nobody's arithmetic.

Back in the kitchen, she took down the coffee tin and counted the money again, as if repetition could conjure the missing twelve dollars, as if coins bred in the dark like mice. Twenty-three forty. She counted it a third time. Twenty-three forty.

She was putting the tin back when she heard, distinctly, the crunch of boots on the frozen path outside, unhurried and official-sounding, and through the window she saw a lantern bobbing up the station walk—and behind it, silhouetted against the snow-glow, the unmistakable outline of August Reyes, census taker, coming to her door after dark with a paper folded in his hand.

Ines closed the floorboard softly over the coffee tin, arranged her face into polite surprise, and went to answer the knock.
