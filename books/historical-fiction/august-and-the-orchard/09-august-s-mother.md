## 9. August's Mother

The letter came on the Thursday mail boat, wedged between a crate of lamp oil and a parcel of someone's Christmas fruitcake that had clearly been traveling since the previous Christmas. August knew the hand on the envelope before he knew the name — his mother wrote in a looping, ornate script she had learned from a parish priest in Oporto and never let go of, as if abandoning it would mean abandoning everything else too.

He carried it unopened through the village, past the chandlery and past Rosa Ferreira's window, where she was pretending very hard not to watch him pretend not to notice. The letter was a small warm weight in his coat pocket, and he found himself, absurdly, saving it. Like a sweet after supper. His life at Point Alder had become so thoroughly composed of ledgers and suspicion that an actual letter from home felt like contraband of the pleasant kind.

The room the station master kept for him above the boat shed was narrow but serviceable: a cot, a stove with opinions about its own fuel, a window that looked out on the gray river and, across it, on the lighthouse standing at the end of its spit like a woman who refused to be moved by anything.

August built up the fire, boiled water for weak tea, and opened the letter.

*My Augusto,* it began. He smiled despite himself. Twenty-nine years old and conducting the government's counting, and still *My Augusto*, as though he were seven and had fallen out of a tree.

*Your father is well, though he complains about his knees the way other men complain about the weather, which is to say constantly and without hope of improvement. Mrs. Duarte's cow got into the churchyard again and ate the flowers off the altar, which Father Mendes says was God's way of reminding us not to worship decoration. I am well also, though my hands ache in this cold.*

Then, three paragraphs down, in the middle of news about a cousin's wedding and a recipe she insisted on including because "you are too thin and living on government biscuits":

*I had a dream about Point Alder last week. Strange, that I should dream of a place I saw only once, and in such a bad season. You know I came through there, of course — you know the shape of that story, if not all its corners. But I have never told you what happened in the winter I passed through, and lately I find myself thinking I should like you to know it, because dreams have a way of being letters we send ourselves.*

August set down his tea.

*It was the cholera winter, as they called it afterward. You will remember the year even if you do not remember the winter — you were four months old and we had left you behind with your grandmother while I came ahead to make a place for us. That was the arrangement, and I have never regretted it, though the crossing nearly finished me before the sickness did. When I landed, there was a warrant out for people like me. Not for the sick — for the foreign. Someone had decided the epidemic was our fault, the way someone always decides something, and so those of us who could prove nothing hid wherever hiding could be found.*

*I was twenty-three and alone and coughing blood into a handkerchief I washed every night so no one would see. I had been told there was work north of Point Alder, but the ferry berth closed early that year and I had no coin and no papers, and I sat on the sea wall in the sleet wondering whether it would be worse to die of the sickness or of the cold, and whether anyone would count me either way.*

*You are a census man, so you will appreciate this next part: what saved me was a woman asking me questions. She found me on the sea wall because I had fainted onto it, more or less, and when I woke she was holding my wrist and demanding my name, where I was from, how long I had been ill, whether I had eaten. I thought she must be some kind of official. I was terrified. And then she said — I have never forgotten the words — "Good. Then you are somebody. Somebody has to eat soup."*

August laughed out loud, alone in the cold room, and then pressed the letter flat against his knee and read it again.

*She was the lighthouse keeper's wife's sister, or the keeper's sister-in-law — the relations there were always tangled — but everyone called her Ines, and she took me back to the light station in a cart with the lantern covered, as if smuggling brandy, and she said nothing about warrants or papers, only fed me soup until I could hold the spoon and quinine until the fever broke, and kept me in a room off the cellar for eleven weeks while the epidemic burned itself out and the marshal's men searched every boarding house in the village twice.*

*Eleven weeks, Augusto. She lied to officials for me. I did not know that then; I thought I was a guest. It was only years later, when I finally told her who I had been running from, that she laughed and said she had known since the second day, because nobody who was legal looked at a door that way when it knocked.*

*I asked her how I could repay her and she said, "Count yourself. Live somewhere, be somebody, put your name in books." I have thought of that often since I heard what work you took. My son goes door to door writing names into the great book of the country. I do not think she would mind. I think she would be proud. But I wanted you to know that the hands you come from were once the hands that needed counting and weren't counted, and that somewhere on that coast there is a woman to whom your mother owes her life, and therefore so do you.*

*Eat properly. Write soon. Your father sends his knees' regards.*

*Your mother,
Lucinda Reyes*

August read the letter a fourth time, then a fifth, and then he folded it along its original creases with the exaggerated care of a man handling evidence, which in a sense it was.

The fire popped. Out the window, the river lay under its first thin skin of ice, and the lighthouse beam swung through the dusk with its old patience.

Ines Sandoval.

Of course it was Ines Sandoval. He sat with his cooling tea and understood that some part of him had known since the moment he'd stepped off the mail boat and seen her standing at the base of the tower — straight-spined, measuring him right back — that she was a person to whom things were owed. He had filed the feeling under *instinct* and mistrusted it accordingly, because instinct was not admissible in a ledger. Now here was the ledger entry he had never made, arriving by Thursday post: one life, saved, interest compounded over thirty years, payable to bearer.

And he had spent two weeks poking around her village like a weasel after eggs.

He winced, remembering. The careful questions at the chandlery. The way he'd stood in Rosa Ferreira's kitchen letting her fill the silence, taking notes in his head about *extra shadows at the light*. The way he'd watched Ines pour tea with steady hands and thought, *flawless, too flawless, I know a liar when I'm served cake by one.* He had prided himself on that recognition — raised by a woman who'd hidden her maiden name for a decade, he could hear a constructed sentence at forty paces — and he had worn the pride like a badge, and the whole time he had been listening to the woman who had pulled his mother off a sea wall and fed her back to life.

Somebody has to eat soup.

He got up and paced the little room, six steps one way, six the other, the floorboards complaining. Because here was the problem, laid out neat as a column of figures: he had reason to believe there were undocumented persons at the light station. One bed too many. One name too few in the household roster against a prior year's log listing four residents. A cellar door that had been freshly oiled, which nobody oils unless they're going in and out of it often, or hoping no one notices them doing either. He was a census taker with a supervisor, a schedule, and a form that demanded truth the way a river demands the sea. And now he was also a man whose entire existence — his mother's coughing, twenty-three-year-old existence, and therefore his own — hung from a single act of exactly the lawbreaking he was sent here to record.

If Ines were sheltering someone — and she was, he was nearly certain she was; he could feel it the way sailors feel weather coming — then she was doing to the government precisely what she had done for Lucinda Reyes. And the government had sent her own debt to collect against her.

"Someone has a sense of humor," August said to the stove, which declined to comment.

He thought about his mother's story from the other side of it — the official side. Somewhere in that cholera winter there had been a census, or a survey, or a marshal's list, and somewhere on it Ines Sandoval had written a falsehood, or an omission, and eleven weeks of a young woman's life had fit inside that lie like a ship inside a bottle. If the false entry had been caught, if some diligent clerk with good instincts had flagged the discrepancy at the light station — four residents one month, three the next, explain please — then Lucinda Reyes would have been taken up, or turned out, or simply buried nameless in the epidemic's arithmetic. There would have been no Augusto. No letter. No son sitting in a boat shed above a frozen river, holding the instrument of his own erasure.

The ground shifted beneath him, quietly, the way river ice shifts — no crack yet, just a sudden deep knowledge that nothing under his feet was solid.

Because here was the thing about debts like this one: they could not be paid. That was what made them debts instead of transactions. He could not arrest her and call it square. He could not look away and call it square either, though God knows part of him wanted to — wanted to write *one resident* in his tidy hand, close the book, catch the next boat south, and let the whole matter dissolve into the general fog of unfinished paperwork. But he had seen her face when he'd asked his question over tea, that half-second of stillness before the flawless denial, and he knew what looking away cost. Rosa Ferreira knew something. The village knew something. A report deferred was not a report denied, and if it came from anyone else's mouth — from Rosa's, hungry and observant, at the right ear for the right price — then Ines would face it without an ally, and the person in the cellar, whoever they were, would face it with less than nothing.

So he could not look away, and he could not report, and he could not unknow what the letter knew.

What he could do — he stopped pacing. What he could do was what Ines had done on the sea wall. Ask questions. Establish the facts. Find out who exactly was in that cellar room, and how many, and how desperate, before he decided anything. Not as a hunter. As a man taking stock of what he might have to carry.

He sat back down and pulled his working notes toward him — the household schedules, the prior station log's numbers copied in his neat clerk's hand, the little marginal observations he'd allowed himself (*cellar door, oiled; flour purchased in quantities exceeding declared household*). Evidence. He had built a case without ever admitting to himself he was building one.

August looked at it all for a long moment, and then, instead of filing it into his document case as procedure demanded, he slid the pages into the plain brown envelope his mother's letter had arrived in, and put that inside his shirt, against his chest, where government paper had no business being.

"You owe a debt," he told himself sternly, buttoning his coat. "That doesn't mean you get to be stupid about it."

Outside, the wind had come around to the northeast, and it had teeth in it. Down the spit the light turned, and turned, and turned. He would go to her — tomorrow, or the day after, when he had thought of a reason that wasn't a confession, because he was not ready to kneel in a lantern room and tell a woman she owned his life and didn't seem to know it. He would find a pretext. He was, after all, trained in constructing sentences.

He was still rehearsing pretexts when the knock came at the shed-door below — two blows, brisk and official — and a voice called up the stair that made the tea in his cup go suddenly cold:

"Mister Reyes? Telegram for the census office. Signed Halloran, District Supervisor. Says to read it directly."

August took the envelope down the stairs with fingers that felt like someone else's. Inside, in the clipped language of wire, the message was short:

*AUDIT POINT ALDER STATION STOP LEAVING FRIDAY BOAT STOP VERIFY ROSTER PERSONALLY STOP FULL DISCREPANCY REPORT REQUIRED UPON ARRIVAL STOP HALLORAN.*

Friday. Two days.

Against his chest, inside his shirt, his mother's letter lay over the borrowed pages of his notes, warm as a coal.
