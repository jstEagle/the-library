## 10. Inspection

The telegram arrived on a Tuesday, which August would later decide was fitting. Tuesdays were the days nothing good had ever happened to him. He'd been born on a Tuesday, according to his mother, who claimed the midwife had sighed the moment he appeared, as if to say, *this one will be trouble*. He'd lost his first job on a Tuesday — a clerkship in a shipping office, ended when he'd corrected his employer's arithmetic aloud in front of a client. And now, standing in the district office in Millbrook with a telegram trembling in his cold-stiffened fingers, he read the word **AUDIT** in block capitals and thought: *of course. Tuesday.*

He read it twice, because the first time his eyes had refused to cooperate.

**SURPRISE INSPECTION LIGHT STATION ROSTERS STOP POINT ALDER INCLUDED STOP INSPECTOR HALLORAN DEPARTS THURSDAY STOP VERIFY ALL HOUSEHOLD RECORDS AGAINST CENSUS RETURNS STOP NO EXCEPTIONS STOP — DURAND**

No exceptions. Durand underlined it himself, which meant Durand had written it while angry, and Durand angry was a weather system all its own.

August set the telegram down on his desk with the exaggerated care of a man handling something that might bite. Around him, the district office went about its morning: the clatter of the telegraph key, the smell of coal smoke and old paper, young Osgood licking an envelope with the concentration of a cat grooming itself. Ordinary sounds. Ordinary morning. Except that somewhere north of here, up a river that was already wearing its winter collar of ice, there was a lighthouse keeper named Ines Sandoval who had told him — smoothly, flawlessly, with the calm of a woman describing the tide — that she lived alone.

And August Reyes knew, with the certainty of a man who had been raised by a liar and had therefore learned to count beds through walls, that she did not live alone.

* * *

He had known for eleven days now, and he had done precisely nothing with the knowledge, which was itself a kind of action. Every night he lay in his rented room above the feed store and conducted the same trial, prosecuting himself in absentia. The evidence: the station log from the previous year, listing four residents — Elias Sandoval, keeper; Ines Sandoval, his wife; and two names in a different hand, smudged but legible if you knew what you were looking at, which August did. The current declaration: one resident. Ines alone. And between those two documents, a drowned husband, a burned ferry manifest, and a boy's mitten left drying on a cellar stair that no one had remembered to hide.

The mitten. That was the detail that undid him at three in the morning. Not the extra bed frame he'd glimpsed through a doorway, not the four bowls stacked by the wash basin when one woman living alone needed perhaps two. The mitten. Small, gray, darned at the thumb with red wool, sitting on the stair like a small animal that had fallen asleep there. Children left things everywhere; August knew this because he had been a child who left things everywhere, and his mother had followed behind him gathering them up with the weary tenderness of a woman collecting evidence of her own heart walking around outside her body.

A woman living alone did not darn mittens. A woman living alone had no one to darn them *for*.

And yet he had sat across from Ines Sandoval at her kitchen table with his ledger open and his questions arranged like cutlery, and she had answered every one of them without so much as a blink, and some traitorous part of him had wanted to applaud.

"You're smiling," said Osgood, appearing at his elbow with the envelope now sealed and slightly soggy. "Nobody smiles reading a Durand telegram."

"I'm not smiling."

"Your mouth is doing a thing."

"My mouth is doing its job." August stood, straightened his coat, and tried to locate the professional self he usually wore like a second skin. It was missing. Possibly it was still back at Point Alder, sitting at Ines Sandoval's table, drinking tea he hadn't wanted and asking questions he hadn't meant. "Where's Halloran?"

Osgood's face performed a small tragedy. "Halloran? He's coming *here*? Oh, no. No, no. I alphabetized everything last month, I did, I—"

"He's coming Thursday." August kept his voice level. "For the light station rosters. Surprise audit."

"But Point Alder's clean. Isn't it?" Osgood frowned, and then, because Osgood's mind worked like a slow but honest plow, his frown deepened. "Isn't it?"

"It's clean," August said, and heard how the words came out of him: smooth, immediate, unblinking. Ines Sandoval's own cadence, borrowed without permission. He almost laughed. Two careful people measuring each other, he'd thought on the mail boat going home, and here he was measuring himself now and finding the ruler bent.

* * *

Durand summoned him at noon. Durand's office occupied the corner of the building with the good windows, though Durand never looked out of them, preferring instead to look at whoever stood before his desk as though they were a column of figures that refused to add up.

"Reyes." Durand did not invite him to sit. Durand believed chairs encouraged loitering. "You did Point Alder yourself this season."

"Yes, sir."

"And your return shows one resident. The keeper, Ines Sandoval, widow." Durand's finger rested on the ledger page like a man pinning a moth. "Previous year's station log shows four."

"The previous keeper, Elias Sandoval, drowned in March. His brother and the brother's family departed — I have their departure noted in my field book." This was true. August had written it himself, in his own hand, in ink, on a day when he had believed — or had chosen to believe, which amounted to the same thing in federal paperwork — that the household had emptied like a nest in autumn.

"Departed where?"

"The north shore settlements. Ferry traffic." August kept his eyes on the middle distance, just past Durand's shoulder, where a map of the river district hung with its little printed lighthouses like stitches closing a wound.

"Ferry stopped running in November, didn't it? Ice."

"After the family had gone, sir."

"Hm." Durand turned the page. Turned another. The sound of it was very loud. "Halloran will want to see the station itself. Walk the tower, check the log against the lamp inventory, verify the roster personally. He'll interview the keeper directly. You'll accompany him — you know the station, you took the census, you can vouch for the record."

"Yes, sir."

"That's all, Reyes." And then, as August reached the door, because Durand saved his worst sentences for doorways the way other men saved dessert: "One more thing. There's been talk in the villages. Somebody wrote to the marshal's office about irregularities at the light stations — harboring, they said. Unregistered persons. Halloran's been asked to keep his eyes open. If anything turns up at Point Alder that isn't in your census—" Durand finally looked up, and his gaze had the flat shine of river ice, pretty and lethal, "—it won't be the keeper who answers for it. Keepers are provincial. But a census taker who falsifies a federal return? That's a penitentiary matter, Reyes. I'd want to be sure your book is exact."

"Exact," August repeated. "Yes, sir."

He walked out into the street feeling the way a man feels stepping onto ice he has been warned about: committed, weightless, and listening for the crack.

* * *

Thursday was two days away. Wednesday evening found him in the back room of Marta's boarding house, where the boarders took their supper and Marta herself presided over the coffee pot with the authority of a harbor master. August had eaten there three nights a week for two years, and Marta had long ago classified him as *thin, works too much, needs feeding*, a category she applied to most unmarried men under forty and addressed with ruthless pot roast.

Tonight he pushed the food around his plate until Marta confiscated it.

"You're either sick or in love," she announced, refilling his cup unasked. "Sick men don't frown at potatoes. They eat them and complain. So."

"So what?"

"So which is it?"

"Neither. I have an audit."

Marta snorted. She was seventy if she was a day, built like the stove she cooked on, and she had opinions about government the way rivers had opinions about bridges. "An audit. A man starves himself over *paperwork*. Sit. Tell me about this lighthouse, since you've been mooning about it since the census run."

"I haven't been mooning."

"Milo," she called toward the kitchen, "he says he hasn't been mooning." From the kitchen, Milo — her son, a large quiet man who fixed roofs — made a sound that in any language meant *sure he hasn't*. August gave up.

"There's a keeper," he said carefully. "At Point Alder. She runs the light alone since her husband drowned."

"And?"

"And nothing. I took her census. One resident. Correct and proper."

Marta studied him over the rim of her cup with eyes that had watched fifty years of boarders lie about smaller things than this. "You know," she said, "when I came to this country, nobody counted me. Nobody counted me for six years. I lived in a cellar in Harwick with my sister and her two boys, and when the tally man came, my sister said *just us, just the four of us*, meaning the family upstairs, and I held my breath in the dark with flour on my hands until he left." She set the cup down. "I was a name too many once, August Reyes. So when you tell me *correct and proper*, I hear a man reciting somebody else's words."

August looked at his hands. They were steady, which surprised him. "If the record is wrong," he said quietly, "and I let it stand, I lose everything. My post. My pension. Possibly more than that — Durand mentioned the marshal's office is involved now. There's a warrant side to it. People hiding from papers."

"And if the record is right," Marta said, "and you make it wrong — what do you lose?"

"That's not how it works."

"How does it work, then?"

He didn't answer, because the true answer was a thing he hadn't yet said aloud even to himself, and saying it in Marta's warm kitchen would make it real in a way that frightened him more than Durand did. The true answer was: *if I write down what I believe is in that cellar room, I hand a child to a penitentiary barge, and I do it with the same pen my mother used to sign our name wrong for eleven years so that no one would ever come looking for us.* The true answer was: *my mother lied to the government her whole life, and I became the government, and I have spent thirty years pretending those two facts can live in the same man.*

"Marta," he said instead, "if a man falsifies a federal document, what would you call him?"

She considered this seriously, the way she considered everything, including whether the bread had risen and whether the new minister was a fool (he was). "Depends what he was protecting," she said. "My sister called our cellar *the counting house*, because we counted everything in it except ourselves." She gathered his untouched plate. "Eat Thursday before you go. Cold river makes a man stupid, and stupid men sign things they regret."

* * *

He packed that night by lamplight, and packing became a kind of argument with himself, conducted in objects.

The field book went in first — his official notes, his observations, the little marginal marks he made for his own memory. He stared at the page where he'd recorded the Point Alder return. *Ines Sandoval, keeper, resident alone. Husband d. March, drowning. Household otherwise cleared.* His own handwriting, neat and certain, lying or telling the truth depending entirely on what was actually in that cellar, which he did not know, which was somehow worse than knowing.

Because that was the thing he kept circling and could not land on: he didn't *know*. He had a mitten and a bed frame and four bowls and a liar's perfect calm, and none of it was evidence, and all of it was true. If he walked into that audit and declared his suspicions, and he was wrong — if the brother truly had gone north, if the mitten belonged to some visiting niece, if he had invented a hidden family out of a lonely woman's tidy kitchen — then he would have destroyed her for nothing, and destroyed himself too, and Durand would pin the moth to the page regardless.

And if he was right, and he stayed silent, and Halloran found what he would find —

August sat down on the edge of his narrow bed and put his face in his hands.

There was a third option, of course. There is always a third option, and it always costs more than the first two. He could alter the record. Not the census — that was signed, filed, sealed in triplicate, beyond reach. But the *roster verification*, the audit copy, the working document Halloran would compare against the station log. If the station log showed four residents last year and one now, well — deaths and departures explained that, provided the departures were documented. Provided there was a paper trail. Provided someone had, say, recorded a ferry passage, a settlement registration, a northern address for a family that had never taken any ferry anywhere.

Provided someone forged them.

His father had been a forger of a humbler kind — signatures on work permits, a doctor's mark on a health certificate, the small criminal calligraphy of the poor. August had learned his letters copying those signatures by firelight, thinking he was learning penmanship. He had the steadiest hand in the district office. Osgood said so. Durand said so, once, in a tone that August now understood had been a warning dressed as praise.

He got up, lit the lamp properly, and pulled a fresh sheet of paper toward him. For a long time he simply looked at it. Then he practiced, once, in a light quick hand, the flourish of a ferry clerk's signature — a man named Brody, whose register August had consulted in October, whose *B* curled like a wave.

It was good. It was very good. That was the terrible thing about it — it was effortless, the way sin is supposed to be hard and never is.

He crumpled the sheet and threw it in the stove, and watched it curl, and then he took out another sheet, because a man should know exactly what he is capable of before he decides what he will do.

* * *

The mail boat left for Point Alder at dawn Thursday, carrying Inspector Halloran, his leather case, his spectacles, and August Reyes, who stood at the rail watching the river ice thicken along the banks and understood that he was traveling toward the point of no return at the speed of nine knots.

Halloran proved to be a chatty man, which was the worst possible kind. He talked about lamp mechanisms and budget appropriations and the price of coal, and then, somewhere past the narrows, he said pleasantly, "You took the census up here yourself, didn't you, Reyes?"

"In October."

"Good season for it. And the keeper — Sandoval, the widow — she struck you as forthcoming?"

August watched a raft of ice turn slowly in the current, white and patient. "She struck me," he said, "as a woman who answers exactly the question that is asked her."

Halloran laughed. "Ha! Best kind. Half these keepers give you a sermon when you ask the time." He adjusted his spectacles and gazed northward, where the Point Alder light was not yet visible but soon would be, a white stitch on a gray shore, keeping its flame over whatever it was keeping below. "Well. We'll see if the books agree with her."

August's hand, resting on the rail, had gone cold past feeling. Below the waterline, beneath the hull, the river moved dark and unhurried, carrying its ice toward the sea, breaking apart and refreezing and breaking again, patient as arithmetic.

Two days, he thought. He had two days to become either the man who reported a hidden family or the man who forged federal documents, and both men were waiting for him at the end of this channel like two versions of a door.

Ahead, the light at Point Alder caught the low sun and flashed — once, twice — steady as a heartbeat.

He found he couldn't look away from it.
