## 11. The Cellar Room

Ines had always believed that fear, properly managed, was a kind of housework. You swept it into corners. You beat it out of the rugs before anyone came to call. You kept the windows of it polished so that from the outside it looked like an ordinary life, and if you did this faithfully every day, most days it stayed put.

She went down the cellar stairs with a lamp in one hand and a list in her head.

The cellar room had been Rafael's idea — her husband's brother, the reason for all of this. *A keeper should have a root cellar,* he'd said the first autumn, cheerful as a man describing a chicken coop, and then he'd built it deeper than any root cellar needed to be, with a door that didn't creak and a shelf arrangement that made room along the far wall for something that wasn't potatoes. She had thought at the time that Rafael worried too much about doors that creaked. Now she understood he had simply seen the world more clearly than she had, and the world had proven him right in every particular but the last one, the one where he lived long enough to hide behind his own clever walls.

She knocked twice, paused, once — their little liturgy.

Marisol opened the door before she finished the second knock, which meant she'd been standing there listening, which meant she'd heard Ines coming down the stairs and had time to compose her face into whatever expression she wanted Ines to see. They were alike in this way, the two of them, and it made honesty between them expensive.

"You've decided," Marisol said. Not a question.

"I'm working on deciding." Ines set the lamp on the shelf. "I've come to prepare you. There's a difference."

"There is," Marisol agreed, "and the difference is usually two weeks."

Behind her, the room showed its small domestic order: the pallets rolled tight against the wall, Rafael's shirts hung on nails like a row of pale flags of a country that issued no passports, Tomás's slate propped against a crate with arithmetic chalked across it in his enormous, earnest hand. *7 × 8 = 56*, written four times, each 5 slightly different, as though the boy were trying to catch the correct number by surprise. The stove ticked quietly. The whole room smelled of paraffin and dried apples and the particular mineral breath of river stone in winter.

"Sit," Ines said. "Both of you. Where's Rafael?"

"Mending the net we're not allowed to use," Marisol said, and there it was — the edge in her voice, thin as fishing line. Her husband emerged from behind the stove where he'd been doing exactly that, spooling twine around two fingers with the concentration of a man performing surgery. He smiled at Ines the way he always did, warm and apologetic at once, a man apologizing for the weather.

"Ines. Good. We were hoping—" He stopped himself, because Marisol had looked at him, and whatever the look contained ended the sentence.

Ines sat down on the crate beside Tomás's slate. "The ferryman has three berths open before the ice breaks. Three. After that, nothing until spring, and spring may be too late. The warrants have teeth now — Father Esteban says there's money in it for informants." She watched their faces take this in. Rafael's hands stilled on the net. Marisol's chin lifted, which with her sister-in-law was what happened instead of flinching. "I have coin for two berths. I am arranging the third. And I need you ready to move the moment I say, which means packed, dressed, and able to walk to the north landing in the dark without lamps."

"When?" Marisol asked.

"Soon. Within days. The ice is talking."

"The ice is always talking." But Marisol glanced toward the low ceiling, beyond which the river lay, and Ines knew she had heard it too — that deep, irregular knocking under the floor of the world, like someone asking to be let in.

* * *

Preparing them turned out to be the easy part. Preparing them, Ines could do with her eyes shut: she hauled up the good traveling bundle from the storeroom, divided the dried apples into three portions and argued with herself about whether a fourth portion counted as optimism or superstition (it counted as love; she packed it anyway), and set Rafael to oiling the boots by lamplight while Marisol mended the torn shoulder seam of the coat Tomás had nearly outgrown.

It was during the mending that Marisol said, conversationally, into the quiet, "Your census man."

"He's not *my* census man."

"He sat in your kitchen drinking your tea for the better part of an afternoon."

"He drank tea in the kitchen of every station on the river. It's called procedure. He had a ledger and cold hands and questions, and I answered them the way one answers all government men, which is to say precisely and not at all." Ines folded apples into cloth with more force than dried fruit required.

"And now he comes back a second time, unannounced, asking about old station logs." Marisol pulled the thread through, snipped, tied. The needle flashed. "You told me yourself he wanted to see the record books from last year. Why would a counting man care how many people slept here a winter ago, unless he'd noticed the arithmetic didn't come out even?"

"Because he's thorough. Thoroughness is the job."

"Yes," Marisol said softly. "And betrayal pays thirty dollars."

The words landed in the room like a dropped iron. Rafael's hands stopped on the boot. Even the stove seemed to hold its ticking.

"That's not fair," Ines said, and hated immediately how it sounded — defensive, cornered, a woman arguing rather than a woman certain.

"No," Marisol agreed. "It isn't. Nothing about this is fair, least of all that I have to say these things in front of my husband while he oils our only boots, in a hole in the ground, in a country that doesn't want us, depending entirely on a woman who is not blood of mine and owes me nothing." Her voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about Marisol when she was truly frightened; she got quieter, more precise, a woman measuring out her words like the last of the flour. "So I will say the unfair thing plainly, because plainness is all I have left. Someone signed papers saying we exist somewhere we don't. Someone carried those papers across a border. Someone knows our names and our faces and the shape of our route, and that someone sold us, and the marshal's men have been asking in villages we passed through six months after we passed through them. That trail didn't follow us, Ines. It was handed forward. And the newest person who knows we are here — the only person outside this room who knows — is a government man with a ledger, sitting in your kitchen, drinking your tea."

* * *

Tomás woke the argument at dusk by existing, which was mostly how children involved themselves in such matters. He came padding back from the privy run — Ines timed his outings to the change of watch, when the tower's rotation gave her a legitimate reason to be at the door — and stood in the middle of the cellar room radiating the specific restlessness of a nine-year-old who has been indoors for eleven days.

"Aunt Ines. If we go on the ferry—"

"If," Marisol said sharply.

"—if we go," Tomás continued with the immovable logic of his age, "will I see the school? On the free side. Mateo's cousin says there's a school with a bell, a real bell, and everyone goes, even farm children, even in winter." He had brought his slate with him, apparently for reference, as though the arithmetic might come up at the border. "Will I be counted there? On the paper? With my name spelled right? Mateo says on the free side they spell your name however you say it, not however they feel like writing it."

Ines knelt to his level. Up here in the world above, the storm season was assembling itself over the lake — she could smell it, that green-black bruise on the horizon — but down here there was only lamplight and a boy holding a slate like a ticket.

"Yes," she said. "You'll be counted. All of you. Properly."

"Then I want to wear my good shirt," Tomás announced, with the gravity of a decree, and went to dig it out of the bundle, and the adults were left alone again in the wreckage of the moment, because it is very difficult to discuss thirty dollars of betrayal in front of a child planning his school bell.

Rafael cleared his throat. "Marisol. Mija. The woman has kept us alive through a winter in a hole I dug myself. Perhaps we let her decide whom she trusts."

"Perhaps," Marisol said, "we ask her to prove it." She turned to Ines, and Ines saw that beneath the hardness her eyes were red-rimmed and exhausted, and that she was not accusing because she believed the worst, but because believing the worst was the only armor she had been able to find that fit. "Ask him, then. Your thorough man. Ask him why the marshal's men reached Millbrook before we did. Ask him what his ledger says about us. And if he answers clean — I'll pack tonight. I'll carry Tomás myself. But I will not put my son on that boat not knowing whether the hand that waves us off is the same hand that pointed."

"I can't ask him," Ines said. "Asking is confessing."

"Then we are already caught," Marisol said simply, "and everything after this is just choosing how to drown."

* * *

Ines climbed the tower afterward because the tower was where she went when the ground stopped holding still. One hundred and eight steps. She had counted them the first week after Elena died — no. Not Elena. She corrected herself the way she always did, with the small mechanical grief of a joint that ached in rain: after *Tomás-senior* died, her own Tomás named for a boy who'd drowned at fourteen in the channel they were now fleeing. Grief in families was an heirloom; it came down with the china.

At the top, the lantern room held its steady turning, and below, the world had gone to iron. The river wore its ice like a lid on a pot with something boiling underneath. Far out, where the channel ran deepest, the dark water showed through in long black veins, and the wind coming off the lake had that wet, mineral smell that meant the storm was closer than yesterday, closer than the almanac promised, early the way everything this year had been early.

She leaned on the rail and let herself think the disloyal thought she'd been refusing since the tea.

*He noticed.* Of course he noticed. August Reyes had the look of a man who had survived childhood by counting exits; such men did not miss beds or names. And yet — and here was the thing she kept circling — he had drunk the tea and thanked her for it and left, twice, and said nothing, done nothing, reported nothing she could see. A man intending betrayal would have been warmer. Wouldn't he? Or colder? She couldn't remember anymore which direction treachery dressed from.

Her father-in-law used to say you could trust any man as far as you could throw his debts. She didn't know August Reyes's debts. She knew his chapped hands, his careful penmanship, the way he'd hesitated half a heartbeat at the word *household* — and she knew, suddenly and completely, standing in the rotating light like a moth pinned to its own orbit, that Marisol was right about one thing regardless of the answer: the not-knowing would kill them faster than the knowing. A household could survive a traitor. It could not survive suspicion eating it from inside the sanctuary walls.

The light swept out, once, twice, and in its flash she saw the mail boat's mooring down at the landing, still tied up — the storm had stranded him, then. He was here. Tonight. Under her roof, in her guest bed, the extra bed, the bed too many.

Below her, a figure came out of the keeper's cottage into the wind, coatless, moving with decision toward the tower stairs. She watched him cross the yard in the strobing light — appearing, disappearing, appearing — a man walking through frames of himself, and by the set of his shoulders she could tell he had come to say something he had been carrying for days, something heavy, something that had kept him pacing a strange bedroom while the river talked under the floor.

Ines put her hand on the lantern housing, felt its warmth, and thought: *Well. Here is the asking, come to find me on its own.*

The tower door banged open below, and the wind came up the stairwell carrying his voice ahead of him.

"Mrs. Sandoval! Mrs. Sandoval, I need to speak with you — and you'll want to hear it before the storm does."
