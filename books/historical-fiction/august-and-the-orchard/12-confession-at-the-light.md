## 12. Confession at the Light

August Reyes had faced down supervisors, surly ferry captains, and one memorable mule on the Kettle Ridge road, but he had never once in his life climbed anything he didn't have to. Stairs were for people with something to prove. And yet here he was at midnight, standing at the foot of the lighthouse tower like a penitent outside a church he wasn't sure would let him in.

The storm had passed through in the afternoon and left behind a sky scrubbed clean and viciously cold. The stars out here were nothing like the stars over the capital — these had edges, points, an air of keeping accounts. Fitting, he thought. Even the heavens at Point Alder were doing arithmetic.

He had told himself he was coming up to check the log entries against the station records again. He had told himself that lie twice on the walk from the cottage, three times climbing the outside stair, and it had worn thin as cheap paper by the time he reached the lantern room door.

Ines Sandoval was already there.

Of course she was. She seemed to live half her life in this glass room at the top of the world, polishing brass that never stayed polished, trimming wicks, watching the river do its slow dark work below. She sat on the low iron bench beneath the lamp assembly with a sock of knitting abandoned in her lap — knitting, at midnight, as if fear were something a person could work off stitch by stitch — and when he came through the door she looked up with no surprise whatsoever, which somehow unsettled him more than shouting would have.

"Keeper," he said.

"Census man." She nodded at the stool by the instrument cabinet. "Sit, if you're staying. The floor's cold enough to read by."

"I won't stay long."

"Everyone says that." She picked up the knitting, considered it, put it down again. "You've been circling my tower for two days like a gull that smells fish on me. Say what you came to say, Mr. Reyes. It's cold, and I have bread to prove in the morning."

August sat. His hands wanted a ledger to hold, a pen, something official between him and this woman. He had none of those things, so he simply spoke.

"Your husband's brother," he said. "Rafael Sandoval. The station log from last winter lists four residents. You, Elias, Rafael, and Rafael's wife. This spring the log lists one. I've asked myself all season where three people go in the middle of winter, and I've stopped asking myself, because I know."

* * *

Ines did not move. That was the first thing. A lesser liar would have flinched, reached for some small adjustment — a breath, a blink, a shift of weight. Ines went still the way water goes still before ice, and August understood suddenly that she had known this conversation was coming since the day he stepped off the mail boat with his ledger under his arm.

"Have you," she said.

"The audit comes Thursday," he said. "Inspector Voss. He doesn't drink tea, he doesn't chat, and he counts beds the way a butcher counts hooks. If he finds what I found—"

"What did you find?" Her voice was level. "Say it plainly. I've earned plain speech."

"A family in your cellar," August said. "A warrant with names on it, and a bounty under the names. And a woman who has kept the greatest light on this stretch of river burning every single night while hiding three souls under her floorboards, which is either the most criminal thing or the most lawful thing I've ever heard of, and I haven't decided which."

The lamp turned above them with a low, patient whir. Its shadow swept the glass, swept the river, came round again. Ines watched it pass like a woman timing something.

"My husband drowned in April," she said. "You may know that from the records you've been so thorough with. What the records don't say is that he drowned going after Rafael's boat when it swamped in the spring thaw — went into water that had killed men hardier than him, because Rafael was family and family doesn't get weighed against safety. Elias promised Rafael, years ago, crossing over themselves with nothing but the shirts they wore: *no one of ours gets sent back.* I married into that promise. When Elias drowned, the promise didn't drown with him. It moved in with me. It sleeps in my cellar right now."

"How old is the boy?"

That landed. For the first time her stillness cracked — not much, just a hairline, the way good porcelain cracks.

"Seven," she said.

August nodded slowly. He had a picture in his head now that he couldn't unhave: Tomás at the frozen window, small hands cupped against the glass. He'd seen the boy once, only once, two days ago, a face at a frosted pane that vanished when he turned his head. He had spent the intervening days telling himself he'd imagined it.

"And Marisol," Ines said. "Rafael's wife. She mends nets better than any man on the river, and she sings when she thinks no one's listening, and she is terrified all day every day, and she does not complain. That's who your inspector wants to put on a boat south."

"Ines." He used her name without meaning to. It felt like stepping off a dock. "I didn't come up here to be persuaded. I came up here to tell you what's coming, because—" He stopped. Started again. "Because there's something else. Something I found out three days ago, in a letter from my mother."

The wind moved around the lantern glass. Downriver, ice groaned — a long, deep sound, like a giant shifting in sleep.

"My mother crossed this river twenty-six years ago," he said. "Cholera year. She came with nothing, sick, with a baby in her arms that the ferryman wouldn't take and the villagers wouldn't shelter. Except one household did. A woman on this side hid them in her keeper's cottage through the whole fevered winter, fed them off her own stores, and never told a soul, not even the priest. My mother never learned her name. She called her *the light lady* until the day she died believing she owed her life to a stranger."

Ines had gone very quiet. Not the careful stillness from before — a different quality altogether.

"That was my grandmother's cottage," August said. "Before my mother ever married, before me. She kept the light here eleven years. And I have spent three days doing sums, Mrs. Sandoval, and every sum comes out the same. The woman who saved my mother is either you or your ghost of a mother-in-law, and either way, I am standing in a debt I didn't know I owed, holding papers that could ruin you."

"You could be lying," Ines said softly.

"Yes."

"You're very good at asking questions. Liars usually are."

It stung because it was true, and because he'd thought the same about her. Two careful people, each certain the other was fluent in exactly their own language.

"So we're agreed," he said. "Neither of us trusts the other as far as a ledger can be thrown."

"Farther," said Ines. "I've thrown a ledger. They don't fly well."

Despite everything, something in August's chest loosened half a notch. It was the first joke anyone had made in his vicinity in weeks, and it came from a woman with everything to lose, at midnight, in a tower.

* * *

They talked for two hours.

Not the way officials and subjects talk — not across a table, not with forms. They talked leaning against opposite sides of the lantern room, the great lamp wheeling between them every eight seconds, throwing its shadow over each in turn like a slow metronome keeping time for their argument.

August laid out the machinery of the audit: Voss arrives Thursday by marshal's sled, checks the roster against the physical station, interviews the keeper, files his report within the week. The bounty on informants — forty dollars a head, sixty for a man with a warrant outstanding — meant half the village would sell their own shadows if the price held. Rosa Ferrer had already made inquiries at the post office; August had seen her face change when she thought no one was looking, the way faces change when they're counting.

Ines laid out her side: the ferryman Dolan held three berths north before the ice broke — three, and no more, and payment in full, and the window closing with every cold night. Four souls in her cellar. Three berths.

"Do the sums yourself," she said, and there was something raw under the steadiness now. "I've done them every night since Dolan named his price. Every arrangement leaves someone behind. Every arrangement breaks somebody's heart, including mine, and I keep arriving back at the same ugly truth, which is that I cannot save them all and I cannot choose, and so I do nothing, and doing nothing is also a choice, the worst one."

"So don't choose tonight," August said.

She looked at him.

"Here's what I can do." He kept his voice low, as though the lamp might repeat him. "Voss expects a preliminary report from me — the census verification, filed ahead of his arrival. I control when it's filed. I can lose it. Misdirect the mail sled, blame the weather, blame the roads — the weather will even cooperate, there's another blow coming Wednesday. That buys you five days, maybe six. Enough to make terms with Dolan, enough for one more attempt at a fourth berth, enough to decide — properly, awake, with everyone in the room — instead of at gunpoint."

"And when the delay runs out?"

"Then it runs out." He spread his hands. "I'm not a rescuer, Ines. I'm a man with a stamp and a filing deadline and a talent for finding people who don't want to be found. All I'm offering is time. Time is the only currency I actually hold."

"Why?" It came out flat, almost harsh. "Give me the true reason. Not the debt. Debts are clean. What's the dirty reason?"

August laughed — a short, surprised sound that fogged the cold glass.

"The dirty reason," he said, "is that I have spent nine years counting people for the government, and in nine years I have never once counted anybody I could look in the eye afterward. I write down names and the names become numbers and the numbers become quotas and somewhere downriver the numbers become boats turned away and families split at docks, and I never see it. It's very tidy. I'm very good at tidy." He looked out at the black river. "Then I come here, and I find a bed too many, and for the first time the number has a face at a window, and it turns out I can't stand it. That's the dirty reason. I'd rather be useful than accurate."

Ines studied him for a long moment, the lamp sweeping its shadow across them both.

"All right," she said. "Here are my terms, and they are not generous. You delay the audit — genuinely delay it, not a paper trick that collapses in a day. In exchange, I decide who sails. Me. Not the family, not the ferryman, not fate. If someone must be left on this bank, I will name them, and I will carry it, and no one else in that cellar carries a grain of it. That's my price for trusting you, Mr. Reyes. You get the government's enemy a little time; I take the weight that would crush her otherwise."

"And if I'm lying? If this is all a long trap?"

"Then I'll have made a fool of myself at the top of my own lighthouse, and I'll add it to the list of things the river takes." She extended her hand — work-rough, steady, chapped at the knuckles. "But I don't think you're lying. Liars ask questions to catch you. You ask questions like a man hoping to be proven wrong."

He took her hand. Her grip was a keeper's grip; it had hauled rope and pumps and drowning husbands' hopes, and it closed around his fingers like a verdict.

"One more term," August said, before he could stop himself. "When you make your decision — the berths. Let me be in the room. Not to vote. To witness. Somebody should see what this costs, and write it down honestly, for once."

Above them the great lamp wheeled on, patient, indifferent, throwing its light out over the broken ice and the far shore and the small stubborn cottage at its base where four hidden souls slept the sleep of people running out of night.

Downriver, the ice boomed again — longer this time, louder, a crack running somewhere out in the dark like the first syllable of a sentence the river had begun.

Ines's hand tightened on his.

"That," she said quietly, "was early."
