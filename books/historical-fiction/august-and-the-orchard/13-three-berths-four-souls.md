## 13. Three Berths, Four Souls

The arithmetic of the thing was simple, and that was what made it cruel.

Three berths on the *Marta Lee* before the ice broke. Four souls in my cellar room. I had done the sums a hundred times since leaving the ferryman's dock, and every time the numbers came out the same way, the way numbers do when they have decided against you. Two berths paid in full. A third promised if I could find twelve more dollars by Friday. And four people downstairs who needed to be on that boat — no, who needed to be anywhere but here, and the boat was simply the best of the anywheres.

I stood at the cellar door with my hand on the latch longer than a sensible woman would have, listening to the sounds of the room below. Rafael was whittling something; I could hear the small dry whisper of the knife. Marisol was mending, which she did the way other people prayed, steadily and without expectation of an answer. And Tomás was asking questions, as he always was now that winter had shut him indoors.

"—but how does the light know where the ships are?"

"The light doesn't know anything, mi corazón. It only shines."

"Then how do the ships know where *it* is?"

"That," said Rafael, "is the sort of question that gets boys sent to bed early."

I opened the door and carried down the smell of the upper world with me: woodsmoke, salt, and the particular cold that lives in a keeper's coat no matter how long she hangs it by the stove. The cellar room had been a root storage once, before I'd spent three weeks of October making it into something a family could breathe in. Rafael had built the false wall himself, back when he still laughed easily. Now he sat on the edge of the bunk and looked up at me, and I saw at once that he already knew why I'd come. Husbands and brothers-in-law who live in hiding develop a sense for it, like dogs knowing weather.

"Well?" he said. "How many?"

"Three."

* * *

The argument began quietly, the way most bad things do.

"Then it's settled," Marisol said, not looking up from her mending. She set a stitch, drew the thread tight, set another. "Tomás goes."

Nobody had settled anything, but I had learned that Marisol liked to begin conclusions as though they were facts, so that by the time anyone objected, the objection sounded like quarreling with the weather.

"Tomás goes," Rafael agreed, and there was a finality in his voice that made my chest hurt, because it was the voice of a man rehearsing a goodbye.

"No," said August Reyes from the corner.

Now, you must understand something about August Reyes. He had been living in my guest room upstairs for six days by then — stranded, officially, by the storm season and the ferry schedule, and unofficially by an arrangement between us so delicate I was afraid to look at it directly, lest it dissolve like sugar in rain. He was a census taker for the United States government, which should have made him the most dangerous man on this coast, and instead he sat in my cellar holding Tomás on his knee some evenings and teaching him card games of dubious moral character. He was a careful man. Careful men make excellent arguers, because they have usually thought through their position twice before opening their mouths.

He opened his mouth.

"Three berths, four people. You're arguing over who stays behind when nobody has to stay behind. You could all go overland."

The needle stopped. Marisol looked up.

"Overland," she repeated, in the tone one uses for a word that has personally offended the family.

"North through the pass, then east along the logging roads. There's a settlement past the ridge — half the people there came the same way your family did. No papers asked, no papers given. I've been mapping it." He produced, from inside his coat, a piece of paper folded into eighths, and unfolded it with the ceremony of a magician producing a dove. "Six days' walk. Hard walking, but the ice will hold for weeks yet."

I took the map and held it near the lamp. It was drawn in the tidy hand of a man who counted things for a living — distances marked, water sources circled, notes in the margins in two languages. *Old burn — shelter in rock overhang.* *Creek likely frozen — carry water anyway.*

"When did you make this?" I asked.

"Tuesday."

"You've been here since Saturday."

"I work quickly when I'm motivated."

Marisol put down her mending entirely, which I had seen her do perhaps three times in the months she'd lived beneath my floor, and each time meant trouble.

"Señor Reyes. August. You are a kind man, and I am going to say this gently, which is more than I feel." She folded her hands. "You are proposing that my husband, who has a warrant with his name on it and a description good enough that the marshal's own deputy nodded when he read it, walk six days through open country with a child and a woman who has never in her life walked further than the market and back. In winter."

"I've walked it," said August.

"In summer."

"...In late summer."

"And when the marshal comes here — and he will come, because someone in that village counts our shadows through the windows, I can feel it like a toothache — and finds the lighthouse empty, what happens? Does he say 'ah, they've gone north walking, good luck to them'? Or does he send men to the pass? Men with dogs? Men who ride, while we walk?" Her voice stayed level but rose in temperature, like water approaching a boil. "A boat arrives, a boat leaves, and the sea forgets. The land remembers everything."

"The land also hides everything," August said. "That's rather its nature."

"My son," Marisol said, with terrible precision, "is seven years old. He has never slept outdoors. When we crossed the border he cried for two nights because the bed smelled of someone else's soap. You want to put him on a mountain."

"I want to keep him off a list," August said, and the gentleness in it took the wind out of her sails so completely that for a moment she just stared at him.

And I — I confess it — I wanted to applaud. Because I had been doing the same arithmetic as everyone else, three minus one, choosing a victim in the privacy of my own skull, and here was this government man with his cold hands and his ledger-heart suggesting the problem might simply be refused.

But Rafael was shaking his head.

"It's a good map," he said slowly. "It's maybe even a good plan. For us." He touched his own chest. "For me and Marisol. But Tomás—" His voice caught on the name the way a sleeve catches on a nail. "Tomás is the whole point. Everything else is decoration."

* * *

Here is what I learned about arguments among people who love each other: they sound like arguments, but they are actually negotiations with grief.

Rafael spoke next, and he did it standing, which was difficult in the low-ceilinged room, so that he stooped like a man bowing to a king.

"If we scatter overland and something goes wrong — and Marisol is right, the land remembers, and so do men on horseback — then I lose all of you at once. If Tomás takes the boat, then whatever else happens, whatever else we lose, there is one of us free. Safe. Growing up somewhere the word 'warrant' means nothing worse than a soldier's uniform." He looked at his sister-in-law — at me — and I made myself meet his eyes, because he deserved that much. "Ines. Tell them. You're the one who knows the water."

Everyone turned to me. Even Tomás, who had been pretending to sleep on his bunk with all the convincing theatricality of a cat pretending not to care about fish, cracked one eye.

"The water," I said, "does not care who rides it. That's the truth and the mercy of boats. The *Marta Lee* leaves, and by morning the river has forgotten every name aboard." I set down the map. "But I won't pretend the choice is mine to make, because it isn't. It's yours. All I can tell you is the cost of each road."

"Then tell us the cost of staying," Marisol said quietly. "Because that's what we're really arguing about, isn't it? Not boat versus mountain. Boat versus *here.*"

The lamp flame bent in some draft from the stairs, and the shadows in the little room leaned and righted themselves like a congregation.

"The cost of staying," I said, "is that we count the days until the marshal comes instead of living them. I've kept a light for eleven years. I know what waiting looks like. It looks like this room looks — tidy, warm, and dug out of the ground."

Silence. Somewhere above us the wind found the gallery rail and ran along it, making its low mournful note, the one visitors always called eerie and I had long ago stopped hearing.

Tomás gave up on pretending. He sat up, hair wild, and looked around at the four grim adult faces with the frank outrage of childhood.

"Why can't everybody go on the boat?"

"Only three places, mijo," Marisol said.

"Then somebody sits on somebody's lap. Boats have laps. The mail boat man let Mr. Reyes sit on the crates."

August smiled despite himself. "Those were federal crates. Very spacious."

"Why don't we ask the boat man for a fourth place?"

"We did, mi corazón. There isn't one."

"There's always a fourth place," Tomás said, with the absolute conviction of a boy whose experience of the world included a mother who could produce food from empty cupboards and a lighthouse aunt who could produce light out of fire. "You just have to ask better."

And out of the mouths of babes, etcetera — except the child was wrong in a way that mattered, wrong about ferries and ferriers and the price of coal oil, and watching Marisol absorb that wrongness without correcting it, without letting one grain of the world's real arithmetic fall on him yet — that was the hardest thing I had witnessed in a winter of hard witnesses.

Rafael cleared his throat.

"Then it's decided. Tomás takes the first berth—"

"The second," I said. "The first is mine."

Every head turned again. Even August's, and August rarely looked surprised, having the kind of face that filed surprises away for later review.

"You paid for two," Rafael said carefully. "Yours and—"

"Mine and my husband's. And my husband is in the river, and the river doesn't need a berth." I heard my own voice go flat and practical, the keeper's voice, the one I used for storms. "I'm not using it. Tomás is. That's the first berth settled and none of you may argue with me, because I own the lighthouse and I'll turn the light off if you do."

"You wouldn't," said Tomás, horrified.

"Try me, small one."

That got the laugh I'd aimed for — a small one, startled out of them, exactly the size a laugh has to be in a cellar to avoid waking into a sob. Marisol reached over and squeezed my hand hard enough to grind bone, which is how she says thank you.

"One berth left," August said softly. "Two adults. And a map."

"The map is fantasy," Marisol said, but her voice had lost its certainty; she was staring at the folded paper now the way a starving woman stares at a window with bread behind it.

"The map is six days," August said. "The warrant is forever. I know which one I'd choose."

"You," said Marisol, "are a single man with good boots and government legs."

"I'm a man whose mother crossed a border in a cholera winter with less than you'd carry in that bag," August said. "She told me once that the difference between the ones who make it and the ones who don't is rarely courage. It's usually whether somebody drew them a map." He tapped the paper. "Somebody drew this one. It seems ungrateful to waste it."

* * *

We talked until the lamp burned low. We talked in circles and spirals, we talked ourselves hoarse, we talked the way families do — which is to say, we said the same four things over and over in different orders until they wore smooth as river stones. Rafael argued the boat. August argued the pass. Marisol argued both sides alternately depending on which man was winning, and I argued mostly that everyone should drink the tea before it went cold, which no one did, so I drank four cups myself out of spite.

By the end, nothing was decided, which I understood was itself a decision — the decision to let the night pass before letting the future arrive. Rafael would think until morning. Marisol would pray, though she claimed she wouldn't. Tomás genuinely fell asleep this time, mid-argument, his head pillowed on August's coat-sleeve, and the sight of it — the government's man sitting perfectly still so as not to wake a boy with no legal existence — did more to settle my heart than all the tea in the territory.

I climbed the stairs last and stood a moment in the dark of the kitchen, listening to the house settle. Through the window, the channel lay black and glittering under a high cold moon, and far out, where the river widened toward the sea, I could see the pale seam of the ice field, holding, holding, holding — for now.

August came up quietly behind me, coat over his arm, bound for the guest room.

"They'll take the map," he said. "Give them two days."

"And if they don't?"

"Then Tomás sails, and we spend the rest of the winter being very brave about it." He paused at the foot of the stairs. "Mrs. Sandoval. Ines. If it comes to the pass — I'll walk with them. As far as the ridge, at least. A guide makes all the difference."

"You'd miss the audit."

"I'd miss the audit," he agreed, and there was something in the way he said it that told me the man had already grieved for his career once, quietly, alone, and come out the other side still standing.

He went up. I banked the stove and checked the clockwork that turned the light, all its small brass teeth marching faithfully round, and I remember thinking, with the strange clarity that visits women at midnight: *we have beaten it. We have found the seam in the wall and slipped through. Two days and it begins.*

I was wrong, of course. It had already begun.

Because down in the village that very night, under a different lamp, a different pair of hands was counting something too — not souls, but dollars — and writing a letter that would reach the marshal's office before my two days were half spent.
