## 14. The Informant

Rosa Quintero had three great talents in this life, and she would have told you so herself if anyone had asked, which no one ever did. The first was baking bread so good that the Methodist minister once forgot his own sermon mid-Sunday service thinking about it. The second was knowing everything worth knowing in Point Alder within a day of it happening, and most things within an hour. The third was arithmetic.

It was the third talent that got her into trouble, because arithmetic was the talent that never let her sleep.

She sat at her kitchen table on a gray Tuesday in late February with a cup of coffee gone cold beside her and a pencil stub in her hand, working sums on the back of her flour bill. The stove ticked. The wind worried at the shutters. And the numbers, as they always did, came out wrong — wrong meaning not incorrect, for Rosa's figures were never incorrect, but wrong meaning unbearable.

Widow's pension: four dollars a month. Egg money: perhaps two more, in a good month, and the hens had been sulking all winter like little feathered creditors. Bread sales to the village: three dollars, when Reverend Ellicott was feeling pious and the miller's wife was feeling generous, which coincided more often than either of them would care to admit. Against this: flour, coal, lamp oil, the mending of the roof she could no longer put off, and a dentist in Port Hanover who wanted eight dollars to deal with the molar on her left side, which had developed opinions and shared them freely, especially at night.

Fourteen dollars short by spring, and that was assuming nothing went catastrophically wrong, and something always went catastrophically wrong. Rosa was sixty-one years old and had buried a husband and two infants and one fully grown fool of a brother, and she had learned that the universe regarded her budgets as suggestions.

The pencil stub hovered over the paper.

There was another figure she had not written down. She had heard it from Tilde Hansen, who heard it from her husband, who heard it from a man passing through on the north road: the marshal's office in Port Hanover was paying five dollars for information leading to the apprehension of persons hiding fugitives from warrants. Five dollars per person. And there was talk — only talk, but Rosa's ears were better than her oven — of a bonus for information leading to a whole household, a family taken together, tidy as eggs in a basket.

Twenty dollars. Perhaps twenty-five.

Rosa put the pencil down and looked at it as though it had bitten her.

"No," she said aloud, to the kitchen, to the cold coffee, to herself. "Absolutely not."

The kitchen, being a kitchen, said nothing back, but the molar throbbed its agreement with the other side of the argument, and Rosa pushed the flour bill away and got up to knead bread she did not need to knead yet, because her hands thought better when they were busy and her hands were, at present, arguing with her heart.

* * *

Here is the thing about Rosa Quintero that nobody in Point Alder quite understood, least of all Rosa: she did not consider herself a gossip. A *keeper of accounts*, rather. In a village of ninety souls balanced on a spit of rock and river-clay between the water and the timber, somebody had to keep track, and Rosa had been keeping track since she was a girl counting the boats out for the herring run. She knew that the Larssons' marriage had gone quiet in October and loud again by Christmas. She knew that Reverend Ellicott watered his communion wine and that God, being merciful, had chosen not to notice. She knew which farmers' daughters were courting which mill workers and what each young man earned in a season and what percentage of it went to drink.

And she knew — because knowing was her gift and her affliction — that things at the Point Alder light station did not add up.

She had been noticing since before Christmas, in the small way one notices a picture hanging crooked. The supply sled from Hargrove's store went out to the lighthouse twice as often as one woman could justify. Mrs. Sandoval ordered flour by the fifty-pound sack now, when for years she'd bought twenty-five like any reasonable widow. There were mitten-sized handprints wiped on the fogged window of the cottage's south side, low ones, child-height, seen on the day Rosa delivered eggs and was kept standing on the step ten minutes longer than necessary while Ines talked very fast and very brightly about the weather.

And last week — this was the item Rosa turned over most — she had seen smoke from the cottage chimney at an hour when Ines Sandoval was visibly up in the lantern room, a black speck against the gray sky, tending the light. Two fires burning in a house where one woman lived alone. Unless, of course, she did not live alone.

Extra shadows, Rosa's mind supplied, and she hated the phrase even as it arrived, because it sounded like something out of one of the dreadful serials Tilde Hansen read, all gaslight and villainy, and because it fit.

She had done nothing about it for six days. She had baked her bread, sold her eggs, endured the molar, and told herself firmly that whatever Ines Sandoval was doing under her own roof was Ines Sandoval's business, and that Rosa Quintero was not a dog to go sniffing at other people's doors. This resolution held beautifully right up until Tuesday morning, when Tilde Hansen came in for a loaf and stayed for an hour and mentioned, in the offhand voice she used for things she wanted repeated, that the marshal himself was expected in Point Alder before the ice broke, making his rounds, asking questions, and that he paid cash.

Cash. Before the ferry berth closed and the river locked itself up white until April.

* * *

That afternoon Rosa walked to the edge of the village on the pretense of checking her muskrat traps, which was a lie she told so rarely that it embarrassed her the whole way, and stood on the rise above the eastern shore where, on a clear day, you could see the lighthouse standing at the end of the world like a candle on a mantel. It was a clear day. The tower was sharp against the pale sky, the keeper's cottage hunched beside it, smoke rising thin and steady from the chimney.

One woman, she told herself. One woman who buried her husband in the fall and took in — what? Who? Why?

Everyone in the village knew the shape of the story, if not its particulars: the drowned husband, the drowned man's brother, the brother's wife and boy come across the border in the autumn ahead of some trouble or other, staying just long enough to bury the brother too, and then — nothing. Gone, everyone assumed. Moved inland, moved north, moved wherever people without papers move, which is away. That had been the comfortable assumption, and Rosa had shared it, and shared also the small warm glow of pity that villages extend to tragedies they don't have to do anything about.

But flour by the fifty-pound sack. Mittens drying on the south sill — she had seen mittens, she was nearly sure, small ones. And Ines Sandoval walking to church alone with her chin up and her eyes everywhere, counting exits the way Rosa counted dollars.

"They're just people," Rosa said to the muskrat marsh, which received the information without comment. "Cold, hungry, frightened people. Same as anyone."

This was true, and it changed nothing, because the trouble with being poor was not that it made you cruel. Rosa had known rich cruel people and poor kind ones and considered cruelty largely independent of income. The trouble with being poor was that it made every decision have a price tag, and then it handed you the pencil.

Five dollars a head. Four heads, if the shadows were four. Twenty dollars. Twenty dollars was the roof. Twenty dollars was the dentist and the coal and a month of not lying awake listening to her own accounts settle over her like snow.

"Same as anyone," she said again, but quieter, and the marsh, which had seen sixty years of Rosa Quintero's arguments with herself, offered no verdict either way.

* * *

She wrote the letter that night. She wrote it three times.

The first version was honest and horrible — *I have reason to believe* — and she burned it in the stove before the ink was dry, hands shaking, disgusted with herself. The second version was so vague it would have convinced nobody of anything, including possibly the existence of Point Alder, and she burned that one too, though more slowly, with regret, because vagueness had a certain safety to it.

The third version was the one she finally folded into an envelope, and it was neither honest nor vague but something in between, the way most sins are: *You will wish to know that the light station at Point Alder draws supplies beyond the needs of one keeper. Extra shadows at the lighthouse, my late husband would have said, God rest him. Come before the ice breaks. R.Q.*

She addressed it to the marshal's office in Port Hanover and did not sign her full name, which fooled no one — the marshal's office knew handwriting the way she knew bread, and half the village bought their stamps from the same drawer — but felt like a fig leaf, and fig leaves had a long and distinguished history.

Then she sat with the envelope under the lamp for two hours.

She thought about Ines Sandoval's face in church, composed and proud and tired underneath, the way a plank is tired underneath its paint. She thought about the boy — because she had become certain there was a boy; the mittens settled it, and once, faintly, through the fog on the lane, laughter, high and quick, gone before she could be sure. She thought about her own babies, thirty-some years in the churchyard, and how the world had made no exceptions for them and owed nobody anything, least of all Rosa Quintero.

She thought about the molar.

And here is what must be understood about Rosa, what she would spend the rest of her life trying to explain to a silence that never answered: she believed, truly believed, in the moment she sealed the envelope, that she was doing a small and forgivable thing. Not kindness — she was too honest with herself for that, mostly — but not wickedness either. A transaction. Information for coin, the oldest trade there was, older than bread. The law was the law; the warrants were real; the family would be sent back across the line and be someone else's parish problem, alive, fed, un-drowned. No one would hang for it. No one would starve. And Rosa would keep her roof, and pull her tooth, and stop doing sums at midnight.

That was the story she told herself, and she told it well, because she was, whatever else, an excellent storyteller, and every excellent storyteller knows that the best lies are the ones you need.

She mailed it Wednesday morning at Hargrove's store, dropping the envelope into the mail sack with a flick of the wrist she practiced on the walk over, casual as tossing scratch to hens. Tilde Hansen was in the store buying kerosene and gave her a look.

"You're up early, Rosa Quintero."

"I'm always up early."

"You've got a look about you."

"I've got a toothache about me, is what I've got." And that, at least, was the truth, and she said it with such feeling that Tilde clucked sympathetically and recommended a poultice of onion and goose fat, which Rosa accepted with grace and intended to apply to absolutely nothing.

Walking home, the deed done and irrevocable somewhere in the bottom of a mail sack, she found she felt — lighter. That was the shameful part, the part she would never tell anyone. Not triumphant, exactly. Lighter. As though she had set down a weight she'd been carrying, and the fact that the weight had been knowledge and she had simply handed it to someone else did not, somehow, make her shoulders less free.

"The Lord forgive me," she said, to the lane, to the gulls, to nobody.

The gulls, having no stake in the matter, screamed cheerfully and stole a fish from Ole Larsen's dock.

* * *

By Friday, Rosa had almost stopped flinching every time a hoofbeat sounded on the village road. By Saturday she had rationalized the matter into a shape she could live inside: the marshal would come or he wouldn't; the family would be found or warned or gone over the ice on their own; Ines Sandoval was a capable woman, a clever woman, a woman who kept a lighthouse alone through winter, and if anyone could outrun a piece of paper, it was her. Rosa baked. She sold four loaves to the mill crew, which was unprecedented in February, and told herself the market was simply improving.

On Sunday she went to church, because whatever else she was, she was regular, and sat in her usual pew third from the back where she could see the whole congregation and be seen by none of them, which was her preferred arrangement with God and her neighbors alike. Ines Sandoval sat near the front, straight-backed, singing the hymns in a clear low voice. Once, during the second hymn, the woman glanced back over her shoulder — a quick sweep, the sweep of a lighthouse lamp itself, round and returning — and Rosa felt the look pass over her like a searchlight over a boat, and ducked her head into the hymnal, and sang louder than she had in years, and was ashamed of the loudness without knowing why.

After the service, in the yard, wrapped in wool and shivering, the congregation performed its ancient ritual of saying nothing at great length. Reverend Ellicott shook hands. Tilde Hansen reported that the ice on the river was talking at night now, booming and groaning like an old man getting out of bed, which meant it would break early this year, maybe two weeks, maybe less.

"And the marshal's coming on the last sled before that," Tilde said, to no one, to everyone. "Tilde says—" she corrected herself, "Hansen heard it from the depot man. Making his rounds. Asking after census matters and such. He'll lodge at the Hansen place, two nights, maybe three."

"Two nights, maybe three," Rosa repeated, because repeating things was her role in these rituals, and her voice came out strange, and Tilde looked at her with mild curiosity and moved on to discuss whether the Hendersons would name the new baby after the grandmother or the cow.

Rosa stood in the churchyard with the cold biting through her boots and did the sum one last time, because sums were all she had. Two nights, maybe three, before the ice broke and the ferry ran north for the final time and the river closed its white door until spring. The letter would reach Port Hanover Monday. The marshal would come Thursday, perhaps Friday. Which left — her fingers moved inside her mittens, counting days — which left very little room indeed for anybody, guilty or innocent, to get across that ice.

She had meant it as a transaction. Small, forgivable, survivable. But standing there in the churchyard with the river muttering out beyond the point, Rosa Quintero felt, for the first time, the full weight of what she had set loose, and understood with terrible clarity that she had not sold information at all.

She had lit a signal fire. And signals, once lit, drew whatever was nearest — marshal or storm, judgment or mercy — and no arithmetic on earth could say which.

She walked home fast, past her own gate, past the muskrat marsh, all the way to Hargrove's store, and stood a long moment looking at the mail sack by the door, already stitched and waiting for the Monday boat.

"Too late, Rosa," said Hargrove, watching her from behind the counter with the mild interest of a man who missed nothing and judged less. "Boat comes at dawn."

"Of course," said Rosa. "I only forgot my tobacco."

She hadn't. She walked home in the darkening afternoon with the wind rising off the water and the first fat flakes of snow coming down fat and slow and wrong for February, wrong for this late, and far out on the river the ice boomed like a struck drum, once, twice, and did not stop.
