## 15. Whiteout

The storm did not arrive so much as it lost its temper on the way.

Ines had been watching the western sky since morning, the way a woman watches a mother-in-law's carriage come up the lane — knowing exactly what was coming and knowing there was no polite way to stop it. The clouds had stacked themselves into gray shelves all afternoon, and the gulls had gone quiet, and the river, which had been groaning about the cold for a week now, went suddenly, eerily still. Old keepers called it the held breath. Her husband Tomás the elder — no, that was wrong now; her husband had been Mateo, and Tomás was the boy sleeping in her cellar, and grief had a way of scrambling names like that, mixing them in a drawer until you pulled out the wrong one at the worst moment — Mateo used to say the river held its breath before it shouted.

He had been wrong about a great many things, her Mateo, but he had been right about rivers.

By four o'clock the wind arrived, and by five it had brought friends.

She was in the kitchen measuring out flour when the first real gust struck the tower, and the whole structure leaned into it like a tired man into a strong arm. The lamp above shivered in its housing. Below her feet, through the floorboards, came the muffled sounds of the cellar room: Marisol singing very quietly to Tomás, one of those endless looping songs from their village that Ines could not understand and could not bear to interrupt. Eduardo was repairing a boot sole with a bent needle and an expression of profound grievance, as though the boot had personally insulted him.

The kettle whistled. Ines poured tea she would not drink. That was another thing the widowing taught you — how much of housekeeping was simply performing the expectation of comfort, going through the motions of warmth so that others might believe in it.

August Reyes sat at her table, storm-stranded since noon, doing his best impression of furniture. He had been trying all day not to look useful and failing at it badly; every time the wind hit the windows he half rose from his chair like a man who believed wind could be reasoned with.

"Keeper Sandoval," he said, for perhaps the ninth time, "should I bring more wood up?"

"The wood is fine where it is."

"Should I shutter the south window?"

"The shutters are latched."

"Should I—"

"Mr. Reyes." She set his cup down in front of him with the gentleness of a woman placing a chess piece. "You have been a federal officer for how long?"

"Six years."

"And in six years, has shouting questions ever once made an answer arrive faster?"

A small, startled smile crossed his face — the first genuinely unguarded thing she had seen him do since the mail boat deposited him here. He sat back down. "No, ma'am."

"Then drink your tea. When I need a man, I will tell you precisely which muscles to use."

Above them the lamp turned, steady as a heartbeat, sweeping its long white arm across the blackening water. Ines listened to it the way other people listen for a child crying in the next room. As long as it turned, everything outside was somebody else's problem.

* * *

The light failed at twenty minutes past seven.

Later, she would reconstruct the sequence for herself a hundred times — the sound first, a deep mechanical *clunk* like a coffin lid, then the silence where the clockwork's tick had been, then the terrible ordinary darkness pouring through the lantern glass. But in the moment there was only the fact of it: the weight-driven mechanism that turned the lens had seized, and the flame itself, starved of its draft by the wind forcing itself down the ventilator, guttered and died like a moth swatted mid-flight.

Ines stood at the base of the tower stairs with her hand on the rail and felt the fear come for her, patient and familiar, an old dog that knew her name.

Out there, past the point, the river channel ran between the shoals. There were boats on the water tonight — the late packet out of Millbrook, if it had sailed, and God knew what else, fishing smacks that should have stayed tied but never did. A dark channel and a blind shore. Men had drowned within sight of this rock in exactly these conditions, and one of them had been her husband, and that thought she put away in its drawer and locked, because drawers do no good open.

"Ines?" Marisol's voice from below, thin through the floorboards. "The light—"

"I know." Already she was moving. Oil can, matches, the wrench for the seized governor weights, the heavy coat, the mittens with the left thumb patched. "Marisol! Light the stove high and keep everyone below. If anything breaks loose down there, hold it, don't chase it."

"What are you doing?" Eduardo's voice now, alarmed. "You cannot go up in *that*—"

"Eduardo." She paused at the stair door. "I am the keeper. It is my light."

"But the wind—"

"The wind," said Ines Sandoval, "is loud but it is not in charge."

August was already on his feet, coat half on, face gone serious in the way of men about to be brave in the wrong direction. "I'll come with you."

"You'll do no such thing. You've been seasick crossing this station's own dock."

"That was—" He stopped, dignity visibly regrouping. "That was a different occasion."

"It was July." She took the oil can off its hook. "Here is what you can do. Go down to the cellar. Sit with them. Tell Tomás a story — any story, make one up, census takers must be good at inventing things. And if I am not back down in an hour, come up after me, because that will mean something has gone wrong enough to need two fools instead of one."

For a moment they looked at each other across the little kitchen, the government man and the widow, and something passed between them that neither would later be able to name — an accounting, almost, of debts being quietly moved from one ledger column to another.

"An hour," he said.

"Fifty-five minutes," said Ines, and opened the door into the screaming dark.

* * *

The wind at ground level was a bully. At the top of the gallery it was a mob.

She climbed the outside steps to the service door with her body canted forward at an angle that would have looked comical from the shore, if the shore had been visible, which it was not. The world had been erased. Snow came not down but sideways, in horizontal sheets, and the air was so full of it that the lantern room above her existed only as a rumor. Twice she lost the wall entirely and found it again by reaching out with one mittened hand and trusting. The third time she did not find it, and the wind took her two stumbling yards across the gallery before her shoulder struck the iron railing and she hung on, cheek against metal colder than charity, while the gale tried its arithmetic on her grip.

One hundred and forty-three steps inside the tower, she had counted them for nine years. Six outside, in weather like this, were worth two hundred.

She got the service door open by throwing her whole weight against it and rode the swing of it inside, and then — blessedly, shockingly — quiet. Not true quiet; the stone walls boomed and moaned and the wind played the tower like an enormous flute. But it was the quiet of a battlefield compared to the open sky, and Ines stood a moment in the black spiral just breathing, letting her heart bang itself out against her ribs like a bird in a crate.

Then she climbed.

Up and up through the dark, one hand on the rail, the oil can knocking gently against her hip with every step like a small companion. Her breath came ragged. Somewhere below her feet, in a cellar under a hill of storm, a boy was singing a looping song and a man was telling him stories and a woman was keeping them all brave, and none of them knew that the reason the channel was dark tonight was a gear of brass no bigger than a saucer, and the reason the gear had seized was probably grit, and grit meant disassembly, and disassembly meant hands, and hands meant bare skin in a lantern room where the temperature was falling like a debt collector down the stairs.

At the top, the lantern room was a cathedral of noise. The glass panes flexed in their frames. Snow scoured the glass from without, hissing like sand.

She lit the work lamp first, a small fixed thing shielded behind its own glass, and by its stub of light saw the damage: the drive shaft stopped dead, the governor weights hanging slack, and when she put her ear close, the faint dry shriek of metal wanting metal. Grit in the works, sure enough, or ice, or both — some grain of the world's ordinary dirt that had gotten into the one machine on this coast allowed no failures.

"All right," she told it conversationally, unslinging the tool bag. "Let's you and me discuss your behavior."

Her fingers were clumsy in the mittens, so she pulled them off, and immediately her hands became two strangers, pink and stupid with cold. She worked anyway. Wrench, screwdriver, the little brass punch Mateo had kept in his coat pocket for years until she confiscated it for the toolbox where it belonged. She broke the assembly down piece by piece in the shrieking dark, laid each part on the cloth in strict order — *left to right, always left to right, a keeper who trusts her memory in a crisis is a keeper who buries fishermen* — and wiped each one clean with the rag kept for exactly this, and found the culprit at last: a thread of frozen packing, hardened to wire, wedged in the gear teeth like a splinter in a thumb.

She picked it out with her fingernails, which hurt, and laughed while doing it, which frightened her a little, and reassembled the train left to right, right to left, checking each seat and seat-mate.

The weights she rewound by the crank, forty turns, and her arms were rope and fire by thirty. The draft valve she adjusted with numb fingers twice, three times, until the wick drew clean.

Then the match.

Here is a thing nobody tells you about striking a match in hurricane wind inside a lantern room: the room breathes. The whole tower inhales and exhales around you, and the flame knows it, and wants to die, and you must cup the little sizzle of fire in your two bare hands like something newborn, like the last of anything, and carry it to the wick through the gusts, whispering — actually whispering, absurdly — *not yet, not yet, wait, wait.*

The flame touched the wick. Flickered. Crouched low.

And rose.

The lens caught it, multiplied it, sent it wheeling out into the white blindness of the night — one flash, two, the great slow heartbeat of Point Alder resuming as though it had merely paused to clear its throat. Ines sat down hard on the lantern room floor with her ruined hands in her lap and watched the beam sweep round and round, and she laughed again, properly this time, alone in a tower at the top of a drowning world.

"Left to right," she told nobody. "Every time."

* * *

Going down was worse than coming up, because coming up, fear had carried her, and fear is lighter than relief. By the gallery her legs were shaking. By the kitchen door her whole body was one long argument with gravity. She let herself in on the second try and stood dripping in the doorway, snowmelt pooling off her coat, and five faces turned toward her — August rising so fast his chair skidded, Marisol with Tomás's head pressed to her shoulder, Eduardo halfway up the cellar steps with the boot forgotten in his hand.

"You're bleeding," Marisol said.

Ines looked down. Her fingertips were cracked along the old frost scars, welling red. "It's winter," she said. "Everything bleeds a little. The light's burning. That's the news."

Tomás wriggled free of his mother and crossed the room and looked up at her with the flat, devastating directness of a six-year-old conducting an inspection. "You went up in the wind," he said.

"Yes."

"Were you scared?"

Ines considered lying to him, and then considered what a waste of a good question that would be. "Terrified," she said. "I was terrified nearly the whole time. But the ships out there are more scared than I was, and they don't get anyone to help them. So up I went."

The boy nodded slowly, filing this away in whatever drawer children keep such things, and returned to his mother's knee. Eduardo was looking at her strangely — with something new in his face, something that had not been there in all the weeks of hiding, and it took her a moment to recognize it as respect, arriving late and awkward like a guest who'd gotten lost.

August brought her a blanket without being asked and draped it over her shoulders, and then stood there holding his own elbows, wrestling visibly with the civil servant's eternal dilemma.

"Ask it," Ines said, sinking into her chair.

"Why?" He gestured vaguely upward, at the tower, the storm, the whole unreasonable arrangement. "The pay is a pittance. Nobody would know if the light stayed dark one night. One storm. Who would ever—"

"I would know," said Ines. She held her cracked hands out toward the stove. "That's the whole arithmetic of it, Mr. Reyes. Every night I keep this light, I am counting myself among the living who are worth their keep. Some nights it's the only count that comes out even."

August Reyes looked at her for a long moment, and then he looked away, out at the rattling black windows, and she saw him go somewhere inside himself, some closed room where ledgers were kept, and she had the odd, certain feeling that somewhere in that room, pages were burning.

She was still warm at last, still watching the steam rise off the refilled cup Marisol pressed into her hands, when the knock came.

Three blows at the front door, measured and heavy, the knock of a man accustomed to having doors opened for him. Everyone froze. The wind howled once around the eaves as if to cover the sound, too late.

Through the frosted pane of the door, dim and swaying in the storm-glow of the turning lamp above, stood the shape of a sled — and on the sled, a lantern, swinging.
