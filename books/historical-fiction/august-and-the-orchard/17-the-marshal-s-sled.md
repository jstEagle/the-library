## 17. The Marshal's Sled

Tomás had learned every sound the cellar made.

He had been down there long enough to know them all like cousins at a family table. There was the groan of the floorboards overhead when Tía Ines walked — two quick steps and a drag on the third, because of the way she favored her left knee in cold weather. There was the tick and swallow of the stove pipe, which sounded like a polite old man clearing his throat. There was Papá's snoring, which Tomás privately thought could be heard clear across the river if anyone cared to listen, and which was why Papá now slept with a rolled sock in his mouth that Mamá called *his little dignity*.

And there was the wind. The wind was new. The storm had come a day early, like a guest who shows up before you've swept the floor, and it made the whole tower sing a low, worried note that never quite ended.

Tonight, though, under all those sounds, Tomás heard another one. Far off. A bell.

Not the lighthouse bell. The lighthouse bell had a certain rusty honesty to it, like an uncle who'd given up pretending. This was a smaller bell, bright and quick, jingle-jingle-jingling, the kind of bell that hung from a horse's harness.

He knew this because back home — before, in the other life where they'd had a street and neighbors and a door that locked because nobody was chasing them — Señor Varga had driven a milk cart with bells just like it. Tomás had loved that sound. It had meant bread.

Now he wasn't sure what it meant, but his stomach folded itself into a small, cold knot anyway.

* * *

The cellar room had gotten smaller over the winter. Not really — Tomás had measured it once with Papá's boot, heel to toe, eleven boots wide by fourteen long, and it hadn't moved — but it felt smaller every week, the way a word said too many times starts sounding strange. The cot. The crates of lamp oil. The shelf of preserves in jars that glowed amber when the lamp was lit, like bottled summer. The four of them, folded into it like clothes in a drawer.

Mamá was mending the same sock she always mended. Papá was reading the almanac for a year that had already ended, because it was the only book, and he read it anyway, every night, as if the weather of last year might change its mind out of embarrassment.

"I hear a bell," Tomás said.

Papá didn't look up. "You hear the wind."

"It's not the wind."

"The wind does many tricks, *mijo*. It whistles. It hums. Once, in the mountains, I heard it whistle my own name, and I did not answer, and I am still alive."

"Papá. It's a harness bell."

That made Mamá look up. Her needle stopped mid-stitch. She and Papá looked at each other the way they did when they were speaking without words, which was a language Tomás was not allowed to study yet but understood perfectly anyway: *Do we tell her? Do we wait? Do we pretend longer?*

Then the floorboards overhead did their thing — two quick steps and a drag — and Tía Ines came down the stairs with the lamp raised high, and Tomás saw her face and forgot how to breathe.

She had gone still in the particular way of animals. "Where's the census man?"

"Upstairs," Papá said. "In the parlor with your logbooks. Why—"

"Stay here." She set the lamp down so gently it was worse than throwing it. "All of you. Don't make a sound. Don't even make the sounds you think are quiet."

"What is it?" Mamá whispered.

"Bell on the north road." Ines was already turning. "Nobody rings a bell on the north road in a storm unless they want to be heard."

* * *

Being told not to make a sound, Tomás discovered, is the cruelest instruction ever invented, because the moment you receive it, your body becomes an orchestra.

His knee cracked. His nose decided it needed whistling. His stomach growled with the dramatic timing of a theater performer. He clamped his hand over his belly and glared at it.

Mamá pulled him against her side. Her heart was going fast; he could feel it through her sleeve, quicker than a bird's, and that frightened him more than the bell had, because Mamá's heart was supposed to be the steady one. Mamá was the one who said *God keeps account of sparrows* and *we will laugh about this someday*, usually both in the same breath.

But upstairs — he could hear it through the boards, faintly — there were voices. Tía Ines and the census man, August Reyes, who wore a coat too big for him and ate his bread crust-first like a person with strange priorities. Their voices were low and urgent, rising and falling like something being argued and un-argued at the same time.

Then the parlor door opened, and boots went down the hall, and the front door opened, and the storm rushed in — Tomás could hear it, a great white roar — and then the door shut again, and one set of boots went away into it. Just one.

"That's the Reyes boy," Papá murmured. "Going out alone."

"Why?" Tomás asked.

Nobody answered him. That was the trouble with being nine. Every important question was treated like a jar on the top shelf.

* * *

Time passed strangely after that. Tomás counted it in stitches. Mamá pulled yarn through yarn, pull-pull-pull, and he watched the needle go in and out until his eyes burned.

Forty stitches. Sixty. A hundred.

Then Papá, who had drifted to the foot of the stairs despite explicit instructions, held up a hand.

The bell again. Closer. Jingle-jingle-jingle, and beneath it now a crunch and hiss that could only be runners on snow, and — worst of all — voices. One voice, deep and official-sounding, the kind of voice that belonged to a man with a mustache you could lose a spoon in. And under it, fainter, the other voice: August Reyes, saying something Tomás couldn't make out, saying it over and over, patient as rain.

They came past the house. Tomás felt them pass the way you feel thunder pass — a pressure, a wrongness in the chest. The light of a lantern slid across the storm window at the very top of the foundation wall, that little window he wasn't tall enough to see out of properly, that showed only a slice of gray-white world.

He dragged the crate over anyway, quietly, quietly, and climbed up, and pressed his face near the glass.

Through the frost ferns on the pane, through the driving snow, he saw them: two dark shapes by the gate. One carried a lantern whose flame fought the wind like a trapped moth. Behind them, half-guessed, half-invented by his own terrified imagination, stood the sled. And harnessed to the sled, huge in the swirling white, a horse — no, *shadows* that might be horses, stamping, steaming, jingling.

Jingle-jingle-jingle.

"They've come for us," he said. His voice came out very small, smaller than he'd intended, as if the words themselves were trying to hide.

"Get down from there," Papá said, but not unkindly, and when Tomás didn't move — couldn't move, his knees had welded themselves to the crate — Papá crossed the room and lifted him down as if he weighed nothing, which Tomás knew was a lie, because he'd grown almost a hand-width this winter, hiding. Growing even here. Life insisting on itself in a cellar, like a weed through a paving stone.

Mamá knelt and took his face in her hands. Her fingers smelled of wool and lamp oil. "*Escúchame*," she said. "Listen. If anyone comes down those stairs, we are the Cortez family, we have papers, we are waiting for the ferry north, do you understand? You are Tomás Cortez. Say it."

"Tomás Cortez."

"Again."

"Tomás Cortez."

"Good. And if they ask about the lighthouse keeper?"

"She's our aunt," Tomás said. This part he was good at. This part he'd rehearsed so many times it had worn smooth, like a stone in his pocket. "Tía Ines. We're just waiting for the ferry."

"Just waiting for the ferry," Mamá echoed, and for a moment something crossed her face that was not fear exactly but something adjacent to it, some grief or guilt, and then she kissed his forehead and it passed.

* * *

The lantern outside moved away from the gate.

Tomás climbed back onto the crate — he couldn't help it; his body simply did it, the way water finds a crack — and watched the two shapes go down toward the boathouse, away from the cottage, away from the cellar, the marshal's voice booming something lost to wind, August's voice threading after it.

Then, impossibly, the shapes separated. The marshal and his lantern and his jingling shadows kept going, down toward the landing, and August Reyes turned around and came back toward the cottage alone.

Tomás watched him come. Watched him stop halfway up the path, bend into the wind, and stand there a moment with his head bowed like a man in church. Then he straightened and went inside the front door, and a minute later the ceiling spoke above them: Tía Ines's footsteps hurrying, a murmur, a pause that lasted three full stitches, and then her feet on the cellar stairs.

She looked different coming down the ladder. Smaller somehow, and older, and also lit from somewhere behind the eyes.

"He's early," she said to Mamá and Papá. "The marshal. A week early. Someone in the village sent word."

Mamá made a sound like a stitch pulling tight.

"But—" Ines sat down heavily on the oil crate, and laughed once, a strange cracked laugh. "August met him on the road. Talked to him in the storm for the better part of a quarter hour. Showed him paperwork." She shook her head slowly. "Forged paperwork, I'd stake the light on it. The marshal's gone down to check the landing — says he'll ride out to the station tomorrow morning instead, in daylight, since the road's a white grave tonight. Says there's no hurry." Her mouth twisted. "August convinced him there's no hurry."

"Can he do that?" Papá asked. "Forge federal documents? For strangers?"

"He's not doing it for strangers," Ines said, and there was something in her voice Tomás couldn't name, some weather system of its own, and Mamá heard it too, because Mamá's hand found hers in the lamplight and squeezed it, and neither woman said anything else for a moment.

Tomás, from his crate, raised his hand.

Tía Ines blinked at him. "Yes, Tomás?"

"If the marshal comes tomorrow," Tomás said, "in daylight — won't he be able to count us?"

The cellar got very quiet. Even the stove pipe seemed to hold its polite old-man throat-clearing.

"Yes," Ines said at last. "I expect he will."

"Then someone has to be on the ferry before daylight."

Everyone looked at him. Tomás shrank a little under the weight of four adult gazes — well, three; Papá was looking at the almanac as though it had personally betrayed him — but he kept his chin up, because he had asked a real question and he wanted a real answer more than he wanted comfort.

"You've been listening at the top of the stairs again," Ines said.

"There's nothing else to do down here except listen," Tomás pointed out. "And count boots."

Something happened to Tía Ines's face then — a softening, a folding, the way snow softens a fence line — and she crossed the small room and sat down beside his crate and looked at him eye to eye, her eyes brown and tired and terribly kind.

"No," she said. "There isn't anything else to do down here, is there? Except grow." She touched his cheek, one rough thumb. "You're right, *sobrino*. Somebody has to be on the first boat. Before the marshal's breakfast. Before the ice breaks, maybe — it's cracking early this year, August says the river's talking in its sleep." She paused, and Tomás watched her look across the room at Mamá, and at Papá, and he watched something enormous happen silently between the three of them, the wordless language again, spoken at speed, concluded in a heartbeat.

Three berths, he'd heard Papá whisper to Mamá weeks ago. Three berths, four souls. He hadn't understood it then. Lying in the dark afterward, doing sums on his fingers, he had begun to understand it now.

"Sleep," Tía Ines said aloud, to everyone, in the voice she probably used on storms. "All of you. Tomorrow the river decides, and we should meet it rested."

* * *

But Tomás did not sleep.

He lay on the cot between Mamá and Papá, under the good wool blanket that smelled of cedar, and listened to the storm work at the tower overhead like a carpenter who would not be hurried. And he listened to something else, farther off, down toward the landing where the marshal's sled must be sheltering in the boathouse.

Jingle. Pause. Jingle-jingle.

Or maybe it was only the wind doing its tricks. Maybe it was the wind whistling his own name, the way it had whistled Papá's in the mountains.

Either way, Tomás made himself very small under the blanket and did not answer, and stared into the dark, and waited for morning — for the ice, and the ferry, and whatever it was that adults' faces did when they had decided which of the people they loved most in the world was going to get on a boat.
