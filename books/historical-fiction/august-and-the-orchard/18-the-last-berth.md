## 18. The Last Berth

The sled lights had gone out like a struck match.

Ines stood at the kitchen window with her breath fogging the glass, watching the place on the river road where the marshal's lanterns had been swallowed by the whiteout, and she counted — as she counted everything now, in the currency of what could be lost — the seconds until August came back through her door.

One hundred. Two hundred. The wind took a run at the tower and the whole house leaned into it like a ship. Three hundred.

"He's all right," Marisol said behind her, though her voice said no such thing. She had Tomás by the shoulder, and the boy was pressed to her side the way iron filings press to a magnet. Rafael stood at the table with his hat in his hands, turning it and turning it, a man who had never in his life had anything to do with his hands except honest work and now could not think of a single honest thing to do with them. "He told us he'd meet them alone. He told us—"

"I heard what he told us." Ines did not turn from the window. "I want to see him walking."

Four hundred. Five hundred. Somewhere beneath them, under the floorboards and the cellar room and six feet of frozen earth, the river spoke. Not the ordinary groaning it had done all winter, the old ice settling its bones the way an old man settles into a chair. This was younger. Angrier. A crack ran somewhere out along the channel with a sound like God tearing linen, and every one of them flinched except Tomás, who was too busy being frightened of the sled lights to be frightened of the ice.

Six hundred seconds. Then, out of the whiteness, a shape. One man, hatless, coat whipping around him, moving with the particular stumbling urgency of someone who has just talked his way past a loaded gun and is not entirely sure yet that it worked.

Ines had the door open before he reached the steps.

"Inside," she said. "Before the wind decides it wants you."

August Reyes came up the stairs two at a time and stood dripping in her kitchen, snow caked in his eyebrows, his ledger — the forged one, the one that would hang him if it ever hung anyone — clutched to his chest like a hymnal. For a long moment nobody said anything. The stove ticked. The river boomed again, farther off this time, toward the north channel.

"Well?" Rafael asked finally.

"He bought it." August set the ledger on the table with hands that were not quite steady. "The paperwork. I told him my supervisor had flagged the station roster for correction before the audit, that there were discrepancies in the ferry manifests going back two seasons, and that any arrests made now would compromise a federal inquiry." He laughed, once, a strange cracked sound. "I told him the census bureau had jurisdiction. He didn't know enough to argue. He wanted to argue — I could see it in his face, he *wanted* to take you all tonight and collect his bounty — but Rosa's message said 'extra shadows,' and shadows aren't evidence, and I made very sure he had nothing that was."

"And he left," Marisol said. "Just like that."

"He left to telegraph my supervisor from the village." August's honesty was a bleak thing; he did not dress it up. "Which buys us until the wires go down. And the wires—" he nodded at the window, at the shrieking dark beyond it, "—are going down, I'd bet my post on it. Which I have. Already have, I mean." He looked at Ines. "It's not much time. But it's some."

"It's some," Ines agreed, and then the river tore its linen again, closer, and they all stood very still and listened to winter coming apart at the seams.

* * *

They gathered at the table an hour later, or what passed for gathered: Rafael pacing, Marisol holding Tomás half-asleep against her, August standing by the stove with the wary posture of a man who knows he is in a room where he has not yet earned a chair. Ines laid out the coins.

She had counted them so many times the numbers had worn grooves in her. Two hundred and ten dollars in the biscuit tin under the loose hearthstone, and of that, sixty belonged to the light itself — oil money, wick money, the government's own coin that she would repay if she lived to be a hundred — leaving one hundred and fifty dollars of honest saving from eleven years of keeping. Ferris the ferryman wanted eighty a berth. Three berths. Two hundred and forty.

Two paid. One owed. And four souls at her table.

"Ferris will take paper promises for nothing," Rafael said. "I'll sign anything. My word—"

"Your word's worth more than mine and we both know it, but Ferris's boat doesn't float on words." Ines kept her voice level, the same voice she used in weather. "He's running the crossing early because the ice is going and after it goes there's no crossing till spring thaw, and he's risking his hull doing it. He wants cash and he wants it before lines are cast off. That's not cruelty, Rafael. That's arithmetic."

"Then it's simple," Marisol said quietly. "Rafael and I stay. Tomás goes, and—" she stopped.

And that was the thing, wasn't it. Ines had watched it assemble itself in the woman's face over the past day, the way frost assembles on glass, pattern by pattern, inevitable. Two paid berths meant two people across the river before the warrants closed like a hand. Four souls meant somebody stayed, and staying meant either the marshal or the storm or the spring — whichever arrived first hungriest. The family had argued it in circles for two days. Marisol would not cross without Rafael; Rafael would not take a berth his wife and son couldn't fill; and Tomás—

Tomás was asleep sitting up, his head rolling against his mother's arm, his breath whistling faintly through a nose still red from crying earlier and pretending he hadn't. Seven years old. He had crossed an ocean and hidden in a cellar and learned to whistle when the marshal's kind of men passed on the road, a warning whistle, taught to him by adults who should have been teaching him to fish. He had asked Ines last week, in the matter-of-fact way children ask the unanswerable, whether the free side of the river had cellars too.

"The boy goes," Ines said. "That's not the question. The question is who else."

"There is no who else," Rafael said. "There's one berth unpaid and none of us has—" He gestured at the coins, at the small sad stack of them, and his voice broke sideways. "Eighty dollars. Eighty dollars and I'd give my right arm, my eyes, I'd—"

"You'll give me your silence for five minutes," Ines said, "and listen."

She had thought about this since the night before, lying awake in the keeper's bed while the wind walked the gallery rail above her like a sentry. She had thought about it in the cold arithmetic part of her mind, the part that wound clocks and trimmed wicks and knew exactly how many hours of oil remained, and she had thought about it in the other part, the older part, the part that had held her drowning husband's name in her mouth like a stone for two years. Both parts had arrived at the same answer. That was how she knew it was true.

"August," she said. "You have the federal papers on you. The forged manifest."

His head came up sharply. Beside him, Marisol went rigid.

"Ines—" he began.

"Hear me. Ferris doesn't read faces, but he reads documents, because documents are what get a ferryman hanged. A signed transit pass, bureau-issued, authorizing the bearer's passage north — that turns the third berth from a favor into an order. He won't ask for eighty dollars. He'll ask for his name to be spelled correctly." She slid the last of her coins back into the tin. "You can write that paper. You've already written worse this week."

The stove ticked. Outside, the wind found a loose board somewhere and worried it like a dog.

"And the bearer," August said carefully, "would be?"

"That's not settled," Marisol said, fast, too fast, and Ines saw it happen — saw the suspicion come back into the woman's face, the old fraying trust, the memory of two days ago when she had stood in the cellar doorway and demanded to know who betrayed them, whether it was this man with the ledger, whether the whole rescue was a longer rope for a deeper noose. "No. No, I know what you're doing, both of you, and I won't—"

"Marisol." Ines put both hands flat on the table, the way she did when the sea got rough, as if she could hold the whole world steady by pressing down on wood. "Look at your son."

Everyone looked at Tomás. Asleep, mouth open, one fist curled shut around nothing. His mother's shawl had slipped off his shoulder and Ines reached over and drew it back up, gently, and Marisol watched her do it and something in her face gave way, not all at once, the way a mooring gives way — line by line, creaking, refusing.

"If Rafi and I stay together, we face it together," Marisol said, and her voice was low and terrible and proud. "That I accept. That I chose already. But if there is one berth more, one, then it isn't Tomás who takes it alone — it's me. I'm his mother. A boy crosses an ocean better with his mother's hands on him than with a paper in his pocket, whatever the paper says. You don't get to decide this for me, Ines Sandoval. Nobody decided it for me when I carried him here in the dark of a ship's hold, and nobody decides it now."

"I know," Ines said. "I know that's true."

"But you've already decided something," Marisol said, watching her. "I can see it. You've got that face. The lighthouse face."

Ines almost smiled at that. The lighthouse face. Eleven years of wearing it, first beside Mateo and then instead of him, the face that said *all is well* to every boat in bad water whether it was true or not. She hadn't known it showed.

"Then let me say it plain," she said, "and then let everyone hate me a little, and then let's get to the river, because the ice isn't going to wait on our feelings." She looked around the table, at each of them. Rafael, gray-faced, gripping the back of a chair. Marisol with her chin up and her knuckles white around her sleeping son. August by the stove, very still, the way men go still when they suspect the next words will concern them.

"The third berth goes to Tomás. Alone."

* * *

The noise that followed was considerable, and Ines let it run its course, because they had earned their noise.

Marisol said *no*, twice, in two languages. Rafael said it was madness, a seven-year-old alone on the north shore with no kin waiting — and Ines said, "There's a Methodist mission house two miles up from the north landing, Father Esteban has written to them, it's arranged, it's been arranged for three weeks," and watched that land on them like a dropped anchor, because they hadn't known, because she had spent three weeks of candle-smoke evenings writing letters she hadn't told them about, not out of deceit but out of the keeper's oldest habit: securing the light before announcing it.

August said nothing for a while, and then he said the true thing, which was his gift even when it was an unwelcome one.

"She's right," he said quietly. "Not because a mother's love doesn't cross water — it does, I've seen it. But because a warrant has a shape, Marisol, and the shape is a family. A husband and wife and child, that's a story the marshal can tell his superiors, that's a capture worth a bounty and a newspaper paragraph. A boy traveling alone on bureau papers is a boy whose father couldn't be located. It's a boring file. Boring files get closed." He spread his hands. "I hate it. I want to be wrong. But I've spent six weeks learning exactly how the government sees this household, and I'm telling you what it sees. It sees a family, or it sees nothing. Give it nothing."

"A paper," Marisol said to August, and her voice had gone very quiet, quieter than shouting. "A forged paper. From your bureau. And when they come asking you—"

"Then they come asking me," August said. "That's the arrangement. That's been the arrangement since the lantern room. I keep the books; the books say what I make them say; and if the books are ever opened, the hand that wrote them is the hand that answers." He almost smiled, that dry careful smile of his. "I've been raised by liars, señora. I know precisely how much a good lie weighs. This one weighs less than most."

Marisol looked at her son. That was all. She looked at him for a long time, and Ines looked at the table, giving her the privacy of it, because there are lookings that ought not to have witnesses.

"He gets seasick," she said finally, to nobody, to the room. "Every crossing since he was born. He'll be sick the whole way over and he'll be mortified about it, because he's seven and boys are mortified of everything." Her voice wavered and steadied, wavered and steadied, a lamp in wind. "Someone has to hold his coat."

"I will," Ines said.

"No." Marisol wiped her face with the heel of her hand, once, hard, the gesture of a woman shutting a door. "No — you're staying anyway, aren't you? Staying to keep the light. So yes. Yes, you hold his coat. You hold it and you stand where he can see you and you don't leave the dock until the boat is out of sight, because he'll look back, they always look back, and the last thing he sees should be a person and not a shoreline." She bent and lifted Tomás, who woke halfway, mumbled a question — *is it time, Mamá, is it the river now* — and was answered with a kiss on his hair and *soon, mi vida, soon,* and Ines felt the words go through her chest like a splinter, because *soon* was true and *soon* was unbearable and both at once, that was the whole of it, that was what love cost when it was spent wisely.

* * *

They were ready within the hour, because people who live hidden learn to be packed forever. Rafael brought the bags up from the cellar room, and the smell of that room came up with them — earth and lamp oil and the small human smell of a family who had slept below ground through a northern winter — and Ines thought, not for the first time, that she would smell that smell in her sleep for the rest of her life and would never once regret it.

At the door, into boots and coats, August paused beside Ines and spoke low, under the wind.

"The audit. If the inspector comes while the wires are down and the ferries are stopped — he could arrive tomorrow or in ten days. When he arrives, the roster shows one resident. It shows what it shows. You never saw me write anything."

"I never saw you write anything," Ines agreed. "I saw a tired young man drink my tea and complain about my biscuits."

"They were excellent biscuits."

"They were terrible biscuits. I bake like a sailor." And for one second, one whole second, they both smiled, two conspirators in a doorway in a storm, and it was nearly warm.

Then the river boomed — the loudest yet, close, south channel, and beneath their feet this time — and the smile died on both faces at once, because that sound was a clock striking. The ice was breaking early. Hours early. Maybe less.

Ferris would cast off when the channel opened, not before, and the channel would open when it pleased, and between the dock at Point Alder and the far shore lay a mile of failing winter with a boy in the middle of it.

"Go," Ines said to all of them. "Now. All of you — walk him down."

They went out into the white roar, a little procession: Rafael with the bags, Marisol with Tomás's coat held ready, August carrying the forged papers inside his shirt where they'd keep dry, and Tomás himself, wide awake now, looking up at the tower as they passed beneath it because he always looked at the tower, because Ines had told him once that the light was a promise and he had taken her literally, as children do.

At the top of the bluff path Ines stopped and turned once to check the lantern — habit, pure habit, checking the light before walking into darkness — and it was then she heard it, under the wind, under everything.

The ice. Not booming now.

Singing. That high thin keening the floes make in the last hour, the sound old ferrymen call the river's confession, the sound that means the channel is opening *tonight.*

She turned back to the others, to the small huddled family at the head of the path, to the boy who was about to be given everything she had left to give.

"The dock," she called. "Run."
