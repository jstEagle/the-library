## 20. The Audit

The inspector arrived on the ninth of March, which was three days earlier than the letter had promised and one lifetime later than August would have preferred.

He saw the boat first from the keeper's cottage window—a government launch, gray as dishwater, nosing through the last pans of river ice with the smug confidence of a vessel that had never once been late for anything. August set down his tea. He had been sitting at Ines's table in his shirtsleeves, going over the forged census sheets one final time, though by now he knew every fabricated stroke of his own handwriting better than he knew his mother's face.

"He's here," he said.

Ines did not startle. He had learned that about her in the weeks since their bargain—she was a woman who converted fear directly into motion, without the intermediate stop at panic. She crossed the room, took the census pages from under his hand, and looked at them not with anxiety but with the appraising eye of a keeper checking her lamp.

"Is it good work?" she asked. "The forgery?"

August considered the question honestly, because she deserved honesty and because there was very little else left to give anyone.

"It's the best work I've ever done," he said. "Which is itself a kind of problem."

"The ink?"

"Aged with tea and dried on the stove. The dates?"

"Backdated through the February supplementals, cross-referenced against the mail boat log." He almost smiled. "I even gave myself a clerical error. Two, actually. A man who falsifies a federal document too neatly might as well sign it."

Ines set the pages down and, to his astonishment, laughed—a short, tired, real laugh, the first he'd heard from her since the storm.

"You are wasted on the government, August Reyes."

"So I am discovering."

Above them, through the ceiling, came the muffled thunder of Tomás practicing his letters by jumping rope in the attic, each landing a small controlled earthquake. Below, in the cellar, Marisol's voice rose faintly through the floorboards, singing to herself in Spanish, a habit August had come to think of as the house's true heartbeat. Five souls in this station. One name in the ledger. He had done the arithmetic; now came the subtraction.

* * *

Inspector Halloran came ashore wearing a coat too fine for the mud and an expression too grim for the weather, which was, for the first time in a week, genuinely pleasant. He was a compact man with a mustache like a shoe brush and the particular posture of someone who had spent thirty years finding fault and being paid by the fault.

"Reyes," he said, extending a hand gloved in kid leather. "You're a long way from your route."

"The storm stranded me," August said. This much was true. He had learned in the past weeks that the most durable lies were built entirely of true things, mortared together with omission. "I've been assisting Keeper Sandoval with repairs."

Halloran looked him up and down. August was aware of how he must appear: unshaved, collar soft, boots salt-stained, a federal man gone native in a fisherman's sweater that Ines had pressed upon him with the immovable authority of a woman who could not bear to see a guest cold.

"Assisting," Halloran repeated, as though the word were a specimen he intended to pin to a board. "How civic. And where is the keeper?"

"Oil store," August said. "She'll be along. She's always along."

They walked up together toward the tower, mud sucking at their boots, and August made himself match the inspector's pace and made himself breathe. Behind the boathouse, Rafa—Marisol's husband, the fourth hidden soul, the man who had spent the winter splitting firewood as if the wood owed him money—was visible for exactly four seconds before he melted behind the stack with a smoothness that suggested long practice. Halloran noticed nothing. August noticed everything. It occurred to him, with a lurch, that this was precisely what he had trained himself all winter to do: notice everything and file it in the wrong drawer.

The ledger lay open on the tower desk when they reached it. August had left it that way deliberately. Guilty men hide documents; honest men leave them in the light, and besides, the ledger was clean. Painfully, immaculately clean. Ines Sandoval kept records the way cathedrals kept saints—every wick trimmed, every oil ration entered, every signature dated and squared. If forgery had a conscience, August thought, it would look like this book.

Halloran ran a gloved finger down the columns.

"Sandoval, Ines. Keeper." His finger stopped. "One resident."

"One resident," August agreed.

"Previous year shows four."

"Her husband drowned in November of '88. Two hired hands departed after—the light's run single-handed now. She manages it alone." He let a note of admiration into his voice, because admiration was true and truth traveled well. "Alone, Mr. Halloran, through the worst storm season this coast has seen in twenty years. I watched her relight that lamp in a whiteout with her own hands. There isn't a man in your service who'd have done it."

Halloran grunted, noncommittal as gravel. He turned pages. The tower creaked around them companionably, as if vouching for its keeper.

"And the census sheets? Yours?"

"Mine."

"They say one resident as well."

"They do."

Halloran closed the ledger with a soft clap and looked up at him, and for a moment the shoe-brush mustache twitched into something that might, in a kinder man, have been a smile.

"You know what's interesting, Reyes? I've been doing this twenty-six years. And in twenty-six years I have learned that the truth has a certain texture. Rough places. Little inconsistencies, little human messes. A lie—" he tapped the ledger "—is smooth. Smooth as ice. You could skate across these books and never once catch an edge."

The tower ticked. Somewhere below, gulls argued over something.

"Unless," Halloran went on mildly, "the smoothness is the point. Unless a man sat down with the truth in front of him and sanded off every rough place until it fit whatever shape he needed." He straightened his coat. "Walk with me, Reyes."

* * *

They walked, and Halloran talked, and August understood that he was not being interrogated so much as being given a long runway, the way one gives a condemned man a cigarette.

"I had a letter from the marshal's office," Halloran said, strolling along the seawall with his hands clasped behind him. "Seems there was an informant. A woman in the village. Rosa Delgado—perhaps you met her, sharp-tongued, sells eggs, would sell her sister if the price held. She wrote that extra shadows passed the lighthouse windows all winter. Four shadows, she said. She counted."

"Shadows," August said. "In a census report."

"She also wrote that a census taker was stranded at the station for six weeks and behaved less like a stranger than a lodger." Halloran glanced sideways. "That part interested my superiors more than the shadows."

The sea glittered, indifferent and beautiful. August found, to his surprise, that fear had not come. What had come instead was a strange clarity, cold and clean as the water, and beneath it a warmth he could only describe as inheritance—his mother's face, thin in the cholera winter, and Ines Sandoval's door opening to her before August had even been born to owe anything to anyone.

"Mr. Halloran," he said. "May I speak plainly?"

"I'd be disappointed otherwise."

"Ines Sandoval kept no undocumented persons at this station. She kept a light. Through a storm that killed two fishermen in the village and put the Point Alder dock under water, she kept a light, and ships came home because of it, and I will testify to that in any court you like. Whatever is in those ledgers, whatever is in my reports—that is mine. My hand. My responsibility. She cannot read English well enough to audit a federal form and everyone on this coast knows it. If there was fraud committed against the Bureau of the Census, it was committed by one man, alone, and you are looking at him."

Halloran stopped walking. For a while he simply regarded August, the way a man regards a clock he suspects of running slow but cannot prove.

"You understand what you're saying."

"Yes."

"You understand that a confession of falsifying federal records ends a career. Not damages it. *Ends* it. No bureau takes a man back from that. No reference letter travels well. You'll be lucky to carry mail."

"I've thought about it," August said. "Most nights, in fact. Usually between two and four, which I'm told is when all honest accounting gets done."

Something flickered across the inspector's face—not sympathy, quite, but recognition. The look of a man meeting a rare animal he had heard about but never seen: a bureaucrat telling the truth at cost to himself.

"And the family?" Halloran asked quietly. "For my own education. Where did they go?"

August looked out at the river. Far north, past the last headland, a smudge of smoke marked the ferry lane, where the ice was breaking up in great grinding plates. Three berths had sailed on the *Marta Lee* ten days ago—one child among them, waving with both arms until the headland took him. The rest had scattered overland, as planned, names changed like coats. As far as August knew—and he had made it his business to know—no warrant had followed them past the river.

"I couldn't say, sir," he said. "I count people. I don't chase them."

* * *

It took Halloran two days, which August thought was rather decent of him. He interviewed the keeper formally in the cottage parlor, and Ines gave testimony with such calm, such perfect, boring, meticulous calm, that August nearly wept with admiration. Had she harbored persons unknown to her? Persons had sheltered during the storm—all persons did in a storm; the Good Book and maritime law were in agreement. Did she know the whereabouts of one Rafael Ortiz, wanted on a warrant? She knew a firewood cutter by that description who had worked the station in the fall and been paid fairly, entries enclosed. Did she swear to the accuracy of the census as recorded? She swore to the accuracy of what the census taker had written, which was the extent of her legal knowledge, and she said it with a level gaze that dared Halloran to find an edge anywhere in it.

On the third morning, Halloran summoned August to the boathouse. The launch idled at the berth. The inspector held a single sheet of paper, and his mustache drooped, and August realized with a small shock that the man looked tired.

"My report goes forward today," Halloran said. "Census for Point Alder Light Station: one resident, verified. Station records: exemplary. Keeper: commended for service during the February storm—I don't commend people, Reyes, so enjoy it, it's rarer than honesty." He paused. "And a supplementary finding. That one census taker, A. Reyes, having confessed in writing"—he held up the sheet, August's own words, given freely two nights ago—"to falsification of returns, misrepresentation of household composition, and obstruction of a federal inquiry, is hereby dismissed from the Bureau of the Census, effective immediately, his final wage enclosed, his references withheld."

He handed over the paper as though handing over a verdict, which he was.

"For what it's worth," Halloran said, and now the mustache twitched properly, "in twenty-six years I have filed perhaps nine of these. And every man before you lied to the end and made me do the digging myself. You're the first to hand me the shovel. It makes the paperwork easier and the coffee taste worse." He settled his coat. "Whoever taught you to tell the truth like that, Reyes—they raised you right and ruined you for government work in the same breath."

"My mother," August said. "She had strong opinions about doors being open."

* * *

The launch pulled away at noon. August stood on the dock with his dismissal letter in one pocket and his final wages—eleven dollars and forty cents—in the other, watching the gray stern diminish, and found that he felt lighter with every foot of water that opened between him and his career. Unemployed. Unreferenced. Ruined for government work.

Free.

Behind him, boots on the planks. Ines came and stood beside him, hands folded in the manner of a woman attending a funeral, though the corner of her mouth was not funeral at all.

"Well," she said. "Census taker."

"Not anymore. Just August Reyes, gentleman of leisure, worth eleven dollars and forty cents."

"A fortune on this coast. You could buy the boat."

He turned to look at her. She was squinting into the sun off the water, and the wind was pulling loose hairs from her braid, and she had the straight spine of a woman who had lied beautifully to a federal inspector that morning and would lose no sleep over it, because her sleep had been spent long ago, on other nights, for other people.

"What does a ferryman earn these days?" he asked.

Ines looked at him then, really looked, and understanding moved across her face like weather.

"The old ferryman's talking of retiring," she said carefully. "Come spring. Someone will have to carry the book north and back. Someone patient. Someone good with lists."

"I'm told I'm excellent with lists."

"I hear you make them smooth as ice."

They stood a moment longer, laughing quietly at the sheer improbability of it—the dismissed man and the widow, the counted and the uncounted, the whole ridiculous arithmetic of survival—while above them the great lens turned in the tower, steady as a heart, sweeping the bright spring water for whoever needed finding next.

Then Ines touched his sleeve.

"Come inside," she said. "There's a letter came on that boat. From the north. It's from Tomás."

Her eyes were shining and afraid at once, and August forgot eleven dollars, forty cents, and every list he had ever made.

"Read it to me," she said. "Please. I want to hear it in the voice of a man who counts."
