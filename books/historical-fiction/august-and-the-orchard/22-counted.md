## 22. Counted

The river taught me everything I know about patience, and it began teaching late.

For twenty-two years I had asked questions for a living. I had knocked on doors with a ledger under my arm and a pencil behind my ear, and I had learned to read the pauses between answers the way a pilot reads water — the snags hidden beneath a smooth surface, the depth sounding shallow where it ran deep. Then one spring the government decided my handwriting was a liability rather than a skill, and the river, which had been waiting all along, offered me different work.

Now I asked questions of no one but the ice.

"April," I said to it that morning, "you're late."

The ice said nothing, being ice, but it had begun to mutter in the nights — that long groaning complaint of a river ready to be done with winter — and I knew the breakup was close. Which meant the *Mariposa*, my ferry, would soon be busy, and I wanted her hull inspected before the first northbound family arrived with their trunks and their chickens and their terrible sense of scheduling.

I was bent over her bow, checking a seam, when I heard footsteps on the dock boards. Light ones. Quick ones. The particular brisk tread of a person who believes the world is made of forms waiting to be filled.

"You'd be the ferryman, then?" said a voice above me. "August Reyes?"

I straightened up slow, the way my knees insisted on these days, and looked at her. Young. Maybe twenty-five. A carpetbag clutched against the wind, a ledger clutched against the carpetbag, and a look of tremendous official determination on a face that still had baby fat at its edges. Her coat was new and her boots were wrong for dock work, and she had tied her hat down with what appeared to be a shoelace.

"I would," I said.

"Miss Odile Ferrand, Department of the Interior." She did not offer a hand; she offered a document, which in her view was probably warmer. "I'm conducting the decennial census. I understand you know this district."

"I've counted it once or twice."

"So I'm told." Something flickered across her face then — the hesitation of a young person deciding whether a thing is gossip or intelligence. "The man in the village store said—" she consulted a note, though she plainly knew it by heart— "he said I should talk to you before I go out to the light station. He said you'd tell me how it's done out there."

I set down my wrench and looked at her properly. Twenty-two years is long enough for a village to forget most things and remember exactly the wrong ones, and I wondered which version Rosa's granddaughter had sold her over the counter: the census taker who froze, the census taker who lied, or the census taker who went quietly into private life after an audit found his arithmetic too generous by three souls.

"Well," I said. "It's done like anywhere else. You knock. You ask. You write down what people tell you."

"The store man laughed when he said it."

"The store man laughs at everything. It saves him from thinking." I wiped my hands on a rag and considered her. There was something in her face I recognized from old photographs of myself: the earnest conviction that the truth was a simple object, and counting it merely required reaching out and picking it up. I had carried that conviction up the lighthouse stairs on a winter night once, and left it there.

"Come with me," I said. "I'm crossing to the point anyway. I'll row you up from the north berth. It'll save you two miles of mud."

"That's not necessary—"

"It's also not refusable. My boat, my rules." And because she was young and cold and curious, she came.

* * *

The river in April is a country remaking itself. We slid past sheets of rotten ice gone the color of old lace, past willow flats loud with red-winged blackbirds, and Miss Ferrand sat in the stern with her ledger wrapped in oilcloth, asking careful questions about population density as though the blackbirds might answer.

"You were a census man yourself," she said. It wasn't a question; she'd been saving it.

"For eleven years."

"Why'd you stop?"

I shipped a stroke and let the boat drift a moment, feeling the current take us, the same current that had taken so many things north and south and occasionally, mercifully, to safety. "I got better at it than the job allowed," I said.

She frowned at that, turning it over, and I watched her decide not to pursue it. Good instinct, that. She'd go far, if the job didn't grind it out of her first.

"And the light station," she said instead. "The records show a keeper named Sandoval. Ines Sandoval. Is she still—"

"Twenty-three years now," I said. "Longest-serving keeper on the river. They gave her a medal once. She uses the case to store buttons."

"A woman keeping a light alone all that time?"

"She wasn't always alone." I pointed my chin toward the point, where the tower rose white and patient above the budding trees, its lantern catching the morning. "You'll see."

The north berth was soft with mud and the smell of thaw, and Ines was waiting at the top of the path. She did that — had always done that — heard the oars half a mile out and appeared as if summoned, though I never could prove she wasn't simply always standing there, the way the tower was always standing there.

She was grey now, mostly, and rounder than she'd been, and the storm of '91 had left a stiffness in her left hip that she refused on principle to acknowledge. But her eyes hadn't aged a day. They still took your measure and filed it away for later, and they still, when they landed on certain faces, went soft as lamplight.

"Ferryman," she said.

"Census taker," I said, and stepped aside to reveal Miss Ferrand, who had climbed the path with more dignity than the mud should have permitted and was already fumbling for her documents.

"Ma'am. Odile Ferrand, Department of the Interior. I'm here to enumerate the residents of the Point Alder light station."

Ines looked at her. Then at me. And I saw it happen — the small settling of her shoulders, the almost invisible bracing, twenty-three years deep — before she caught herself, and laughed, and put it down.

"Then you'd best come in and count us," she said. "We're just sitting down to coffee, and Marisol makes enough for six whether we are six or not."

* * *

The keeper's cottage had grown another room since I'd first stood in its doorway with frozen hands and a ledger full of suspicion, and the cellar room below had long since stopped being a hiding place. It was a pantry now, and a root cellar, and the place where Tomás — home from the north for the spring, big as a door, laughing like his mother — kept his crates of books and his half-finished maps of rivers he hadn't run yet.

There were five of them at the table when we came in, and I counted them the way you count your own heartbeat, without thinking, without fear, the way I had not been able to count them that winter so long ago.

Ines, at the head, pouring. Marisol beside her, greying beautifully, scolding the biscuits. Rafael — who had crossed the ice a fugitive and stayed to become the station's second keeper, its mechanic, its fixer of impossible things — arguing cheerfully with Tomás about whether a person could love both engineering and poetry or had to choose. And at the end, half-hidden behind a loaf, seventeen-year-old Luz, Marisol and Rafael's daughter, born the summer after the storms in the very bed her parents had once hidden in, who looked up at the stranger in the doorway with frank, unafraid curiosity.

Five souls. Five names. None hidden.

"Sit," Ines commanded, and there was no refusing that either.

Miss Ferrand stood at the end of the table with her book open and her pencil poised, and I watched her take in the room: the good furniture worn smooth with use, the photograph on the wall of a young woman in a keeper's uniform with a medal case full of buttons, the boy Tomás pulling out a chair for the official with the easy manners of someone raised in a house where strangers were fed first and questioned later, if at all.

"Names," she said, recovering herself. "If I could have the names of all residents. Ages. Occupations. Places of birth."

And here was the moment — I confess I held my breath, some old reflex rising in me like the ghost of a winter that never quite thaws — here was the moment where, once, every answer would have been a small knife-edge, every name a coin flipped.

"Ines Sandoval," said Ines. "Keeper. Sixty-one years old. Born right here on the river, and staying."

"Marisol Reyes Sandoval." Marisol winked at me across the table at the shared surname, the one Rafael had taken legally years back so the paperwork would quit stuttering, and which bound our families together on paper the way they had long been bound everywhere else. "Fifty-eight. Born in San Cristóbal, and don't you write anything less than the whole name, they shortened it at Ellis Island and my husband's been correcting the record ever since."

"Rafael Reyes Sandoval. Second keeper. Fifty-nine. San Cristóbal." He grinned. "Occupation: everything Ines tells me to do."

"Tomás Reyes Sandoval. Twenty-six. Engineer. Born—" he glanced at his mother, and she nodded, and something passed between them that had taken twenty years to build— "born in San Cristóbal. Resident of Toronto, visiting family."

"Luz Reyes Sandoval. Seventeen. Student." The girl smiled at Miss Ferrand, unhidden, unhurried. "Born right upstairs, according to my mother, who tells the story much too often."

Miss Ferrand wrote it all down. Her pencil moved steadily, and I watched her face for the flicker — the pause at places of birth, the hesitation at names that traveled — and it never came. Or if it came, it drowned in something better. Because Marisol had pushed a plate of biscuits at her, and Tomás was telling a story about a bridge inspection gone sideways in Ontario, and Luz was asking the census taker, with devastating sincerity, whether she got to keep the pencil afterward, and Miss Ferrand was laughing before she knew she was the kind of person who laughed at strangers' tables.

She finished the page. She read it back. Every soul present, every name given, every line true.

"Sign here," she said, "to certify," and Ines signed, and Marisol signed, and Rafael signed with a flourish that used up half the margin, and the book lay open on the table with all five names in it like five stones set in a wall.

I looked at that page a long time.

* * *

I walked Miss Ferrand back down to the boat in the gold end of the afternoon. The ice muttered out midstream. Somewhere above us the lamp would be getting trimmed and lit by hands that had done it through hurricane and grief and ordinary Tuesday evenings alike, and the beam would sweep the dark river the way it had swept it every night of my adult life, faithful as breathing.

"You knew them before," she said, as I shipped the oars. "Before today. That's why the store man sent you."

"He didn't want you making a fool of yourself."

"That's not it." She was quiet a while, watching the tower lean past. Then: "There's a record in the archive. An old census schedule, 1889, Point Alder station. One resident listed. But there's a working copy underneath it in a different hand, and the different hand has corrections, and the corrections were never explained, and the clerk who made them resigned rather than face an audit." She looked at me. "I found it my first week. I've been wondering about it ever since."

The oars dripped. The river carried us.

"What did you conclude?" I asked.

"That a man who counts people for a living eventually learns the difference between a lie and a mercy." She said it carefully, the way you say a thing you've rehearsed. "My grandmother came across in the hold of a ship with a name that wasn't hers. Somebody wrote that name down and let her be somebody else until she could be herself again. So when I found your corrected schedule, I didn't file a report." She opened her ledger, showed me the fresh page, five names in a neat column. "I filed this."

I found I couldn't speak for a moment. The river helped, filling the silence with its April business.

"You'll do well," I managed finally, "if the job lets you."

"The store man said that too," she said, grinning suddenly, twenty-five again. "More or less."

At the north berth I helped her up the bank to the road that would take her to the next farmstead, the next door, the next knocking, and I watched her go with her shoelace-hatted, form-carrying, world-improving stride, carrying five true names out into the country like a lantern.

Then I went back to the boat, because the ice was breaking up early this year, and there would be families on the south bank by morning wanting crossing — some with papers in order, some with papers that told only part of the truth, all of them, every one, worth counting.

I lit the *Mariposa*'s running lamp against the dusk. Across the water, right on schedule, the great light at Point Alder woke and swept the river twice, steady as a promise kept.

"Counted," I said to the river, and cast off for home.
