## 1. The Weight of Small Things

The knife first. It was the only thing Jonah owned outright, and even that was a lie of sorts, because the knife had been his father's and his father had signed it away with everything else when he put his thumbprint on the indenture paper. But papers could be argued with. Papers were only words, and words belonged to whoever held them last. The knife had a bone handle worn smooth as sea glass, and it fit his palm the way a key fits a lock, and so in the ledger of things that mattered, Jonah wrote it down as his.

He laid it on the cot beside the shell.

The shell was a cowrie, no bigger than his thumbnail, cream-colored with a row of brown teeth along its lip like a tiny closed mouth. His father had given it to him three seasons ago, fished out of his own pocket with a grin, saying, *First one I ever found that wasn't cracked. Kept it through two wrecks and a marriage. Now it's yours.* Jonah had asked him what it was for. His father had turned it over twice in his fingers and said, *Nothing. That's the point of it. Everything else has to be for something.*

Then the letter.

It lived inside the oilcloth packet under the floorboard by the cot's left leg, where the board had warped just enough to admit a fingernail. Jonah pried it up now, in the grey hour before the rain started again, and took out the letter he had read so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth. Four pages, written in a hand that slanted hard to starboard, as if the man writing it were always leaning into weather.

*Dear Jonah — If you are reading this I am either rich or at the bottom of the strait, and knowing my luck you should check which before you spend anything.*

Jonah knew the rest by heart. He read it anyway, all four pages, because reading it was a kind of standing at attention, and because the last line was the only will his father had ever made:

*Whatever they tell you about me, count the days yourself.*

He folded it along its soft creases and did not put it back under the floorboard. He put it inside his shirt instead, against his skin, where it would stay from now on. Some things stopped being stored and started being carried, and there was no ceremony to mark the change. You just noticed one morning that you'd done it.

Outside, the trading post of Port Merrow lay steaming under the first pressure of the monsoon. It was not yet raining, but the air had the weight of rain in it, the whole sky sagging like wet canvas. The island sat a half-mile off the mainland coast, close enough to see the green wall of the hills, far enough that the only way across was the long timber bridge — the causeway, everyone called it, though it was really a bridge — that ran on pilings through the shallows and connected the island's quay to the mainland road. In dry months, carts went over it morning and evening. In monsoon months, the sea took it back. The company closed the gates at both ends and posted notices, and for five months Port Merrow became less a place than a held breath.

Jonah had been on the island four years. He knew its geography the way other boys knew their prayers: the chandlery with its smell of tar and rope; the drying sheds where the oysters came in by the barrow-load; the company house, two stories of white-painted timber with Halloway & Finch painted across the lintel in letters that flaked a little more each year; the barracks where the divers slept between tides; the clerk's office with its single window and its typewriter that clattered like rain even when it wasn't raining. And the quay, always the quay, where the luggers came in and the shells went out and the money moved in directions Jonah was not supposed to track.

He tracked anyway. That was the trouble with him, according to Mr. Corliss, the manager. *You count things, boy. Counting is above your station.* Corliss said it without malice, the way you'd tell a dog not to chase carts. He genuinely believed some men were born to be counted and others to do the counting, and that the arrangement was as natural as tide.

Jonah's father had been counted. That was the phrase the company used, in the end. *We lost a diver this season,* the foreman said, in April, not even looking up from his slate. *Ashgrove senior. Weighed and counted.* As if his father were stock. As if eleven years of diving, of bringing up shell worth more per ounce than silver, could be summed in a foreman's shrug and a number chalked on a board.

Eleven years. His father had come out under contract with Halloway & Finch when Jonah was nine, signing them both — that was how it worked, the family clause, the boy bound until nineteen or buyout, whichever came cheaper to the company. They'd been told the pearl grounds were rich and the season short and the pay such that a man could clear his debt in six years and send for passage home besides. None of it had been true, exactly. The pay was real but the deductions ate it: gear rental, food at company prices, the doctor's fee whether or not the doctor came, a fee for the room, a fee for the lamp, a fee, once, for *administrative irregularity*, which nobody could explain and nobody dared ask about twice. The debt never shrank. It was a hole with a ladder painted on the bottom.

And then, this season, the deaths.

Three divers gone since March. Not lost at sea — gone. Pulled up blue-lipped, or simply not pulled up at all, their lines cut loose by their own hands or by the panic of the men above. The old divers said the beds had been worked too deep too fast, that Corliss had pushed them past the safe marks to make quota, that a man's body kept its own ledger and settled accounts in blood. The company said weather. The company said carelessness. The company said, in the case of the third man, desertion — that Tammas Reeve had slipped away on a mainland boat and abandoned his contract, and that his debt would be pursued.

But Tammas Reeve had been fifty-one years old and afraid of deep water his whole life, which was why he'd been a shore-hand, and everyone on the island knew it, and everyone also knew better than to say it aloud.

Jonah's father had died in May, on a day so calm the water looked poured rather than lying there. No storm to blame. No weather. He'd gone down on the outer bank in forty feet, which was nothing, which was a depth he'd worked a thousand times, and he hadn't come up. They brought his body in at dusk and the foreman weighed and counted him and entered something on a slate, and Jonah, fourteen years old and suddenly the property of a company that had just consumed his father, stood on the quay and watched the number go onto the board and thought: *that isn't right.*

Not the death. The death was wrong in the way the sea is wrong, vast and indifferent, a thing you could rage at forever without changing it. What was wrong was smaller and sharper. The number. Because Jonah had counted the days himself, the way the letter told him to, and the days didn't match. His father had been paid out against the debt through the spring — Jonah had seen the entries, had copied them secretly into the back of his primer, because counting was the one thing he was good at and the one thing nobody thought to hide from a boy. By his arithmetic his father owed almost nothing by May. By the company's arithmetic, entered after the fact, he still owed two years' wages, which meant his effects could be held, which meant his letter and his knife and his shell technically belonged to Halloway & Finch, which meant the company had taken a dead man's small treasures and priced them against a debt that had already been paid.

Numbers could lie. Nobody had ever told him this. It was the sort of thing adults assumed you'd learn eventually, the way you learned the sea could kill you. But learning it from a slate on a quay, watching a dead man's life get rounded down to a figure that served the living men who held the chalk — that was a different education. That was the day Jonah Ashgrove stopped being a boy who trusted what was written down.

He sat on the cot now with his three possessions laid out in a row, and beyond the window the rain finally arrived, fat and warm, hammering the tin roof of the barracks annexe where he slept among the stores. The season was over. The luggers were hauled up on skids, the divers gone quiet, the drying racks empty, and the island had begun its long exhale into the wet months. Somewhere across the water the mainland hills had disappeared entirely behind the rain curtain, and the bridge, when the company walked it tomorrow to close the gates, would stand empty as a rib.

Five months sealed in with the people who owned him. Five months of ledgers and quotas and Corliss's mild voice saying *above your station*. He was used to it. He had survived four monsoons already. He told himself this the way you tell yourself the water is cold only at first.

Then he heard boots on the boardwalk, unhurried, and a voice calling through the rain — the clerk's voice, thin and precise, calling for someone to run to the office, the ink was out, the annual accounts wouldn't wait, and the voice said a name, and the name was his.

Jonah blew out the lamp, slid the knife and the shell into the oilcloth packet, and pressed the floorboard down over them. The letter stayed against his chest. He pulled on his coat and opened the door onto the rain, and the last thing he did before stepping out was touch the paper through his shirt, once, the way you'd touch a talisman, and go to fetch ink for the man whose typewriter never stopped talking.

What he would find beneath that typewriter's carriage, he could not yet imagine. But the rain had come early this year, and the island was already holding its breath.
