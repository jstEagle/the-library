## 3. Orders Come with the Rain

The rain did not arrive so much as take possession.

It came in the night, two full weeks before the almanac said it should, and by morning the world outside the barracks had been reduced to a grey hiss. The strait between Ashgrove Island and the mainland vanished entirely — no water, no far shore, just a curtain of water falling on water. The bridge, that rickety lattice of bamboo and chain that connected us to everything, swayed and shone and disappeared into the wet.

Jonah stood at the dormitory window with his forehead against the glass and watched the last man cross.

He was a mail runner, one of the Lascar men who carried dispatches to the mainland launch before the season closed. He moved carefully, bent low, hands gripping the guide rope, and halfway across he stopped and pressed himself flat while a gust tried to peel him off the planks like a label off a crate. Then he went on, small and stubborn, until the far end swallowed him and he was gone.

After him, four company men came out with mallets and lanterns and began unhooking the plank sections nearest the island. It was procedure. Every year at monsoon the bridge came apart — the storms would tear it to splinters otherwise — and every year it went back up when the winds turned. But watching those men work, methodical as ants dismantling something they'd built, Jonah felt the click of it in his chest: *closed*. The word had weight now. The door had shut behind them all.

Behind him, in the long room, fourteen divers' sons and deckhands were waking to rice and salt fish and the particular boredom of a stranded station. Someone was already arguing about cards. Someone else was crying quietly, which happened more this season than any before it, because there were beds that stayed made and bowls set out for men who weren't coming back, and everyone walked past them pretending not to count.

Jonah counted. He couldn't help it now.

Three weeks since the ledger. Three weeks since he'd knelt on the clerk's floor with the ink tin forgotten in his hand and read his father's name written three times in a column where it had no business being — once as *lost overboard*, twice as *deserted post* — and understood that the sea wasn't the only thing on this island that swallowed men whole. He had put the second ledger back beneath the typewriter's carriage exactly as he'd found it, aligned to the pencil mark Adaeze Okafor kept there, and he had walked back to the dive office with the ink and said nothing, and he had said nothing every day since, and the silence sat inside him like a swallowed stone.

He collected his bowl, ate standing up, and went to work.

* * *

The dive office smelled of lamp oil and mildew and, faintly, of the sea itself — brine worked so deep into the timber that no amount of scrubbing would ever get it out. Jonah swept. He fetched ink, as he always did; that errand had become his, and neither Mr. Prewitt the head clerk nor anyone else questioned why a boy was so punctual about a bottle of ink. He swept around the desk where the Remington sat under its canvas hood, and he did not look at it, and he looked at it constantly, the way you can't stop touching your tongue to a broken tooth.

The false ledger lay open on the side table, waiting for the day's entries. Season totals. Dive counts. Compensation disbursements. Neat figures in Mr. Prewitt's hand, each one a small tidy lie. Forty-one divers contracted for the season. Thirty-four currently drawing rations. Seven names that existed nowhere except in that book, where they had been converted from dead men into runaways.

His father was not among the seven. His father had died two seasons ago, and by now his name had migrated entirely out of the living columns and into the archives — but Jonah knew. He had seen the old pages. *Deserted post, March 1909.* His father, who had once swum out through a squall to cut a fouled anchor line because there was no one else, deserting anything? The lie was so lazy it was almost an insult, and that laziness was the worst part. They hadn't even needed a good lie. Who would ever check?

Nobody, was the answer. Nobody inside could check, because everybody inside belonged to Halloway & Finch — body, breath, and signature. Indentured men didn't testify. Indentured men barely counted as witnesses to their own lives.

At half past nine the rain doubled its fury, and Manager Corliss arrived.

You heard him before you saw him: the cane first, its iron ferrule striking the verandah boards in a rhythm like a slow clock, then the voice, carrying ahead of him the way weather does. He came into the office trailing two assistants and the smell of cigar smoke, and he took off his hat and shook it once, precisely, spraying the doorway rather than the floor, because Corliss never made a mess where Corliss had to stand.

"Paper," he said, and Mr. Prewitt scrambled.

What followed was announced as routine, delivered as routine, and received as routine by everyone in the room except Jonah, who felt the floor tilt very slightly under his feet, the way the ground goes strange just before a wave arrives.

"The annual evidence shipment," Corliss said, "will sail Friday next, tide permitting, aboard the *Meridian*. Magistrate Ellery has consented to make the crossing aboard her rather than wait for the packet steamer, which saves us six weeks of correspondence and saves His Majesty's service six weeks of per diem." A thin smile traveled the room without landing anywhere. "The seasonal records, the compensation rolls, and the incident summaries will be sealed in my presence Thursday evening. Prewitt, you'll prepare the manifest. Nothing leaves this office between now and then that isn't on that manifest."

"Yes, sir."

"Conditions on the crossing will be poor. That is irrelevant. The magistrate wishes to conclude the island's affairs before Christmas and I wish him to have everything he requires, correctly bound and correctly totaled." He drew on his glove, finger by finger. "Gentlemen, we are a company that keeps accurate books. Let us be certain the Crown sees exactly that."

He turned to go, and then, as if it were an afterthought, his eyes found Jonah in the corner with his broom.

They rested there a moment. Not hostile. Worse than hostile — *inventorying*. The way you look at a crate to confirm the stencil matches the contents.

"Ashgrove's boy," Corliss said.

"Sir." Jonah's voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect.

"You'll want to keep the floors dry this week. Paper and damp don't mix." And he left, cane ticking, the door banging shut behind him on a wall of rain.

Mr. Prewitt exhaled through his nose and began pulling files. Jonah swept. The broom moved, the dust gathered, and inside Jonah's skull a single thought turned over and over like a stone in a breaker:

*Friday.*

* * *

He thought about it all day, and all through the evening meal, and it assembled itself piece by piece the way a wreck comes apart — slowly, then all at once.

The evidence shipment. Of course. Every year the company sent its season records to the mainland assessor's office, and every year they came back stamped and filed and closed, the whole machinery of accountability running smooth as a pulley. The false ledger would go aboard the *Meridian* — bound, sealed, witnessed. It would sit on a magistrate's desk in some dry government room, and Ellery would read forty-one names and thirty-four survivors and seven desertions and find it all perfectly in order, because it was, in order, the way a well-made coffin is in order.

And the true ledger — the one hidden under the typewriter, the one with the real counts and the real deaths — would stay here. On an island with no bridge. In an office belonging to the company. Among witnesses who legally could not witness.

That was the shape of it. That was the trap entire, and it wasn't even a trap laid for him; it was simply how the machine ran, year after year, grinding men into *desertions* and shipping the arithmetic home to be approved. No one inside could testify against the false ledger because everyone inside was owned by the same hand that held the pen. The only people who knew the truth were a dead man's son and a frightened clerk, and neither of them could cross thirty miles of open water, and in eleven days the magistrate would read lies in good handwriting and sail away satisfied, and the season after next there would be a new list of names, and someday, maybe, a fourth line with *Ashgrove* in it.

Unless the true pages crossed instead.

The idea arrived not as courage but as arithmetic, which is how the biggest ideas come to boys who have spent a season learning what numbers can do. If the false ledger could travel, the true one could too. Papers didn't have faces. Papers didn't have contracts. A document, properly folded, properly carried, could pass through doors no indentured man could knock upon.

But someone had to carry it onto that ship. Someone small enough to be overlooked and known enough to be recognized, which meant someone exactly like him, and there were three places between this room and that gangway where men knew his face.

The rain hammered the roof like a fist on a door, hour after hour, and the barracks walls seemed to draw inward, the ceiling lowering, the air going thick. Jonah lay in his bunk listening to it and understood, finally, what the monsoon actually was. Not weather. A seal. The island was a locked room, and on Friday a ship was leaving it carrying the proof of everything, and the only way in or out ran past three men with orders and eyes.

Across the room, someone snored. Rain found the gap in the eaves and traced a cold line down the wall.

Eleven days, he thought. Two checkpoints he could see, and one he couldn't yet. A satchel that didn't exist yet. An ally he hadn't dared speak to since the night he found the ledger — Miss Okafor, up above the chandlery, who typed lies with steady hands and kept her pencil marks straight, and who must have been counting these same days from the other side of the compound, lying awake under this same drumming roof.

She had made the copies. He was almost sure she had made the copies. Which meant she had already decided something, or was deciding it now, tonight, while the bridge came apart plank by plank in the dark.

Tomorrow, Jonah told the rain. Tomorrow I'll climb the chandlery stair.

The stone in his chest had stopped feeling like drowning. It was starting to feel like ballast.
