## 4. Miss Okafor's Room Above the Chandlery

Adaeze Okafor heard the boy on the stairs before she saw him, because everyone on this island announced themselves whether they meant to or not. The steps were salt-swollen and sang under weight — a different note for every body. She had learned them all in eight months: Corliss's heavy tread, the divers' bare-footed whisper, the clerks' shuffling half-step of men who were paid too little to hurry.

This was a light step, trying to be lighter. A boy who did not want to be heard.

She slid the carbons back into their oilcloth sleeve and under the loose floorboard before he knocked. Three soft raps. Paused. Two more.

She opened the door a hand's width. Jonah Ashgrove stood in the rain-dark corridor with water beading in his hair, though it hadn't rained yet tonight — that was spray off the harbour, flung against the shutters by a wind that had been rising all day like something waking up.

"Miss Okafor." His voice cracked in the middle of her name, and he hated it; she could see him hating it.

"Boy from the counting house." She let the door open another inch. "It's near nine bells. Clerks are abed."

"I need to ask you something I can't ask inside the office."

That was true of nearly everything anyone asked anyone on this island, but she knew already — had known since the day she typed the word *desertion* over a dead man's name and felt the keys bite her fingers like they knew — that this boy's question would not be a small one.

She looked past him down the stairwell. Empty. The chandlery below was dark, its owner gone to his sister's on the east point until Sunday.

"You have two minutes," she said, "and you'll stand where I can see your hands."

* * *

The room was smaller than a ship's cabin and twice as tidy. A cot, a washstand, a typewriter under an oilcloth — *her* typewriter, the company's property loaned to her the way a man loans a mule, expecting it back unspoiled. A lamp with the wick trimmed low. And everywhere the smell of ink and coconut oil and, beneath it, the faint sweetness of the paper she kept where no clerk should keep anything.

Jonah stood just inside the door with his hands at his sides, palms out, the way you'd approach a dog you suspected of biting.

"My father," he said. "Elias Ashgrove. He dove the outer banks in April season."

"I know the name." She had typed it. She did not say so.

"The company says he deserted. Walked off the quay at night, took a dory, no one knows where." The boy's jaw worked. "My father couldn't swim to the horizon and he wouldn't leave me. So either the company is lying, or—" He stopped. He could not finish the sentence, and she respected him for not pretending he could.

"What do you want from me?"

"I want to know if the ledger is true."

There it was. Laid out on her floor between them like something gutted.

She could send him away. She had thought about sending herself away a hundred times — walking into the sea at low tide, or more practically, signing onto any ship that would take a coloured woman who could type ninety words a minute and keep her mouth shut, which was the whole of her market value out here. She stayed because leaving meant the lies won, and staying meant she might drown for knowing. Neither option had ever seemed like living.

"Sit down," she said.

* * *

She took up the floorboard while he watched, lifted out the oilcloth sleeve, and laid three sheets of thin black-backed paper on the cot. Carbons. The ghost-copies that came off the roller when you pressed hard enough and used the good ribbon — the ribbon she bought with her own wages, because the company's ribbon printed pale enough to deny.

"When Corliss dictates an entry," she said, "I type it once, into the fair ledger. But the machine remembers everything it touches. That's what most people don't understand about documents. Paper isn't a record of what happened. It's a record of what somebody wanted written down. And underneath — sometimes — there's what actually happened, pressed through."

Jonah picked up the top sheet as though it might burn him. She watched him read his father's name and watched fourteen years of a boy's believing come apart in his face, quietly, without sound.

"*Ashgrove, E.*" His voice was very flat. "*Lost overboard, logged as desertion per instruction.* Per instruction. Somebody wrote *per instruction.*"

"Somebody always does. Instructions protect the man who follows them. They think it makes it clean." She tapped the second sheet. "Look at the dates. Three entries, same name. Your father didn't die once, boy. On paper he died three times, in three different months, because the count had to balance at quarter-end and dead men are easier to move around than missing pearls."

"He went down in April. Once. One time."

"One time," she agreed. "But the log needed him twice more, so twice more he died. Numbers are obedient. That's why men like Corliss love them. A number never asks *who told you to write this?*"

The lamp guttered in the wind. Outside, the harbour had gone the colour of wet slate, and somewhere below, the chandlery's sign swung and knocked against its bracket, patient as a clock.

"Why do you have these?" Jonah asked. "Why keep them? You could burn them and be safe."

Safe. She almost laughed. "Because I typed the lie. Do you understand what that means? My hands made those words exist. If the lie stands, then I'm not a witness, I'm an author. These copies aren't courage, boy. They're the only confession I'll ever get to make." She gathered the sheets, squared their edges on her knee. "You came here to find out if the ledger is true. It is. Now you know, and knowing is a debt. What do you intend to pay?"

* * *

He sat very still for a long moment, and she let him. This was the part no one taught — that grief, given work, becomes something with edges.

"There's a ship coming," he said finally. "Bray told my father's old crew that the evidence shipment sails when the weather breaks. The false ledger goes aboard with the magistrate's seal on it, and once it reaches the mainland registry it's truth forever. Nobody from this island can testify — the bridge closes with the monsoon, nobody gets passage, nobody gets heard." He looked up. "Except paper. Paper travels."

She felt something turn over in her chest, slow and cold, like a hull coming about. The boy had understood in a week what she'd failed to act on in eight months: that the documents didn't need a witness standing in a courtroom. The documents *were* the witness. All they needed was passage.

"They search the mail satchel at the customs shed," she said slowly. "And again at the company gate. And Corliss walks the gangway himself for anything leaving on evidence day."

"I know."

"And every guard at every checkpoint knows your face, because you've delivered their tea and swept their floors for two years under indenture."

"I know that too."

"Then we don't hide the ledger. Hiding is for things that look hidden." She was standing now, moving without deciding to, pulling her sewing box from under the cot, and her hands had gone steady in the way they always did once a thing became inevitable. "We make it furniture. Mail goes to the magistrate every voyage — complaints, petitions, invoices. Crown post is sacred. Even Corliss hesitates before opening Crown post, and hesitation is a door."

Jonah watched her thread the needle. "You'd help me. You'd risk — all of this." He gestured at the room, the carbons, her life in its small square inches.

"I'd stop being useful to him," she said. "There's a difference, and I've been too long confusing the two." She looked at him properly then — this soaked, stubborn child with his dead father's name in his mouth — and said the true thing, because frightened people owe each other truth or nothing. "Understand what we are to each other, Jonah Ashgrove. You are the only person alive who knows what I kept. I am the only person alive who believes what you know. If they take you, they take me by morning. If they take me, you're a boy with a story and no proof. We are each other's only witnesses. There is no version of this where one of us is brave alone."

The wind chose that moment to arrive in earnest — the real wind, the monsoon's outrider, slamming the shutters so hard the lamp jumped. Below them the harbour began its long metallic muttering, chain against chain, boats worried on their moorings.

Jonah put out his hand. Not a child's handshake; something older, the grip of a man swearing before judges.

"Witnesses," he said.

"Together," she said, and took it.

Then, over his shoulder, through the gap in the shutters, she saw the light burning late in the counting-house window across the yard — Corliss's window — and saw the silhouette cross it twice, quick, agitated, and stop.

She blew out the lamp.
