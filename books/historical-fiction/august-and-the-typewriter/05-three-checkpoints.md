## 5. Three Checkpoints

We drew the route on the floor of Adaeze's room, in pencil, because pencil could be rubbed out with a sleeve.

She had a sheet of paper salvaged from a packing crate, and she smoothed it flat against the boards with both hands the way she smoothed everything — as if disorder were a personal insult. Then she drew the island in miniature: the long spine of the ridge, the company compound like a clenched fist at its heart, the chandlery, the barracks, and finally the quay curving out into black harbor water like a bent finger pointing home.

"Now," she said. "Tell me what you see between here and here." Her pencil tapped the compound, then the quay.

"Rain," I said.

"Jonah."

I looked at the map properly. I had walked that ground every day for four years, but I had never once thought of it as territory. It was just the way the world was shaped. Now she was asking me to see it the way the company saw it — as a thing with doors in it, and men standing at each one.

"Three places," I said slowly. "Three places where they can stop you."

"Say them."

"The customs shed, past the salt house. There's only one path down and it goes right by the door." I put my finger on it. "Sergeant Bray sits there most days. He knew my father."

Adaeze's pencil paused above the paper. She didn't ask. That was one of the things I had learned about her in the three nights since I'd come to her window with my father's name still wet on my tongue: she never asked for anything you hadn't already decided to give her.

"Next," she said.

"The company gate. The main gate, not the coal gate — the coal gate's chained since the season ended. There are always two men at the main gate, but one of them is enough to know me. I've carried crates through it since I was ten years old. Mr. Tam knows my face better than my own mother would have."

"And the last?"

I didn't want to say it. Saying it made it real, the way writing a thing in a ledger made it real, even if the thing was a lie. Especially then.

"The gangway," I said. "The mail satchel goes aboard by hand. Company rule. The purser's boy meets whoever carries it at the foot of the gangway, and Corliss is usually there to see the magistrate off proper. He'll be watching everything that goes up those planks."

"Corliss." She wrote the name and underlined it twice, hard enough that the pencil snapped. She sharpened it again without comment, and I understood that the breaking had not been entirely an accident.

* * *

That night we did nothing but talk, and talking turned out to be harder work than carrying ever was.

"Understand something," Adaeze said. She had lit no lamp; the rain against the shutters gave us a gray half-dark that felt safer than darkness, somehow, because things in it kept their edges. "The ledger alone proves nothing. A book of numbers is just a book of numbers until someone stands up and says *this is how they were made*. I typed those false entries. I can prove the machine that typed them, because every typewriter has a fingerprint — a bent letter, a heavy strike. Mine drops the crossbar on the H. Every H in that false ledger limps. The true ledger is written in Corliss's own hand, but the carbons—" she tapped her chest of papers "—the carbons came out of my machine. Put them side by side and the documents speak."

"Speak," I said. "Documents don't—"

"They do. You've just never listened before. Your father's world was the water; mine is paper. Paper is where they killed him, Jonah. So paper is where we fight them."

I thought of my father's letter, folded soft as cloth from four years of handling. He had been a man of water and weight and breath held against pressure, and he had died in ink, in columns, in a clerk's neat hand recording him as *deserted — presumed gone overland*. Gone overland. From an island. The lie was so lazy it was almost an insult, and that laziness was the thing that had finally made me unable to sleep.

"Then the papers go to the magistrate," I said.

"They go to the magistrate. And they get past three men who know your face to do it." She leaned back against the wall. "So we don't send your face. We send the satchel."

* * *

The mail satchel arrived the next morning inside a delivery of rope, coiled in the middle like a snake sleeping in a basket.

It was company property — the canvas kind, stamped HALLOWAY & FINCH over the crown, with a brass lock whose key hung on a nail in the clerk's office where any man with a chair and a minute could reach it. Once a fortnight it went out to the mainland stuffed with dive reports, requisitions, complaints nobody read. Once a fortnight it went aboard by hand, and once a fortnight nobody looked inside it, because the lock was the looking.

"That's the beauty and the stupidity of it," Adaeze said, turning it over in her lap. "The lock says *we have checked this*. The lock means no one else ever has to. Corliss trusts his own paperwork the way a drunk trusts his own legs." She worked the key and opened the mouth of the bag. Inside: the ordinary fortnight's mail, thin now the season had drowned. Two requisition sheets. A complaint from the cookhouse. A funeral bill.

"A funeral bill," I said.

"There's always a funeral bill." She said it flatly, and I understood she had typed dozens of them, each one a man, each one filed under weather or desertion or *misadventure*, and that she had signed her initials small in the corner of each page the way she initialed everything, because her one rebellion had to fit in a space the size of a thumbnail.

Then she took up her sewing kit, and I watched craft happen.

First she measured the ledger — my father's life and death and the lives and deaths of eleven other men, sixty pages in Corliss's crabbed hand — and frowned at its thickness. Too thick. It would make the lining stand away from the canvas like a swallowed meal. So she took it apart.

I nearly stopped her. Taking it apart felt like taking him apart. But she caught my wrist with her fingers, not roughly, just certainly, and said, "The binding is company binding. Company thread, company needle, company glue. If anyone unpicks that seam they'll know where it came from in one look. We rebuild it as something else. Trust me — I have taken apart many documents and I have never yet lost one."

So she took it apart. She split the ledger into three thin gatherings of twenty pages each, and she wrapped each gathering in wax cloth against the wet, and then she turned the satchel inside out and set to work along the back seam, where the canvas doubled over itself below the stamp.

"Here," she murmured, more to herself than to me. "Everyone looks at locks. Nobody looks at stitching. Locks say *I am hiding something*. Stitching says nothing at all." Her needle went in and out, fast and even, and the first gathering slid behind the doubled canvas and vanished, and when she ran her hand over the outside you would never know. The second followed. Then the third, and she sewed the seam closed with a stitch pattern copied exactly from the factory work two inches away, so that even a suspicious eye would see only what it expected: old canvas, old seams, old mail.

It took her two hours. She never hurried. Fear makes some people fast and stupid; it made her slow and exact, and I tried to learn that the way I'd once learned to hold my breath — by doing it until it wasn't a decision anymore.

When she finished, she weighed the bag in her hands and handed it to me.

"You'll carry it," she said.

"Me? They know my face at all three—"

"Exactly." She folded the satchel shut and locked it, and the little click of the brass was very loud in the small room. "At the shed, Bray will stop you because he knows you. At the gate, Tam will wave you through because he knows you. Familiarity isn't only a danger, Jonah — it's also a habit, and habits open gates. A stranger with a company satchel gets questioned. A company boy with a company satchel is furniture. We use what they built." She looked at me for a long moment. "And if it goes wrong — if you're taken — you know nothing about the contents. You were sent to deliver the fortnight's mail, as you've done a hundred times, and the key is on the nail where it always is, and you are fourteen years old and frightened, and every part of that is true, which is the best kind of lie. The truth, arranged differently."

"And you?"

"I'll be on the quay with the others seeing the magistrate off. Every free soul on this island will be at the railings, because a magistrate's visit is the only theatre we get. If they take you, I walk to the gangway and I tell Ellery myself. They can't stop us both without making the noise they're desperate to avoid." She said it steadily, but I saw her hands were pressed flat against her skirt, pressing down, as if to keep something in her from rising.

* * *

We rehearsed it for two days.

I walked the route in my head until my feet ached with walking nowhere: the salt house, the shed, Bray's table with its chipped mug and its manifest book. The gate, the smell of tar and horse, Tam's lazy salute. The quay, the gangway, the purser's boy with his clipboard he couldn't read. Adaeze made me say it aloud until the words wore smooth. *Fortnight's mail, sir. Fortnight's mail, sir.* She timed me against the ship's schedule — evidence shipment sails on the morning tide with the magistrate aboard, third day from now, weather willing, and the monsoon had stopped being willing about anything.

On the second evening she finished the last of it: the ordinary mail repacked on top, the funeral bill placed uppermost where an idle glance would land on it and find exactly the dullness it expected. She locked the bag and set it on the table between us, and for a moment neither of us touched it.

"Do you know what I keep thinking?" she said quietly. "That your father is in there. Under the canvas. Riding in a Halloway & Finch bag, wearing their stamp, going where they wanted him to go — except not where they wanted at all." She shook her head. "There's a justice in it that frightens me."

"He'd have liked it," I said. "My father. He'd have liked being trouble."

She almost smiled. Then the rain thickened against the shutters, and we both looked up, because rain had become a kind of language between us — and beneath the rain, faint but unmistakable, came another sound. Voices in the compound yard. Boots. And cutting through both, in Corliss's carrying drawl, an order that turned my stomach to stone:

"Move the sailing up. Tide tomorrow, dawn — magistrate's orders, storm coming. The satchel goes out with the *first* light."

Adaeze met my eyes across the table.

"Dawn," she said. "Then you leave tonight."
