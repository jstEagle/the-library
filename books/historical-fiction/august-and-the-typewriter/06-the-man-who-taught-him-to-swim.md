## 6. The Man Who Taught Him to Swim

The customs shed sat at the head of the quay like a man who had fallen asleep at his post and never been relieved. Its clapboards were silvered by eleven years of salt, its roof sagged under a beard of dead moss, and the Crown's painted crest on its door had faded to something that looked less like authority than a bruise. Jonah knew every plank of it. He had grown up in its shadow, waiting for his father to come off the water, and later — after — for Sergeant Bray to come out and say nothing at all, which was Bray's way of being kind.

Jonah was not supposed to be near it now. Nobody from the compound was supposed to be anywhere on the quay without a chit, and Corliss had made it plain that until the *Meridian* sailed, the whole waterfront belonged to Halloway & Finch as much as it ever had. But the mail satchel with the ledger sewn into its lining was already stowed behind Adaeze's loose floorboard, and there was nothing left to do but wait, and waiting, Jonah had learned, could rot you from the inside if you didn't walk it off.

So he walked. And his feet, which had their own loyalties, took him along the seawall past the net racks and the tar barrels, and set him down on the low stone bollard fifty yards from the shed, where he could watch Bray's window and pretend he wasn't watching anything.

He lasted eleven minutes.

"Boy."

The voice came from behind him, and Jonah's heart performed a maneuver he had no name for. He turned slowly, arranging his face into the dull blankness of a boy with nowhere to be and no reason worth asking about.

Sergeant Elias Bray stood with his hands in the pockets of his uniform coat, which had gone grey at the seams and which he wore the way some men wear apologies — constantly, and without quite meaning to. He was a big man going soft at the middle, with a face like a spade that had been left out in the weather, and eyes that had once missed nothing and now worked hard at missing things professionally.

"You're a long way from the compound," Bray said.

"I was walking."

"Walking." Bray said the word as though tasting it and finding it spoiled. "In the rain."

It was true; the drizzle had come on while Jonah sat, fine as sieved flour, beading on his shoulders. He hadn't noticed. On this island you stopped noticing rain the way fish stopped noticing salt.

"There's a curfew on the quay," Bray said. "Corliss posted it Tuesday. You can read?"

"I can read."

"Then you've read it." Bray didn't move closer. That was a thing about him — he never crowded anyone, not even boys, not even suspects. He stood at exactly the distance a man stands when he wants you to know he could come closer but is choosing, this once, not to. "Eleven minutes you've been sitting there."

"You counted."

"I count everything. It's the job." Bray looked past him at the shed, at the grey water beyond, where the *Meridian* rode at anchor with her evidence hold sealed and her magistrate aboard, patient as a knife in a drawer. "Your father used to sit on that same bollard. Did you know that? After a bad season. Just sat and watched the water like it owed him an explanation."

"I know," Jonah said. His voice came out smaller than he wanted.

* * *

Here was what nobody on the island talked about anymore, because talking about it cost more than silence:

In 1906, when Jonah was nine, he had nearly drowned in the channel off Turtle Point, dragged down by an undertow with a grip like a company contract. His father had gone in after him — fully clothed, boots and all — and hauled him up through the green dark, and on the beach afterward, coughing half the sea onto the sand, Jonah had heard his father laughing. Not at him. At the sheer size of the world's appetite.

The man who taught him the swimming proper, though, the man who stood in the shallows for three summers running making Jonah kick until his legs felt unstrung — that man was Sergeant Elias Bray. They had been friends, his father and Bray. Divers and customs men don't usually mix, but the two of them had shared a tent during the cyclone year, played endless games of draughts with a board scratched into a crate lid, drunk whatever the chandlery passed off as rum. When the deaths began — when the dive logs started filling with the word *deserted*, one line at a time, tidy as hymn verses — it was Bray his father went to.

And after his father became a line in a ledger, it was Bray who came to the compound gate, took off his cap, and stood there for a full minute saying nothing, which was Bray's way of being kind. It was Bray who paid, out of his own pocket and against all regulation, for a proper stone instead of the numbered concrete marker the company issued. It was Bray who taught Jonah, in the winter after, how to gut a fish and how to sit with grief without letting it teach you to hate — lessons, Jonah understood now, that Bray may have been teaching himself.

Bray had not come near the compound in four years.

"Why are you here?" Bray asked now. Not harshly. Wearyingly. The question of a man who already suspected the answer and was giving the boy time to build something better.

"For the air," Jonah said. "The compound smells of ink and boiled mutton."

That almost worked. Almost. Bray's mouth did the thing it did instead of smiling, one corner lifting like a door coming unstuck.

"Air." He nodded slowly at the sea. "Plenty of that." Then, quieter: "You've grown, Jonah."

Nobody called him Jonah. On the island he was *boy*, or *Ashgrove's lad*, or, in Adaeze's case, *you*, delivered flatly enough to mean a dozen things. Hearing his own name in Bray's mouth did something unfair to his chest.

"You knew my father," Jonah said. Careful. Testing the ice with each word. "You were his friend."

"I was."

"Friends tell each other things."

Bray looked at him then — really looked, the old look, the look that had missed nothing before it learned to look away — and for a moment Jonah thought: *he knows*. It rose in him like the undertow at Turtle Point, cold around the ankles. Does he know about the ledgers? Has someone talked? Is Adaeze safe?

But Bray only said, "Friends also bury each other. I've done more of the second than the first, these last years."

"What does that mean?"

"It means," Bray said, and here his voice dropped into something Jonah had never heard him use — not the sergeant's bark, not the swim teacher's patience, but a low, worn-down register, like a channel dredged by too many ships — "it means go home, boy. Whatever you're doing on this quay, whatever's turning over in your head — put it down. The season's over. The ship sails Friday. There's nothing on this waterfront for you but trouble with your name on it."

"And if the trouble has my father's name on it too?"

The words were out before Jonah could weigh them, hanging in the wet air between them like smoke. Bray went very still. A gull screamed somewhere out over the anchorage and got no answer. Rain ticked on the bollard, on the tar barrel, on the brim of the sergeant's cap.

"That," Bray said finally, "is an unlucky sentence."

"So is a ledger that says a man deserted when he's at the bottom of the channel."

He hadn't meant to say it. Or he had — some part of him, fourteen years old and drowning again in a slower way, needed it said aloud to a man who might still be his father's friend. But the instant it left him, Jonah saw the mistake for what it was. He had shown Bray the shape of what he carried. And Bray was customs at checkpoint one, and the satchel would pass within ten feet of this man's hands, and everything — Adaeze's carbons, his father's name, the whole fragile architecture of their plan — now balanced on whatever lived in Sergeant Bray's face.

Which was, for a long moment, unreadable as weather.

Then Bray stepped forward — closing, for the first time, the distance he always kept — and gripped Jonah's shoulder. His hand was enormous and heavy as an anchor fluke, and it was shaking. Slightly. Only slightly.

"You listen to me." Low. Fast. Urgent in a way that frightened Jonah more than shouting would have. "There are men on this island who'd drown their own mothers for a pension, and I am not going to stand here and find out which kind of man I am twice. Go home. Stay home. Let the ship carry what it carries, because—" and here his voice caught on something, snagged like a boot on coral— "because some things are already decided, and a boy who fights a decision gets measured for a concrete marker with a number on it. Do you hear me?"

Jonah heard. He heard everything underneath it too — the confession folded inside the warning, the knowledge wrapped in the refusal, *I know, I have always known, and I cannot, I will not, go home.*

"Yes, sergeant," he said.

"Good." Bray released his shoulder as though it burned. He straightened, and the sergeant came back into his posture like a man putting on a coat, button by brass button. "Curfew's in an hour. Anyone sees you on the quay again, I'll have to write it up. Understand?"

"Understand."

Bray turned toward the shed. Stopped. Didn't turn back — spoke to the rain instead, to the grey anchorage, to the sealed hold of the ship where the false ledger lay sleeping among the Crown's other papers.

"He kicked like a mule, your father. First time I ever took him out past the breakers. Clung to the gunwale and swore at me in two languages." A pause. "I think about that more than I think about most things."

Then he walked to the shed, climbed the two rotten steps, and shut the door behind him with the gentleness of a man closing a coffin.

* * *

Jonah didn't remember walking back. He arrived at the chandlery stairs with soaked boots and no memory of the streets between, the way you arrive at the surface after a long dive with no recollection of kicking.

Adaeze was at her table with a candle stub and a cup of tea she had forgotten to drink. She took one look at him and put down her pen.

"He knows," Jonah said. "Or he suspects. He told me to go home. He said—" He stopped, wringing rain from his sleeve, trying to get the sentence right. "He said some things are already decided."

Adaeze was quiet for a moment. In the candlelight her face was all planes and shadow, and Jonah watched her do the arithmetic — the same arithmetic she'd taught him to do with documents: what does a man gain by saying a thing, what does he lose, what is he afraid of, and who sent him.

"A man who knows nothing tells you to go home because you're breaking curfew," she said at last. "A man who knows something tells you to go home because he's begging you not to make him choose." She turned the cup a slow quarter-turn on its saucer. "Which do you think he was doing?"

"The second," Jonah said. And hated the small treacherous hope in it, and hated more the fear beside it: that kindness was just loyalty wearing a kinder coat, and that either one could be a trap with his name sewn into the lining.

"Then we have until Friday," Adaeze said, "to decide whether Sergeant Elias Bray is our worst danger or our last friend." She blew out the candle, and the dark came in close as deep water. "Because tomorrow, Jonah, the satchel goes to the post room — and the first hands it passes belong to him."
