## 7. A Bargain at the Company Gate

The rain came down the way it always did on the island — not falling so much as arriving, all at once, as if the sky had simply given up holding itself together. Adaeze Okafor stood beneath the corrugated awning of the company gate and watched the water sheet off the tin and thought: *I have lied to customs officers before. I can lie to this man.*

She almost believed it.

The satchel hung from her shoulder, heavy as a sleeping cat. Somewhere in its lining, flat against the canvas where her needle had gone through and through, lay sixty pages of the truth. She had sewn them in by lamplight with Jonah keeping watch at the window, a boy of fourteen trying to be brave in the particular way boys are brave — loudly, uselessly, with his whole face. She had sent him the long way round, past the customs shed, because checkpoint one belonged to Sergeant Bray and Bray knew Jonah's face better than God did. But no one watched her. No one ever watched her. That was the whole arithmetic of being Adaeze Okafor on this island: a clerk, a woman, a colonial subject three times over. Invisible until someone needed a signature.

Today someone needed more than a signature.

Corliss came out himself. She had expected an under-manager, a boy with a clipboard, but the manager of Halloway & Finch walked through his own gate into the rain-smell of the morning with his coat collar turned up and his hands behind his back, and he smiled at her the way men smile when they have already counted your price.

"Miss Okafor." Her name, pronounced correctly. That was the first move; she noted it. "You're up early."

"The mail doesn't wait for weather, Mr. Corliss."

"No," he said. "It doesn't." He gestured toward the guardhouse, a lean-to beside the gate with a stool, a brazier, a kettle that had never once been cleaned. "A moment out of the wet?"

Everything in her said no. Every instinct she owned — instincts sharpened across four years of typing what she was told to type — said *stand in the rain, keep the gate between you, keep witnesses*. But refusal was a document too, and it would be filed and read. She ducked under the lintel and set the satchel on her knee, not on the table, and kept her hand on it.

He poured tea he didn't drink. Small courtesies, offered like coins.

"I'll be direct," Corliss said. "You've been with us six years."

"Five years and nine months."

Something flickered in his eyes — approval, maybe. A liar who rounded down was a liar worth talking to. "Six years," he repeated, "and you've never once taken leave. Your contract ends this season. I know you've been writing to the mission society about passage." He let that sit. Everyone wrote to the mission society about passage; only the mission society's patience decided who sailed. "There's a berth on the evidence ship. Tomorrow's tide. Calcutta, then home passage if you want it. Lagos is a long way, I know, but the company pays as far as Freetown without being asked twice."

Home. The word did what he wanted it to do before she could stop it. She saw her mother's compound in the rain, saw cassava drying on mats, heard a language spoken at full volume without translation standing between her and it. Six years of English in her mouth had made a thin skin over all of that, and Corliss had put one fingernail under the edge of it, gently, like a man lifting paint to see what color was beneath.

"And in exchange," she said, keeping her voice level, "you'd want my freedom papers signed off clean. Early release. No debts recorded."

His smile widened a fraction. He thought she was negotiating. Good. Let him think that; negotiators reveal their sums.

"In exchange," he said, "I want anything irregular in your possession surrendered to me before you board. Papers, carbons, correspondence. Anything the clerical office produced that isn't reflected in the official files. I'm sure you understand — a magistrate coming aboard, we like our records tidy."

"Tidy," she said.

"You're an intelligent woman. You know exactly what I'm asking."

She looked into her tea. The truth was that she did know, and the deeper truth was that she knew something else besides: she had been waiting for this conversation the way one waits for a tooth to finish breaking. For four years she had typed *deserted* where a man had drowned, typed *returned early* where a man had never surfaced, and each keystroke had cost her something she could not name and could not get back. She had told herself it was survival, and it was. She had copied everything in secret, and told herself *that* was survival too, or conscience, or insurance — she had never settled on which lie suited her best.

Now the manager of Halloway & Finch sat across from her offering her the exact shape of her life back, and she found she hated him for it, and hated herself more, because some small honest part of her — the part that still spoke Yoruba in her dreams — wanted to take it. Wanted it so badly her hands ached. Take the berth, take the papers, hand over whatever copies he guessed at (he couldn't know about the lining; he was fishing, he had to be fishing), go home, grow old, tell no one. Survive. She was so tired. Survival had been her full-time occupation for six years and it paid nothing but itself.

"Mr. Corliss," she said, and set down the cup. "I'm a clerk. Irregular documents are my trade. Of course I have copies of things — every clerk does, or no clerk would ever be paid. If you want my working papers surrendered, that's a matter for a written requisition, countersigned, itemized. I don't hand company property to anyone at a guardhouse over tea. Not even to you." She paused, and delivered the beautiful lie with the flatness it deserved. "I have nothing irregular. My files balance."

*My files balance.* Four words, and they went out of her like birds released, winging straight to the gallows. Because they didn't balance. They balanced only if you counted the pages against her ribs, the carbon copies hidden in the false bottom of her sewing box and now, most of them, riding in a mail satchel toward a magistrate. What she had just told him was a masterpiece: technically deniable, precisely worded, warm with the credibility of a woman known for pedantry. It was the best lie she had ever told, and it came out of her the way handwriting comes out of the hand — easily, fluently, without consulting her.

That was what she hated. Not the danger. The ease.

Corliss studied her for a long moment. Rain hammered the guardhouse roof and filled the silence for both of them.

"Well," he said at last, rising. "I'm glad we understand each other. The offer stands until boarding, Miss Okafor. Berth, papers, passage. Tidiness is worth that, to me." At the flap of the tent he turned, half into the grey light, and added, without heat, almost kindly: "My wife's family had a saying about people who carry other people's secrets. It wasn't flattering."

Then he was gone through the rain toward the office, and Adaeze sat alone with two cold cups of tea and understood that the negotiation had not been about documents at all. It had been an announcement. *We know. We don't know what. Bring it, or lose everything.*

* * *

Her room above the chandlery held a bed, a trunk, a sewing box, a kerosene lamp, and forty-one books she had carried across two oceans, which was how she measured wealth now. She climbed to it that evening by the outside stair, in the dark, without a light, the way she had not done since her first year on the island when a drunk deckhand had made the mistake of waiting for her once. Old habits rose like floodwater. She stood in the black of her own doorway for a full minute, listening.

Nothing. Rain, and the sea working at the pilings below.

Inside, she lit the lamp — because a searched room noticed darkness, and she refused to look like a woman with something to hide, even to herself — and then she saw it. Small things. Always small things. That was the first lesson of searching and being searched: nobody moves the books right, nobody replaces the trunk lid at its original angle, nobody remembers the exact fold of a cloth.

Her trunk's hasp was latched a half-turn off true. The blue cloth that covered her worktable had been refolded lengthwise instead of crosswise. And on the floor by the bed, one long dark hair that was not hers — hers were pinned tight and always had been — lying in the seam of the boards like a sentence someone had dropped.

They had been gentle. They had been thorough. They had taken nothing, because there was nothing left to take: the carbons had gone into the satchel two nights ago, all but the three pages she had kept back — she could not say why, even to herself; perhaps because handing over everything felt like handing over herself, perhaps because a witness who surrenders all her evidence surrenders her importance, perhaps simple clerkish superstition, never file your only copy. Three pages, folded small inside the false bottom of the sewing box, under spools of thread the color of storm water.

She did not touch the box. She made herself tea instead. She sat at her table under the lamp and opened a ledger — the real one, the official one, the tidy one — and began entering tomorrow's requisitions in her small even hand, because that was what an innocent woman would be doing, and because her hands needed work the way other hands needed prayer.

And while she worked, she let herself feel the full weight of the morning. Not fear — fear she could carry, fear was just another load, you shifted it from hip to hip and walked on. It was the other thing. The bargain sitting in her chest like swallowed stone. *Berth, papers, passage home.* He had priced her silence generously, which meant he was afraid, which meant the pages mattered, which meant she had been right — and none of it warmed her, because the price had been real, and she had looked at it, and some traitorous cellar of herself had *considered.*

She had lied beautifully. She had always known she could. Six years of typing lies had taught her their grammar, their rhythm, where the commas went so a falsehood would pass any audit. What she had never admitted until tonight, alone above the sea with a lamp burning honest yellow, was that she was good at it. That the words had come out smooth. That a woman who can lie beautifully to save her life can, with no change of effort, learn to lie beautifully for worse reasons, and that the skill does not ask permission before it becomes a character.

Jonah would come at the second bell, as arranged, to walk her the dark stretch to the quay road. The boy trusted her — she had seen it arrive in stages over these weeks, shyly, the way trust comes to children who have been taught by indenture not to spend it. He believed she was doing this out of justice. She was doing it out of justice, yes. Also out of spite, also out of terror, also out of the cold bookkeeping fact that a witness who testifies keeps her skin. Justice was a bundle; you carried it however you could.

But she had told him one thing that was not yet true, and she sat with it now: *I will stand up in front of the magistrate and say my part.* She had promised that. And tonight, with Corliss's offer still warm in her ears — berth, papers, home, home, *home* — she understood that promises made by women who lie beautifully are worth exactly what they are backed by.

Below her window, footsteps on the chandlery stair. Too light for Jonah. Too deliberate for the wind.

Then a knock at her door, soft, and a boy's whisper pitched low to pass for a grown man's: "Miss Okafor? It's me. There's trouble at the customs shed — I need to tell you before second bell."

It was Jonah's voice. It was almost exactly Jonah's voice.

But Jonah would never knock.
