## 8. Bray's Choice

The rain woke Sergeant Tomas Bray before dawn, as it had every morning for eleven days, drumming the tin roof of the customs shed like a fist demanding entry. He lay a while in his cot with his boots on, which was a habit his wife would have mocked and his doctor forbade, and listened to the harbour working itself into a fury. Somewhere out there the *Meridian* rode at anchor, lamps burning yellow through the wet, and aboard her a magistrate who had not yet set foot on the island and would not until the tide turned.

Bray was fifty-three years old. He had spent nineteen of those years on this rock, checking manifests, weighing cargo, stamping papers for men who dove to the bottom of the sea and pulled up other men's fortunes. He had buried eleven divers from this station. He knew the number because he kept his own list, in pencil, in the back of his Bible, where no clerk's ink could reach it.

His list said fourteen.

That discrepancy had lived in him for three years like a splinter too deep to dig out. He had raised it once — once — with Corliss, early on, over brandy in the company house, and Corliss had smiled the way he did when a man asked a question that revealed more about the asker than the answer could ever reveal about anything else. *Desertions happen, Sergeant. Men run. Islands are close to the water.* And Bray, who had watched a diver's widow carry her husband's boots home in a sack because there was nothing left of him to fill them, had said nothing further, and hated himself for the silence, and told himself it was discretion.

He rose. He lit the lamp. He began the morning's work, which today meant the mail.

* * *

There was one satchel of outbound mail, sealed with wax at the post office the evening before, and by regulation — a regulation so old it predated him, and which he enforced precisely because it was one of the few things on this island nobody had yet thought to corrupt — every satchel leaving on an official sailing passed across his table and through his hands before it went aboard. The *Meridian* was carrying mail as well as a magistrate. Therefore the satchel came to Bray.

It weighed wrong.

He noticed it before he even set it down. A mail satchel has a certain heft, a soft shifting weight, letters settling like sand. This one had a spine. Something inside sat stiff and square against the canvas, and when he laid the bag flat under the lamplight the outline of it showed through the weave: a book, or books, bound tight against the fold of the strap.

Bray stood very still. Rain hammered the roof. Down the track toward the quay he could see the grey light coming up over the water and the shape of the gangway already rigged, lanterns swinging along its length.

He should send for Corliss. That was procedure too: any irregularity in outbound materials reported directly to the company manager, who was also, on paper, the island's resident magistrate's secretary, an arrangement Bray had never once found clean but had never found a way around either. Nineteen years of finding no way around things.

Instead he took out his knife and cut the seal.

Later he would tell himself it was duty. It was partly duty. It was also three years of a pencil-written list in the back of a Bible, and a splinter, and a widow with a sack of boots, and the simple animal fact that when something weighs wrong you want to know what's inside it.

The ledger came out stiffly, having been sewn — sewn, with needle and thread, someone had worked hard and fast — into the lining beneath the folded mail. It was a company ledger. He knew the binding; he'd carried enough of them to the incinerator at year's end. He opened it under the lamp with fingers gone suddenly clumsy.

Dive counts. Dates he recognized. Names he knew better than he wanted to.

And beside the columns, in a second hand, small and precise: the real ones.

He turned pages. The rain kept its rhythm. Bray read about his island as if reading about a place he had never visited, some foreign shore where men went down and did not come up and were written down honestly, where the word *drowned* appeared in a clerk's careful script instead of *deserted*, where numbers matched what widows knew in their bodies. He found the Okafor woman's initials in the margin of the false column — she typed for Corliss, everyone knew that — and he understood, with a lurch, that this book was a confession written twice: once by the company, once against it.

Then he turned a page and found a name he had been avoiding for six years.

Ashgrove.

Three entries. Three dates, each one a day Bray could have placed if pressed — the third of them the day the sea came up grey and stayed that way, and Jonah Ashgrove's father went down with four others and only two came up, and the company log called it equipment loss and desertion in the same breath, and Bray signed off on the manifest because signing off was his job, and put a line in his Bible, and drank half a bottle alone.

Jonah. Fourteen now. A quiet boy who fetched ink for clerks and looked at Bray sometimes with his father's eyes, which was unbearable, so Bray mostly managed not to look back.

The sergeant stood in his shed with the true ledger open before him and felt the whole architecture of his nineteen years settle around his shoulders like wet canvas. Here it was. Everything. Proof, in company ink, of the thing he had suspected, listed, buried, ignored. All he had to do was carry it to Corliss — or carry it nowhere — or carry it aboard the *Meridian* himself, walk it up the gangway, put it in Ellery's hands, and be done with three years of splinter.

He thought about all of it. He was a methodical man, and he thought about all of it, and the rain counted the seconds for him.

Then he thought about his pension.

* * *

It was a small thought, which is how such thoughts get in. Thirty-one months to a full pension. A cottage inland, near his sister, dry ground, no sea. He had spoken of it aloud maybe four times in nineteen years, but it lived in him the way the list lived in the Bible — private, kept, looked after. And standing there with the truth in his hands he understood with terrible clarity exactly how the next hour could go: he reports the irregularity, Corliss smooths it, the sailing proceeds, and Bray becomes a problem to be handled, and problems on this island were handled with postings and withheld pay and quiet references lost in transit. Or worse. Men who knew too much about dive logs had a way of learning to dive.

Or: he reseals the bag, stamps it through, tells himself the magistrate will do whatever magistrates do, and collects his pension on dry ground while his knees still work.

He resealed the bag. He used his own wax, his own stamp, pressing the crest deep so the repair read as original. His hands were steady, which shamed him more than shaking would have. Then he hung the satchel on its hook, took down his coat, and walked through the rain to the company house, because there was one conversation he still intended to have — one last transaction between whatever he had been and whatever he was about to become.

* * *

Corliss received him in the office behind the counting room, where the shutters were battened against the monsoon and the lamplight fell on ledgers stacked like masonry. The manager looked up without surprise. He never seemed surprised; it was his chief talent.

"Sergeant. Early for our usual."

"The mail satchel weighed wrong," Bray said. "I opened it per regulation. There was a second ledger inside. The true counts." He laid it flat on the desk between them — he had brought it, he realized, so that there would be no pretending afterward, no version of this morning where either of them could claim ignorance. "I've come to tell you I'm putting it back and passing it through. I want you to know that I know. That's all I came to say."

Silence, except the rain. Corliss studied the ledger the way a jeweller studies a stone, then looked up, and his face did something Bray had never seen it do: it settled into plainness, all the polish drained out, and what remained was tired and almost human.

"Your Bible list," Corliss said quietly. "Fourteen names. You think I don't know about your list?"

Bray's stomach dropped through the floorboards of the world.

"Eleven is what goes to the mainland, Sergeant. Eleven is arithmetic. Fourteen is a grievance, and grievances sink ships, and ships carry pearl revenue, and revenue keeps two hundred people on this island fed through a monsoon. You are not the first good man to stand in this room holding the difference between eleven and fourteen." Corliss rose, poured two measures of brandy without asking, and set one at Bray's hand. "You're just the first who announced it beforehand. Which I confess I find restful. Dishonesty is exhausting; candour I can work with."

"I'm not for sale."

"No," Corliss agreed mildly. "You're for retiring. There's a difference, and it flatters you." He sat again, folded his hands. "Full pension, effective at the end of this sailing. Backdated to include the monsoon quarter at hardship rates. The cottage allowance. Your sister's parish has a school needing a caretaker; I'll write the reference myself, and my references travel." He slid a sheet across the desk — already drafted, already signed, dated tomorrow. Bray stared at it and understood that this conversation had been prepared the way a grave is prepared: in advance, by someone who knew the measurements.

"All you must do," Corliss said, "is what you have done for nineteen years. Stand at your table. Weigh what comes across it. Stamp it through. If the boy comes to your shed with that satchel — and he will, Bray, he's his father's son — you will look at him the way you look at every manifest, and you will let the paperwork decide. Can you do that? Can you let the paperwork decide?"

Bray looked at the brandy. He did not drink it. He picked up the pen because his hand decided before he did, the way a man's hand reaches for a rail when the deck tilts, and he signed his name under Corliss's, and the nib scratched like a small animal.

"There's a boy," Bray said, standing, his voice gone gravel. "Jonah Ashgrove. Whatever happens at that quay—"

"Whatever happens," Corliss said pleasantly, "will happen according to procedure."

Bray walked back through the rain to his shed. The satchel hung where he'd left it. He lifted it, tested the weight — the wrongness of it, the stiff square spine of the truth riding against canvas — and hung it back on its hook, stamped and sealed, cleared for the *Meridian*, cleared for the gangway, cleared for whatever hands would carry it up the plank into the sight of a magistrate who did not yet know what was coming across his own mail.

Then Bray sat down at his table, took out his Bible, turned to the back page, and added a fifteenth line to the list in pencil.

*My own,* he wrote. *This date.*

Out on the grey water, the tide had begun to turn, and down the track from the chandlery, small through the rain, came a boy carrying a mail satchel that weighed wrong — and Bray, good man, comfortable man, rose to meet him and let the paperwork decide.
