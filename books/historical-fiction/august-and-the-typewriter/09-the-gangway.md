## 9. The Gangway

The rain had thinned to a fine drifting mist by the time I reached the quay, the kind of rain that didn't fall so much as hang in the air like a held breath. Beyond the harbour wall the *Meridian Star* sat at anchor, her lights blurred to smears of gold, her funnel breathing smoke into a sky the colour of wet slate. She was scheduled to lift with the evening tide, and the tide was already turning. I could feel it in the water slapping against the pilings — quicker now, restless, as if the sea itself knew there was business to finish before dark.

The mail satchel hung from my shoulder on a strap that crossed my chest. Adaeze had sewn the true ledger into its lining three nights ago, working by candlelight with sailmaker's thread and a curved needle, and she'd done it so well that when I ran my fingers along the canvas all I felt was canvas. But I felt it anyway. Every step from Miss Okafor's room above the chandlery down through the sleeping town, I carried that book the way you carry a burn — not heavy, exactly, but impossible to forget.

Fourteen pounds of paper. Forty-one names. My father's name, three times over.

*You don't have to be brave,* Adaeze had said at the door, pressing the strap into my hands. *You only have to be exact. Brave is for soldiers. Exact is for witnesses.*

I said the words over to myself like a rope thrown across water: *exact, exact, exact.* And then I came around the corner of the customs shed and saw Sergeant Bray standing at the foot of the gangway, and the rope went slack in my mind.

He wasn't supposed to be here. Bray's post was checkpoint one, the shed, half a mile back along the quay. But there he was, hat dripping, lantern raised, planted at the base of the gangway like a fencepost driven deep. He saw me the moment I saw him. Of course he did. He'd known my face since before I could write it.

"Jonah." My name in his mouth sounded like something he was chewing. "What are you doing out, lad?"

"Carrying the mail," I said. It was even true. Adaeze had signed the satchel into the mail run that morning under her clerk's seal; officially, it was company correspondence bound for the mainland office, and officially, a junior indentured clerk could carry it. Officially covered every inch of this plan the way oilcloth covers cargo — thin, but it keeps off most of the rain.

Bray looked at the satchel. Then he looked past me, up the dark length of the quay, as if expecting Corliss or one of his men to come striding out of the mist behind me. When no one did, he looked back, and something moved across his face that I hadn't seen on an adult before — not anger, not suspicion. Shame, arriving late and finding nowhere to sit.

"You should go home," he said quietly.

"I can't."

"Jonah—"

"You opened it." I don't know where the words came from. They came out level, which frightened me more than shouting would have. "The satchel. You opened it two days ago at your shed, and you looked inside, and you sewed it shut again. Didn't you."

His jaw worked. The lantern hissed as a drop of water found the flame. Behind him, a ship's officer leaned over the gangway rail and called something down about mails for the tender, five minutes, and the sound of it broke the moment open like an egg.

"Yes," Bray said finally. "I opened it."

"And you gave me to Corliss anyway."

"I took what was owed me," he said, and his voice cracked down the middle like dry timber. "Thirty years, boy. Thirty years, and they offered me a pension and a cottage in Weymouth, and I—" He stopped. He straightened. When he spoke again his voice was flat and hard, a soldier's voice. "I stood aside at my checkpoint because it isn't mine anymore. But hear me, Jonah Ashgrove. That man is on that ship, and he will stop you at the top of that gangway, and he does not care about you the way I do. He'll take that satchel and put you in the hold until the season turns, and no one will ask after you until Christmas. Go home. Live. Let it be enough that the pages exist. Someone else—"

"There is no someone else," I said. "There's only me."

I stepped past him. He did not reach for me. I heard him exhale, long and slow, the sound of a man setting down something he'd carried too far, and then his boots followed mine on the wet planks — not to stop me, I understood, but to stand near me, which was all he had left to give.

The gangway rose ahead of us, narrow and slick, strung with a single line of electric bulbs that burned yellow through the mist. At the top of it, silhouetted against the deck lights, stood a figure in a dark coat with his hands clasped behind his back, waiting.

He had been waiting all along.

* * *

Corliss came down three steps to meet me, which I hadn't expected. Managers don't descend toward clerks; they summon. But here he was, boots ringing on the plates, and up close I could see he'd been drinking — not drunk, never that, but sharpened by it, the way a whetstone sharpens without softening.

"Well," he said. "The Ashgrove boy. Carrying the mail." He put out his hand, palm up. It was a small gesture and enormous, the whole architecture of the company compressed into an open palm: give it to me, because I am the company, and you are nothing but a debt we haven't yet collected. "Hand it over, Jonah. There's been an irregularity reported. Irregular documents don't travel under Halloway & Finch seal."

My heart was going so fast I could feel it in my teeth. Every plan Adaeze and I had made assumed I might get past Bray with a lie. None of them had accounted for Corliss himself, standing between me and the deck of the only ship that could carry the truth off this island before the monsoon sealed it for eight months.

Eight months. Long enough to search every room twice. Long enough for Adaeze to be put ashore somewhere quiet. Long enough for me to be transferred inland, my indenture sold onward, my name lost in another ledger.

*I only have to be exact.*

"The satchel is Crown mail," I said.

Corliss blinked. For one full second, the sentence simply didn't parse — and I watched it fail to parse, watched the confusion land, and understood in that instant what Adaeze had meant when she said documents speak. Words are documents too. Spoken aloud, in the right order, they bind.

"It's entered in the mail register," I went on, and my voice didn't shake, which amazed me. "Under the postal act, once mail is registered it's Crown property in transit. Interfering with it is a felony. Not a company matter. A Crown matter. You can search your own ledgers all night, Mr. Corliss. This one you can't touch without a warrant — and there's a magistrate aboard who issues them."

Behind me, Bray made a sound that might have been a laugh bitten in half.

Corliss recovered slowly, the way ice recovers over a hole. "Magistrate Ellery is aboard as a passenger," he said softly, "at the invitation of the company whose property you're stealing. Do you imagine he'll take the word of an indentured boy over mine? Give me the bag, Jonah, and I'll forget your name by morning. Keep it, and I promise you—" he leaned close, and I could smell the whisky and the bay rum beneath it— "you'll spend the rest of your indenture in a place where nobody remembers names at all."

The mist drifted between us. Down at the foot of the gangway, Bray stood with his lantern raised, lighting the scene like a man obliged to witness a hanging.

And I heard my father's voice, absurdly, from ten years and a world away: *Deep water doesn't scare, son. Shallow water scares, because in shallow water you can touch bottom and still drown.* Corliss was shallow water. He was touching bottom with every word. Men who are winning don't threaten fourteen-year-olds on gangways.

"Then arrest me," I said. "Loudly. Here. In front of the mails crew, the tender officer, and the magistrate's own steward, who is standing at that rail right now watching you threaten the Crown's mail."

It was half a bluff. The steward was there — a small grey man leaning on the rail, and I'd marked him the moment I boarded the gangway's light — but whether he'd heard anything was beyond my knowing. It didn't matter. Because Corliss turned to look, and turning was losing, and we both knew it the moment his shoulders moved.

The grey man at the rail straightened. And then, unhurried, with the mild irritation of a gentleman interrupted at dinner, Magistrate Ellery himself came down the gangway toward us.

* * *

He was smaller than I'd imagined, and older, with a scholar's stoop and eyes that went first to the satchel, then to the register tag knotted at its buckle, then to my face — in that order, and I could have kissed Adaeze for insisting on the tag, properly tied, properly sealed, properly *exact*.

"Crown mail?" he said.

"Registered this morning, sir," I said. "Under the company seal, which is precisely the problem." And then I heard myself add, in a voice that belonged to some braver version of me standing slightly to my left: "Begging your pardon, Magistrate, but before this satchel goes any further aboard, you should know what's sewn into its lining. There are forty-one names in there. My father's is one of them. He didn't desert. He drowned. They wrote 'desertion' because drowning costs money and desertion doesn't."

The mist hung. The ship's engine room clanged somewhere below our feet. Corliss said, "Magistrate, this is outrageous, the boy is deranged, he's—"

"Mr. Corliss." Ellery did not raise his voice. He simply stopped the other man's sentence the way a lock stops a door. Then he held out his hand to me, palm up — and it was nothing at all like Corliss's palm, though I couldn't have said why, except that one asked and one demanded.

"A knife," the magistrate said, "if one of you gentlemen carries one."

Bray stepped forward and put his customs knife in Ellery's hand, handle first.

The magistrate turned the satchel over, found the seam Adaeze had stitched, and cut it open with three careful strokes while Corliss stood frozen mid-protest and the tide turned beneath us, the whole harbour swinging almost imperceptibly toward the sea. Rain beaded on the open ledger's cover. Ellery wiped it with his sleeve, read the first page, and went very still.

"Forty-one," he said at last, quietly, to no one.

"Forty-one," I said.

He closed the ledger, tucked it under his arm like a schoolbook, and looked up the gangway at the lit deck of the *Meridian Star* — at the evidence crates already stowed below, at Corliss's white face, at the whole floating machinery of the lie preparing to sail.

"Mr. Corliss," said Magistrate Ellery pleasantly, "you will accompany me aboard. Sergeant — my compliments to the ship's master: nothing leaves this harbour tonight."

And above us, one by one, the gangway lights began to come on along their full length, as if the ship itself had decided to watch.
