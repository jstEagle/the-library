## 10. Morning Tide

The rain had thinned to a fine grey mist by the time Magistrate Ellery came up on deck, and the *Kestrel's Fortune* lay at her moorings like a dog that knew it was about to be kicked. Ellery was a narrow man, sixty or thereabouts, with the kind of face that had been assembled for reading rather than being read. He carried the mail satchel with both hands.

He had not wanted to open it on deck. He had argued, briefly and without heat, that Crown mail ought to be examined in his cabin, in private, with due process arranged like furniture around him. But Jonah Ashgrove — fourteen years old, salt-crusted, soaked to the ribs, standing at the foot of the gangway with a company manager's hand clamped toward his shoulder — had said the words that settled it: *"Then everyone will watch you carry it below and wonder what you found."*

So Ellery opened it on deck, under an awning stiff with damp, with the mist beading on the ledger's cover like sweat.

The false ledger was on top. He read it the way he read everything — quickly, lips barely moving. Dive counts. Season tallies. A neat column of desertions. He set it down.

"There is another beneath it," Jonah said. "Sewn into the lining. Miss Okafor did the sewing."

Corliss made a sound that began as a laugh and died as something else.

Ellery worked the stitching free with his penknife. The true ledger emerged in pieces — pages cut from their binding, folded flat, wrapped in oilcloth against the wet. He unfolded them across the chart table and weighted the corners with his inkwell, a brass compass, a cup of cold tea.

Jonah watched him read.

He had imagined this moment for six days, through three checkpoints and one act of betrayal, and in every imagining it had felt like thunder — a great noise, a great light. What actually happened was smaller and worse. Ellery read. His finger moved down the columns. At some point he took off his spectacles, cleaned them on his handkerchief, put them back on, and kept reading, and it was the cleaning of the spectacles that told Jonah the numbers were landing where they should.

"Desertion," Ellery said at last. He said it to no one. "Thirty-one men listed as desertion over four seasons." He turned a page. "Divers do not desert. There is nowhere to desert *to*."

"The bridge closes," Jonah said. "The island closes. Everyone knows—"

"I know what everyone knows," Ellery said, mildly. "That is my profession."

He read for another quarter hour. Corliss stood at a distance calculated to suggest disinterest, and failed at it completely. The anchor detail waited by the capstan. Somewhere below, the cook banged a pot.

Then Ellery looked up, past Jonah, past Corliss, at Sergeant Bray, who had come aboard uninvited and stood dripping near the rail like a man attending his own trial.

"Sergeant. You inspected this satchel at the customs shed."

"Yes, sir."

"And?"

Bray's throat worked. Jonah watched him — the man who had taught his father to swim, who had resealed the satchel with wax and a steady hand, who had stood aside at the gangway that morning with eyes that would not meet a boy's.

"And I found these pages, sir. I understood what they were. I sealed them again and said nothing."

"You said nothing," Ellery repeated.

"To Mr. Corliss. Who offered me—" Bray stopped. Started again, because whatever he was going to say first had been a lie, and he seemed to have run out of room for those. "Who bought me, sir. With a pension. I took it. Then I stood aside anyway, this morning, which does not balance anything. I only want it written down that I knew. Put my name in your book."

And he did have a name in the book — Ellery wrote it himself, in pencil, while the mist came down and the tide turned, and Bray stood very straight and gave it to him: rank, years of service, dates, amounts. It was, Jonah thought later, the most orderly confession he would ever witness, delivered by a man emptying himself out item by item like a customs manifest.

* * *

Adaeze Okafor came aboard at half past seven, because Ellery sent for her, and she climbed the gangway with her chin level and her hands still.

She had not slept. Jonah could see it in the bruised skin beneath her eyes, in the way her room above the chandlery showed behind her — drawers pulled out, mattress slit, her few papers strewn and pawed. Corliss's men had come in the night, after her lie at the gate, and found nothing but the mess they made. She had sat among the wreckage until dawn, Jonah learned afterward, re-reading her carbon copies by candlelight, making sure every page matched every other page.

"Miss Okafor," Ellery said. "You typed the entries in this ledger."

"I typed all the entries in all the ledgers, sir. That is my employment." Her voice was even. Only her hands, folded very tight, betrayed her. "The false ones I typed under instruction from Mr. Corliss, who instructed me in writing, because he believed paper was safe if it stayed in the office. Men who falsify accounts always believe this. It is their one consistent error."

She produced her carbines — thin onionskin sheets, ghost-images of every entry, every instruction, every order given verbally and confirmed by memo — from a canvas roll she had worn beneath her dress for eleven days. She laid them on the chart table beside the true ledger and matched them, sheet to page, date to date, initial to initial, and she did it aloud, in a clear clerk's voice, and Jonah understood that documents speak, that Adaeze had taught him this and now performed it before the whole deck: *paper remembers, paper repeats, paper does not forgive.*

Corliss interrupted twice. Both times Ellery raised one finger, without looking up, and both times Corliss stopped as though the finger were iron.

When she finished, Ellery asked her only one question. "Why did you keep the copies? You could have been safer without them."

Adaeze looked at Jonah. It was a small thing, that look, and it lasted less than a breath.

"Because someone had to survive who remembered," she said. "And because the dead cannot testify, so the living must file paperwork on their behalf."

* * *

They arrested Corliss before the anchor lifted.

It was not dramatic. That was the thing Jonah could never explain afterward to anyone who asked. Ellery simply read out the warrant provisions — emergency powers, evidence of conspiracy to conceal deaths, obstruction of a Crown inquiry — and two of the magistrate's own marines, who had been aboard all along in plain clothes, walked Corliss to the aft hold and shut the door. Corliss talked the whole way. About procedure, about his rights, about twenty-two years of service to Halloway & Finch. His voice went on behind the closed door for some time, muffled, like rain on the far side of a roof.

The agents ashore were taken by noon — the paymaster, the boat foreman, the surgeon who had signed the false causes of death. Ellery dispatched Bray to make the arrests, which was either punishment or penance or both; Bray went with his hat straight and his jaw set, and did not come back to say goodbye, and Jonah decided he could live with that. Some men are best remembered mid-task, doing the hard correct thing too late.

The *Kestrel's Fortune* sailed on the afternoon tide with the true ledger, the carbons, two witnesses, and three prisoners aboard. Jonah stood on the quay and watched the sails fill and did not feel triumphant. He felt the way the beach feels when the wave withdraws — scoured, exposed, waiting to learn what the sea leaves behind.

Adaeze had sailed with it. She had shaken his hand at the gangway, formally, clerk to witness, and then ruined the formality by gripping hard and saying, "You write them letters, boy. Every name. Their families deserve better paper than mine."

Then the island was quiet, and the monsoon settled in properly, and there was nothing to do but wait for the bridge.

* * *

It reopened on a Tuesday.

A bell rang at the customs shed at first light — Bray ringing it himself, arms over his head like a man calling church — and by mid-morning the first carts rolled onto the causeway planks, and people stood at both ends staring across at each other as though the water had parted and might close again out of spite.

Jonah packed in under an hour, because he owned almost nothing.

The knife. The shell — spiral, pink-throated, the one his father had brought up from thirty fathoms the last good season, saying *even down there things make a home.* And the letter. His father's last letter, soft as cloth now from handling, its ink gone brown at the folds. He did not reread it on the quay. He knew it whole. He carried it inside his shirt where it had lived for months, over the heartbeat, like a second pulse that knew more than he did.

His indenture was voided — Ellery had seen to it before sailing, a single line in a single order, *contract annulled by act of Crown inquiry,* and Jonah had read that line perhaps forty times. Fourteen years old and unowned. It felt less like freedom than like a door suddenly open in a house he'd already learned to walk through blind.

At the foot of the bridge, Bray was waiting. They regarded each other across a yard of wet planking.

"They'll want you on the mainland," Bray said. "To testify. The assizes."

"I know."

"Your father—" Bray stopped. His voice did something Jonah had never heard it do. "Your father swam like a stone and laughed about it. I taught him the laughing part. I should have—" He didn't finish. He put out his hand instead.

Jonah looked at it. Thought about leaving it there.

Then he shook it, once, brief and hard, the way you let go of a rope.

He walked out onto the bridge. The planks were slick with mist and sounded like drums under his boots. Behind him the island shrank — the chandlery, the sheds, the dormitory where the divers' bunks stood stripped and bare for a season that would begin again under different owners or none at all. Ahead of him the causeway ran grey and endless into the mainland haze, into a country he had crossed oceans beside but never once set foot in, fourteen years old with a knife, a shell, a letter, and a name that belonged to nobody now but him.

Halfway across, the wind came up off the water and pushed at his back like a hand between the shoulders, and Jonah Ashgrove — diver's son, ledger thief, witness — started to run.
