## 1. The Weight of Paper

The lamp on the copying desk had a habit of guttering at half past nine, and Beatrix Okafor had long ago stopped trying to fix it. There was something honest about a light that admitted its limits. It flickered, it dimmed, and then—because the wick was trimmed properly, because Marisol saw to that—it steadied and burned on. Beatrix liked to think the household was the same way.

The shop smelled of glue and old linen and the particular dust that rises from cut paper, a smell so familiar that Beatrix no longer noticed it except when returning from a delivery, when the door would open and the whole history of the place would rush out like a held breath released. Forty years an atlas-maker's shop; six years a courier post nobody official knew about. The front room still displayed its brass instruments and its wall of framed maps—the coastlines of Vessany rendered in fading blues, the river systems in patient greens—and behind the counter, through a door marked *Private*, the copying desk did its quieter work: dispatches transcribed, sealed, and carried downriver by a person whom no one, anywhere, could describe with any confidence.

That was the craft of it. Not the penmanship, though Beatrix's hand was fine enough—a small, even script that slanted neither left nor right, that gave away nothing. The craft was being unremarkable. A courier who was memorable was a courier who got followed home. So Beatrix had spent six years sanding down every edge of distinction: the hair kept plain, the clothes mended but never patched in any way worth commenting on, the face arranged into an expression of mild, forgettable competence. In Callowmere there were perhaps four hundred people, and Beatrix had made certain that at least three hundred and ninety of them would struggle, if asked, to say anything about the courier from the atlas shop beyond *reliable* and *quiet* and *always seems to be going somewhere*.

It was, Beatrix thought now, dipping the pen and beginning the evening's transcription, a strange kind of ambition. Most people wanted to be seen. Beatrix had built a life on the discipline of not being seen, and had come to find a real comfort in it—the comfort of a stone worn smooth by water, of a name spoken so rarely it had almost shed its weight. *Beatrix Okafor.* Even the name sounded better unspoken.

The dispatch was routine. Grain quotas for the southern districts, a reshuffling of toll schedules on the lower Vess, a circular reminding magistrates that harvest census forms were due by month's end. Beatrix copied each in turn onto fresh stock, matching the clerk's cramped hand as closely as honesty allowed—for the copies went to the people who needed to know what the government was saying, and the government said one thing to its districts and another to itself, and someone in the towns downstream deserved to hear both versions. That was the whole of the work. Not treason, exactly. Translation.

Outside, the river moved in the dark with its low continuous voice, and somewhere upstream a barge horn sounded, once, and faded.

"You're burning the midnight oil again," said Marisol from the doorway, though it wasn't oil and they both knew it. She stood with her arms folded, a flour-dusted cloth over her shoulder, her grey hair pinned up in the style she'd worn since before Beatrix met her and would wear, presumably, until the end of the world. "Supper's ready. Whatever you're copying will still be true tomorrow."

"Quotas," Beatrix said. "Tolls. A census reminder."

"A census." Marisol snorted, a sound of profound administrative contempt. "As if anyone in this town has ever been counted correctly in their lives."

She said it lightly. Beatrix laughed, and the laugh was easy, and neither of them knew yet what the sentence would come to mean.

* * *

Supper at the old atlas-maker's shop was served at a table built for displaying folios, which meant it was too long, too low, and slightly warped in the middle, so that gravy migrated toward Petra's place at the center the way all troubles in the household eventually migrated toward Petra. Seven chairs, seven places, none of them matching. Marisol at the head because she cooked and therefore ruled. Tomas at the far end near the window because he liked to check the light against his memory of the light. Between them: Dev the deserter, who ate like a man making up for two bad years; Hanne, who had arrived with nothing but a suitcase of other people's letters and stayed to become the household's conscience and its worst singer; Old Wren, who was not actually old but had earned the name by refusing, at thirty-one, to discuss his past; and Petra, fifteen, elbows on the table, spooning up the gravy pool with the shameless efficiency of youth.

"—and I'm telling you, the miller's boy heard it from the toll-keeper, who heard it from a bargeman off the Kessel run," Dev was saying, gesturing with his fork. "Grey uniforms. Real ones, not militia grey. Army grey. They requisitioned the granaries at Tarnholt."

"Tarnholt is forty miles upriver," said Hanne. "And the miller's boy would say the sky was falling if he thought it would get him invited to supper twice."

"They took the bridges at Tarnholt," Dev insisted. "Bridges don't get taken by rumor."

"Bridges get taken by floods, too," said Hanne, "and nobody panics about floods."

"People panic about floods constantly," said Old Wren mildly, into his plate.

"The point," Dev pressed, "is that war doesn't stay upriver forever. Rivers flow downhill. Everything comes down the Vess eventually—grain, taxes, disease, armies. Ask any historian."

"I am asking a historian," said Hanne, nodding at Tomas. "Tomas. Is there going to be a war?"

Tomas had been quiet through all of it, turning his wine glass slowly by its stem, watching the candlelight move in the red. He was a precise man, Tomas, in everything except his own history; he could draw a coastline from memory that surveyors wept over, and he could not, or would not, explain how a cartographer of national reputation came to be disgraced, ruined, and drifting down the Vess with nothing but his hands and his instruments. He'd arrived at the shop five years ago asking to rent the back table by the week. He had never left.

"There is always a war," Tomas said at last. "Somewhere. The question is whether this one intends to visit."

"And does it?"

Tomas turned the glass another quarter-turn. "Armies travel on roads and rivers. We have both. If I were drawing the campaign map—" he stopped himself, the way he always stopped himself, with a small grimace, as if the profession were a wound he kept forgetting to stop touching. "Eat your supper. Speculation is free and worthless."

"It's not worthless," Petra said. "I bet you anything the soldiers come here. I bet they take the shop."

"Take the shop for what?" Marisol demanded. "What are we? Mapmakers?"

"You're a courier post," Petra said, with the devastating simplicity of the young, and the table went briefly, terribly silent.

"We are an atlas-maker's shop," Marisol said, in the voice she used for ending arguments, "that sells maps. Some of which are delivered. By our courier. Who is standing right there and would like everyone to stop discussing his livelihood in front of his supper."

"My livelihood," Beatrix said, "is quotas and tolls, and I would thank this table to remember it."

But under the laughter that followed, Beatrix felt the first small wrongness, the way one feels a draft from a window that appears to be shut. Because Petra was right, of course. Petra was always right in the way that children and dogs are right—by paying attention instead of talking. The dispatches had changed their flavor over the past months. More circulars about grain reserves. More requisition forms pre-printed and waiting. And tonight, tucked among the routine papers, a single sheet Beatrix hadn't mentioned at the table: a movement order for a signals unit, destination field left blank, effective date left blank, as if the army itself couldn't quite bring itself to write the town's name down yet.

Beatrix had copied it anyway. That was the rule. Copy everything, decide later what it means.

* * *

After supper, the household performed its nightly liturgy without knowing it was a liturgy. Hanne washed and Dev dried, arguing companionably about whether the miller's boy was a fool or a prophet. Old Wren banked the stove. Tomas retreated to the back table with his lamps and his dividers, and the soft scratch of his pen joined the river-sound through the walls. Marisol sat by the fire darning a sock that belonged to no one present, which was somehow the most maternal thing about her. Petra read aloud from a battered novel, badly, doing all the voices, until Marisol told her that the villain did not sound like a goose, and Petra informed her that all villains sounded like geese if you listened honestly.

Beatrix stood at the copying desk finishing the last page, and looked around the room, and felt the thing that Beatrix always felt on ordinary nights and never said aloud: that this was the entire country Beatrix had ever wanted to belong to. Seven mismatched chairs. One sock. A villain who sounded like a goose. None of it written down anywhere. None of it existing on any record, in any ledger, in any census—which was precisely why it was safe, and precisely why it was theirs. The republic had never asked them to be anything. In exchange, they had never asked the republic for anything, including protection, including recognition, including, when it came to it, rescue.

A person could live a whole life in the margins of a map. Beatrix had assumed that was a kind of freedom.

The lamp guttered at half past nine, exactly on schedule, and steadied.

It was nearly eleven when the knock came—not at the shop door, which would have meant a customer, but at the river door, the low one down the steps, the one only three living people knew existed. Three knocks, pause, two knocks. The courier's knock, from the courier's side of the world, meaning: *come alone, come now.*

Beatrix set down the pen. Across the room, Tomas looked up from his table, and his eyes went to the river door, and then to Beatrix, and he said nothing, because he understood doors better than most men.

The night outside was cold and smelled of coming rain. On the steps stood a boatman Beatrix had never seen before, soaked through, breathing hard, holding a bundle wrapped in oilcloth against his chest the way one holds a child, or a wound.

"From Merle," the man said. "The clerk. He's dying, and he says you have to take it tonight, and he says—" the boatman swallowed, glanced back at the black water as if something might be following him up it, "—he says the army is three days out."

Beatrix took the bundle. It was heavier than paper should be. It was heavier than anything Beatrix had carried in six years of carrying other people's secrets, and it was warm from the stranger's body, and through the oilcloth Beatrix could feel the ridged spine of a ledger, and the embossed seal of a republic that had never once written down Beatrix Okafor's name.

Behind, in the warm lit room, Petra's voice rose and fell, still reading aloud, still doing the voices, while the river whispered against the steps below, bringing things down from upriver, the way rivers do.
