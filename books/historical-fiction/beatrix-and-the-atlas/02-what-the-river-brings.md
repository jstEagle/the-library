## 2. What the River Brings

The summons came by water, as most urgent things did in Callowmere, where the roads went to mud half the year and the river never did.

Beatrix was on the shop's back step, mending a wick, when the boy arrived — one of the ferry children, barefoot even this late in the season, breathing hard from running the length of the towpath.

"You're the courier," the boy said. Not a question. In Callowmere everyone knew what Beatrix was, and no one said so above a murmur, which was the way Beatrix preferred it. Six years of being unremarkable had taught them the value of a murmured reputation: known just enough to be found, unknown enough to be passed over.

"Who sent you?"

"Old man at the customs wharf. The government one." The boy said *government* the way other people said *wet dog*. "He's bad sick. He's got something for the courier and he won't let anyone else touch it. He's been asking since morning."

Beatrix set down the wick. There was a rule, unwritten but iron, about not taking work from strangers who arrived unannounced with government seals — a rule that had kept Beatrix alive through two changes of administration and one brief, ugly civil disturbance that Callowmere still called *the trouble* as though it had been an argument over fencing rights. But rules bent for dying men. Dying men had no use left for tricks.

"Wait here," Beatrix said, and went inside to fetch the satchel.

* * *

The customs wharf sat a half-mile downriver from the town proper, a low stone building with a green door that had not been repainted since the Republic of Vessany was young and optimistic. Beatrix walked there along the water, past the fish racks and the tar barrels, past the moored punts knocking their soft wooden conversation against the pilings, and all the while felt the day behaving strangely. The light was ordinary autumn light, thin and gold. The gulls were ordinary gulls. But under everything ran a wrongness, like a wrong note held too long in a hymn, and it took Beatrix most of the walk to name it: there were no boats coming down from the north. None. On any given morning there should have been six or eight, grain barges and timber rafts and the mail skiff, the whole slow commerce of the upper valley drifting toward the sea. Today the river ran empty as far as the eye could follow it, silver and silent, as if the country upstream had been wiped clean.

Inside the customs house, the smell told the rest before any eyes adjusted: camphor and wet wool and the sweetish undertone of a body failing faster than its owner would like admitted.

He was lying on a camp cot behind the counter, propped on rolled ledgers — of course he would be propped on ledgers, Beatrix thought, they were the only pillows such men ever trusted — an old clerk in a coat too fine for his post, grey at the temples, greyer in the lips. His boots stood beside the cot, polished, absurdly polished, as though he had dressed for an audience. A single candle burned on the desk despite the daylight, and beside it lay a parcel wrapped in oilcloth and tied with waxed cord, and Beatrix understood immediately, in the way one understands fire or falling water, that the parcel was the entire reason for the room.

"Courier Okafor," the man said. His voice was dry paper. "You came. Half of me thought you wouldn't."

"You have the advantage of me," Beatrix said carefully.

"Hessing. Second Bureau of Records." He attempted a smile and abandoned it halfway, having judged, correctly, that neither of them had the strength for ceremony. "Third Bureau now. They merged us. Nobody told me until I arrived at my own office and found my desk carried out." He coughed then, a long, folding-over cough, and when he surfaced again he pressed a hand flat to his chest as if holding a lid on himself. "Water. Please."

Beatrix poured from the jug on the desk and watched him drink, and watched his hands. Clerk's hands, ink-stained at the cuticles in the permanent way that no washing ever cured. Beatrix knew those hands like a farmer knows weather.

"You've come downriver from Vessel," Beatrix said. "That's four days' travel."

"Three days ago it was four days' travel." Hessing set the cup down with the exaggerated care of a man refusing to let his hands shake in front of witnesses. "The bridges are out above Kelder's Ford. I came the last stretch by night, dark water, no lamp. Do you know what the river looks like at night with no lamp on it, courier?"

"I do."

"It looks like nothing. It looks like the world before the world." He looked at Beatrix then, fully, and his eyes were terrible not with fear exactly but with the effort of arranging fear into sentences. "The Grey Column crossed the border nineteen days ago. Everyone who believed the radio is dead or running. The army is three days behind me. Maybe less if they've taken the towpath road, which they will have, because I would have."

Outside, a gull cried once and was answered. The pilings knocked. Somewhere impossibly far away, the world continued being ordinary, and Beatrix felt the belief they had carried comfortably for thirty-nine years — that history was a thing that happened to other people, in cities, in other languages, on paper — begin, very quietly, to crack.

"That's not possible," Beatrix said, and heard how young the voice sounded saying it.

"Yes," Hessing agreed. "It's quite impossible. I've filed the impossibility three times. The forms were very thorough." Another attempt at a smile; this one made it further before dying. "Courier Okafor, I'm going to give you the Atlas."

* * *

They had heard the word, of course. Everyone downstream of the capital had heard the word by now, the way one hears a word through a wall — *evacuation schedules*, *priority registries*, *the Atlas will be maintained*, the soothing liturgy of a government insisting on its own continuation. Beatrix had copied dispatches containing the word a dozen times without once thinking it meant anything. Words in dispatches were weather in a painting. You could see them; you could not be rained on.

Hessing pulled himself up higher on the ledgers, and the effort cost him visibly, and he went on anyway, because clerks go on.

"There are twenty-two berths assigned to this district. Twenty-two names on the final barge out of Callowmere, nine days from now. The Atlas holds those names, the priority codes, the authentication marks. Without the Atlas, the barge master cannot load a single soul — no ledger, no evacuation, those are the regulations, and God help me, the regulations are the last thing standing between those people and nothing at all." He laid his hand on the oilcloth parcel and did not push it forward yet. "There were supposed to be two of us bringing it down. My colleague took the eastern road. I have had no word from the eastern road in eleven days, and I am not going to get word from the eastern road, because I am going to die in this room, probably tonight, certainly by tomorrow, and my colleague—" He stopped. Rearranged himself. "The Atlas needs to reach the barge master at Callowmere quay. That's you. That was always going to be someone like you: nobody official, nobody marked, a face the soldiers haven't catalogued. That was the design. Invisibility as infrastructure."

"And you trust me because—"

"I don't know you at all," Hessing said, with something close to satisfaction. "That's precisely why. The Bureau spent forty years learning that the people you know betray you slowly, out of love, and strangers betray you never, out of professional indifference. You're a stranger with a good local reputation for discretion. That's the strongest endorsement my department ever managed to produce." He pushed the parcel across the desk. "Twenty-two people, courier. Some of them are children. The list is alphabetical. Don't read it."

Beatrix looked at the parcel and did not touch it yet. "Why not?"

"Because names are heavier than you think, and you'll need your shoulders for the weight of the thing itself." Hessing's voice had begun to fray. "And because if you don't deliver it, it's better you never know whose faces went with the numbers. That's mercy. We wrote it into the protocols. First merciful thing the Bureau ever wrote, in my opinion, and I drafted the language myself."

The candle guttered in a draught from the ill-fitted door. Down at the water a punt knocked twice against its piling, patient as a clock. Beatrix thought of the shop on the square, the warm room behind it, Petra upstairs doing the voices of every character in her ridiculous serial novels, Marisol scolding Tomas away from the salt, the whole improbable household of strays that had assembled itself around an atlas-maker's ruined business like sparrows around spilled grain. Twenty-two names. Beatrix did not yet know that none of them belonged to anyone in that house. That knowledge was waiting nine days downstream, patient as rivers are.

"The oath," Hessing said. "Before I hand it to you. Say it after me."

"I didn't know there was an oath."

"There's always an oath. It's short. The Bureau was efficient about exactly one thing." He closed his eyes, reciting from some internal card catalogue that death had apparently not yet reorganized: "*I carry what is written. I do not add. I do not subtract. I do not read what is not addressed to me. What is entrusted travels; what is true remains.*"

"What is true remains," Beatrix repeated, and would remember, later, that they had sworn it easily, standing in a dying man's borrowed lamplight, before understanding a single word of what it would cost.

Hessing opened his eyes and studied Beatrix for a long moment — the way, Beatrix would think much later, a man studies the last signature on the last form he will ever file.

"You think history happens somewhere else," he said. It was not an accusation; it was almost gentle. "I could tell from the door. It's in the face of everyone who's lived this far from the capital. I thought so too, courier. Forty years I thought so, filing other people's catastrophes in triplicate. Then one morning the catastrophe arrives at your own desk and asks you to initial page four." He pushed the parcel the final distance. It slid across the scarred wood with a whisper, like something deciding. "Take it. Go now, before dark, and don't take the towpath road."

Beatrix took the bundle. It was heavier than paper should be. It was heavier than anything Beatrix had carried in six years of carrying other people's secrets, and it was warm from the stranger's body, and through the oilcloth Beatrix could feel the ridged spine of a ledger, and the embossed seal of a republic that had never once written down Beatrix Okafor's name.

"Hessing," they said at the door, because some instinct insisted the man should not end unaddressed. "Can I send you anything? Get you anyone?"

The clerk considered this with genuine care, as though it were a form requiring completion.

"File it," he said at last. "Whatever becomes of all this — file it properly. That's all any of us were ever doing."

* * *

Beatrix walked back along the river with the parcel inside the courier's satchel, against the hip, where the body's warmth could pretend it was nothing unusual. The empty channel ran beside them the whole way, wide and silver and silent, and Beatrix caught themselves scanning the northern bend — the place where the water came down from Kelder's Ford, from the bridges that were out, from the impossible thing three days away and closing — and seeing, each time, nothing. Nothing on the water. Nothing on the road. Nothing yet.

At the fish racks, old Sela waved; Beatrix waved back and did not stop. At the tar barrels, two boys were arguing about whether the war would reach the price of sugar; Beatrix passed within earshot and said nothing, and understood, with the first cold settling of dread, that silence had just become a job. From here forward, every hour not telling the town what was coming would be a kind of labor, performed daily, unpaid.

Behind, in the warm lit room at the top of the lane, Petra's voice rose and fell, still reading aloud, still doing the voices, while the river whispered against the steps below, bringing things down from upriver, the way rivers do.

Beatrix climbed the steps to the shop, and paused with a hand on the door, feeling the ledger's seal press its ridged circles into their ribs like a question. Three days. Nine days. Twenty-two names.

Through the window they could see Marisol setting the supper table, laying out seven plates with the unthinking certainty of a woman who believed, absolutely, that there would always be seven of them — and Beatrix stood a moment longer in the cold than they needed to, before going in.
