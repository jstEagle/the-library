## 3. The Requisition

The boots woke them before the bells did.

Beatrix had slept badly, the oilcloth bundle still beneath their mattress where it had spent two nights breathing its slow dread into the dark, and so they were already at the window when the first grey shapes came down the Mill Road—not marching so much as flowing, the way water finds a town's lowest points and fills them. Soldiers in grey, maybe sixty of them, then more behind them, and behind those the supply carts with their canvas covers beaded in morning mist. No banners. That was the detail Beatrix's mind snagged on and would not release: no banners. An army that did not need to announce itself was an army that had stopped worrying about being resisted.

Below, in the kitchen, Marisol was already moving. Beatrix heard her without seeing her—the banked stove stirred alive, the good bread brought out from where she'd hidden it, the kettle set on with the particular violence of a woman who refused to be found unprepared. Tomas's door stayed shut. Petra's feet pattered to the top of the stairs and stopped.

"Don't go down," Beatrix said.

"I want to see."

"You'll see enough soon."

Even as they said it, Beatrix understood it was wrong. The whole trick of their life had been that there was never enough to see—a courier town too small for garrisons, a river bend too shallow for gun barges, a household assembled from people the republic's ledgers had mislaid or discarded. Invisibility had been their architecture. Beatrix had built a life inside it the way a snail builds a shell, whorl by careful whorl: unremarkable face, unremarkable errands, unremarkable name signed in unremarkable hand on dispatch after dispatch that history would never read.

The soldiers reached the square at first light and began, unhurried, to make it theirs.

* * *

By eight o'clock Callowmere belonged to the grey coats in the specific, administrative way that towns change hands—not with fire but with paper. Beatrix watched from the shop doorway as a lieutenant with a clipboard walked the market stalls, chalking numbers on the scales. Two soldiers took the customs house. Four more went door to door along the quay with a list, counting heads the way a farmer counts stock before winter. Nobody ran. That frightened Beatrix more than shouting would have. Fear makes crowds stupid; resignation made this one precise, obedient, each shopkeeper standing at their threshold with their ledger open like a man baring his throat because he has calculated that baring it is the only move left.

At half past eight, three soldiers crossed the square toward the atlas-maker's shop.

Beatrix had perhaps forty seconds. They used all of them.

The Atlas lay beneath the mattress upstairs, but Beatrix had not slept beside it in innocence these two nights—they had studied it, and studying it had taught them its shape, its weight, the particular arrogance of its binding: government calfskin, brass corners, the seal of the Evacuation Commission stamped blind into the cover. It looked like what it was. Anything that looked like what it was would not survive the morning.

The hollowed folio had been Tomas's, back when Tomas still made atlases instead of merely repairing them—a great elephant folio meant to hold the plates of some projected *Atlas Hydrographica* that the republic's collapse had cancelled before the first map was drawn. The plates were long gone; the folio remained, thick as a Bible, its spine cracked into deep grooves where the copper had once sat. Last winter, bored and bitter, Tomas had cut away the inner tray of the largest groove and lined it with waste paper, so that the folio held a secret cavity exactly the length and width of—

Beatrix did not let themselves finish the thought. There was no time for what the thought meant: that they had been preparing for this without knowing it, that some part of them had understood since the clerk's boat came upriver that the shell would one day need to be thicker than the body inside it.

They slid the Atlas into the cavity. It fit the way a name fits a grave marker—exactly, finally. Over it they laid the three remaining dummy plates, sheets of heavy paper ruled faintly with meridian lines, and closed the folio, and wrapped the whole thing in the shop's ordinary clutter: rolled maps of coastlines that no longer belonged to anyone, bundles of proofs, a wooden case of dividers. Then they carried it down past Petra's white face at the banister and set it, casually as a thing set down a thousand times, atop the flat file cabinet by the drafting table, under a dead lamp, beneath a tin of blotting sand.

The knock came. Three measured blows.

When Beatrix opened the door, the man on the step was not looking at them at all. He was looking past them, into the shop, with the gaze of a surveyor taking bearings—and Beatrix felt the room stripped naked in that look, every cabinet weighed, every drawer inventoried, the folio noted and passed over in the same glance.

"Captain Ilves requisitions this building," the soldier said. "Signals office, effective now. You will have one hour to remove personal effects. Civilians may remain in residence as domestic staff, provided they register." He produced his own clipboard, and Beatrix saw that the future had arrived in Callowmere holding a list.

* * *

Marisol took charge of the hour the way she took charge of everything—by making survival look like housework.

"Servants," she said quietly, gathering the household in the cellar's dimness among the pickling jars and the winter apples. "That's what we are now. We were never anything else." She looked around at their faces, counting them as she counted loaves: Tomas, hands already trembling; Odile the deserter, who had once shot a corporal and could not be within arm's reach of a uniform without her jaw going tight; the twins, Fen and Ferrand, orphaned somewhere east of here and fluent only in silence; little Petra, whose face was an open window with the shutter torn off; and Beatrix, who had spent thirty-one years practicing the art of being nobody and discovered, now, that practice is nothing against performance. A choice you make is freedom. The same act compelled is theatre. And the difference between them is everything.

"They will ask names," Marisol said. "Give the ones on your papers. Nothing else. If they ask how long you've served here, say two years. Say you came with the old owner's estate." Her voice did not rise; it lowered, which was worse. "We have buried two households before this one. I know the steps of this dance. Follow my feet."

Tomas cleared his throat. "And if they ask about the trade? About the plates?"

"Then you are a repairman who has never cut a plate in his life," Marisol said, "and you will say it until your tongue believes it, because your tongue believing it is the only version of you that walks out of this building."

Beatrix heard what she was really saying—that the family's existence, the whole loose generous accidental architecture of it, must be folded flat and pressed into the shape of a lie small enough to fit through registration. Their love, which had never needed papers, suddenly needed a forged pedigree. It struck Beatrix then, standing among the apples, that they had lived invisible for so long they had mistaken invisibility for safety. But a thing hidden is not a thing protected. It is only a thing not yet found.

Upstairs, the soldiers were already running wire.

* * *

The transformation of the shop took less than a day, and it was this speed that taught Beatrix the true nature of the army outside: not a horde but an institution, rehearsed, its cruelty delivered with the blandness of a postal service. By noon the drafting tables held signal lamps and codebooks. By two o'clock a corporal had fixed an antenna mast to the old sign bracket where the shop's painted name—*Hollander & Sons, Atlases and Charts*, dead men both, a lineage of paper that had never actually existed—still faced the square. The smell of the place changed first, Beatrix noticed. Forty years of size, glue, and linseed gave ground, hour by hour, to boot oil, cold tobacco, and the ozone tang of the wireless set. A house can be evicted without anyone leaving it.

The household became furniture.

Marisol cooked for the officers and did it magnificently, because excellence was armor—an indispensable cook draws no scrutiny. Odile hauled coal with downcast eyes and a peasant's mumbled dialect she put on like a shawl. The twins swept and carried and vanished into their own silence so completely that soldiers stopped seeing them within a day. Petra was ordered to stay in the cellar rooms, and obeyed with the strained, luminous obedience of a child saving up everything she saw for later.

And Beatrix stayed close to the flat file cabinet.

This was the discipline that nearly undid them: to remain in the room where the Atlas hid, hour after hour, while strangers handled the things near it. A signals officer claimed the drafting table four feet from the folio. Another draped his coat over the flat file itself. Once, a bored corporal, waiting on a transmission, lifted the tin of blotting sand, weighed it in his hand, set it down again, and picked up the folio's corner to straighten it against the wall—and Beatrix, sorting incoming flimsies ten paces away, felt their heart perform a long slow dive and did not let their eyes flicker once toward the cabinet, because eyes are drawn to the thing a person cannot bear to lose the way moths are drawn to glass. The corporal straightened the folio. He put the sand tin back. He scratched his neck and read a newspaper and was, in himself, entirely harmless, which was the horror of it. The machinery that would kill them, if it killed them, would do it as paperwork.

That evening Captain Ilves arrived.

Beatrix saw him only briefly and at a distance—he came with two aides, toured the converted shop, paused at the antenna bracket, and stood for a long moment before the flat file cabinet, not searching it, merely regarding it, as a man regards a painting he has been told is significant. He was lean, grey-templed, with the unhurried hands of someone who had never once needed to grab at anything. Before leaving he accepted a cup of Marisol's coffee, drank it standing, thanked her in careful, accented Vessani—*thank you, it is very good*—and said to no one in particular:

"A well-run office. Someone kept excellent records here."

Beatrix, refilling the lamp by the door, kept their head bowed and their face arranged into the mild vacancy of a domestic. Inside, something answered him silently: *You have no idea what records.*

* * *

Registration happened the next morning in the square, at a trestle table under the customs-house eaves, and it was there that the performance became total.

A clerk with a fountain pen took each household member separately. Beatrix watched the others go through it: Marisol first, giving her true name because false names invite questions and Marisol's name, unregistered anywhere in Vessany's living records for eleven years, was safe precisely because it was real and forgotten. Then Odile, whose papers named her as Odile Ferencz, laundress, though the woman who had once worn a sergeant's chevrons stood behind those words like a fugitive behind a door. The twins. Tomas, who emerged pale but intact—the clerk had glanced at his ink-stained fingers and asked if he was a scrivener, and Tomas had said yes, a copyist, nothing more, and the lie had come out of him smooth as varnish, and Beatrix loved him and feared for him in the same instant.

Then it was Beatrix's turn.

"Name."

"Beatrix Okafor."

The pen moved. "Occupation."

"Courier." There was no merit in denying it; couriers were everywhere, registered everywhere, the republic's last honest infrastructure. "Domestic staff, presently."

"Employer?"

"The estate. The late Hollander's estate." The dead fiction, deployed on schedule. Marisol had said *two years*; Beatrix said two years; the clerk wrote it without looking up, and Beatrix understood, watching the nib scratch, that none of it mattered—not the answers, not the truthfulness, not the fear sweating down their spine. The clerk was not collecting facts. He was collecting *submission*. The form existed so that each citizen of Callowmere would stand one meter from a grey uniform and answer questions in a certain order and leave smaller than they arrived.

Invisibility chosen had been a philosophy. Invisibility performed was a sentence.

"Sign."

Beatrix signed—unremarkable hand, unremarkable loop on the k—and walked back across the square with their heart beating strangely high in their throat. Behind them the clerk called, "Next," and the line shuffled forward, twenty-two souls in a river town becoming entries on a grey list that now knew they existed.

Twenty-two. The number arrived in Beatrix's mind unbidden and unwelcome, and it brought its companion with it, the one the dying clerk's oilcloth bundle had carried upriver: the Atlas, lying hollowed in the folio ten meters from a captain who kept excellent records, contained the promise of evacuation for every soul in the district whose name the republic had bothered to enter.

Beatrix had not yet opened it to check.

All day the thought had circled like a bird over water. Now, crossing the square in the flat afternoon light, passing the barge schedules chalked on the customs-house board—*River traffic suspended except military; civilian passage by commission priority only*—the bird landed.

That night, after the officers' lamps went dark, Beatrix waited for the sentry's turn at the corner, drew the folio from beneath the coat draped over the flat file, and carried it to the cellar under the pretense of fetching apples. Down among the jars, by shielded candlelight, they worked the dummy plates free and slid the Atlas into their hands.

It opened to the index of districts. Callowmere and the river settlements occupied a page near the end, columns of entered names in the clerk's even bureaucratic hand—names Beatrix half-knew, faces attached, the cooper, the ferry widow, the schoolteacher. Page after page. Beatrix ran a finger down column one, then column two, reading the way one reads a guest list for a wedding one is not invited to.

Then they turned to the space where the next entry should begin, the margin wide and clean and waiting, and Beatrix sat very still among the apples and let the truth arrive, cold and complete, the way the soldiers had come down the Mill Road.

The columns ended. The page was not full. And nowhere on it—no line, no letter, no smudge of an abandoned draft—appeared the name Okafor, or Ferencz, or Hollander, or any of the other names this household wore like borrowed coats. The republic, in its final accounting of everyone it meant to save, had never heard of them.

From above, through the floorboards, came the soft clicking of the wireless key, transmitting the new regime's first messages into the dark— and Beatrix, alone in the cellar with the book of the saved, began, without deciding to, to count.
