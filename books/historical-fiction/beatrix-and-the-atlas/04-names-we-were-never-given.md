## 4. Names We Were Never Given

Marisol had learned long ago that bread forgives you for touching it too much. Dough wanted hands. It wanted warmth and patience and a certain rough tenderness that no recipe could write down. She worked it now on the scarred table in the kitchen behind the atlas-maker's shop, the window above the basin fogged with steam from the great copper pot, and she let her mind do what it always did when her hands were busy: it wandered back along the road of how they had all come to her.

Nine years since the first of them.

She had been alone then, widowed twice over by the sea and then by the fever, keeping the kitchen of a man named Aldous Ferrant who made atlases and forgot to eat. He had hired her for her soup and kept her for her silence. The shop smelled then as it smelled now—sizing and old paper, lampblack and linseed—and Marisol had thought herself finished with belonging, finished with the whole exhausting arithmetic of loving people who could be taken from you. She was forty-one years old and she had decided, quietly and without ceremony, that she would be the last of her line, a full stop at the end of a sentence nobody had bothered to read.

Then Petra had arrived, carried through the door in Beatrix's arms like a bundle of wet kindling.

* * *

It had rained that night too. Marisol remembered because the child's shoes had left small red prints on the flagstones—mud, she had assumed at first, until she knelt and saw otherwise. A girl of six or seven, no one could say precisely which, walked half the length of the river road by a courier woman she'd never met before, because the courier woman had found her crouched under a customs bridge where her parents' boat had gone aground and burned. There were questions, of course. There were always questions. Where a magistrate would have seen an orphan requiring assignment, Marisol saw a child who flinched at raised voices and ate as though food might be legislated away from her. Aldous shrugged his narrow shoulders. Beatrix said nothing at all, only watched with those flat dark eyes while Marisol fed the girl eggs and bread and watched her hide a heel of the loaf inside her shirt.

"Nobody's coming for her," Beatrix said, the third night, standing in the kitchen doorway.

"Nobody came looking," Marisol corrected. "There's a difference."

And that was the whole of the arrangement. No papers signed, no fee paid, no name entered anywhere but the one Marisol gave her privately, at the table, in candlelight: *Petra*, because the child had answered to everything and nothing, and Marisol liked the sound of stone. A name like a thing that endures.

The others came the way water comes into a cellar—not all at once, not through any single crack, but finding every low place.

Tomas had come second, though he'd deny the order still, insisting he was first among equals or some such cartographer's nonsense about scale. He arrived in winter with frostbitten fingers and a satchel of stolen plates, a man of forty who looked sixty, dismissed from the Royal Survey for a map that told the truth about a valley the ministry preferred uncharted—or so he claimed when drunk, and contradicted when sober, until none of them ever knew which version was the wound and which the bandage. Aldous took him in as a fellow tradesman; Marisol took him in because he ate standing up, like a man expecting to be sent back to wherever he'd come from. It had taken three years before he sat down through a whole meal.

Sefa came deserting. That was the word for it—*deserting*—as if she had abandoned something, when what she had done was refuse, at nineteen, to aim a rifle at a line of fishermen on a dock in some harbor whose name changed depending on which government was currently pretending to own it. She turned up at the shop door with her service coat rolled under her arm and her boots worn through, and she asked not for shelter but for work, because pride is the last thing a person surrenders and Sefa guarded hers like a lit lantern. Marisol put a knife in her hand and taught her onions. Within a year the girl could bone a hare and disassemble a rifle and neither skill ever came up again, which was its own kind of treaty.

The twins, Ansel and Rell, were not twins at all—that was simply what the household called them, two boys of thirteen and fifteen who arrived within a month of each other off the same broken barge line, unrelated by blood and inseparable by everything else. They had been warehouse children in Corvel, sleeping in the loft of a granary that burned, and after the fire there had been no granary and no reason to stay and no record anywhere, Marisol was nearly certain, that either of them had ever existed at all. They came asking after work and stayed because Petra, by then eleven and ferociously proprietary, informed them at the supper table that this was her family and there wasn't room for more, and then cried for an hour when she thought they meant to leave.

And Beatrix. Marisol saved Beatrix for last, the way you save the strongest thread for the final stitch. The courier had been delivering to the shop for four years before the child came—polite, punctual, forgettable, a person built entirely out of smooth surfaces. It was only after, watching Beatrix carry a half-drowned stranger through the rain without hesitation and then apologize for dripping on the floor, that Marisol understood: here was someone who had made themselves so unremarkable that they had vanished even from their own life. No family mentioned. No history offered. Once, late, over Marisol's plum brandy, Beatrix had said only: "There was a house. There isn't anymore." And Marisol, who knew better than to pry at a scab that size, had poured another measure and said, "There's a house here."

* * *

Now she stood in her kitchen with her hands buried in dough and counted them the way she counted them every evening before sleep, a rosary of the living: Marisol. Petra. Tomas. Sefa. Ansel. Rell. Beatrix. Seven. Seven plates at the long table, seven coats on the pegs, seven names spoken in her heart with their little histories attached like charms.

Seven names that existed nowhere else on earth.

The thought arrived the way cold water arrives—through a seam she hadn't known was there—and it stopped her hands mid-knead.

She had seen the ledger yesterday. Of course she had; she was the one who had dried it. When Beatrix brought the oilcloth parcel in from the boat, dripping river onto the boards, it was Marisol who unwrapped it while Beatrix stood dripping worse, and it was Marisol who hung the heavy book open near the stove, fanning its pages because the damp had made them cling. She hadn't meant to read it. But pages fan open to faces, and a face had been there, staring up at her: a printed form, columns ruled neat as garden rows. *Name. District. Priority class. Berth assignment.* And beneath the columns, entry after entry, name after name—the Vessany that mattered, the Vessany that counted, the clerk's sons and the mill owners' widows and the harbormaster's entire extended family, entered in a hand so confident it looked typeset.

Two hundred and eleven names for Callowmere. She had counted. Counting was what she did; she counted cups and spoons and candles and days, and she had counted the names without knowing why, the way a tongue finds the gap where a tooth used to be.

Two hundred and eleven, and not one of them was Okonkwo-Marisol-Vasquez, born across the strait, widowed twice, cook at the sign of the atlas-maker, keeper of seven.

Not one was Petra, age uncertain, address formerly a bridge.

Not Sefa, who had renounced a uniform. Not Ansel or Rell, whose births were smoke. Not Tomas, struck from the Survey rolls with an efficiency he himself could have described. Not even Beatrix—a courier in the republic's own service, carrying its most precious paper—and Marisol would wager her last copper kettle that the name *Beatrix Okafor* appeared nowhere in that book except perhaps in the margin, in ink so faint it might as well be apology.

They were invisible. Nine years of building a family, of soups and fevers and the twins' growth lines penciled on the pantry doorjamb, and to the republic that was now deciding who lived, the whole of it weighed exactly nothing. Less than nothing. You cannot evacuate a shadow. You cannot put a berth on a barge for a person who does not exist.

Marisol punched the dough down harder than it needed.

Here was the horror of it, and she made herself look at it squarely, because she had buried two families already and had promised herself she would never again be surprised by the ways of the world: being nobody had kept them safe. That was the bargain she had never spoken aloud but had enforced in a hundred small instructions—*don't register, don't complain, don't sign, stay out of ledgers, stay out of sight.* Invisibility was the wall around the garden. And now the flood was coming, three days out or five, and the wall that had kept the wolves from noticing the garden would keep the ferryman from noticing it too. Their protection and their doom were the same paper.

She could hear them through the floorboards. The shop above had gone quiet after the soldiers requisitioned the square—grey uniforms at the windows where the map cabinets used to face the street—but sound still traveled down the stairwell, as it always had, and what came down now was the sound of her family arguing about war the way people argue about weather, which is to say loudly and with the secret hope that their arguing has any bearing on it. Sefa's low voice. The twins' overlapping ones. Petra's bright laugh, too loud, pitched the way she pitched it when she wanted reassurance. And Beatrix's voice underneath it all, level, careful, saying something Marisol couldn't make out and recognized anyway, because Beatrix had been saying it for days: *it won't reach us here.*

Everyone needed to believe it wouldn't reach them. Belief was also a kind of bread; Marisol had baked plenty of it in her time.

She wiped her hands and untied her apron and climbed the stairs, because there are moments when a matriarch must stand in a doorway and simply look at what she has, the way you look at a table set for company before anyone arrives.

They were gathered around the worktable where the great folios usually lay. The Atlas itself sat closed at the table's center like a guest of honor, oilcloth folded beside it, and they had arranged themselves around it without realizing—as people will gather around a fire or a grave. Petra sat cross-legged on a stool. Sefa leaned against the press. The twins had their heads together. Tomas stood apart, arms folded, already halfway into whatever private cartography of worry he conducted. And Beatrix stood nearest the book, always nearest the book now, with the particular stillness of someone holding a weight nobody else could see.

"We should talk about it," Sefa was saying. "The ledger. Whether we're—"

"We're not in it." Marisol heard her own voice say it before she had decided to say anything at all. The room turned. "I read it while I dried it. Two hundred and eleven names for Callowmere. None of them ours."

The silence that followed had a texture, almost fibrous, like the air after lightning. Petra's face did something complicated—young, suddenly, all the performed brightness stripped away—and Marisol crossed the room without hurrying and put a hand on the back of the girl's neck, thumb against the soft jaw, the way she had done since the red footprints on the flagstones.

"That doesn't mean anything," Tomas said quickly, too quickly, in his lawyer-of-the-map voice. "Registration lists are separate from evacuation manifests. Priority classes. There'll be supplementary rolls—"

"You know there won't be," Sefa said quietly.

"There won't be," Beatrix said.

Marisol looked at the courier then, really looked, and saw the thing flicker behind those flat dark eyes—the same look from nine years ago in the doorway, *nobody's coming for her*—except now it was aimed inward, at some calculation just beginning to turn its wheels. Beatrix's hand rested on the Atlas's cover, lightly, the way you rest a hand on a sleeping animal you may need to wake or steal.

And here was the moment, Marisol thought later. Here was the fork in the road, and she took it with her eyes open.

She could say it all now. Could say: *then we go to whoever holds the pen, tonight, and we beg, we register, we put ourselves in their hands—* She could demand action, demand a plan, demand that someone fix in a day what nine years of careful invisibility had built against them. She could watch Beatrix's face while she demanded it and see exactly how far the courier's oath ran, how deep the fear went.

Instead she said nothing. Because she had learned—two families buried, remember; she had earned this knowledge the hard way, at funerals, holding other women's children in churchyards—that panic spends faster than it earns. Because the soldiers in grey were at the windows twenty feet away. Because whatever was turning behind Beatrix's eyes deserved one night to turn in peace before it was interrogated. And because—she admitted this to no one, least of all herself, not yet—some stubborn animal part of her believed, still believed, after everything, that love which predates paperwork must surely outweigh it. That the world could not possibly be so crude as to count lives by entries in a column. That someone, somewhere, held a pen and a decent heart in the same hand.

"Supper's in an hour," she said instead. "Barley and the last of the smoked neck. Petra, you're peeling. Ansel, wood. Rell, stop whispering with your brother and fetch water. Tomas—" she paused, softened it with the smallest tilt of her head, "—come help me with the stove."

They moved, gratefully, obediently, relieved to be given ordinary tasks in an unordinary hour, and the room emptied around the Atlas until only the two of them remained—Marisol and the book. She looked down at it for a long moment. Leather gone stiff with damp and drying, brass clasps, the embossed seal of a republic that had never once asked her name. Two hundred and eleven souls, officially. Officially, seven fewer than lived in this house.

"Someone wrote you down," she told it softly, in the trade tongue of kitchens everywhere, addressed to objects because objects don't argue. "Somebody sat at a desk and wrote down everybody who mattered. And everybody who mattered got to matter."

She laid her palm flat on the cover, feeling the faint residual warmth of the stove-soaked pages, and thought of Petra at six, hiding bread in her shirt. Thought of Sefa refusing the order to shoot. Thought of Tomas sitting down through a whole meal at last, and the pencil marks climbing the pantry jamb, and Beatrix saying *there was a house, there isn't anymore.*

"Well," Marisol said to the ledger, to the republic, to the grey-coated century generally, "we'll see about that."

She turned down the lamp and went below to feed her family, and she did not yet know—for how could she?—that the person now scrubbing carrots beside Petra at the basin, sleeves rolled, silent as a held breath, had already begun the arithmetic that would cost them everything: counting heads against berths, love against ink, and finding the sums short by exactly seven.
