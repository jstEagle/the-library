## 5. The Berth Count

The dispatch came in at dawn, folded inside a fishmonger's invoice, and Beatrix read it three times before the numbers would sit still.

*Final evacuation barge, Callowmere landing, departing morning of the fourteenth. Berths allocated by priority class as appended. Unclaimed berths revert to district authority at sailing.*

They read it a fourth time by the light of the shop's back room, holding the paper close to the shuttered lamp while the house slept above them, and this time they did the arithmetic. It was not difficult arithmetic. That was the cruelty of it. A child could have done it. Beatrix had couriered ledgers through two border wars and a famine year, and they had learned that the worst documents were never the complicated ones. The complicated ones at least required you to think, and thinking kept other things out.

The appended list ran to four pages of priority classes: ministries first, then registry clerks, then physicians, then skilled tradesmen whose trades the incoming army might find useful, and so on down through the long gradations of usefulness until one arrived at people like cooks and cartographers who had been struck from the guild rolls and couriers who had never officially existed. Callowmere's allocation was ninety-one berths. The town's registered population was four hundred and six.

And the final line, the line Beatrix read with their lips moving like a schoolboy at prayer: *one berth remaining unclaimed by priority.*

One.

Beatrix set the paper down on Tomas's drafting table, next to the brass weights and the half-finished map of a coastline that had probably already changed hands, and pressed their palms flat against the wood until the grain printed itself into their skin. Outside, the river moved in the dark, indifferent as arithmetic.

Nine days.

* * *

The trouble with a life built on being unremarkable, Beatrix thought over breakfast, is that it leaves you no practice for being counted.

They sat at the long table in the shop's front room with the shutters half open—grey uniforms in the square now, so full openness was over, but total darkness invited suspicion, so the household had learned to live in a kind of permanent dusk, moving between the slats of what light remained. Marisol had stretched the oats with chestnut flour again, and Petra was pushing hers into a border pattern, fortifying her bowl against an invasion she had no intention of allowing. Danya was reading yesterday's news-sheet aloud in the flat voice he used for bad news, which was all of it now. Old Fen sat with his hands around his cup not drinking, which was how he prayed.

Six of them at the table. Six, and the two lodgers upstairs who had come with the autumn and never left, and the Kess family in the cellar rooms since their own house near the granary had been requisitioned, and old Yeva who was not a member of anything but had eaten at this table every Sunday for eleven years and had nowhere else to be Sundays or any other day. Beatrix counted without wanting to. Counted the way a tongue finds the gap where a tooth was.

Twenty-two mouths that belonged, in some loose and undefended way, to this shop.

One berth.

"You're doing it again," Marisol said, not looking up from her pot.

"Doing what?"

"That thing with your eyes. Like an abacus." She ladled the last of the oats into Fen's bowl with the tenderness of a woman arming a soldier. "Whatever number you're running, stop running it. Numbers before breakfast are bad luck."

"It's nothing," Beatrix said, and heard the lie come out of their mouth smooth and warm as poured honey, and understood with something like vertigo that they had never lied to Marisol before. Eleven years of shared bread, of shared silences, of the particular language that households invent and governments cannot decipher—and the first lie was this small, this necessary, this easy. "Dispatches. Courier business."

Marisol looked at them then, one long look over the rim of her spectacles, the look that had undressed every deserter and orphan who ever arrived at her door claiming to be fine. Beatrix held it. It was like holding a door shut against wind.

"Hm," said Marisol, and let it go, which was worse than argument.

Afterward Beatrix washed bowls at the basin and found that their hands had begun the counting again on their own. Twenty-two. One. The division did not resolve. It hung in the air of them like smoke with nowhere to go, and every face they passed that morning—Danya arguing politics with the wall, Petra whittling, Tomas hunched over his plates—arrived attached to its number, and the numbers refused to arrange themselves into mercy.

* * *

By afternoon Beatrix could bear their own skull no longer and went out, walking the long way to the landing through streets that wore occupation like a badly fitted coat. Grey sentries at the corners. A queue outside the registry office of citizens hoping, in defiance of all evidence about how empires treat lists, to be added to one. Beatrix kept their eyes level and their gait dull and was, as always, unseen. Invisible. It had been the great talent of their life.

At the landing the barge lay moored where the requisition notice said it would be, low and grey-bellied, flying no flag at all, which somehow was more ominous than any flag. A harbormaster's clerk with chilblains checked Beatrix's courier seal twice and then talked, because everyone talked to couriers; couriers were the confessional of a collapsing state.

"Ninety-one berths and I'd wager my boots half of them never walk down that gangway," the clerk said, jerking his chin at the barge. "Ministry people have other ways out. Private boats, papers that say *urgent*, friends upstream. It's the ones at the bottom of the list—" He stopped himself, the way people had stopped themselves all month, mid-sentence, before the sentence became treason. "You want the manifest? Official copy?"

"I want to see the allocation schedule," Beatrix said. "Verification only."

The clerk fetched it, licking his thumb to turn pages that smelled of ink still drying, and there it was again in a different hand, confirmed, cross-referenced, stamped: priority classes one through nine, berths claimed, berths reserved, and at the bottom of the column a single figure standing alone in its box like a man left on a platform after the train has gone.

*Unclaimed: 1.*

"One," Beatrix said aloud, just to hear how it sounded in the open air, and it sounded exactly as it had sounded in the dark of the back room, which is to say like a knife laid on a table.

"Aye." The clerk lowered his voice. "Between us, they'll fill it from the district rolls if nobody claims it. District authority decides. And district authority these days—" he glanced at the grey coats at the end of the pier and finished, shrugging, "—is whatever the gentleman with the rifle says it is."

Beatrix walked home along the towpath slowly, watching the water carry the evening downstream toward a capital that had stopped answering its mail, and permitted themselves, for exactly the length of the towpath, to think the unthinkable thought in its plainest form: *Who.*

Not whether. Whether was settled; the arithmetic admitted no other verdicts. But *who*. Whose name went in the empty box. Their own—they were the courier, they knew the routes, they might yet be worth more outside the town than in it. Marisol, who was sixty-three and had knees that sang before rain and who was, though she would deny it with a wooden spoon, the load-bearing wall of everything. Tomas, whose hands could forge a watermark but could not forge his way past a checkpoint without shaking. Danya, Petra, Fen, Yeva, the lodgers, the Kesses, the baby—the Kesses had a baby, eight months old, who figured on no list anywhere in the world—

Beatrix stopped at the rail of the footbridge and gripped it and made themself breathe.

This was how it started. This was the mechanism, the little machine they built in you before they built anything else: give people one berth and twenty-two names and they will do your work for you, they will sort themselves, they will lie awake dividing the beloved into the saved and the spent. No soldier needed to hold a gun. The ledger did it. Paper did what armies only dreamed of.

*I am not going to be the person who counts,* Beatrix told the river.

The river, being a river, did not answer, and Beatrix walked the rest of the way home counting anyway.

* * *

Supper that night was barley and the last of the salt pork, and Marisol had made it stretch, because Marisol could make anything stretch except time. The talk was of the festival—the harvest festival was still officially scheduled, though the whole town knew it would be cancelled, and talked about it anyway with the stubbornness of people insisting on a future. Petra wanted to dance the ring dance even if she danced it alone in the square. Fen said his father had danced it in the year of the flood and the flood receded by morning, and that this was either a story about luck or about dancing, he had never determined which.

Beatrix ate and smiled in the right places and underneath the smiling ran the column of names like a debtor running sums, and hated themself with a clean and thorough hatred that was, they noticed, almost a comfort, because hatred of oneself was at least a form of company.

Twenty-two. One.

After supper, when the others had drifted to their tasks—Petra to the soldiers' laundry she took in for bread, Danya to barter, Fen to sleep in his chair as he now did most evenings—Marisol stayed at the table with mending, and Beatrix stayed too, drawn and repelled in equal measure, orbiting the person they had lied to at breakfast.

"The barge comes on the fourteenth," Marisol said, to her needle, not to Beatrix.

"So the dispatch says."

"And how many berths does the republic spare Callowmere?"

"Ninety-one."

"Mm." The needle went in and out, in and out, dragging its thread through the sock's heel like a slow seam being opened. "And how many does it spare us? Us meaning this table, this shop, everyone under this roof and in those cellars. How many names of ours are written down somewhere official?"

The lamp hissed. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard spoke under someone's weight.

"You know the answer to that," Beatrix said quietly.

"I know it. I've known it since the clerk's boat came. I buried two households before this one, Beatrix—I know exactly what the state keeps and what it throws away." She bit her thread, cleanly, and looked up, and her eyes in the lamplight were dry and very old and very steady. "I'm asking what *you're* going to do about it."

And here was the moment. Here was the door standing open, the truth lying on the table between them like a dropped knife anyone could pick up. *I'm going to forge the Atlas,* Beatrix could have said. Could have said: Tomas has found the plates, the watermark can be matched, we can write ourselves real, we can commit treason with a pen nib and save every soul in this building. They opened their mouth to say it.

What came out instead, smooth and warm as poured honey, was: "I'll get us berths. However I have to. Trust me."

Which was true, and was also a lie, because it withheld the shape of the how, and the shape of the how was everything—it was the oath Beatrix had sworn over a courier's seal, it was the difference between rescue and forgery, between saving this family and making criminals of them, and Beatrix handed Marisol the honeyed version because some cowardly, protective chamber of their heart had calculated—there it was again, the arithmetic, everywhere, corrosive, inescapable—that Marisol's blessing was easier to obtain without the bill of particulars.

Marisol studied them across the mending for a long moment.

"However you have to," she repeated.

"However I have to."

"Well then." She bent back to her sock, and Beatrix felt the lie settle into place between them, quiet as a splinter working its way under skin, unnoticed now, destined to be felt later. "In that case, eat something. You've got a conspirator's face tonight, and thin conspirators make mistakes."

She knew. Some part of her knew, the way she had always known—but she asked no further questions, and Beatrix escaped up the stairs to the back room where Tomas sat waiting with the atlas plates laid out under cloth like surgical instruments, and the two of them looked at each other across the drafting table, and neither spoke, because speaking would make it real.

It was very late when the knock came at the shop's front door—not the household knock, not any knock belonging to the world of family. Three measured blows, military in their patience. Through the shutter slats Beatrix saw lamplight in the square, and grey coats, and at their center a tall figure consulting a pocket watch with an air of a man arriving precisely on time.

Captain Ilves had come to the shop, and he was not alone, and he was carrying, Beatrix saw with a cold that started in the scalp and went downward, a document case stamped with the seal of the district census office.
