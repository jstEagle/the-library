## 6. Captain Ilves

The soldiers came back on the fourth morning with a captain.

Beatrix knew it was a captain because of how the others arranged themselves around him—not in formation exactly, but the way iron filings arrange themselves around a lodestone, without being told. He walked through Callowmere's square at a pace that suggested he had all the time in a world that did not, and he stopped in front of the atlas-maker's shop the way a man stops in front of a shop he has already decided to buy.

He was not what Beatrix expected. They had spent four days learning to expect brutes—men with the war already written on them in mud and old blood—but this one wore his grey uniform as if it were merely the coat that had been handed to him that morning. He was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples, with a face built for patience rather than violence. He carried no sidearm that Beatrix could see. He carried instead a leather folio under one arm and, tucked into his belt where another man would keep a pistol, a small brass instrument whose purpose Beatrix recognized only because of who they lived with: a surveyor's sighting compass.

*Oh,* Beatrix thought, standing behind the counter with their hands folded like a shopkeeper's, *oh no.*

"You are the courier." It was not a question. "Okafor. The records list you as neutral transport. I have read your file." The accent placed vowels oddly, made each word sit a half-beat apart from its neighbors, but there was nothing broken in it. "You may breathe. I am not going to shoot you for breathing."

"I hadn't planned to stop," Beatrix said, and immediately heard how it sounded, and hated themselves for it, and could do nothing about it now except let it stand.

Something moved at the corner of the captain's mouth. Not quite a smile. A smile's distant cousin, one removed twice over. "Good. I prefer people who answer. The ones who apologize make me nervous; I begin to wonder what they are apologizing for."

Behind Beatrix, in the doorway that led back to the workshop, Marisol stood very still. She had been kneading bread when the grey column entered the square, and she still had flour to her wrists, and she held her hands up slightly away from her apron as though flour might be mistaken for something incriminating. Petra was supposed to be sweeping the front step. There was no sign of Petra, which meant she was either hiding or watching, and Beatrix prayed—for the first time in years, sincerely, without knowing to whom—that it was watching, because Petra who watched learned what faces to fear and Petra who hid learned only that hiding was necessary.

"Captain," Beatrix said. "You haven't told me your name."

"Ilves." He said it plainly, the way a man says a word he has carried so long it has worn smooth. "Kaspar Ilves. And you will forgive me—" he turned, slowly, taking in the shop with the unhurried attention of a man reading rather than looking, the map drawers, the drying racks of freshly pressed sheets, the great press itself squatting in the corner like a patient animal, "—but I have wanted to see this place since before I ever saw this town. My father was a surveyor. In the border provinces, when I was young, every map we used came out of shops like this one. Vessany paper. Vessany ink. You were famous for accuracy." He touched the edge of a drying rack, lightly, the way one touches an artifact in a museum. "Accuracy is a kind of honesty, don't you find? The most honest thing a country produces. More honest than its speeches. Certainly more honest than its ledgers."

"The shop is closed to new commissions," Beatrix said. "Since the requisition."

"Yes. I signed the requisition." He said it without apology, which was somehow worse than if he'd offered one. "I need the front room. Windows on two sides of the square, sight lines down both streets, and a building the townspeople already trust enough to walk into without flinching. We will be running signals from here. Telegraph wire comes through tomorrow. Your workshop stays yours—I am not interested in your maps." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on a table. "Unless you are interested in showing me some."

*There it is.* Beatrix kept their face doing the small, bored work of a tired shopkeeper. "We sell maps, Captain. We don't perform them."

"No," Ilves agreed pleasantly. "I imagine you don't."

He opened his folio then and produced papers—requisition orders, billeting schedules, the bureaucratic sediment of occupation—and laid them on the counter between them like a man laying out cards he intended to win with rather than play. Beatrix read them because refusing to read them would be worse than reading them. Behind the counter, below the level of the captain's sight line, Beatrix's own heart performed its treachery: steady, steady, steady. Years of carrying dispatches through checkpoints had taught their body this trick, that fear is loudest in the breath and quietest in the hands, and so they kept their hands flat and still on the wood.

*And underneath those hands, six feet of floorboards, and beneath the floorboards a cellar, and beneath the cellar's false wall a hollowed folio holding the names of every soul in this province promised a boat out—and above all of it, this man, reading the room the way Tomas reads terrain.*

"The army arrives in full within three days," Ilves said. "You will hear it before you see it—the river carries sound strangely here, I'm told. When it comes, this town becomes a supply node. That is not cruelty; it is arithmetic. But arithmetic can be done gently or brutally, and which of those it is depends considerably on whether the people I deal with give me reasons to complicate my sums." He looked up from the papers, and his eyes were pale grey, river-grey, the color of a thing that moves around obstacles without ever appearing to change course. "I am told you are the head of the household here."

"I run the shop."

"There are seven of you registered as servants and staff." He consulted a page. "A cook. An apprentice. A cartographer—retired, the record says, though the guild rolls show him struck off rather than retired, which is a different word for a different thing. Two laborers. A girl of fourteen with no surname on file at all, which means someone chose not to give her one, which means someone was protecting her from something, or protecting something from her. And yourself." He closed the folio. "Seven people in a shop that employed three, in a town where everyone knows everyone, and no one thought to mention the discrepancy to the census clerks. Curious, isn't it, how the invisible live. They require such careful tending."

The silence stretched. Beatrix counted it—one, two, three—because counting kept the voice level when it finally came.

"We take in strays," Beatrix said. "It's a bad habit. Marisol would tell you it's hers, originally."

"And yet you say *we*." Ilves gathered his papers with two precise taps against the countertop. "Captain Okafor—" he paused, and Beatrix saw him register the mistake even as he made it, saw the faint adjustment behind his eyes, "—Courier Okafor. Forgive me. Old habit. Everyone in uniform is a captain to me until proven otherwise." He almost smiled again, and this time the cousin-of-a-smile reached further, and Beatrix understood suddenly that the errors were not errors at all, that this man misspoke the way a fisherman jigs a line, to see what rises. "I will want to speak with you regularly. Not interrogations—I find those produce lies, and I have enough paperwork without adding lies to it. Conversations. About the town, its moods, its stores, who is hoarding, who is frightened, who is lying. Small things." He settled his cap. "In exchange, your household remains unregistered beyond its current fiction, your shop keeps its roof, and when the lists come—and lists always come, Courier Okafor, I have watched them come in three provinces—your seven names will be found where they do you the least harm."

*Your seven names.*

Not eight. He said seven. He counted seven, and either he had miscounted the household by one, or he was leaving a space open—a berth, a blank, a hook—and waiting to see who swam toward it.

"That is very generous," Beatrix said.

"It is practical," Ilves corrected. "Generosity is expensive and I am a frugal man. Practicality costs nothing and buys everything." At the door he stopped, one hand on the frame, and glanced back at the great press in the corner with an expression Beatrix could not entirely read—something older than the war, something almost like grief. "My father used to say that a country is a story its maps agree to tell. When I was nineteen I left home to fight for a country whose story I believed. Now I requisition map shops." He straightened. "Think about what I've offered. Not tonight. Tonight, think about supper. What is the cook making? Something with onions, unless my nose has died along with everything else."

"Lamb," Marisol said from the doorway, before Beatrix could speak, her voice flat as a slate. "With onions."

"Then I envy you, and I will try not to hold it against you." And Kaspar Ilves stepped out into the square, into the thin autumn light, and his men folded around him, and the shop door closed, and the bell above it rang once, small and bright, like a coin dropped into a well.

* * *

They did not talk about it until nightfall. This was discipline, not cowardice; the house had learned in four days of occupation that walls in a requisitioned building belong to whoever requisitioned them, and that grief and strategy must both be conducted in whispers or in the garden, under the noise of wind.

So they worked. Beatrix copied dispatches at the signals desk where a corporal with chilblained ears dictated in a bored monotone, transcribing orders about grain tonnage and bridge inspections, and Beatrix wrote them in a fair hand and thought, beneath each line, *I am copying the sentences of the thing that is coming to erase us, and my handwriting has never been neater.* Tomas repaired a jammed drawer in the map cases with his one good hand and his teeth, holding the screwdriver like a pen. Marisol cooked lamb with onions for eleven soldiers and seven liars and served both tables with the same face.

It was past ten, after the corporal had gone to his billet and the lamps in the square had been doused to regulation darkness, that the household gathered in the kitchen—the one room whose window faced the garden, the one room with a rug over loose boards—and Beatrix told them all of it. The compass at his belt. The file with Beatrix's name in it. The count of seven.

"He knows," Tomas said. He sat with his ruined hand cradled in his lap, the way he always sat since the accident that had ended his career and begun, though none of them had known it, the life that became this one. His eyes had the fixed brightness they got when a problem had him by the throat. "Not everything. But he knows the shape of the hole. Seven names where eight people breathe—he wasn't reading a report, Beatrix, he was setting out bait. He wants us to correct his arithmetic for him. Whoever does that tells him everything."

"Or he genuinely miscounted," Petra said, from the shadows by the stove, where she had installed herself with the shameless entitlement of the youngest. "Servants all look the same to officers. Maybe he just lost track."

"He didn't lose track." Beatrix heard their own voice come out flat and certain and wished it hadn't, because certainty is contagious and this was not a disease anyone in the room needed to catch. "Men like that don't lose track. Losing track is what other people do while he watches."

"So what do we do?" That was Dario, one of the laborers, a deserter from a war two borders away who had not spoken his own family name aloud in nine years. He was wringing his cap in his fists. "Do we take what he's offering? Protection is protection. If the lists come and our names are found 'where they do us least harm'—"

"—and what harm does he mean, exactly?" Marisol's voice cut across the kitchen, quiet and total, the way a blade is quiet. She had not sat down. She stood at the cold stove with her arms crossed, flour still ghosting her forearms though the day's baking was hours dead. "You've heard it before. I've heard it before. Twice, in two countries. *Give me small things,* they say. *Who is frightened, who is lying.* And the small things get bigger, and one morning the man who took your small things is standing in your kitchen asking why you didn't mention the family in the attic, and the protection he sold you turns out to have been a receipt." She looked at Beatrix. Only at Beatrix. "What did you tell him?"

"Nothing. I told him we take in strays."

"Is that what we are?"

The question landed in the middle of the kitchen and lay there. Beatrix met her eyes and could not look away from them, brown and fierce and older than any ledger, and said, "That's what we are to him. To us—we're whatever we decide to call ourselves. That's the whole point of us. Nobody gave us our names. Nobody gets to count them but us."

Marisol held the look a moment longer. Then she nodded once, and began banking the stove coals, and the conversation was over the way her conversations ended—by decree of the weather in the room. One by one the others went up to bed. Petra lingered, and Beatrix caught her sleeve at the stairs.

"When he was talking," Beatrix said quietly, "you weren't hiding. You were watching. Where were you?"

"The step. With the broom." Petra's chin lifted, defensive. "I swept. Nobody stops a girl with a broom. I got close enough to see his boots."

"What about his boots?"

"He stands like Tomas does when he's drawing." She said it simply, the way children say devastating things, and went up the stairs, and left Beatrix standing in the dark kitchen with the words turning over like a stone in water.

*Like Tomas when he's drawing.* Weight forward. Eyes measuring. The stance of a person reading the world in order to record it.

Beatrix banked the last coal, climbed to the loft above the workshop where they slept, and lay awake listening to the river. The sound carried strangely here—Ilves had been right about that, damn him—and somewhere out on the black water Beatrix could almost convince themselves they could already hear it: the deep, patient knocking of an army coming downstream, three days out, wrapped in fog, wrapped in oilcloth, wrapped in the ordinary dark.

*Cooperation in exchange for protection.* The offer sat in Beatrix's chest like swallowed lead. And the worst of it—the part that would keep them staring at the rafters until well past midnight—was not that the offer was evil. Evil could be refused cleanly, the way you refuse a blow. The worst of it was that the offer was *reasonable*. A roof. A name found kindly on a list. Seven lives made gentle by arithmetic. All it cost was small things, told to a courteous man with a poet's mouth and a surveyor's eye, small things that would grow the way small things grown by such men always grew.

Beatrix thought of the file with their name in it. *Neutral transport.* They had spent thirty years building a self so unremarkable that history would slide past without catching on it, and in one afternoon a man in grey had read the whole architecture off a page and stood in the shop touching the drying racks like a pilgrim.

Sleep came late and thin. And it was barely past dawn, gray light just beginning to show the dust motes above the press, when Beatrix came down the ladder and found Tomas already at the workbench, hunched under a lamp, the Atlas's empty twin—an ordinary folio of the same binding, pulled from stock—open before him. He held a sheet of blank security paper up to the window glass, angling it against the light, and his face had gone the color of the paper itself.

"Beatrix," he said, without turning, without greeting, in the voice of a man standing at the edge of something. "Come here. You need to see what I've just realized we can do."
