## 7. Ink Under the Fingernails

Tomas Veyra had spent thirty years learning to see what other people's hands had done, and the first thing he saw when Beatrix set the Atlas on his workbench was that it had been made by someone afraid.

He knew fear in paper the way a physician knew fever. The clerk who had assembled this ledger—some grey functionary in the capital, dead now or fled, it hardly mattered—had pressed every page with the same anxious perfectionism that Tomas himself had once brought to his plates. Even margins. Even spacing. The hand of a person who believed that if only the record were clean enough, the world would leave it alone.

Tomas turned to the window and tilted the third folio toward the last of the evening light.

And there it was.

A watermark, ghost-pale, rising out of the sheet like a fish surfacing in dark water: the seal of the Republic of Vessany, the twin rivers braided through a compass rose, and beneath it a line of micro-lettering so fine it resolved only when the light came through at an angle—the words *Verum et Fidelis*, true and faithful, repeated around the circumference of the rose like a rosary.

He had cut that rose himself.

Not this one. Not this exact plate. But he had cut its grandmother, twenty-two years ago, when he was still Tomas Veyra, Master Engraver of the Republic Survey, when his name appeared on the colophon of every atlas used in the state schools, before the scandal, before the disgrace, before Marisol had found him sleeping in the doorway of her kitchen with his tools in a sack and his hands shaking from something he refused to call grief. The design had been his. He could feel the decisions of the original cutter in the lines themselves—the way the western river forked a hair too early because the engraver had been working against a deadline, the way the compass points were slightly elongated north-to-south because the plate had been reworked after a first proof came back too squat.

He knew these habits because they were his habits.

"Tomas." Beatrix stood in the doorway of the workroom, still in their courier's coat, oilcloth rain on their shoulders. "You've been up here three hours. Marisol wants to know if you're eating."

"Come look at this."

They crossed the room the way they did everything—quietly, economically, a person who had trained themselves out of taking up space. They bent over the folio. Their breath fogged the paper faintly, and Tomas watched the watermark swim in and out of visibility under it, alive, patient.

"That's the security mark," Beatrix said. Flat. Careful. "Every page."

"Every page. And do you know who drew the original?"

Beatrix looked at him. In the lamplight their face was all planes and watchfulness; they had the kind of face that made people underestimate how young it was. "No," they said, though their voice had already begun to suspect.

"I did. Or near enough. The Survey commissioned the seal in my fourth year. I cut the master plate myself." He touched the edge of the page, not the mark—he would not touch the mark, not yet, as if it might go off. "The republic taught me everything I know about making things that cannot be faked. And then it took my name off everything I'd ever made, and put me out into the street. So you'll forgive me if I find a certain irony in what I'm about to say."

"Don't," Beatrix said.

"The shop can forge this."

The word landed between them like a dropped glass. Downstairs, faintly, Marisol was moving pots; the household sounds rose through the floorboards, the ordinary music of supper being made by people who did not yet know they were uncounted.

"You don't know that," Beatrix said.

"Trixie." He almost never used the name; it was Petra's name for them, borrowed once and never returned. "I have the plates. In the cellar, behind the drying racks, wrapped in wax cloth. The old atlas plates—the ones the Survey declared obsolete when I was struck off. Including two runs of the state seal, cut for the commemorative edition that never went to press because the war ministry decided maps were a security risk." He laughed, short and bitter. "They were right. Maps are a security risk. So is a man who knows where every border stone is buried."

Beatrix straightened. They did not sit; they never sat when something frightened them. "The courier oath says—"

"I know what the courier oath says."

"—that the courier carries the record and alters nothing. That the record belongs to the republic, not to the courier. That the courier's neutrality is the only thing that makes them worth trusting with anything." Beatrix recited it the way other people recited scripture, without inflection, which somehow made it worse. "I took that oath. You didn't."

"No," Tomas agreed. "I took a different one. To the Survey. To make the map true. And when the map stopped being allowed to be true—when they redrew the eastern provinces to please a minister—I objected, and they destroyed me for it, and the republic went on lying beautifully in my absence." He pulled the lamp closer. "So let me tell you what I know about oaths, Trixie. An oath is ink. It is only as binding as the thing it binds you to. And the thing your oath binds you to—" he tapped the Atlas, gently, the way one taps a coffin, "—is dead. It died three days ago when that clerk handed it to you with his hands shaking. What's left is paper. And paper can be written on."

"Not by me."

"By us."

* * *

Marisol sent up bread and hard cheese and a jar of the plum preserve she hoarded like a dragon, and neither of them ate much of it. The argument went on past midnight, circling, wearing grooves in itself.

Beatrix paced the length of the workroom, four strides each way, turning at the window and the door like water in a lock. "If we alter the Atlas, we aren't saving anyone. We're gambling twenty-two lives on a forgery surviving an inspection."

"And if we don't alter it, we're choosing," Tomas said. "Right now, tonight, while everyone sleeps. You've done the arithmetic—I've seen you doing it, behind your eyes, every time you count heads at supper. One berth. Twenty-three of us if you count yourself, and you always forget to count yourself, which is its own confession. So either you choose who goes, or you make the ledger lie so that no one has to choose. Those are the options. There is no third door marked *virtue*."

"There's the door marked *oath*."

"The oath is a door painted on a wall."

Beatrix stopped pacing. When they turned, their face had gone very still, the stillness they wore like armor. "You think I haven't thought about it? Every night since the requisition, I lie awake and I count them. Marisol. Petra. Old Ferro with his bad lungs. The twins. You. I know exactly what one berth means. I have known since the day the soldiers marched in." Their voice stayed level, which was how Tomas knew the cost. "But the moment I put a false name in that book, I stop being the person the republic trusted and start being the person who proved it right not to trust anyone. Everything I am—everything that kept us invisible, that kept the soldiers' hands off this house—is built on being exactly what I said I was. A courier. Nothing more. The second I forge that ledger, I'm a criminal, and criminals get searched, and searched houses get emptied, and families get registered."

"And unregistered families get put on what list, exactly?" Tomas asked softly. "When the barges come and the lists are read, what happens to the names that were never written down?"

Silence. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the house ticked and settled, and outside, far off, a soldier's boots rang on the cobbles of the square—the occupation keeping its own kind of time.

"They don't happen," Beatrix said finally. "That's the answer. People whose names aren't written down don't happen. That's what the Atlas is. It isn't a passenger list. It's a proof that we existed."

"Yes," Tomas said. "Now you understand why I want to forge it."

He got up and went to the corner cabinet, the one with the false back he'd built in his first month at the shop, back when hiding things had seemed like a game. He lifted out the wax-cloth bundle and unwrapped it on the workbench with the reverence of a priest handling relics: six copper plates, green with age, each one engraved with the braid of rivers and the compass rose, each one cut by his own hand in a year when he had still believed the republic deserved him.

Beatrix stared at them for a long time. "These are evidence," they said at last. "Possession of state seal plates is—"

"Treason. Yes. I've been committing it for twenty years by owning them. I simply hadn't gotten around to the interesting part." He picked up the oldest plate and held it to the light, and his reflection swam in the copper, warped, an old man made of rivers. "Here is what I can do, and you should hear all of it before you decide, because you will decide, Trixie—you're the only one of us who decides things; the rest of us just survive them. I can pull proofs of the seal that will pass any casual inspection. Under lamplight, at arm's length, even in a clerk's hands. What it will not survive is an expert with a loupe and time. My cutting hand has changed in twenty years. The tremor alone—"

"You have a tremor?"

"I'm sixty-one and I drink. Of course I have a tremor." He set the plate down. "But the watermark isn't checked by clerks. It's checked by archivists, and only when something smells wrong. And nothing will smell wrong, because the entries will be in your hand, and your hand is known to the system—you've carried dispatches through three provinces. Your signature is on file in two capitals. If anyone doubts the Atlas, they doubt you, and no one doubts a courier. That's the whole architecture of your life, isn't it? Being beyond suspicion?"

It was not a kind thing to say, and he watched it land. Beatrix sat down for the first time that night, slowly, onto the stool by the bench, as if the sentence had weight enough to require it.

"If I do this," they said, "I can't undo it. There's no version where I hand back a clean ledger afterward. Either the forgery holds and we live as liars, or it fails and we die as forgers."

"Or it holds," Tomas said, "and someday, long after the war, some archivist opens this book and finds twenty-two names in a careful hand, entered late, in ink that doesn't quite match—and understands that somebody loved those people enough to commit treason against a dead government on their behalf. And that entry will be truer than every honest line above it." He shrugged. "History isn't what happened. History is what got written down by someone who cared. I spent my whole career believing otherwise. Don't waste your youth on my mistake."

Beatrix looked at the plates a long moment. Then: "Show me the watermark process. Just show me. I'm not agreeing to anything."

"Of course," Tomas lied, gently, because they both knew better.

* * *

They worked until the sky over the rooftops went the grey of dishwater. He showed them the dampening of the sheet, the precise pressure of the press, the way the seal rose in the fibers rather than sitting on them—a ghost living inside the paper instead of a mask worn on its face. He showed them how the micro-lettering caught light at ten degrees and vanished at fifteen, a secret that revealed itself only to the patient. Beatrix learned fast, hands steady, saying nothing, and Tomas understood that silence for what it was: a decision being made somewhere deep beneath the argument, in the place where Beatrix kept the things they never said aloud.

Near dawn, Beatrix washed the ink from their hands at the basin and stood looking at the water going grey.

"My mother was a registrar," they said, unexpectedly. "Back home, before. She wrote down births. Whole villages of them. She used to say a name on paper was the only immortality a poor person got." They dried their hands slowly. "She'd have hated me for even considering this. And she'd have done it herself by now."

Tomas said nothing. Some doors you did not push.

At the window, Callowmere woke under occupation: smoke from army cookfires, the square's fountain dry because the soldiers had drained it, a queue of townsfolk already forming at the requisition office with their papers clutched to their chests like children. Everyone suddenly eager to be written down. Everyone hoping their name was somewhere in someone's book.

"We'd need the real ink," Beatrix said quietly. "The state iron-gall, not the shop stock. And the entry format. And time."

"Three days for the plates. A week to be safe."

"The barge leaves in nine days."

"Then we'd best begin tonight," Tomas said, and began laying out the copper like a man setting a table for ghosts.

Beatrix stood in the doorway a moment longer, half in the room, half in the old life. Then they pulled the door shut behind them—not quite all the way—and Tomas heard their footsteps pause on the stair, heard the long breath they took, and understood that the argument was over, that it had been over for hours, and that downstairs, in a kitchen that smelled of bread and woodsmoke, twenty-two people were asleep who did not yet know they had just been saved.

Or damned. The copper gleamed. He could no longer always tell the difference, and suspected, in his darkest hours, that there had never been one—that every map ever drawn was a forgery of the world, and love was simply the willingness to keep drawing anyway.

He was cleaning the press when he heard, from the square below, the sharp triple whistle of the signals office, and then Captain Ilves's voice, clear across the morning air, giving an order Tomas could not make out—but whose tone he recognized from another life, another country, another set of men in grey:

Someone was coming to the shop.
