## 8. The Festival They Cancel

The notice went up on the customs house door on a Tuesday, nailed there by a soldier so young his belt rode up under his ribs, and by noon the whole town had come to read it and pretend they hadn't.

*By order of the occupying administration,* it said, in the flat bureaucratic hand that had lately begun to appear on everything from bread ration cards to the shutters of the chandlery, *the annual harvest festival of Callowmere is suspended for the duration. Public gatherings of more than five persons are prohibited. Violations will be treated as sedition.*

Marisol stood at the edge of the square with a market basket on her hip and read it twice, because the first time her eyes had refused the words, skittering off them like water off waxed cloth. Suspended. As though the festival were a clerk who could be sent home without pay. As though four hundred years of threshing floors and fiddle music and the blessing of the river's first catch were a matter of administrative duration.

Around her, the town did what towns do when grief arrives wearing an uniform: nothing, loudly. Old Ferenc spat into the gutter with theatrical precision. The baker's wife pressed her hand to her mouth as if at a funeral. A boy asked his mother what *sedition* meant and was hushed so sharply he began to cry.

Marisol bought her onions, counted her change, and walked home through streets gone quiet as held breath.

She had seen this before. That was the thing no one in Callowmere understood about her, because she kept it folded away like good linen saved for occasions that never came — she had seen exactly this before, twice, in two countries that no longer existed under those names. The first thing armies cancel is never the thing you'd expect. Not weapons, not travel, not even speech. It's the feast. The dance. The night when a hundred strangers hold hands in a circle and remember, bodily, in their feet and their lungs, that they belong to each other. Armies understand something about feasts that governments never do: that joy is an allegiance, and unmanaged joy is treason waiting to happen.

So they had cancelled it, and Marisol, standing in her kitchen with the onions sweating in goose fat and the smell of them rising like ordinary time itself, decided — with the same calm finality with which she had once crossed a border with false papers sewn into her coat lining — that they would have it anyway.

* * *

She told no one for two days. This was not secrecy so much as architecture; a thing built hastily collapses, and she wanted this one sound.

She began with the wine. There were six bottles of the black Vessian red left from the household's winter stores, and she had watched the level drop with a housekeeper's disapproval since the soldiers came. But there was also the cellar beneath the shop, and in the cellar, behind the rack where Tomas stored his blank plates, there was a bricked alcove from some older use of the building — an ice house, maybe, or worse — and in the alcove the old atlas-maker had kept a dozen bottles of the good vintage, the year of the comet, dust-furred and forgotten. Marisol had found them her first month in the house, while chasing a runaway jar of rendered fat across the cellar floor, and she had said nothing to anyone, because a household survives on its hidden stores, both the kind you drink and the kind you keep in your chest.

Now she took down three of the comet-year bottles and wiped them clean with a rag, and holding the first up to the candle flame she saw the dark glass glow like garnet, like a church window, like something that had been waiting all along for its occasion.

Then the food. This was harder. Requisition had thinned the town the way winter thins a flock — not to dying, but to that careful, feather-fluffed economy of the half-starved. She traded. Marisol had spent forty years learning the grammar of kitchens and she spoke it fluently: two hours of kneading for the baker's wife in exchange for flour the ledger would never see; a jar of Marisol's pickled quince, which had a reputation in three provinces, slipped wordlessly across the fishmonger's counter to come home wrapped in paper as a pair of river eels. She plucked and gutted them at the sink with her sleeves rolled, working by feel, and thought of her mother's hands doing the same in a kitchen whose country had already been erased from the maps, and how her mother had sung while she worked, always, as though song were a preservative and grief would spoil otherwise.

On the third evening, after the signals office lamps went dark across the square and the grey patrol made its nine o'clock circuit, Marisol gathered them in the kitchen and told them, in the voice she used for carving poultry and for settling arguments, that tomorrow night there would be a festival, and attendance was not optional.

* * *

Petra nearly ruined it inside of a minute, which was Petra's gift. "But it's *illegal*," she said, eyes enormous, and Beatrix — who had been sitting very still by the window all week, in the way Marisol had learned to read as either worry or scheming, and lately could not tell which frightened her more — looked over sharply.

"It's supper," Marisol said. "It's supper with candles, because we haven't paid the lighting tax and I'm thrifty."

"But the notice said—"

"The notice," Marisol said, wiping her hands on her apron, "was written by a boy younger than you who has never eaten anything cooked slowly. In my experience such boys are easily impressed by eels." She let her voice soften just enough. "Petro. Little bird. Some laws are walls, and some are weather, and you learn to tell which is which, or you spend your whole life indoors."

Petra chewed on this. It was one of the things Marisol loved about her, that ferocious appetite for ideas, as though the girl had arrived at their door at eleven years old starving for words more than bread, though God knew she'd been starving for both. "And if a soldier asks?"

"Then we are having supper," Marisol said, "like every family in the world," and turned back to the stove before anyone could see what the sentence cost her, because it wasn't true, it hadn't been true for weeks, and every one of them at that table knew it. Every family in the world was not having supper in a town full of men in grey with a ledger somewhere that decided who lived and who was left. She felt the knowledge sit in her like a swallowed stone. She had been carrying it since the day the clerk died and the oilcloth bundle came up from the boat, since the evening she had stood in the doorway of Beatrix's room and seen the Atlas open on the desk and understood, in one cold instant, what was missing from its pages — but that was a stone for another hour, and tonight, tonight she was building something, and you do not set your foundation stones in the middle of the feast.

* * *

They held it in the cellar, because the cellar had no windows facing the square, and because — though Marisol only admitted this to herself as she carried the first candles down — a festival belongs underground now, the way seeds do. Winter wheat. Something planted in the dark on faith.

Tomas brought up two trestle boards and the household's mismatched chairs, and for an hour the cellar rang with the small domestic percussion of a table being made: boards knocked together, chairs scraped, Petra dispatched up and down the stairs with armloads of spoons until she protested she was being used as a pack animal and Marisol said yes, and a beloved one. Candles went onto every surface they could find, stubs and ends and the three tall beeswax tapers Marisol had been hoarding against a catastrophe she had supposed would be grander than this. Their light moved on the low stone vaulting like water, like the underside of a bridge, and the twenty-two of them — the whole strange arithmetic of the household, everyone the roads and rivers and wars had sent to her door these fifteen years — sat down around one table for the first time in longer than any of them could say.

That was the thing. That was the thing Marisol understood while the eels went round in their butter and the bread, baked that afternoon in secret, gave off its smell of crust and patience: they had lived together for years and never once *sat together*. There had always been someone at the market, someone on courier rounds, someone keeping watch out of habit even before there was anything to watch for. A family, she thought, passing the wine, is not a thing you are given and not even a thing you build. It is a thing you keep meaning to get around to, and then an army cancels your festival, and you find you cannot bear it, and so you finally sit down.

The comet-year wine was alive still. It poured the color of old blood and tasted of smoke and cherries and some darker fruit she couldn't name, and old Ivo — deserter, insomniac, the man who had taught three generations of the household's children to whistle — drank his first sip with his eyes closed and pronounced it worth whatever sin they'd commit to finish the bottle. The second bottle opened faster than the first. Conversation loosened and braided itself: Petra recounting, in a whisper pitched to carry to the far wall, the soldier who had tripped over his own rifle on the quay; the twins arguing about whether the eel had souls; Tomas, flushed with wine, telling a story about the cartography bureau that ended with him saying *so then the minister says, that's not a mountain, that's my mother-in-law*, and the table going up like dry straw.

Marisol sat at the head with her cup and mostly listened, which was her role and her pleasure and her disguise. From here she could count them, and she did, the way she had counted them every night since the requisition — a glance down the table, lips moving faintly, a mother's arithmetic. Twenty-two. Her eye snagged, as it always did, on the empty chair that was not empty of anything but a person: the place they still set for the woman who had run off north with the ferryman's son the spring before last, because Marisol did not strike places from the table, ever, on principle, and because hope was cheaper than plates.

"Sing," said little Ana, who was six and feared nothing, standing on her chair with her cup of milk raised. "You have to sing at festivals. It's a rule."

"We don't know any festival songs," said one of the twins. "We know work songs and funeral songs."

"That's all anybody knows," said Ivo. "Festival songs are just work songs played too fast and funeral songs played too loud."

"Sing the river one," Ana demanded. "The one Marisol sings when she thinks nobody's listening."

There was laughter at that, warm and teasing, and Marisol felt her face go hot and was glad the candlelight hid it. "I sing to keep the eels calm," she said, "it's a professional technique," but they were already chanting, all of them, even Beatrix — quiet Beatrix, careful Beatrix, who watched the stairs with a courier's habit even holding a wine cup — even Beatrix was chanting *the river one, the river one*, and so Marisol stood, because a matriarch knows when resistance becomes rudeness, and sang.

It was an old song, older than the republic whose evacuation ledger lay hidden upstairs in a hollowed folio, older than most of the borders on any map Tomas had ever drawn or unmade. It was a rowing song, really, a woman's song about carrying a boat past the shallows, about how the river does not ask your name but carries you all the same. Her mother's melody, her grandmother's, worn smooth as a stair stone by generations of women singing it in kitchens whose countries had been cancelled. She sang the first verse alone, and by the second Ivo's low rasp had joined her, and by the third the whole table — Tomas off-key with total commitment, Petra shrill and certain, the twins harmonizing out of spite, Beatrix's voice barely there but there, *there*, underneath, steady as a keel — and Marisol stood in the candlelight with her hands open at her sides and conducted them without moving, the way mothers conduct, by sheer standing-ness, and the song rose into the stone vaulting and filled the dark of the cellar the way water fills a well.

For the length of that song — two minutes, three — the household at the atlas-maker's shop was simply a family. Not registered, not listed, not counted in any column. Officially nobody. And singing like the whole world.

When it ended they were all quiet for a moment, the way people are after singing together, that raw sweet hush. Then Ana said, "Again," and they laughed and poured the last of the second bottle, and Marisol sat back down and let someone else carve the honey cake she had smuggled down under her shawl, and for one entire hour she did not think about ledgers or berths or barges, did not do the arithmetic she had forbidden herself and performed nightly, did not look at Beatrix across the candlelight and wonder what was being planned behind those careful eyes. For one hour the stone in her chest was just a stone, and the wine was just wine, and joy was not defiance but simply joy, which was the most defiant thing of all.

It could not last. Nothing that beautiful is allowed to last; she had buried two families' worth of proof of that. Near midnight the patrol passed above on the square — she heard the boots through the floorboards, four sets, unhurried — and the singing stopped on its own, twenty-two people going silent in the same breath, and the candles seemed suddenly very small, and the dark beyond the cellar door seemed suddenly very large. The boots faded. After a while Ivo started a slower song, and they got through half of it before Marisol called the ending, because a wise host ends the party while the guests are still happy rather than afraid.

They cleaned the cellar by candle stub, quietly, efficiently, erasing themselves. Chairs stacked. Boards carried up. Wax scraped and crumbs gathered for the morning's birds. By dawn the room below the shop would be a cellar again, innocent of everything but wine racks and blank plates.

It was Petra who lingered, hanging back on the lowest step while the others climbed, tugging Marisol's sleeve.

"Marisol. If we're not in the book — the book upstairs, the name book—" The girl's voice dropped to almost nothing. "Does the song still count? Does any of it count, if we're not written anywhere?"

Marisol looked down at her for a long moment. Sixteen years old, flour on her cuff, wine-stained mouth, the bravest person in the building asking the question Marisol had been swallowing for a month.

"It counts," she said. "We were singing before there were books, little bird." And she kissed the top of Petra's head and sent her up to bed.

But long after the candles were out, Marisol sat alone in the dark kitchen with the last inch of comet-year wine in her cup, and the question stayed. Because she had seen the Atlas, and she knew what was missing from it, and she knew — had known since the night she'd stood in Beatrix's doorway — that Beatrix knew it too, and that something was taking shape in that careful, quiet head, ink-colored and dangerous.

Upstairs, past midnight, she saw it begin: light leaking under the door of Beatrix's room, and within it, faintly, the small industrious sound of a pen.
