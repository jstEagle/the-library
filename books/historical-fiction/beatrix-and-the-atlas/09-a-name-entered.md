## 9. A Name Entered

The signals office kept its own hours, and they were not the hours of living people. Between the last relief of the night watch and the first grey stirring of the soldiers in the square, the shop that had once smelled of glue and calf-skin and the particular chalky sweetness of fresh map ink now smelled of lamp oil, cold iron, and Beatrix's own fear, which had a scent by now—copper and lavender soap, because Beatrix scrubbed their hands too often, trying to wash off something that lived deeper than skin.

Three o'clock was the hour. Beatrix had learned it the way one learns anything under occupation: by watching, by counting, by pretending to be duller than a doorpost. Captain Ilves dismissed his night clerk at three. The clerk—a soft-bodied boy from the capital with a perpetually chapped nose—took the corridor stairs down to the barracks kitchen, where a pot of barley soup sat on the hob until dawn, and he did not come back up for forty minutes at least. Forty minutes. Beatrix had timed it eleven times, hunched over requisition forms in the back room with a pencil, appearing to work, while the pocket watch Tomas had repaired for them years ago ticked against their ribs like a second heart.

Tonight was the twelfth.

Tomas had made the watermark plate two nights ago, working by feel more than sight, his ruined eyes bent close to the copper as though listening to it. The Atlas's security mark—the twin rivers of Vessany braided around a mountain, so fine that the eye read it only as a shimmer beneath honest paper—was now scratched into a plate no bigger than a playing card, and the flaw he had warned about was invisible to Beatrix, which meant nothing, because Beatrix was no expert. But the test impressions had been good. They had dried between the pages of an old school atlas, pressed flat under Marisol's bread board, and when Beatrix held one up to the lamp, the rivers shimmered. The paper believed it was official. Paper could be lied to. That was the whole terrible discovery of Beatrix's life, arriving late, like everything else.

The hard part was not the watermark. The hard part was the hand.

* * *

Beatrix had spent two evenings with the Atlas open to its blankest page, not writing, only reading—not the names, but the way they were written. Clerk's hands were like accents; every ledger office in Vessany taught a slightly different slant, and this book had been begun in the provincial registry at Halden Cross and finished, apparently, in three other towns besides. Entry one hundred and twelve was cramped and righteous. Entry four hundred and sixty was loose and tired, the letters leaning east like wheat after wind. The final entries, made by whoever had inherited the dying task, were careful to the point of fear—each name set down as though the pen were afraid of waking someone.

Beatrix practiced those last entries on butcher's paper until the practice sheets filled a drawer. *Varga, Emil. Varga, Emil. Varga, Emil.* Over and over, adjusting the pressure, the little hesitation before the final vowel that the last clerk always allowed himself, as if checking the spelling against some inner list. Beatrix learned his boredom and his diligence both. By the second midnight, Beatrix could produce the man's hand better than the man could have, probably, if the man had still been alive—which, given what the river brought and took these days, was not a safe assumption.

"You write him better than God made him," Tomas had said, watching over their shoulder, and Beatrix had not laughed.

Because that was the thing about forgery that nobody warned you: it was not a trick. It was an intimacy. To forge a clerk you had to become him for the length of a name, to inhabit his tired wrist, his small hesitations, his obedience to a state that had already stopped deserving any of it. And once you had been inside another person's hand once, the door did not close behind you. You could go back in whenever you liked. That was what frightened Beatrix—not being caught, not yet—but how wide the door stood.

* * *

At seven minutes past three, the clerk's boots clumped down the stairs. Beatrix counted to two hundred, listening to the house breathe—the soldiers' snoring through the floorboards like distant surf, the settling of old timbers, the rain beginning somewhere over the roof in a fine, indifferent drizzle.

Then they went to the desk.

The signals office occupied what had been the atlas showroom, and the army had changed almost nothing, which was its own kind of obscenity. The long maple counter where customers had once spread maps of the coast and traced holidays with their fingers now held signal ledgers, a semaphore codebook, a lamp with a green glass shade that turned everyone's hands the color of drowned things. And there, in the locked drawer whose key Ilves wore on a chain under his tunic—no. Not the drawer. Beatrix had watched Ilves return it two nights ago, and tonight, unusually, carelessly, it sat half-open under a stack of intercepted mail. Perhaps the clerk's laziness. Perhaps something else. Beatrix would think about that later, or spend years failing not to.

The Atlas came out heavy as a sleeping animal. Oilcloth cover, brass corners gone green, the embossed republic seal worn smooth by ten thousand hands that had trusted it. Beatrix set it on the counter and did not open it right away. Instead they stood with their palms flat on either side of it, the way one stands at the edge of cold water.

Underneath the fear there was a silence, and in the silence a single sentence kept repeating itself: *Her name is not in the book.*

Marisol Reyes. Sixty-one years old, cook, matriarch, keeper of the cellar and the household's whole fragile calendar of saints' days and birthdays and the anniversary of the flood. She had buried a husband in the first war and two children in the peace between wars, and she had crossed half the country with nothing and built this family out of strays the way other people build fires—piece by piece, from whatever was lying around cold. She had fed deserters off the books and orphans off her own plate. She was the reason the word *household* meant anything at all in that building. And in the ledger of every soul promised rescue from the coming army, she did not exist. None of them did. Twenty-two people, and the republic's memory contained zero.

One stroke of a pen, and she would exist.

Beatrix opened the book to the final completed entry and laid the forged leaf beside it—the watermark sheet, cut to size, edges roughened with a fingernail and a breath of tea to age it—and tipped the lamp lower. The green shade made the blank paper look like river ice.

They wrote.

The pen was the dead clerk's own, left in the drawer with his ink, which had felt like a sign at the time and felt now like standing in a dead man's shoes. *M.* The clerk began his capitals with a small downward stab, impatient, certain. Beatrix gave him that certainty. *a-r-i-s-o-l.* The letters leaned east, tired, true. The nib caught once on the sizing and Beatrix's heart slammed—*stop, blot, don't fight it*—and then released, and the rest of the name flowed out of the pen as if it had been waiting in the ink all along, *Reyes*, with the little hesitation before the final *s*, the clerk checking his inner list, finding her there, allowing it.

Below the name: parish of origin. Below that: priority class, and here Beatrix committed the second crime inside the first, entering her under *household dependent, registered*—a category that existed, a category that was true in every way except the one the state cared about.

The ink took perfectly.

That was the unbearable part. Not difficulty but ease. The paper drank Marisol's name the way soil drinks rain, without discrimination, without judgment, welcoming poison and blessing alike. There was no thunder. No line of soldiers at the door. The lamp hissed softly. Rain ticked at the dark windows. Somewhere below, Tomas turned over in his sleep, and the whole world accepted the lie as readily as it had ever accepted the truth. Beatrix had believed, in some unexamined chamber of themselves, that the universe guarded such things—that names were stitched into existence by lives, by love, by the accumulated witness of other people, and that to counterfeit one would require something enormous, some Faustian machinery. It required eleven seconds and a dead man's pen.

*Marisol Reyes* shone wetly in the lamplight, and then dried, and became simply a fact.

Beatrix pressed the heel of their hand against their mouth. They thought: *I could do this forever.* They thought: *that is exactly why I must do it only twenty-one more times.* Because the ease did not fade—it compounded. Every name entered would make the next one lighter, and there was a version of Beatrix, waiting a few steps down that widening road, who could write anyone into or out of anything, who could remake a town, a province, a history, and sleep soundly after. The oath of neutrality they had sworn as a courier—*carry, never create; witness, never author*—had been designed, Beatrix realized now, not to protect the state from its couriers, but to protect the couriers from precisely this vertigo.

Too late for that. Beatrix blew on the ink, slipped the leaf into the binding with the others, and closed the Atlas.

* * *

"An interesting hour for correspondence."

The voice came from the doorway to the corridor, and Beatrix's body performed a betrayal entirely of its own: they turned, slowly, eyebrows lifting into mild, sleepy inquiry, one hand drifting to the back of their neck in the universal gesture of the harmless woken worker. Later they would be astonished by the performance. In the moment there was no performance, only an animal calm with nothing behind it.

Captain Ilves stood in the doorway in his shirtsleeves, suspenders hanging loose, an unlit pipe in one hand. He looked as though he had been awake for hours or had never slept; his eyes, grey as the weather, held that quality Beatrix had come to dread more than any shout or threat—the quality of a man reading a difficult document and enjoying it.

"Couldn't sleep," Beatrix said. "I came down for the requisition forms. The grain counts for tomorrow." Their voice did not shake. Their heart was striking the inside of their ribs hard enough to bruise.

"The grain counts." Ilves moved into the room without hurry, and Beatrix understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that he was walking toward the desk, that there was nothing between his stride and the closed Atlas but air and Beatrix's nerve. "You know, I grew up in a house like this one. My father was a surveyor—did I tell you? Every surface covered in paper. You could not sit down in that house without sitting on somebody's borders." He reached the counter. He set his unlit pipe down beside the semaphore codebook, an inch from the Atlas, and looked at the ledger's cover the way a man looks at a face he is trying to place. "I have been reading your republic's paperwork all week, courier. It is very beautiful paperwork. Do you know what strikes me most about it?"

"How thorough it is," Beatrix said.

"How *incomplete* it is." He said it lightly, almost fondly, turning the Atlas a few degrees on the counter, aligning its spine with the desk's edge—a surveyor's tidiness, a habit of squares and true lines. "Armies run on incomplete records. We prefer them. A gap is a place to put a bayonet." Then, without any change of tone at all: "You've moved my ledger."

The room went very narrow. "I opened the drawer for the forms," Beatrix said. "It was already out."

Something flickered across his face—amusement, or its military cousin, contempt for an answer that was technically possible. He regarded Beatrix for a long moment, and the lamp's green light made his eyes look like canal water, deep and cold and holding things at the bottom. Beatrix stood in his regard the way one stands in rising water, measuring how much longer the ground will be there.

"Halden Cross hand," Ilves said suddenly, almost to himself, tapping the Atlas's cover once with one finger. "This registry. The last entries have a Halden slant. I trained in surveying, you know—we copied clerks for practice, whole pages of them, learning to verify a man's field notes by his letterforms. Forged coordinates kill people. So we learned to read hands the way you people read maps." He picked up his pipe. He smiled, and it was the worst smile Beatrix had ever been offered, because it was genuine. "Goodnight, courier. Sleep, if you can. I find I never can, in requisitioned houses. All that paper in the walls. One imagines it whispering."

He went out. His boots receded down the corridor, unhurried, and a door closed somewhere, and did not lock—or locked; there was no telling.

Beatrix stood at the desk for a full minute without moving, breathing through their mouth. Then they returned the Atlas to the drawer—closed it properly this time, aligned, innocent—and climbed the dark stairs to the attic where the household slept in a warm tangle of blankets and breath.

Marisol lay nearest the door, silver hair loose across the pillow, her face in sleep stripped of all command, looking suddenly like every year she had carried for other people. Twenty-one more names, Beatrix thought. Twenty-one strokes of a dead man's pen, and then the audit, whatever form it took, and Ilves's surveyor's eye moving over the ledger page where *Marisol Reyes* now existed because Beatrix had wished it so.

The ink had taken perfectly. That was the miracle and the wound of it. Beatrix lay down beside the sleeping family they had just, in the eyes of heaven and the republic alike, created—and could not tell, even then, whether what they felt was guilt, or the first cold draft of freedom.

Down in the square, a soldier coughed. The rain went on. And in the signals office below, in a locked drawer, twenty-two people's fate lay drying quietly into the historical record—one name entered, twenty-one to go, and four days in which to do it.
