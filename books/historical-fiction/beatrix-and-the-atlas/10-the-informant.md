## 10. The Informant

Petra knew every crack in Callowmere's cobbles by the soles of her feet. She had learned the town that way, back when she was new to it—nine years old and hollow-eyed, walking the streets because walking was better than sitting still in a strange house with strange people who might send her away again. Her feet knew where the gutters ran shallow, where a loose stone hid a puddle that froze first in winter, where the baker's boy dropped burnt ends of loaf behind the shop when he thought no one watched.

Now her feet carried her through the occupied square at half past seven each morning, and she counted soldiers the way other people counted sheep.

There were fourteen on morning rotation, usually. Twelve if it rained. The tall one with the chapped hands was called Dren by the others, and he smiled sometimes. The short one with the whistling breath was Corporal Amsel, who liked his coffee sweet and would pay a child two coins to fetch honey from Varga's stall without asking where the child got it. Petra had learned within three days that soldiers were not one animal but many, and that some of them could be fed, and that fed things grew slower teeth.

She kept her eyes down and her steps even and she did not think about the bread. That was the trick of it. You did not think about what you were earning while you earned it, because thinking made your face do things. Faces were dangerous now. She had watched a woman in the market queue let her mouth twist with hatred for half a second, just half a second, and two grey coats had peeled off the square's edge like bark and followed her home. Petra did not know what had happened after. She had made herself not follow.

This morning there was honey to fetch and a letter to carry to the quartermaster's desk—the letters all looked alike, sealed with grey wax, and Petra had long ago stopped wondering about them—and a list chalked on the signals office slate: candles, lamp oil, more of the sweet coffee. She copied it into her head the way Tomas had taught her, picturing the words as shapes drawn on the dark inside her eyelids, and recited them back until they sat solid.

The tall soldier, Dren, held the door for her at the signals office. "Little courier," he said, not unkindly. "You know this whole town better than we do by now."

"I suppose," Petra said.

"Ever get lost?" He laughed. "No. I bet you don't. I bet you know every cellar and attic from here to the weir."

Her feet, which never betrayed her on cobblestones, went strange on the flat floorboards. She kept them moving. She set the honey on the desk beside Corporal Amsel's cup and said, "Varga only had clover," because that was true, and clover cost more, and Amsel grumbled and paid it, and Petra walked out into the bright ordinary morning with her heart going like a bird trapped under a hat.

*Every cellar.*

He hadn't meant anything. She understood that. It was nothing, a joke, a soldier's small talk. But the words had gone into her like a splinter goes in under a fingernail, too deep to grip, sitting there with every beat of her pulse.

Because she *did* know the cellars. And there was one cellar in particular, cool and candle-scarred beneath the old atlas-maker's shop, where twenty-two people slept in shifts, and where the Atlas itself lay wrapped in oilcloth inside a hollowed folio, and where last night Marisol had sung low and Tomas had poured stolen wine into cups that didn't match, and Beatrix had let themselves laugh for the first time in days.

The army had requisitioned the front of the shop. They had taken the map room, the counters, the great drafting table where Tomas had once ruined his eyes drawing coastlines. They believed the household were servants left behind by the absent owner—that was the story, rehearsed until Marisol could deliver it with the flat boredom of a woman discussing laundry. But the cellar. The cellar had a door behind the coal chute, and a second door into the alley, and a third, hidden, that Tomas had found years ago behind a false shelf—a cartographer's door, leading into the old storm drains, because the man who built this shop had been a man who liked ways out.

Twenty-two people lived down there. Twenty-two names that existed nowhere except scratched into the doorframe with a nail, one for everyone who had ever arrived, including PETRA in wobbly capitals from her first frightened night.

And she had run errands for these soldiers for six weeks. She had stood in this office. She had drunk their coffee money. If anyone thought to ask the little courier what she had seen—

*They wouldn't hurt you,* she told herself, walking fast now, past the fountain with its dry basin full of leaf skeletons. *You're a child. Children don't get—*

But she had seen the eastern quarter burning, or heard it, a red smear of light over the rooftops two nights ago and the smell since, sweet and wrong, and the soldiers had called it a sanitary measure in voices as smooth as the quartermaster's wax. Sanitary. As if the town were a wound and the people in it were the infection. Whatever rules she thought existed about children, about mercy, about the shape of things—those rules had burned with the eastern quarter.

By the time she reached the alley behind the shop, she was crying without having decided to, tears running hot and silent, which was its own skill, crying quietly was something you learned in her old life, and she wiped her face on her sleeve twice before she worked the side door, because Marisol always knew, Marisol read faces the way Tomas read watermarks, and Petra could not be read today. Not yet. Not until she had decided.

Except that deciding turned out to be the hardest thing she had ever done.

* *

She found Beatrix in the kitchen, alone, which was rare enough to feel like an omen. The rest of the house had gone quiet in the way it did mid-morning—Marisol down in the cellar sorting stores, Tomas sleeping off another night hunched over plates and acids, the others scattered through the rooms pretending to be servants dusting furniture nobody used. Beatrix sat at the scrubbed table with a dispatch open before them, copying it in a fair, anonymous hand, and the scratch of the pen was the only sound, and the light through the window fell across the table in a long pale bar.

Petra stood in the doorway. In her old life, in the years between her mother and Marisol's kitchen, she had learned that adults were weather—you endured them, you did not ask things of them. But Beatrix had carried her on their shoulders through the flooded lane the first spring, and had taught her to read a river's surface for what it hid, and had never once, not once, looked at her the way the matron at the ward had looked at her, tallying her like inventory.

"Petra." Beatrix glanced up. "You're white as the flour sack. Sit down."

"I'm fine."

"You're standing like a fence post someone forgot to dig the hole for. Sit."

She sat. The pen was capped. That small click sounded enormous in the room.

For a while Petra looked at her hands, at the hangnail she'd worried bloody on the walk, at the flour ground permanently into the creases of her knuckles from helping Marisol. And Beatrix waited. That was a thing about Beatrix that most people missed, because Beatrix was quiet and good at being overlooked—they could wait like stone waits, patient as geology, and it made lying to them very difficult.

"The soldiers," Petra said. Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "I run errands for them."

"I know." No judgment in it. Just the fact laid on the table between them like a coin. "It's bread, and it keeps you moving and watching. It's well done."

"It's not—" Her throat closed. She forced it. "Dren said something today. He was joking. He said I must know every cellar in Callowmere by now."

Beatrix did not move. But Petra saw the stillness arrive, the particular stillness of an animal that has scented something on the wind, and she understood with a sick lurch that Beatrix understood everything already, was perhaps three sentences ahead of her and had been since the doorway.

"And you're frightened," Beatrix said carefully, "that someday someone will stop joking."

"He'd only have to ask." The words came out in a rush now, the whole confession tearing loose like a stitch pulled. "That's the thing, Beatrix, he'd only have to *ask*, and I don't even know what I'd do. I've thought about it all morning and I don't know. Everyone thinks—I thought—that when it came to it I'd be brave, or I'd lie, but what if my face does a thing? What if I just stand there and go red, and that's the same as telling them? I know the cellar doors. All three. I know about the shelf that isn't a shelf. I know there are more of us than servants. I know—" and here her voice cracked clean in half, nine years old again, hollow-eyed in a doorway—"I know I could be the reason everyone—"

"Stop."

Beatrix's hand came across the table and closed over both of hers, flour-rough and warm, and gripped hard enough to hurt a little, and the hurting was somehow the best thing that had happened to Petra in weeks.

"You listen to me," Beatrix said, and their voice had a register Petra had never heard in it, low and absolutely certain, iron laid under velvet. "You are not a weakness. Do you hear me? You are not a crack in something. You are a nine-year-old girl who has been braver than any adult I know, running into that square every morning with your chin up, learning those soldiers' names and their habits, keeping us informed, bringing home bread earned with your own feet. What you are is a child who has been asked to carry weight no child should carry, and the fear you're feeling right now is not a failure. It is the correct response to a genuinely dangerous situation. Fear means your mind is working."

"But if I talk—"

"Then that would mean they had done something monstrous enough to break a child, and the fault would be theirs, entire, all of it, forever." Beatrix's thumb pressed against her knuckles, steady as a pulse. "But they won't. Because we are going to make sure they never have reason to ask. From today, things change. You'll keep your errands—we can't have you suddenly stop, that draws eyes—but you'll take a different route home, through the tannery lane, and you'll vary it. If anyone asks you about this house, about who lives here, you tell the truth exactly as we've rehearsed it: servants, an owner in the capital, nothing else. Truth is easier to hold than lies; it doesn't shift in your hands. And if anyone presses past that—" Beatrix leaned forward, and their eyes were very dark and very calm— "you come to me. Not tomorrow. Immediately. Whatever hour it is. You wake me."

"What if they don't let me go?" The question was a pebble dropped down a well; Petra heard it fall a long way. "What if they ask me things in a room with the door shut?"

Beatrix was quiet for a moment. Outside, boots crossed the square in measured rhythm, and the sound came through the window glass thin and cold as wire.

"Then I will come and get you," Beatrix said, "and I will stand in that room beside you, and I will say whatever has to be said, and I will not leave without you. I swear it on—" They paused, and something crossed their face, an old grief moving under it like a fish under ice. "On Marisol's kitchen. There is nothing in this world I hold above the people under this roof. Nothing. Not the oath I took, not the law, not my own neck. So you are going to stop carrying this alone as of this minute, and you are going to eat something, because you've gone grey around the mouth."

It was such a Marisol thing to say appended to such a fierce thing that Petra laughed—a wet, hiccupping sound, half sob—and Beatrix pushed back from the table and cut a heel off yesterday's loaf and smeared it with the last of the plum preserves, and put it in front of her like medicine, which it was.

Petra ate. The sweetness spread through her like warmth returning to frozen hands, and gradually the terrible splinter feeling eased—not gone, she suspected it would never entirely go, it would sit under the nail of every morning errand from now on—but held, shared, halved at least.

"I'm sorry," she said, when she could speak. "For being scared."

"Being scared is the rent we pay on staying alive," Beatrix said. "Marisol says that. She says it about the oven, actually, but it generalizes."

* *

Marisol found them both twenty minutes later in the kitchen doorway, and whatever passed between the older woman and Beatrix in that look—two seconds, three—Petra couldn't read, though she sensed a whole conversation happening over her head in a language of eyebrows and shoulders. Then Marisol simply opened her arms and Petra went into them and was held, hard, smelling of yeast and lamp oil and the cellar's cool stone, and Marisol said nothing at all, which was somehow louder than anything.

Later, when Petra had been sent upstairs on the transparent pretext of needing to nap, she paused at the landing where the stairwell bent and the voices below rose just enough.

"She can't run those errands anymore," Marisol said, low and flat, a tone Petra had heard maybe five times ever, the tone that preceded someone leaving the table.

"If she stops suddenly, they notice her. Noticing is the danger now."

"Then we end it differently. We end it *this week*. Beatrix—" and Marisol's voice dropped further, and Petra had to strain, pressed against the cool plaster of the wall— "that audit Ilves announced. Four days. And now his men are making jokes about cellars. That is not a coincidence. That man knows this town is hiding something and he is looking for the loose thread, and our loose thread is nine years old and eats bread in his office."

Silence below. Long enough that Petra's heart climbed into her throat.

"Then we move sooner," Beatrix said at last, very quietly. "Whatever we're going to do about the Atlas—we do it before the audit. Not four days. Two. Maybe less."

"And the barge?"

Another silence, longer, and though Petra could not see it, she would remember forever the quality of that silence, the way a held breath has a texture.

"One problem at a time," Beatrix said.

Petra crept up the rest of the stairs on feet that knew every board that creaked, and lay down on her pallet among the sleeping forms of her family, and stared at the ceiling beams, and did not sleep at all. Above her the soldiers' boots crossed and recrossed the square, patient as a clock, counting down toward something. And somewhere below, in the lamplit cellar, Beatrix was already reaching for the ink.
