## 11. Twenty-Two Names

The house went quiet the way a held breath goes quiet.

Beatrix had asked for one night, and Marisol had given it—not with words, but by turning the supper pot off the fire early, shooing Petra and the others up the stairs with the particular firmness she reserved for matters she had already decided were settled. Only Tomas remained below, standing at the long workbench where atlas plates had been engraved for sixty years, laying out his tools in a neat row like a surgeon or an executioner.

"You don't have to do this," Beatrix said.

"I know." Tomas pulled the lamp closer. His hands—once steady enough to rule a hairline across half a degree of arc without a tremble—were steady now too, and that steadiness frightened Beatrix more than any shaking would have. "That's why I'm doing it."

Outside, Callowmere lay under the grey occupation the way a field lies under frost: not destroyed, only changed at the root, everything green in it killed a little each night. The signals office occupied the front rooms of the shop now, its shutters marked with stenciled numbers, its windows glowing past midnight with the blue light of field telegraphy. Somewhere beyond the bridge, the army was three days' march closer than it had been yesterday, and the final barge was eight days out, and twenty-two people who had never existed on paper were going to keep not existing forever unless someone committed forgery against the Republic of Vessany before dawn.

Beatrix opened the Atlas on the bench beneath the lamp.

It still did that thing it always did—the small intake of breath, involuntary, as if the ledger itself were a living thing disturbed. The paper was cream-heavy, rag-edged, watermarked along every leaf with the republic's seal: a river crossed by a bridge, woven into the sheet so that it appeared only when the light came through at an angle, a ghost country hidden inside the real one. Beatrix thought, not for the first time, that whoever had designed that watermark had understood something true about nations—that they were always two documents at once, the one written and the one meant.

Twenty-two names. They had agreed on the list over three nights of argument and arithmetic, Marisol ruling the table like a tribunal, every claim weighed, every priority contested and resolved. The deserters first, because deserters died twice if caught. The children next. Then the household proper, oldest to youngest, Marisol insisting on being placed last and being overruled by unanimous vote, which was the only way to overrule her.

Now the names had to be made real.

Tomas worked first. He had spent four days preparing—dissolving size from blank leaves of the same rag stock, pressing them between the shop's old plates until they carried a watermark shallow enough to pass lamplight inspection but deep enough to satisfy an expert's eye. Tonight he cut each ghost-leaf to the Atlas's exact dimensions, feathered its edges with a bone folder, and slotted it into the binding beside the genuine pages, one by one, with the patience of a man laying bricks into a wall he intended to live behind for the rest of his life.

"Third leaf is proud of the spine," he murmured. "Hold the light there."

Beatrix held it. In the raking glow, the forged page showed its river-and-bridge seal, fainter than its neighbors, a paler ghost among ghosts. Tomas took a needle and worried the gutter margin until the leaf sat flush.

"Better?"

"Better." He sat back and rubbed his eyes with the heels of his hands. "You understand what we're building."

"A lie."

"No." He shook his head slowly. "I've built lies. Coastlines that never were, mountain ranges invented to please a duke. A lie is a thing you make because the truth won't sell. This—" he touched the open ledger, almost tenderly "—this is a correction. The clerk who kept this book forgot twenty-two citizens of Vessany. We are simply remembering them for him."

"The oath says otherwise."

"The oath," Tomas said, "was written by men who assumed the state would outlive us. It won't. We might." He picked up his needle again. "Ink, courier. The night isn't getting longer."

* * *

The pen Beatrix used had belonged to the dying clerk—a cheap government-issue nib, the same make that had entered eleven thousand true names into the Atlas, purchased from a stationer's drawer in the capital and carried to Callowmere wrapped in oilcloth like a relic. Ink was harder. Official ink had a body to it, an iron-black sheen that faded to a particular grey-brown over years, and no stationer in a requisitioned town stocked it. In the end they had cooked their own from the clerk's formula, which Beatrix had memorized along with everything else the old man had gasped out in his last hours: gallnuts, copperas, gum arabic, a measure of wine vinegar, boiled down to the exact viscosity that would sit *in* the paper rather than upon it.

The first name was Anselm Vey.

Beatrix wrote it slowly. Not hesitantly—hesitation shows in a nib-stroke, pools at the turn, betrays—but slowly, the way the clerk would have written it, bored and precise, the letters of a man processing the eleventh thousandth entry of a career. *Anselm Vey, born Ferrule County, occupation farrier, category: essential civilian transport.* Each field in its place. Each abbreviation in the clerk's own shorthand, which Beatrix had studied by lamplight until it ran under their fingers like a mother tongue.

The ink took perfectly.

That was the horror of it. Beatrix had expected resistance somewhere—in the paper, in the hand, in some deep civic instinct that would rise up and stop the pen. But the paper wanted the ink. The paper had been made for exactly this. Anselm Vey, who had arrived at the shop four winters ago with frostbitten feet and a stolen army coat and no name he would answer to for the first month, became a citizen of the Republic of Vessany in forty seconds of careful handwriting, and the universe did not object. No thunder. No crack in the floorboards. Just the small scratch of the nib and the smell of hot iron from the fresh ink.

"Anselm," Beatrix whispered, testing it. "Anselm Vey." The name felt different now, heavier, as if it had been picked up off the ground and hung on a hook where officials could find it.

"Next," said Tomas, without looking up from his watermark plates.

They found a rhythm. Tomas feeding prepared leaves; Beatrix entering names; the lamp trimmed low and shaded toward the river side of the room so nothing showed at the shutter facing the square. Outside, a patrol passed at the half-hour, boots ringing on cobbles, and they stopped breathing until it faded. A dog barked at the bridge. The clock in the hall that nobody had wound since the requisition ticked anyway, or seemed to—some phantom of habit in the wood itself.

Della and her boy. Old Wray, the ferryman, category: river transport. The twins, Petra's friends from the east quarter, whose parents were dead and whose existence no record anywhere acknowledged. Each name a small resurrection. Each entry a crime against the state carrying a penalty Beatrix refused to think about in the specific terms Ilves's regulations specified, though the terms were posted on the signal office door in three languages and Beatrix walked past them daily.

Somewhere past the middle of the list the pen began to feel less like a weapon and more like a plow. That frightened Beatrix too—how natural it became. How easily the hand learned treason.

* * *

Marisol had not gone upstairs after all.

She appeared at the cellar door sometime past two o'clock, carrying a tray—bread, hard cheese, the good tea hoarded against no occasion in particular—and set it down at the edge of the bench without asking whether they wanted it. She had put her shawl on, which meant she had been sitting in the cold stairwell for hours, listening, keeping watch in the dark the way she had kept watch through two wars and three countries that no longer existed under their old names.

"I told you to sleep," Beatrix said.

"You told the children to sleep." Marisol pulled a stool to the end of the bench and sat, folding her hands. From there she could see the stairs, the yard door, and the seam of light under the front-room partition where the signals office slept its watchmen. Her post, chosen without discussion. "Someone has to listen for boots."

For a while she only watched them work. She had a way of watching that made a body aware of its own hands. When Beatrix finished a page and Tomas slotted the next leaf, Marisol leaned forward and studied the fresh entry with narrowed eyes.

"Whose hand is that?"

"The clerk's," Beatrix said. "I've been practicing his hand for a week."

"I know whose hand it is." Marisol tapped the page once, gently, above the line. "I mean—it's yours too. Underneath. Look how you press harder on the downstrokes when you write someone you love. You did it on the address labels you used to copy for me, all those years. Every parcel to my sister, the ink dug in like you were angry at it." She looked up. "You press hard on all of these."

Beatrix stared at the entries. Now that it was said, Beatrix could see it—a faint excess of pressure running through all twenty-two names so far, invisible to any eye that wasn't looking for love specifically, and therefore visible only to Marisol, and possibly to God.

"Can you lighten it?" Marisol asked. Not accusing. Asking, the way she asked whether a dish needed salt, already knowing the cook would taste it properly.

"Yes." Beatrix flexed the ache out of their writing hand. "Yes. I'll go back over the spacing on the earlier ones so they read tired instead of tender."

"Tired is better," Marisol said. "Nobody audits a tired man's book." And then, after a silence in which the kettle of her attention seemed to weigh them both: "Petra's name goes in tonight?"

"Petra's name goes in."

"Then write it well," Marisol said, and poured the tea.

* * *

The night broke into movements, the way fever does.

There was the stretch after midnight when the ink turned treacherous—the batch cooling, thickening, dragging at the nib—until Tomas remembered the clerk's instruction about warming the vessel and they set it in a dish of water heated on the spirit lamp, and the flow came back like a river unbinding in spring. There was the hour when Petra's footsteps creaked on the stair above and froze there, and everyone below went stone-still, listening to a child decide, alone in the dark, whether to come down and see what her family was becoming. She decided against it. The creak retreated. Marisol closed her eyes briefly, and Beatrix understood that she had been praying, in whatever language she prayed in now, for the girl to stay innocent a little longer.

There was the moment, near four o'clock, when Beatrix entered the name of Jem Sol, the twins' elder brother, dead two years and buried under the pear tree, and realized halfway through the fields that they were writing a man who could never board any barge—and kept writing anyway, because the point had shifted somewhere in the night. It was no longer only about berths. The Atlas claimed to contain the republic entire. If it must be the record—if historians, if whatever government rose from the ashes on either bank ever consulted it—then Jem Sol had lived, and the pear tree held a citizen, and the ledger was a grave marker no army could burn. Beatrix finished the entry and did not tell Tomas, and saw by the set of his shoulders that he suspected, and said nothing about that either. Some forgeries are for the dead.

By five, the list was done. Twenty-two names, entered across nine inserted leaves interleaved among the true ones, in dates and districts scattered credibly through the clerk's original campaign. Twenty-two citizens manufactured out of ink and gallnuts and refusal. Beatrix fanned the pages in the lamplight and watched the ghost-seals swim up out of the paper—river, bridge, river—and felt something that had no clean name: pride and nausea in the same mouthful, the exact flavor of having done the right thing by the wrong method, or perhaps the right method by the wrong light. Beautiful and criminal, and both adjectives true, and no way to choose between them.

"It will hold," Tomas said quietly. "The watermark, the stock, the hand. It will hold for anyone honest."

"And for anyone dishonest?"

Tomas was silent for a moment. He gathered his needles and plates and bone folder into their roll of felt with great care, not meeting Beatrix's eye.

"Anyone dishonest," he said at last, "will look longest at what we've done best. That's all I can tell you. That's everything I know."

* * *

They cleaned up as the sky over the river went from black to iron. Ink scrubbed from the vessel, vessel buried in the coal scuttle, leaves of evidence pressed back into the binding until the Atlas sat on the bench looking exactly like what it purported to be: a boring, complete, official book. Tomas took the felt roll down toward the cellar to hide it among Marisol's preserve jars. Marisol banked the lamp and stood a moment at the window slit, watching the square.

"Look," she said.

Beatrix joined her. Dawn was coming up over Callowmere the way it had for six hundred years—grey water, grey roofs, smoke rising from the bakery because even occupying armies wanted bread. At the far end of the square, two soldiers were raising the flag over the signal mast with the unhurried competence of men who had done it a hundred times. Everything looked ordinary. The town wore its requisition like an old coat.

But the calm Beatrix felt was terrible, and they recognized it as terrible, and could do nothing about its terribleness. It was the calm of a deed finished, of dice already leaving the hand. Whatever came next would come to a family that had finally, fully, together—every hand on the same page, every name in the same book—committed its whole heart to one act of treason, and there was a strange purity in that, and purity is a kind of exposure.

"We should sleep," Marisol said.

"In a minute."

Below in the square, the flag reached the top of the mast and snapped open. And at that same moment the signals office door opened ahead of its usual hour, and Captain Ilves stepped out into the pale morning with a paper in his hand, and stood reading it—not the flag, not the river, not his dispatches—with his head tilted slightly toward the atlas-maker's shop, the way a surveyor tilts toward a contour line that doesn't match his map.

He folded the paper. He looked up directly at the window slit, at the two faces in the dark, at a distance too great for recognition and arranged precisely for it anyway.

"He's early," Marisol said softly.

"He's early," Beatrix agreed, and the terrible calm in their chest acquired, at last, a name.
