## 12. Audit

The morning the audit was announced began like any other morning of the occupation: grey water, grey sky, the smell of lamp oil and wet stone, and Beatrix copying signals at the requisitioned desk that had once been Tomas's map table.

They had grown fluent in the language of the office by then—the clipped codes, the rotation of couriers, the way the soldiers' boots sounded different on the stairs depending on whether they came with orders or only boredom. Beatrix had learned to read a room the way they had once learned to read a road: by pressure, by habit, by the small deviations that meant weather was coming. And for three days, something in the weather of the signals office had been changing. The men spoke less when Beatrix entered. The ledgers were locked at night now, where before they had been left out under a soldier's sleeping head. Ilves had begun arriving early.

Beatrix told no one this. There are fears you carry the way you carry a cracked rib—close to the body, breathing around it—because to say it aloud would be to make it real, and to make it real would be to watch Marisol's face do the thing her face did when she was frightened and refusing to be.

So Beatrix copied dispatches, kept their handwriting deliberately plain, and waited.

The announcement came at nine, in front of everyone.

Ilves stood at the head of the long table with a telegram in his hand, and he did not raise his voice, because he never needed to. He had the particular authority of a man who had discovered early that quiet made soldiers stand straighter than shouting ever had.

"By order of the Provisional Command," he said, "a full documentary audit will be conducted of all offices in the river district. Every ledger, every register, every record of transit, supply, and personnel will be verified against central archives." He let his eyes move down the table, unhurried. "The audit party arrives in four days."

Four days.

Beatrix's hand, resting flat on the desk, went very still. Four days. The barge left in six. The arithmetic assembled itself in their skull without permission, cold and instant as a blade laid against a wrist: two days between verification and departure. Two days in which twenty-two forged names would sit in a ledger under an expert's eye, waiting to be compared against a central record that contained none of them.

*We have until then,* some calm and distant part of them thought. *And not one hour more.*

"Every ledger," Ilves repeated, and here he paused—and Beatrix felt the pause the way you feel a hand pass close over the back of your neck without touching. "I want it understood that this is routine. The command requires accurate records. A republic is remembered by its paperwork, gentlemen, even after the republic itself is gone."

He said it lightly. It was almost an joke, and two of the younger soldiers laughed obediently. But Beatrix heard the sentence settle into the room like a coin dropped into deep water, sinking and sinking, turning as it fell, and they thought: *he knows what a ledger is worth. He has always known.*

Then Ilves looked at them.

Not past them. Not the general sweep of a commander accounting for his staff. He looked at Beatrix—at their hands on the desk, at the ink stain along the side of their right forefinger—and he held the look perhaps three seconds longer than the look required, perhaps four, and in that time Beatrix understood that everything else he had said that morning had been spoken to the room, and whatever he was saying now was being said only to them, silently, in a language neither of them would consent to speak aloud.

Then he looked away, folded the telegram, and gave the office its ordinary morning's orders as though nothing had happened at all.

* * *

Beatrix walked home through the square with a basket they did not need, because a courier carrying nothing attracted no attention, and because their legs wanted the work. The town had that thin, stretched quality of places living under occupation too long to keep pretending it was temporary. Shutters half-opened. Bread queues short and silent. A grey sentry at the fountain chewing slowly, watching the water fall.

*Four days.* They turned the phrase over and over, looking for a seam in it, some misreading that would give them back their margin. But there was no seam. Central archives meant the regional registry at Vessany-sur-Onde, which had been evacuated south but whose records—Beatrix knew this from the courier routes themselves, from the very dispatches they carried—had been seized intact by the advancing army. The invaders had the republic's memory in their hands. All of it. Every name ever properly entered.

And the Atlas, sitting in its hollow folio behind the false shelf-back in what had been the atlas-maker's parlor, held twenty-two names that had been entered nowhere else on earth.

At the mouth of the shop-lane Beatrix stopped and made themselves breathe. Above the crooked rooftops the gulls were working the river wind, crying their thin cries, indifferent as they had always been to who owned the town below. Beatrix watched them and thought, absurdly, of the night in the cellar when Marisol had sung—really sung, voice cracked and off-key and magnificent—and how Petra had fallen asleep against Tomas's shoulder mid-verse, and how Beatrix had stood at the back with a stolen cup of wine thinking: *this. Whatever else is taken, we had this.*

They went inside.

Marisol knew before Beatrix got the basket onto the table. She always did; she read faces the way Beatrix read rooms. She was kneading bread dough with the sleeves of her dress rolled to the elbow, flour to her wrists, and she looked up once and stopped moving.

"Tell me," she said.

"Audit," Beatrix said. "In four days. Everything verified against central records."

The kitchen went quiet in the particular way kitchens go quiet—as if the silence were something rising, filling the room from the floor up. Tomas set down the lens he'd been cleaning. Petra, at the window seat, pulled her knees in. Old Ferenc didn't react at all; he simply continued shelling beans into the bowl, one by one, with the terrible patience of a man who had survived three governments and expected to survive no more of them.

"They'll check the Atlas," Tomas said. Flat. Already past shock, already working. His fingers had begun, unconsciously, to move against the tabletop—drawing, always drawing, the cartographer's tic that never left him. "Names against registry rolls. Watermark against plate records." He stopped. His jaw tightened. "The watermark."

"The watermark holds," Beatrix said, faster than they meant to, and saw Tomas flinch.

"It holds," Tomas agreed, too evenly, and said nothing else, and Beatrix filed the flinch away in the growing drawer of things they could not afford to examine.

"So we run," said Petra. Her voice came out smaller than she wanted it to; Beatrix watched her hear that herself and hate it. "Before the audit. We take the Atlas and we go east, on foot, tonight—"

"And leave the barge?" Marisol didn't look at her. She was washing her hands, slowly, taking the flour off finger by finger. "Twenty-two people, child. On foot, in winter, with the whole country between us and anywhere. The barge is the only door out of this that opens outward."

"Then we fix it," Petra said. "Whatever needs fixing. We have four days."

Marisol dried her hands. Then she looked at Beatrix—not at the others, at Beatrix, directly, and asked the question nobody else would ask.

"How much time do you actually need, if you had to change anything? To make it right?"

And there it was, lying open on the kitchen table between them: the confession neither of them had spoken. Marisol asking *how bad is it*, and meaning *I know there is something to be bad*, and Beatrix standing in the doorway of the lie they had built plank by plank over weeks of ink and lamplight.

"Not enough," Beatrix said. Which was true. Which was also, somehow, an answer to every question in the room, and Marisol held their eyes a moment longer, and nodded once, and turned back to her dough, and Beatrix understood that they had just been forgiven something—or warned by something—that had not yet been named aloud.

That night Beatrix lay awake listening to the house tick and settle, doing the arithmetic again because the mind returns to the wound like a tongue to a broken tooth. Four days. Two names an hour, if the worst happened and they had to strike entries, alter dates, build a false provenance for twenty-two people who had arrived in this household by desertion and orphanhood and disgrace, by night, by river, by luck. You could not forge a life in an hour. You could barely forge a paragraph.

Somewhere below, faintly, they heard Tomas's pencil moving. He wasn't sleeping either. Neither of them said so in the morning.

* * *

The second day, Ilves summoned them.

It was done with perfect propriety—a soldier at the door, a message written in a fair round hand: *the captain requests the courier's assistance with routing schedules, at his convenience.* Nothing in it a censor could fault. But Beatrix's stomach dropped as they climbed the stairs to the signals room, because convenience, in Ilves's vocabulary, was never convenient.

He was alone. That alone was answer enough. The shutters were half-closed against the afternoon glare, and the light came through in slats, laying pale bars across the maps spread on the table—river charts, district surveys, the whole watershed rendered in faded ink. Ilves stood over them with his hands clasped behind his back, and he did not look up when Beatrix entered.

"You know the Onde above Callowmere," he said. It was not a question. "Your routes. Tell me—which of these tributary roads floods first in thaw?"

Beatrix gave him the answer. He nodded, moved a marker, asked another. For ten minutes it was genuinely work: honest questions about fords and ferries and load limits, the kind of conversation they might have had in another life, in another country, between two people who respected each other's competence. Beatrix found, to their shame, that part of them relaxed. This was the seduction of Ilves, and they knew it, and fell for it a little anyway—he made you feel reasonable. He made collaboration feel like conversation.

Then, without transition, still studying the map, he said:

"I served four years in the survey corps before the war. Did you know that?"

"No," Beatrix said.

"Watermarks interest me." He turned a chart a few degrees, absently squaring it with the table edge. "Paper interests me. A document is a machine, courier. Every part made by someone, in some place, at some time. You can read a sheet of paper the way I read this river"—his finger traced the Onde, downstream, toward the sea—"and find where it came from. Where it was made. Who made it." He looked up. The slatted light fell across his face in bars. "Most people think forgery is about ink. It isn't. It's about paper."

The room was very quiet. Downstairs a door slammed; boots crossed the hall; the ordinary machinery of the occupation ground on, indifferent.

"Is there a point to this, Captain?" Beatrix asked, and was proud that their voice came out level.

Ilves smiled. It was not an unkind smile, which was the worst thing about it. "Only that the audit will be thorough," he said. "Central records are remarkably complete. Names, births, registrations—even, in recent years, the paper stock of government presses, catalogued by batch." He straightened the last chart, precisely, so its edge met the table's edge exactly. "Remarkable, what survives a collapsing republic. Almost everything, in my experience. Except its people."

He dismissed them then, courteously, with a nod and a murmured thanks for the routing advice. Beatrix walked to the door with their spine held very straight and their heart going like a fist against a locked door, and on the threshold Ilves spoke once more, mildly, to the maps rather than to them:

"Give my regards to your household. Six of you, isn't it? Servants, the register says." A pause, exactly as long as the pause in the office two days before. "Registers can be so unreliable."

* * *

Beatrix did not go home. They walked to the river instead, down the weedy stair cut into the bank, and stood on the mud-flat where the final barge would tie up in five days, and watched the brown water carry sticks and leaves and one white feather seaward, out of reach of armies.

*He knows.* The thought would not leave; it circled like the gulls. He knows, or he knows enough, or he is fishing—but Ilves did not fish, Ilves trawled, dragging the whole river, and what he had said about paper was not a guess. It was a curriculum. He had told them, almost gently, exactly how the trap worked: the watermark traceable to its press, the names traceable to their absence, the audit not a formality but a noose drawn tight with four days of rope.

And still—still—there was the other reading, the one Beatrix clung to as the light failed: that Ilves had offered them something in that shuttered room. Not the crude bargain of the office and its protections, but information. *He told me the shape of the test.* Why tell the condemned the method of their execution unless some part of him wanted them to beat it? Unless the man quoting poetry over survey charts was, in his own sealed and soldierly way, on the side of the names?

Beatrix could not decide which was worse: an enemy closing in, or an enemy leaving the door open and watching to see if they'd dare use it.

The lamps of Callowmere came on one by one along the bluff. Somewhere behind Beatrix, in the shop above the lane, a folio sat behind a false panel holding twenty-two lives in careful, criminal, beautiful ink. Five days of margin, once. Now four days of audit, and a man who could read paper like water.

Beatrix turned back up the stair. There were names to defend, and less time than there had ever been, and one thought burning steady beneath the fear like a lamp in a window:

If the audit was coming to the Atlas, then before it arrived, the Atlas would have to come to them first.
