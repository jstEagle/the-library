## 14. The Cartographer's Confession

Tomas had not slept properly in eleven days, and his hands had begun to betray him.

They trembled now at the worst moments—not when he was carrying a plate of Marisol's barley stew up from the cellar, which would have been forgivable, but when he was doing nothing at all. When he sat by the cellar lamp with the forged page drying before him. When he watched Beatrix's hand move across the Atlas with that terrible, beautiful steadiness and thought: *I built the teeth of this trap, and I have not yet told them where the teeth are set.*

The watermark was the problem. It had been the problem since the first night, and every night since he had told himself it wasn't.

He sat in the far corner of the cellar where the household had taken to gathering after dark—the signals office above them now belonged to Captain Ilves and his grey-coated clerks, and the shop itself was a place they entered only in daylight, playing servants, playing invisible. Down here there was no pretense. There were candles stubbed into bottles, Petra asleep against Marisol's shoulder with her mouth open, and the twenty-one names already entered into the republic's most guarded ledger, each one rendered in Beatrix's flawless imitation of a clerk's hand.

Twenty-two, counting the one Beatrix had done first. Marisol's name, the test stroke, the point of no return crossed in ink so convincing that Tomas had wept a little, privately, into his sleeve.

He should have felt proud. He had spent thirty years making maps and twelve of them unmaking himself, and here at last was work worthy of his ruin: a census of nobodies, a state document of the stateless. But pride had curdled in him around day four, when he had held the forged folio up to the lamplight and seen what he saw now, what he had seen every night since and refused, coward that he was, to say aloud.

The lion of Vessany floated wrong.

* * *

A watermark is not printed. That is the first thing they teach you, or taught you, in the guild halls before the guild halls burned. A watermark is a wound in the paper itself—wires sewn into the mould in the shape of the mark, thinning the pulp where they lie, raising the fibers where they don't. The light passes through the thinned places and the emblem appears, not on the page but *in* it, part of its body the way a scar is part of skin.

Tomas had spent three days building their counterfeit the only way left to him: wax, fine wire salvaged from a broken clock, and a heated stylus, impressing the lion-and-crosses into each sheet from behind while the paper was dampened to mimic the original pulping. It was good work. Under ordinary lamplight it was perfect work. Under candlelight it was indistinguishable from grace.

But hold it to a strong, single-source light—a surveyor's lantern, a clerk's examination window—and the truth announced itself. The true watermark's edges dissolved gradually into the surrounding paper, fibers tapering away over tenths of millimeters like a coastline seen from altitude. His forgery ended abruptly. A border. A line drawn where nature drew none.

Only an expert would catch it. Only someone who had spent years reading paper the way sailors read water.

And Captain Ilves—Tomas had confirmed it that afternoon, standing in the requisitioned shop with a tray of tea he was not supposed to overhear anything while carrying—Captain Ilves had trained as a surveyor. Two years at the military academy at Dreznik before his commission, said the sergeant who did not lower his voice because why would he, the servant was furniture. Surveying corps, border triangulation, six years of it. A man whose entire profession consisted of detecting exactly the kind of falsification that maps are heir to: displaced benchmarks, shifted meridians, a river moved two degrees to hide a village. A man who looked at documents the way Tomas once had—with the eye that sees not what is drawn but how the drawing was made.

*You made the trap,* Tomas told himself, turning his cup in circles on the tray until the tea sloshed. *Now tell them it has teeth.*

* * *

He waited until the household assembled that night, because some confessions require witnesses.

Marisol sat at the head of the plank table they'd laid across two barrels, her hands folded as she folded them every evening, as if the cellar were a kitchen and the barrels a table set for supper. Beside her, Beatrix, ink still ghosted under the fingernails despite Marisol's scrubbing—some stains become the person wearing them. Yannick and Odile pressed together like bookends. Petra, awake tonight, watching everything with those quick dark eyes that had learned too young how soldiers' attention worked. Old Ferenc, half-deaf, half-wise, impossible to say which half governed.

"I need to tell you all something," Tomas said, "and I need you not to interrupt me, because if you interrupt me I will lose my nerve, and I have been losing my nerve for a week."

Marisol's hands went still. Beatrix set down the pen.

"The watermark will pass any inspection conducted by a soldier, a clerk, or a bureaucrat," Tomas said. "It will not pass an inspection conducted by Ilves." He laid the forged sheet on the table, took the candle, and slid it beneath the paper, then tilted the flame close so the light pooled through the lion. Even to them, even untrained, the abrupt edge must have been visible—he saw Odile frown, saw her lean in. "Do you see? The real one fades. Ours stops. Like a fence instead of a shoreline. It's a flaw of perhaps a tenth of a millimeter along the entire perimeter. You would need to know what you were looking for."

"And Ilves knows," Beatrix said quietly. Not a question.

"He surveyed borders for six years. Before that he studied cartography at Dreznik—I heard it today, in the shop, from his own sergeant's mouth." Tomas's voice cracked and he hated it, hated the old man's tremor in it. "There are maybe forty people in the whole republic who could catch this flaw, and we have installed one of them directly above our heads."

The silence that followed was not empty. It was full—the silence of arithmetic being done all around the table, the same corrosive sum they had all been performing since the berth count, except now the numbers had changed. Not one berth against twenty-two souls. Four days against one audit.

"An audit verifies against central records," Yannick said slowly. He had been a customs man once, in his first life, before the war took his first family and the road delivered him to Marisol's door. "If central records don't contain your names—"

"Then the audit doesn't find the missing names," Beatrix finished. "It finds the extra ones. Twenty-two names that appear nowhere in the republic's archives, written in ink on paper bearing a watermark that fails under a surveyor's lamp, in a ledger kept in a room that Ilves sleeps beside." Beatrix's voice stayed level, which Tomas recognized as effort, as craft. "It was never a general audit, was it. Audits are random. He named the date."

"He's known," Odile whispered. "Or he suspects. Either way—"

"Either way it's a hunt," Petra said, "and we're the only animal in the forest."

From a fourteen-year-old it landed harder than it would have from anyone else. Marisol's arm came around the girl without seeming to move at all, the way her arms did things before the rest of her decided.

Tomas looked down at his hands. They were trembling again, and this time he let them.

"I want to be clear about the order of my failures," he said. "First, I found the flaw four nights ago and said nothing, because saying it meant admitting the work was imperfect, and I have lost everything else—my guild, my license, my name on anything legitimate—and I could not bear to lose the one perfect thing I've made. So I let you all believe we were safe because believing it was the last luxury I had." He heard Marisol draw breath, and raised a hand. Please. Let me finish; I rehearsed this in the privy like a schoolboy. "Second failure: I designed the forgery using methods from my own plates, my own papers, my own habits. If Ilves catches the flaw, he does not merely catch a forger. He catches a style. And there are perhaps five men alive whose work resembles mine, because I trained three of them, and I am the only one who ever came to Callowmere."

"You're saying he could find *you*," Ferenc said, too loud, the deaf man's volume.

"I'm saying he could find us all through me. I'm saying the audit has one target and I built its teeth." The words tasted of copper. "I have spent twelve years being sorry, Marisol. This is the first time I have been ashamed."

* * *

What surprised him was that no one raged.

He had prepared for rage. He had, in the privy, in the dark of his cot, rehearsed defenses against accusations he deserved—that his vanity had doomed them, that his silence was its own treason. He had imagined Beatrix's controlled fury, Marisol's terrible quiet. Instead the candle burned down another finger's width and the family simply turned, as one body, toward the problem, the way they always did, the way they had turned toward every deserter and orphan and ruin that had washed up on Marisol's step. The problem arrived; the family bent around it.

"Options," Marisol said. One word, level as a shelf.

"We burn the Atlas," Yannick said. "Steal it back tomorrow and put it in the stove. No ledger, no audit, no forgery to find."

"And no names," Odile said. "No barge. We go back to being nobody, permanently, in a town that's about to be nobody too."

"There's no going back to invisible," Petra said. "Not after the eastern quarter burned. They see us now. Everyone. Being unseen isn't a thing you can choose anymore—it's a thing they take away." She said it flatly, a child reciting a lesson learned in the correct language, and Tomas flinched at it.

"Then we fix the pages," Beatrix said.

All eyes went to them. Tomas felt the idea arrive in his own chest a half-second later, arriving with dread and hope in equal measure, twins.

"You said the flaw is at the edge. The taper. The true watermark dissolves and ours stops short." Beatrix was speaking fast now, the courier's mind running routes. "How long is the taper, on the genuine sheets?"

"A few tenths of a millimeter. Perhaps less."

"Can it be made? After the fact? Can you feather the edge—wet the paper, work the fibers outward, make a fence into a shoreline?"

Tomas stared at the lion glowing in the candlelight. Twelve years since anyone had asked him a question worthy of his training, and now the question was whether he could perform surgery on paper with a needle and steam, twenty-two times, in less than four days, in a cellar beneath the ears of the man hunting them.

"It can be attempted," he said. "It cannot be guaranteed. And if Ilves examines the sheets before I finish—if the audit moves up—"

"It won't move up," Marisol said, and then, softer, in the voice she used for truths: "Unless he decides to move it."

"That's it, then." Beatrix stood, and the candle flames leaned in the draft of their rising. "Tomas works the pages. I'll keep the ledger moving through the signals office as normal—Ilves watches me, so let him watch me being ordinary. And we prepare for the other possibility." They stopped, and Tomas watched the sentence assemble behind their eyes before they spoke it, watched the courier weigh it like a parcel.

"If he moves the audit early," Beatrix said, "we don't wait to be found. We take the Atlas out of that office ourselves."

The word *ourselves* settled over the table like snow. Steal it back. From a signals office, from an occupying garrison, from a captain who quoted poetry and missed nothing. Tomas looked around the faces—Yannick's set jaw, Odile's white knuckles, Petra wide-eyed and listening to everything—and understood that the moment the words were spoken they became inevitable, the way a contour line on a map becomes, in time, a road walked.

"One more thing," Tomas said. His voice was steadier now, emptied out, scoured. "When this is over—however it ends—someone should write down that the watermark was mine. The flaw and the fixing both. If the record survives and the record is honest, it should say that a disgraced mapmaker named Tomasz Reyes Vail made a lion that didn't fade, and it cost his family four days of terror, and it might cost them everything else." He paused. "History happened elsewhere, all my life. I'd like my own history, at least, to happen on purpose."

Marisol reached across the table and closed her hand over his trembling ones, stilling them the way she stilled everything she loved—by force, by warmth, without asking permission.

"Your history is in this cellar, old man," she said. "Everything else is just paper."

"No," Tomas said gently, looking at the ledger, at the twenty-two names glowing faintly in the light of the dying candle. "That's the trouble, Marisol. Now it's all paper. Everything we are is on that page. Which means everything we are hangs on whether a tenth of a millimeter can be made to look like mercy."

He picked up his needle, his pot of size, his steaming cloth, and began.

Above them, through the floorboards, they could hear the signals office settling into its night watch: boots crossing the boards, the click of the lamp-hood, and then—faintly, twice, unhurried—the sound of Captain Ilves laughing at something in his reports.

At dawn, Beatrix would carry the Atlas upstairs as usual, and set it on the desk as usual, under the eye of a surveyor who read paper like water.

And sometime in the next four days, one way or another, the lion would either fade—or it wouldn't.
