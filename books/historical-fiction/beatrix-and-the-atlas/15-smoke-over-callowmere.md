## 15. Smoke Over Callowmere

Petra smelled it before anyone told her.

She was on the steps of the provisioner's shop, balancing the loaf Captain Ilves's corporal had given her for running his messages that morning, when the wind shifted and carried the smell up from the eastern quarter — not woodsmoke, which she knew the way she knew Marisol's hands, but something thicker, sweeter, wrong. It was the smell of things burning that were never meant to burn. Straw mattresses. Curtains embroidered by grandmothers. The oilcloth off someone's winter windows.

She stood very still on the step, the bread pressed to her chest, and watched a column of smoke stand up over the rooftops on the far side of the river, straight as a pillar in the still morning air, as if the sky had grown a grey tree.

The soldiers in the square did not look up. That was the first thing she understood, standing there with her heart going like a trapped bird against the loaf: they had been told about this. They had known since breakfast. Maybe since yesterday. And nobody had rung the bells, because the army had taken the bells three weeks ago along with everything else that could be rung or read or believed.

*Sanitary measure.* She would hear those words later, spoken by a lieutenant with a clipboard, in a voice like a man reading a recipe. *The eastern quarter is being cleared under sanitary order. Typhus in the tenements. Contagion protocols.*

But Petra had run messages through the eastern quarter every day for two weeks, and she knew what was in the tenements there: old Halvorsen who repaired clocks and paid her in peppermints; the Ferro sisters who sang while they mended; forty-some people who hung their washing out in rows like flags of a country that had never done anything to anyone. There was no typhus. There were only houses on the river side of the bridge, and houses were easier to burn than to garrison.

She ran home.

She didn't decide to run; her legs decided, and her mind caught up somewhere around the fountain, which was dry now, its bronze maiden streaked black where soldiers had burned something in her basin. The loaf bounced out of her arms at the corner and she left it — left bread, which she would not have left a month ago, which showed how the world had changed, though she didn't think about that until much later, lying awake in the cellar.

Marisol was in the kitchen when Petra burst through the door, kneading dough with the fierce rhythm she used when her hands needed something to do besides shake. The whole house smelled of flour and yeast, an ordinary smell, a smell from before, and Petra stood in the doorway gasping and could not make words come out.

"Slowly," Marisol said, not stopping the kneading, because if she stopped she would have to look at Petra's face properly and then she would know.

"The east quarter," Petra got out. "They're burning the east quarter."

The kneading stopped.

* * *

By noon, the smoke had thickened until it lay over the town like a lid, and ash came down soft as terrible snow. It settled on the windowsills, on the empty fountain, on the shoulders of the grey soldiers who went about their work with their collars turned up. It settled on the atlas-maker's shop, on the shutters Beatrix had closed at midday for the first time in the shop's history, because the shop had always traded on light and openness and now light was a liability.

Petra was sent to the cellar, which was how she knew something serious was happening, because Petra had not been *sent* anywhere in months — she was the runner, the errand-girl, the one with clean papers and a quick face who could go where the others couldn't. Being sent to the cellar meant she had become someone to be protected, and protection, in her experience, always arrived too late and cost too much.

She didn't stay in the cellar.

She couldn't have explained why, then or after. She climbed the ladder, lifted the trapdoor a finger's width, and watched through the gap as the household moved through the kitchen like a crew working a ship in a storm. Marisol filling every pot with water. Tomas pulling maps from the drawers — the old ones, the beautiful ones, the plates he'd sworn he'd melted down and hadn't — and stacking them behind the false panel. Beatrix at the window with a crack of shutter open, watching the square, and Petra could see only a sliver of them: the set jaw, the ink-stained fingers gripping the sill, the stillness of someone doing arithmetic they hated.

Twenty-two names in a ledger. One barge. Six days.

And now the eastern quarter burning, and everyone who had lived there suddenly with nowhere to be counted.

It was Halvorsen who knocked first.

Three soft raps at the back door, the rhythm clockmakers used, and Marisol opened it on the chain and then unchained it fast. He came in carrying nothing but a leather tool roll and a wall clock held against his chest like a child, his eyebrows gone to singe-curl, and behind him in the yard the Ferro sisters, and behind them more shapes in the dusk of the smoke, and Petra, watching through her finger-width gap, counted them the way she counted everything now, automatically, helplessly: seven. Nine. Eleven.

"We heard you had room," Halvorsen said, which was a strange thing to say, and Petra saw Marisol understand it the way adults understood things, instantly and completely: word had traveled through the occupied town, mouth to mouth across yards and wash-lines, that the household at the old atlas-maker's shop was the kind of place that took people in. Their invisibility had made them a rumor, and the rumor had come home.

"There's room," Marisol said, and stepped aside, and the river of neighbors began.

* * *

The cellar had been built by a cartographer and a cook, and it turned out they had built better than they knew. Tomas's map cabinets ran deeper than furniture needed to be; Marisol's stores had been quietly doubled for a month, sacks of beans and barley buried under the floor stones that Beatrix pried up without being asked, which told Petra that Beatrix had planned this even before there was a this. By midnight there were nineteen extra souls below the shop, sleeping shoulder to shoulder on sacking, and the air was thick with breath and candle tallow and the smell of scorched wool coming off the Ferro sisters' coats.

Petra should have slept. Instead she sat against the wine racks with her knees pulled up and watched, because watching felt like a job, and having a job kept the fear in a box.

Old Halvorsen sat nearby with his clock in his lap, not winding it, just holding it. His hands, so steady on gears all his life, were trembling.

"They gave us twenty minutes," he said, to no one, to everyone. "A lieutenant with a paper. Twenty minutes and a sanitary order, as if my shop were a chamber pot." He looked at the clock face. "I saved the clock. I don't know why the clock. Forty years of tools and ledgers and my wife's letters, and I saved the clock."

"Because you could carry it," said one of the Ferro sisters, in the dark. "That's all. You saved what your arms could hold. Nobody saves more than that."

Petra thought of the loaf in the street, abandoned. She thought of the Atlas upstairs, wrapped now inside its hollowed folio among the signals office documents, holding twenty-two names that had been written in secret by lamplight, in ink mixed by Tomas, on pages that would either save everyone or hang them all. She thought: *that is what my family is saving. Not what our arms can hold. Something lighter than any of us and heavier than all of us.*

She had not thought of Beatrix and the ledger as *my family's* work before. It had been Beatrix's dangerous secret, Tomas's forgery, Marisol's watch-keeping. Adults' business. Lying in the dark with nineteen refugees breathing around her, Petra understood for the first time that the business belonged to all of them — that if the ledger failed, the soldiers would come not for Beatrix alone but for the cellar, for Halvorsen's clock and the Ferro sisters' voices and every sack of buried barley. The stakes had walked down her own stairs and lain down beside her.

She wasn't frightened the way she had been. That surprised her. Frightened was small and cold and alone. What she felt now was larger and hotter, and it had no name yet, though she would learn years later to call it witness — the thing that happens when a person stops asking whether something will touch them and starts remembering exactly what it touches.

Upstairs, faintly, through the floorboards, she heard the sound of Beatrix writing.

* * *

In the morning the eastern quarter was gone.

Petra went up to the yard to fetch water and stopped as if she'd walked into glass. From the rise behind the garden she could see clear across the river, and where the eastern quarter had been — the crooked roofs, the washing lines, the tower of the old tannery that she'd used as her compass point on every errand since she was six — there was now a plain of smoking rubble, black and geometrical, chimneys standing alone in the wreckage like gravestones that had forgotten whose they were. Grey soldiers moved through it in files, spraying something from pumps. Steam rose where the spray hit.

The smoke pillar had fallen overnight into a haze that covered everything, and in that haze the living town went about its business. The baker opened. A soldier bought tobacco. Two women argued cheerfully about a fish stall. Petra stood on the rise and stared at them and felt something break and reset in her chest.

*They know,* she thought. *They can see it. They're smelling the same air I'm smelling. And they are buying fish.*

She had believed, in the simple arithmetic of childhood, that horror was contagious — that once people saw, they would have to act, would have to refuse, would have to at least stop buying fish. But the people of Callowmere walked through ash-fall with their eyes fixed at the middle distance, and Petra understood that they were doing the same thing her household had done for years: performing invisibility, pretending not to see, because seeing obliged you, and obligation in an occupied town was a rope.

The difference — and she held onto this difference the whole way back down to the yard, like a coal cupped in both hands — was that her family had stopped pretending. Whatever happened next, whatever the audit brought, however Ilves's long pale gaze lingered on Beatrix, the household had crossed over. Nineteen strangers slept beneath their floors. You could not un-shelter people. You could not unknow the pillar of smoke.

In the kitchen, Marisol was feeding eleven people breakfast out of a pot meant for six, stretching barley with onions and the last of the smoked ham hock, moving with a calm so total it looked almost serene until you noticed her eyes, which were everywhere at once, counting heads, counting exits, counting the light outside for the sign that meant soldiers in the lane. Petra set the water bucket down and picked up a ladle without being asked, and Marisol looked at her — really looked, the way she rarely let herself during the day — and something passed between them that Petra had no words for and never would need words for again.

"You've been up on the rise," Marisol said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"Yes."

"And?"

"It's gone," Petra said. "All of it. The tannery tower too." Her voice stayed level. She heard it stay level and was proud of it, and ashamed of being proud, and confused about which feeling was the true one. "There's ash on everything. It looks like — " she searched — "it looks like the town has a shadow even though the sun's out."

Marisol stirred the pot. For a moment her face did the thing it almost never did: cracked, all the way down, showing the young woman underneath, some girl from decades ago who had also watched a place burn and learned to cook instead of weep.

"Eat first," Marisol said finally. "Then take the basin down to the cellar before the soldiers change their minds about curfew. And Petra —" she caught her wrist, flour-white fingers on a thin brown arm — "when you run the captain's messages today, you will see things. I can't tell you not to look. You're past that now, I think. But you carry what you see down here to me, every night, and you give it to me, and you don't keep it in your bed with you. That's what I'm for. Do you understand?"

Petra understood that Marisol was offering her the only thing she had: a place to put the unbearable. She nodded.

At the door, the corporal was already waiting with the message satchel and his bored, dangerous smile, and Petra squared her shoulders and put on her quick, blank, errand-girl face, the performance of nobody-in-particular, and stepped out into the ash.

But the face was a mask now, and she knew it was a mask, and knowing made all the difference. Underneath it she was a clerk of the disaster, taking inventory: here the fountain, dry; here the smithy, shuttered; here the quay, where the final barge lay low in the water with five days left on its schedule, and beside it, unmistakable even at a distance, Captain Ilves standing with his hands clasped behind him, looking up at the atlas-maker's shop the way a surveyor looks at ground he intends to measure.

He was looking at it for a long time.

Then he took out his notebook and wrote something down, and Petra, who had delivered enough messages to read the body language of paperwork, understood with a cold, settling certainty that whatever he had written was about her family.

She turned toward the signals office to collect her morning orders, walking at exactly the speed of an unremarkable girl, and began composing in her head the sentence she would say to Marisol tonight: *You need to tell Beatrix. He's started writing us down.*
