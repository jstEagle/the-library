## 16. The Bargain Refused

The summons came at dusk, carried by a boy of perhaps twelve who wore the grey coat of the occupation like a borrowed skin, too large in the shoulders and buttoned wrong. *The captain requests the courier.* Not orders. Requests. Beatrix noted the difference the way they had been trained to note everything—wind direction, river height, the number of boots on a corner—and understood that something had shifted in the arithmetic of their danger.

They crossed the square as the lamps were being lit by a soldier with a pole, one flame after another blooming against the violet sky. Callowmere at evening had always been Beatrix's favorite hour, the town folding itself into lamplight the way it had folded itself into obscurity for two hundred years, content to be a smudge on the maps, a footnote in the almanacs. Now the same streets were strung with wire and chalk marks, and soldiers stood in doorways that had held only cats and old men with pipes. The town was still trying to be invisible. It no longer had permission.

The atlas-maker's shop—*their* shop, though it had not felt theirs since the flags went up—glowed from every window. Ilves had turned the front rooms into his signals office, and the light he kept burning late had become a feature of the town's new skyline, a small stubborn constellation that everyone walked past without looking up, because looking up had become a thing one did not do.

A sentry opened the door. Inside, the smell of the shop was still there under everything—the size-smell of rabbit glue, the mineral breath of inks—which struck Beatrix as an obscenity, as if the walls themselves were collaborating.

Captain Ilves sat at Marisol's kitchen table.

That was the first shock. He had moved the interrogation out of the office, out of the room with the telegraph key and the stacked dispatch boxes, into the long low room at the back where the household ate its meals, where Tomas had carved his initials into the table's edge at nineteen and Petra had lost her first tooth biting a plum stone. He had set out two cups. Steam rose from both. He had even found the good tea, the tin Marisol hoarded against disasters, which meant either that his men had searched the pantry or that someone had offered it. Both possibilities were terrible.

"Courier Okafor." He gestured to the chair opposite. "Sit. Please."

He said *please* the way he did everything—precisely, with the faint irony of a man who knew the word had lost its meaning in his mouth and used it anyway to keep track of what things used to mean. He was not a large man. His uniform fit him the way his sentences did, close-tailored, nothing wasted. On the wall behind him, someone had pinned a signal chart over the shop's framed map of the eastern waterways, but the frame's gilt corner showed through like a wound.

Beatrix sat. Did not touch the tea.

"You're wondering why I asked for you at supper hour," Ilves said. "I could have had you brought at dawn. I chose this because I wanted you to see the light." He tilted his head toward the window, where the last of the dusk was draining off the river. "In my country we have a saying about the hour when the lamps are lit. That it is the honest hour. Too late for lies about what the day was, too early for lies about what the night will be." He smiled, thin as paper. "I learned your republic has a saying too. Something about rivers and truth?"

"We say the river doesn't argue," Beatrix said. "It just carries."

"Yes." He looked pleased, genuinely, the way a collector is pleased. "That's the one."

He drank. Set down the cup with a click that sounded, in the quiet room, like a small door closing.

"I am going to speak to you tonight," he said, "as one keeper of records to another. You may find that offensive. I ask you to hear it through anyway, because after tonight I will not be able to speak this way again. Do you understand? After tonight there is only the other voice. The official one." He folded his hands. "So. The Atlas."

Beatrix kept their face still. Nine days of practice had taught them at least that—to hold the name of the ledger inside their body without letting it move a muscle. But Ilves watched them the way he watched everything, and Beatrix understood suddenly and completely that the stillness told him exactly as much as movement would have. He nodded, almost gently, as if confirming a suspicion he'd been carrying around like a stone.

"There is a clerk in the capital—a man named Ferenc, do you know him? No, of course. He audits evacuation registries for the Interior Ministry, or what remains of it." Ilves reached beside his chair and lifted a leather folio onto the table. He did not open it. "Four days ago my office received a routine request. All local ledgers to be verified against central duplicates before transit. Standard procedure for moving population records through a war zone." He paused. "Ferenc's covering letter contained a private line. A note to me personally. He said—" and here Ilves's voice changed, took on a dryness that might have been distaste or might have been admiration, "—he said that in thirty years of auditing, he has never once received a registry that was entirely true. That every census in the history of every state is a negotiation between who people are and who the paper says they are. And that the forger's art, properly practiced, is older than the state itself."

The kettle on the stove ticked as it cooled. Somewhere upstairs Petra's floorboard creaked, the third plank from the door, and Beatrix thought: *she is listening, she should be asleep, we should never have let her learn to listen.*

"I don't know what you're accusing me of," Beatrix said.

"I'm not accusing you of anything." Ilves spread his hands, and for a moment the gesture was so open, so nearly honest, that Beatrix felt the floor of the room tilt. "That is precisely the point. I am offering you the only version of the next four days in which nobody hangs."

He opened the folio.

Inside was not a warrant. It was a single sheet of paper, typed, with blank spaces left where names and dates would go—a confession, pre-drafted, waiting only for a hand. Beside it he laid a fountain pen, uncapped, nib pointed toward Beatrix like something offered rather than aimed.

"The document reads as follows," Ilves said, and recited it—he had memorized it, Beatrix realized; he had written it himself, in the lamp-lit hours, choosing each clause the way Tomas chose contour lines. "*I, courier of the fourth district, certify that the evacuation ledger known as the Atlas contains entries falsified prior to its transfer to my custody, and that the practice of entering unauthorized persons into state registries was widespread in the final administration of the Republic of Vessany.*" He looked up. "You will sign it. You will surrender the ledger to my office tonight, unaltered, and tomorrow you will repeat this statement before a recording officer. In exchange—"

"In exchange," Beatrix said, because he had stopped, and the silence had a weight that pressed on the chest.

"In exchange, the twenty-two persons registered in your household are struck from the prosecution list. Their entry in the Atlas becomes evidence of administrative corruption, not individual fraud—they were victims of a falsifying bureaucracy, not authors of a forgery. They receive transit papers. Safe passage on the barge, protection through the occupied zone, resettlement status across the border." He slid the folio another inch across the table. "Every one of them lives. That is the offer, courier. It does not expire politely."

For a long moment Beatrix could not speak—not because courage failed them but because the offer was *good*. That was the horror of it. Twenty-two names, purchased with one signature and one lie, and the lie wasn't even a lie, not really—it was the truth the republic deserved, that its records had always been fiction dressed in watermarks, that the state had been forging its own people for generations and had simply run out of ink. Beatrix could sign that paper and sleep. Could stand on the barge deck at dawn with Marisol's hand on their shoulder and watch Callowmere shrink into fog, telling themselves the sentence was technically accurate, the sacrifice symbolic, the whole transaction a kind of mercy.

Ilves watched the calculation happen. He had, Beatrix saw, already seen it before—he had drafted the confession knowing it would look like this, knowing that the best traps are baited with things the quarry actually wants.

"You've thought about it," Ilves said quietly. "Good. I'd think less of you if you hadn't."

"What happens to me," Beatrix said. "Under your offer."

"You go on the barge with them." He shrugged. "Your testimony makes you a witness for the prosecution. Witnesses travel first-class, historically." The thin smile again. "Everyone lives, courier. That is genuinely the entire architecture of the arrangement. I want to be clear about that, because I know what you believe about me—that I am the wolf who talks, the officer who quotes poetry while he burns granaries. Perhaps I am. But I have read the requisition orders for this district. I know what happens to towns that resist documentation. I have seen the eastern quarter's allocation of coal for next month, and it is zero." He let that sit. "I am offering you the only soft exit from a hard corridor. Refuse it, and I must become what the uniform requires."

Beatrix looked at the pen. The nib gleamed, a black bead of readiness.

And underneath the fear, in the cellar-dark of themself where they kept the things they actually believed, Beatrix found the thought they had been circling for nine days, since the first perfect stroke of Marisol's name had dried on the forged page: *a republic is only the sum of the names someone bothered to write down.*

If they signed, the names survived—but as victims, as errors, as the paperwork of a corrupt age, erased by the very confession that saved them. The household would live in the new state's memory as nobody, as twenty-two administrative casualties, unentered, unwritten, invisible forever—the thing Beatrix had spent a lifetime being and had sworn, in the dark, on Marisol's kitchen table, never to make anyone be again.

"No," Beatrix said.

The word came out smaller than they wanted. They said it again, and the second time it filled the room.

"No. I won't sign."

Ilves closed his eyes briefly, and Beatrix would spend years afterward trying to name what passed over his face in that half-second. It was not anger. It was closer to grief—the specific, rationed grief of a man watching the cheaper outcome die.

"Courier." He spoke now with great care, laying the words down like a mason laying stones. "Do you understand what an audit means? Four days from now, a clerk named Ferenc arrives on the morning steamer with the central duplicate of the Atlas. Every entry will be compared. Paper, watermark, ink chemistry, the pressure signature of the pen strokes, the sequence of page openings—did you know ledgers remember how they've been opened? The binding remembers. Ferenc knows. And when he finds twenty-two entries that were not in the original—" He shook his head slowly. "It will not be a trial. Trials require the possibility of doubt. This will be an inventory. Of what was taken. And then of what must be paid."

"Then I'll pay it," Beatrix said. "But the names stay in the book."

"The names damn everyone who carries them—"

"They're *in* the book," Beatrix said, and heard their own voice crack open, all the nine days of careful neutrality splitting along its seam. "That's the whole point, Captain. They're in the book. Marisol Reyes, cook, arrived in Callowmere in the year of the eastern floods. Tomas Herrera, cartographer, one hand, draws better than any man with two. Petra Ilves-Voss—" they saw him flinch at nothing, at the accident of the surname, and pushed on, relentless now, "—eleven years old, afraid of thunder, runs errands for bread. They exist. They worked and grieved and buried each other and planted beans in a war zone. And when the republic died, somebody wrote them down. Somebody bothered. Whatever your audit finds, whatever your clerk proves about watermarks and binding-memory, he cannot prove they were never here. The book is true, Captain. It's the only true thing this republic ever made, and I made it, and I would make it again with a gun at my neck."

The silence afterward was enormous. The lamp hissed. Upstairs, the third floorboard creaked again, and neither of them looked up, bound by a mutual pretense of privacy more delicate than anything either army could have negotiated.

Ilves rose. He gathered the confession, folded it once, precisely, and put it away in the folio—and then, after a pause that Beatrix would remember at odd hours for the rest of their life, he placed the fountain pen on top of the table, aligned parallel to its edge, as if returning a borrowed instrument to its place.

"I trained as a surveyor," he said, at the window now, his back turned. "Before the war. Do you know what surveyors learn first? That the map is never the territory, and yet everything depends on the map. Bridges are built where the lines say. Armies march where the lines say. People starve or eat according to lines drawn by tired men in bad light." He turned. His face had reassembled itself into the officer's mask, but the eyes behind it were still the surveyor's, still measuring. "You have made a map of your family, courier, and drawn it onto the territory of the state. It is either the bravest or the stupidest act of drafting I have ever encountered. I honestly cannot tell. I hope, for both our sakes, that I never find out which."

He picked up the bell-pull and rang it once. Boots in the hall.

"The audit stands at four days," Ilves said, as the sentry opened the door and the cold came in off the square. Then he stopped, and Beatrix watched him weigh something—watched the scales move behind his face, duty on one side and something else on the other, something that might have been respect or might have been its opposite. "No," he said softly. "Change of plan. Move it forward. Tomorrow morning. First light."

* * *

Beatrix walked home through the lamplight with the sentry three paces behind, and the town seemed to lean in around them, every shuttered window a held breath. Tomorrow. Not four days. One night. One night to get the Atlas out of the signals office, past the sentries, down the lane, into the water—one night, and Tomas's flawed watermark, and Petra's listening ears, and Marisol's kitchen where the good tea sat cooling in two cups, one of which Beatrix had never touched.

They came in the back door and the household rose from the table as one body, six faces turning, and Beatrix stood in the doorway with the cold still on their coat and said the only thing that mattered:

"He moved it. The audit comes at first light. We go tonight."

Tomas was already reaching for his coat.
