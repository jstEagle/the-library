## 17. The Night of Ink and Water

The rain began at dusk and did not intend to stop.

Beatrix stood at the attic window of the atlas-maker's shop, watching it come down over Callowmere in grey sheets, filling the gutters, drowning the lamps in the square, turning the whole occupied town into something drowned and dreaming. Good weather for thieves. Bad weather for everything else.

Behind them, on the long table where Tomas had once engraved coastlines, the household gathered in a half-circle of candlelight, no lamp showing at the windows. Marisol had dressed for it the way she dressed for funerals—in black, high-collared, her silver hair pinned so tight it looked like armor. Petra sat on the flour bin with her knees drawn up, too young to be included and too implicated to be left out; she knew the soldiers' rotations better than any of them now, bought with crusts of bread and a child's careful smile. Old Ferren was there, and the Halven twins, and the others from the cellar rooms, twenty-one faces when you counted them, and Beatrix had spent enough nights counting lately to do it without looking.

"The audit is tomorrow," Beatrix said. "So tonight."

"We've been through it," Marisol said, not unkindly, in the voice she used to end arguments at supper. "Say it once more, plain."

"Every ledger in the signals office gets verified against the central records by noon. When they reach the Atlas, they'll find our names—twenty-two people who were never registered anywhere—and a watermark with a flaw in it that only a surveyor would catch." Beatrix let that sit. "And Ilves was a surveyor."

"So we don't wait to be found," said Tomas.

He stood at the far end of the table, and in the candlelight he looked older than his fifty years, his ink-stained fingers laced together like a man at his own trial. He had barely slept since the night he'd confessed the flaw—the hair-thin misregistration in the plate, a fraction off true, invisible to any eye but one trained to read the world in coordinates. He had built the forgery's teeth and then discovered the trap's jaw was shaped to his own hand.

"There are two ledgers on Ilves's desk," Tomas went on. "The Atlas, and the signals log. The Atlas is kept in the right-hand drawer, locked, but I've seen the key. It lives on his watch chain when he's in the office and in the top drawer of the map cabinet when he isn't. He isn't, tonight. He dines with the commandant every third night, and tonight is the third night, and the rain will keep him at table longer than usual because commandant Ostrev likes his brandy and hates company that leaves early."

"You've thought about this a great deal," Marisol said quietly.

"I've thought about nothing else for nine days," Tomas said. "That's what guilt does. It makes an excellent cartographer."

Beatrix almost smiled. Almost. "Petra?"

"Sentry changes at the square at eleven. Two men on the office door until then, then one—Rennick, the sleepy one, he stands under the awning out of the rain. The window on the river side is latched but the frame's rotten; I've leaned on it. And the alarm—" She hesitated, and Beatrix watched her gather herself, this child who had been running errands for occupying soldiers since autumn, learning their habits the way you learn a dangerous dog's. "The alarm wire runs along the skirting to the bell in the stairwell. If anyone crosses the office floor after midnight without lifting the trip-plate by the desk leg, the bell rings and the whole garrison hears it. The plate is brass. You step *over*, not around. Rennick told me they can never remember which desk leg."

"Rennick talks too much," Ferren grunted.

"Rennick talks exactly as much as bread buys him," Petra said, and there was something in her voice then, flat and old, that made Beatrix's chest hurt. Innocence, weaponized by circumstance. They had all done this to her, simply by staying alive in interesting times.

"Then we go in at half past eleven," Beatrix said. "Tomas lifts the key. I take the Atlas. We leave the signals log in its place, wrapped, same weight—we're not trying to hide forever, only past dawn, past noon, onto the barge. Once we're on the water, the audit finds an empty drawer and a town full of nobodies, and by the time they sort out what happened, the Atlas is evidence in another country."

"And if it goes wrong?" asked Sela Halven, who always asked.

Beatrix looked at the candle flame a moment before answering, because the honest answer was a shape none of them should look at directly.

"Then we run for the river anyway," they said. "All of us. The barge or no barge."

Marisol crossed the room and took Beatrix's face in both hands, the way she had when Beatrix arrived at the shop eleven years ago, feverish and half-starved and lying about their name. She smelled of cardamom and woodsmoke, of every supper the family had ever eaten.

"No one is left behind," she said. "You hear me? Not for a ledger. Not for a republic. A republic is paper. You are my child, and you come back, or none of the rest of it means anything."

"Yes, Mama," Beatrix said, using the word for the second time in eleven years, and saw it land in Marisol like a stone in still water.

* * *

The rain was a mercy and a menace both.

They went down through the garden in the dark, Beatrix and Tomas, while Marisol held the rest of the household in the cellar with the door barred and the lamp turned low—her orders, given in a voice that permitted no appeal. The garden wall between the shop and the square had crumbled at one corner since before the war; they went over it single file, Beatrix first, Tomas breathing hard behind, and came out among the flooded stalls of the market, awnings sagging, the cobbles running silver.

The signals office had been the wool merchant's guild hall before the army came. Beatrix knew its every line—they had copied maps of the river district on its floorboards in another life. Now it wore grey paint and a flag, and under the awning by the door, one sentry stood with his collar up and his shoulders rounded against the wet. Rennick. True to Petra's word, true to bread.

The river-side window gave with less protest than Beatrix had feared, the rotten frame sighing open like an old man making room. Tomas went through first, and Beatrix followed, and for a moment they stood inside the enemy's house dripping on the parquet, listening to the building breathe.

The office smelled of lamp oil, cold tobacco, and Ilves's cologne—something austere, resinous, that Beatrix had come to dread the way one dreads a certain tone of voice. Moonlight never came; the rain owned the sky. They worked by shuttered lantern, a coin of light no wider than a hand.

The map cabinet's top drawer slid silently. The key was there, small and brass, warm somehow, as though recently touched.

"He was here late," Tomas whispered.

"Then we're quick."

The right-hand drawer of the desk opened on oiled runners, and there it was: the Atlas, fat and official, its cover stamped with the seal of the Republic of Vessany, holding within it—now—twenty-two forged names and the whole weight of their lives. Beatrix lifted it and felt it the way one feels a heartbeat, external and essential at once. Beneath it lay the signals log. Together, in the dark, they swapped the wrappings: oilcloth, cordage, matched knots, Tomas's hands doing the fine work while Beatrix held the light. Three minutes. Four.

It was going too well. Beatrix knew it the way sailors know weather.

They had the drawer closed and were moving toward the window when the floor spoke.

Not the alarm—not yet—only the smallest complaint of an old board, a sound like a struck syllable, but in the silence of that room it might as well have been a gunshot. Beatrix froze. And in the freeze, in the terrible half-second of listening, Beatrix's heel came down beside the desk leg instead of clear of it, and the brass trip-plate tipped with a click like a lock turning.

Somewhere below, in the stairwell, the bell began to ring.

For one heartbeat neither of them moved; the absurdity of it held them—the whole plan undone by a plank and a plate. Then the night tore open. Boots in the barracks across the square, lights blooming in windows, a whistle shrieking, and under the awning Rennick abandoning his post to run for the guardroom, shouting words the rain ate whole.

"Window," Tomas said.

"The Atlas—"

"*Window.*"

Beatrix went through first, clutching the ledger to their chest, and dropped into the flooded yard as the office door burst inward behind them. Tomas was halfway out when the first shot came—wild, high, punching a hole in the frame above his head—and he twisted, lost his grip, fell beside Beatrix into the mud. He got up. That was the thing Beatrix would carry afterward, through everything that came after: he got up, and he shoved the Atlas harder into Beatrix's arms, and he turned toward the gate instead of away from it.

"They'll cut us off at the quay," he said. "Someone has to make them look at the wrong runner."

"Tomas—"

"I forged a republic's census, Bea. Let me forge them a diversion." He grinned, wild and white in the dark, and then he was gone through the gate, crashing deliberately loud through the market stalls, shouting—actually shouting, laughing as he did it—*"This way, you grey bastards!"*

Whistles answered him. Boots turned toward the wrong runner. Beatrix ran for the river with the Atlas under their coat and the rain hiding everything, including, God help them, whether Tomas was still ahead or already down.

* * *

The quay came out of the rain in pieces: mooring posts, coiled rope shining black, the humped shapes of barges asleep against the pilings. The final barge sat third from the ramp, low in the water with cargo, her stern lamp burning—a single defiant point of gold in all that grey. Beatrix ran the length of the planks, lungs full of knives, and had nearly reached the ramp when a voice came out of the dark, unhurried, cultured, terribly awake.

"You're out past curfew, courier."

Ilves stood beneath the harbor master's lamp, and he was not wet, which meant he had been standing there some time, sheltered, waiting—which meant, in some cold chamber of Beatrix's mind, that he had known. Behind him, two soldiers, rifles slung, and beyond them the rain, and beyond the rain the river running fast and blind toward the border.

Beatrix stopped. The Atlas pressed against their ribs like a second heart.

"You dined with the commandant," Beatrix managed.

"I left early. Commandant Ostrev hates company that lingers, and I dislike brandy when I'm thinking." Ilves stepped forward into the rain as though it were merely a courtesy extended to him. Up close his face was tired; there were lines around his eyes that poetry-quoting captains acquired honestly. In his hand, incongruous, domestic, he carried a folded umbrella he had not bothered to open. "Where is your cartographer?"

The question hit like cold water. Beatrix said nothing, and the nothing lasted too long, and Ilves read it fluently. Something moved behind his eyes—not triumph. Regret, maybe, or its well-dressed cousin.

"Ah," he said softly. "So there was a cartographer."

Somewhere back in the town, muffled by rain and distance, a rifle cracked. Then another. Beatrix's whole body turned toward the sound before the mind could forbid it, and Ilves watched that happen, watched the truth cross Beatrix's face, and did not smile, and did not look away.

"He's alive," Ilves said, and it was not quite a guess. "My men are instructed to wound runners. Corpses generate paperwork." A pause, the rain ticking on his shoulders. "I'd like to say the same policy protects you. But you've made yourself complicated, Beatrix Okafor."

"You know my name."

"I've known it since the requisition. I read everything in this town—it's the only way to know what a place is, once you've taken it." He held out his hand, palm up, patient. "Give me the ledger."

And here was the arithmetic of the quay, performed in a single breath: two soldiers, one river, twenty-two names against Beatrix's skin. Run, and be shot with the Atlas sinking into the mud, the family's names drowned unwitnessed. Surrender it, and—what? Ilves was not a brute. That had been the danger of him from the first week, the seduction of him: a captain who quoted poetry, who suspected precisely and Bargained fairly. But fair was not the same as kind, and the audit was at noon, and the flaw in the watermark was shaped like Tomas's hand.

Beatrix's fingers found the oilcloth under their coat.

Downriver, faint as a memory, a barge horn sounded—one long note, the note of a boat making ready to take on passengers before dawn.

Ilves heard it too. His eyes went to the water, and when they returned they had settled into something final.

"Well," he said. "Let's discuss berths."

He knows about the barge, Beatrix thought, and the rain came down on Callowmere like a curtain falling on the first act of something much worse.
