## 18. One Berth

They held Beatrix in the customs house, in a room that had once been used for weighing cargo, and the first thing Beatrix noticed was the scale.

It stood in the corner like an abandoned god—brass pans hanging from chains gone green at the joints, the dial face clouded with salt-fog from the river. Someone had left a rusted iron weight in one pan, a five-kilo measure stamped with the old republic's seal, and it sat there balancing nothing, weighing air. Beatrix found they could not stop looking at it. There was something obscene about a scale with only one side loaded, something that itched at the mind the way an unrhymed line itched at the ear of a man who had spent his life copying other people's words into perfect order.

The room smelled of hemp and old brine and, beneath it, gun oil—the smell of the army that had come up the river road three weeks ago and made itself comfortable in Callowmere's bones. A single window, high and barred, let down a grey light that did not warm anything it touched. Somewhere below, boots on stone. Somewhere farther, the river, which Beatrix could not see but could feel the way one feels a tooth that has been bothering him for days: constantly, lowly, insistently. The barge was meant to leave in thirty-one hours. He knew this the way he knew his own pulse now. Everything in him had narrowed to arithmetic—hours, heads, one berth—and the arithmetic had teeth.

He had been given a stool, a table bolted to the floor, and a cup of water he had not touched. His wrists still bore the marks of the cord from the quay, faint red lines like the ghost of a sentence half-erased. His coat was damp where the river had reached him. Tomas's blood was on his sleeve—he had not been able to wash it off, and he was not sure he wanted to. It had dried brown, the color of old ink, and there was a terrible part of him that kept making the comparison and could not stop.

Tomas was alive. They had told him that much, or rather Ilves had, in that maddening level voice, as though reporting the depth of water under a bridge. *Shot through the shoulder. The surgeon says he will keep.* As if that were mercy enough. As if keeping were the same as living.

The door opened without ceremony, because Ilves did not believe in ceremony; he believed in arrival. He came in carrying a leather folder under one arm and two cups of coffee, and he set both on the table—one in front of Beatrix, one at his own place—and sat down across from him with the unhurried ease of a man settling in to read a good book by a fire.

Beatrix did not touch the coffee.

"You're wondering why I haven't had you beaten," Ilves said.

"The thought had crossed my mind."

"It's inefficient." He opened the folder. "Pain produces noise. I need quiet things from you. Precise things." He looked up, and in the grey light his face was older than it had seemed in the signals office, the lines around his eyes deeper, as though the war had been carving on him too, slowly, with a dull blade. "You slept, I hope."

"I didn't."

"Neither did I." He said it without self-pity, simply as fact, and pushed the second cup an inch closer to Beatrix, an offering that asked nothing. "Your cartographer is being treated well. Clean bandages, laudanum when he needs it. The girl Petra has not been questioned. The others are confined to the shop, which is, I am told, more comfortable than their own homes would be. These are not threats. I am telling you the current state of the world, so that when I change it, you understand the exchange rate."

Beatrix folded his hands on the table to keep them from shaking. They were ink-stained even now—scrubbed twice this morning under guard, but the stains lived in the cracks of his knuckles, in the crescents under his nails, ten years of courier's work ground into the skin like a confession written too many times to wash away. Ilves looked at the hands, then at his face.

"You went into the river for it," Ilves said. "I watched you come up the quay holding that ledger above your head like a man lifting a child out of a flood. I have seen soldiers do less for their own wounded." He paused. "It was a remarkable thing to watch. It was also, I want you to know, entirely predictable. I have known since the watermark."

"I know," Beatrix said quietly. "Tomas figured it out before you did, incidentally. That it was flawed. He tried to tell me."

"He would. Surveyors are honest about terrain. It's their curse." Ilves drew a paper from the folder and laid it flat on the table, turning it so Beatrix could read it right way up—a courtesy, or a cruelty, hard to say which. It was typed in the army's cramped field hand, stamped twice, and the stamp had bled slightly at the lower left corner, and some distant clerk-part of Beatrix's mind noted that the ink they used out here was cut with river water, that was why it feathered, that was a thing worth knowing, and he hated himself for noting it. He read it anyway. Everyone in the room was named. Every charge. Falsification of state records. Theft of government property. Conspiracy. And beneath each name, the word that mattered: *unprosecuted.*

"Here is what happens next," Ilves said, "if we do nothing. In thirty-one hours the barge leaves. Your family—your household, forgive me, I know you are particular about the word—will be detained pending audit verification from central records, which arrived yesterday by launch, incomplete, damaged in transit, conveniently useless. Without a verified census, everyone in this town is a question mark. Question marks get put on lists. Lists go to labor battalions east of the mountains, and I have seen the ledgers from east of the mountains, Courier Okafor, and they make very short reading." He folded his hands, mirroring Beatrix's, a parody of intimacy across the scarred table. "Or."

"Or," Beatrix repeated. His voice came out steadier than he expected.

"Or the Atlas leaves on that barge as authentic. Verified. Filed. Every name in it travels under its protection, and no one in that shop is ever troubled again, because the record will be clean and the record, in my experience, is the only thing anyone upstairs ever actually reads." He slid the charges aside and placed a second document over them, blank except for a heading. "In exchange, you confess. Fully. Publicly enough for the file. You state that you alone forged the Atlas—that the household knew nothing, that the cartographer's plates were stolen without his knowledge, that you acted alone out of"—he glanced at the phrase he had prepared, faintly distasteful—"personal ambition and sentiment. You surrender yourself. You take the berth or you don't take the berth, that part is indifferent to me, but you go into custody and you stay there, wherever they send you, until someone upstairs decides you are no longer interesting. Which may be years. Which may be never."

*One berth.* The words landed in Beatrix like a stone dropped down a well. He heard them fall a long way before anything answered.

"And the flaw," Beatrix said. "The watermark. If anyone ever looks closely—"

"They won't. Audits verify names against names, not paper against memory. And I will be the one who signs off on the verification." Ilves said it plainly, without making it a gift, without letting it become kindness. "You should understand what you'd be trusting. I am not doing this for you. I trained as a surveyor, Okafor. Do you know what a surveyor does? He records what is there. Not what should be, not what was promised—what *is*. Right now, what is, is a ledger containing twenty-two names that no other document on earth contains. If I expose it as forged, those people cease to exist. If I file it as true, they exist forever. I have decided I prefer them existing. That is the whole of my morality in this matter, and I recommend you don't mistake it for more than it is."

For a long moment Beatrix could not speak. He looked at the scale in the corner, at its single loaded pan, and thought: *that's wrong, that's all wrong, a scale needs two sides,* and then understood, with a sensation like ice going down the spine, that the scale had been right all along. There was only ever one weight. Everything else was air.

There was a version of him—an older version, the version who had walked into Callowmere eleven years ago with a courier's satchel and a policy of being nobody—who would have taken the deal differently. Who would have bargained, hedged, kept something back. That version had died somewhere between the first forged stroke of Marisol's name and the moment Tomas went down on the signals office floor with his hand pressed to his shoulder, saying, *go, go, go* in a voice that cracked down the middle.

"I'll dictate it," Beatrix said.

Ilves's eyebrows rose a fraction. He had expected negotiation; Beatrix watched him recalibrate, and took a small fierce pleasure in having surprised him.

"But I'm not writing what you drafted," Beatrix said. "No ambition. No sentiment. Those are your words, and they're lies, and if I'm going to spend years paying for this then the confession is going to be true, every line of it, or it's worthless. Bring your clerk. Bring paper. And bring the Atlas—I want to see it before I sign anything. I want to see that it's intact."

"It's intact. Twenty-two names, all entries original to the page." Ilves studied him. "You realize you're dictating a longer sentence for yourself. Every true detail adds weight."

"I know exactly how much it weighs." Beatrix finally picked up the coffee. It had gone lukewarm, and it tasted of chicory and burnt acorns, the taste of every occupied town in every war ever fought, and it was the best thing he had ever drunk, because it was warm and it was offered and because his hands needed something to hold while he said what came next.

"Write this," he said. "Begin with my full name. Beatrix Okafor, courier, fourth district, sworn to neutrality eleven years last spring, oath administered by Registrar Henneman, who is dead now, and who I hope would forgive me, though I doubt it. State that on the night of—" he closed his eyes and the date came to him whole, because dates always came to him whole, that had been the gift and the curse of his life, "—on the night of the ninth, I entered twenty-two names into the Atlas of Evacuation using plates from the atlas-maker's shop on Tanner's Row, and that the security watermark was reproduced by Tomas Ferro's method, and that Tomas Ferro had no knowledge of the forgery, none, write that twice, I want it unmistakable, and that Marisol Achebe, Petra Ilves—no, Petra Achebe, she took her name from Marisol years ago and no one gave it to her, she chose it, write that too—"

His voice caught. He had not meant it to. He swallowed and kept going, faster now, the words coming the way the river had come up over the quay, and Ilves, to his credit, did not interrupt, only signaled through the door for his clerk and sat back and listened the way a surveyor listens to terrain: recording what is.

"—and that every one of them knew nothing, agreed to nothing, and were entered because they deserved to be, not because they earned it, there is no earning a berth, there is only someone willing to write your name down, and I was willing, I am the only one who was willing, and I would do it again tonight with the rope already around my neck. Put that in. *I would do it again.* Underline it." He stopped. Drew breath. The clerk's pen scratched behind him like a small animal in a wall. "And end it with this: that the Republic of Vessany kept a ledger promising evacuation to every citizen, and it forgot these twenty-two, and a ledger that forgets people is worse than no ledger at all, and if the record I leave behind is a forgery, then let historians note that it is the only honest document the republic ever produced, because at least it doesn't leave anyone out."

Silence. The clerk's pen stopped. Ilves looked at Beatrix across the table for a long moment, and something moved behind his eyes—not warmth, Beatrix would not insult either of them by calling it warmth—but recognition, the look one craftsman gives another across an impossible standard.

"That last paragraph," Ilves said slowly, "will follow you for the rest of your life. Into every cell, every camp, every file. It reads like a manifesto."

"It is a manifest," Beatrix said. "Twenty-two names. Read it again."

* * *

They brought the Atlas at dusk.

A soldier carried it in wrapped in oilcloth—the same oilcloth, Beatrix realized, in which the dying clerk had handed it to him a lifetime ago on the dock, dread arriving by boat—and laid it on the table between them, and for a moment neither man touched it. Then Ilves unwrapped it and turned it toward Beatrix, open to the final pages, and there they were. All twenty-two. Marisol's strong careful letters of state ink, Petra's small fierce scrawl, Tomas with his title restored—*cartographer*—because Beatrix had written it so. Old Jozef, the twins, the deserters from the ferry garrison, the orphan girl with the burn-scarred wrist whose name Beatrix had copied from the inside cover of her only book. Twenty-two names under the lamplight, sitting on the page as calmly as if the state itself had written them, and perhaps it had, Beatrix thought. Perhaps that was all a state had ever been: somebody, somewhere, willing to pick up the pen.

"The barge loads at the north quay at dawn," Ilves said. "One berth remains unclaimed. Priority class—it will pass any inspection." He laid a travel chit beside the ledger, cream-colored, official, absurd. "This is yours if you want it. I am not required to offer it. I am offering it because you dictated forty minutes of confession without once asking what happens to you, and because I have spent three weeks trying to find the seam in you and there isn't one, and a man gets tired of shooting at a wall that turns out to be a person."

Beatrix picked up the chit. It weighed almost nothing. Paper, cream and smooth, printed with a number—*1*—and a destination across the border, and he held it and felt the whole weight of the scale in the corner settle onto that single pan, and understood that this was the last choice of his life and that it had, in truth, been made years ago, in a shop that smelled of glue and lamp-black, by a family that had taken him in when he was nobody's.

If he boarded, the confession stood and the family sailed free and he would carry his freedom across the border like a man carrying a stolen coat that fits badly. If he stayed—

There was no *if*. That was the discovery. He turned the chit over, looking for fine print, and found himself instead looking at his own hands, ink in the cracks, ink under the nails, and heard Marisol's voice as clearly as if she stood behind him: *you don't count heads in this house, boy, you feed them.*

"No," Beatrix said.

He set the chit down on the table, squared its edges with the ledger the way he had squared dispatches for eleven years, and slid it back across to Ilves.

"Give it to the girl with the burn scars. Her name's on page forty-one. She'll need proof she exists, where they're going." He stood, and his legs nearly failed him—not fear, only the exhaustion of a man setting down something he has carried too far—and he saw Ilves rise too, and for a moment they faced each other across the table like two men at a graveside who had dug it together. "The confession stands. I go into custody. The Atlas goes on the barge filed as authentic, and you sign the verification, and you live with it, surveyor. You record what is."

Ilves picked up the chit. He looked at it a long time, and then he tore it in half, and then in half again, and dropped the pieces in the cold coffee, where they floated and darkened like dead moths.

"There was never a berth," he said quietly. "I offered it to see. Forgive me. It was the last test I had." He gathered the confession, the folder, the Atlas, wrapping it again in oilcloth with the care of a man handling a relic, and at the door he stopped, his back to the room. "For whatever it is worth to you, Courier Okafor—the new administration burns records it considers tainted. Yours will survive precisely because I am filing it as genuine. History is not what happened. It is what someone was willing to certify." A pause. "Dawn is in six hours. They will come for you at first light. I would sit down."

The door closed. The lock did not turn—there was nowhere to run to, and Ilves knew it—and Beatrix stood alone in the customs house with the empty scale and the grey light dying in the high window, and he found he was smiling, aching, ruined, smiling, because somewhere out there in the fog coming up off the river, a barge was preparing to carry twenty-two names into the future, and every one of them belonged to him.

At dawn, when the boots came up the stairs, the fog on the north quay was so thick that Marisol, counting heads on the deck, would find the count wrong by one—and begin, in front of soldiers and strangers and God, to scream.
