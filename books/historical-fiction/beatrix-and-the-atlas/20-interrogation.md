## 20. Interrogation

The cell smelled of wet stone and lamp oil, and Beatrix had counted the bricks so many times that they had begun to feel like a text—some old, dull scripture of the Republic of Vessany, laid down by hands that had never imagined anyone would read them this closely. Two hundred and eleven. Give or take. The ones behind the cot were too dark to count properly, and after a while Beatrix had decided that uncertainty was a kind of mercy. A person could go mad trying to know everything exactly.

Three days, they thought it had been. Or four. The bucket in the corner kept its own calendar of sorts; the slot in the door opened twice a day for bread and water, and once, absurdly, for a tin cup of tea with too much sugar in it, which Beatrix had drunk before remembering to be suspicious. The sugar had felt like a message. Everything did, now.

When the bolts finally drew back—not mealtime, then—the light from the corridor came in at an angle Beatrix hadn't earned yet, and Captain Ilves stood in it with a folder under his arm and his coat brushed clean, as though he were calling on them socially and merely needed to borrow a chair.

"They've given you tea," he observed, glancing at the empty cup. "Good. I told them you'd take it sweet."

"I didn't ask who you were," Beatrix said. Their voice came out rustier than they expected, scraped thin by days of silence. "That first night at the shop. I never asked who you were. You never said."

"Major Ilves. Signals and records division." He set the folder on the small table bolted to the floor, dragged the single stool over, and sat down with the weary economy of a man who had done this many times and taken no pleasure in any of it. "Before that, surveyor. Before that, a boy in a river town rather like this one, copying maps for a man who beat his apprentices. You'll forgive me if I don't romanticize my origins. Everyone in this war has origins, and most of them are squalid."

"You quoted poetry in my kitchen," Beatrix said. "You ate Marisol's bread and complimented her saffron rolls. You let Petra carry your messages because she was hungry."

"Yes." He opened the folder without hurry. "And now we're here, in a cell with two hundred bricks, and you're going to tell me what I already know."

*Two hundred and eleven,* Beatrix almost said, and bit it back. Some part of the mind refused to surrender even to itself.

He laid the papers out one by one, like a man dealing cards he had already memorized: the signals log from the requisition, the inventory of seized documents, the courier's oath Beatrix had signed nine years ago, in a government office that no longer existed except as ash somewhere east of Callowmere. Last of all, wrapped in its oilcloth, the Atlas.

Beatrix's heart moved when they saw it—a single hard knock against the ribs, involuntary as a flinch from a blow—and they hated that it moved, hated that Ilves watched it move and noted it, the way he noted everything, with those grey surveyor's eyes that measured distance and bearing and the exact weight of other people's griefs.

"The audit found what I expected it to find," he said. "Twenty-two names, entered in a hand consistent with the ledger's own clerks. Watermark correct to the eye. Registration seals present. And every name belonging to one household on Copper Lane, a household that does not exist in any census, tax roll, or conscription list going back eleven years." He folded his hands. "Tell me how."

"No."

"Beatrix Okafor." He said the whole name deliberately, tasting it, the way one tests a coin between the teeth. "Courier, second class. Sworn to neutrality. Nine years carrying documents you were forbidden to read, and by your own record you never once broke that rule. Not once. Do you know how rare that is? Do you know how many couriers skimmed, sold, traded? You held the line for nearly a decade, and then in the space of nine days you forged the single most important document in the republic." He leaned forward slightly. "I don't want a confession extracted under duress. I have plenty of those; they're worthless. What I want is to understand what happened to you between the oath and the ink."

Beatrix looked at him for a long time. The lamp guttered and steadied. Somewhere above, boots crossed the courtyard, unhurried, ordinary, the sound of a world continuing to run its machinery regardless of what happened in the cellar of it.

And Beatrix understood, slowly, the way dawn comes—by degrees and then all at once—that this was not an interrogation in the way they had feared. It was something stranger. It was a man asking a question he genuinely could not answer himself.

"All right," Beatrix said. "I'll tell you how. But you're going to hear all of it, and you're going to write it down yourself, in your own hand, because I'm done letting other people hold my words."

* * *

They talked through the morning, or what passed for morning underground. Ilves took notes in a small precise script, and Beatrix talked: about the clerk on the boat, dying, pressing the oilcloth bundle into their arms with fingers already cold. About the hollowed folio and the soldiers' boots in the shop, and the household standing in a row pretending to be servants in their own home. About the berth count—the arithmetic of it, twenty-two souls against one unclaimed place, the way the numbers had crawled into Beatrix's head at night and multiplied there like something diseased.

"That's where it began," Ilves said quietly. "The arithmetic."

"That's where it began." Beatrix's hands were steady in their lap; they made sure of it. "I lied to Marisol's face. I sat across from her at supper and told her everything would be arranged, knowing that 'arranged' meant choosing which of them got to live. Twenty-one people I love more than I have ever loved anything, and one berth, and I caught myself—" their voice caught instead, and they let it, because they had decided to give him truth and half-truths were just lies wearing better clothes— "I caught myself *counting*. Weighing. Tomas is useful, he can map anywhere. Petra is young, she has the most years ahead. Marisol is old. My Marisol, who fed every hungry thing that ever crossed our doorstep, and I stood in the dark doing sums on her life like a quarrelsome merchant haggling over goods. That's what the state does to people, Captain. It teaches you that love is a ledger. And then I thought: if the ledger is the only language this world understands, then perhaps I should learn to write in it."

"And the forgery itself?"

"Tomas found the watermark flaw. The plates were still in the shop—old atlas plates, the security pattern built into the borders of the provincial maps. He inked it. I entered the names." Beatrix paused. "All twenty-two. In one night. Marisol kept watch at the top of the stairs with a rolling pin, which I mention only because you should know the republic's greatest act of treason was guarded by a woman with a rolling pin and a jar of pickled plums."

Something moved at the corner of Ilves's mouth. It might have been the ancestor of a smile, exiled three generations back.

"You understand what you confessed to," he said. "Falsification of state records. The penalty—"

"I know the penalty. I did the arithmetic on that too, and unlike the berth count, this one came out clean. Every number in it was a person, and every person gets to keep breathing. That's the whole confession, Captain. That's the entire crime. Now write it down."

Ilves set down his pen. For a while he simply looked at the Atlas, lying on the table between them like a patient between physicians.

Then he said, "Do you know why I trained as a surveyor?"

"Because you liked maps."

"Because maps are the only honest documents a country produces." His voice changed—lowered, slowed, taking on the cadence of a man reciting something he had rehearsed alone for years without an audience. "A census lies about who counts. A tax roll lies about who owes. But a map only says: here is where things are. Here is the river. Here is the road. Here is the town. When I surveyed Callowmere, eight years ago—it was on the secondary triangulation, you'd never have known—I put the atlas-maker's shop on it myself. A small square building on Copper Lane. I remember thinking it was an odd thing to bother with. A map shop in a town nobody visits." He tapped the ledger. "And now I find that the entire population of that odd little building was invisible to the republic. Eleven years. Twenty-two people, born and fed and buried and married, and officially none of it happened. Tell me, courier—which of us falsified the record first?"

The question landed in the cell like a dropped lantern, lighting walls Beatrix had never seen.

"The republic," Beatrix said slowly, "was never the parliament. You know that. It was never the ministries, or the registers, or the men who signed the surrender terms last spring in a room full of flags. A republic is—" they searched, and found it waiting, because they had been building this argument for months without knowing it, laying it brick by brick in the dark like their two hundred and eleven bricks— "a republic is the sum of the names someone bothered to write down. Nothing else survives. Nothing else was ever real. When the invading army comes and the government runs and the archives burn, the only thing left of a country is whatever some clerk, some courier, some cook with a rolling pin decided was worth putting into the record. The Vessany that mattered wasn't in the capital. It was twenty-two names long, and it lived above a map shop, and until nine days ago it was *unrecorded*, which means as far as history will ever know, it did not exist."

"And so you wrote it into existence."

"So I wrote it into existence." Beatrix met his eyes. "You can call it forgery. I call it the census. The true one. The only one anyone will ever do honestly, because I wasn't counting taxpayers or soldiers or votes. I was counting *people*. Marisol Ferreiro, who arrived with nothing but a knife and a recipe. Tomas Beck, who ruined himself correcting other men's errors. Petra, who was left in our doorway in a beet crate one frost morning and grew up believing she'd been delivered by storks with bad aim. Every one of them real. Every one of them fed somebody, taught somebody, mended somebody. If the new state wants a record of what Vessany was, it should start there, because that household contained more of the republic than the entire Ministry of the Interior ever did."

Silence. The lamp burned steadily. Ilves looked down at his notes for a long moment, and when he looked up, his face had settled into something Beatrix had not seen on it before—not the courteous mask of the occupier, not the sharp attention of the interrogator. Something tired and human and very old.

"When I was a boy," he said, "my mother kept a family Bible. Births on the front page, deaths in the back. When our town burned—this was the border war, before your time—the Bible burned with it. My sister asked me, years later, *did we happen? Were we real, if no one wrote us down?* She was joking. Mostly." He closed the folder. "I have spent thirty years writing down where things are, courier, and I have come to believe it is the wrong question. You have spent nine years learning *who* things are. I begin to suspect you had the better of it."

He rose, gathered the papers, and paused with his hand on the Atlas.

"There will be a tribunal," he said. "There must be. Your confession will be entered, and it will name you alone—you have been clear on that point, and the record reflects it. No accomplices. No household. The twenty-two names will be described as clerical error in the original registration process, corrected by a diligent courier acting within the spirit of their office." He met Beatrix's stare evenly. "That is not what happened. You and I both know precisely what happened. But I am the ranking records officer in this district, and when the new administration asks me whether the evacuation ledger of Callowmere is authentic, I am going to tell them that it is. Because I have examined it, and cross-checked it, and verified its watermark, and I find it accurate. Twenty-two persons. All real. All accounted for." He lifted the ledger with both hands, carefully, the way one carries water. "A record is true, courier, if it tells the truth about the world. This one does. Whatever else it is."

"Why?" The word came out of Beatrix before they could weigh it, and it sounded, to their own ears, astonishingly like a prayer.

Ilves stood in the doorway, half in lamplight, half in corridor shadow, and for a moment he looked every year of his age and every mile he had marched away from the boy with the maps.

"Because someone bothered to write them down," he said. "It seems to be the only test that matters. Goodbye, courier Okafor. I suspect history will be harder on both of us than either of us deserves, and I find I no longer much care what it thinks."

The door shut. The bolts turned. And Beatrix sat alone in the lamplight with two hundred and eleven bricks and the strangest sensation of victory they had ever carried—weightless, unbloodied, entirely made of paper—until they realized they were laughing, or weeping, or both, quietly, in the dark, like a person set free in a room with no exit.

* * *

They came for Beatrix a week later—no tribunal, in the end; the paperwork of collapsing states moves faster than its justice—and put them on a transport east with forty other prisoners whose crimes, whispered along the wire fences, ranged from desertion to hoarding to nothing at all. The work camp received them without ceremony. Beatrix learned the rhythms of it: the whistle, the count, the mud that never dried, the particular silence of people conserving themselves for whatever came next.

They learned other things too. That Marisol's name was spelled correctly in the new state's evacuation registry—Petra's, Tomas's, all twenty-two, filed and stamped and real. That a guard could be bribed with a story. That the human heart, given enough darkness, will manufacture its own lamps.

And in the fourth winter, when the cough had settled into Beatrix's chest like a tenant negotiating a long lease, an orderly brought the camp post with the gruel, and among the thin stack of envelopes was one addressed in a hand Beatrix knew better than their own—careful, generous letters, the *r*s looping like riverbends on one of Tomas's maps.

Beatrix sat down on the edge of the cot, held the envelope for a long moment, feeling its weight, which was far more than paper.

Then they opened it, and began to read.
