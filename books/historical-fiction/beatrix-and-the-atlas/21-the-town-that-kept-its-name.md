## 21. The Town That Kept Its Name

The town had no name when they arrived, only a cluster of stone houses with their windows blown out and a surveyor's chalk mark on the well that read *Resettlement Site K-7*. The new authorities, in their mercy, let the refugees choose a name. There was a long argument about it in the square, conducted in six languages, and in the end it was an old man from the vineyard country who won them over by standing on a cart axle and shouting that a town without a name was a grave without a stone.

They called it Vessany's End, at first, out of grief. Then, when grief got too heavy to carry up the hill every morning for water, they called it Callowmere-in-Exile, out of stubbornness. Then the border commission sent a clerk who informed them that names referencing defeated states or foreign municipalities were not permitted under Article Fourteen, and after that everyone simply called it *the town*, which suited Petra fine. A town that kept its name in its mouth instead of on its signs could never have it taken away again.

Six years had done what six years do. The broken windows were glazed with mismatched panes, amber and bottle-green, so that in the evenings the whole lower street glowed like something fermenting. There was a school with a slate roof, a chapel used on alternate Sundays by two congregations who hated each other politely, and a bakery whose chimney had been rebuilt three times by a mason who refused payment and refused, equally, to explain why.

And there was Marisol's kitchen, which was not so much a room as a weather system.

Petra came down the hill just before dawn, as she did every morning now, carrying the day's first argument in her pocket — a letter from the schooling commission, which wanted to know by what authority a nineteen-year-old taught writing to forty children in an unlicensed room. She had learned from Beatrix, years ago, that you carried bad documents close to your body where they couldn't surprise you.

The kitchen door stood open because it always stood open, winter included; Marisol said a kitchen with a closed door fed nobody. Inside, steam rose off three pots in columns that leaned east with the draft, and Marisol herself stood at the long table with flour to her elbows, tearing yesterday's bread into a bowl with the efficiency of a woman who had buried kitchens before and would bury this one eventually too, but not today.

"You're up early," Marisol said, not looking up.

"The commission wrote again."

"Put it under the salt box. Paper keeps; bread doesn't."

Petra put the letter under the salt box, which was where all official correspondence went to die. It was a system with a perfect record. Then she took up the knife Marisol handed her without either of them looking — the choreography of six years — and began cutting onions, and for a while there was nothing in the world but the sound of work being done by people who trusted each other to do their share.

It was Halvard who broke the silence, coming in from the yard with frost still on his shoulders, a former deserter turned keeper of goats, holding his cap in his hands the way he still did whenever he had news he thought might be bad.

"Rider on the north road," he said. "Border post colors. Coming here."

Nobody stopped working. That was the discipline they had learned: when soldiers or their cousins appeared, you kept your hands moving, because hands that stop moving look like hands with something to hide, and this family had spent enough of its history being looked at.

"Coming here for what?" Marisol asked, still tearing bread.

"For deliveries, probably," Halvard said. "Post goes to the bakery, they sort it there." He put his cap back on, took it off again. "There's a letter, though. Already come. Baker's boy brought it up." He produced it from inside his coat at last — a thin envelope, softened at the corners as if it had traveled a long way and been held often. "It's addressed to the household. To all of us. I didn't want to open it without—"

Marisol wiped her hands on her apron, slowly, taking the full time of it, and Petra understood she was making herself finish the bread first, because some things you did seated and some things you did standing and the difference mattered more than anyone ever admitted.

* * *

The letter sat in the middle of the kitchen table through breakfast, and through the washing-up, and through the hour when the children began arriving for lessons and had to be steered, all forty of them, around the room where the grown-ups kept glancing like people passing a church during a funeral.

By midmorning the whole household had gathered without anyone sending for anyone. This was how it worked now: Sera came because Sera always came when Marisol stood in one place too long; the twins appeared out of whatever second profession they ran out of the back of the cooper's shed; and Tomas came down from the loft above the stable where he drew his maps, one hand flat on the rail, moving carefully the way he had moved for six years on legs that had never quite forgiven him for the night on the quay.

He was grayer now. His left hand was a shape at the end of his sleeve that he had learned to use anyway — he pinned paper with it, held corners, turned pages with a dexterity that made strangers stare and made the family forget to notice. He had redrawn half the old republic from memory, plate by plate, and the border commission kept confiscating the plates and he kept drawing them again, and both sides treated this as a reasonable arrangement between professionals.

"Open it, then," Marisol said.

"It's addressed to the household," Tomas said. "You're the household."

"We're all the household. So open it, since your hands are free."

It was a joke they had been making for six years, the kind of joke that is also a way of saying *we are still us*, and Tomas took the knife and slit the envelope with the care of a man opening a document he had once forged professionally, and spread the single sheet on the table.

The handwriting hit Petra before the words did.

She knew that hand. She had watched it every evening for a year of her childhood, slantwise, across a signals desk or a supper table — the narrow letters, the crossbar on the T drawn like a level line, the small habit of leaving the loop of the L open as if the writer expected to come back and close it later and never did. She had copied that hand secretly for practice, thinking it was beautiful, thinking everything about that person was beautiful and ordinary and permanent.

Beatrix Okafor had been dead to them for six years, officially. Unofficially, they had never once said so aloud.

Tomas read it silently first, lips moving, and Petra watched his face do something complicated — the flinch, the disbelief, the thing underneath both that he swallowed before lifting his head.

"They're alive," he said. "They're alive. A work camp, north — Vereny province, hospital ward. They've been—" He stopped, pressed the heel of his good hand against his eyes for a moment, an uncharacteristic gesture, and started again. "They've been writing this letter for four years. Sending it in pieces with people who were released. This last part came with a nurse who emigrated. It took four years to get here and it was written the whole time as if we'd answer."

"Read it," Marisol said. "All of it. Out loud. We've earned the whole thing."

So Tomas read it, and Petra stood by the window with the morning light coming through green glass and falling on her hands, and listened to the voice of someone she had loved at eleven years old and mourned at twelve and never stopped setting a place for.

*My dear impossible family,* it began. *I am writing this assuming you are alive, because if you are not, then I have wasted a great deal of paper, and I refuse to begin my account of the last six years with an apology to furniture.*

There was laughter in the kitchen then, sudden and ragged, the kind that stands in for weeping when weeping would take too long. Even Marisol laughed, and dashed her wrist across her face while she did it, pretending it was the onions, though there were no onions cut.

The letter told it in Beatrix's order, which was never chronological but always exact. The camp at Vereny. The years of sorting other people's records, because a courier's hands were considered useful and a traitor's word was not. The fever, the hospital. And then, near the end, the part that made Tomas sit down slowly on the bench as though his knees had received separate instructions:

*I have news you will not believe, and I barely believe it, and I watched it happen. When the new state consolidated its archives, they found the Atlas. Our Atlas — the one Ilves filed, God rest his ambiguous soul, as authentic. And because the invading commission destroyed the original census in the burning of the capital — you will remember the smoke, even across the border, you must remember the smoke — ours was the only complete record that survived. Every name we entered. Every name we saved.*

*Historians cite it now. Schoolchildren copy from it. There is a plaque in the capital, of all absurdities, listing the "faithful registry clerks of Callowmere." The republic we betrayed turned out to be, on paper, mostly made of us. I find I cannot regret it. I have decided, after long review, that this is the correct finding.*

Petra looked up at the green light in the window and thought about smoke, because of course she remembered the smoke; she had been the one standing on the roof of the stable the day the eastern quarter burned, twelve years old and done with being frightened, learning what witnessing was. She thought about the cellar festival, the stolen wine in teacups, Marisol singing low so the patrol on the corner wouldn't hear. She thought about teaching children now, every morning, the same lesson she had been taught in that shop: that your name is the smallest thing you own and the only thing no one can carry off if you keep saying it.

At the bottom of the page, below the signature — the open-looped L, exactly as she remembered — there was a postscript in fresher ink:

*P.S. The camp is closing. Releases are being processed, slowly, badly, but processed. If this letter has found you alive, and I have gambled four years of ink that it has — then write to me at the address below. Or better: don't write. Come.*

Marisol was quiet for a long moment, the particular quiet Petra knew meant a decision being made at the depth where arguments couldn't reach. Then she pushed back from the table, took the letter, folded it along Beatrix's own creases with something dangerously close to tenderness, and tucked it into the bodice of her dress, over her heart, where important documents had gone in that family for as long as anyone could remember.

"Halvard," she said. "How many goats can we sell by week's end?"

Halvard blinked. "Enough. Why?"

"Because I'll need flour for a journey, and this kitchen will need somebody keeping it until I'm back in it." She was already untying her apron, already turning toward the stairs where the travel chest lived, a woman of sixty-three suddenly moving like the cook who had once smuggled nine people past a checkpoint in a grain wagon. "Pack the maps, Tomas. Petra — tell your children there's no school tomorrow."

Petra stood in the green light with her heart going like a bird against glass.

"And me?" she asked. "What do I tell them I'm going to do?"

Marisol paused in the doorway, and for a moment she was entirely the woman from the old shop, from the cellar, from the other country that no longer existed except in this kitchen and on one sheet of paper.

"Tell them," she said, "their teacher is going to bring the person who taught her home."

Outside, the north road lay empty in the morning sun, all the way to the border — and, somewhere far down it, a barge route, a river, and the beginning of the way back.
