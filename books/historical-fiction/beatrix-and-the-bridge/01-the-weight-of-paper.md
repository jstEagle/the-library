## 1. The Weight of Paper

The satchel arrived at dusk, carried by a boy too young to understand what he was holding.

Paloma Iversen watched him come up the wet cobblestones of the Courier's Quarter — the last quarter in Corven that still flew the blue and silver of Vellmar without irony — and knew before he spoke. There was a way important things traveled now: not with ceremony but with children and clerks and tired men who avoided eye contact, as though the republic could be smuggled out of history in pieces small enough to fit inside a coat.

"Courier Iversen?" The boy's breath fogged. He couldn't have been more than fourteen, with chapped hands and the collar of a uniform cut for someone broader. "From the Office of State Transmission. You're to sign."

He held out a ledger instead of a receipt book, and that was the second thing Paloma noticed. Receipts were for parcels. Ledgers were for oaths.

The rain had stopped an hour before, leaving the streets glossed and lamplit, every puddle holding its own small inverted city. Above them, on the rooftops of the Ministry quarter, workmen were taking down the flag from the Archive tower — not lowering it, which would have been surrender, but unhooking it panel by panel, folding it as one folds linen, because even now someone believed the annexation commission might be moved by courtesy.

Paloma signed. The boy looked at the signature, then at the satchel, then at Paloma again, and there passed between them the briefest exchange — not of words but of understanding. He knew what he'd delivered. Not the contents; no one knew the contents. But he knew the *kind* of thing it was, the way you know a coffin is a coffin regardless of the wood.

"He said you'd take it north," the boy said. "Even now."

"I take everything north," Paloma said. "That's the job."

"He said you'd take it even if the job stopped existing."

Paloma had no answer for that, so they took the satchel.

* * *

It weighed too much.

That was the first thought, standing there on the cobbles with the boy's footsteps fading behind them, and it was an absurd thought, because Paloma had carried this same courier's case for eleven years and knew its weight the way a sailor knows the pull of the sea. Fourteen hundred grams empty. Documents added their grams honestly, like anything else. A treaty weighed what a treaty weighed. Paloma had once hauled a full set of naval charts through the pass at Ostenfeld in February, and their shoulders remembered the arithmetic of it still.

This was different. This was not weight in the hands but weight in some other organ entirely — a downward drag behind the sternum, as if the satchel had been hung from a hook inside their chest rather than slung across their back.

They stood in the doorway of the transmission office for a long moment, letting the feeling speak, because a courier learned early to trust the body's testimony over the mind's assurances. The body had felt the change in a border town's mood three days before any dispatch confirmed it. The body had known the war was lost in autumn, when everyone official was still using the future tense.

Inside, the office was half-emptied already. Filing cabinets stood open like mouths. A clerk Paloma had known for a decade — Sereth, gray, meticulous, a man who alphabetized his griefs — was feeding papers into a cold stove he was no longer permitted to light, because the building's heat was being rationed along with everything else.

"You accepted," Sereth said, without looking up.

"I signed the ledger."

"That's accepting." He closed the stove's door gently, as if tucking something in. "Come into the back, then. There are things to say that shouldn't be said near windows."

The back room smelled of dust and lamp oil and the particular staleness of rooms where decisions have been made all day. On the table lay a map of Vellmar with pins in it — fewer pins than there would have been a year ago, and the ones remaining clustered in the north like the last birds on a wire. Corven sat in the south, ringed already in red pencil. The evacuation fleet was marked at Halden Harbor with a small inked ship, pathetic as a child's drawing.

"The codes," Paloma said. Not a question. Everyone in the service had known the final protocol existed the way you know your own funeral will someday happen: theoretically, and never on a Tuesday.

Sereth nodded slowly. "The cipher keys. Diplomatic channels, resistance contacts abroad, the fleet's authentication seals. Everything that lets a Republic of Vellmar exist anywhere other than in memory." He drew a folded document from his coat — orders, written in the tight hand of Director Ambrose herself. "You deliver these to Fleet Command at Halden. Personally. No relays. No dead drops. No copies. You carry the seal—" he slid a heavy brass cylinder across the table, cold as a river stone "—and the codes travel sealed under it, verified only at the far end."

"Why me?"

"Because you're the best we have left, and because everyone better is dead or has already decided to be someone else's citizen." Sereth said it without bitterness, which somehow made it worse. "And because you have family in the north. Family means motive, and motive means you'll actually go."

The word *family* landed strangely, the way a key lands in a lock you'd forgotten existed. Paloma kept their face still. Eleven years of carrying other people's secrets taught a person to keep their face still.

"My family arrangements are twenty years old," they said.

"All our arrangements are old," Sereth said. "The country itself is only ninety-one. Old is not the same as finished." He pushed the orders across the table. "Three hundred kilometers to Halden. Roads are unreliable past Drenmark. The annexation commission has checkpoints on everything moving north, and they know — we believe they know — that a final transmission is planned. They want those codes, Paloma. They've wanted them since the armistice talks began. If the codes reach the fleet, Vellmar survives somewhere. A government in exile, ships on water, signatures that still mean something. If the codes don't—"

He didn't finish. He didn't need to. If the codes didn't, then the fleet was just boats full of people, and the people were just refugees, and the refugees had no country except the ones kind enough to pretend otherwise.

"And if I'm stopped?" Paloma asked. "If it can't be helped?"

Sereth met their eyes for the first time. His were pale, almost colorless, the eyes of a man who had spent forty years reading documents in bad light.

"Then you destroy them," he said. "Above all else, above delivery itself — they must not fall into the commission's hands intact. Swallow that instruction whole. It's the only part of your orders that matters more than your life."

* * *

Paloma walked home through the dark with the satchel against their spine, and the city taught them its new lessons on the way.

Corven had always been a capital that wore its politics openly — banners, murals, the great painted façades of the Guildhalls with their heroes of the founding. Now it wore its defeat the same way. Annexation flags hung from the customs house, gray crossed with black, the colors of the Meridian Compact, crisp and new and smelling of dye. Someone had thrown red paint on one, and someone else had scrubbed at the paint until only a rust-colored ghost remained, which was somehow more eloquent than either act alone.

In the square by the old fountain, a queue had formed outside the Commission of Population Transfer — hundreds of people in the cold, holding folders of documents, waiting to register their names in the new order. An old woman near the front was arguing with a clerk in a low, exhausted voice. "I was born here," she kept saying. "I was born here. Look at my papers. My mother was born here." And the clerk, who was perhaps nineteen, kept repeating, not unkindly, "That's what we're verifying, madam. That's exactly what the line is for."

Paloma crossed the square rather than pass the queue. It wasn't shame, exactly. It was the instinct of a fox crossing open ground — the knowledge that couriers were becoming interesting, and interesting was a word that got people sorted into lines.

At home — a third-floor room above a shuttered print shop, one window, one bed, one strongbox bolted to the floor joists by a previous tenant who had clearly expected worse times and been right — Paloma set the satchel on the table and lit the lamp and looked at it for a long time.

It really did look ordinary. That was the craft of it. Gray canvas, worn straps, the plain brass fittings of ten thousand courier cases across the republic. Anyone who searched it would find laundry and ration chits and a woman's wool gloves two sizes too big, because disguise worked best when it was mostly true. The sealed packet itself sat in the false bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, a rectangle the size of a hymnal.

Fourteen hundred grams empty. Perhaps eighteen hundred now.

Paloma lifted it. Set it down. Lifted it again, testing, the way one tests a tooth that aches.

There it was again. That wrongness. Not in the hand — the hand reported nothing unusual — but underneath, in the deep place where the body keeps its own ledgers. As if the case contained not paper but ballast. As if it had been packed with river stones, with iron, with the entire ninety-one years of the republic pressed and dried flat.

*You know why,* they told themselves. *Because it isn't documents. It's continuity. It's the difference between a nation and a memory of one.*

But that wasn't quite it either, and lying in the dark long after the lamp was out, Paloma found the truer shape of the dread and turned it over carefully, the way you turn over a stone to see what lives beneath it.

Every run they had ever made had carried words meant to be read. Orders, reports, appeals, treaties — language aimed at eyes, at minds, at outcomes. Even the failed runs, even the ones where the message died in a burned village or a frozen ditch, had been *meant* for someone. This was the first time in eleven years that Paloma carried something whose entire value lay in it never being opened. Whose purpose was to remain forever sealed — or, failing that, to cease to exist. A message addressed to no reader. A cargo whose destination might be a desk in Halden or the inside of a fire.

Carrying words was a trade. Carrying silence was something older, and heavier, and Paloma suspected — lying awake while Corven's church bells rang the hour, all nine of them, because even the bells hadn't been told to stop — that they were not carrying it for the republic at all.

They were carrying it for whatever came after. For the idea that somewhere, someday, someone would need to prove that Vellmar had existed, had negotiated, had promised and been promised — and that this hymnal-sized rectangle of oilcloth was the proof, the deed, the last original document of a country about to become a footnote.

Sleep came late and thin.

* * *

Morning brought frost and a decision already made, because Paloma had learned years ago that decisions made at night were the honest ones; daylight only gave people chances to talk themselves out of survival.

They rose, dressed in traveling wool, checked the satchel's straps twice, and took down from beside the strongbox a second case — smaller, scarred, its leather gone dark and supple with age. Their father's courier case. Anders's and theirs, once, before everything; the one object each sibling had taken from the house on the night the family ended, separately, without coordinating, and neither had ever mentioned to the other since.

Twenty years Paloma had carried it. Never used it for work — superstition, or sentiment, or both — but never discarded it either. Inside it lived three things: a spare seal-blank, a folding knife, and a letter their father had written and never sent, which Paloma had never read, because reading it would have meant deciding what to do about it, and there had been no version of that decision that didn't break something further.

They almost left it. They stood with it in their hands for nearly a minute, weighing — and there it was again, that strange interior arithmetic, the sense that objects could gain mass from meaning alone.

Then they packed it. Some weights you choose.

Outside, Corven lay white-rimmed under a sky the color of dishwater. The north road began beyond the Grain Gate, and Paloma joined the thin morning traffic toward it — carts, a doctor's gig, a family walking with bundles tied in blankets, all of them moving north, because north was the harbor, and the harbor was the story now, and everyone in the republic was trying to reach the last page before someone tore it out.

At the Grain Gate, a compact officer in gray-and-black checked papers with mechanical boredom, waving most through. When Paloma's turn came, the officer glanced at the courier's warrant, glanced up, and something flickered — not recognition, but category. *Courier. Interesting.* A mark made invisibly, filed invisibly, in whatever ledger was being kept behind those flat eyes.

"Purpose of travel?"

"Courier run. Routine dispatches to the harbor administration."

The officer held the warrant a moment longer than necessary. Then handed it back.

"Safe roads," they said, in the toneless voice people had begun using for sentences that meant nothing, and waved Paloma through.

Three hundred kilometers. Checkpoints, frozen passes, a country dissolving behind them mile by mile. Paloma settled the satchel's strap across their chest and felt it settle against the sternum, that impossible weight finding its familiar wrong place, and walked out through the gate arch — under the last Vellmar banner on the last wall it would hang from, blue and silver, stiff with frost, cracking faintly in the wind like something clearing its throat to speak.

Behind them, the clerk at the gate office picked up a telephone and dialed a number that had been circulated that week on a list of numbers worth dialing.

Ahead, the north road ran white and empty toward Drenmark, toward the mountains, toward the Kessel Bridge and the storm country beyond it — and toward everything, though Paloma could not yet have known it, that the weight in the satchel was going to cost.
