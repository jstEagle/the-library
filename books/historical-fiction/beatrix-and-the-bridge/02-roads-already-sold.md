## 2. Roads Already Sold

The first flag Paloma saw flying where a republic's colors should have been was in the village of Haren-Moll, and it was not raised — it had simply appeared overnight, the way frost appears, the way rot appears, without anyone admitting to having invited it. Paloma stopped at the well at the center of the square to refill their flask and watched the cloth turn on its pole in the wet wind: gray field, black bar, the new emblem of annexation that everyone in Vellmar had spent two years pretending would remain a rumor. An old man was drawing water beside them, bucket scraping the stone lip, and he did not look up at the flag at all. That was the thing Paloma noticed. Not the flag itself but the practiced blindness of everyone in the square, the way a family stops looking at a stain on the ceiling because naming it would mean agreeing on how it got there.

"Long road north?" the old man asked, in the tone of a man who already knew the answer and wanted to know something else instead.

"Kessel," Paloma said.

"Mm." He hauled the bucket up, set it down with a thud. "Roads are open. Roads are always open. That's what they keep telling us." He filled his own flask, then, after a pause that cost him something visible, filled Paloma's without being asked. "Kindness is getting expensive," he said. "Better take it while it's cheap."

Paloma thanked him and gave him nothing in return, because they had nothing yet to give, and understood as they walked out of the square past the blind-eyed houses that this was the currency of the collapsing country now — small mercies, extended on credit, each one a debt someone would come to collect.

* * *

The satchel had not grown lighter since Haren-Moll. Paloma had weighed it once, out of professional discipline, on the kitchen scale of the safehouse in Delve before departing: sealed codes for the evacuation fleet, four hundred grams of paper and wax and ribbon. Four hundred grams. It felt like carrying a sleeping animal, something warm and alive that would die if dropped. Other couriers had told Paloma about this sensation over the years, and Paloma had always privately disbelieved them — paper is paper, wax is wax, weight is weight — but every mile north the satchel grew heavier against the hip, and by the third day Paloma had stopped arguing with the feeling and started listening to it instead. The bag knew something Paloma didn't. The bag knew what it carried. The bag knew that the Republic of Vellmar had been reduced, in its final season, to whatever could be sewn into oiled canvas and run across a frozen country before the doors closed.

Paloma walked the old courier roads, the ones that ran from market town to market town rather than along the military highways, and counted the flags. One in Haren-Moll. Two in Brast, one on the granary and one, obscenely, on the chapel. Four in Osterhagen, where the annexation commission had already installed a civil office in the former post house and posted its tariffs in both scripts on the door. By the fifth day Paloma stopped counting, the way the villagers had stopped looking, and understood that this too was part of the erosion: first you lose the territory, then you lose the ability to measure what's lost.

In Osterhagen Paloma traded their first favor. It was small and it was ugly and it took eleven minutes. A woman named Grete Voss ran the coach yard behind the Broken Wheel inn, and her coaches still went north twice a week because the annexation authorities had not yet decided whether to stop them or tax them, and until that decision arrived she moved goods and people and made money from the confusion. She looked at Paloma's courier's papers — the ordinary ones, the decoy set that named them an inspector of postal routes — and shook her head.

"Inspector rides free when there's an inspectorate left to inspect," she said. "You want passage to the Kessel fork, I want something back."

"Name it."

"There's a clerk at the commission office who owes me for a shipment of leather he confiscated in autumn. He says it burned. I say otherwise. You're a courier; couriers know how documents move. Find out if his confiscation report ever got filed, and if it didn't, remind him gently that things unfiled can be found." She smiled without warmth. "I'm not asking you to break anything. I'm asking you to make a man remember that he can be remembered."

So Paloma spent eleven minutes in the commission office, under the gray-and-black flag, being pleasant to a young clerk with damp hands, mentioning in passing that they were a route inspector reviewing autumn paperwork, watching the color leave his face and return, and leaving with the understanding — conveyed entirely in silences and a single bead of sweat — that Grete Voss's leather had indeed never been filed and never would be. In exchange she put Paloma in the boot-box seat of a grain wagon, wedged between sacks, and the wagon rolled north through a landscape of sold fields.

That was the phrase that came to Paloma somewhere along that stretch, dozing against the burlap with the satchel strapped across the chest like a child: *roads already sold*. Not conquered, not closed, not defended. Sold. Every farmstead they passed had a commission stamp nailed to its gatepost — a lead seal the size of a coin, punched through with the black bar — and the stamps were fresh enough to gleam. Someone had gone door to door through the whole valley with a bag of lead seals and a ledger, and the people inside the farms had opened their doors, and here was the terrible thing Paloma kept circling and could not stop circling: most of those doors had opened without a shot. The republic had not fallen like a wall. It had gone out of business, quietly, item by item, and its customers had lined up politely at the closing sale.

* * *

At Brast there was no wagon to be had and Paloma walked again, two days along the river road, and paid for a bed at a ferryman's house with the last of the small silver they carried, which meant the arithmetic of the journey turned sharply downward from that point onward. Couriers of the republic were issued expense funds, but Paloma's final assignment had come through in such haste that the treasury disbursed only half the standard rate — the treasury, in its last weeks, was itself running low on everything except seals — and so the favors began to matter more than the money, and Paloma learned quickly what favors cost in a dying country.

They were not bribes, exactly. Bribes implied a functioning system beneath the corruption, rules worth bending. What Paloma engaged in on the road north was closer to barter conducted between drowning people: you gave what you had — information, access, a letter carried, a name spoken in the right ear — and received what you needed, and neither party pretended the exchange meant anything more than survival. A miller wanted to know if his son's name had cleared the naval evacuation lists; Paloma memorized it and promised to check at the docks, knowing the promise was worth little and the miller knowing it too and accepting anyway, because in a country ending, the shape of help mattered more than its substance. A widow who kept the last tea room on the Kessel road fed Paloma for two days in exchange for a letter carried south to her sister, hand-delivered, no commission post involved — an act technically illegal now, since all private correspondence above personal notes was supposed to pass through commission review, and Paloma carried the letter sewn into their coat lining next to the codes, the treasonous and the sacred riding together against the heart, indistinguishable to any search.

The tea room widow, whose name was Ilse, cried when Paloma left, and pressed a parcel of boiled sweets into their hands "for the road, for whoever's waiting," and refused payment, and Paloma walked out onto the frosted road understanding that Ilse's kindness was also a price — paid not to Paloma but against some future reckoning Ilse had already accepted, a settling of accounts with the person she intended to go on being after the country stopped existing. Everyone on the road north was paying themselves, Paloma thought. Every small decency was a deposit into an account only the depositor could see.

And everyone else was collecting. At the ferry crossing, a commission surveyor was measuring the bridge timbers and writing figures in a ledger, and Paloma understood without asking that the figures were not about engineering. They were inventory. The bridges, the mills, the chapels, the children's schools — everything was being entered into a great book somewhere in the annexation capital, and once entered, a thing ceased to belong to anyone. Even the fog over the river seemed catalogued, assigned, claimed.

* * *

The checkpoint came on the eighth day, at a place called Wendt's Cut where the road narrowed between a rock face and the frozen canal, and Paloma saw it from far enough away to consider the alternatives — a goat track over the ridge, a long detour through marsh — and to reject them both, because the satchel was heavy and the marsh thawed at midday and the ridge track had killed two couriers Paloma had known, and because, Paloma admitted, standing in the middle of the empty road arguing with themselves, there was a part of them that wanted to walk straight through. Wanted to look the new authority in the face and pass. Wanted the republic's seal to work one more time, even as a lie.

There were three soldiers at the barrier, but only one of them mattered: a lieutenant of the annexation forces, young, precise, with the particular cleanliness of a man who had arrived after the fighting — if there had been any fighting — and inherited an occupation that consisted mostly of paperwork. His name tag read BRANDT. The two regulars flanking him were older, local conscripts, and they stood with the loose shame of men guarding a road through their own homeland.

"Papers."

Paloma produced the decoy set. Route inspector, postal directorate. Brandt read them thoroughly, front and back, and then looked at Paloma with the frank curiosity of a man with all the power and none of the experience.

"You're a long way from any route, Inspector. Postal directorate withdrew south in December."

"The directorate withdrew," Paloma said. "The routes didn't. Somebody has to account for what's still moving on them." It was the kind of sentence that worked because it was true, and Paloma watched it land.

"And your cargo?"

"Official correspondence. Sealed."

Brandt held out his hand. It was the moment Paloma had rehearsed for eight days, and the rehearsal evaporated, and what remained was the flat professional instinct of twenty years: give a little, withhold the rest, let the withholding itself be the proof. Paloma drew out the courier's seal — not the satchel, not the codes, but the brass instrument of office, the matrix cut with the republic's device, worn smooth where their father's thumb had worn it before theirs — and held it up in the gray light.

"I can't open sealed diplomatic material on the road, Lieutenant. But I can show you this. And I can tell you that anything I carry is covered by the armistice annex on postal continuity, section four, which your commission has chosen to honor so far. I'd hate to be the correspondence that ended the honoring."

It was a gamble dressed as courtesy. Section four existed; whether it covered this, whether Brandt knew, whether anyone in the annexation bureaucracy still answered questions about it — all unknown. Brandt studied the seal. He did not touch it at first, and then he did, taking it in both hands with a strange gentleness, turning it over, reading the reversed letters of the device, and his face did something complicated — hunger and reverence and a flicker of something like grief, all crossing quickly, badly hidden.

"It's heavier than I imagined," he said.

"They usually are."

"How long have you carried it?"

"Twenty years." True, and it surprised Paloma to hear it said aloud, and truer than it sounded, because the seal had changed hands twice in that time but had never really left.

Brandt handed it back. Then he stepped aside and lifted the barrier himself, waving his conscripts back with a small motion, and as Paloma passed he spoke quietly, not quite meeting their eyes.

"For the look at the seal. That's my price. I wanted to see one before they're all melted down." He paused. "There'll be a second checkpoint at the Kessel approach. Not mine. They don't honor section four. Whatever you're carrying — carry it somewhere they can't weigh it."

Paloma walked through the barrier and did not look back, because looking back would have required deciding what to feel about a man who had just traded the integrity of a checkpoint for thirty seconds holding a piece of brass, and Paloma was not ready to decide. The kindness had a price, as the old man at the well had said. So did the cruelty. Everything on these roads was priced, everything was moving, everything was for sale including the conscience of a young lieutenant who missed his own country's honor enough to buy a glimpse of someone else's.

* * *

That night Paloma slept in a hay barn two miles past the Cut and woke once, certain they had heard the seal speaking. Just the wind in the boards. Just the satchel strap creaking as they shifted. Four hundred grams of paper. Paloma lay in the dark doing the arithmetic they had avoided for eight days: the funds were gone, the favors were spent or nearly — Grete Voss paid, the miller promised, Ilse's sweets still in the pocket, Brandt's curiosity discharged — and ahead lay the Kessel Bridge, the fleet, the last door of the republic, and at least one more checkpoint that would not be moved by brass or law. The country's kindness was a purse with a hole in it, and Paloma had been reaching in all week and finding less each time, and now the reaching hand came up nearly empty against the lining.

What was left to trade? Twenty years of silence. A name. A face Paloma had spent two decades refusing to remember and which rose now, unbidden, in the dark of the barn — Lysa's face, the set of the jaw, the way the family resemblance pooled in certain cheeks and chins — though why it should rise *now*, on this road, Paloma couldn't have said. Grief does its own bookkeeping. Somewhere in the north a family Paloma had erased was presumably scattered or dead or absorbed into the new order, and the debt there could never be discharged in favors.

Sleep came late and thin. In the morning the frost on the barn boards had the gray cast of the new flags, and Paloma broke the ice in the trough, ate the last of Ilse's sweets — sweetness bought with kindness, consumed without ceremony — and walked the final stretch of road toward the river. By afternoon the Kessel Bridge was visible in the distance, a long dark stroke across the white valley, and below it, at the foot of the northern approach, sat a building with lit windows and smoke rising from the chimney: the last inn on Vellmar soil, the Bridge Inn, where according to the fleet's instructions Paloma was to wait for the escort that would complete the run.

The escort, when Paloma reached the inn, had been waiting three days and given up and gone. That discovery took ten minutes. What took longer, what stopped Paloma dead in the doorway of the inn's common room with the satchel suddenly weighing what it had always weighed and more, was the small figure curled asleep against the cold stone of the doorstep — a child, alone, in a coat too thin for the season, with a note pinned to the lapel in careful block letters.

Paloma knelt. Turned the note toward the light.

*Forget the face.*

And then the child stirred, and the firelight from the windows fell across her cheek, and Paloma forgot nothing, because the face looking up out of twenty drowned years was Lysa's — Lysa's jaw, Lysa's chin, Lysa's eyes opening slow and unfrightened on a stranger kneeling in the snow.

The republic's last codes were still strapped against Paloma's heart. They had never felt so light.
