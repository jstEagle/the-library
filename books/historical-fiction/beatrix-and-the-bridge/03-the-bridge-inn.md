## 3. The Bridge Inn

The storm found Paloma an hour before the inn did.

It came down off the Kessel highlands the way winter always came to Vellmar's northern edge — not arriving so much as descending, a gray wall of sleet and wind that erased the road, the fences, the distant smoke of chimneys, until there was nothing in the world but the next step and the one after it. Paloma leaned into it with their shoulder, the way they'd learned to lean into doors that stuck and seasons that refused to end. The satchel rode against their chest under the oilcloth, strapped tight, and even through the layers they could feel it there: that impossible weight, heavier than paper had any right to be.

*This is the last run*, they told themselves for the hundredth time since Halden. *Deliver it, sign the manifest, and let someone else be the republic after this.*

But the republic was thinning like the light. Every village Paloma had passed through since dawn flew the annexation banner now — the black bar over the old blue, hung crooked from balconies where the Vellmar colors hadn't quite been taken down, as if the houses themselves couldn't decide which country they belonged to. The people were the same way. They gave directions with their chins and took payment with eyes that didn't meet yours. Everyone was practicing, it seemed, for the country that was coming: the shrug, the lowered voice, the careful neutrality of a face that had already surrendered everything it intended to keep.

Paloma walked through the storm thinking about none of that, because thinking about it was a luxury, and instead thought about the bridge. The Kessel Bridge was the last crossing into neutral territory before the coast road forked toward the evacuation fleet at Brannvik Harbor. Three days' walk past it, if the weather allowed. Four if it didn't. Five if the commission had already moved its checkpoints up to the span itself, which by every report they would, because the annexation officers wanted exactly what Paloma carried, and they knew it had to come this way.

The codes. Twelve pages of cipher keys and routing tables, sealed in lead-and-wax, that could still — the Ministry claimed — coordinate the fleet's dispersal, keep three thousand refugees from sailing blind into waters that had been sold out from under them. Or so the seal said. Paloma had never opened it. That was the whole discipline of the craft: you carried what you carried, and you never once let yourself imagine what was inside, because imagination was how couriers got bought, and bought couriers got people killed.

The satchel bumped against their sternum with each step, patient as a second heart.

*Almost*, Paloma told it. *Almost.*

Then the storm split for half a breath — a gust tearing a hole in the gray — and Paloma saw the inn.

It sat alone at the base of the bridge approach, a long low building of dark timber with a stone chimney bleeding smoke sideways into the wind. Behind it, vast and half-glimpsed, the Kessel Bridge rose out of the weather like the rib cage of some drowned leviathan: stone arches marching across the gorge, iron railings furred with ice, lamplights strung along its length swinging in the gale like lanterns held by drowning men. Even ruined-looking, even half-swallowed by the storm, it was beautiful. Everything in Vellmar was beautiful lately, and everything in Vellmar was about to belong to someone else.

A hand-painted board creaked above the door. *The Bridge Inn*, and beneath it, smaller, in older lettering: *Rooms. Stables. No Credit.* The word *Stables* had been struck through with black paint sometime in the last year; armies requisitioned stables first, then horses, then trust.

Paloma shouldered the door open and stepped into warmth so sudden and total it felt like falling into water.

* * *

The common room was low-ceilinged and amber-lit, smelling of woodsmoke, wet wool, and the particular sourness of beer poured for customers who weren't coming. A fire cracked in a hearth wide enough to stand in. Half a dozen travelers were scattered among the tables — a wool merchant, two teamsters, a woman in commission gray who sat apart from everyone and drank nothing — and behind the bar stood a broad, capable woman in her fifties with flour on her forearms and the unbothered expression of someone who had outlasted four governments and expected to outlast a fifth.

"Bridge is closed," she said, before Paloma had finished shaking off the rain. "Wind's over the limit. Nobody crosses till it drops."

"Then I'll need a room."

"You'll need a room and everyone else in this room will need a room, and I have four." She looked Paloma over with the frank arithmetic of an innkeeper: boots, coat, the telltale bulge at the chest where a courier's satchel rode. Her gaze paused there, one beat too long, then moved on. "Courier rates are double. Nothing personal. It's just that when the soldiers come asking who stayed here, I'd rather have been paid for the trouble."

"That's honest, at least."

"Honesty's the only thing left nobody's annexed." She slid a key across the bar. "Marta. Top of the stairs, end of the hall. Supper's whatever's in the pot, and you'll eat it before you're told what it is, because knowing makes people difficult."

Paloma paid — too much, in coin that might be worthless within the month — and carried their supper to a table near the fire. The stew was venison or mutton or secrets; it was hot, and that was enough. Around them the travelers talked in the hushed, fragmentary way people talked now, sentences that kept stopping at the border of dangerous subjects: *the fleet sails when — did you hear about — they took the whole family at Ostmere, just for the — *and then silence, and the fire filling in.

The woman in commission gray read a newspaper dated nine days old.

Paloma ate slowly and let the heat work into their bones, and it was only when they rose to take their bag upstairs that they saw the door again — the front door, standing ajar where the wind worried at it, rocking in its frame like something trying to get in.

And on the doorstep, curled against the cold in the lee of the jamb, was a child.

* * *

For a moment Paloma thought the storm had brought them a hallucination, some trick of light and exhaustion. But no. A girl, six years old perhaps, maybe seven — small for either — asleep on the stone step with her knees drawn to her chest, her head tipped against the doorframe. She wore a red coat, good quality once, now soaked through at the shoulders. Her hair had come loose from its braiding and lay across her cheek in wet ropes. One small fist was closed around the collar of her coat as though she had fallen asleep holding herself together.

No one else in the room had noticed, or had chosen not to. The teamsters argued softly over cards. The merchant counted coins. The commission woman turned a page.

Paloma set down their plate and went to the door.

The cold hit like a slap. Up close the girl was unmistakably real, unmistakably asleep, and unmistakably alone — no boots worth the name, no scarf, no adult anywhere in the yard beyond, which the lightning briefly showed as a churned white waste with the bridge looming beyond it, impossibly far and impossibly close.

"Hey." Paloma knelt, keeping their voice low. "Hey. Wake up. You can't—"

Then they saw the note.

It was pinned to the child's coat at the breast, a square of cheap paper fixed with a rusted tack, and it had been written fast, in a hand that pressed hard enough to tear:

**FORGET THE FACE.**

Nothing else. No name, no instruction, no reward offered. Just those three words in a furious scrawl, and beneath them — Paloma had to lift the paper toward the lamp inside to see it — a small stamped mark, ink gone blurry with damp. Not an official seal. Something cruder. A bird with a broken wing, stamped in violet.

Paloma had spent twenty years making sure they would never see that mark again, and here it was, pinned to a sleeping child in the last inn in Vellmar.

Their hands were suddenly very steady, the way hands go steady before a fall.

They unpinned the note, folded it into their palm, and gathered the girl up. She weighed almost nothing. She was burning hot with fever under the wet coat, and she smelled of woodsmoke and river water and something faintly herbal, like she'd been slept in a room full of dried flowers. Her head lolled against Paloma's shoulder. Her lips moved around words that didn't come.

Paloma stood in the doorway with the storm screaming past behind them and the warm room at their back, and understood, in the way one understands a thing all at once and much too late, that the run had just changed shape entirely.

Because the face beneath the tangled hair — fever-flushed, rain-washed, slack with sleep — was a face Paloma knew.

Not knew. Had *erased*. Had spent two decades carefully, deliberately forgetting, the way you burn a letter or sink a body: thoroughly, ritually, with witnesses to your grief.

It was Lysa's face. Aunt Lysa, their mother's younger sister, who used to sing in the kitchen with flour on her hands; who had stood in the doorway the night Paloma fled and had not called after them; whose mouth this child had, and chin, and that same slight fold at the corner of the left eye that Lysa had gotten from their grandmother and passed down like contraband through the generations.

*Forget the face*, the note said.

As if anyone alive could look at that face and do anything but remember.

* * *

Marta found them ten minutes later in the corner booth farthest from the window, where Paloma had installed the girl wrapped in their own dry cloak, fed her broth spoonful by spoonful from a height calculated to avoid scalding, and was now watching her sleep with an expression they couldn't have named under oath.

The innkeeper set down two cups of something dark and medicinal-smelling, pulled up a stool, and studied the child for a long moment.

"Dropped off during the change of watch, I'd guess," Marta said quietly. "Someone left her while my stable boy was seeing to the last coach. Before the storm shut everything down." She turned her cup without drinking. "You know her?"

"No."

"You're lying, courier, and badly." There was no accusation in it. It was simply an observation, filed away. "She's got somebody's features, that's certain. Somebody local?"

"South," Paloma said. "Years ago."

"Mm." Marta drank at last. "Well. Here's the arithmetic, since you're a numbers person. Annexation commission has an office in Brannvik now, and a sub-office at the tollhouse this side of the bridge. They post lists. They pay finders' fees for — " she tilted her head at the girl — "persons of interest. Unregistered minors traveling without papers fall under the resettlement statutes. If I report her, which is the law now, she goes to a processing center, gets assigned a new name and a number, and vanishes into the part of the ledger nobody audits. If I *don't* report her, and they find out I didn't, my license dies, my name goes on the blacklist, and this inn gets given to somebody more cooperative." She said it flatly, the way one reads a weather report. "So. In the morning, I do the sensible thing. You understand. We've all got to be sensible now. It's practically patriotic."

Paloma looked up from the sleeping child. Something in their own face must have shown, because Marta stopped, cup halfway to her mouth, and waited.

"I'll take her," Paloma heard themselves say — heard it as though spoken by a stranger, in a voice that sounded remarkably like their father's. "Whatever happens in the morning, she was in my custody when I found her. That's courier law. Found property travels under the carrier's seal until delivered. It's in the old statutes."

"The old statutes," Marta repeated, "are dead."

"They haven't been repealed," Paloma said. "Nobody's bothered."

For a moment the innkeeper's professional blankness flickered, and beneath it Paloma glimpsed something tired and amused and very old. "Twenty-two years behind this bar," Marta said. "Kaisers, councils, commissars. You know what I've learned? The regimes all write new laws because the old ones have people like you in them." She stood, collected the cups, and paused. "There's blankets in your room. Feed her when she wakes. And courier—" she glanced once more at the small flushed face in the borrowed cloak, "—whatever it is you're not telling me about whose face that is, I'd decide fast whether you're going to remember it. Storms pass. Lists don't."

She went back to the bar. The fire settled. Outside, the wind threw sleet against the shutters in long exhausted sheets, and somewhere above the gorge the great bridge groaned like a ship at anchor.

Paloma sat in the amber light with a sleeping stranger wearing their aunt's face, and reached into their coat, and took out the note, and read it again by the fire's glow.

*Forget the face.*

Twenty years ago, on the worst night of their life, Paloma had walked out of a farmhouse south of here carrying one satchel and nothing else, and had made themselves a promise at the end of the lane: *none of them will ever find me, and I will never look back.* Forgetting had seemed like mercy then — theirs to give, freely, to themselves. Now the order came from outside, pinned to a child's chest with a rusted tack, and it fit like a shackle.

The girl stirred. Her eyes opened — Lysa's eyes, gray-green, fever-bright — and found Paloma's face, and studied it with the grave, unhurried attention of someone deciding something important.

"You've got the seal," she said.

Her voice was small and rough with sleep, and the words came out slurred, dreamlike, half-lost — but Paloma heard them. All of them.

"What did you say?"

But the girl's eyes had already closed again, her breath evening out, one small fist still knotted in the cloak at her throat — and Paloma sat frozen, the fire cracking, the storm wailing, the satchel heavy as a heartbeat against their ribs, understanding that the door they had locked twenty years ago had just opened from the other side.
