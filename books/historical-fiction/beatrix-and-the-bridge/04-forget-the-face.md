## 4. Forget the Face

The storm arrived before dawn and did not leave. It came off the northern fens the way grief comes to a house — first as a sound at the shutters, then as a presence in every room, until the whole inn groaned under it and the fire in the common room's hearth seemed a small act of defiance against the sky.

Paloma Iversen sat near that fire with their hands wrapped around a cup of barley tea gone cold an hour ago, and watched the child sleep.

She had not moved from the bench by the window since Marta had carried her in from the doorstep. She lay curled beneath a horse blanket with her boots still on, her small fists tucked under her chin, her breath coming slow and even, and Paloma could not stop looking at her face.

It was Lysa's face.

Not exactly — no face survives twenty years intact, and this was a child's face, unlined, unfinished, the nose still soft at the bridge of it, the mouth wider than her mother's had been. But the architecture underneath was unmistakable. The pale lashes lying nearly white against her cheeks. The way her brow furrowed even in sleep, as though she were working at some problem too large for her. Paloma had last seen that brow furrowed like that on a woman standing in a doorway holding a lamp, saying nothing, while Paloma walked out of the family house for the last time and did not look back until the road bent.

The note was on the table where Marta had put it, pinned corner down by the salt cellar as if it were something dangerous. It was written in a careful, upright hand, the kind taught in clerical schools or learned by people who wanted very much not to be identified: *To whoever finds her — forget the face. Do not ask her name. There is money enough in the lining of her coat for one fare south. Take it and let her be someone else's.*

Below that, folded twice more, a bank draft drawn on a Vellmaran merchant house that had, Paloma happened to know, been annexed in the autumn. Whoever wrote the note had not known how fast the world was closing. Or had known and had nothing else to give.

*Forget the face.*

Paloma turned the cup in their hands. Outside, the wind found a gap somewhere in the eaves and made a thin, rising whistle of it, like a kettle far away in another house.

They had been forgetting for twenty years. They were, if anyone was, qualified for this.

The satchel sat against the wall by their feet, its strap wound around their ankle out of habit rather than necessity — there was no one in this room to steal from them but the storm. The codes inside it rode against the floorboards, that familiar weight that had grown heavier with every mile north, heavier than paper had any right to be, as though the republic itself had been pressed into the seals and signatures and dried there. Paloma had stopped opening the satchel except to check the seals. There was nothing inside it they were permitted to know.

But the child knew nothing either, and she was permitted nothing, and she slept anyway. There was a lesson in that, probably. Paloma declined to learn it.

The stairs creaked and Marta came down, wiping her hands on an apron that had once been white in some previous decade and some previous country. She was a broad woman with the particular economy of movement that innkeepers develop, wasting nothing, including opinions, until an opinion became necessary.

"She's still asleep," Marta said, though she could see it as well as Paloma could. It was an opening move. Paloma recognized the shape of what was coming and let it come.

"Yes."

"You looked at her coat lining?"

"I read the note."

"Then you know what she is." Marta lowered herself onto the bench across the table and kept her voice at the low register innkeepers keep for dangerous subjects. "Nobody pins a note like that to a child they mean to see again. Somebody's cutting ties. Somebody with money, and money like that means somebody connected, and connections like that—" she tipped her head toward the window, toward the north, where the annexation flags would be flying over Kessel Bridge if the storm allowed anyone to see them— "mean the commission will want to talk to her. Sooner or later they always want to talk."

"Talk," Paloma said. "Is that what they do now?"

Marta's mouth flattened. She had served tea to customs men from three governments in her time; her face could hold a great deal without committing to any of it. "You know what I mean."

"I know what you mean."

"The roads are full of children this winter. Half of them are nobody's, and the other half are somebody's, and it's the somebody's ones that bring trouble through my door." She leaned forward, and for a moment the innkeeper fell away and there was only a tired woman in her fifties who had buried a husband under one flag and would likely bury her business under another. "Courier. You've been kind enough under that hood, and you paid for the room without haggling, which marks you as either honest or desperate, and I've no quarrel with either. But hear me. When the roads clear, the first patrol over that bridge will stop here whether I like it or not. They'll ask who slept under my roof. And if I have to tell them there was a girl — a girl with no papers, a girl somebody went to trouble to disown — then everything I've built in thirty years goes into their ledger. My name, my license, my sons' names. You understand? A blacklisted name up here isn't a word anymore. It's a door that doesn't open again."

"I understand," Paloma said quietly.

"Then hand her over when they come. Tell them you found her on the road, tell them anything, but don't stand between a child and the commission with nothing in your hands but good intentions. I've seen what stands between this winter and people with good intentions." She spread her hands. "Nothing. That's what. Nothing stands there. Good intentions freeze same as everything else."

Paloma looked at the child again. In sleep she had shifted, and one fist had escaped the blanket and lay open on the bench, palm up, fingers slightly curled, the way children sleep when they have been carried a long way by someone who finally set them down.

"No," Paloma said.

Marta waited. The wind filled the silence for her.

"They won't hurt her," Marta tried. "Children aren't the ones they — the commission takes custody. There are forms. There's a process. She'd be fed. Schooled, even, eventually, in whatever schools they—"

"Marta." Paloma heard their own voice come out flat and final, a courier's voice, the voice for *this seal does not open.* "No. I'm not handing her to anyone. I don't care about the forms."

"Why?" And it was an honest question, asked without heat. Marta genuinely wanted to know, because Paloma could see her running the arithmetic behind her eyes — a stranger, a courier, no visible stake in this child, refusing a sensible course at real cost — and the arithmetic would not balance. "Who is she to you?"

And that was the question, wasn't it. Paloma opened their mouth to say *no one,* which was true in every way that could be proven, and closed it again, because the truth that couldn't be proven was sitting right there in the shape of her sleeping face, and had been since the moment the lantern light found it on the doorstep.

"I don't know," Paloma said, which was also true, and perhaps the truest thing they'd said all winter.

* * *

Twenty years ago there had been rain instead of snow, and a different door.

Paloma remembered it the way one remembers all the worst nights of one's life — in fragments too sharp to be useful, each detail polished by handling until it cut. Nineteen years old, and the satchel already packed, though it held almost nothing: two shirts, their father's old courier case with the brass fittings gone green, and the letter of acceptance from the Republic Courier Service with the eagle-and-key stamp that had seemed, that spring, like a door out of the world.

Their father had been in the front room. He had not gotten up. He sat in his chair with his hands folded on his knee and his face composed into the expression he wore for verdicts, and he said, "You'll carry things for them, then. Papers. Secrets."

"Yes."

"And when they ask you about your family — because they will ask, Paloma, the service always asks — what will you say?"

Paloma had stood in the doorway with the rain coming down behind them and understood, in the way one understands things at nineteen — completely, and wrongly — that this was a test of courage. That the correct answer was the clean one. That love that could not survive being spoken plainly was not worth the speaking.

"I'll say I have none," they said. "I'll say I left."

Something had moved behind their father's eyes. Paloma had taken it then for anger, and had been glad of it, anger being easier to carry than anything else. Only later — much later, in the way of such things — had it occurred to them that it might have been satisfaction. Or relief. Or grief wearing its work clothes. A father's face in lamplight holds all three without changing.

Anders had been the one to walk them to the gate. Sixteen, lanky, furious in the boneless way of sixteen-year-olds, hands jammed in his pockets against the rain. He didn't say goodbye. He said, "You think you're better than us now," and Paloma, who thought no such thing but said nothing to correct him, let him believe it, because letting Anders believe ugly things had become, by then, a kind of habit. A kindness in reverse. Or not a kindness. They had never decided.

Aunt Lysa had been the one in the upstairs window with the lamp. Their mother's sister, widowed young, living in the east room the way furniture lives in a room — permanent, assumed, unnoticed until removed. She had raised half of Paloma's childhood without ever once raising her voice. She stood at the glass with the lamp held high, and she did not wave, and she did not turn away, and her brow was furrowed as though she were working at a problem too large for her, and Paloma had looked up at her for three seconds, four, five — long enough that the image etched itself wherever such images go to wait — and then walked down the darkening road and did not look back again.

Forgetting was supposed to be a mercy. That was what Paloma had told themselves for twenty years, in the courier hostels and border stations and rented rooms of a life spent carrying other people's words because their own had nowhere to go. *You left so you wouldn't be a liability to them. You cut the thread cleanly. Forgetting was a mercy — to them, and to yourself.*

But mercy, Paloma thought now, watching the child breathe, implies someone consents to receive it. Nobody had asked Lysa if she wished to be forgotten. Nobody had asked Anders. They had simply been left, the way one leaves a station — without ceremony, because ceremony would have meant admitting the leaving mattered.

And now here was a girl on a doorstep with Lysa's face and a note ordering whoever found her to complete the erasure. *Forget the face.* As if a face were a document that could be sealed. As if twenty years of practiced not-looking-back amounted to anything at all against one child asleep by a window with her fists tucked under her chin.

Paloma had refused the annexation officers without knowing why. Sitting there in the storm-dark with cold tea, they began to suspect the why, and suspected that they had known it all along and simply never had the courage to phrase it:

They were not refusing to forget the girl.

They were asking her forgiveness for having forgotten everyone else.

* * *

The child woke in the grey hour when the storm slackened to something merely relentless. She woke the way children wake who have learned not to wake loudly — eyes opening all at once, body perfectly still, taking inventory of the room before permitting herself any sound. Paloma had seen soldiers wake that way. The comparison sat badly in the chest.

Marta put bread and dripping in front of her, and milk warmed on the hearth, and the girl ate with the same controlled efficiency, watching both adults over the edge of the bowl. Her coat — the coat with the money in the lining — hung on the peg by the door where Marta had searched it and re-hung it, and the girl's eyes went to it once, briefly, the way a courier checks a satchel, and Paloma felt something cold move through them that had nothing to do with the weather.

"What's your name?" Marta asked, gently, for the third time since the doorstep.

The girl looked at her bread. She had not spoken yet. Whether she could not or would not, Paloma could not tell, and the not-knowing was starting to feel like a splinter under the skin.

"It's all right," Marta said. "You're not in trouble here."

The girl's gaze slid — deliberately, Paloma was almost certain — to the table where the note lay under the salt cellar. To the note, and past it, to Paloma's satchel against the wall. She looked at the satchel the way no child looks at luggage. She looked at it the way one looks at a thing one has been told about. Warned about.

Paloma's pulse ticked up one notch.

"Can you speak?" Paloma asked. Kept the voice low, unhurried, the voice for skittish animals at checkpoints. "It's all right if you can't. Nodding works fine."

The girl studied them for a long moment — studied them, Paloma thought, *assessed* them — and then she nodded, once, a small precise motion.

"But you don't talk."

A pause. Then a shake of the head — but slower than the nod, less certain, and her brow furrowed, that familiar furrow, and Paloma understood suddenly that the answer was neither yes nor no but something more complicated that a four-year-old — five, perhaps — had no words for.

"All right," Paloma said softly. "Nodding works fine."

Outside, the wind dropped for a moment, and in the sudden quiet they could hear the river below the fens, swollen and fast, and beneath the river another sound: wheels, many of them, and the muffled cadence of voices, coming along the north road. Marta rose from the table without a word and went to the window, and Paloma saw her shoulders change.

"Storm's breaking early," Marta said. "God help us, that'll be the road clearing behind somebody."

The girl had gone very still, her warm milk forgotten, her eyes fixed on the door. And when she spoke at last — her first words, small and clear, in the accentless Vellmaran of the eastern districts, the districts that no longer existed —

"Not them. Please. Not the gray coats. Mama said wait for the one who carries the seal."

Paloma's blood went cold, and colder still when the girl's finger rose and pointed, unwavering, at the satchel against the wall.
