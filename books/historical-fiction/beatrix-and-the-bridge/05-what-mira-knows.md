## 5. What Mira Knows

The storm kept its grip on the Kessel Bridge all through the night, and the inn creaked like a ship that had lost its way inland. Paloma did not sleep. They sat in the narrow room above the common hall with the satchel between their feet and listened to the wind worry at the shutters, and below them, in the warm dark of Marta's kitchen, a child slept who should not have existed.

Paloma had put her there themselves — carried her in from the doorstep with the snow still crusted on her coat, laid her on the bench near the banked stove, watched Marta tuck a horse blanket around her small body with the brisk tenderness of a woman who had done it before for other refugees and expected nothing from any of them. The girl had not woken. Her breathing was slow and even, the breathing of someone exhausted past dreaming.

*Forget the face.* The note was folded now in Paloma's breast pocket, riding against their ribs like a second heart. They had read it perhaps forty times since the bridge inn's lamplight first showed them that impossible face — Lysa's face, the aunt they had buried alive in memory two decades ago, along with everything else of the house at Verre Hollow. The handwriting meant nothing to Paloma; it was cramped, hurried, the script of someone writing against time or cold or worse. But the instruction itself meant everything. Nobody wrote *forget the face* about a child unless the face mattered. Unless someone might be looking for it.

And so Paloma had spent the night doing what couriers do, which is to say: thinking like the enemy.

* * *

Morning came gray and windless, the storm spent, the world outside rebuilt overnight in white. When Paloma came down the stairs, Marta was already at the stove, frying something in fat that smelled of better years, and the girl was awake — sitting up on the bench with the blanket pooled around her waist, watching the door with an attention that no sleeping child should possess.

"Good morning," Paloma said.

The girl looked at them. She did not answer. Her eyes were brown, ordinary brown, and yet they held the same quality Paloma remembered from their aunt — a stillness, a watching-back, as though the person behind them were taking inventory. She was perhaps seven. Maybe eight. Undernourished the way children of collapsing countries are undernourished: not skeletal, but thinned, drawn downward, every feature slightly too sharp for her face.

"She hasn't spoken," Marta said without turning from the pan. "Not a word since I found her yesterday. Not when I asked her name, not when I asked where her mother was, not even when I burned my thumb and swore loud enough to wake the dead." She plated the food — eggs, black bread, a smear of preserves gone sugary at the edges — and set it in front of the girl. "Eat, little one."

The girl ate. Carefully, neatly, without haste. She ate like someone who had learned that meals could be taken away.

"Does she know her name?" Paloma asked.

"I tried. Mira, she had pinned to her coat — you saw the tag." Marta wiped her hands on her apron and lowered her voice. "Look. I'll say what I said last night and then I won't say it again. There are annexation officers working the road south of here. They pay five crowns for every runaway turned in, ten if the runaway turns out to be somebody's escaped household servant. And there's a man comes by Thursdays collecting names of anyone unregistered. If you're keeping her—" she let the sentence die into a shrug that carried its own arithmetic.

"I'm not keeping her," Paloma said. "I'm trying to find out whose she is."

"Nobody's, most likely. Or somebody's, which is worse." Marta set a cup of chicory coffee in front of Paloma and one in front of the girl, though whether the child would drink it was anyone's guess. "In this weather, whoever left her couldn't go on. That's the only mercy in it — they didn't abandon her. They ran out of road."

*They left her where someone would find her,* Paloma thought. *On the doorstep of the last inn before the bridge. On the doorstep of the only route out.* It had the shape of design, and Paloma distrusted shapes of design the way sailors distrust calm water.

They sat down across from the girl and did the thing they were trained to do, the thing twenty years of carrying other people's secrets had built into their hands and eyes: they observed without staring, questioned without asking.

"You're Mira," they said.

She chewed. Watched.

"Mira is a good name. My mother's sister had a friend named Mira once, in Verre Hollow. Did you live in Verre Hollow?"

At the name of the town, something happened. Not much. A pause in the chewing, half a second, the kind of hitch a clock makes when dust gets in the works. Then she went on eating, and Paloma would have believed they'd imagined it if they hadn't spent their whole life reading faces at checkpoints.

"Where is your mother, Mira?"

Nothing.

"Your father?"

Nothing. Marta, wiping down the counter, shook her head slightly — *you're wasting your breath* — and Paloma understood that she was right, and kept going anyway, because the hitch at Verre Hollow would not leave them alone.

* * *

Through the morning, Paloma tried everything the trade had taught them.

They tried direct questions, and got silence. They tried indirect ones — pointing at objects, offering choices — and got shrugs, or the particular blankness children learn from adults who punish wrong answers. They tried writing words on a scrap of paper and sliding it toward her, and this produced the first real reaction of the day: she looked at the written word *mother*, and her chin trembled once, precisely once, and then stilled, and she pushed the paper back across the table as if returning something she had no right to keep.

*Trained,* Paloma thought, with a coldness that surprised them. *Someone has trained her not to speak.*

Because that was what it was. Not muteness, not shock — although God knew there must be shock in her somewhere, banked like coals. This was discipline. The silence had structure. She spoke nothing except in fragments, and the fragments came only when some reflex stronger than her training overrode it — a "thank you" mumbled at Marta when she refilled the plate, automatic as breathing; a "no" when offered the preserves again. Small courtesies drilled deep. Words that cost nothing to give away.

Words that revealed nothing.

By noon Paloma had learned three things: the child answered to Mira, she flinched at the word *Verre Hollow*, and she counted her food. That last one Paloma noticed almost by accident — the girl's lips moving faintly as she divided her bread into pieces, arranging them by size, eating the smallest first. Rationing. A child who rations is a child who has been hungry in a way that teaches arithmetic.

Paloma sat with that knowledge and felt the old guilt shift in its grave. Twenty years ago they had walked out of a house full of people who loved them, because loving them had become dangerous, and they had told themselves the leaving was mercy — theirs to give, the family's to receive. *Forgetting was supposed to be a mercy.* And now here was the price of forgetting arriving on a doorstep in the snow, wearing Lysa's face, rationing bread by weight.

*You don't get to make her yours,* Paloma told themselves. *Half the republic has brown eyes. Faces repeat. You're a courier seeing patterns because patterns are your sickness.*

But the courier's sickness had kept them alive through nine wars' worth of borders, and it said: *the note, the doorstep, the flinch. Someone chose this inn. Someone chose you.*

* * *

It happened in the afternoon, while Marta was out checking her smokehouse and the inn stood empty except for the two of them and the wind's aftertaste.

Paloma had given up on questions and taken out work instead — courier's work, the maintenance of craft. Their satchel lay open on the table, ostensibly empty, holding nothing but the tools of a dying profession: wax, a seal matrix wrapped in oilcloth, spare cord, a folding knife, and the false-bottomed document case their father had carried through the last war, which Paloma carried now out of superstition more than sentiment. They were re-wrapping the seal matrix, running their thumb over the worn crest of Vellmar, when they felt the small presence beside them and looked up.

The girl was standing at the table's edge, looking at the case.

Not at the satchel. At the case — the old leather document case, scuffed, unremarkable, the kind of thing sold in any market in any town. She was looking at it the way Paloma had seen customs men look at luggage: with the whole body, with hunger and fear braided together.

"Do you know what this is?" Paloma asked, gently.

Silence. But her eyes stayed on the case.

Paloma lifted it, turned it over, set it down again. An ordinary gesture. And the child tracked it — tracked it with a fluency that no ordinary child would bring to a piece of old leather, the way you track a thing you have been told matters more than your life.

Something moved in Paloma's chest, slow and terrible, like ice shifting on the northern lakes.

*Mira,* they almost said. *Listen to me carefully.* And stopped themselves, because the next part required certainty, and certainty required proof, and proof required asking a question that could not be unasked.

They closed the satchel. They pushed it aside. They took out instead a scrap of paper and the stub of pencil Marta kept by the till, and in the flat, unhurried voice they used at borders — the voice that made nothing seem important — they said:

"My mother used to sing me a song when I couldn't sleep. Do you have a song?"

The girl's breath caught.

For a long moment she stood absolutely still, and Paloma watched a war happen inside her, watch it in the small muscles around her eyes, in the whitening of her knuckles where her hands gripped the table's edge. Whatever had been drilled into her, whatever promise or threat held her silence together, was being weighed against something else — and Paloma understood, with the sick clarity that sometimes arrives just before disaster, that the something else was need. Weeks on the road. A mother gone. A note pinned to her coat ordering strangers to forget her. And now a stranger showing her a battered courier's case and asking about songs, exactly the way — Paloma would have bet the seal, the satchel, both hands — exactly the way someone had told her it would happen.

She opened her mouth. Closed it. Opened it.

And then, in a voice barely louder than the settling snow outside, she recited:

*"Kessel-nine. Vane. Ostrevik-doublet-four. Hallow-end. Kessel-nine."*

She said it the way children recite — flat, rhythmic, the stresses falling in a pattern that had nothing to do with meaning, because there was no meaning in it for her. She said it twice through without pausing for breath, the way you sing something, the way you hold a rope someone taught you never to drop.

Then she stopped, and her face flooded with color, and she clapped both hands over her mouth as if she could take the words back bodily, and fled — around the table, toward the stairs, gone, her footsteps hammering up to the room where the blanket still held her warmth.

Paloma sat without moving.

*Kessel-nine. Vane. Ostrevik-doublet-four. Hallow-end.*

They knew the shape of it before they knew the content, knew it in the spine the way a musician knows a false note. Group-cipher, Vellmar diplomatic pattern, the four-part construction used for fleet authentication codes — the very class of sequence Paloma had spent their career protecting, sealed in wax, weighted in satchels, worth more than the lives of everyone entrusted with it. *Kessel* was a fleet prefix. *Ostrevik-doublet* was a coordinate pair. Whoever had taught that song to a seven-year-old had encoded the evacuation fleet's authentication chain into a child's lullaby, drilled it into her until it lived in her bones, and sent her walking toward the bridge with instructions that could be sung only to someone who could prove they carried the seal.

Paloma looked down at the seal matrix in their hands, at the worn crest of a country that had maybe six weeks left to exist, and understood.

The satchel on the table — the assignment they had accepted, the codes bound for the fleet, the weight that had ridden heavier than any document should — was a decoy. Or a duplicate. Or a dead letter. It hardly mattered which, because the true codes had legs. The true codes were asleep upstairs under a horse blanket, rationing bread, flinching at the name of a town they had erased from their own heart twenty years ago.

The mission wasn't in the satchel. The mission was a child.

*She isn't just family,* Paloma thought, and the thought arrived with horror and calculation fused into a single cold stone. *She's the run. She's the last dispatch of the Republic of Vellmar, walking and breathing, and someone — Lysa, if Lysa still lived, or whoever wore her face — trusted it to me without ever meeting me.*

To whom? Why? How had anyone known that a courier named Iversen would stop at Marta's inn on the last road north? Unless they hadn't known — unless the child had simply been aimed at the bridge, at the seal, at anyone, and the family resemblance was coincidence stacked on coincidence until coincidence became indistinguishable from fate.

Paloma pressed their palms flat against the table until the wood bit back.

Every answer required the one thing they could not afford: certainty. Because if the girl carried the codes, then she was not a refugee to be sheltered or a niece to be mourned. She was cargo of state, and every annexation officer on the road between here and the coast was — knowingly or not — hunting her. The note said *forget the face*. Of course it did. The face was the only key. Forget the face, and the codes stayed safe inside a song nobody knew was a song.

Paloma rose, took the lamp, and climbed the stairs slowly, each step rehearsed.

The girl was sitting on the bed in the corner, knees pulled to her chest, watching the door with those inventory-taking eyes. Waiting to be punished. Waiting to be handed over. Waiting, perhaps, for the only thing her mother had promised her: someone with the seal.

Paloma sat down on the opposite bed, set the lamp between them, and unwrapped the oilcloth with deliberate care, letting the firelight catch the crest of Vellmar.

The child's whole body changed. The fear did not leave her — Paloma doubted anything would ever fully chase it out — but it made room for something else, something that broke open in her face like ice going out on a river.

"That's right," Paloma said quietly. "Now. Tell me your mother's name."

And Mira, in a whisper, gave the one fragment Paloma had prayed for twenty years and dreaded for twenty years, the fragment that undid every mercy of forgetting:

"Lysa," she said. "Mama Lysa. She said you'd come."
