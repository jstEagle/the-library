## 6. The Brother at the Fleet

The harbor at Vellmar-Nord smelled of tar and panic.

Anders Iversen had been standing on the same stretch of wet stone for three hours, and in that time he had counted the things he could see from it the way other men counted rosary beads: eleven ships at anchor, four of them seaworthy; a crane with a bent arm that no one would ever fix now; the commission's barge, black-hulled, sitting out past the boom like a spider waiting at the edge of its web. He had counted the queue for manifest verification twice. The first time it was nine hundred people. The second time it was more, because word had come down the coast road overnight that the annexation was complete in the south, that the flag over the Assembly Hall in the capital had been changed before dawn, and every hour brought another hundred souls carrying what they could — a birdcage, a sewing machine, a portrait of a grandmother wrapped in oilcloth against the sleet — toward the last ships of a country that no longer existed except on paper, on manifests, on the lips of clerks.

Anders was fifty-three years old and he had spent twenty of those years being useful to the people who were doing this. That was the arithmetic he could not escape, standing there. He had signed papers. He had named roads. He had sat across tables from men in gray coats and told himself each time that he was keeping something intact — a family, a district, a life — by giving up something smaller. It had never once felt like betrayal while he was doing it. It only ever felt like betrayal afterward, in the nights, which was why he had arranged his nights so carefully for two decades: full of work, full of drink when work failed, never full of silence.

His name was on the list. Berth one hundred and twelve, *MV Solvang*, final sailing of the evacuation fleet, allocated under family right — the old law, older than the republic even, that kin of registered maritime families could claim passage as inheritance. His father's name was still on the registry, dead fifteen years, and through that dead man's ink Anders had purchased his place on a ship bound for a country he had never seen. There was a joke in that somewhere. He had not been able to find it.

He was looking at the road. He had been looking at the road all morning.

Because of the girl.

* * *

He had told himself a hundred versions of the story by now, and every version made him braver than the last. In the best version, sending Mira away had been strategy: the commission was watching his household, had been watching since autumn, since her mother Sera died in the way that official reports called *detainment-related* and everyone else called what it was. A child in Anders's custody was a child they could take. A child on the road was a rumor, a shape in a crowd, nothing. In the worst version — the true one, the one that surfaced whenever he closed his eyes longer than a blink — he had simply passed the weight along, the way he had learned to do, the way his whole career had taught him to do: find someone else's shoulders, sign the transfer form, go home to your warm room and tell yourself it was mercy.

He had pinned the note to her coat himself. He had written it in his own hand, in the careful clerk's script that had won him his first position at thirty: *To whoever finds her — forget the face. She is no one. She is safe as no one.* He had paid a carter three days' wages to take her north, away from the docks, away from the lists, to the bridge country where the annexation was moving slowest and an unnamed child might dissolve into the general sorrow of the season like salt into water.

Forget the face. He had meant it kindly. He had repeated it to himself like a prayer: she will be safer unnamed, unremembered, a child without a file is a child without a cage.

Then why, when he thought of her asleep in that cart among the turnip sacks, did his hands want to shake?

They were shaking now, a little. He put them in his pockets where he couldn't see them and watched the north road come down out of the hills toward the harbor in a long gray ribbon of sleet and mud.

That was when he saw the courier.

* * *

He knew the walk before he knew the face. Twenty years, and grief and grievance had blurred everything else about Paloma Iversen — he could no longer have said with certainty what color their eyes were, or whether the scar was on the left brow or the right — but the walk had stayed exact, filed and preserved like an evidence photograph. Paloma walked like someone carrying breakables. Shoulders square, chin level, steps measured to protect whatever was inside the coat. Their father had walked that way. So, once, had Anders, before he learned to walk like a man who wanted to be invisible in every room.

They came down the road out of the sleet with a courier's satchel riding high on one hip, and Anders felt the sight of it land in his chest like a dropped anchor. He knew what a sealed diplomatic case looked like. He had processed enough of them, in the early years, before they stopped trusting him with anything but names. The republic was dead; its last codes were walking toward him on the shoulders of the sibling he had ruined.

Paloma saw him at forty paces. Anders watched it happen — watched recognition arrive and get suppressed, arrive again, get suppressed again, the whole skirmish crossing their face in the space of two heartbeats. Then the walk changed. It became, for a moment, sixteen years old again, coming up the garden path the night after the arrest, and then it hardened back into the courier's stride, and Paloma crossed the remaining distance and stopped three feet away, close enough that Anders could see the sleet melting in their hair.

"You're supposed to be dead," Paloma said.

"I'm sorry," Anders said. "I know that's disappointing."

It came out wrong — came out the way everything came out of him these days, armored and glib — and he saw it land badly, saw Paloma's jaw set. He tried again. "There was a report. After the border fighting. I thought—" He stopped. Started over. "I heard you'd gone north. I hoped you'd gone further."

"I went far enough." Paloma looked past him at the ships, the queue, the black barge. Their eyes came back to his face and studied it the way one studies a document for forgery. "You look well, Anders. The regime agrees with you."

"The regime doesn't agree with anyone. It just takes." He unfolded his hands from his pockets, realized he didn't know what to do with them, folded them again. "Paloma—"

"Don't." The word was quiet and absolute. "Not here. Not with all of them watching." A small motion of the head toward the manifest clerks, the gray-coated observers, the crowd. "Why are you on the list?"

"Family right. Father's registry."

"Of course." Something moved through Paloma's expression that was almost a laugh and was worse than weeping. "Dead men's rights. This country runs on them."

* * *

They ended up in the back room of the harbormaster's office, because Anders still had friends — that was the thing about collaboration, he had learned; it bought you exactly enough goodwill to get a warm room and no farther — and because Paloma would not talk on the quay. The room held a stove, a desk scarred with thirty years of initials, a window fogged with sea-salt. Paloma stood the entire time, near the door, satchel still on its strap, as though they expected to need to leave through it quickly. Perhaps they did. Anders poured tea neither of them drank and sat on the far side of the desk because sitting on the near side would have felt like an interrogation, and God knew there had been enough tables between Iversens already.

"Father's dead," Paloma said. It wasn't a question.

"Fifteen years."

"I know. I sent nothing."

"He knew. He kept the file open anyway. Your file." Anders turned his cup in his hands. "He used to say you'd deliver yourself eventually. One last run home."

"That isn't why I'm here."

"I know why you're here." Anders said it too fast, and hated how fast, and made himself finish. "The fleet. Everyone who can reach the water is here for the fleet. And the ones who can't reach it—" He stopped.

"The ones who can't reach it are being sorted," Paloma said. "Yes. I came through two of the sorting towns. I've seen the sorting."

Silence. The stove ticked. Out in the office, clerks were calling numbers, and each number made someone in the crowd either sit down or stand up, and Anders found he could not stop hearing it, the arithmetic of hope and despair reduced to digits shouted through a doorway.

"Sera's dead," he said, because it had to be said, and because saying it was the only penance he could think of. "Mira's mother. Last winter."

"I know," Paloma said, and now it was Anders who stared.

"How could you possibly—"

"Because of Mira."

The name fell into the room like a lamp knocked off a table. Anders felt the blood leave his hands — he watched them whiten where they gripped the cup — and he set the cup down very carefully, because if he did not give his hands a task they were going to shake, they were going to shake the way they had shaken every night since the carter's cart had rolled north with the sleeping girl in it.

"I don't know what you mean," he said.

"She's alive, Anders. I found her. At the bridge inn, on the doorstep, in the storm, with a note pinned to her coat telling whoever found her to forget her face." Paloma's voice stayed level, courier-level, the voice for delivering terrible things intact, and somehow that was the cruelest part — that they had learned to carry horror the way he had learned to carry papers. "So I didn't forget. I don't imagine that was what the author of the note intended."

Anders stood up. He didn't remember deciding to stand. His legs did it for him, and then there was nowhere for the standing to go, so he stood at the fogged window with his back to his sibling and looked at nothing — the gray harbor, the gray ships, the gray water — and heard his own voice come out thin.

"She was taken from me. After Sera. The commission took her into 'protective custody,' which means—" He swallowed. "I know what it means. Children go into those houses and come out with new names and new loyalties and their old lives filed away in a drawer. I couldn't let them file her away, Paloma. She was all that was left of Sera. All that was left of any of us that wasn't poisoned."

"So you sent her onto the road alone. In winter. With a stranger."

"With a carter I trusted."

"A carter you *paid*."

"Yes." The word came out flat as a slammed ledger. "I paid. That's what I do. You'll want to say it, so say it: it's what I've always done. Everything has a price and I'm the one who pays it, or finds someone to pay it, or becomes it. Go on. You've had twenty years to write the speech. I've read drafts of it in my sleep."

He heard Paloma move behind him. When he turned, they were watching him with an expression he couldn't read — not triumph, which he had expected, not coldness, which he deserved, but something searching, something that looked almost like a person reading a letter for a second time because the first reading hadn't matched the memory.

"The note said to forget her face," Paloma said quietly. "Whose idea was the wording?"

"Mine."

"And the pinning of it. To her coat. Where anyone could read it."

"Mine." He crossed back to the desk, braced both hands on it. "I wrote it so no one would look at her twice. An unnamed child is invisible. A remembered child is a record, and a record is a handle, and handles are for pulling people out of crowds. I was protecting her."

"Is that what you told yourself when you informed on Father?"

It landed like a slap, and Anders took it, stood there and took it, because he had been taking it for twenty years and had earned every year of it — except that lately, in the last few weeks, in the last few days especially, some load-bearing wall inside him had developed a crack, and when Paloma said it, he heard himself answer, in a voice he did not recognize as his own:

"I don't know anymore."

The stove ticked. Somewhere outside, a number was called, and a woman began to cry, softly, the way people cry when they have learned that loud grief gets you moved to the end of the line.

"I don't know anymore," he said again, and sat down heavily, and put his face in his whitened hands. "I told myself I did it to protect you. Both of you. Father had been running things through the house for years — you didn't know, you couldn't have known, he kept you clean on purpose, the courier and the student, his untouchable children. But they were closing on him, Paloma. I saw the file. I was already inside by then, I'd already given them the little things, the road schedules, the names of the ferrymen — and I saw what was coming, and I thought: if I give them Father, they stop digging. If I hand them the head of it, the investigation dies with the arrest. It was supposed to contain it. It was supposed to be a *surgery*, not a—" His breath caught. "They took everything anyway. The house. The registry. They just left us our names so we'd have something to ruin."

When he lowered his hands, Paloma had not moved from the door. But their face had changed — minutely, the way ice changes before it shifts — and Anders, who had spent his life reading officials' faces for the flicker that meant yes or no, saw it and did not dare name it.

"Forget the face," Paloma said slowly. "You wrote that."

"Yes."

"Your hands shake when you say it."

Anders looked down. His hands were shaking. He folded them together hard, knuckle over knuckle, and could not make them stop.

"Here is my problem, brother," Paloma said, and the word *brother* went through him like a nail. "Someone drilled a diplomatic cipher into that child's memory. Someone taught it to her as a song, so thoroughly that she sings it in her sleep. And now the last sealed codes of the Republic of Vellmar are sitting in a satchel on my shoulder, and I am beginning to suspect the seal opens on what that child knows — and that the person who hid a state secret inside my niece's skull, and then put her on a winter road with a note telling the world to forget her, is sitting in front of me pretending he did it all to save someone."

"That's not—" Anders started, and stopped, because the denial had nowhere honest to attach itself. He looked at the satchel. He looked at his shaking hands. He thought, with a clarity that felt like falling: *she remembers the song. They'll search the ship. They're going to search the ship.*

"Paloma," he said, very carefully, "what does the commission do to couriers who can't produce what their seals promise?"

Paloma's hand came to rest on the satchel flap.

Out in the office, a bell rang, and a clerk's voice carried through the door, flat as slate: "*Solvang* manifests close at six. Final boarding at seven. Passengers holding berths ninety through one-twelve, have your papers ready."

Berth one hundred and twelve. Anders's number. His by right, by registry, by the dead man's ink — and he watched Paloma hear it, and watch him, and understand at the same instant he did that nothing in that sentence was going to survive the night.
