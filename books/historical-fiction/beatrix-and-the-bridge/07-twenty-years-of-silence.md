## 7. Twenty Years of Silence

The berth office smelled of tar and cold iron, and Anders Iversen had been standing in it for three hours pretending to read the manifest when his sister walked through the door.

He knew her before he saw her face. He knew the way she moved — that courier's economy, no gesture wasted, as if the air itself charged tolls. Twenty years, and Paloma still walked like someone being followed. He supposed they always had been, in a way. Their father's shadow followed all of them; it was only a question of who had learned to walk beside it and who kept trying to outrun it.

"Paloma."

They stopped. The satchel across their chest seemed to weigh on them differently now, he thought — or maybe that was just what guilt did to a person's posture. He'd had twenty years to study guilt from the inside. He was an expert in how it sat on the shoulders.

"You're alive," Paloma said.

"So are you." He almost smiled. "I checked the casualty lists every week for two years after Father died. Then I stopped checking. It felt like hoping."

"I wasn't hoping."

"No," Anders said. "You never were."

* * *

They took a table at the harbor canteen because it was the only place with chairs, and because neither of them could bear the intimacy of standing close. The room was full of people waiting for ships — families with their belongings wrapped in bedsheets, clerks with ledgers they would never file again, old men who had served the Republic of Vellmar since before Anders was born and who now stared at their hands as if trying to remember what they'd been built for.

Paloma sat with the satchel strap still around their neck.

"You can take it off," Anders said. "No one's going to steal it here."

"You'd be surprised."

"I wouldn't." He turned his cup in its saucer. The tea was terrible, made of some reconstituted leaf the annexation commission hadn't bothered to ban yet. "I heard you took Father's route. The northern run."

"Somebody had to."

"Somebody always did. That was his whole philosophy, wasn't it? Somebody always did, so nobody ever had to say why." He heard the bitterness come out sharper than he'd intended, and watched it land. Paloma didn't flinch. They never flinched. That was the cruelest thing about them — they received every blow like a receipt, filed it, and gave nothing back.

"Say what you came to say," Paloma told him.

"What I came to say?" Anders laughed, and it came out wrong, too loud, and a woman at the next table looked over. He lowered his voice. "Twenty years, Paloma. Twenty years you've carried one sentence about me like a stone in your shoe, and now we're sitting at a table and you want me to go first?"

"You informed on him." There it was. No decoration, no hesitation. "You went to the magistrate's office in Vellmar City and you told them where he kept the correspondence. You were seventeen."

"I was sixteen."

"That's not better."

"It's not meant to be better. It's meant to be accurate." His hands were shaking. He put them flat on the table so Paloma couldn't see, which was absurd, because Paloma had already seen everything there was to see about him years ago. "You've had two decades to make it simple. Brother betrays father. Clean story. Fits in a pocket. Do you want to know what it actually looked like from where I stood?"

"I've imagined it plenty."

"You've imagined your version of it." He leaned forward. The tea went cold between them. "Do you remember the winter the magistrates came to the house? Not the last time — the first time. When I was fourteen. They put Father against the wall and they went through Mother's linens looking for seditious pamphlets, and Mother stood there apologizing to them. Apologizing. For the state of her own drawers. And you — you were eleven, and you hid under the stairs, and afterward Father found you and he didn't comfort you. He asked you what you'd seen. He made you recite it. Badge numbers, the order they searched the rooms, which cabinet they opened first. And when you got it right he said, 'Good. That's the family trade.'"

Anders watched his sister's face. Nothing moved in it, but something moved behind it — he'd learned long ago that Paloma's face was a door and the interesting weather was all behind it.

"I remember," Paloma said quietly.

"He was training us. Both of us. You he trained to carry, and me—" Anders stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "Me he trained to be caught. Don't you see it? Someone in that house was always going to be taken. He built his whole life like a man building a house in a flood zone — he knew the water was coming, and he arranged the furniture so that when it did, something would survive. You were the thing that survived. I was the flood door."

"That's a beautiful story," Paloma said. "You invented it after the fact."

"I invented it *during* the fact." His voice cracked and he hated it. "They had me in a room for six hours, Paloma. Six hours. A man named Ostrava with soft hands and a list of names, and mine was already on it, and yours was under mine, and Mother's under yours. He didn't threaten me. That's what nobody understands about those men — they don't threaten children. They explain. Ostrava explained to me, very patiently, with diagrams, exactly what happened to families of collaborators' relatives. He showed me photographs. And then he said, 'Your father knows this. Your father has always known this is how it ends. Give us the correspondence room and your family becomes a closed file.' And I believed him. God help me, I believed him, because believing him was the only version of events in which Father would forgive me."

The canteen noise went on around them — a child crying somewhere, the clatter of tin plates, a loudspeaker outside announcing revised departure times in a voice that tried to sound routine. Anders became aware that he was gripping the edge of the table hard enough to hurt his fingers, and made himself let go.

"And did he?" Paloma asked. "Forgive you?"

Anders laughed again, quieter this time. "He never spoke to me again. Not once. Fourteen months I lived in that house after — fourteen months eating at the same table — and he addressed every word he ever said to me through Mother. 'Tell your son the wood needs stacking.' 'Tell your son he'll take the southern road this week.' As if I'd stopped being a person and become a message. Which, I suppose, in that house—"

"In that house everyone was a message," Paloma finished.

For a moment neither of them spoke. Outside the window, a crane swung a crate over the quay, and the shadow of it passed across the table like a bird.

"Then why," Paloma said at last, and their voice had changed — lower, more careful, the voice they used for sealed things, "did you send for the family right? Why claim a berth under our name if he disowned you?"

"Because he didn't disown me." Anders reached into his coat, slowly, letting them see his hand the whole way, and drew out a folded paper gone soft at the creases from years of handling. He didn't open it. Just held it flat on the table between them like a card played face-down. "Two days before they took him for the last time, he left this under my door. I've never shown it to anyone. I've never even decided what it says. Some nights it's an apology. Some nights it's a test. With him it was always both — you know that better than anyone. Every kindness he ever did us had a hook in it."

"Then don't show me," Paloma said. But their eyes stayed on the paper, and Anders saw — with the strange clarity that grief grants, grief wearing grievance like a borrowed coat — that his sister's hands had finally gone still on the table. Perfectly still. The stillness of someone listening to a sound they'd spent twenty years pretending not to hear.

"You wonder," he said. "After all this time. You still wonder if I'm telling the truth."

"I wonder if it matters."

"It's the only thing that matters. It's the only thing that's ever mattered between us." He pushed back his chair and stood, and the paper stayed on the table between them, unopened, its creases white as scars. "Read it or don't. Keep it or burn it. But know this much, Paloma, and know it from me: I have regretted many things in my life, but I have never once regretted that you weren't in that room with Ostrava. Whatever else I am to you — whatever you need me to be, traitor, coward, fool — I was the flood door. And it held."

He turned toward the door, toward the gray light and the loudspeakers and the ships waiting to carry away whatever the country couldn't hold anymore.

Behind him, he heard the small sound of paper unfolding.

* * *

Outside, the wind off the harbor smelled of diesel and ice. Anders walked without direction, the way he'd walked for twenty years, past queues of families and stacks of luggage and a merchant clerk nailing a new passenger list to a board by the customs shed.

He stopped when he saw the list.

One name remaining on the final vessel's manifest, the ink barely dry: a single berth, held under family right, pending verification. His name. And beneath it, in smaller type, the notice that chilled him worse than the wind:

*All manifests subject to inspection by the Annexation Commission. All passengers subject to verification of record.*

His record. Sixteen years old, a signed statement, a closed file that had bought his family's safety — a file that cut both ways, that had always cut both ways, and that some clerk in the commission's offices might open at any hour now, looking for collaborators to detain before the ships sailed.

Anders stood in the wind a long moment. Then he thought of Mira — of the note he'd pinned to her coat with hands that shook, of the inn at the bridge, of whoever might find her — and he began, quickly, to look for his sister.
