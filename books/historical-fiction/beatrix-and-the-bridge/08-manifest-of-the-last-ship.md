## 8. Manifest of the Last Ship

The harbor at Vellmar-by-the-Sea did not look like a place of rescue. It looked like a wound.

Paloma came down the coast road in the last grey light of a day that had never properly begun, and stopped where the road bent, and looked. The fleet rode at anchor in the outer roads — eleven ships, twelve once, before the *Meridian* burned at her moorings in a fire no one had explained and no one would investigate — their hulls dark against a water the color of old tin. Beyond them, out on the horizon where the neutral waters began, nothing. No escort. No promise. Just the flat line where the republic ended and the indifferent sea took over.

Nearer in, the docks themselves were a lesson in arithmetic. Paloma had spent twenty years among numbers — weights of parcels, distances between relay stations, coins exchanged for favors — and so they read the quay the way they read a manifest, at a glance, and the sum was wrong. There were too many people for the berths. Thousands pressed behind the customs barriers with their papers held above their heads like prayers, and every one of them could count too. They knew what the ships could carry. They knew what the ships would leave.

The wind came off the water carrying coal smoke and brine and the particular smell of fear that Paloma had learned on the roads north — a smell like wet wool and unwashed bodies and something underneath, animal and thin, that was simply terror given time to settle.

They moved into the crowd.

* * *

The courier seal got them through the first barrier faster than any passenger paper would have. A young woman in the green armband of the evacuation authority glanced at it, glanced up at Paloma's face, and her expression did something complicated — relief and resentment in the same breath, the look people gave the republic's servants now, as if the whole apparatus of state were a doctor who had arrived too late and was still charging for the visit.

"Courier Iversen," Paloma said, and gave their number, and watched the woman find it on her list.

"Final run," she said. Not a question.

"Final run."

"The naval liaison will want you at the harbormaster's office. Codes verification." She hesitated, then leaned close, dropping her voice beneath the crowd's noise. "Everyone wants to know if you actually have them. Half the dock thinks the fleet sails without them and the annexation ships let us go out of contempt. The other half thinks—"

"I know what both halves think," Paloma said. "Where is the harbormaster's office?"

She pointed, and Paloma went, and behind them they heard the murmur pass down the barrier like wind through wheat: *a courier, a real courier, they've come.*

It was strange, Paloma thought, to be believed in again after being given up on. It was worse than being ignored. Every pair of eyes that followed them now carried a weight, and Paloma already knew — had known since the bridge inn, since Mira asleep on the doorstep with a note pinned to her coat — that whatever they did next would spend that weight one way or another, and there was no spending it without cost.

* * *

The harbormaster's office smelled of lamp oil and cold tea. Maps covered the walls, and someone had drawn lines across the shipping lanes in red grease-pencil, and the lines told the story faster than any broadsheet: the corridor of neutral water was narrow, and shrinking, and the annexation gunboats sat along its edges like patient dogs.

Three men waited inside. The naval liaison, Captain Reiner, whom Paloma had met twice in ten years of runs to this coast — a square, tired man who had served the Republic of Vellmar his whole life and now commanded the eleventh-largest fleet it had ever possessed and by far its last. Beside him stood a clerk from the evacuation authority with a ledger open, and beside the clerk, uninvited and entirely comfortable, a man in the slate-grey coat of the Annexation Commission.

"Courier." Reiner gestured to a chair. Paloma stayed standing.

"You've brought them," Reiner said. "The codes."

Paloma said nothing. The satchel hung at their side, and its weight — which had felt heavier than any document should from the very first hour, which had felt like carrying a stone with a country folded inside it — seemed suddenly loud in the quiet room, the way a secret is loud.

"They've brought them," the Commission man said mildly. He had a soft, pleasant face, the kind that made a career of delivering hard terms, and he spoke with the unhurried cadence of someone whose authority required no raising of the voice. "Corvin Hessel, Provisional Commission for the Transfer of Sovereign Functions." He did not offer a hand. "Courier Iversen, thank you for coming. This is fortunate. We had begun to worry the codes might be lost, and lost codes create such unfortunate complications."

"Complications," Paloma repeated.

Hessel spread his hands toward the window, toward the harbor, the fleet, the thousands at the barriers. "Let me be direct, because we haven't much time and I suspect you're a direct person. Twelve thousand, four hundred souls are manifested on this fleet. The transfer protocols require all instruments of state encryption to be surrendered intact upon annexation — that is the treaty language, you needn't take my word for it. Your cargo falls under those protocols. Deliver it to this office tonight, and tomorrow the fleet sails on schedule, unmolested, every soul aboard accounted for." He smiled faintly. "Fail to deliver it, and the treaty permits us to consider the fleet potentially non-compliant. Potentially non-compliant vessels are subject to inspection. Inspection means delay. Delay means winter crossings. And I don't need to tell a courier what the winter sea does to overloaded civilian ships."

The room was very still. The clerk had stopped writing.

"You'd search refugee ships," Paloma said.

"We'd inspect them," Hessel corrected gently. "Words matter, Courier. Yours especially. That's rather the point of your profession." He stepped closer, and lowered his voice as though the kindness itself needed privacy. "I'll tell you something else, off the record, because I think you deserve honesty more than most people I meet. If you deliver the codes, no one will ever know but the three of us. The republic dies quietly, the fleet sails, the child — ah."

He paused. Paloma felt their own heartbeat stop and restart.

"Yes," Hessel said softly. "We know about the girl. We know she was found at the Kessel Bridge inn. We know whose daughter she was — is. Her mother was on a list I keep in my own desk, Courier, and the list has not been crossed off. You see, we believe the codes may exist in more than one form. Paper burns. But memory—" he tapped his own temple with one finger, an almost fatherly gesture, "—memory has to be found. So here is my honest offer, all of it at once. Bring me the sealed case tonight, and bring me the girl, and the fleet sails clean. One transaction. Everyone aboard goes free, including your brother."

"My brother," Paloma said, "is not on any manifest you've seen."

"No?" Hessel raised his eyebrows, and the clerk turned the ledger around on the desk and slid it across, and Paloma looked down at the page and the floor tilted very slightly under their feet.

There it was. **IVERSEN, ANDERS — berth 9 — family right, verified.**

* * *

Paloma walked out of the harbormaster's office with the ledger entry printed behind their eyes and found Anders exactly where twenty years of absence should have made him impossible to find: leaning against the customs shed wall, hat pulled low, watching the gangway of the ship that held his berth.

He saw Paloma coming and straightened, and for a moment neither of them moved. Then Anders said, "You look like Father," and Paloma said, "You look terrible," and that was as much reunion as either of them could manage in the open air of a dying country.

"How," Paloma said.

"The family right. Kin of registered evacuees get last-berth priority; some clerk dug up the old household record. Father's name was still on it. Mother's." He shrugged, a gesture that tried to be careless and failed. "I didn't ask for it, Paloma. I want that said plainly. The berth came to me like a debt collector comes — I just answered the door."

"And Mira?"

Something crossed his face then, quick as a fish under ice, and was gone. "Later," he said. "Not here."

"There's a man inside," Paloma said, "who knows about her. Knows the inn, knows her mother's name. He's offering me the fleet's safety for the codes and the child together."

Anders went very still. When he spoke, his voice had dropped to almost nothing. "Then he's lying about part of it, because the fleet's safety isn't his to sell. Look at the gunboats, Paloma. Count them. That fleet sails when they permit it and not one hour sooner, codes or no codes, and everyone in that office knows it. The demand isn't real. It's theater." His hands, though — his hands shook, fine and constant, the way they had shaken sixteen years ago outside a closed door in their father's house, and Paloma watched them and thought: *theater requires actors who can hold steady, brother, and yours never could.*

"And the berth?" Paloma asked quietly. "Is that real?"

Anders looked at the gangway. At the ship. At the water beyond it, where freedom was a line on the horizon that kept moving when you sailed toward it.

"It's real," he said. "That's the cruelest thing I know right now. Everything else is fog, but that berth is real, and it has my name on it, and somewhere on that quay there are four hundred people with no names on anything."

* * *

Paloma left Anders at the shed and walked the length of the quay alone, because there was a calculation to do and it could not be done in front of anyone, least of all the brother whose name sat in the ledger at berth nine.

Here was the trap, laid out plain. Deliver the sealed codes to the Commission tonight, and the fleet sailed — perhaps; maybe; if Hessel's word was worth the paper it wasn't written on — and the codes died in a government drawer instead of in ash, and every cipher the republic's agents abroad still trusted became an instrument for hunting them. And Mira's memory, the song her mother had drilled into her, would remain the only copy in the world — which meant the girl remained valuable, which meant the girl remained hunted, forever, until someone found her and opened her head like a letter.

Withhold the codes, and the Commission declared the fleet non-compliant, and inspection became delay, and delay became winter, and winter became drowned children off the bar at Halden Point — Paloma had delivered the casualty lists from the last wrecked evacuation, thirty years ago, and had never fully forgotten the smell of the ink.

Either way, the codes decided. Either way, Mira paid. Unless —

Unless the codes stopped existing in any form at all. Paper and memory together, burned and sung away, until there was nothing left to surrender and nothing left to hunt, and the Commission's whole elegant trap collapsed for lack of a prize.

But that required reaching the inn first. That required Mira. And Hessel's men knew where the girl was, and Paloma had been standing in a lamplit office explaining themselves while the clock ran.

Paloma turned back toward the town, walking fast now, past the barriers and the held-up papers and the faces that had believed in them for one afternoon, past a sentry who called out something about curfew and thought better of chasing a courier. Behind them the fleet lay black on the black water, twelve thousand souls balanced on a ledger page.

One berth left, and two names that could fill it, and a man in a grey coat who wanted a child thrown in with the cargo.

Paloma reached the top of the coast road as the first snow began to fall, and looked south toward the dark line of the river, and the Kessel Bridge somewhere beyond it, and the inn with its cellar, and ran.
