## 9. Marta's Price

Marta Hollis had kept the Bridge Inn standing through four governments, and she had learned the arithmetic of survival early: every flag that came down the Kessel road was a customer, and every customer was coin, and coin did not care what color it was painted. She had poured beer for Vellmar's border wardens and charged annexation officers double for the same, and she had never once lost sleep over either. The inn sat at the edge of everything — the last lit window before the bridge, the first lit window after it — and edges belonged to whoever paid for them.

She was wiping down the taproom counter when the officers arrived, three of them, their grey coats dark with the storm that had been chewing at the mountains since morning. The tall one in front had the particular politeness of men who enjoyed being feared. He stamped the snow from his boots on her clean floor without looking at it.

"Innkeeper." He set a folded paper on the counter as if laying down a card. "We're conducting a census of transient persons along the border corridor."

"Census." Marta folded her rag with great care. "In this weather. That's devotion."

"It's orders." His eyes went past her, moving over the room — the empty benches, the dead hearth, the stairs rising into shadow. "You have guests?"

"You see the room," Marta said. "Nobody crosses the Kessel in a storm unless they're fools or couriers, and the couriers stopped coming two weeks ago. I've got a cellar full of turnips and nobody to eat them."

The officer smiled. It was not a warm smile; it was the smile of a man showing his work. "A courier came through here. Three days ago, perhaps four. We have a witness at Tarn Mill who saw them heading north."

Marta let her face do what it wanted, which was nothing. Forty years of serving strangers had given her a face like a shut door, and she blessed it now. "Couriers come through all autumn. Half the republic's secrets have slept in my beds. If you're going to arrest my linens, take the lot."

The second officer, younger, with rain still beaded in his close-cropped hair, flipped open a ledger. "The courier would have been traveling alone. Carrying a diplomatic satchel, seal-marked. Possibly armed."

"Then I'm glad they didn't stay," Marta said.

"Did they?"

The taproom was very quiet. Rain worked at the shutters. Marta counted the seconds the way she counted change, and made her decision in the space between one breath and the next — though she would not understand until much later that a decision was even being made. Later she would lie awake in another country's bed and trace the moment backward through memory: *I could have said yes. I could have pointed at the cellar door and been paid.*

"They drank half my brandy and left before dawn," she said. "Went north, toward the fleet roads, if it helps your paperwork."

It did not help, because the tall officer was no longer listening. He had turned toward the kitchen, where a low door under the stairs led down to the cellar, and he stood very still, the way dogs stand still when they catch something on the wind.

"What's below?" he asked.

"Turnips," said Marta. "And apples going soft. And forty years of bottles I haven't had the heart to throw out."

He started toward the door, and something happened in Marta Hollis then that she had no name for and no history of. It was not courage — she would have laughed at anyone who called it courage. It was closer to arithmetic, the old arithmetic failing her all at once, the columns refusing to add. Somewhere beneath her feet, behind a false wall of casks she'd built herself with wedges and sweat thirty years ago when the first war sent deserters past her window, a child who was not hers was holding her breath in the dark. And Marta found that the sum of that small held breath outweighed everything else on the ledger — the inn, the licenses, the good standing she had kept spotless through every regime, the whole careful edifice of a life spent owing nothing to anybody.

She stepped in front of the cellar door.

It was not dramatic. She simply moved, the way a barmaid moves, sideways and quick, and put her body between the officer and the handle, and picked up a cask lid as if to carry it somewhere, and dropped it with a crash that filled the room.

"Mind the floor," she said. "The boards down there are rotten. Took a man's ankle clean through, two winters back. Broke it in three places. Had to haul him up myself and nurse him a month, and he never paid his bill." She met the tall one's eyes. "If you want to inspect my turnips, you'll sign a damage waiver first. New regulations, I expect you know them. Property claims against occupation forces. My cousin filed one in Drennmark and waited eleven months for a reply."

It was nonsense. Most of it was nonsense — there were no such waivers, or if there were, no officer on this road cared about them. But nonsense delivered confidently is a kind of wall, and the young one hesitated, and looked at his superior, and in that hesitation Marta understood the shape of her own life with terrible new clarity: she had spent forty years being useful to power, and usefulness was a currency, and she was spending it now, all of it, in a single handful.

The tall officer studied her. He had a long face, patient, the face of a man who had learned that most doors open eventually if you lean on them slowly enough.

"Your name, innkeeper?"

"Hollis. Marta Hollis. It's above the door, if you'd like it for your ledger too."

"I'll have it for the ledger regardless." He took out a pencil, unhurried. "License number?"

She gave it. There was no sense withholding it; he could read it off the frame by the hearth. He wrote it in slow block letters, and she watched him do it and felt the ink drying on her own future. A flagged license. A blacklisted name. By spring there would be no grain allocation, no coal ration, no right to sell passage or lodging along the corridor. The inn would die of paperwork, quietly, the way everything in Vellmar was dying now — not by fire but by signature.

"Thank you for your cooperation," the officer said, and the courtesy in it was the worst part, because it meant he knew exactly what he had just done to her, and considered it fair exchange for her insolence.

* * *

They did not leave. That was the trouble. They settled in.

The tall one — Captain Reiner, the young one called him, though he never once answered to it — ordered tea and took the table nearest the stairs, and set his paper on it, unfolded, like a man prepared to wait out a season. The third officer went out to check the stable and came back shaking rain from his collar, reporting horses gone, tracks northward, half-buried already.

"North," Reiner repeated, pleasantly. "Toward the bridge."

"The bridge goes somewhere," Marta said. "That's generally why people build them."

She served the tea because her hands needed work and because refusing service was a confession, and while she poured she listened — she had always been a listener; it was half of what her trade was — and pieced together what they knew. A courier, seal-satchel, seen at Tarn Mill. A child reported missing from a convoy south of the river, unregistered, no papers. And a third thing, mentioned only in the captain's pauses, only in the way his finger tapped the folded orders: that the codes themselves were the true cargo, and that the commission believed — badly, hungrily — that they had passed this way.

Someone upstairs was asleep. Marta carried the thought of her the way you carry a full cup across an uneven floor. Mira. Four days now in the loft above the kitchen, in the narrow space behind the winter blankets where Marta had hidden deserters and smugglers and once, in the first war, a wounded warden whose politics she never learned and never asked about. The girl ate what she was given without question, which frightened Marta more than crying would have. Children who don't ask about food have learned that food is uncertain. On the second night, hearing footsteps, Marta had found her awake, sitting up in the dark with her knees pulled to her chest, humming — a strange little tune, repeating, precise, nothing like any lullaby Marta knew.

"That's an odd song," Marta had said.

"It's not a song," the girl had said, and rolled over, and would say nothing else.

Now Marta cleared cups and thought about the courier — Paloma, the guest register said, *P. Iversen*, a name written in a hand that had tried to disguise itself and failed — and about the way they had stood over the sleeping child on the doorstep with a face like a man seeing a ghost walk past in daylight. She had watched them from the window, that first storm-night, watched the recognition hit, and she had thought: *there is a story here older than the war.* People carried their histories the same way they carried luggage; some of it always showed through the wrapping.

And she had thought, with the cold practicality of forty years at the edge of things: *not my business.* The child was trouble wrapped in a note. The note said *forget the face*, which was the sort of instruction Marta's whole life had obeyed. Forget the face, forget the convoy, forget whatever cruelty had left a six-year-old alone on a doorstep in the last winter of a dying republic — none of it was Marta's to hold.

Except that the child hummed in her sleep. Except that she had eaten Marta's stew on the second night and said, quietly, in the flat voice of a child rehearsing a fact, "My mother says I must remember, so remembering is my job," and gone back to eating. Except that when Marta banked the kitchen fire each night, some animal instinct in her — older than arithmetic, older than regimes — checked the loft ladder first.

The tall officer finished his tea and thanked her again, and did not leave. He sent the young one out to requisition quarters in the village, keeping the taproom, keeping the stairs in view, settling in beside the cellar door like a man guarding a vault he hadn't opened yet. Night came down. The storm thickened. And Marta stood behind her bar polishing glasses that were already clean, and understood that she was now in a siege of one, and that sieges are won not by strength but by patience, and patience was the one currency she had never run short of.

* * *

Near midnight he stirred. Reiner rose, stretched, crossed the taproom with the easy stride of ownership, and put his hand on the cellar latch.

"Boards are rotten," Marta said from the bar, without turning around. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved.

"Then we'll walk carefully."

He went down. She heard his boots on the treads, heard the match flare, heard the small sounds of a man searching a cellar: casks thumped, shelves dragged, glass clinking against glass. She stood with both hands flat on the bar and did not pray, having never learned how, but performed instead the closest act she knew — she recited inventories. Turnips, nine sacks. Apples, softening. Rye, half a barrel. Brandy, twenty-two bottles minus those drunk by governments. It was a rosary of ordinary things, and she told it to herself over and over while the light moved below, and the false wall of casks — wedges driven tight, sawdust packed in the seams, invisible to anyone who had not built it — held.

His boots came back up. He emerged dusted in cellar mold, and for one suspended moment his eyes went to the ceiling, to the floors above, and Marta felt her heart stop against her ribs like a bird against glass.

"The loft," he said.

"Bedding and mice," said Marta. "Take my bed if you want it. You'll find it no cleaner than my conscience."

But he went up anyway, boots on her stairs, and searched her rooms with a thoroughness that was itself a message, and found blankets, a cracked mirror, thirty years of a solitary woman's undramatic life. He did not find the space behind the winter blankets because he did not know to look for depth in a shallow place, and when he came down he was quieter than before, and that quiet frightened her more than anything yet.

At the door, pulling on his gloves, he paused.

"You've survived every government this road has produced, Frau Hollis. That takes talent." He settled his cap. "Talent should know when its government has changed."

"We'll see whose government it is come spring," Marta said. "They usually rotate faster than my cellars rot."

He almost smiled. Then he was gone into the snow, and she listened to the horses until the wind ate the sound, and only then did she allow her knees to inform her that they had been locked for three hours.

* * *

She brought the girl down from the loft by lamplight, and fed her at the kitchen table — eggs, the last of the good bread, butter she had been saving for a day worth butter — and sat across from her and studied the face that a note had ordered everyone to forget.

"You'll have to go," Marta said. "Tomorrow, if the storm lifts. He'll be back, and next time he'll bring men who move the casks."

The girl — Mira, the courier had named her; Marta had pried it out of the register entry and the child's own reluctant mouth — went on eating, small and methodical. Then she looked up.

"You'll get in trouble."

"I've been in trouble since before your mother was born," Marta said. "It's an old coat. It fits."

"Paloma said—" The girl stopped. Whatever Paloma had said was weighed and kept, a child already learning that some information is cargo. "Paloma will come back."

"Couriers always think so." Marta reached across and wiped a smear of butter from the girl's chin with her thumb, an act so far outside her practiced self that she noticed it only afterward, like finding a stranger's handwriting in her own journal. "Listen to me. There's a family in Drennmark — Ostrow, at the tannery yard. If I don't come with you, if something keeps me, you tell them Marta Hollis sent you and they owe me a debt from the first war they'd rather not discuss. You knock at the gate twice and wait."

"Why wouldn't you come with me?"

Because my name will be blacklisted by noon. Because this inn is the only thing I have ever owned and I will spend my last weeks watching it starve. Because I have spent my whole life charging for kindness and I am too old to learn a new trade.

"Because someone has to stay and pour the tea when they come back," Marta said, "and make them believe there's nothing under my stairs but turnips."

The girl considered this with grave attention, then nodded, accepting it as children accept the illogic of adults — as simply the weather of the world. She finished her eggs. And then, pushing back from the table, she began to hum again — that small precise tune, repeating, turning over on itself like a lock taking a key — and Marta listened, and this time she heard what she should have heard on the first night: that it was not a melody at all. It had the cadence of lists. Of letters. Of something counted.

*Not a song,* the girl had said.

Marta sat very still in her kitchen, in the last inn of a dying republic, with a blacklisted name drying somewhere in an officer's ledger and a child humming state secrets over her empty plate, and she thought about the arithmetic she had lived by, and how it had failed her, and what had replaced it. Nothing had replaced it. That was the truth she kept circling. There was no sum on the other side, no profit column, no clever edge-of-the-border trade to make this pay. There was only a child with butter on her chin and a storm coming, and Marta Hollis, sixty-one years old, discovering at the end of a long career in survival that she had, apparently, been saving up for this.

Outside, far off down the Kessel road, a horse sounded through the wind. Then another. Coming north — fast, too many of them, and among the hoofbeats, faint under the storm, the girl heard it too and went silent, mid-hum, her eyes going wide and fixed on the door.

Marta rose, snuffed the lamp, and put her hand on the child's shoulder.

"Cellar," she whispered. "Behind the casks. Don't sing."
