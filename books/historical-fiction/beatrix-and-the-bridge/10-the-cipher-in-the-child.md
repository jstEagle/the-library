## 10. The Cipher in the Child

Mira knew the cellar by its smells before her eyes adjusted: cold earth, lamp oil, the sweet-rot of last autumn's apples stored too long. Marta had lowered her down through the trapdoor with a blanket and a stub of candle and told her, in the firm voice she used on drunks, to be *a stone*. Stones made no sound. Stones waited. Mira had been a stone for three hours now, and she was very good at it.

Above her, boots crossed the floorboards of the inn's common room. Men's voices, flat and official, asking questions in the clipped accents of the commission. She pressed her back against the stone wall and pulled her knees up under Marta's blanket and sang — not aloud, never aloud — inside her head, where no one could take it from her.

*Low bridge, low bridge, the water's dark and deep.*

Her mother's voice taught it to her, syllable by syllable, in a kitchen that smelled of dill and candle wax, in a city whose name Mira was not supposed to remember either. Her mother had said: this is a lullaby, and lullabies are the only things in the world that are true and also safe. Nobody arrests a child for singing to herself. Nobody burns a song out of a person's memory, because songs live somewhere the fire can't reach.

She had believed that, then.

The trapdoor opened without warning and lamplight poured down like something spilled, and Mira flattened herself against the wall so hard she felt the seams of the stones print into her shoulder blades. A face appeared at the edge of the square of light — not an officer's face. Paloma's. The courier who had come to the inn two nights ago with the storm and the satchel and the look of someone carrying a country in their coat.

"They're gone," Paloma whispered down. "It's safe for now. Come up where it's warm."

Mira did not move. "Marta says be a stone."

"Marta," Paloma said, and there was something strange in the courier's voice, something cracked down the middle, "is downstairs signing a paper that says she never saw you. She's paying for that. The least you can do is come have some soup."

Mira considered this. It had the shape of fairness, and fairness was a language she understood even at six-going-on-seven, which is what she told people, though her mother had once said *nine* when the doctor asked, and Mira had decided not to correct anyone because numbers were another thing that could be taken away. She climbed the ladder. Paloma's hand closed around her wrist at the top — careful, dry, warm as bread — and did not let go until they reached the kitchen.

* * *

The kitchen after midnight was Marta's kingdom and no one else's. Marta sat at the big table with a cup of plum brandy she wasn't drinking, and her eyes were red-rimmed in a way that frightened Mira more than the officers had, because officers were weather, but Marta being hurt was a wall falling.

"You'll eat," Marta said. "Both of you. And then the little one goes back down, or into the attic, or up a chimney if she has to, until we know the road is clean."

"She won't need hiding much longer." Paloma set the satchel on the table — the satchel, the one with the seal stitched under the flap, the one Mira had seen them touch the way sailors touch a charm. "I'm going to the fleet tomorrow. I'll settle everything there."

"Settle." Marta laughed without any laugh in it. "That's what my second husband said, and I buried him under a pear tree."

Mira ate her soup. It was fish and barley and too much salt, the way soldiers' soup is, and it was the best thing she had ever tasted because she'd eaten nothing since morning except half a raw turnip Marta had slipped her through the trapdoor. While she ate, she watched Paloma watch her, and the watching had a weight to it, like hands.

"You talk in your sleep," Paloma said quietly.

Mira's spoon stopped. "No I don't."

"You hummed. Last night, through the floor. A tune."

"It's just a song." Mira put all the boredom she owned into her voice, every grain of it. "Nursemaids sing it. Everybody knows it."

"Everybody," Paloma repeated, and reached over, slow as honey pouring, and turned the satchel's flap back so that the seal showed in the lamplight: the triple tower and the broken crown of the Republic of Vellmar, pressed in grey wax the color of old teeth.

Something happened inside Mira's chest, a door swinging on a hinge her mother had oiled years ago and told her never, ever to touch.

Her mouth opened before her mind could stop it. "*Only to the bearer,*" she recited, in the sing-song cadence drilled into her, "the one who carries the mark of the three towers crowned in breaking, who proves it by showing the seal unasked, twice, in different light—"

She stopped. Paloma had gone white. Not storm-white, not fear-white — a whiter thing, the white of paper right before it burns.

"That's not a nursemaid's rhyme," Paloma said.

"No," said Mira, and her voice came out small, and she hated how small. "It's mine."

* * *

They sent her to bed in the loft above the kitchen, with the blanket and the candle and Marta's promise that no one would come through the night. But Mira lay awake listening to the murmur below — the low urgent weave of Paloma's voice, the rumble of Marta's — and she knew they were talking about her the way people talk about a stove left burning, about a well that might be poisoned.

Good. Let them. Because her mother had also said — on the last morning, kneeling to fix Mira's collar with fingers that shook so badly the button took three tries — *if anything happens to me, you find a courier. Couriers are the only ones who keep promises for a living.* And here was one, sleeping ten feet below her, keeping promises even now, in their sleep, probably, the way Mira hummed.

She slid out of bed and down the ladder on sock feet, because she had been a stone all day and stones get tired of it.

Paloma was still at the table, alone now, the seal lying between their hands like a dead thing. They looked up when she came in and didn't tell her to go back to bed, which told Mira more than anything said all evening.

"Show me again," Paloma said. "How your mother taught you. All of it. From the beginning."

So Mira climbed onto the bench across from them, folded her hands the way her mother used to fold hers, and began.

* * *

"It starts with the proving," she said. "Because Mama said the song would find the right ears by itself if I just followed the rules. Rule one: never sing it whole unless someone shows you the seal unasked. Not asked-for. If you ask to see it, liars will show you anything. It has to come out of their pocket like it's nothing. Like this." She nodded at the wax on the table. "Like you did. You didn't show me on purpose, it just fell open, and that's how Mama said I'd know."

"And rule two?"

"Rule two is I sing the counting part first. The nonsense words. *One for the tower, two for the crown, three for the river that won't run down.* If the person is real, they'll hear that it isn't nonsense, because it counts in sevens, not ones. Seven towers, seven crowns, seven rivers. Only someone who knows Vellmar ciphers counts in sevens." She shrugged. "I don't know why. Mama said I didn't need to know why. Knowing why is for the other end of the song."

Paloma's hands had gone very still on the tabletop. "And the rest of it? The whole song?"

"The rest is the letters." Mira said it simply, because to her it had always been simple; it was the adults who kept looking at her like she'd said the house was on fire. "Every verse has sounds that don't quite fit, and the sounds that don't fit are the message. Mama drilled them in pairs. She'd tap the rhythm on the table and I'd have to sing back the wrong-sounding parts only, and if I got one wrong she wouldn't hit me, she'd just look sad, and that was worse, so I learned them all. There's forty-one wrong-sounding parts." She paused. "Forty-one, and then the ending verse, which isn't code. That one's just for me."

"What does the ending verse say?"

Mira looked at the candle instead of at Paloma. "That the singer's mother loves her and is sorry, and that when the song is done the singer should eat something sweet and think of the sea."

The silence that followed was long enough to hold a funeral. Somewhere outside, wind worried at the shutters, and the storm off the Kessel valley threw a handful of sleet against the east window like a beggar asking to be let in.

"Your mother," Paloma said at last, and their voice had gravel in it, "was a courier."

"I think so. She never said. But she had a case like yours, older, with a burnt spot on the corner. And she could walk forever without getting tired, and she knew which roads were lies." Mira traced a crack in the tabletop. "She said the song was a present for the future. She said most presents get smaller when you carry them, but this one gets bigger, and that's how you'd know it mattered."

"Does she—" Paloma stopped. Started again, softer. "When did you last see her?"

"The tag day." Mira touched the coat hanging by the kitchen door, the one with the hole in the lining where the note had been pinned. Someone had taken the note away — Paloma had it, she'd seen it folded in the inner pocket — but the pinpricks were still there, two small wounds in the wool. "There was a lot of shouting and everyone was going toward the water, and Mama put me in a line of children going north with a woman who was supposed to take us to relatives. Then the line got scattered at a checkpoint, and the woman went one way and I got pushed another, and after that I walked. I walked a long time. I found the inn because it had a light." She said it all flatly, the way you recite, because if you put feeling into those sentences they got too heavy to lift. "Then you came."

"Then I came," Paloma echoed.

"You knew my face," Mira said. "You went white the first night too. When you picked me up off the doorstep. I was pretending to be asleep but I wasn't." She leaned forward. "Who did I look like?"

For a moment she thought Paloma wouldn't answer. The courier picked up the seal, turned it over, set it down again — stalling, the way adults stall when they think children can't see the machinery working behind their faces.

"Your great-aunt," Paloma said finally. "Lysa. She had this way of tilting her head when she was deciding whether to trust someone. You tilted it tonight, before you started the song. Exactly like her."

"Lysa." Mira tried the name. It tasted like a word from a story, which maybe it was. "Is she alive?"

"No one from those days is alive," Paloma said, "except apparently everyone I least expected," and Mira didn't understand that yet, but she filed it away with all the other adult-shaped boxes she'd been collecting, to be opened later, in order, like her mother's verses.

* * *

"Sing it for me," Paloma said.

Mira's whole body went cold. "Not without the rules. Rule one—"

"You've seen the seal. Twice — once tonight, once when I checked it by the fire your first morning here, in different light. Both times unasked." Paloma spread their empty hands on the table, palms up, the gesture of a merchant showing scales balanced. "By your own law, Mira, I'm proven. Whatever the song is — whatever it carries — it was meant for someone carrying that seal, going somewhere the seal still means what it says. I'm the last one. Maybe in the world."

"But if I sing it, then it's yours, and it stops being mine." Mira heard her own voice crack and despised it. "Mama put it inside me so it would be safe, and if I give it away, then what am I carrying? Nothing? Just me?"

"Oh, child." Paloma came around the table and knelt, which made them suddenly smaller than her, which was clearly deliberate, a courier's trick, a kindness shaped like a trick. "Listen to me. I have carried things my whole life — papers, seals, orders that ended lives I never saw. Do you know what I learned? The carrying doesn't stop when you hand something over. What your mother gave you isn't the code. The code is just ink wearing a costume. What she gave you is the fact that somebody trusted you with something bigger than you. That doesn't transfer. That stays."

Mira wiped her nose with the back of her hand. "You sound like a book."

"I am a book. Several. It comes of walking everywhere with nothing to do." Paloma smiled, and up close their face had lines around the eyes like a map of roads, and Mira thought: *she walked roads too, my mother, and this is what the roads do to you.* "But you don't have to decide tonight. Sleep on it. Songs keep."

"They're the only things that keep," Mira said, with her mother's exact intonation, and saw Paloma flinch as though the ghost itself had spoken.

She didn't sing that night. She went up the ladder to the loft and lay under Marta's blanket and held the song inside her chest the way you hold your breath, and she examined it, really examined it, for the first time in her life. Forty-one wrong notes. A lullaby that counted in sevens. A mother's apology sewn into the final verse like a coin into a hem. She had always known, in the way children know things they aren't allowed to know, that the song was important. But she had thought important meant *precious*, the way a locket is precious. She had not understood, until she watched Paloma's face go paper-white above the seal, that important could also mean *dangerous* — that her mother had hidden a weapon in the one place no soldier would ever search, and then handed the weapon to a girl who still sometimes wet the bed during storms.

*She didn't have a choice,* Mira told the dark ceiling. *Nobody had a choice. That was the whole disease of this year.*

But the thought sat badly, and somewhere past the smallest hours she dreamed of her mother tapping rhythm on a table that stretched away into fog, tapping faster and faster, and in the dream Mira couldn't remember whether the ninth wrong note came before the tenth or was the tenth, and her mother's sad look opened like a door, and behind the door was the sea.

* * *

Morning came grey and rinsed-out, the storm spent, the road south steaming faintly like something breathing. Marta cooked eggs with onions and didn't speak, and Paloma repacked the satchel with the thoroughness of someone avoiding conversation, and the three of them ate in a silence that had stopped being uncomfortable and become almost companionable, the silence of people who have survived a night together and may survive others.

Then hooves on the road. Two riders, blue-and-grey armbands, moving fast southward toward the bridge — messengers, not searchers — but the sight of the armbands emptied the kitchen of air anyway. Through the window Mira watched them pass the inn without slowing, and she understood, with the terrible speed of children, exactly what they were: news outrunning safety. News of the fleet. News of berths. News that somewhere ahead of her, lists were being written and names were being weighed, and her name was on none of them because officially she did not exist.

Paloma stood, shouldered the satchel, and pulled on their coat.

"You're leaving," Mira said.

"For the docks. Today, before the weather turns again." Paloma hesitated at the door, one hand on the frame, and looked back at her — at her, specifically, the way no adult had looked at her since the tag day, as if she were a destination rather than luggage. "Mira. About the song."

"I know the rules," Mira said. "Rule three. I sing it only when the bearer asks properly. Word for word, the way Mama taught: *Bearer, I am ready; prove the seal in daylight.*" She swallowed. "Is today daylight enough?"

Paloma's jaw worked. For a heartbeat Mira thought they would refuse, would leave the song asleep inside her and carry whatever lesser papers filled the satchel, and something in her — something small and furious and her mother's — rose up to object.

But Paloma only said, "When I come back for you, I'll ask properly. Be ready."

And the door closed behind them, and Mira stood in the warm ruin of the kitchen, next to Marta, watching the courier shrink down the south road into the steam and the distance — carrying her mother's voice in a stranger's body, and knowing, the way she had known on the doorstep that first night, that everything now depended on a question not yet asked, and an answer she had been keeping like breath for longer than she could remember.

She turned to Marta. "Teach me the words for proving things. Big words. In case he forgets how."

Marta laughed — a real one, cracked and smoky. "Girl," she said, reaching for the frying pan again, "you may end up teaching him."
