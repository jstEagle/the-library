## 11. Anders's Bargain

The docks at Vellmar-haven smelled of tar and panic. Anders had been standing in the same queue for six hours, and the queue had not moved, because the queue was not really a queue anymore — it was a crowd pretending to be one, held together by the fiction that the manifest clerk behind the rope barrier had the power to save them. Behind him, the evacuation ship *Meridian* rode low in the gray water, her holds stuffed past decency, her rails crowded with faces that all wore the same expression: the look of people watching a country end from the best seat available.

Anders had spent twenty years learning how to wait in lines. It was, he had come to understand, the principal skill of a collaborator. You waited while the new authorities took your name down. You waited while they decided what your cooperation was worth. You waited, and you smiled, and you kept your hands where they could see them, and you told yourself — every time, you told yourself — that this was protection, this was prudence, this was love wearing the practical clothes of survival.

His hands were shaking again. He put them in his pockets.

He had seen Paloma across the dock an hour ago and had not gone to them. He had watched instead, from behind a stack of crated books destined for no library that would ever exist, as his sibling stood at the gangway with a courier's satchel strapped across their chest like a bandolier, arguing quietly with a manifest officer. Paloma had not changed. That was the cruelty of it. Twenty years, and Paloma still stood the way Father had taught them both to stand — weight forward, chin level, the posture of a person who expected to be believed.

Anders had changed. He knew that. He saw it every morning in the shaving glass: a man grown soft in the middle and hard around the eyes, a man whose face had learned to arrange itself into whatever the room required. He had been arranging it for so long that some mornings he woke and could not find the original underneath.

The child was the reason he was here. Not the berth — the berth he'd secured weeks ago through channels he did not like to name, family right, the last legal ghost of a household that no longer existed. The child was the reason his hands shook.

He had sent her away. He wanted, desperately, to believe the sentence when he said it in his own head: *I sent her away to save her.* Her mother — Sera, dead these four months now, dead in the way the annexation killed people without bothering to kill them, a fever in a requisitioned hospital, a bed given to someone with better papers — Sera had come to him in the autumn with a madwoman's plan. *They'll search everyone,* she had said. *But who searches a child? Who looks into a child's face and sees anything but a child?* And she had drilled the codes into Mira the way their mother had once drilled psalms into them both, note by note, until the girl could sing state secrets in a lullaby's clothing and giggle at the wrongness of it, not knowing, never knowing, what she carried.

Anders had said no. He remembered saying no. He remembered the exact quality of the lamplight in Sera's rented room, the way it made her look already like a photograph of herself. And then he had said yes, because Sera had looked at him the way Paloma used to look at him, before, and because he had spent his whole life unable to refuse the people he loved their terrible ideas.

Then she died, and the codes kept singing in the child, and the annexation commission began asking its questions, and Anders did the thing he always did. He arranged his face. He wrote a note. He pinned it to his daughter's coat — *his daughter's*, though no paper in the republic would say so, though he had never dared claim her, though claiming her would have been the one honest act of his life — and he paid a cart driver to leave her at the Bridge Inn, far from the docks, far from the searches, with instructions that read like a curse:

*Forget the face.*

He had meant it as mercy. He had written it at three in the morning with the ink shivering because his hand was shaking even then, and he had meant: forget her, lose her in the crowd of war orphans, let her become nobody, because nobody survives. He had not known — could not have known — that the cart driver would set her down at the one inn in Vellmar where a courier with Paloma Iversen's face might be storm-bound.

The world was small that way. The world took your sins and delivered them back to you by the fastest route available.

* * *

Paloma found him at dusk, when the floodlamps along the quay came on and turned the harbor into a stage set for some bleak final act. Anders heard the footsteps before he saw them — courier's steps, economical, heel-to-toe, the walk Father had drilled into them on the long road between villages when they were children carrying letters too important to run with.

"You've been watching me," Paloma said, "for an hour."

"I've been watching everyone for an hour. It's a crowd."

"It's a hostage exchange." Paloma stepped beside him, close enough that he could smell woodsmoke and wet wool and, beneath it, something from home — the lye soap Marta sold at the inn, which meant Paloma had been back to the inn, which meant Paloma had found her, which meant the whole fragile architecture of his lie was already leaning. "Mira is in the cellar under the Kessel Inn. Marta hid her from two officers today. Marta, who would sell her own shadow if the light was right."

"Marta is a survivor."

"Marta is a coward who finally tripped over something worth being brave about." Paloma turned and looked at him directly, and Anders felt the old reflex rise — the smile, the open hands, the reasonable tone — and strangled it. Not this time. Not with Paloma. "You wrote the note."

It was not a question. He made himself meet it anyway.

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because she'd be safe." His voice came out lower than he intended. "Because a child with no face is a child no one hunts. Because Sera—" and there it was, the crack, his voice splitting on the name like ice on a pond, "—Sera put those codes in her head, and if the commission ever heard that song, they wouldn't take the girl, they'd take the song out of her however they had to, and I have seen—"

He stopped. Paloma waited. Paloma had always been good at waiting; it was the other half of the courier's craft, the half no one praised.

"You've seen what?" Paloma said.

"Nothing. I've seen nothing. That was my talent, wasn't it? Seeing nothing. Hearing nothing. Reporting—" He laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "You know exactly what I reported. You've spent twenty years knowing it."

* * *

They walked the length of the quay while the tide went out, because Anders could not have that conversation standing still, and Paloma, who understood everything about carrying things, understood that some things could only be carried walking.

"I informed on Father," Anders said at last, to the water, to the gulls, to anyone but his sibling. "Yes. You know it. Everyone in three provinces knows it, it's in the annexation files, it's why my name gets a flag instead of a berth now. But do you know what he asked me? Do you know what he actually said to me, the night before?"

"He ordered you to."

"He *tested* us." Anders stopped walking. The floodlamp above them buzzed like a trapped insect. "He sat us down — you were seventeen, I was nineteen — and he said, one of you will be approached within the month, and the family will be judged by what that one does. He said it like a weather report. Like he was telling us it would rain. And then he looked at *you*, Paloma, not me, and he said, 'And the courier will carry whatever she's given.' And I understood — God help me, I understood in that moment — that he had already chosen. I was the one to be broken. You were the one to be trusted. And I let him do it, because if I didn't, he'd have found another way, and if I refused and warned you, we'd all have gone down together, and someone had to survive to—" 

"To what?"

Anders opened his mouth. Closed it. Twenty years of answers, rehearsed in shaving glasses and queues and dark rooms, and every one of them collapsed now like wet paper.

"To survive," he said finally. "That's all I ever did. Survive. Badly. At everyone else's expense."

Paloma said nothing for a long moment. Out on the water, the *Meridian* ran her loading lamps, and the light came off the harbor in long trembling columns.

"The commission searched the manifests today," Paloma said at last, and the change of subject was so abrupt it was clearly deliberate. "They're demanding the diplomatic codes be surrendered at dawn or they board every ship and turn out every hold. Every passenger becomes a suspect. Every family on that boat becomes leverage."

Anders felt the ground tilt under him, the particular vertigo of a man realizing the trap has been around him all along. "And you have them."

"I have what I have."

"And the girl has them too. In her head. In a *song*." He heard his own voice climb and forced it down. "Paloma. Listen to me. If they board that ship and find nothing, they will tear the coast apart looking. They will go inn to inn. They will reach the Kessel Inn, and Marta will hold for an hour, maybe two, and then they will find a small girl in a cellar who sings in cipher, and—"

"I know the arithmetic, Anders. I've been doing it since the doorstep."

"Then give them to me." The words were out before he had decided to say them, and once out, they assembled themselves into shape with terrifying speed, the way the worst and best ideas do. "Give me the codes. I'll surrender them to the commission myself. My name is already in their files — a collaborator coming forward, returning state property, cooperating fully. They'll accept it. They'll clear the fleet. No boarding, no searching, no inn-to-inn. And in exchange—" his breath went ragged, "—Mira takes my berth. Family right transfers. There are forms. There are always forms; I've spent my life learning which forms forgive."

Paloma stared at him. In the floodlamp light their face did something complicated — suspicion and hope fighting for the same ground, neither winning.

"You're giving up your passage," Paloma said slowly. "The last berth. For a child you abandoned on a doorstep with an order to forget her."

"For a child I *saved* on a doorstep—"

"Don't." The word cracked like a whip. "Don't rewrite it yet. Not yet. Give me one hour where you're still the man who wrote that note, because I need to weigh him honestly, Anders, I need to know whether this is sacrifice or whether it's the oldest trick you know — hand over the codes, control the surrender, make yourself indispensable to the commission one more time, and the berth was never really yours to give, was it? Maybe flagged names don't sail. Maybe this is how you get me to destroy my own mission while you stand clean in front of the officers you've served for twenty years."

Each word landed, and Anders took each one, because he had earned every one. But underneath the flaying there was something else, and he seized it like a drowning man seizing driftwood: Paloma was *arguing*. Paloma had not walked away. Somewhere inside the suspicion, hope had gotten a foothold, and hope was doing what hope does — refusing to die quietly.

"If I wanted the commission's favor," Anders said quietly, "I'd have gone to them yesterday. I know where their office is. I've drunk tea in that office." He pulled the folded transfer form from inside his coat — he had filled it out that morning, before he knew Paloma would come, before he had any right to expect this conversation, the ink dry and waiting like a confession. "I filled this out at dawn. Before you arrived. Before I knew if you were alive. That's either proof or it's rehearsal, and I can't tell you which, because I don't know anymore myself. That's what twenty years does, Paloma. It makes your own motives a country you need papers to enter."

Paloma took the form. Read it. Their thumb moved once across the line where his signature sat — *Anders Iversen*, the name their father gave him, the name in the annexation files, the name that was now a flag and a sentence.

"The commission arrives at first light," Paloma said. "They verify manifests at the gangway. If your name is flagged—"

"It will be."

"—then this transfer may not protect her at all. It may just put her name next to yours on a list."

"I know."

"And if it fails, she's on the dock with no papers, no name that holds, and no one—"

"*You*," Anders said. "She'd have you. That was always the design, Paloma — Sera's design, maybe Father's design, maybe the whole rotten family's design running back generations. Someone carries. Someone walks the other road. We're a family of couriers; did you think the last delivery would be paper?"

The floodlamp buzzed. Somewhere down the quay, a whistle blew, and the crowd surged and settled like water.

Paloma folded the form along its crease, precisely, the way they folded everything, and put it inside their coat against their chest, next to the satchel, next to the seal, next to whatever decision was still being carried there unopened.

"Dawn, then," Paloma said. "Be at the gangway. And Anders — if this is another betrayal, I won't testify for you. Whatever happens after tonight, I won't speak your name to anyone ever again. That's the only inheritance I have left to threaten you with."

Anders nodded. It was more than he deserved, and less than he needed, and it was, he understood suddenly, exactly what forgiveness looked like in their family: a transaction, sealed, with terms.

He watched Paloma walk away down the quay, into the dark between the lamps, toward the bridge and the inn and the child, and he stood alone in the buzzing light with his empty hands at last hanging open at his sides, and he thought: *now we learn what I am.*

Down the harbor road, lanterns were moving. Many lanterns, in formation, coming fast.

The commission had left early.
