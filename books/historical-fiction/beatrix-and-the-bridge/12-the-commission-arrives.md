## 12. The Commission Arrives

Paloma had gone perhaps four hundred paces down the harbor road when they heard the boots.

Not the shuffling, sodden tread of dockworkers ending a shift, nor the scattered hurry of refugees who had learned to run only in short bursts, conserving strength the way other people conserved coin. This was cadence. Boots striking stone in unison, forty pairs or more, and beneath it the creak of leather satchels and the flat metallic syllables of officers speaking to one another in the clipped register of men who had never once needed to raise their voices to be obeyed.

Paloma stepped into the shadow of a fish-drying shed and pressed their back against the salt-bleached planks.

The lanterns came first — a double column of them swinging in time with the march, throwing moving bands of light across the wet cobbles so that the whole road seemed to be breathing. Then the column itself: annexation commissioners in gray greatcoats, their collars marked with the new insignia, the iron ring that had replaced the republic's broken star on every flagpole from here to the capital. Behind them came clerks with portable desks folded under their arms like bakers carrying trays, and behind *them*, two soldiers leading a dog that kept its nose down and worked the ground the way a needle works cloth.

They were headed for the docks.

For the *Vessa Lindholm*.

Paloma stood very still and did the arithmetic they had been refusing to do for days. The commission had been promised at week's end — that was what the harbormaster's clerk had whispered, that was the schedule printed in the annexation bulletin nailed to the customs house door, and everyone at the docks had arranged themselves around that promise the way plants arrange themselves around a predicted frost. Three days remained. Someone had decided not to spend them.

*The commission had left early,* Anders had said — no. That had been Paloma's own thought as they walked away, carried down the quay like a smell. And here it was, made flesh and gray wool, arriving in the dark between lamps exactly as feared.

The codes sat against Paloma's ribs inside the courier's coat, in the inner pocket sewn by their own hands twelve years ago. The sealed folio had not grown lighter since the bridge inn. If anything it had gained weight, the way stones gain weight in water, and Paloma understood now that this was not metaphor but physics of a kind: every day the republic grew smaller, the paper grew heavier, until the burden would exceed any shoulder designed to carry it.

They could go back. They could warn Anders.

They went back.

* * *

By the time Paloma reached the head of the quay, keeping to the shadowed side behind stacked crab pots and a rusting winch, the commission had transformed the boarding area into something between a courtroom and a slaughtering floor. Portable desks unfolded with a clatter. Lamps were hung from the *Vessa Lindholm*'s rail on hooks that had never held anything brighter than net floats. A clerk with a voice like dry paper began calling names from the manifest, and one by one the passengers queued — the queue of people who have already surrendered, Paloma thought, the queue that does not need to be formed because it forms itself, because terror is orderly long before soldiers arrive to make it so.

Anders stood apart, near the gangway, where family right had placed him for eleven days now: first among equals on the manifest, the last name with a legitimate claim to the last berth. He had changed his coat — Paloma noticed this with a strange, distant grief. He was wearing his good coat, the gray one with the intact buttons, as if he had understood before anyone else that tonight was an examination and had dressed accordingly, the way their father used to dress for tax assessments and conscription reviews: respectably. Invisibly. Like a man who has done nothing.

The lead commissioner was a woman. Paloma had expected a man — the annexation administration sent men to do its visible cruelty — but this one was tall and unhurried, with spectacles and the patient hands of someone who had spent decades with ledgers. She did not shout. She did not need the dog. She moved down the manifest line with a pencil that made small, terrible marks, and beside her a junior officer held a second document, and Paloma saw — felt it in the stomach, the way one feels a step missing on a staircase — that the second document was not a manifest at all.

It was a list of names. An older list.

"Next," said the clerk.

"Name?" said the commissioner.

"Fenn, Oskar. Fisherman. Wife, two children."

"Papers."

Papers were produced, examined, stamped. The pencil made a light mark, a mark that meant *continue*, and the Fenn family passed through the lamp-light onto the gangway like ghosts being permitted, temporarily, to remain ghosts.

"Next."

Paloma counted backward through the manifest in their head. They knew the order; they had helped the harbormaster verify it two nights ago, standing in his office while rain ticked the window, and the family-right entries came near the end because the law of the old republic — the dead, generous, stupid law — had always placed blood before purchase. Berths bought were berths confirmed, but berths claimed by kin outranked them all. It had seemed, once, like the last decent clause in a dying constitution.

"Name?"

"Iversen." Anders's voice carried across the water. It was steady. He had practiced it, Paloma realized; he had stood somewhere alone and said his own name until it stopped shaking. "Anders Iversen. Family right, entry fourteen, section nine."

The commissioner looked up from her ledger for the first time.

She looked at Anders the way Paloma's father used to look at a map — not at the place named, but through it, to the terrain around it, the rivers feeding it, the roads leaving it. Then she took the older document from the junior officer's hands, and she set the two papers side by side on her desk, and she laid one finger on each.

"Section nine," she said, "confers priority of passage upon persons of verified civic standing." Her voice was not unkind. That was the worst of it. "Civic standing is determined by record. You have a record, Mr. Iversen."

Somewhere in the queue a child began to cry and was hushed.

"The year twenty-two," the commissioner continued, reading aloud now, and Paloma heard the words come off the page in her flat, patient voice and felt twenty years collapse into a single winter night. "Informant designation V-slash-eleven. Testimony rendered before the provisional authority at Torshavn, concerning the activities of one Dag Iversen, courier, of the same address. Testimony material to the detention and subsequent—" she paused, finding the administrative word, "—the subsequent disposition of the above-named."

She looked up again.

"You informed on your father, Mr. Iversen. Under the republic, that made you a collaborator. We have been reviewing the republic's collaboration lists." She smiled without warmth, without cruelty, with nothing at all — the smile of arithmetic. "Under the annexation, we find your file *incomplete*. No follow-up testimony. No continued service. A man who informs once and then goes silent is either a coward or a sentimentalist, and neither qualifies for civic standing. Section nine is void."

The quay had gone very quiet. Even the sea seemed to lower its voice.

"You can't—" Anders began, and then stopped, because there was no end to the sentence that was true. *You can't use that against me* — they could; they had invented the list. *That was for the other side* — there was no other side anymore; both sides were the same side now, and his betrayal belonged to whoever held the paper. *My sister will tell you* — his sister was hiding behind crab pots in the dark with a nation's secrets against her ribs.

"Your berth," the commissioner said, "is revoked pending review. Step out of the queue."

Two soldiers moved toward him. Not roughly. There was no need for roughness; roughness was for people who still believed in outcomes.

Paloma watched their brother — watched him take one step sideways, out of the queue, out of eleven days of waiting, out of the gray good coat's careful invisibility — and felt something break loose in their chest that was not pity exactly, and not fear, but the thing underneath both: the animal recognition that the trap had not closed on Mira at all, had never been about Mira, but had closed here, tonight, on the person Paloma had spent twenty years insisting they did not love.

And the berth —

Paloma's mind, the courier's mind, the mind that had been trained since childhood to see systems and their seams, did what it always did under pressure. It calculated.

A berth revoked was a berth vacant. A berth vacant, under section nine, reverted to the manifest — to the next name in precedence. And the next name in precedence after the Iversen family claim, Paloma remembered with sickening clarity, remembering the harbormaster's office, the rain, the ledger open under the lamp, was a merchant transfer clause: berths unclaimed by qualified persons within twenty-four hours of sailing passed to the shipping line's discretionary list. The discretionary list. Where names appeared that had no papers, no family right, no history at all — names written in by captains for reasons of charity, bribery, or whim.

There was still a path for Mira. Paloma saw it whole in three seconds: the revocation, the lapse, the merchant list, the seal. The courier's seal, which had opened every door in Vellmar for fifteen years and which the annexation had not yet formally invalidated because bureaucracies devour slowly and digest slower.

But the same calculation ran its other column, cold and parallel: a man flagged as an incomplete informant, a collaborator under the old law and a suspect under the new, stripped of his berth on a ship about to be searched by a commission hunting missing state codes — such a man would not simply miss the ship. Such a man would be *kept*. Held for review. Held for the follow-up testimony he had so conspicuously failed to give. Held until the commission found what it was looking for, or decided it had found it.

They would take Anders the moment the queue cleared. Paloma could see it forming, could see the junior officer already writing something at the bottom of his page, could see the soldier by the gangway shifting his weight from one foot to the other with the boredom of a man waiting for a routine errand to become due.

And Paloma could do nothing. That was the shape of it, exact and unbearable. To step out of the dark was to answer questions about the seal, the satchel, the road north, the inn at the bridge. To stay hidden was to watch it happen. Every road out of the moment led through the same fire, and Paloma stood in the fish-smell and the lamplight spilling over the crab pots, and understood with total clarity what their father must have understood at some desk somewhere, some year before the end: that there are designs you set in motion knowing you will not control how they conclude.

"Mr. Iversen." The commissioner had closed her ledger. Around her, the queue shuffled forward, closing over the gap where Anders had stood, the way queues do, the way countries do. "You will remain at the docks for questioning regarding your file. You needn't be alarmed. This is standard."

Nothing about her face said *you needn't be alarmed*. Nothing about the soldier's face said it. Down the quay, past the last lamp, the harbor road ran black toward the bridge and the inn and the child asleep — if she was asleep — in Marta's cellar, and Paloma thought with sudden vertigo: *I was walking away from him when the lanterns came. I chose the child. The choice was already made before I knew I'd made it, and now the price has arrived before the morning.*

Anders was led to a folding chair beside the commission's desks, and he sat, and he did not look around the quay for his sister, which Paloma understood to be his final gift: even now, even flagged and forfeit, he did not look for the person whose looking might condemn her.

The commissioner drew the older document toward her and made a note at the bottom of it. The pencil scratched. Somewhere out on the black water, the *Vessa Lindholm*'s anchor chain shifted and groaned, counting down hours nobody aboard yet knew had grown shorter.

And then the commissioner paused.

She turned the older document slightly in the lamplight, frowning — the small frown of a clerk who has found a discrepancy, a figure that doesn't sum, a name that appears twice where it should appear once. She raised two fingers, and the junior officer leaned in, and she spoke to him too quietly for even the wind to carry it up the quay.

But Paloma had spent fifteen years reading lips across customhouses and border yards, and the words were simple, and they were only two.

*"Fetch the file."*

The junior officer set down his pen and started up the harbor road — toward the customs house, toward the archives, toward whatever lay filed under *Iversen* in the annexation's growing catalog of the republic's sins — and Paloma sank deeper into the dark behind the crab pots, heart hammering, as the truth assembled itself with the terrible neatness of all their father's designs:

Whatever was in that file, it was not the testimony of a frightened boy in the winter of twenty-two.

Someone had added to it. And the commission was about to read what.
