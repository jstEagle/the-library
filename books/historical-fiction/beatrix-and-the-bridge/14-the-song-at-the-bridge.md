## 14. The Song at the Bridge

Mira had never seen water so wide.

The river under the Kessel Bridge did not look like the streams near her grandmother's house, which you could step across on stones, or even like the canal in Halden where the barges went slowly and the boys dove for coins. This was a gray country all its own, moving without hurry under the bridge's iron legs, carrying ice in long white plates that turned and slid and vanished south. The wind came off it sideways, smelling of cold metal and old salt, and it pushed her coat against her legs and tried to push her too, as if the river already knew she was small enough to take.

She held Paloma's hand. That was not an instruction. Nobody had told her to. It was simply that her hand had been cold for a long time — since the inn, since before the inn, since her mother stopped walking beside her — and Paloma's was warm, and dry, and gripped back.

They stood at the foot of the bridge where two stone pillars rose up into the dark, taller than any house Mira had ever been inside, and between them hung the gate: a lattice of black iron, closed, with a lantern burning above it that the wind could not put out because the lantern was glass all around. A soldier would have stood there once. Today there was no one. The storm had taken the border for itself.

"Mira."

Paloma crouched down to be her height. Up close their face was tired in a way Mira recognized from her mother's last weeks — tired not of one night but of many, tired all the way through, so that the tiredness looked almost like calm. Snow had gathered on the shoulders of their coat. They did not brush it off.

"You remember what we said at the inn?"

Mira nodded. Then, because her mother had taught her that nodding was lazy and words were gifts you owed people, she said: "The song goes to whoever shows the seal. Only them. And after I sing it—" Her voice caught on what came next. She made herself go on. "After I sing it, I don't have to keep it anymore."

"That's right." Paloma reached into the courier case and brought out the seal — the brass disc in its leather cup, the crest of Vellmar worn nearly smooth by forty years of thumbs. They held it where the lantern light could find it. "Look carefully. Is this the one?"

It was. Of course it was. Mira had seen it pressed into wax more times than she could count, in the red candle stubs her mother kept in a tin box, sealing letters that always left with strangers and never got answers. She knew the shape of the crest the way she knew the shape of her own front tooth — by tongue and habit, without looking.

But she looked anyway. She looked a long time, because this was the last time anyone would ask her to look, and because her mother had told her: *When they show it, make them hold it still. A liar waves the seal about. A true courier lets you read it.* So Paloma held it still, steady as a stone, and Mira read every line of the worn crest, and the wind howled through the iron gate above them, and somewhere far behind, back along the road they had come, she thought she heard wheels — but maybe it was only the river.

"It's the one," she said.

* * *

Her mother had taught her the song in a kitchen, the way mothers teach songs.

Not this kitchen — an older one, in a house with blue tiles behind the stove, before the tiles were cracked, before they moved, before everything became moving. Mira had been four, maybe five. She remembered the song better than she remembered the house, which was the point; her mother had said so once, laughing in that low way she had, almost under her breath, as if the joke were only for herself: *Houses burn, little bird. Songs don't.*

The song had no name. When Mira asked what it was called, her mother said, "It's called Thursday," and laughed again, and Mira understood that the song was not supposed to have a name, and stopped asking. It had verses that didn't mean anything — a miller with nine daughters, a fox who paid his debts in feathers, a door with no wall around it. It had a melody that wandered, going up where songs usually went down, pausing in strange places, holding certain notes too long. Other children's songs marched. Hers drifted. For years she thought her mother had simply never learned it right.

Then came the evening — much later, another kitchen, this one with a crack running through its table like a river on a map — when her mother sat her down, put both hands on either side of her face, and said, in a voice Mira had never heard her use, flat and careful as a person laying out cups for company they feared:

"Little bird. The song is not a song."

She explained very little. That was the discipline of it, Mira understood now — understanding is a thing that can be taken out of a person, the way soldiers search your bag at a checkpoint, but a melody hides where searches don't go. What her mother gave her was this: the song must never change. Not one note, not one pause, not the places where the voice dropped almost to nothing. If she forgot a verse she must stop and start again from the beginning rather than guess. And she must sing it whole, once, only once, to a person who could show the seal — and she would know the seal when she saw it, because her mother had shown her a drawing of it until she could draw it herself in the frost on windows, in spilled sugar, in the dirt with a stick, and then had made her wash the stick-drawing away with her own boot.

"Why?" Mira had asked. Five years old, six. "Why do I have to carry it?"

Her mother was quiet for a while. Outside, that evening, there had been the sound of boots in the street, several pairs, passing and then coming back, and her mother had listened to them the way other mothers listen for rain before deciding whether to bring the washing in.

"Because they will search everyone," she said at last. "They will search wagons and coats and hems and the spaces under floors. But nobody," and here her mother smiled, and the smile was the saddest thing Mira had ever seen, and she would see it again, years later, standing on a riverbank in the wind — "nobody has ever yet searched a child's bedtime."

After that, the song was sung every night. Bath, quilt, lamp, song. It became the shape of safety itself, so that even after her mother stopped explaining anything, even after the houses got smaller and the roads got longer, the song meant: *you are in bed, you are hidden, the day has let go of you.* Mira sang it in cellars and haylofts and once, memorably, in a woodshed while men argued on the other side of the door about whether to open it. The song was her mother's voice kept in a jar. As long as she sang it, some part of the kitchen with the blue tiles still stood somewhere, warm, unburned.

And now she was going to give it away.

* * *

Paloma spread the oilcloth on the stones at the pillar's base and knelt behind it, out of the wind, and set three things upon it: the seal, the sealed packet of codes, and the courier case with its worn brass corners. Mira knelt opposite. The river spoke underneath them, enormous and indifferent, in the voice of something that had watched empires arrive and did not expect to be impressed.

"The packet first," Paloma said. "Show me where it lived."

"In the song." Mira touched her own chest, over the heart, because that was how her mother had gestured when she explained it: *not in a place, little bird. In a rhythm.* "The long pauses are numbers. The notes are letters. The fox verse is the beginning of the key and the door verse is the end, and if you sing them in order and write down what the pauses count—" She stopped, suddenly afraid she had already said too much, here in the open, at the edge of a country that no longer existed but whose ears might still be working.

"I know," Paloma said gently. "I know what the pauses count. I spent eleven years learning what your mother spent eleven months teaching you. We were trained in the same house, she and I. Different wings. Same house."

Mira stared at them. "You knew my mother?"

For a moment Paloma's face did something complicated, like paper folded and refolded until the creases become part of the paper. "I knew of her," they said. "I think everyone who worked the northern routes knew of her. She was called the Lark." A pause. "I never met her. I'm sorry for that now."

"She said someone would come," Mira offered. It felt important, suddenly, to deliver this like a message, because it was one, the last one in her keeping besides the song. "She said, *if I am not there, someone will come who can prove themselves. Wait for the proof. Don't trust the uniform. Trust the seal.*"

"And if no one came?"

"She didn't say." Mira looked down at her hands. "I think she didn't want to teach me that part."

Above them the lantern swung in its cage of glass, and the shadows of the gate's iron bars crawled over them like slow fingers. Paloma took up the sealed packet — the satchel's whole reason, the weight that had ridden against their hip across a sold country — and broke the outer wax with their thumb. Inside, Mira glimpsed paper: dense, numbered columns, a courier's clean hand. It meant nothing to her. It would have meant everything to the commission waiting downriver.

"Sing it to me," Paloma said. "All of it. However long it takes. I'll verify against the seal's cipher wheel as you go, and I won't stop you unless I must. Do you understand what happens after?"

"After—" Mira's throat was tight. "After, you burn the papers. You said. And the song—"

"The song is yours," Paloma said. "No one can take it from you, and no one can ask it of you again. Once it's given and verified, it stops being a secret and becomes a memory. Those weigh nothing." They almost smiled. "You'll be able to sing it whenever you like. To anyone. Badly, even, if you want. You can change the words."

"I don't want to change the words," Mira said, with a fierceness that surprised her. "They're Mama's words."

"No," Paloma agreed quietly. "I don't suppose you do."

Mira took a breath. The wind took it away and gave her another. She thought of the woodshed, of the cellar under Marta's inn with its smell of apples and earth, of all the dark safe places where this song had kept her like a lid keeps in warmth. She thought: *after tonight, when I sing it, I'll just be singing.* She could not decide yet whether that was a loss or a rescue. Perhaps, she thought, it was both, and perhaps that was what growing up was: things becoming both at once.

She began.

* * *

The miller with nine daughters. The fox who paid in feathers. The door with no wall.

Her voice was thin at first, thinned further by the wind, and she worried the river would eat it, but Paloma leaned close, ear bent, one hand on the cipher wheel nested in the opened seal, and Mira saw the first character resolve in their eyes — saw recognition move across their face like light under a door — and she steadied. Her mother's discipline entered her then, entering through the melody itself: *never guess, begin again, hold the long notes.* She sang the pauses full length, counting inside them the way she'd been taught, four beats, seven beats, eleven, her heart beating its own count beneath, and Paloma's fingers moved on the wheel, click, click, rest, matching pause to number, note to letter, and every few lines they breathed a word — *yes* — or touched two fingers to the oilcloth — *correct* — the way her mother used to touch her knee under the table when she recited her seasons right.

Somewhere in the seventh verse the storm found its own rhythm and began, absurdly, to accompany her — gusts arriving on her held notes, dying away in her rests — and Mira, eyes shut, imagined for one strange moment that the whole gray world was listening the way children listen to each other in the dark of shared rooms, learning the song in order to fall asleep.

She sang the fox verse twice, as required, once plain and once with the drop. She sang the door verse, and in it, at the place where the voice falls almost to nothing — the place her mother called *the threshold*, because of course she did, because of course there was a door in the song — Mira felt the tears come at last, hot and fast and immediately chilled, and she did not stop singing. Her mother had been explicit. Tears were permitted. Stopping was not. And so Mira crossed the threshold of the song crying the way her mother must have known she someday would, delivering the last of the letters, the ones that weren't written down, straight from the kitchen with the blue tiles into the cold air of a country's final night.

The song ended where it always ended, on an unresolved note, hanging.

Silence. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

Paloma did not move for a long moment. When Mira opened her eyes, she saw that the courier was crying too — silently, without changing expression, tears simply present on their face as though they'd always been there — and that both their hands lay flat on the oilcloth on either side of the cipher wheel, palms open, empty, the way hands rest on a book at a funeral.

"It's complete," Paloma said. Their voice was rough. "Every figure. Every key element. It matches the seal's derivation exactly." They looked up at Mira, and their eyes were the eyes of someone receiving bread in a famine. "Your mother built it perfect, and you carried it perfect. Do you hear me? Perfect. There is nothing in that song anymore that anyone needs from you. It's finished."

And Mira waited to feel emptied, hollowed, robbed — waited for the jar to break — but what she felt instead, rising through the tears, was something closer to the moment after a fever breaks, or the moment her boots hit the far bank after jumping the flooded ditch at her grandmother's farm: terror and flight resolved all at once into solid ground. The song was still in her. Nothing had been taken. It had only been *answered*, received, witnessed — and a witnessed thing no longer weighs on one person alone.

"Can I still sing it?" she asked. "At bedtime? Even though—"

"Especially then," Paloma said. "It's a good song. It has a fox in it."

Mira laughed — a wet, hiccupping laugh — and wiped her face with her sleeve, and Paloma drew the sealed papers toward them across the oilcloth, and the lantern above the gate chose that moment to gutter and flare, throwing their two shadows huge up the stone pillars, child-shadow and courier-shadow leaning together like figures carved on an old monument.

Paloma struck the match.

And in the sudden bloom of it — before the flame even found the corner of the packet — Mira heard what she had half-heard earlier and dismissed as the river: wheels. Real ones. Heavy, many, coming fast along the south road, with the particular rhythm of military axle and iron rim, and beneath them, faint but unmistakable across the water, voices calling in cadence.

Paloma froze, match burning down toward their fingers, and turned their head toward the sound, and Mira watched the courier's face go through recognition, calculation, and decision in the space of one heartbeat.

"They're early," Paloma said quietly, and shielded the match, and fed it the papers.
