## 15. Burn the Orders

The song ended and the wind came back.

That was how Paloma would remember it afterward — not the melody itself, though it would live in them for years, surfacing unbidden at odd hours like a splinter working its way out — but the silence afterward, when Mira's small voice stopped and the world resumed its business of blowing sleet across the foot of the Kessel Bridge. The child stood with her hands folded in front of her coat, exactly as her mother must have taught her to stand when the singing was done, waiting for something. Approval. Instruction. Absolution.

Paloma knelt in the wet gravel and took both of the girl's cold hands.

"You sang it perfectly," they said. "You don't ever have to sing it again."

Mira considered this with the grave attention she gave everything, her aunt Lysa's face arranged around a thought too old for it. "It was Mama's," she said. "Now it's yours."

"Yes." Paloma's throat ached. "And I'm going to take very good care of what it says. And then I'm going to let it go."

"Where does it go?"

Paloma looked up the river road, toward the lean of a ruined boathouse half-collapsed against the bank, its roof open to the grey sky like a ribcage. "Somewhere no one can follow."

* * *

They made camp inside the boathouse because there was nowhere else and because Paloma needed, desperately, to do one thing alone before anything else happened. Mira slept almost at once, curled on a fold of rotten sailcloth with her back against the wall, exhausted by the song the way other children were exhausted by running. Her breathing evened out into the shallow rhythm that Paloma had already learned meant safe, meant sleeping, meant trust given without reservation by someone who did not yet know the price of things.

Paloma sat facing the broken doorway, where the river showed itself in slabs between the pilings, and took out the courier case.

Their father's case. Forty years of Vellmar service worn into its leather, the corners soft as old bread, the brass clasps still catching light when there was light to catch. They had carried it across two decades and three wars' worth of borders, and inside it, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, lay the last sealed diplomatic codes of the Republic of Vellmar: eleven documents, each under the republic's black wax, each bearing the seal whose impression Paloma carried pressed into their own palm these six weeks like a stigmata.

The mission. The word felt strange now, examined. For twenty years it had been the spine that held Paloma upright — not this mission specifically, but *mission*, the principle of it, the clean arithmetic of carrying. You were given something that mattered. You carried it. Nothing else about you mattered; your hunger, your grief, the family you had walked out on in the rain — none of it weighed anything against the thing in the satchel. A courier could be anyone. A courier could be nothing. That had always been the mercy of it, and the punishment.

They unwrapped the oilcloth.

The seals gleamed dully in the last grey light, black wax over white paper, the republic's crest — the kestrel over the twin bridges — stamped deep and perfect one final time on every one. Somewhere in those eleven packets were naval frequencies, troop dispositions, the names of officials who had bought passage out with information, the whole terrible ledger of a country folding up its affairs before the annexation commission arrived to read them first. Paloma knew roughly what they carried; couriers always knew roughly. Knowing roughly was enough to make the satchel heavy. Knowing exactly, Mira's song had taught them tonight, was enough to make it unbearable.

Because here was the thing the song had shown them, sitting in the dark of the ruined boathouse with a sleeping child behind them and a dead republic in their lap: the codes were already worthless. Not worthless — worse than worthless. The fleet at the docks would be searched unless the commission was satisfied. The commission wanted the codes surrendered or the fleet held hostage, and either way the papers themselves changed nothing. Vellmar was finished. The ships would sail into neutral water and become arguments, relics, leverage for whoever held them. The codes could save no one now except whoever traded them — and everyone who traded them became, forever after, a person who had sold a country's last secrets for a berth.

Or a person who burned them.

Paloma took out their knife, the courier's knife, short-bladed, dull-handled, good for cutting cord and wax and, once, in another life, for less clean work. They set the first packet on a flat stone and held it a long moment, feeling the whole weight of the assignment settle into their wrists.

*This run is different.* They had known it at the ministry, taking the satchel from the undersecretary whose hands shook too badly to tie the cord properly. They had known it at every checkpoint, every flag flying wrong over villages that had been Vellmar since before Paloma's grandmother was born. The weight had never been paper. It had been the question they hadn't let themselves ask until a child sang it to them at the foot of a bridge: *carried for whom?*

Not for the republic. There was no republic. Not for the father, whose case this was, whose letter Paloma had found folded inside his old courier's kit like a landmine left for whoever grieved him hardest. Not for the fleet, which the codes could only endanger. Not even for the mission, because the mission was only ever a shape you poured yourself into so you wouldn't have to look at what else you were.

For whom, then?

"For nobody," Paloma said aloud, and cut the first seal.

* * *

The fire was a small one — discipline, even now, even for this; a big fire at the foot of the Kessel Bridge would draw every eye for a mile — but it caught the wax beautifully. Black wax burned with a smell like tar and church incense, and the paper beneath went up pale and eager, pages lifting orange at the edges, curling inward as if trying to hold their secrets a moment longer.

Paloma fed the fire one document at a time and did not read them.

That was the discipline, and it was harder than any border crossing they had ever made. The courier's instinct screamed with every page: *read it, know it, carry it* — because knowing was the deepest form of carrying, because a courier who memorized could lose the paper and survive the loss. Twenty years of training told Paloma to scan the frequencies, note the names, fix the cipher keys in the mind's locked room where no commission could search.

And that was precisely why they must not.

If Paloma memorized the codes, then Paloma *was* the codes, walking and breathing — and Mira had already been that, a child drilled full of state secrets like coin stuffed in a mattress, and Paloma would not build another such person, least of all out of themselves. If Paloma memorized, then the commission could never stop wanting them, and every dock, every exchange, every negotiation for the rest of their life would have a number attached. If Paloma memorized, the republic would not die; it would just move house, taking up residence behind their eyes, charging rent in vigilance forever.

Let it die, they thought, feeding in the fourth packet. Let it die the way countries should die — completely, and owned by no survivor.

The flames threw their shadow huge against the boathouse wall, and in the flicker the shadow looked briefly like their father, tall and straight-backed in his courier's greatcoat, and Paloma understood — not forgave, perhaps never forgave, but understood — that he had burned things too. His letter had confessed it: the test he had designed for his children, the betrayal he had ordered Anders to commit, the loyalty he had measured out in ruin. He had spent his whole life believing that what you carried defined you. He had died still weighing his children like freight.

Paloma was done being weighed.

The seventh packet contained the cipher key itself, the mother-key, the root of the whole system, and their hands slowed over it. Habit is a kind of love, and Paloma had loved this work. The characters marched in their familiar columns, elegant as fence rows, and somewhere in the pattern was the same music Mira had sung — literally the same, the child's lullaby mapped note for note onto the code groups, her mother's voice turned into an instrument of state. Paloma could feel their own memory reaching greedily for it, the old machine engaging, and they held the page over the flame and watched the first column blacken and curl and let the reaching go with it.

"You don't get to keep me," they told the burning paper quietly. "None of you got to keep any of us."

Behind them, Mira stirred in her sleep and murmured something — a word, maybe, or the ghost of a note — and Paloma glanced back at her small shape and felt the strangest sensation, like a door opening in a house they'd believed condemned. She didn't have to carry it anymore either. That was what the burning meant, really. Not the destruction of secrets but their release: the child freed from being a vault, the courier freed from being a hand. Whatever Mira remembered would fade now, unreinforced, unmaintained, becoming what a lullaby should be — a tune without teeth, a mother's voice instead of a nation's.

The ninth packet burned. The tenth.

The last one was thin, and Paloma knew before opening the oilcloth fold what it would be, because the undersecretary had handled it differently from the others, had set it on top with both hands as if laying a wreath. It was the republic's final communiqué — the message the government-in-exile had meant to send ahead to the fleet, whatever farewell or instruction or lie a dying state composes for its survivors. One page. One seal.

Paloma broke it and allowed themselves this single reading, because some things owed their carriers a goodbye.

It was four lines long. An order to the fleet to preserve all records for future restoration of the lawful government. Signed with a title for a country that had thirty-six hours left to exist under that name.

Paloma laughed — a short, cracked sound, swallowed by the wind — and held the page to the fire until the signature curled away from the truth of it.

Future restoration. Future. As if the future belonged to governments, and not to children asleep on sailcloth in ruined boathouses, and innkeepers who chose at last to lose something, and brothers standing in queues at docks with forged papers and shaking hands, and couriers who finally, at the end of every road, put the bag down.

* * *

The last of it was ash by full dark. Paloma stirred the embers with a length of piling, ground the fragments fine, and scraped the whole grey mess out through a gap in the boathouse floor into the river, where the current took it in a slow black fan and unraveled it downstream toward the sea the ships would sail and the countries that would never read it.

Then they sat back on their heels and waited for the grief.

It should come, they reasoned. Twenty years of identity, six weeks of purpose, the final act of fidelity to a father and a flag both long dead — surely there should be weeping, or at least the hollow ache of amputation. They had built their whole life on the carrying. What was left when the thing was carried no longer?

What was left, it turned out, was breath.

Paloma noticed it gradually, the way you notice a headache has gone. Their chest moved fully. Their shoulders had come down from wherever decades had pinned them. The satchel, empty now of everything but oilcloth and their father's case, lay against the wall like a shed skin, and when Paloma looked at it they felt not loss but a wild, almost frightening lightness, as if they had been carrying ballast all these years in the belief it was keeping them from floating away, and had only now discovered they could fly.

They breathed, and the river breathed, and the wind through the broken roof played the boathouse like a low pipe.

Mira woke near midnight, sat up, and found Paloma by the dead fire. She looked at the empty stone where the papers had been, and then at Paloma, with her aunt Lysa's clear, unsparing eyes.

"Did you take care of it?"

"Yes," Paloma said.

"All of it?"

"All of it."

She nodded, satisfied, and came to sit beside them — not asking permission, simply assuming her place, which was somehow more moving than any thanks. Together they watched the river run. After a while she leaned her head against Paloma's arm, and Paloma, who had not touched another living creature voluntarily in nineteen years, put an arm around her and held her the way they had learned to hold the sealed case: carefully, steadily, aware of the preciousness and the danger and the absolute impossibility of letting go.

"What happens now?" Mira asked, in the small voice of a child who had learned that questions about the future were dangerous.

"Now," Paloma said, "there's a ship. And a name on a list. And we get you on it if the world allows."

"Anders?"

The name landed like a stone dropped down a well; Paloma still couldn't hear its depth. "Anders sails too. That was the bargain."

"Who stays?"

Paloma opened their mouth to answer — *no one, everyone, we'll see* — and stopped, because down the river road, faint through the wind, came the sound they had been listening for since dusk without admitting it: bells. Not church bells. Dock bells, rung hard, rung in threes, the evacuation signal for early boarding, and beneath them, thinner and much worse, the whine of vehicle engines on the north approach.

Headlights swept the far end of the Kessel Bridge, two of them, then five, then a dozen, painting the span in cold white light. The annexation commission had not waited for morning. And the boarding call was ringing while Paloma still sat here, three miles from the docks, with an empty satchel, a sleeping-in-earnest child, and a bargain whose next installment was due before the tide turned.

Paloma stood, shouldered the light, light satchel, and lifted Mira onto their hip, where she fit with terrifying ease.

"Hold on to me," they said, "and don't look back at the fire."

There was nothing left of it anyway. Only steam, rising off the stones where the ash had met the rain — the republic's last secret going up as weather, falling on annexation flags and neutral water and everyone alike, belonging to no one at last.
