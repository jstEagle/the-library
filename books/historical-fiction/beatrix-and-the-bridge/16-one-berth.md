## 16. One Berth

The horn of the *Vellmar's Daughter* sounded three times, and each blast fell on the docks like a verdict.

Paloma stood at the foot of the gangway with Mira's small hand swallowed in their own, and understood that all the roads of the last twenty years had narrowed to this one plank of salt-wet wood. Above them, lanterns swung in the gale. Below, the black harbor water heaved against the pilings as if it too wanted passage. And between those two lights—the lamplight above and the dark water below—Paloma Iversen held the last free berth of the Republic of Vellmar in their fist like a coin they had stolen.

The manifest clerk had pressed the chit into their hand an hour ago, when the commission's wagons were still a rumor on the coast road. One berth remaining, he'd said, and family right would decide it, and then he had looked at Anders's name on his ledger and his face had done something complicated and sorry. Because Anders Iversen was flagged now. The commission's riders had come through the crowd an hour past with a list, reading names into the wind, and Anders's name had been on it—not as a collaborator seeking refuge but as an asset to be retained, a man whose testimony the new administration required, whose file from the old days had been pulled and read aloud by lamplight while the queue for boarding shuffled and stared.

*Ander Iversen, formerly of the Ministry of Interior. Detained pending transfer. His berth is forfeit.*

That was what the officer had said. Forfeit. As if a berth were a thing a man could misplace, like a glove, like a country.

And Mira had no papers at all. No name in any ledger, no birth record, no stamp. The annexation commission had made a country out of paperwork, and the child standing at Paloma's side existed nowhere in it. To the clerks she was not a person. She was a blank space, and blank spaces were what the commission feared most.

"Paloma." Anders's voice, behind them. Low, roughened by the sea air and by everything else. "The tide won't wait."

"I know when the tide waits and when it doesn't," Paloma said. "I was raised by tides. Father kept his boat in the cove below the house, do you remember? He said the water forgives nothing and forgets everything, and that a courier should be the opposite."

Anders came around to stand where Paloma could see him. In the lantern light his face looked older than it had that morning at the warehouse district, older than it had in twenty years of absence. His hands were in his coat pockets, and Paloma knew without looking that they were shaking. They had shaken when he spoke of the note pinned to Mira's coat. They were shaking now.

"They'll take me off the gangway," he said. "There are two of them already walking toward it. You can see their breath from here."

"I see them."

"So decide." He said it without bitterness, which was somehow worse. "You've been deciding my life since you were nineteen years old, Paloma. You decided I was a traitor before you heard one word of explanation. Decide this too. It's only fair that the last one should be yours."

Mira's fingers tightened inside Paloma's grip. She had not spoken since they left the inn district—since Marta had pushed them out the back door into the alley with a heel of bread and a blessing in a language from a country that no longer bordered anything. But the child watched everything. She watched the officers coming. She watched the gangway. She watched Anders with the particular, terrible attention of a child who has learned that adults are weather, and that survival means reading the sky.

*Forget the face,* the note had said. Someone had written those words and pinned them to her coat, and Paloma had spent nine days failing to obey them.

* * *

The officers reached the foot of the gangway and stopped there, because stopping was a kind of power. One of them carried the ledger. The other carried a truncheon, though he held it loosely, the way a man holds a tool he has never needed among civilians. The tall one—Ledger—consulted his book with the ceremony of scripture.

"Iversen," he said. Not a question. A sentence being pronounced.

"That's me," Anders said.

"You're listed for retention. Your berth—" Ledger turned the page, savoring it, or perhaps just reading slowly enough to be seen reading. "Your berth has been reassigned at the discretion of the annexation commission."

"To whom?" Paloma asked, before they could stop themselves.

The officer's eyes came up. They were pale eyes, patient, the eyes of a man who had spent the winter processing a nation and found it tedious work. "To whoever holds the chit," he said. "Family right governs reassignment. That's still the law until midnight, when none of it is law anymore. Do you hold the chit?"

Paloma opened their fist. The boarding chit sat in their palm, damp with sweat and sea spray, stamped with the seal of the Republic of Vellmar—a seal that had weighed almost nothing this morning and weighed everything now.

"I hold it," Paloma said.

"Then name your passenger." Ledger produced a pen. Held it over the page. "One name. One. And understand that whatever name you give becomes a matter of record. If you give the man, we take him into custody on the pier and the berth goes vacant. If you give the girl—" his gaze slid down to Mira, and Paloma watched him perform the arithmetic of her—her size, her silence, her lack of papers—"—then you'll need to account for her documentation, which does not exist, which makes her cargo, and cargo requires a manifest entry and a fee."

"How much?" Anders said sharply.

"More than a fleeing family has," Ledger said mildly. "Unless." He let the pen hover. "Unless someone vouches under seal. A courier's certification. There's provision for it—there was, once. A sealed officer of the Republic may certify an undocumented minor as diplomatic baggage."

*Diplomatic baggage.* Paloma felt the words land in their chest like stones dropped down a well. Somewhere far below them, in the dark, was the sound of everything this word *baggage* had ever meant: the satchel, the codes, the father's case, the song Mira had sung at the bridge with her mother's voice rising through every ciphered syllable.

"You're telling me," Paloma said carefully, "that the child can board if I spend the last authority of a dead republic on her."

"I'm telling you the law has provisions," Ledger said. "Provisions are how the law forgives itself. Whether you use yours is not my concern." He smiled without warmth. "But the chit covers one berth, and the seal covers one child, and the man is flagged regardless. So I wonder what exactly you're weighing, courier. It seems to me the scale already tipped."

* * *

It had not tipped. That was the horror of it. Every road Paloma had walked since accepting that final satchel had led here, to this pier, this pen hovering over a page—and the choice was still open, still balanced, still capable of going either way, which meant that whichever way it went would be theirs alone.

They thought of Anders at nineteen, standing in the doorway of the Ministry with the rain behind him, saying *I did it for us, I did it so they'd leave the rest of you alone*, and of Paloma turning their back before the sentence finished, because a sentence unfinished could be believed forever, and Paloma had needed a traitor more than they needed a brother. Twenty years of carrying that shape in the chest—a brother-shaped hole where a certainty used to be. And then the letter in their father's case, the confession that undid even that: *I ordered it. I tested them both. Anders passed. Paloma failed.* Their father had built the betrayal like he'd built his courier routes—deliberately, redundantly, with a fallback for every failure—and Paloma had lived inside his design for two decades without once checking the map.

And they thought of Mira asleep on the inn doorstep, snow gathering on her eyelashes, the note fluttering against her coat in the wind. *Forget the face.* Whoever wrote that note had known her face was worth remembering. Her mother had drilled a nation's secrets into her as a lullaby, and somewhere in Vellmar's last winter a person with power had decided that the safest place for that singing memory was nowhere—no ledger, no record, no face. The commission had not lost track of her. They had erased her on purpose. And erasure, Paloma knew now, was not the opposite of carrying. It was carrying with the weight hidden, the way grief hides in the body, the way codes hide in songs.

The officers' breath hung in the lantern light. The gangway creaked under the feet of the last legitimate passengers. Down the quay, a merchant vessel—the *Tern of Salden*, neutral-flagged, half-laden, indifferent to flags altogether—was taking on crates with none of the ceremony of the evacuation ship. Paloma had noticed her that morning. Had noted her master's habit of signing on deckhands who paid in goods rather than names. Had noted it the way couriers note things: automatically, against a future that hadn't arrived yet.

It had arrived.

"Paloma." Anders's voice again, very quiet now. "Listen to me. Take the girl."

"Anders—"

"Take the girl and don't say anything. Don't make speeches. I've heard your speeches since you were six years old and you always cry halfway through and I can't—" His voice broke, and he mastered it the way their father had taught them both to master things: brutally, and too late. "She's four, Paloma. Whatever else I am—whatever you've decided I am for twenty years—I know what a four-year-old is worth compared to me. Even flagged. Even guilty. Even if the guilt is mine, which I have stopped expecting anyone to believe."

"The guilt isn't yours." Paloma said it flatly, factually, the way one reports a road washed out. "Father ordered it. I found the letter. He tested us, Anders. You passed his test. I failed mine by refusing to ever test it again."

Anders stared at them. For a moment the pier, the horns, the commission, the whole dissolving republic seemed to recede, and there were only two children of the cove below the house, standing in the spray, listening to their father say that the water forgives nothing.

"He *what*," Anders said.

"There's no time."

"There's never been time. That was always his genius—he kept us running so we'd never stop to read our own mail." Anders laughed, a broken sound, and wiped his face with the back of his shaking hand. "Twenty years. Twenty years I let you hate me because hating me was the only solid thing you had after he died, and you're telling me now, on a pier, with ninety seconds left—"

"Fifty," said Ledger, consulting his watch with relish. "And the name."

Paloma looked at the chit. Looked at Mira. The child looked back, and in her face—Lysa's face, the aunt they had erased themselves from, the family line running forward through this small body like current through wire—Paloma saw the whole arithmetic of inheritance laid bare. What is passed down. Not the house in the cove. Not the name in the ledger. Not the codes, burned; not the seal, soon worthless. What was passed down was the carrying. Their father had carried his design. Their mother had carried her silences. Lysa had carried a song into a child. Anders had carried a false guilt for twenty years because someone had to hold the weight and he had volunteered, the way big brothers volunteer, stupidly and completely.

And Paloma had carried the grudge. Faithfully. On schedule. Delivering it intact across two decades like the world's most obedient courier.

No longer.

* * *

Justice outside the law. That was the phrase Paloma had been circling all winter without knowing it, and now, on this pier, in the last minutes of the Republic of Vellmar, they finally understood what it cost. The law tonight said: one berth, family right, the man forfeits, the child is cargo. The law said Anders belonged to the commission, that Mira belonged to no one, that papers made persons and seals made truths. And the law was about to die at midnight anyway, dissolving along with everything else, which meant that for one night—for this one night only—whatever Paloma Iversen did would be the whole of the law that mattered.

So be it. Then let them be the law's opposite. Let them rule unjustly by every statute on the pier and justly by the only court that would survive the winter: the one that met in the chest, at night, alone, and never adjourned.

Paloma turned to the officer.

"My name," they said, "is Paloma Iversen. Courier, first class, sealed officer of the Republic of Vellmar, number—" and they gave the number, the one etched into them during training, the one that had opened every border and closed every argument for fifteen years. "I certify the undocumented minor Mira Iversen as diplomatic baggage under the provision you cited, effective immediately, fees waived under Article Twelve."

Ledger's eyebrows rose a fraction. "There is no Article Twelve."

"There is," Paloma said. "My father wrote it. Check your copy—it's the one he made sure couldn't be repealed, because he foresaw a night exactly like this one, and he wanted his children to have a door out even if he never gave us one anywhere else." They held out the chit. "The berth goes to Anders Iversen."

"Courier." The officer's voice hardened. "The man is flagged. Taking him aboard doesn't shelter him—it makes the ship complicit. If the commission searches the fleet—"

"Then the commission will find a man who broke the law of a dead republic by trusting his brother," Paloma said. "Write it up. I'll sign it. I'm told my signature still carries."

For a long moment the pale eyes studied them—not with anger, Paloma realized, but with something like exhaustion, the look of a functionary who had processed ten thousand impossible choices this winter and was quietly relieved to witness one made badly by the rules and well by any other measure. The pen moved. *Ander Iversen, transferred berth, certified by P. Iversen, courier first class.* Then, beneath it: *Mira Iversen, minor, diplomatic baggage, Art. XII.*

"Board," Ledger said. "Both of them. Before I remember which laws I actually serve."

Anders made a sound that was not quite a word. Paloma pushed the chit into his shaking hands and folded his fingers over it, and for one second—one second only—they rested their forehead against their brother's forehead, the way they had at night in the cove house when the storms came, two children under one roof, certain the roof would hold.

"She goes with you," Paloma said. "You get her to Salden territory. You get her to Marta's cousins in Havn if the ship diverts. You don't explain yourself to her until she's old enough to ask, and then you tell her the truth, all of it, including the part where her uncle took the last berth he didn't deserve."

"Paloma, the ship—"

"The *Vellmar's Daughter* will be searched. You know it and I know it. A flagged collaborator, a ghost child, a courier's seal fresh on the manifest—the commission won't be able to help themselves. They'll tear that ship apart looking for codes that don't exist anymore." Paloma stepped back. The horn sounded again, longer, lower—the final call, no mistaking it. "But the *Tern of Salden* takes hands who pay in goods. And I have a satchel full of good leather, a coat worth money, and a seal that's about to be worthless everywhere except to men who buy neutrality by the crate."

"You're not boarding," Anders said. It wasn't a question. It was a man watching a door close and knowing, finally, whose hand was on it.

"I'm a courier," Paloma said. "I deliver. And my last delivery is standing next to you, so I'll walk her across the bridge myself, the way I was trained—carefully, and against all instructions." They lifted Mira onto the gangway's first step, and the child went, trusting them, which was the worst and best thing that had happened all winter. "Go, Anders. Go now. And when they search your ship and find nothing—when they ask you where the codes are—tell them the truth. Tell them a courier destroyed them. Truth is the one cargo no commission knows how to weigh."

Anders hesitated one heartbeat more—the heartbeat in which twenty years might have been said, or forgiven, or mended outright—and then the officers' hands were on his sleeves, guiding rather than dragging, and the gangway swallowed him, and Mira's small face turned back once from the rail, and the ropes came off the *Vellmar's Daughter* with a sound like something being untied from the country itself.

Paloma stood on the pier until the lanterns of the ship became a smudge, then a suggestion, then nothing. Behind them, upriver, the Kessel Bridge waited with its towers black against the storm sky, and beyond it, neutral ground, and beyond that, everything unwritten.

They picked up the satchel. They turned toward the bridge.

Behind them, on the dark water, searchlights bloomed one after another along the fleeing fleet—like a row of cold eyes opening—and Paloma began to walk.
