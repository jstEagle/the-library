## 17. The Choice

Anders Vey had spent forty-one years learning the exact weight of his own guilt, and so he knew, before Paloma said a word, what they had decided. It was in the set of their shoulders as they came back across the frost-blanched boards of Pier Three — that particular squareness, that courier's posture of a body carrying something it has already resolved not to put down.

"You're on the manifest," Paloma said. "The *Meridian Star*. Berth eleven."

Anders looked at them and did not understand at first. Then he understood, and wished he didn't.

"No," he said. "No. Paloma — the berth is hers."

"The berth," Paloma said quietly, "was never yours either. You were listed by family right. A dead man's claim through a traitor's name." They said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse than cruelty would have been. "The commission flagged you an hour ago. Did you feel it? The whole dock shifted. Every officer on this pier has been walking past us twice since noon, looking at your face twice as long as anyone else's. By morning there will be a warrant with your name on it, and the name will open doors for no one — least of all a child with no papers at all."

"She has no papers because I made sure she'd have none," Anders said. "I made sure of it precisely so that no record could find her. Don't you see — that was the whole—"

"I know what you intended." Paloma's voice stayed level. Twenty years of carrying sealed things had taught them to keep their voices level while everything inside them burned; Anders remembered that voice from their father's kitchen, from the night the soldiers came, from every letter that arrived after and said nothing. "I've spent three weeks deciding whether to believe what you intended. Here is what I've decided: it doesn't matter anymore. What matters is what survives. And a grown man who knows how to disappear — who has done it before, who has lived under other names in other countries and come out the far side — can survive a winter that would kill her. She's seven years old, Anders. She sings cipher she doesn't understand and she still cries when the gulls fight over the harbor refuse. She'll cross a border when she's thirty. You'll cross one by Friday."

The wind came off the water in long gray sheets, carrying the smell of coal smoke and bilge and the particular iron tang of a harbor preparing for war or surrender — in Vellmar, that final winter, the two smells had become indistinguishable. Behind them the *Meridian Star*'s engines turned over in slow, patient strokes, like a heart deciding whether to keep beating.

"And if I refuse?" Anders said. "If I simply walk off this pier?"

"Then Mira sails on a forged name on a ship I have no authority over, and I spend the rest of my life wondering if the forgery holds. Or she stays here with me, and we take our chances with the annexation commission, and they take her apart looking for codes that no longer exist anywhere but in her sleep." Paloma paused. "You won't refuse. I know you won't. You've been refusing things for her since before you met her."

That was true, and Anders hated how precisely his sister had found the shape of it.

* * *

He had sent Mira away in October, when the first snows came early to the hill country and he understood that the republic's arithmetic had changed — that children were no longer being evacuated but inventoried. He had written the note himself, sitting in a borrowed room above a shuttered schoolhouse, and the words had come out wrong four times before he got them right:

*She is nothing to you. Forget the face.*

Five words of instruction and one act of erasure. He had pinned it to her coat with hands that shook, and told himself the shaking was the cold.

It had been Lysa's idea originally, the forgetting — no. That wasn't honest. It had been Lysa's idea to make Mira into a vessel at all, to drill the codes into her daughter as though they were a lullaby, so that no satchel, no seal, no searchable document would ever hold them. His wife had done it gently; he would grant her that forever. She had sung the cipher to Mira every night for two years the way other mothers sang about sheep and stars, until the girl carried in her small head the complete diplomatic keys of a dying republic and thought of them only as the song Mamma liked. When Lysa died — fever, in the third week of November, buried in a municipal grave because the churchyards had been requisitioned — Anders inherited both the child and the terrible knowledge of what she was worth.

To the annexation commission, she was evidence. To any faction still fighting for a free Vellmar, she was the last working key. To him, she was the only thing he had ever helped create that was entirely good.

So he had walked her north through villages flying flags he refused to learn the names of, and left her on the doorstep of the Bridge Inn, where a woman named Marta had once shown his father kindness over a disputed toll, and where the storm would keep travelers stranded long enough for someone — anyone — to find her. He had chosen the inn because it stood on the road to the Kessel Bridge, and the bridge led out, and out was the only direction left that meant anything. He had not known that Paloma would be the someone. He had prayed to a god he'd stopped believing in at nineteen that it would be anyone else.

*Forget the face.* As if he could. As if he hadn't spent twenty years memorizing it in absence — Lysa's jawline, their mother's crooked incisor, the family face repeated down its generations like a signature on a document no one reads anymore.

* * *

They sat in the shadow of a cargo crane, out of the wind, sharing bread Paloma had traded for with the ship's cook. Anders ate because refusing would be theater and he had no appetite left for theater. Across the harbor, dockworkers were loading the last crates of the evacuation fleet under lamps that swung and guttered; the crates went up the gangways empty-side-heavy, and everyone pretended not to notice that half of what Vellmar was evacuating was air.

"When did you decide?" he asked.

"This morning. Maybe earlier. Maybe the night Marta hid her in a cellar and took a blacklisted name to do it." Paloma turned the bread over in their hands. "I watched a woman who has sold eggs to every flag that's flown over that inn choose, finally, to lose something. And I thought: I have spent twenty years choosing nothing. Choosing to carry and not to judge, to deliver and not to read. Neutrality as a virtue. The courier's creed." A bitter almost-smile. "Father wrote the courier's creed. I found that out too late."

"I know what's in the case," Anders said carefully. "Lysa knew. It's why she did what she did. The codes were never just codes, were they. They were his last test."

Paloma was silent for a long moment. Gulls screamed somewhere out over the black water, fighting, always fighting, over nothing.

"He engineered all of it," Paloma said at last, very low. "Even you. Even informing. He ordered it, Anders. He built the betrayal to see which of us would break faith and which would break family. And then he died and left us holding the pieces, each believing the worst of the other, for twenty years." They looked at him then — really looked, the way they had not looked at him since they were young enough to look without armor. "I'm not saying I forgive you tonight. I'm saying I've stopped being certain there was anything to forgive. Those aren't the same thing. But they might lead to the same place eventually."

Anders found that his eyes were wet and did not trouble to hide it. The wind would explain the wetness to anyone who glanced their way. The wind explained everything in Vellmar now; it had become a nation's alibi.

"Paloma. If I take this berth — if I let you do this — I need you to hear what you're actually giving me. Because it isn't rescue." He made himself say it plainly, the way he had once made himself say the other plain thing, the thing to the soldiers, the thing that broke his family. "A man my age, flagged for collaboration by two governments' worth of records, boarding the last ship of a drowned republic — I'm not being saved. I'm being sentenced. You're sending me into exile alone, without the child, without the family, carrying nothing but the knowledge that my sister bought my life with a forgery and gave away the future to pay for it. I will live decades, probably. I will live every one of them knowing exactly what this cost."

"Yes," Paloma said.

"That's not mercy. That's punishment dressed in mercy's coat."

"It's both," Paloma said. "Most true things are." They brushed the crumbs from their coat and stood. "Come say goodbye to her. She's asleep in the customs shed; Marta's cousin is watching her. You'll want to do it before the boarding call, when there's still time to lie to her gently."

* * *

Mira slept curled against a mail sack in the customs shed, under a blanket that smelled of lamp oil and other people's journeys. In the thin light of a shielded lantern her face was Lysa's face entire — the same broad calm brow, the same mouth that went stubborn even in sleep. Watching her, Anders felt the old vertigo, the sense of standing at the edge of everything he had ruined.

He knelt beside her. He did not touch her; touching would wake her, and waking her meant explaining, and there were no explanations in any language he spoke.

"Mira," he whispered, though she couldn't hear, though perhaps whispering was only for himself. "When you're older, and someone asks about your family, you tell them this: your mother was braver than every general in the republic. Your aunt Marta lost her name for you. And your—" he faltered on the word, tried again— "your cousin Paloma gave away the thing they loved most, which was doing right, in order to actually do it. None of us deserve you. That's your inheritance. Take it anyway."

Her eyelids fluttered. For one terrible, merciful second he thought she would wake, and ask him to come, and he would have to say no to her face instead of her sleeping back. But she sighed — the deep, boneless sigh of a child held safe by strangers — and turned toward the mail sack, and slept on.

Paloma stood in the doorway, waiting, saying nothing, offering him that final courtesy: the fiction that he had privacy for his cowardice.

* * *

The forgery took Paloma eleven minutes.

Anders watched from the shadow of the customs shed as his sibling worked at the merchant office of the *Ferry Queen*, a coastal trader taking on late cargo two piers down. Paloma had gone to the purser with the courier's case open, the great brass seal of the Republic of Vellmar gleaming in the lamplight, and had spoken for a while in the low fluent cadence of officialdom — Anders caught fragments: *manifest correction, family dependent, authorization under emergency maritime provision* — and the purser, a tired man who had checked too many documents in too short a life, nodded along, because the seal was real and the paper was real and the authority behind them had died only recently enough that its ghost still signed things.

Then Paloma wrote. Anders saw their hand move with the old precision, letters formed like engraving, and saw them pause once — only once — over the space where a name should go, and fill it in.

*Mira Vey. Dependent minor. Cleared for passage, coastal registry.*

Vey. His name. Their father's name. Given to her in ink by a hand that had every reason to strike it from every record in existence, and chose instead to write it one last time, beautifully, on her behalf.

Paloma pressed the seal into hot wax, held it there a moment longer than necessary — and Anders understood that this was a funeral. The last act of a courier's authority, spent not on statecraft or survival but on a seven-year-old girl's paperwork. When Paloma lifted the seal, the impression was perfect: the bridge-and-star of Vellmar, sunk forever into red wax, on a document that would carry one child out of history.

The purser stamped the manifest. Somewhere across the harbor, a bell began to ring — two bells, three, the pattern every soul in Vellmar knew by heart now, the boarding call of the last fleet of the republic.

* * *

They woke Mira together. She came up out of sleep confused, clutching the blanket, and when she saw Anders her face did something complicated — joy arriving and suspicion arriving at the same moment, the two expressions colliding and canceling each other into a solemn neutrality no seven-year-old should own.

"You're going on a boat," Anders told her. "A big one. It will take you somewhere the war can't reach. There will be a lady called Signe Tomasdottir meeting you at the far dock; she has a yellow scarf and a loud laugh and she is kind, I promise you, I checked, I checked three times."

"Are you coming?"

"Not on this boat."

"But later."

Anders knelt so their eyes were level. This was the moment he had rehearsed for two days, and every rehearsal had failed, and now he understood why: because there was no version of the truth that a child could carry, and he had sworn, twenty years ago in a different kitchen, never to lie to family again. The oath had cost him everything and kept almost nothing of itself intact, and yet.

"Mira," he said. "Listen to the song in your head. All of it. Keep singing it every night, even when you forget why. One day a person will ask you for it, and you'll know them because they'll show you a seal shaped like a bridge with a star. Until then it belongs to you and no one else. Can you keep something for me? Just keep it, safe, the way you kept it for your mamma?"

She nodded slowly. Then, with the devastating practicality of childhood: "Is it a present?"

"It's the most important present anyone ever gave anybody," Anders said, and his voice cracked on the last word, and Paloma, standing behind him with the satchel and the lantern, looked away toward the water.

* * *

At the gangway of the *Meridian Star*, an officer checked Anders's papers twice, frowned at the flag notation, and let him pass because the line behind him was three hundred souls long and the bell had rung four times. At the top of the ramp he turned.

Paloma stood below with Mira on their shoulders, the child's arms around their neck, the courier's case hanging from one hand like something that had finally become merely heavy. The harbor lamps haloed in the mist behind them. Anders raised his hand — not waving, he told himself, just raised, just acknowledging — and Paloma raised theirs in answer, and between those two lifted hands stretched twenty years of silence, one betrayal, one forgiveness given as action rather than absolution, and a forged name sailing east in a child's coat pocket.

*A sentence,* he thought, as the deck plates shivered beneath his boots and the mooring lines splashed into black water. *And a gift. And mostly a sentence.*

The *Meridian Star* stood out of the harbor at dawn, and Anders Vey stood at the rail watching Vellmar shrink — the hills he had informed on, the roads he had sold, the inn at the bridge where a woman named Marta was even now packing her single suitcase, the pier where his sibling and a small keeper of songs grew smaller and smaller and did not turn away until the mist took them.

He was halfway to neutral water, and free, and damned, when the lookout called down from the mast that a commission launch was putting out from the naval yard, running lights dark, heading directly for the evacuation fleet.

Anders gripped the rail and understood, with the terrible clarity that had been his only consistent gift, that whatever the launch wanted, it was looking for the codes — and that the only two people who knew they no longer existed were standing on a pier he could no longer reach.
