## 18. Across the Bridge

The Kessel Bridge appeared out of the snow like something remembered rather than seen — a long gray spine of stone and iron stretching across the frozen river, its far end lost in the white murk of neutral territory. Paloma had crossed it twice before in their life: once at nine, holding their father's hand, and once at twenty-two, running, with nothing but a satchel and a name they'd chosen for themselves. Both times it had been a bridge. Now, standing at its foot with Mira's cold hand swallowed whole inside theirs, it looked like a door.

Doors closed. That was what doors did.

The wind came down the river valley with nothing to stop it, carrying grit and ice crystals that stung like needles flung sideways. Above them, the bridge's iron braces groaned — not dangerously, just the sound of old metal remembering it had once been young, back when someone had cared enough to oil its joints and paint its rails. The railings were rust-brown now, peeling in long ribbons that curled like dead leaves no one had raked.

Behind them, Vellmar.

Paloma did not turn around. They knew what was there: the road they'd traveled, the villages flying annexation colors, the inn where Marta stood this morning in her doorway with flour on her hands and a black mark already settling against her name like a bird of ill omen finding its perch. They knew what was behind them because they had spent three weeks walking through it, watching a country die not with a scream but with a shrug — flags changed, signs repainted, neighbors suddenly careful about which window they waved from.

"Is it far?" Mira asked.

Her voice was small under the wind, and Paloma crouched to hear her better, pulling the child's collar up around her ears. The girl had Marta's old scarf wound twice around her neck and a coat two sizes too large with the sleeves rolled in thick cuffs, and she looked, Paloma thought, like a parcel someone had wrapped hastily in the dark and sent off without checking the address.

"Half an hour if we walk well," Paloma said. "Less if we don't stop."

"I walk well." It was said with the flat certainty of a child stating the weather. "I walked from Heldermark."

"You did?"

"With my mother. Before." Mira did not say before *what*. Children learned early, in this collapsing year, which words were trapdoors. She looked past Paloma at the bridge instead, squinting into the white. "Does anyone live over there?"

"Not yet," Paloma said. "People will."

They started forward.

The first hundred paces were the hardest, because the first hundred paces were still Vellmar — the stones worn concave by two centuries of cartwheels and boots and funeral processions, the lampposts bearing the republic's faded blue paint beneath a newer, hasty coat of gray. Annexation crews had come as far as the bridgehead sometime in the autumn; you could see where their work stopped, mid-rail, one lamp stripped of its fixture while its twin ten paces on still held its glass intact. A country being dismantled took its lamps first. Paloma had read that somewhere, or dreamed it, or lived it.

Mira's grip tightened. Not from fear, Paloma thought. From concentration. The child walked the way Paloma carried codes — eyes forward, steps measured, every ounce of attention given to the thing entrusted to her. And what was entrusted to her now? Nothing, officially. The codes were ash. The song was sung and given away, its syllables scattered like seed after harvest. All Mira carried now was herself, which was, Paloma was beginning to understand, the heaviest cargo there was.

*Carefully,* their father used to say, laying his hands flat on a courier case before sealing it. *You carry it carefully, and against all instructions.* Paloma had been eleven and hadn't understood. Instructions were instructions. You followed them or you failed. It had taken twenty years and a burned satchel to teach them what he meant: that the instructions were written for the papers, and the papers could burn, and what remained — the carrying itself, the care, the refusal to set down what mattered — that was the actual cargo, and no instruction had ever been written for it.

Now they carried a child the same way. Carefully. Against all instructions. There had been instructions, in a sense — pinned to her coat that night on the inn doorstep, in a neat bureaucratic hand: *forget the face.* Paloma had obeyed everything else the republic ever asked of them. This one order they had finally, irrevocably broken.

* * *

A quarter of the way across, the river showed itself through the gaps in the railing, and Paloma made the mistake of looking down.

The Kessel was frozen hard, white-gray ice shelving out from both banks with a channel of black open water down the middle, moving slow and patient under its breath of steam. Snow had drifted against the bridge's piers in great pale mounds. Somewhere below, fish went on living in the dark water, indifferent to borders, indifferent to republics. Paloma envied them with a completeness that startled them.

"Don't look down," Mira said, without looking down herself. Her gaze stayed fixed ahead, on the vanishing point where stone met fog. "My mother said. On the Heldermark crossing she said, look at me, not at the water, and I looked at her face the whole way and it wasn't scary at all."

Paloma looked at the child instead of the water. In the flat winter light, with her chapped cheeks and her breath streaming sideways, the resemblance to Lysa was less a resemblance than a haunting — the same stubborn jaw, the same way of setting her mouth when she'd decided something. Lysa had set her mouth that way the last time Paloma saw her, twenty years ago, standing in the doorway of the family house while the argument about Anders cracked the air like river ice. *You'll regret this,* Lysa had said. *Not today. But you'll wake up one morning and the door will be closed and you won't remember closing it.*

She had been right, which was the worst of it. Paloma had woken a hundred mornings to a closed door of their own manufacture, and had told themselves for two decades that forgetting was a mercy — that a person who erased themselves from a family spared everyone, themselves included. It had felt like mercy. It had even, some mornings, felt like peace.

Then a child had appeared on an inn doorstep with that face and a note ordering the world to forget it, and Paloma had understood in one cold instant that there was no such mercy, that faces kept themselves, that blood was a courier that never lost its delivery.

"How old are you, Mira?" Paloma asked. They realized they had never asked directly. Fragments, so far. Fragments and cipher-song.

"Almost eight." A pause, wind filling it. "I was seven when Mama taught me the song. She said it was too big for me but I would grow into it, like the coat." She plucked at a sleeve. "This is Marta's though."

"It is."

"Marta cries at night. In the kitchen. She thinks I'm asleep."

"Yes," Paloma said. "She does that kind of thing."

"She gave us bread for the road and she didn't have much bread." Mira said it as accusation, as though the world's bread supply were a moral failing to be answered for. Then, quieter: "Are we going where she can't follow?"

Paloma weighed the lie and found none equal to the moment. "Yes. For now. When things settle — and they do settle, eventually, whatever they settle into — we'll send for her. Or go to her. Borders are lines drawn by tired men, Mira. People cross them anyway. People always cross them anyway."

"Like smugglers?"

"Like couriers," Paloma said, and the child laughed — a short, surprised bark of a laugh, gone almost before it arrived, stolen by the wind.

* * *

Halfway across, they met the last people leaving Vellmar.

An old man with a handcart stacked with bedding and birdcages — empty birdcages, Paloma noticed, the birds sold or eaten or simply released, and yet he pushed them all the same, keeping the shapes of his life even when the lives themselves had been pared to cloth and wire. With him a younger woman, maybe his daughter, carrying a cat in a sack that grumbled and shifted. They walked slowly, heads down against the wind, and when they drew level Paloma stepped aside to let them pass, the courtesy of the road performed automatically, muscle memory from a country that still existed in the body if nowhere else.

The old man looked up. Looked at Paloma's face, then Mira's, then the road behind them, empty and white.

"Last ones?" he asked.

"Perhaps," Paloma said.

"They sealed the ferry at Drenthe yesterday. Soldiers on the dock, checking papers." He said it the way men report weather, or crops, or deaths in distant families. "You've papers?"

"We do."

He nodded, satisfied, as if papers were a kind of weather too — favorable winds. Then his gaze snagged on Mira, on the too-large coat, the child's face under the child's scarf, and something moved through his eyes that Paloma recognized: the quick arithmetic of a man deciding whether seeing was safer than not-seeing. He chose, visibly, not-seeing. He touched his cap brim to them both.

"Good luck on the far side," he said. "They ask questions there too. Different questions. But they ask."

And then they were past, the cart wheels squeaking, the cat complaining, the empty cages swaying — and Paloma stood a moment watching them diminish toward Vellmar, going the wrong direction, or the right one, who could say anymore. Going toward the soldiers at the ferry dock. Going home, maybe, if home was still a word with anything under it.

Mira tugged Paloma's sleeve. "Why did he bring empty cages?"

"Because they were his," Paloma said, and heard, as the words left them, how much of their own life that sentence explained. Their father's courier case, carried twenty years across every exile, opened only last week to find a letter that unmade everything they'd mourned. They had carried empty cages too. Everyone did. The carrying was the shape love took when it had nowhere left to land.

* * *

Two-thirds of the way across, Paloma heard the trucks.

Not saw — heard. The sound came up through the stones before it came through the air, a low industrial grinding that the child felt before either of them consciously registered it, because Mira's hand clamped down hard enough to hurt. Paloma turned.

At the Vellmar bridgehead, perhaps four hundred meters back, trucks were arriving. Gray trucks with canvas tilts lashed down, and soldiers spilling from them with the loose efficiency of men who had done this at six other bridges that month. Even at this distance Paloma could see the pattern of it: two to the tollhouse, two to the near railing, one already unwinding a drum of wire, another planting posts. They were going to seal the bridge. Of course they were. Every step Paloma and Mira had taken forward was a step a country took backward, and now the country had caught up to the door and was closing it behind them with wire and posts and the flat competence of occupation.

"We have to run," Mira said. It wasn't fear in her voice. It was logistics. The child had learned somewhere — from her mother, from the roads, from the year itself — that certain sounds meant *run*, and she reported the requirement the way she reported the weather.

"No," Paloma said, and was surprised by their own voice, level as a scale pan. "We walk. Look at me, not at the water. Not at the soldiers either. We're two travelers crossing a bridge in daylight with papers in our pocket. Running is a confession. Walking is a Tuesday."

"Is it Tuesday?"

"I honestly don't know anymore. Walk anyway."

So they walked, and it was the longest four hundred meters of Paloma's life, longer than any courier run, longer than the night they left home with the satchel strap cutting their shoulder, because now they measured each step not in distance but in the shrinking gap between their backs and the wire. Behind them, faintly, came the sounds of the seal being made: the ratcheting of a post-driver, the metallic complaint of wire paying out, a soldier's voice lifted in some bored order. The republic ending not with trumpets but with fence work.

Paloma kept their pace even and put their body slightly between Mira and the rearview world, the way they had once angled a satchel away from a checkpoint officer's idle eye. Carry it carefully. Carry it against all instructions. The child's hand in theirs was small and freezing and alive, alive, alive, and Paloma thought: *this is the last thing the republic will ever ask me to deliver, and it never asked, and that is why I will get it there.*

The far side came up to meet them the way shorelines do — gradually, then all at once. First the fog thinned. Then the stones changed, newer repairs, a different wear-pattern, and then the railing itself changed, painted a plain municipal green, maintained, oiled hinges on the little gate at the border line. Neutral territory began with a gate that stood open, because neutral territory had no interest in who left a dying country, only in who entered it with intentions.

A customs officer sat in a heated booth beside the gate, a heavyset woman with a tin stove and a newspaper folded to the shipping news. She looked up at the sound of their boots, took in the pair of them — gaunt adult, bundled child, one bag between them — and asked the questions the old man had promised. Different questions. Where from. Purpose of travel. Funds. Relations on this side.

"Exiles," Paloma said. "From Vellmar. We have no relations here. We intend to find work."

The officer stamped their entry slips with the boredom of a woman who had stamped five hundred exiles that month and would stamp five hundred more. "Quarantine house in the village takes families," she said, sliding the papers back. "There's soup." She glanced past them at the bridge, at the figures of the soldiers now visible at the far end, small in the snow-haze, stringing their wire. Her mouth tightened — the only crack in the official boredom, quickly plastered over. "Better keep moving. And you," she added, to Mira, with something almost gentle, "hold on tight to your—"

She paused. Paloma watched her decide what word to use. Guardian? Parent?

"Cousin," Paloma said.

The officer looked at them a moment longer than necessary. Whatever she saw, she filed it away or discarded it; her face gave nothing back. "Cousin," she repeated, and returned to her newspaper, and that was that. The gate stood open. They passed through it.

* * *

On the neutral side, the road climbed away from the river through bare birches, and from its first bend there was a view back down at the bridge entire — the whole gray spine of it laid across the white valley, and at its Vellmar end the tiny figures still working, the wire now visible as a thin dark thread drawn across the road. Sealed. Done. Twenty years Paloma had been able to go home and had not; now the option was being taken from them by men with fence posts, and the strange truth of it was that it hurt less than expected. A door held shut by your own hand is a prison. A door wired shut by strangers is just weather.

They stood at the bend a long moment, letting other exiles pass — a family with a pram, two students with rucksacks, a priest. Down on the bridge, the wire glittered. Somewhere south of it, Anders sat in a berth he didn't deserve aboard a ship that hadn't sailed, wearing Paloma's forgiveness like a coat that might or might not fit. *It is a sentence as much as a gift,* he'd said, and Paloma had let him believe the sentence was guilt. They had not told him the fuller sentence: that giving him the berth was also a wager — that the man who had once chosen the state over his family would, given one more chance, choose differently this time. Paloma had bet a child's future on their brother's spine. The wager was placed. Nothing now remained but to learn the number that came up.

"Can we rest?" Mira asked. "Just a minute."

"Just a minute."

They sat on a fallen birch at the roadside, and Paloma shared the last of Marta's bread, and Mira ate hers in small careful bites, saving the heel in her pocket the way children do when scarcity has gotten into their bones. Below them the river ran black in its channel, indifferent, borderless. The wind had died. Snow began to fall again, fine and dry, softening the bridge, the wire, the gray trucks, the whole loud century, laying a clean page over everything.

"Paloma," Mira said, around the bread. "What happens now?"

What happened now was a quarantine house and soup and a village of strangers; was learning whether the merchant manifest Paloma had forged with a dead republic's seal would hold or hang them; was waiting for news of a ship and a brother and a search that had surely begun by now. What happened now was everything, and Paloma had no way to say any of it to a seven-year-old on a fallen tree.

"Now," Paloma said, "we carry what we carry."

Mira considered this, nodded as though it were sufficient, tucked her head against Paloma's arm. Within moments she was asleep — a child's treachery, falling unconscious anywhere, anywhen — and Paloma sat still as a post so as not to wake her, looking down at the bridge disappearing under new snow.

That was when they saw the rider coming up the road from the border booth. Fast. Wrong speed for routine. A telegraph boy on a shaggy pony, leaning forward, scanning the exiles by the roadside one by one as though matching a description — and then straightening, driving his heels in, coming directly toward the fallen birch where Paloma Iversen sat with the last thing Vellmar ever asked them to carry sleeping against their arm.
