## 19. The Search of the Fleet

The news came with the milk, which was to say it came with the morning, which was to say it came too late to be undone.

Paloma had taken a room above a bakery in Halversund, the last town on the neutral side of the Kessel Bridge, because the smell of bread was the only thing that had made Mira sleep through the night. For three days they had lived there in a kind of borrowed quiet — Mira learning the baker's daughters' games, Paloma learning to sit with their hands empty, which was harder than any run they had ever made. The satchel was gone. The codes were ash. The seal, used one final time to forge a name onto a merchant manifest, had been left in a river shallows on the far side of the bridge, and Paloma had watched it sink with the strange lightness of a man setting down a stone he'd carried so long his spine had grown around it.

Then the baker's wife came up the stairs two at a time, flour to her elbows, and said: "They're talking in the square. About the fleet."

Paloma was down the stairs before the sentence finished.

* * *

The square at Halversund was small and cobbled and always half-full of carts, but that morning it had the density of a held breath. Refugees mostly, the ones who had crossed on foot in the last weeks, the ones with no berth and no papers and no country, standing in the cold with their collars up, trading news the way they once would have traded salt. Paloma moved through them with the old courier's instinct — not pushing, not hurrying, just finding the current and letting it carry them toward the loudest voice.

A fisherman was doing the telling, a broad man with a Vellmarish accent gone soft from years across the border, and he had the crowd the way a good liar does and a truthful man sometimes does better.

"Searched her stem to stern," he said. "The *Meridian's Grace*, the big one, the last out of Kessel harbor. Commission men boarded her before the lines were cast off. Every hold, every cabin, every crate of herring and every trunk of wedding linens. Four hours they were aboard. Four hours." He held up four fingers as though the number were a relic. "And they found nothing."

A murmur went through the crowd — relief, at first, the animal relief of people who had not been on that ship. Then the fisherman's face did something complicated, and the murmur died.

"Nothing," he said again. "But the commission had expected something. You don't search a ship for four hours on a guess. Someone had told them the diplomatic codes were aboard. Someone high up. And when they found nothing —" he turned his hand over, palm up, an empty gesture, "— they wanted to know why. And there was a man aboard, a Vellmarish man, traveling on a family right, one of the last berths issued — and his name was already in their books."

Paloma's hands went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the morning.

"They took him off at the pier," the fisherman said. "Quiet, no fuss. He didn't argue. I heard he didn't say a word. Just went with them, looking back at the ship like —" the fisherman searched for it and found it, "— like a man leaving his own funeral."

The crowd began to dissolve the way crowds do, the news passed from mouth to mouth, growing details as it went the way a stone gathers moss. Paloma stood in the middle of the square and did not move.

*Anders.*

They had put him on that ship with a forged name and a clear manifest and a berth he did not deserve, and they had walked away across the bridge believing that the arithmetic was done: one child saved, one brother given his chance, one courier's debt discharged. Paloma had even, in the worst private hour of the night, allowed themselves the thought that it was *clean*. That the ledger balanced.

But the commission had come looking for codes that no longer existed, and the only man aboard who might have been expected to carry them — the brother of a courier, the son of the republic's chief cipher-keeper, the man whose collaboration record had flagged him in their books years ago — could not explain where they were. Because there was nowhere to explain. *Burned*, Paloma thought. *He'd have to tell them burned, and they'd never believe it, because no one burns the codes. No one in the history of our family has ever burned anything. We carry. It's all we do. We carry until our backs break.*

And Anders, who had spent his whole life surviving by telling powerful people what they wanted to hear, had stood in front of the commission with nothing to say — because the truth was unbelievable and the lie was worse.

Mira found Paloma in the square. She had a roll from the baker clutched in both hands, and she had learned in the last three days to find Paloma in any crowd, the way she had once, presumably, been taught to find her mother. She looked up at Paloma's face and stopped chewing.

"Is it the man from the ship?" she asked. She knew Anders only as a figure on a dock, a tall man who had crouched down to her level and given her his gloves because hers were too small, and who had watched her walk away with an expression she had not had words for then and perhaps had words for now.

"Yes," Paloma said.

"Is he in trouble?"

"Yes."

Mira considered this with the terrible economy of children. She took another bite of the roll. Then she held the rest of it up to Paloma.

"You should eat," she said. "You look like the bridge looked."

* * *

Back in the room above the bakery, Paloma sat by the window with Mira dozing on the bed behind them, and did the arithmetic they had sworn they were finished with.

The facts arranged themselves with the cold clarity of a manifest. One: the commission had Anders in custody in Kessel harbor, on the annexation side, in a country that no longer existed except as a set of administrative offices. Two: the charges against him would be concealment of state materials at minimum, and his old collaboration file would be pulled, and the file would cut both ways — proof he could be useful, proof he could not be trusted. Three: the codes did not exist. Paloma had burned them. Paloma had watched the wax bubble and the paper curl and the last of the Republic of Vellmar's secrets go up the inn chimney in a column of sparks, and they had felt *free*, and the freedom had lasted four days.

Four: there was a version of events in which Anders went to whatever passed for a tribunal in the annexation zone and did not come back from it. And there was a version in which he came back, and the price of his coming back was testimony — a name, a description, a courier who had crossed the bridge on foot with a child, a face that the annexation authorities would very much like to have.

Paloma had spent twenty years believing they understood their brother. They had spent twenty years being wrong about him in alternating directions: first certain he was a traitor, then certain he was a coward, then — on that dock, with the gloves and the shaking hands and the berth he'd tried to give away — uncertain in a way that had felt almost like grief. Now the uncertainty resolved into something simpler and worse. Whatever Anders was, he was in a cell because of a choice Paloma had made. The choice had been right. Paloma would make it again with the same hands, the same certainty, the same child's cold fingers in theirs. And it had still cost a man his liberty, and the cost had simply been *deferred*, the way a courier defers a payment, and deferred costs always came due.

"You're doing the face," Mira said from the bed. She wasn't asleep after all. She did that — surfaced silently, watched, reported. "The face where you're carrying something."

Paloma almost laughed. "What face is that?"

"The one where your shoulders go up." She demonstrated, hunching dramatically, and then flopped back against the pillow. "My mother did it too. Before we —" she stopped. She had learned to stop, in the last weeks, at the edges of the country that had been her mother's; not because she'd forgotten, but because she had decided, with a child's iron sovereignty, when to spend the memory and when to keep it. "Before," she finished.

"Mira." Paloma turned in the chair. "Do you remember the man from the dock? The one who gave you the gloves?"

"Anders."

"What did you think of him?"

Mira frowned at the ceiling, taking the question with the seriousness she took all questions, as though somewhere an examination were being administered and she intended to pass it. "He was sad," she said. "But he was trying to be not-sad, which is worse. My mother said you should just be sad when you're sad, because pretending takes up the room the sad needs." She rolled onto her side. "Are you going to go back for him?"

Paloma had not said a word about going back. Had not said a word about anything, in fact, since the square. "Why would you ask that?"

"Because you have the face," Mira said, "and because when we were crossing the bridge, you kept looking back, and I thought it was at the bridge, but it wasn't. It was at the ship. You look at things the way I look at my mother's song. Like you gave it away but it's still in you."

The room was very quiet. Below, the baker's ovens were being raked, and the smell of the first loaves came up through the floorboards like a tide.

"Mira," Paloma said carefully, "if I went back, you would stay here. With the baker's family. You'd be safe here."

"I know," Mira said, as if this were the least interesting part. "But you'd be on the other side of the bridge, and the bridge closes at night, and you'd be carrying something again, and you're bad at putting things down. I watched you try with the satchel. You kept reaching for it in the night and it wasn't there and you woke up every time." She said it without accusation, the way she said everything, and it went through Paloma like a courier's knife. "So you'll go back. I already knew. I just wanted you to say it."

Paloma looked at this child — six years old, perhaps seven, carrying a nation's cipher in a lullaby and her dead mother's wisdom in her mouth — and felt the full weight of the thing they had been refusing to feel since the square: that they had not escaped the family trade at all. That the codes had burned, but the carrying had not. That some inheritance is not a document but a disposition, and Paloma Iversen had it in the marrow, and so, God help her, did the child on the bed.

"Yes," Paloma said. "I'm going back."

* * *

The preparations took a day, and every hour of it Paloma spent arguing with themselves in a voice that sounded, increasingly, like their father's.

*You'll accomplish nothing.* The voice was reasonable. The voice had always been reasonable; that had been the horror of it. *You'll be detained at the checkpoint. Your face is known — the commission will have the innkeeper's description by now, the bridge garrison's, half of Kessel's. You'll trade one prisoner for two and the child will be an orphan twice over, and for what? Anders made his choice on that dock. He took the berth. He knew what it was.*

But that was the thing, wasn't it. He had taken the berth *knowing it was a sentence as much as a gift* — Paloma had seen him understand it, had seen the knowledge move through his face like weather — and he had taken it anyway, and he had put Mira's name where his own had been, and he had stood on the deck of the *Meridian's Grace* and watched them walk away, and he had said nothing. He had not said *wait*. He had not said *the codes, at least, tell me where the codes are*. He had stood there in his dead father's coat and let them go, and Paloma had spent four days telling themselves that silence was peace, when it might just as easily have been a man swallowing a question he knew the answer to and would not make his sister carry aloud.

*Or,* said the other voice, quieter, the one that had been getting louder since the bridge, *he knew exactly what they'd ask him, and he decided in that moment that the codes were worth his life, and he has been sitting in a cell for two days keeping that decision with his teeth.*

Paloma packed the way they had packed for twenty years: little, fast, and in the dark of their own understanding. Bread from the baker's wife, who asked no questions and pressed a second loaf on them and said only, "Bring him back, then, if he's yours." A map of the annexation zone drawn from memory. And at the bottom of the bag, wrapped in a shirt, the one thing they had kept from the burning: nothing. A space where the seal had been. They had left it in the river, and they were glad of it, and they were afraid of it, and both of those were new.

Mira stood at the bakery door in the grey of the next morning, wearing the gloves, which were still too big.

"You'll come back across," she said. It was not a question. She had learned interrogative certainty from someone; Paloma suspected, now, that it had been drilled into her along with the cipher, a courier's child down to the grammar.

"I'll come back across."

"And you'll bring him."

"I'll bring him if I can."

Mira studied them for a long moment, weighing the hedge, deciding whether to spend a memory on it. Then she held out her hand, palm up, formal as a little magistrate. "Then you have to take the oath."

"What oath?"

"The courier oath. My mother's. You put your hand on mine and you say *what I carry, I deliver*." Her chin was trembling, very slightly, and her eyes were not, and Paloma understood that this was the child's way of not being sad, and that her mother would have told her to stop pretending, and that Paloma was not going to be the one to tell her.

Paloma knelt on the bakery step in the cold and put their hand on the small one, and said, "What I carry, I deliver."

"And you won't put it down."

"And I won't put it down."

Mira nodded once, satisfied, the contract executed, and then — before Paloma could rise — she threw her arms around their neck and held on with the whole desperate weight of her, and said into their ear, in a voice that was not a magistrate's at all but a very small girl's on a very cold morning: "Come back. Both of you. I'm keeping your face."

Paloma held her until the baker's wife's hand came gently onto both their shoulders, and then they stood, and shouldered the bag, and walked down through Halversund toward the river, where the Kessel Bridge stood in the morning fog with its garrison lights burning, and the country on the far side of it was closing like a hand.

Behind them the bread smelled like a world that still worked. Ahead, the fog took the bridge and kept it.

Paloma walked into it, carrying the only thing left to carry: a promise, a brother, and a child's oath — *I'm keeping your face* — which was, they understood now, the one order they had never been able to obey, and never would.
