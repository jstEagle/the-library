## 20. Testimony

The garrison at the foot of the Kessel Bridge let Paloma back across without a fight, which was the first sign that the country had already finished dying. A war closes like a fist; an annexation opens again too easily, the way a door does when no one is left inside to bar it. The sergeant of the watch looked at Paloma's courier seal — the last true stamp of the Republic of Vellmar still moving through his hands — and waved them through with the weary courtesy of a man who had stopped believing that anything crossing his bridge could matter.

"Business?" he asked, because the form required it.

"Confession," Paloma said.

The sergeant did not laugh. That was the second sign. Twenty years ago a word like that on a border would have bought a search, a cell, a set of questions asked under lamplight. Now the man only nodded, as if confessions came across the Kessel Bridge every morning with the fog, and stamped a slip of paper that meant nothing, and pointed down the quay toward the fleet.

The evacuation ships were still there. That surprised Paloma more than anything else — that the commission had not sailed, that the *Meridian* and her two sister vessels still sat against their moorings in the gray water, funnels cold, gangways watched, the whole harbor held like breath. They had come back expecting wreckage. What they found instead was a machine that had simply stopped: crates half-loaded on the dock, a child's boot abandoned by a bollard, the manifest office burning one lamp against the early dark.

And Anders. Anders was not in a cell.

They found him in the warehouse beside the pier, sitting on a crate of Vellmar's archived maps, under guard but unshackled, hands flat on his knees like a schoolboy called before a magistrate who has not yet decided his name. He looked older than he had five days ago at the docks. Or perhaps Paloma had simply never looked at him properly until now; grief wears people differently depending on where you stand.

When he saw them he stood up so fast the guards' hands went to their belts, and then he stood very still, and said nothing, and that silence told Paloma everything the news from the bridge had not: he had not talked. He could not talk. He did not have the codes, and he would not invent them, and the commission did not know what to do with a man accused of carrying secrets that refused to exist.

"You shouldn't be here," Anders said hoarsely. "Paloma. You shouldn't have come back."

"Yes," Paloma said. "I know."

* * *

They gave Paloma tea, because bureaucracies are the same in every century and every collapse: there is always a form for detention, always a queue for interrogation, always, inexplicably, tea. The annexation commission had requisitioned the harbor master's offices, and the harbor master's offices smelled of tar and old ledgers and someone's attempt at normalcy. Three officers sat behind a table that had been turned so its scarred face faced the room. The middle one — a woman with a gray coat and the patient eyes of someone who had spent her career counting other people's lies — introduced herself as Commissioner Vael, and set a single sheet of paper in front of her.

"The evacuation vessel *Meridian*," she read, "was to carry four hundred and eleven souls out of annexed territory. Under the terms of surrender, all diplomatic materials are forfeit to the Commission. Her passenger manifests were verified twice. Her cargo holds were searched once, thoroughly, and were about to be searched again." She looked up. "Because of your brother."

Paloma said nothing. It was an old courier's discipline: never fill a silence that is working for you.

"He is listed here," Vael continued, tapping the sheet, "under family right — berth reserved by bloodline, the Republic's own sentimental statute, may it rest. And he is flagged in our records. Anders Iversen. Informant, year nineteen of the Republic. The man who gave up his own father." She folded her hands. "A collaborator, we would have said, in our favor. A loyal son of Vellmar, in yours. Either way — a man who knows how to trade information. And now the codes he was trusted to carry out of the country cannot be found. You see our difficulty."

"I see it," Paloma said.

"Then help us with it. Where are the codes?"

"In my head," Paloma said, "and then nowhere at all. And then nowhere at all is where they will stay."

Vael waited. The lamp hissed. Somewhere out on the water a gull made its one argument and gave up.

"I am Paloma Iversen," Paloma said, and heard their own voice do something strange — settle, the way a load settles when the strap is finally cinched right after miles of shifting. "Courier, fourth grade, Republic of Vellmar, commission number seven-three-one. I accepted the final diplomatic pouch at Halversund on the ninth of Frostmonth. I carried it north. My brother carried nothing. He never saw the pouch. He was given his berth by family right and he took it for the reason anyone takes a berth — because he wanted to live. There is no crime in that. There is barely even a sin."

"And the codes?"

"On the night of the twelfth, at the foot of the Kessel Bridge, I opened the seals, memorized the contents, and burned the documents in the brazier at the toll house. Then I walked into neutral territory and forgot what I had memorized. Not pretended to forget." Paloma met the commissioner's eyes. "Forgot. I have spent twenty years learning how to put things down and leave them behind me. This I put down deliberately. Your men can search every ship in this harbor, every crate, every coat lining, every body cavity of every passenger, and they will find nothing, because there is nothing. There is only a dead republic's arithmetic, and arithmetic does not burn."

It was mostly true, which is the only kind of testimony that survives an expert listener. The codes had been sung, not read — a child's lullaby drilled into Mira by a mother who understood that paper burns and songs get carried. Paloma had verified the cipher against the seal at the bridge's foot and then, yes, let it go — not forgotten, exactly; no one forgets a child singing in the fog. But emptied of it. Surrendered it the way you surrender a held breath. The distinction mattered to no one in this room, and Paloma intended to keep it that way. Mira's name appeared nowhere in this story. Marta's cellar appeared nowhere. The song had died with the singer's permission, and that was the whole of it.

Commissioner Vael studied them for a long moment. "You understand what you're saying."

"Yes."

"You are telling me that the Republic of Vellmar's final cryptographic material was destroyed by its own courier, in violation of the surrender terms, and that the man we have been holding for six days is innocent of everything except having the wrong name."

"And the right brother," Paloma said. "Yes."

Vael leaned back. "Why?"

It was the first honest question of the evening, and it deserved better than procedure. Paloma thought about lying anyway — about some clean motive, duty or mercy or spite — but the room was small and warm and they were so tired, and there is a limit to how long a person can carry a thing without ever once setting it on a table.

"Because twenty years ago," Paloma said, "my father sent my brother to inform on him. On purpose. To test us. I found his letter — his last undelivered letter, in his own case, in his own hand — and I learned that the ruin of my family was a design, not an accident, and that I had spent two decades punishing Anders for obeying the same man I mourned. Someone in my family has been answering for my father's cleverness since before I was born. I decided it ends tonight. With me. In this room. In whatever cell you have that faces the water."

Silence. The gull again, further off, arguing with nobody.

Commissioner Vael looked down at her paper, and up, and something moved behind her patience — not sympathy, exactly. Recognition. The look of a woman who had also spent a career carrying things for institutions and knew precisely how heavy paper got.

"You'll sign it," she said. It was not a question. "Full confession, sole responsibility, exculpating Anders Iversen completely. And you understand the consequences. Detention pending tribunal. Loss of passage — permanent. When the exchange lists are drawn, your name goes to the bottom, and the bottom may not sail for years. If ever."

"I understand," Paloma said.

"One more thing." Vael turned the sheet over, and her voice lost its official register entirely, dropped to something almost private. "The codes themselves. Whatever they were worth — to us, to your Republic-in-exile, to whoever pays best in this new country — you burned them rather than sell them, rather than trade them, even now. Even for your brother, you could have offered them. You chose ash." She paused. "I'd like to know why that, specifically."

Paloma thought of Mira at the bridge's foot, small in the fog, singing her mother's voice into the dark. Thought of the way the child had given it up — solemnly, terribly, like communion — and how Paloma had received it and then refused to become the vault everyone had designed.

"Because a country is not its secrets," Paloma said. "I believed that for twenty years without knowing it. Now I know it. That's all the answer there is."

* * *

They signed at dawn.

The confession ran three pages in a clerk's careful hand, and Paloma read every line, and corrected one — the date, which the clerk had taken from Anders's arrest record rather than the actual burning — and then wrote their name beneath it all in the round, deliberate script of a person taught that a signature is a promise. The pen scratched. Outside the window the harbor was turning from black to iron to silver, and the *Meridian*'s funnel coughed its first smoke in six days, and Paloma watched the plume rise and thought: *she sails today*, and felt the strangest sensation, grief and relief braided so tightly together they could not be told apart, like two strands of one wire.

They let Paloma see Anders for ten minutes before he boarded.

He came into the little room rubbing his wrists where the shackles had been, and for a moment neither of them spoke, and then Anders crossed the floor and did the thing he had never once done in their entire shared childhood: he put his arms around his younger sibling and held on.

"I don't accept this," he said into Paloma's shoulder. His voice was wrecked. "You understand that. I don't accept it. You take my place on a ship and call it forgiveness and I have to live the rest of my life knowing what it cost—"

"That's rather the point," Paloma said, muffled. "Living is the cost. Living with it. I had a berth too, once, in a manner of speaking. I gave it up twenty years ago when I walked out of Lysa's house and never came back. I've simply made it official."

Anders laughed — a broken, wet sound — and pulled back and gripped Paloma's shoulders hard enough to bruise. "The girl. Tell me about the girl."

"She's across the bridge. Safe. Fed. Singing songs that are only songs now." Paloma hesitated. "She has your aunt's face, Anders. Our aunt's. And she laughs like Father did, before any of it. I'm keeping her face. It's the one order I intend to go on disobeying."

Anders nodded, jaw working. Then, at the door, with the guard waiting, he turned back, and what he said was quiet and terrible and Paloma would carry it longer than any code:

"When they ask you why — the tribunal, whoever comes after — don't say you did it for me. Say you did it because someone had to stop paying for Father. Let it be mine to deserve. I'll spend the voyage earning it. That's the bargain I'm making with you whether you agree or not."

Then he was gone, down the corridor, across the dock, up the gangway of the *Meridian*, a small figure in a gray coat among four hundred and ten others, and the gangway came up, and the lines were cast off, and the ship leaned away from the annexed world like a person leaning out of a burning house to breathe.

Paloma stood at the barred window of the harbor office and watched her go, all the way out past the breakwater, all the way to where the fog took her, the same fog that had taken the bridge, the country, the codes, the name of the republic — everything, everything handed over to the gray — and felt the satchel-strap of their whole life finally, fully slack.

* * *

The tribunal took ninety minutes. The detention order took five.

They processed Paloma in a room that had once been a customs hall, fingerprinting them with the bored tenderness of clerks handling one more anonymous unit in a ledger that would outlast all of them. An officer with a rubber stamp asked their occupation for the record.

"Courier," Paloma said.

"Of what state?"

"None," Paloma said. "Formerly of one."

The stamp came down. *DETAINED — PENDING EXCHANGE REVIEW.* The word *pending* was doing quiet, heroic work in that sentence, and everyone in the room knew it. Annexation commissions did not hurry to free people who confessed to destroying valuable things; a confession like Paloma's was an asset, and assets were stored, not spent. The exchange lists between the annexation authority and the neutral territories across the Kessel Bridge moved at the speed of spite. Some detainees waited years. Some waited out the era entirely and emerged into a country that had changed names around them while they stood still.

They put Paloma in a wing of the old fortress above the harbor, in a cell with a window too high to see out of but angled enough to hold a strip of sky, and a cot, and a Bible some previous occupant had left annotated with furious marginalia about the book of Exodus. Paloma read Exodus. It seemed relevant. A people walking out of a closed country, carrying nothing they were permitted to carry.

The days arranged themselves into a shape that should have been torment and somehow was not. Paloma woke when the garrison bell rang, ate what was brought, walked the yard in the hour they were allowed, and in the evenings lay on the cot and practiced, systematically, the discipline of a lifetime: putting things down.

They put down the mission. That was easiest; it had ended in ash at the bridge's foot and had never truly belonged to them anyway — it had belonged to a dead man's design, and the design had failed, and there was a fierce, unchristian satisfaction in that.

They put down the republic, slowly, province by province, the way you fall asleep: Halversund and its bread-smelling streets, the checkpoint officer who had traded his glance at the seal for a kindness, the villages flying flags they didn't believe in, the whole slow erosion of a country that had run out of prices it could pay. Gone. Carried nowhere now except in a detainee's memory, which was, Paloma reflected, the safest archive left.

They could not put down Mira. They did not try. Some weights are not burdens; some are ballast, and the boat needs them.

And Anders — Anders they carried differently now. Not as a betrayal to be rehearsed each night, the way it had been carried for twenty years, but as a debt actively being paid, a sentence being served in parallel: his on the open water, bound for exile and whatever reckoning waited there; theirs behind stone, bound for nothing yet. Two siblings, both serving time for a father's test, each taking the other's portion. It was not forgiveness. Forgiveness, Paloma was coming to understand, is a feeling, and feelings cannot be sentenced. It was its action. It was the doing of it, daily, in the absence of the feeling, the way a courier walks a road in the absence of certainty.

In the third week, a guard — young, annexation-born, incurious in the way that sometimes looks exactly like mercy — began leaving newspaper scraps on the tray. Shipping news mostly, which the censors allowed because it was dull. Paloma read every column twice.

In the fifth week, one of those scraps mentioned, in eight lines buried below a freight rate dispute, that the neutral territories across the Kessel Bridge had concluded a prisoner and civilian exchange framework with the annexation commission, first lists to be drawn within the quarter, priority given to — Paloma read it four times to be sure the words would stay still — *family reunification cases involving minors.*

Minors. Family. Across the bridge.

Marta's blacklisted name. Marta, who had chosen at last to lose something, and lost it, and packed up her inn and her ordinary courage and — the baker's wife had said it, hadn't she, in Halversund, in another life five weeks ago — *gone where the girl went.* A minor, in a reunification case, on the far side of the Kessel Bridge, with a face Paloma had sworn to keep.

Paloma sat on the cot with the newspaper scrap trembling faintly in their fingers, in the cell with the strip of sky going gold at the edge of the window, and understood that the lists were being drawn, and that names were being weighed, and that somewhere in a harbor office a commissioner with patient eyes had a confession in her files with one name at the bottom of it — and that bottom, at last, might move.

Footsteps in the corridor. Keys. The slot in the door slid open, and the young guard's voice, carefully blank:

"Iversen. You've got a visitor."
