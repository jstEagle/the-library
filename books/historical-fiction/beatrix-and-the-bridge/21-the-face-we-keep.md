## 21. The Face We Keep

Mira counted the days by the bread.

Not by the calendar on Marta's wall, which was a year out of date and had been printed in Vellmar before the word *annexation* meant anything more than a lesson in history books. Not by the bells of the town across the water, which rang for a republic that no longer existed and then, without apology or explanation, began ringing for something else. She counted by the bread, because every third day Marta baked, and on the days she baked, the whole house smelled like the inside of a warm mouth, and Mira would stand in the kitchen doorway and let the smell find her the way light finds a room through a keyhole — narrowly, but completely.

Every three days was one loaf. Every loaf was three days. She had been at the house on the neutral side of the Kessel Bridge since the first hard frost, and when she counted the loaves backward, it came to one hundred and eleven days since Paloma Iversen had been taken away in a gray coat with their hands showing the commission officers that they were not holding anything at all.

One hundred and eleven days. Thirty-seven loaves.

She knew other numbers too. She knew them the way she knew the song — without wanting to, without being able to stop. She knew that Marta's cellar had held her for nine nights while the officers came twice. She knew that the blacklisting notice had named Marta Ostrand *inimical to the orderly transfer of sovereignty*, seven words that cost Marta an inn, a license, a name on a register, and gained her nothing except a girl who could not sleep unless someone else was awake in the house. She knew the exchange had happened on a Tuesday, at the bridge's midpoint, in the rain, twelve prisoners walking south and eight walking north, and that Marta had stood at the rail of the footpath for six hours waiting to see whether a gray coat would come down the span or turn back.

Mira had not gone to watch. That was the part nobody understood, the part Marta told her gently, over and over, that she did not have to explain. But Mira remembered everything now — that was the trouble, that had always been the trouble — and she remembered what her mother had said about watching: *never watch the thing you cannot survive seeing*. So she had stayed home and kneaded bread dough with her fists until her shoulders burned, and when Paloma finally walked through the door at dusk, thin as a winter branch, smelling of river and iron and someone else's soap, Mira had been so covered in flour that they laughed — the first thing either of them had done in one hundred and eleven days — and said, "Well. At least one of us is white as the dead," and then stopped laughing because it wasn't funny, and then hugged anyway, which was funnier than anything.

That had been four loaves ago. Twelve days.

Tonight Marta was baking again, and Mira sat at the table doing the thing she did every night now, the thing that had started as a game and become a craft and was becoming, though she didn't have the word for it yet, an inheritance: she was copying addresses.

The letters came from everywhere. From the annexed provinces, slipped across the bridge in fishermen's boots and hymnals and hollowed cheeses; from refugee rows on the neutral side where Vellmaris slept four to a bed and dreamed in a language the new maps refused to print. Families torn open like stitched seams, everyone pulled to a different side of some line. And there were people — more of them than anyone would have guessed, more every month — who would pay anything, risk anything, for the small impossible miracle of a letter that crossed.

Paloma had explained it once, sitting where Mira sat now, turning a sealed envelope over in fingers still scarred from detention: "A courier doesn't carry paper, Mira. Paper is just what the carrying leaves behind." They had said it lightly, the way they said most true things, and then had gone quiet, and Mira had understood that the sentence was also about her — about the song, about the codes drilled into a child's skull by a mother who had known she might not live long enough to deliver them herself. Paper burns. Carrying survives.

She copied an address onto a slip in her careful hand — *Ansel Brandt, fish market row, third door past the cooper's* — and thought about how strange it was that names could be treasure. Somewhere behind her, in the province that used to be Vellmar and was now only ink on a treaty, a woman was waiting to hear whether her sister had survived the winter. The woman couldn't cross. The sister couldn't write openly. But a slip of paper no bigger than a prayer card could go into Marta's false-bottomed flour bin, and from there into a fish cart, and from there across the Kessel Bridge in the pocket of a girl too young to be interesting to anyone, and the two women would be joined again by a thread thinner than thread.

Mira was that thread now. She carried things. It was, she was discovering, what her family had always done — not the codes, not the seals, not the betrayals, but the carrying itself, the stubborn insistence that what mattered must move from one hand to another even if the world forbade it.

"Mira." Marta's voice from the kitchen, roughened by oven heat. "You'll set the third place?"

Third place. The words did something to her chest that she pretended not to notice, because noticing made it worse, and worse meant crying, and she had decided — age ten, in a cellar, listening to an innkeeper lie beautifully to armed men about an empty room — that she was finished with crying for reasons this small.

* * *

Paloma came home at dark, the way they always did now, shaking rain off a coat that had been mended so many times it was less a garment than a geography of repairs. They moved differently since the detention camp. Slower on the stairs. Careful with their left hand. But when they smiled at Mira across the table — and they always smiled first at Mira, before Marta, before the fire, before anything — the smile arrived intact, unguarded, exactly as it had on the doorstep of a bridge inn on the worst night of Mira's life.

"Anything?" Marta asked, meaning the drop point by the millrace.

"One." Paloma set it on the table between the salt and the lamp: a letter, damp at one corner, addressed in a hand that shook. "Old man Hessler. He wrote to his daughter in Tarnow. Took him four months to find someone going our direction."

"Four months," Mira said.

"That's how it goes now." Paloma sat, winced slightly, settled. "A letter takes a season to travel forty miles. The republic used to promise next-day delivery to every provincial capital." They said it without bitterness, which somehow made it heavier. "Empires are fast at everything except the things that matter."

They ate. This too had become liturgy: Marta's bread, a stew of beans and the smoked fish the fisherman Kroll brought in trade for silence about his routes, the lamp turned up high because all three of them had spent enough time in dim places to have earned brightness forever after. Marta talked — Marta always talked — about the widow upstairs, about Kroll's rotten politics and excellent herring, about the price of flour under the new administration, which she reported with the grim satisfaction of a woman who had predicted the price and hated being right.

And then, in the pause after the plates were cleared, Paloma said the thing they had been carrying home in their pocket along with old man Hessler's letter.

"There's news from the fleet side."

Mira's hands went still around the dish towel.

"The prisoner lists were posted in the annexation gazette. Routine. Most of them are names I don't—" Paloma paused, and Mira watched them choose, deliberately, slowly, the way her mother used to choose words when the truth was a heavy object being set down on a fragile surface. "Anders testified. In his own hearing. He told them he never saw the codes. That I destroyed them alone, before the docks. That he'd been trying to reach me for weeks to arrange exactly that, and failed, and lied to the commission about why."

"You knew he'd testify," Marta said quietly. "You told them yourself—"

"I told them what happened. He told them what didn't." Paloma turned their cup in their hands, a full rotation, another. "He's released. End of the month. Deported to the neutral side — they don't want collaborators back, even ones who recant. Even ones whose collaboration consisted, apparently, of informing on a father who had ordered him to do it."

The sentence hung there. Mira looked at it the way you look at something dropped from a great height, wondering if it will break or bounce.

"He ordered him," she said. "Grandfather ordered him."

"We found the letter, yes." Paloma's voice was very level. "In the case. After everything."

"And Anders obeyed. To protect you."

"To protect us." A muscle moved in Paloma's jaw. "And I let him carry that alone for twenty years because I preferred my grief simple. Yes. I've done the arithmetic."

Mira dried a plate. Put it in the cupboard. Picked up another. Her mother had taught her that hands could think when heads couldn't, that kneading or folding or wiping was a way to hold still while something enormous rearranged itself inside you.

"He's coming here," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"He has nowhere else. He has no papers worth the ink, no trade the neutral authorities recognize, and one relative on this side of the water, who owes him—" Paloma stopped. Started again. "Who owes him a berth, as it happens. Though this one comes with a roof instead of a sea."

Marta snorted. "This house gets fuller and fuller. At this rate I'll need to take up blacklisting as a recruitment strategy."

But Mira saw her face when she said it, the softness under the gruffness, the face of a woman who had profited from every regime until the day she chose to lose something, and lost the wrong thing entirely — an inn, a country, a legal existence — and had never once, in one hundred and twenty-three days, mentioned any of it as a debt.

"When?" Mira asked.

"A week. Maybe two. There's processing." Paloma looked at their own hands, the scarred knuckles, and Mira realized with a jolt that they were afraid. Paloma Iversen, who had stared down checkpoint officers and commissions and the whole collapsing machinery of a state, was afraid of sharing a table with their brother. "Twenty years is a long time to rehearse a conversation you never got to have."

"He'll be nervous too," Mira said.

"I know."

"He'll bring something. People bring things when they're ashamed."

Paloma looked up at her — that look, the one that had undone her completely on the first night, the recognition that traveled both directions at once — and something eased in their shoulders. "What could he possibly—"

"Nothing," Mira said. "It doesn't matter what. It matters that he needs to."

* * *

She woke in the night and lay listening to the house breathe: Marta's snores through the wall, the tick of the stove cooling, rain starting on the roof tiles. Below her, faintly, the sound of the lamp being relit and pages turning — Paloma, keeping the vigil they kept every night, reading whatever letters had accumulated, learning addresses the way priests learn psalms.

Mira slid out of bed and went down the ladder to the main room, barefoot on cold boards.

Paloma glanced up, unsurprised. They were rarely surprised by her anymore; it was one of the quiet mercies of their arrangement, this mutual lack of astonishment at each other's midnight habits. "Bad dream?"

"No. Good dream. That's why I'm up." She climbed onto the bench across the table. "I dreamed about my mother's voice. Singing."

Paloma went still.

"Not the cipher," Mira said quickly. "Or — yes, the cipher, but underneath it. She put it under a real lullaby, did you know? About the heron on the marsh? So even when I sang the numbers, I was singing about a bird going home." She picked at a knot in the tabletop. "I keep thinking I should forget it now. It's delivered. It's verified. It's ash. Nobody needs it. But I can't, and I've stopped trying, because here's the thing I figured out: the numbers were never the song. The song is that she wanted me safe and chose the safest hiding place she had, which was me. That part I should keep forever."

The lamp hissed. Rain worked at the roof.

"Your mother," Paloma said at last, "was a better courier than the entire Republic of Vellmar ever certified."

"She'd have liked that you said so."

"I'd have liked her to hear it." Paloma closed the ledger of addresses. "Mira. There's something I've been meaning to tell you, and I keep finding reasons to wait until morning. So." They drew a breath. "When I gave your berth to Anders — I want you to know it wasn't forgiveness. Not then. It was arithmetic, the cold kind: a grown man can survive what a child can't. I told myself it was justice. It took the bridge, and the search, and the testimony, and one hundred and eleven days in a cell to understand that it was neither. It was fear. I gave him the berth because facing him was the one thing left I hadn't had to do yet, and putting him on a ship delayed it indefinitely."

Mira said nothing. She had learned that from Paloma too: that silence could be a gift if you filled it with attention.

"But then you crossed the bridge beside me," Paloma went on, "singing a heron song full of state secrets, and when we reached the far side and the troops sealed the span behind us, you weren't afraid. You were hungry. You asked for bread. And I understood that you already knew the thing it took me thirty years and a destroyed republic to learn — that home isn't a place you're issued. It's a thing strangers build when they decide, against all instructions, to carry each other." They pushed the ledger across the table. "So. Ansel Brandt, fish market row, third door past the cooper's. Tomorrow that's yours. And Thursday there's a widow in Tarnow. And next week, when a man steps off the ferry with nothing but twenty years of bad conscience and whatever shameful gift he's brought — that's yours too, if you want it. All of it. The trade. The carrying. What my father built and misused and what your mother hid in the safest place she had."

Mira looked at the ledger. At the addresses marching down the page in Paloma's careful hand, each one a person waiting on the far side of some line for proof that they had not been forgotten.

"What do couriers call it," she asked, "when the delivery is a family?"

Paloma smiled — tired, cracked open, entirely unhidden. "We don't have a term. We just mark it *delivered*."

Outside, the rain thickened, and downriver the Kessel Bridge stood dark and empty in the weather, closed to armies and commissions and the flags of every regime, but not — Mira was certain, absolutely certain, with the certainty of a child who remembers everything — not forever. Somewhere across that water, in a processing shed at the end of the month, a man would step off a ferry holding a parcel he couldn't explain, and walk up from the dock not knowing whether he'd be turned away, and the door he was looking for was this one, and the bread would be fresh, because Marta always baked on the third day.

Mira reached for the pencil, opened the ledger to a clean page, and printed carefully at the top, the way her mother had taught her, the way Paloma had refined, the way she would teach it someday to someone standing where she stood:

*Deliveries pending.*

Then she added the first address, and beneath it, in smaller letters, a note for whoever carried:

*Expect him hungry. Keep a light burning.*
