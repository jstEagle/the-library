## 1. The Ledger Under the Floorboard

The rain had been falling for eleven days, and the hospital tents had begun to drink it.

Emrys Brightwater noticed such things because noticing was the one part of the work no one had thought to forbid. They noticed how the canvas sagged at the seams and wept thin brown streams down the support poles, so that by morning each tent pole stood in its own small moat of muddy water. They noticed how the straw bedding fermented in the damp until the whole ward smelled like a brewery for horses. They noticed that Private Donnelly, who had come in on Tuesday with his hip opened to the bone, had stopped asking whether he would play cricket again, and had started asking instead whether anyone had written to his mother—and that when no one answered quickly enough, he answered for them, in a voice gone strangely cheerful: *No, of course not. No one's had the time.*

Noticing was dangerous work. Emrys did it anyway, the way other people bit their nails or said their prayers—compulsively, quietly, and with a low constant ache beneath it.

"More water over here, Nurse," called Sister Agnes from the far row, though she was already pouring it herself, her broad back a wall between the orderlies' chaos and the men's modesty. Agnes ran the infirmary the way a lighthouse keeper runs a light: without opinion, without pause, and with an absolute refusal to see anything she was not required to see. It was, Emrys had long ago concluded, not blindness but discipline—a survival craft as difficult and deliberate as surgery. In three wars she had learned exactly how much a person could witness before the witnessing killed them, and she rationed herself accordingly.

Emrys was still learning the opposite skill: how to witness everything and survive anyway.

"Brightwater." The voice came from the tent flap, clipped and clean amid all the wetness. Lieutenant Hale stood there with rain beaded on his shoulders, holding a clipboard inside an oilcloth sleeve as if the paperwork might catch cold. His uniform was immaculate in the way that only a man who had never once knelt in this mud could manage. "Casualty return for your signature. The colonel wants the weekly figures by nightfall."

Emrys wiped their hands on their apron, which made no difference whatsoever, and crossed the ward. Around them the wounded lay in two long rows, some sleeping, some pretending to sleep, all of them listening with the animal attention of men who had learned that information meant survival.

"The figures," Emrys said, taking the board.

"As reported by your section." Hale's face did nothing at all. That was the trick of it; they had been children together on the same grey northern street, had fished the same polluted river, had shared the same stolen apples, and Emrys could still read him—but only just, and only if they worked at it. The army had taught Hale a beautiful stillness. "Fourteen wounded. Three dead."

Emrys looked at the list. Fourteen wounded. Three dead. And below those tidy numbers, in the space where the previous week's totals had been struck through and re-inked, a faint ghost of different arithmetic showing through the paper like a bruise through skin.

"And the men who died at the ford?" Emrys asked, lightly, the way one asks about weather. "Before the withdrawal. They'd have come through here first."

"They didn't come through here." Hale's pen was already extended, offered handle-first, a small ceremony ending a small conversation. "There's no record of them coming through here."

"No record," Emrys repeated. They signed. Their hand did not shake; they had trained it out of that months ago, after the first time. "Of course. How convenient for the record."

Hale's eyes came up—one flicker, quick as a fish turning—and then were still again. "Mind the figures, Brightwater. The colonel is particular."

"The colonel," Emrys said, "is particular about stories. Figures are just the words we've agreed not to say."

For a moment neither of them breathed. Rain drummed the canvas overhead, patient as a debt collector. Then Hale took back the clipboard, saluted with a neatness that felt like a door closing, and was gone into the grey.

Emrys stood a moment longer among the rows of breathing men, listening to the rain, and felt the familiar heat rise behind the breastbone—not rage exactly; rage was too clean a word for it. This was older and duller, something like standing in front of a locked door knowing someone was starving behind it.

*Three dead,* the paper said.

Emrys had counted six.

* * *

Night came early in December and brought the fog with it, rolling up from the river in great slow banks until the lanterns of the camp floated like drowned stars. By ten o'clock the ward was quiet except for the coughing, the moaning that men tried to disguise as dreaming, and Sister Agnes's footsteps making their final rounds—slow, even, unseeing by design.

Emrys waited until those footsteps reached the far end of the tents. Then they knelt beside their cot in the narrow cubicle of salvaged crates and canvas that served as the nurses' quarters, hooked two fingers under the warped floorboard at the corner where the frame met the ground, and lifted.

The gap beneath was shallow and dry only by accident—a fold in the earth, a lucky plank—but it held the ledger perfectly. A plain exercise book, bought in a market town whose name Emrys had already begun to forget, bound in brown card gone soft with handling. Inside, in a small, deliberately dull hand—the handwriting of a person who has practiced being unremarkable—were names.

Emrys took it out, weighted the floorboard back into place, dragged their cot half an inch over it, and sat cross-legged on the mattress with a candle stub cupped in a tin dish, shielding the light with their own body the way a mother shields a flame from wind.

They turned to the page headed, in pencil, *The Ford — 14 November.*

Six names. They read them again, though they knew them by heart, because reading them was a kind of duty, like folding a flag. Corporal Iwan Reece. Private Daniel Okafor. Private Tomas—no. Not that name yet. Emrys turned the page quickly, as if the ledger could feel hesitation.

The habit had started small, the way avalanches start with a single settling stone. In the spring, a shell-shocked boy had died in Ward Two, and afterward his bed simply ceased to exist in the returns—he had never been admitted, apparently, and so his death required no telegram, no pension, no awkward questions about why a seventeen-year-old had been at the front at all. Emrys had written his name down that night almost without thinking, the way one writes down a telephone number one intends to use later. Then there had been another. Then four more, in a single week of bad fighting, men who arrived dying and were recorded as *returned to duty*, because *returned to duty* kept the section's numbers respectable and respectable numbers kept Colonel Marsh's reports consistent and consistent reports kept Major Corvane's recommendations moving upward like bubbles through honey.

That was the thing Emrys had understood slowly, and then all at once: no one had ordered this. There was no memo, no conspiracy requiring signatures. It happened the way rot happens in a ship's timbers—every man doing his small necessary part, the carpenter planing away the soft wood, the caulker filling the gaps, everyone keeping the vessel floating and no one mentioning what was holding it up. A dead man erased was a pension unpaid, a widow unnotified, an inquiry unnecessitated. Forgetting was efficient. Forgetting was, in its way, patriotic.

And so every night, while the camp slept off its fear and its cheap gin, Emrys Brightwater lay on a cot above a hole in the floor and committed the one crime that seemed to matter: they remembered.

Tonight there was a new name to add. Old Havelock, the stretcher-bearer, the man who had carried more wounded men out of fire than any officer had ever carried a reputation, had died at dusk of the fever that had been eating him for a month. He would appear nowhere. Stretcher-bearers were civilian contractors, technically; their deaths were administrative noise. Emrys dipped the pencil stub, steadied their wrist against their knee, and wrote:

*Havelock, Josiah. Bearer Corps. Died 19 December, 19:40, of fever. Carried eleven men from the ridge on 3 October. Sang hymns badly. Nobody's son, nobody's father—somebody's friend. Mine.*

The last line they hesitated over, then kept. The ledger was evidence, yes—or would be, someday, if someday ever came—but it was also the only obituary column in the world for these men, and Emrys had decided early on that the entries could grieve as well as accuse. Accuracy of feeling was also a kind of accuracy.

A board creaked outside the cubicle. Emrys closed the book with a soft thump of heart and palm together, slid it beneath the blanket, and looked up with a nurse's mild, empty face—the face they wore like a uniform within a uniform.

Sister Agnes stood in the entrance, lantern in hand, her round face unreadable as bread dough. Her eyes passed over Emrys, over the blanket, over the tin dish with its guttering candle. She saw none of it. She was very good at seeing none of it.

"Ward Two's coughing again," she said. "I'll sit with them till the laudanum takes. You should sleep, Brightwater. You look like something the tide left."

"So do the tide's better offerings, Sister."

"Hmph." But she lingered a moment, and in that moment something moved behind her eyes—something she immediately, professionally, escorted back out of view. "Curfew was an hour ago. Whatever you're about, be careful about it. This camp eats careless people."

Then she was gone, lantern swinging, and Emrys sat in the dark wondering—for perhaps the hundredth time, and certainly not for the last—how much the woman who survived by seeing nothing actually saw.

* * *

Sleep came late and thin. Emrys dreamed, as they often did, of a kitchen in the north: their brother Aled's boots by the door, too large for him still, growing into themselves; their mother's compass on the shelf, its brass case worn bright by thumbs. Aled had enlisted at eighteen with the shining certainty of boys fed on parade grounds and newspaper maps. The telegram had used the word *desertion* the way a stamp uses glue—to attach the letter firmly and finally to its destination, closing it against all argument. Missing, absent without leave, presumed. Filed. Emrys had been nineteen and powerless and had believed, with a fury that had no address, that somewhere in some drawer there was a true version of the story, and that the drawer had simply been locked by someone who found the truth inconvenient.

It had taken a war to teach them that there were many drawers, and that locking them was practically a profession.

They woke before dawn to the sound that had replaced birdsong in their life: the groan of ambulance wheels in the mud, and beneath it, distantly, artillery speaking its flat sentence over the hills. The new day's work arrived wrapped in the old day's work. Emrys rose, dressed, retrieved the ledger one last time to check the ink was dry—it was, and the name Havelock sat on the page looking both permanent and terribly insufficient—and put it away.

By seven they were moving down the ward with a basin and a sponge, past the men who slept and the men who pretended, past Donnelly asking again about his mother, past the boy in the third cot who had come in overnight, delirious, calling for a village whose name no one recognized.

At eight, Sister Agnes appeared at the tent flap with a strange set to her mouth.

"There's a soldier at the gate," she said. "Been there since before light, by the look of him. Won't give his name. Says he'll only speak to whoever nursed him through the fever in September." She paused, and her voice dropped into a register Emrys had never heard her use—as if, for once, she were allowing herself to see something. "He's got a child with him, Brightwater. Asleep in a blanket, on a lead rope like it's a dog. And he keeps looking at the tents like the devil's behind him."

Emrys set down the basin. Water slopped over the rim onto the mud, and they did not notice that either.

Behind Agnes, through the grey morning, a figure stood at the edge of the camp—a man thin as a fence post, holding a bundle that was breathing, and glancing over his shoulder at the road as though the road owed him something terrible.
