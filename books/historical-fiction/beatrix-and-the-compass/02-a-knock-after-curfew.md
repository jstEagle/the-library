## 2. A Knock After Curfew

The knock came at half past eleven, when the wind had found its voice again and the canvas walls of the infirmary shuddered like a man trying not to cry.

Emrys heard it through the floorboards first—three soft raps, the kind a person makes when they do not want to be answered—and then through the door itself, hesitant as a confession. They set down the pen mid-word. The ledger slid beneath the loose plank by the dispensary shelf with the ease of long practice; Emrys had learned to hide it in under four seconds, which was, they sometimes thought in the small hours, a strange skill to have cultivated after eight years of nursing.

Lamp low. Shawl over the shoulders. The hospital at this hour was a country of its own: forty men breathing in ragged chorus, the smell of carbolic and gangrene and wet wool, one lamp guttering near the amputation tent where Private Oakes had died at sunset without ever regaining his name for more than an hour at a time. Emrys moved between the cots out of habit, touching a blanket here, angling a pillow there—not because the men needed it, but because the walking helped, because hands that were always busy could not be seen shaking.

The knock came again.

Curfew had been called at nine. The sentries on the perimeter were meant to challenge anything that moved, but the war had worn holes in every regulation, and soldiers had learned which posts slept and which did not. Emrys unhooked the latch and opened the door a hand's width, letting in a blade of cold air and the smell of frozen mud.

A soldier stood in the lee of the doorway, cap pulled low, greatcoat buttoned to the chin. He was thin in the way that men got when they had been in the field too long—thinned from the inside out, as though something had been scooped away and the coat merely hung where flesh used to argue with it. Emrys knew him. Of course they knew him. They had held his hand while the surgeon dug shrapnel out of his shoulder six weeks ago, had counted his pulse through three bad nights, had written *Corporal R. Voss* on the card above his bed in their own careful hand.

"Corporal." Emrys kept their voice level, pitched under the wind. "You'll be shot for this."

"I know it." His voice was gravel dragged over gravel. He did not come inside. That was the first wrong thing; soldiers pressed to the door of any lit building the way moths argued with glass. The second wrong thing was what he carried.

At first Emrys took it for a kit bag, slung against his chest the way men carry wounded comrades. Then the bundle shifted. A small boot slipped free of the blanket. A child's foot, pale as a peeled egg, curled against the cold.

Everything in Emrys went very still—the stillness of a pond when a stone is falling toward it, before the splash.

"Voss," Emrys said slowly. "What have you got there."

He looked over his shoulder at the dark, then back, and for a moment the cap brim lifted and Emrys saw his eyes properly. They had been blue and rather foolish once, quick to laugh at the nurses' jokes even when the jokes were old. Now they were the eyes of a man standing at the edge of something he could not walk back from.

"He wouldn't wake for anyone else," Voss said. Which was no answer at all.

Emrys stepped out onto the frozen ground and pulled the door nearly shut behind them, an instinct older than thought: whatever came next must not be witnessed. The cold bit through the shawl immediately. Somewhere beyond the wire, a dog barked twice and gave up.

"Give me the child," Emrys said.

"Not yet." And there—it surfaced now, beneath the exhaustion, beneath the fear—a kind of terrible resolve. Voss had walked a long way to stand on this exact patch of mud, and he would not be hurried off it. "I need you to hear me first, Nurse Brightwater. Because after tonight there won't be another chance, and I—" He stopped, jaw working. "I haven't got the words. I never had. You were the one who talked me under the knife, remember? You'll find them better than I will."

"Then let me find them inside, by a fire, with the boy warm—"

"No." It came out sharp enough to startle them both. He gentled at once, rocking the bundle slightly, an unconscious motion, a father's motion or a brother's. "No. Listen. There's no time. They're moving my company up at first light. Whole battalion, north column. I'm to be on the transport whether I've said my piece or not."

"The front." Emrys felt the words land somewhere below the ribs. "You're fit for duty. I signed nothing—you were still on my list, I'd have kept you another fortnight if—"

"You'd have kept me forever if you could." A ghost of the old smile, gone as fast as it came. "That's why it has to be you."

The wind shifted and the child stirred, making a small sound—not a cry, something quieter and worse, a sound like a word that had been swallowed so many times it had forgotten how to be spoken. Voss tucked the blanket closer with hands that shook.

Emrys looked at him properly then, and understood, in the way one understands a fracture on an X-plate before the surgeon says a word, that everything about this moment was wrong in a specific and deliberate shape. Soldiers did not carry children through hostile country after curfew. Soldiers did not seek out nurses in the dark unless they wanted something done that daylight would not permit.

"What village," Emrys said quietly.

Voss flinched as though struck.

"I've nursed two hundred of you," Emrys went on, keeping their voice soft, almost indifferent, watching his face the way they watched a pulse. "I know what a man looks like when he's carrying a village. You've had that look since the day they brought you in. I never asked. It wasn't mine to ask. But you've brought the village to my door, Corporal, or what's left of it, and now I think it is mine to ask."

For a long moment the only sound was the wind working at the guy-lines, the canvas groaning, the muffled sea-sound of sleeping men.

"There was a place," Voss said at last, each word laid down like a man crossing ice, testing his weight. "Six weeks back. Before my shoulder. We were told it was held by irregulars. We were told a great many things." His throat moved. "It isn't there anymore."

"It burned."

"It burned." He said it flatly, the way men repeated things they had been made to say until the words wore smooth. "And there were people in it, and we were told afterward there weren't. That's the version that goes in the file. That's the version my colonel signed and Major—" He stopped himself hard, teeth clicking shut on the name, and Emrys watched him choose silence with visible effort. "There's a version in the file," he finished. "And then there's what happened."

Emrys's heart was beating strangely, slow and heavy, a bell in deep water. They thought—could not help thinking—of the ledger beneath the floorboard, of the names inked in their small cramped script, of the arithmetic of erasure they had been keeping all these months. *Another page,* they thought. *It wants another page.* And hated themselves for the thought even as it came, because the page in question was asleep in a corporal's arms.

"And the boy?"

"The boy is what happened." Voss looked down into the bundle, and his face did something complicated and unbearable—grief and tenderness and shame all contending for the same few inches of skin. "Found him three days ago. Not near the village—we'd have never gotten him past our own lines, not with the way they're checking everything now. A farmer's family had him, took him in when he turned up wandering. Then word came down that patrols were sweeping the district. Asking after a missing child." His voice dropped until Emrys had to lean in. "*Asking*, mind. Not searching. There's a difference, and the family heard it. They sent for me because I'd treated their boy last spring and paid fair for the poultice. They said—if anyone asks, they never saw him. They put him in my arms at midnight and closed the door."

"And you brought him here."

"Where else?" Voss's laugh had no humor in it anywhere; it was just breath shaped like laughter. "I've got no wife. No sisters living. My company moves north in six hours and doesn't stop moving, and if I leave him in the town he's picked up by noon, and if I keep him he's picked up with me, and either way—" He stopped. Started again. "They don't want him found, Nurse. Understand that. This isn't a lost child they're looking for. It's a loose thread."

The cold had gotten into Emrys's knees, into the joints of their resolve. They thought of Sister Agnes asleep in the matron's quarters, of the orderlies who rose at four, of the visiting medical officers who arrived without warning and toured the wards like generals reviewing troops. An infirmary was the worst place in the world to hide anything. It was also the only place Emrys knew how to be invisible in.

"His name," Emrys said. "Does he have one?"

"He doesn't speak. Hasn't said a word, the farmers said, not since—not since. Maybe he can't. Maybe he won't." Voss swallowed. "I've been calling him Tomás. It was on a scrap pinned to his coat when they found him, or the farmer thought it was—half the letters burnt. It might be anything. It might be nothing."

*Tomás.* Emrys filed it away the way they filed everything, in the cabinet behind the eyes that no inspection had ever found.

And then Voss did the thing that Emrys would turn over and over for years afterward, in the shepherd's hut and the judge's parlor and the long year that followed: he shifted the child to one arm, and from inside his glove he drew a folded square of paper, and he pressed it into Emrys's hand.

The paper was cold and slightly damp. In the light leaking under the infirmary door, Emrys could make out only that it was torn from a paybook, and that the writing on it was hasty, pencil, the letters crowded as though the writer feared running out of page before running out of nerve.

"Forget the face," it read. Nothing more.

"What does that mean?" Emrys whispered. "Voss. What does that *mean?*"

"It means what I can't." His voice cracked down the middle and he mastered it, barely. "It means if they come asking, you never saw a child. You never saw me. You forget the face, and the boy lives. That's the only arithmetic I've got left to offer anybody." He stepped forward, and now at last he surrendered the bundle, transferring it into Emrys's arms with a care that undid something in Emrys's chest—the weight was nothing, seven stone of soldier handing over perhaps three stone of child, and yet it landed like ballast, like fate. "But I need somebody to do the opposite of what the note says. Somebody who writes things down. I saw your hand going all those months in the ward, Nurse. Always writing. I know a record-keeper when I've been nursed by one."

The night tilted. Emrys held the child and could not speak.

"I never—" they began, and had no ending for it.

"If you keep nothing," Voss said simply, "then keep him. If you keep everything"—his eyes flicked, one last time, toward the infirmary, toward whatever instinct had shown him the shape of Emrys's secret—"then keep him too. Either way, he's yours now, and God forgive me for the asking."

He touched his cap brim. He turned. The dark took him in three strides, and he was gone the way soldiers go—without ceremony, without looking back, because looking back is a luxury issued only to those who survive.

Emrys stood alone on the frozen ground with a sleeping child against their chest and a note in their fist, and the wind came across the plain carrying the smell of snow, and far to the north, though they could not have known it, a transport train was being made ready, and a corporal with a badly healed shoulder was boarding it with the rest, carrying his silence to the front like luggage he could not set down.

* *

Inside, Emrys did not sleep.

They laid the boy on the narrow bed in the linen room—the one warm corner of the infirmary, walled in by shelves of folded sheets that smelled of lye and someone else's clean living. The child did not wake. That frightened Emrys more than crying would have; children who did not wake had usually decided, somewhere below language, that waking was not worth the risk. Up close the boy was perhaps five, perhaps six—underfed, so that age became guesswork—with a scab on his brow gone yellow with healing, lashes singed at the tips, and hands that even in sleep stayed curled, fists against his own chest, guarding something.

Emrys eased one fist open, gently, the way they had eased bandages from wounds.

In the palm lay a single button. Brass, regimentally stamped, its shank still trailing a thread of grey wool. Emrys turned it toward the lamp and read the stamp, and felt the floor of the world shift a half-degree, the way a ship shifts when it passes over something vast and submerged.

Emrys knew that stamp. Every nurse in this camp knew it, because that regiment appeared in no muster roll, answered to no dispatch, existed in no file—and yet its wounded had come through this infirmary all autumn, carried in at night by escorts under orders of silence, patched and shipped out before dawn, their cards marked only with numbers. Emrys had written some of those numbers into the ledger. Forty names, gathered from fevered lips, from dying murmurs, from the identity discs pried out of dead men's hands before the burial details could melt them down. Forty men of a regiment that, according to the Army of which Emrys was a sworn servant, had never been deployed.

The boy's fist closed again around the button, small and absolute, and Emrys sat down slowly on the edge of the bed and looked at him for a very long time.

Outside, the wind dropped for a moment, and in the sudden quiet Emrys could hear the whole camp breathing—the horses, the sentries, the forty broken men in the ward, the enormous machinery of the war clearing its throat for the morning's movement north. Somewhere in that machinery, papers were being stamped. Somewhere, a file was being assembled that would say a village had never contained people. Somewhere, a name was being written that would overwrite a hundred others.

And here, in a linen room that smelled of soap, a child slept with evidence in his fist and a note that said *forget the face* folded in Emrys's pocket like a live coal.

Emrys thought of Voss on the transport, of the farmer's family closing their door, of the arithmetic of survival that always, always billed someone who could not pay. They thought of their brother, and stopped thinking of him, in the practiced way of eleven years.

"All right," they said aloud, very softly, to the sleeping boy, to the button, to the note, to the whole listening dark. "I'm bad at forgetting. It's the one thing I've never managed."

They banked the lamp. They did not return the ledger to its floorboard. Instead they sat with it open on their knees in the lamplight, pen uncapped, and beside the entry for the fortieth name they ruled a fresh line, and above it, after a long moment, wrote not a name but a date—the date the child had arrived—and beneath it, in their small cramped hand: *witness, living.*

The knock did not come again. But Emrys sat awake until the sky went grey, listening for boots.
