## 3. The Boy Who Wouldn't Speak

The boy had been in the infirmary for three days, and in that time he had spoken exactly four words.

Not to Emrys. To the kettle.

Emrys had come down before dawn on the second morning to find the child crouched beside the great black range in the corner of the ward, watching the water heat with an attention so total it seemed almost devotional. As the first thread of steam rose from the spout, his lips moved and a whisper escaped—two words, soft as moth wings. Emrys caught only the shape of them, not the substance. When she set her boots deliberately on the floorboards, the boy shut his mouth like a drawer and did not open it again.

The other two words came that afternoon, directed at a bowl of barley soup he refused to eat until Sister Agnes salted it herself. *More salt.* Two words. Then nothing.

Otherwise he was silent the way stone is silent—not empty, but full of something that had no intention of coming out.

He would not say his name. He would not say where he had come from, or who had left him on the infirmary step three nights past with a note folded into his coat like a prescription: *forget the face*. He would not even cry, which frightened Emrys more than anything else, because she had nursed enough children in enough field hospitals to know that a child who does not cry has decided, somewhere deep in the small architecture of himself, that crying is no longer worth the trouble.

So Emrys watched him instead, the way she watched wounds—for what they told her against their will.

He was perhaps six years old, though it was hard to be certain; hunger made younger children old and older children young. His hair was dark brown, cut badly by someone in a hurry. There was a healing scrape along his jaw and an older bruise gone yellow-green on his forearm, the kind that comes from being gripped hard by an adult hand. He slept curled into a knot so tight Emrys suspected his own knees must ache by morning. And he kept one fist closed at all times—the right one—so persistently that when she tried to examine his palm during a fever check, he bit her.

Not hard. But decisively. A boundary drawn in teeth.

"All right," Emrys had said, cradling her hand, keeping her voice level because Sister Agnes was watching from across the ward. "All right. It's yours. Whatever it is."

Whatever it was, she could guess its shape through the skin of his fist: small, round, flat-edged. She had seen the outline pressed into his palm once while he slept, when his fingers relaxed just enough—a circle the size of a shilling, and beneath it, faintly inked into his skin by pressure, the suggestion of stamped lettering around a rim.

A button. Military issue, if her guess held, and it held with a weight that sat in her stomach like swallowed river water.

* * *

On the fourth day, the fever came.

It was not dramatic—nothing like the putrid fevers that emptied the typhoid tents in hours—but it was steady, a dry heat that flushed his cheeks and made his eyes glitter and unfocused. Children burned quickly; that was the terror of them. Emrys sponged him down, dosed him with willow bark tincture, and settled into the chair beside his cot to wait out the long afternoon, and it was there, in the gray hour between the end of visiting and the beginning of night rounds, that the fist finally opened.

He was asleep. The fever had pulled him under like a current. His fingers uncurled one by one, slow as a flower deciding against blooming, and there in the cup of his small palm lay the button.

Brass. Regimental. Stamped around the rim with a device Emrys knew better than she wanted to: a stag's head above crossed rifles, and the motto she had read on a hundred collar plates belonging to men whose names she had written in a ledger hidden under a floorboard.

The 9th Regiment of Foot. The very same 9th that, according to every deployment order, casualty list, and official dispatch issued in this entire campaign, had never set foot in the colony at all.

Emrys sat very still. Somewhere down the ward a man moaned in his sleep, and the wind worried at the shutters, and the boy breathed in quick shallow sips of air, and Emrys looked at that button the way one looks at a thing that has just changed the shape of one's life.

*Forget the face*, the note had said. She was beginning to understand that the note had never been about charity at all.

* * *

She waited until the ward was asleep. This took hours. The infirmary never fully quieted—there was always a cough, a rattle, the man in bed nine who spoke to his mother in his sleep in a dialect she'd never learned—but there came a point past midnight when the lamplight pooled gold and undisturbed and the breathing of forty men fell into something like rhythm. Emrys lit no candle. She knew the floorboard by feel: third from the wall, beneath the foot of her own cot, the one whose nail head she had loosened with a teaspoon over the course of a week last spring.

The ledger came up wrapped in oilcloth, and she carried it to the window where the last of the moon gave just enough light to read by, and she did not allow herself to hesitate, because hesitation was a luxury and she had spent hers already, months ago, the first time she wrote a dead man's name in a book that officially did not exist.

Private Aldous Wren. Private Samuel Okafor. Corporal James Whitlock. Drummer boy Peter Halloran, aged sixteen, listed in the muster as transferred, listed in Emrys's memory as buried behind the eastern ridge without ceremony or marker.

Forty-one names she had gathered over eight months, most of them ordinary soldiers erased for ordinary reasons—desertion invented to cover cowardice that hadn't happened, sickness reclassified as absence, deaths rewritten so that no officer's report need carry the stain of them. Forty-one names, and she had believed, in her careful way, that she understood the shape of her ledger. Small corruptions. Individual erasures. The war's ordinary grinding.

Then she turned to the pages she kept in the back, the ones she had filled with rumor, and began to cross-reference.

The 9th Regiment appeared in the colonial dispatches exactly twice: once in a supply requisition dated the eleventh of September, and once in a general order reassigning the unit to garrison duty "on the coast," which was a way of saying nowhere, since the coast held no garrison and the requisition had been signed by a quartermaster who had, Emrys confirmed from another entry, died of dysentery in October. Between those two documents lay a gap of nineteen days, and in that gap, according to the stories the wounded men told when the lamps were low and they thought no officer listened, something had happened in the hill country beyond the Kereth River. A village. A column ambushed—or not ambushed. Retaliation—or not retaliation. Nobody agreed on details. Everybody agreed there had been fire.

And now, in the moonlight, Emrys counted.

She started with the regiment's published strength—she had copied the figures months ago from a newspaper the officers discarded—and subtracted the transfers the records acknowledged, and the number that remained, the number of men who had simply stopped existing between one page of the army's arithmetic and the next, was forty.

Forty.

Her hand was not quite steady. She set the pencil down and picked it up again and counted once more, because a number like that demanded to be wrong twice before it could be true once. Forty names omitted. Forty men erased not one at a time, for private convenience, but wholesale—regimentally, administratively, as a matter of policy. Not a name slipped through a clerk's carelessness. A regiment deleted.

And a child clutching its button had been carried through the dark and laid on her doorstep by a soldier who had once, for six weeks, trusted her with the contents of his own body, and who had left behind five words on paper because five words were all he dared risk: *forget the face*.

Emrys looked over at the cot. The boy had turned in his fever, cheek pressed to the rough pillow, and his open right hand lay on the blanket with the brass button glinting dully in its center, and Emrys understood—with the cold, clean certainty of a blade going in—that she was not looking after an orphan. She was guarding evidence. The boy was not a charge. He was a witness. The button was not a keepsake. It was a signature, torn from the coat of a dead man, carried by a living one.

And somewhere in the administrative machinery of this war, someone had decided that forty men had never existed, which meant that anyone who could prove otherwise was not merely troublesome. He was impossible. And things that were impossible were dealt with thoroughly.

*Forget the face.*

Too late, whoever you are, Emrys thought. I have never in my life managed to forget a face. It is the only talent I have ever possessed, and I have paid rent on it in blood.

* * *

The fever broke near dawn, the way fevers do—all at once, sweat flooding through the linen, the small body going limp with relief rather than rigidity. Emrys wrung out a cloth, wiped his face and throat, and was preparing to return the ledger to its floorboard when a voice stopped her.

"You're the one who writes."

She turned. The boy was awake. His eyes were bright—not with fever now, but with something sharper. He was looking not at her face but at the ledger in her hands, at the pencil, at the small desperate economy of a person writing in the dark.

Emrys's heart performed a complicated maneuver. "What did you say?"

"You're the one who writes." The same words, firmer. His voice was hoarse from disuse, a rusted hinge of a voice. "The man said. The man who carried me. He said, find the nurse who writes the names. He said the names are safe with her." His fist was closing again, slowly, around the button, drawing it back into the fortress of himself. "Is my papa's name in your book?"

The ward was utterly still around them, forty sleeping men and one question hanging in the candle smoke.

Emrys knelt beside the cot so that their eyes were level. Up close she could see how the fever had scoured him, how thin the skin was over his small wrists, how much it had cost him to break four days of silence for this.

"I don't know your father's name yet," she said carefully. "But if you tell me it—if you tell me yours—we can look together. That's what the book is for. Names go in, and they stay in, and nobody gets to take them out. Not majors, not generals, not anyone."

The boy studied her for a long moment with the grave, measuring gaze of someone who had learned early that adults' promises came in varying weights and most of them were tin. Then, in a whisper:

"Tomas. Tomas Ferro." A pause, and his thumb moved over the button like a prayer bead. "My papa is Sergeant Rui Ferro. Of the Ninth." He said it the way other children recited the catechism, with the fixed conviction of a thing learned by heart and clutched against the dark. "He told me if ever I was lost, I should keep the button and find the nurse who writes the names."

The room tilted slightly. Emrys gripped the edge of the cot.

Sergeant Rui Ferro. She turned the pages of her memory backward through the ledger, and found it—not a name she had written, but one she had heard, in the mutterings of a dying lance-corporal in October, a name spoken alongside a burning village and a colonel who wept and a major who did not. A name from the gap in the record. A name from the nineteen missing days.

Which meant the ledger was no longer forty names short of the truth.

It was forty-one.

"Sleep now, Tomas," she said, and was surprised at how steady her voice came out. "In the morning, we'll write it in. Your father's name. In ink, so it can't be rubbed out."

He nodded once, gravely, and let his eyes close, and within minutes his breathing had found the shallow tide of true sleep, his fist curled around the button, holding the whole terrible arithmetic of it against his palm.

Emrys stood in the gray light with the ledger open in her hands and felt the ground shift beneath the careful life she had built. For eight months she had believed she was a record-keeper, a quiet clerk of the forgotten, invisible and therefore safe. Now she understood that she was holding the only copy of a crime the army had committed in company strength—and that the living proof of it slept ten feet away, feverish and six years old, with orders stitched into his arrival: forget the face.

Outside, beyond the shutters, she heard the change of sentries, the crunch of boots, the bugle's thin complaint. Another day beginning at the hospital. And somewhere across the parade ground, in a warm office smelling of ink and coffee, an officer with polished buttons was signing papers that declared this child's father had never existed.

Emrys slid the ledger back beneath the floorboard, pressed the nail home with her thumb, and made herself a promise in the space between one breath and the next.

Tomorrow, Major Corvane was scheduled to inspect the infirmary.
