## 4. Major Corvane Inspects the Infirmary

The rumor arrived before the man did, the way bad weather arrives—first as a change in the air, then in the behavior of those who ought to know better.

Sister Agnes came through the ward at half past six, earlier than her usual rounds, and set a hand briefly on Emrys's shoulder as she passed. She did not stop. She did not look at them. She said only, "The major inspects at nine. Wash the windows," and moved on to Private Calloway's dressings as though she had said nothing at all.

Wash the windows. In three years Emrys had never once been asked to wash the windows of a field hospital that stood in a churned field forty miles from any town worth admiring. But Emrys had learned the grammar of this place, where every instruction was a letter and only some of them were about glass. Wash the windows meant: make everything visible. Make nothing hidden. A clean window is a window one can see through.

Emrys washed the windows.

They washed them slowly, methodically, and while they washed, they thought—not about glass but about a boy asleep in the linen store behind the boil room, a boy of perhaps six who had not spoken a word in four days and who held a regimental button in his fist the way other children held prayer beads. The floorboard under the third cot from the stove held a ledger with forty-one names in it now, forty-one men who had officially never died because they had officially never been there, and somewhere south of the river was a village that had officially burned by accident, and in that village someone official had reported a child missing.

*Someone official.* That was the part Emrys kept circling back to, wringing out the rag, watching gray water run gray over their knuckles. Missing children were reported by mothers. By grandmothers. By neighbors. Not by officials. Unless the official wanted it known that he knew—that he had asked after the child, that his interest was on record, that anyone harboring the boy should understand exactly whose attention they had attracted.

Or unless the report itself was the trap's first tooth.

Emrys finished the last pane and stepped back. The winter light came through clean and colorless and fell across the ward like a sheet laid over a body: sixty cots, fifty-eight occupied, two empty and stripped. Every face in the room was catalogued in Emrys's memory—the fever cases, the gut-shot boys, young Wetherell who would lose the arm by Friday—and Emrys found themselves doing what they always did when fear arrived, which was to convert it into inventory. Fear was useless raw. Filed properly, it became preparation.

By eight o'clock the ward smelled of carbolic and scrubbed pine, the floors had been swept twice, and Sister Agnes had rearranged three orderlies so that no one stood near the corridor to the boil room. She did all of it without explanation, without acknowledgment, in the flat competent voice of a woman who had survived three wars by noticing everything and admitting nothing. Once—only once—her eyes met Emrys's across the ward, and something passed between them that neither would ever name. It might have been warning. It might have been apology for the warning being necessary.

At nine o'clock precisely, Major Corvane arrived.

He came with an aide and two mounted escorts who remained outside, which was somehow worse than if he had brought ten men. His boots were clean. That was the first thing Emrys noticed, and hated themselves for noticing—the boots of a man who walked on planks and paving stones while the war went on in mud. His greatcoat was fitted rather than issued. His gloves were gray kid leather, unlined, thin enough that he might have felt the texture of things had he chosen to touch them. He removed them as he entered, folding each finger back with a small deliberate care, and handed them to his aide as though the aide were furniture.

"Major Corvane, Headquarters Staff." He said it to Agnes, though he had certainly sent word ahead of himself, and certainly knew her name. "I'm told this is the best-run infirmary in the sector."

"You're told correctly, sir," said Agnes.

He smiled. It was a good smile. It reached his eyes, or appeared to; Emrys would spend the rest of the morning trying to decide whether it had actually done so or merely performed the reaching, the way a well-made prosthetic performs a hand. "Then I shan't be disappointed, and neither shall you." He turned to the ward, and here Emrys watched him do the thing Emrys had been warned about by soldiers' gossip and had not believed until now: he looked at faces.

Not wounds. Faces. A touring officer looks at wounds—counts them, assesses them, converts them into supply requests and casualty figures. Corvane moved down the aisle between the cots with his hands clasped behind him and his gaze moving unhurried from one man's face to the next, resting a moment on each, the way a customs officer turns over coins to check for counterfeits. At Private Dunmore's cot he paused. Dunmore was sleeping, his jaw slack, twenty-three years old and looking sixty.

"Typhoid?" Corvane asked.

"Meningitis," said Agnes.

"Ah." He moved on. The *ah* had a small satisfied shape to it, and Emrys understood, with a cold sliding feeling beneath the ribs, that Dunmore's face was not on whatever list Corvane carried behind his eyes, and that the major was pleased to cross him off.

At Wetherell's cot Corvane stopped longer. The boy's arm was wrapped to the shoulder, and he was awake, and he pulled himself slightly straighter against his pillows when the major bent over him.

"Grenade?" Corvane asked kindly.

"Shell, sir. Third of December, sir."

"The third. Hard day." Corvane rested one hand—bare now, the kid gloves put away—very lightly on the rail of the cot, near enough to Wetherell's shoulder that the boy might have felt the warmth of it, or imagined he did. "You'll go home to England, I expect. There are worse fates." He said it gently, warmly, and Wetherell smiled up at him with the naked adoring gratitude of a wounded boy for a decorated man, and Emrys stood four feet away with their hands folded and their pulse hammering in their ears and thought: *he is telling the ward, in front of witnesses, that this one will survive and be discharged, and he is checking whether I flinch at the arithmetic.*

Because that was the thing. That was the whole hideous thing. Corvane was not inspecting the hospital. He was auditing it. He was comparing the living against the dead—or against the dead who did not exist—and every face he studied and found accounted for was a small door closing quietly in some ledger of his own. Emrys had spent months building a ledger of erased men. It had not once occurred to them, until this moment, that the eraser might keep accounts too.

Corvane straightened and turned, and his eyes found Emrys.

There was a fractional pause—a hitch no longer than the space between heartbeats—as his gaze crossed Emrys's face and stopped. Emrys felt it like a fingertip drawn down the spine. Then the major's attention continued its smooth progress around the room, and he was saying something to Agnes about ventilation, and the moment closed over like water.

But Emrys had seen the hitch. And Emrys understood that in the catalogue behind those pleasant gray eyes, one entry now read: *nurse, tall, watches too carefully.*

"I'd like to see your stores," Corvane said pleasantly. "And your records, of course."

"Of course, sir." Agnes led him toward the office at the end of the ward, and Emrys followed at the correct distance of two paces, because following was expected and refusing would be memorable, and above all things now Emrys could not afford to be memorable.

The office was a converted linen closet: one desk, one lamp, one shelf of admission books. Corvane did not sit. He stood over the desk while Agnes laid out the current admission register, and he turned the pages with one finger, reading with the quick flat competence of a man who read documents the way hawks read fields.

"Admissions, discharges, deaths." He tapped a column. "Your numbers run high for dysentery and low for wounds this month. The front is quiet?"

"The front is never quiet, sir. But the convoys have been small."

"Convoys have been small." He repeated it without mockery, filing it. He turned another page. Emrys watched his finger travel down the columns and made themselves breathe. The names in the admission book were true names, honestly recorded, dull as gravel—there was nothing to find, nothing *could* be found, the danger slept forty feet away under a floorboard wrapped in oilcloth—but the body insisted on its own truths, and Emrys's body was insisting quite loudly that the man three feet away could hear a heartbeat at ten paces if he cared to.

"Efficient," Corvane said at last, closing the book. "Genuinely. I've inspected eleven facilities in this sector and I will say so in my report: this is the only one where the paperwork and the patients agree with each other." He smiled again, that good warm smile, and included both of them in it. "Order is a form of mercy, Sister. Most people don't grasp that. Chaos kills more men than shells do. A hospital where every man is counted, every dose logged—" he gestured at the shelf of registers with something close to reverence "—this is civilization. This is the alternative to the swamp."

"Yes, sir," said Agnes.

"And yet." He said it lightly, turning toward the door, an afterthought already half-discarded, a man remembering an umbrella. "And yet one hears the strangest things. My aide tells me a child was reported missing from Kesselva. You know Kesselva? No, of course, it's nothing—a village, burned in October, regrettable business, ammunition stores. The report says a boy of five or six. One imagines he wandered into the hills before the fire." He shook his head with mild, genuine-seeming regret. "One hopes. Children are resilient, but the hills in December—" He let the sentence die, and picked up his gloves from the aide's arms, and worked his fingers back into them one by one.

Emrys heard their own voice before they had decided to use it.

"We had a woman through in November, sir. Refugee column off the southern road. She'd lost two children—one dead of fever on the road, one gone in the crowd at the rail crossing. She was asking every hospital along the line." A pause, measured, apologetic. "I don't recall if Kesselva was hers. The lost ones blur, sir, God forgive me. We bury so many we can't hold all the griefs apart."

It was not entirely a lie. That was the discovery that frightened Emrys most, even as they spoke it: the lie was built almost entirely of truth, load-bearing truth, a real woman, a real missing child, a real weariness—only the conclusion bent, only the direction pointed wrong. Corvane listened with his head tilted, courteous as a confessor, and Emrys watched him weigh it and watched something loosen fractionally in his shoulders.

"Well," he said. "If she turns up, the military police at headquarters would want to know. For the record." He settled his gloves. "For everyone's sake, everything should be in the record. Don't you agree, Nurse—"

"Brightwater, sir."

"Brightwater." He tried the name as if tasting it. "Don't you agree?"

"I believe everything should be remembered, sir," Emrys said. "That's rather why I keep the books."

It was a gamble dressed as innocence, and it landed perfectly: Corvane laughed, a short genuine bark, and clapped the doorframe once with his gloved hand. "*Remembered!* If only the entire army shared your religion, Brightwater. Half my troubles are men who forget things at convenient moments." He was still smiling as he turned into the corridor. "Carry on. Sister—my compliments to your staff. Finest facility in the sector."

His boots rang down the boards, unhurried, and the outer door opened and shut, and the cold came in and went out with him, and then there was only the ward breathing, fifty-eight men breathing, the lamp hissing faintly.

Emrys stood very still until the hoofbeats faded. Then they became aware that Agnes was looking at them—really looking, for the first time in three years, with the seeing-nothing finally switched off.

"He knows about Kesselva," Emrys said quietly.

"He knows *something*," Agnes said. "That's not always the same. But he left the way a man leaves a room where he's planted something and wants time to let it grow." She began restacking the registers with brisk, steady hands. "He'll be back. They always come back when they're not sure. Certainty doesn't visit twice."

Emrys nodded slowly. Their hands, they noticed, were entirely steady—which surprised them, given that somewhere inside their chest a door had come unlatched and was swinging. They had lied to a superior officer, smoothly, successfully, with architecture and foundation and a convincing roof. They had discovered a talent for it on the worst possible morning, and the talent felt less like armor than like infection.

Through the ward's clean windows the winter light lay flat on the frozen field. Behind the boil room, a small boy who would not speak was waiting for his midday bread, holding a button from a regiment that had never existed, and Emrys lifted the hem of their apron, dried their palms on it, and went to fetch him—walking, for the first time in their life, like someone who knew they could be watched from anywhere, and refusing, all the same, to look over their shoulder.

* * *

That night, after lights-out, Emrys knelt on the cold flags beside the third cot from the stove and slid up the floorboard, and took the ledger into their lap, and added by candle stub a single line—not a soldier's name this time, but a note, in the cramped private shorthand only they could read:

*Kesselva reported missing. He asks after children now. He knows my name.*

Below it, after a long moment, they added:

*I lied today. I was good at it. Watch this.*

They blew out the candle and sat in the dark listening to the ward breathe, and it occurred to them, with the terrible clarity that comes only at midnight, that Corvane had praised the hospital for one quality above all others—for the agreement between its records and its reality—and that from this night forward, keeping those two things from agreeing would be the whole of Emrys's life.

In the linen store, Tomas turned in his sleep and murmured something. Emrys rose and went to him, and stood a long while in the doorway watching the small chest rise and fall.

The boy's fist had loosened in sleep. The button lay in the hollow of his palm, catching what little light the stove threw, bright as a coin at the bottom of a well.
