## 5. Sister Agnes Knows

Sister Agnes had learned, over three wars and twenty-two years of service, that the safest place in any hospital was the doorway between rooms. Not inside a room—inside a room you belonged to something, to a patient or an order or an argument about laudanum dosages—but in the doorway, where you were passing through, always passing through, seeing nothing worth reporting.

She stood in the doorway between Ward Two and the linen store on the evening of the seventeenth of December, and she saw nothing at all.

What she saw was this: Nurse Brightwater, thinking themselves alone, kneeling on the cold flagstones beside the third cot from the window—the one with the loose board beneath it, the one Agnes had known about since the second week of the occupation—and sliding a small book wrapped in oilcloth into the dark space under the floor. What she saw was the way Brightwater's hands moved: quick, certain, practiced. This was not the first time. This was perhaps the hundredth time. What she saw, above all, was the expression on the young nurse's face as they rose and smoothed their apron—that particular stillness, that look of a person who has decided what they are willing to be hanged for and has made their peace with the rope.

Agnes withdrew into the shadow of the doorway before Brightwater turned, and by the time the nurse passed her with a murmured *good evening, Sister*, she was counting bandage inventory in a ledger of her own, her eyes fixed on the page, her mind elsewhere entirely.

She had survived the Ashanti campaign by not hearing what the surgeons said when they thought the morphine had taken the orderlies too deep. She had survived the frontier postings by not reading the letters officers asked her to burn unopened. And she was surviving this war—the one nobody at home would name, the one that arrived in her wards as boys with no regimental numbers and wounds that told stories the paperwork contradicted—by seeing nothing, three times a day, every day.

It was a discipline. Like prayer. Like scrubbing.

And like prayer, it had never once made her believe she was innocent.

* * *

The child had been at the infirmary nine days now, and Agnes had watched Emrys Brightwater make a series of small, fatal decisions with the methodical calm of a person defusing a shell.

The boy—Tomas, though only Brightwater used the name, and only when they thought no one could hear—slept in the corner of the dispensary behind a screen that had been positioned, Agnes noticed, precisely to block the sightline from the door. His meals came from Brightwater's own ration, weighed out on the dispensary scales with apothecary precision so that the kitchen ledger would show nothing amiss. When Major Corvane's adjutant had come through on inspection, the boy had been folded into a laundry basket with a blanket and a biscuit, and Brightwater had sat on the lid mending socks, hands steady, face pleasant, heart presumably going like a drum.

Agnes had signed the laundry inventory that week without remarking that it was four pounds heavier than the linens accounted for.

She told herself, in those first days, that her silence was prudence. That a woman of fifty-eight with a bad hip and a pension contingent upon good conduct could not afford entanglement. That whatever game Brightwater was playing against the military machine, the odds were long and the house always won, and Sister Agnes Okafor-Reyes—she had dropped the Reyes decades ago, but she kept it folded inside her like a letter never sent—had no intention of dying in a colonial backwater for another woman's conscience.

But there was the matter of the button.

She had seen it too, of course. The boy clutched it in his sleep the way other children clutched cloth mothers, and one afternoon while he dozed against Brightwater's shoulder she had looked at it properly: brass, regimental, the crest of a unit that appeared on no deployment roster she had ever processed. The 4th Battalion, Northumberland Rifles. A unit that, according to the official record, had never left England.

Agnes had processed enough supply requisitions in her career to know what it meant when forty men's rations were drawn and forty men's names went nowhere. She had seen the wagons come back from the eastern valley burned to their axles, and she had seen the quartermaster's report describing a training accident, and she had signed the stores reconciliation without comment because signing was her job and commenting had never once been.

So when she found herself, on the evening of the nineteenth, standing outside the linen store with her hand raised to knock—a thing she had not done in twenty years, because knocking announced interest and interest was death—she understood that some part of her had already decided.

She knocked.

* * *

Brightwater opened the door with a candle in hand and something flickering behind their eyes that might have been fear trying on the costume of courtesy. "Sister. Is it the dysentery ward? I was just—"

"Inventory," Agnes said. "I've come to do inventory."

"It's rather late for—"

"The third board from the window is loose," Agnes said. "You'll want to know that I know, before you decide how this conversation goes."

She watched the color leave Brightwater's face in stages, like water draining from a basin. It was, she reflected, a terrible thing to do to a colleague—to strip them bare in their own hiding place—but she had learned long ago that kindness delivered slowly was cruelty compounded, and that the only mercy in a hard conversation was speed.

"You'd better sit down," she said, and pushed past them into the linen store, into the smell of starch and carbolic and the ghost of camphor, and lit the second candle from the first with hands that did not shake, because hers never shook, that was the whole trick of her, that was the entire architecture of her survival.

Brightwater did not sit. They stood with their back against the shelving, arms crossed—not defensive, Agnes thought, but holding themselves together, the way you'd press a dressing to a wound.

"How long?" Brightwater asked.

"Since the second week." Agnes set the candles on the inventory desk, side by side, two small flames making a pool of light in all that grey. "Since the night you first put something under that floorboard. You were careful. You waited until the orderlies' card game got loud. You checked the corridor twice." She looked up. "You checked it in the wrong direction. You always check toward the wards, because your mind is on the patients. Nobody checks toward my office. Nobody ever has. In three wars, nobody has."

"And you—" Brightwater's voice caught and steadied. "You never said anything."

"What was there to say?" Agnes spread her hands. "'Nurse, I notice you're committing treason'? What would you have done? Stopped? Hidden it better? Reported me?" She almost smiled. "No. There was nothing to say. There was only something to decide, and I decided it, and I have been deciding it again every morning since, the way one decides to go on breathing."

"Why?" The word came out raw. "Why keep my secret?"

And there it was—the question Agnes had been turning over for nine days like a stone in the pocket, looking for its smooth side.

She took her time answering. She lowered herself onto the inventory stool, which complained under her hip as it always did, and she looked at the two flames, and she began.

"I was younger than you at my first war," she said. "Kumasi. You'll not remember it; it wasn't called a war either. I was a probationer, and I believed what the matron believed, which was that our work was nursing and everything else was above our station. There was a captain on my ward—fever case, not wounded—who talked in his delirium. Talked for three nights. Orders given, orders countermanded, a village, a number of dead that did not match any report I would later read." Her voice stayed level. It had taken her thirty years to make it stay level, and she was not going to lose the trick now. "On the fourth night he was lucid, and he knew what he'd said, and he begged me to write it down. He said someone should hold it somewhere. He said a fever could take him and then it would be as if none of it had happened, and he couldn't bear that, he said it was the dying he minded least."

"What did you do?"

"I brought his medicine," Agnes said. "On time. Correct dose. And I did not write anything down, and he died on the sixth day, and I attended the burial, and I have remembered every word he said for thirty-one years. Every word. Do you understand what I'm telling you, Nurse Brightwater? I kept his secret so well that I became the secret. There is a dead man's testimony living in my head with nowhere to go, and it has lived there through two more wars, growing heavier, the way stones grow heavier the longer you carry them even though they don't change at all."

The candles guttered in a draft from under the door. Neither of them moved to shield them.

"So when I watch you kneel at that floorboard," Agnes went on, "I am not watching a criminal. I am watching the person I failed to be. And I will tell you plainly, because you deserve plainness: I resent you for it. I resent you the way one resents the young—easily, unjustly, and with love underneath. And I have spent nine days watching Corvane circle this infirmary like a crow over a lambing field, and I have decided that I am done carrying stones."

Brightwater's eyes were bright. They didn't let the tears fall; Agnes noted that with professional approval. Control was a currency here, and the young nurse understood its exchange rate.

"The boy," Brightwater said quietly. "Tomas. If Corvane finds him—"

"He won't find him here. Not if we move him." Agnes leaned forward, and now her voice changed—dropped into the register she used for triage, quick and practical, the register that sorted the dying from the salvageable. "Listen to me. You cannot hide him forever in a laundry basket, and you cannot hide that ledger under a floorboard until Judgment Day. Corvane's commission comes through at the new year—I hear things; the matron of the garrison hospital drinks gin with the paymaster's wife—and when it does, every record in this colony becomes his to burn. You have twelve days. Perhaps less."

"I know." Brightwater's jaw tightened. "I haven't worked out the road yet. Three checkpoints between here and the capital, and I'm one nurse with a forged pass and a child who doesn't speak."

"You're not one nurse." Agnes reached into her apron pocket and produced a folded paper, creased soft as cloth from years of handling. She smoothed it on the desk between the candles: a route map, hand-drawn, annotated in her own small precise script. "This is the hospital supply road. It runs from the river landing to the rail yard to the governor's gate—it is the same three checkpoints, but traveled by carts that soldiers have stopped searching since the year dot, because everyone knows the medical supply chain is sacred. Cholera bands, quinine crates, corpses in lime. No sergeant wants to open those boxes, and the ones who did once found typhus bedding and regretted it professionally."

Brightwater stared at the map. "The supply road goes through the checkpoints?"

"The checkpoints exist to inspect the supply road. But the inspections are theater, dear—they always were. Sergeant Doyle at the river owes me his life; I pulled shrapnel out of him in '96 and he knows I know what he was doing when he caught it, which is a bond stronger than money. The ferryman, Voss, takes my tea and my silence twice a month and asks no questions, which is the finest quality in a man. At the rail yard—" she tapped the paper, "—there is a freight clerk named Okonjo who owes the mission hospital a debt he can never repay, and who loads our coal shipments personally. And the governor's gate at year's end is chaos—holiday traffic, half the guard drunk on the garrison's Christmas issue. A woman in a nurse's cloak with a child at her skirts walks through crowds like that invisible, provided she looks bored."

Brightwater looked up slowly. "You've had this planned."

"I've had this road mapped for twenty years," Agnes said. "For supplies, for deserters, for a midwife's daughter fleeing a husband, twice. A hospital that cannot move people quietly is only a building where people wait to die. I built the quiet part deliberately. I simply never expected to use it for this." She met Brightwater's eyes. "Now tell me what's in the book under the floorboard, and how many, and whether the boy is what I think he is. All of it. If I'm to spend my pension and probably my liberty on this, I'll not do it blind."

For a moment Brightwater hesitated—and Agnes saw the last internal wall standing, the instinct of the hunted to trust no one, and she respected it, and she waited it out the way she waited out fevers.

Then Brightwater knelt, drew up the loose board, and lifted out the oilcloth bundle, and unwrapped it on the inventory desk between the two candles, and opened it.

Forty names. Forty-one, Agnes corrected herself, counting the fresh ink at the bottom of the last page, the handwriting hurried, the entry dated yesterday. Names, ranks, a unit designation that matched the button in a sleeping child's fist, and dates, and in the margin beside each date a single word in Brightwater's careful hand: *omitted*. *Omitted*. *Omitted*, forty times over, like a heartbeat.

"Sergeant Hollis dictated the last one," Brightwater said softly. "He died this morning at six. He held a position on Corvane's written order and no relief ever came, and the report says his company was never deployed, and he wanted—" their voice frayed, held, "—he wanted to be counted while he could still count himself."

Agnes looked down at the ledger for a long moment. Then she did something she had not done since Kumasi: she closed her eyes and spoke aloud, into the candlelit dark of the linen store, the words a fevered captain had given her thirty-one years ago. The village. The order. The number of the dead. She gave them up like stones set down after a long march, and when she opened her eyes, Brightwater was writing—writing fast, adding her testimony to the ledger in a new hand, a second witness at last.

"There," Agnes said, when it was done, and found her voice rougher than she intended. "Thirty-one years late. Write that down too, why don't you. *Delivered by a coward, eventually.*"

"You weren't a coward," Brightwater said. "You survived."

"Both things can be true." Agnes stood, took up the candles, and handed one to Brightwater. "Now. We have twelve days, three checkpoints, one silent child, and a major who inspects my wards like a man counting spoons. Here is what happens tomorrow..."

* * *

They talked until the candles burned to pools and the orderlies' card game broke up and the frost began to feather the little window high in the linen store wall. By the time they parted, the plan had bones: Voss and the river on the twenty-ninth, Okonjo and the rail yard the same night, the governor's gate on the thirtieth with the holiday crowds as cover. Agnes would draw double rations through the supply channel to explain the cart weight. Brightwater would sew the ledger into the lining of a child's coat. And Sister Agnes would stand in her doorway, seeing nothing, exactly as she had for twenty years—while everything, this time, moved right past her in the dark.

At the door, Brightwater paused, the candle cupped in their hands, and said, "Why now? After all this time—why break the rule?"

Agnes considered the question honestly, which was more than she usually managed.

"Because I buried a man's truth once and it cost me thirty years of pretending the grave was shallow," she said. "And because I'm old, and old women get one extravagance." She turned back toward her office, then added, over her shoulder, "Sleep, Nurse. Tomorrow I'll introduce you to a ferryman. And tomorrow night, you'd best be ready—because I heard the garrison orderlies talking at supper, and Major Corvane has requested a second inspection of this infirmary. Day after tomorrow. He's bringing the provost marshal with him."

Behind her, in the linen store, Agnes heard the loose floorboard being lifted once more—quickly, quietly, a book changing its hiding place—and she smiled at the dark, and went to count what could be counted, and to leave uncounted everything else.
