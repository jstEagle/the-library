## 6. What the Button Belonged To

The snow came in the night, thin and grudging, dusting the canvas roofs of the infirmary like flour sifted over a corpse. Emrys noticed it first as a change in the sound of the ward—the coughing seemed to echo differently under a cold sky—and then as a brightness at the tent seams, the kind of pale, sourceless light that meant winter had decided to stay.

They had brought in eleven men from the forward positions overnight. Eleven, out of what had been forty. Emrys did the arithmetic before their feet had finished crossing the threshold, because that was what they did now; they counted things, and the counting had become a kind of breathing.

Sister Agnes passed them a basin of water hot enough to sting. "The one in the corner," she said, without preamble, because Agnes never wasted words on preamble. "Sergeant. The surgeons have seen him. They've seen him and they've walked away."

Emrys looked down the ward to the corner cot. The man there was propped half-upright against a folded blanket, which meant someone had bothered to arrange him, which meant he had asked to see, to look out at the world while he still owned eyes to look with. His face was grey in the particular grey way of men whose blood was leaving them by inches, and his hands lay open on the coverlet, palms up, as if waiting to receive something.

"Name?" Emrys asked.

"He won't give it," Agnes said. "He says names are for the end of things. He's been waiting since midnight for somebody to come and be his end."

* * *

Emrys worked the ward first. This was discipline, not callousness—Agnes's law, learned across three wars: the living get your hands before the dying get your ears, or else you will lose both kinds of patient. So Emrys changed dressings, spooned broth, held a boy of nineteen through the shaking fit that followed amputation, wrote two letters home in a hand steadier than the letters deserved. All the while the sergeant in the corner watched, and Emrys felt the watching like a hand between the shoulder blades, patient and unyielding.

At last there was nothing left but him.

Emrys pulled the stool close and sat, and up close the man smelled of iodine and the cold he'd carried in from the field, and Emrys saw that he was older than the grey face suggested—forty perhaps, a career soldier, the kind the army used up and called expendable in the same breath.

"Sergeant," Emrys said. "I'm Nurse Brightwater."

"I know who you are." The voice was rough but level, a voice that had given orders across gunfire and knew how to make itself heard without shouting. "You're the one who walks the rows at night. I've seen you. With the little book."

The word landed in Emrys's chest like a dropped surgical instrument. "I don't know what you mean."

"Don't you." The sergeant almost smiled. It cost him something; the smile kept breaking apart at the edges. "Braydon. Sergeant Elias Braydon. Fourth Company, Ninth Regiment of Foot." He said it slowly, formally, the way a man recites the articles of his own faith, and then he watched Emrys's face while he said it, and whatever he found there made him nod. "That means something to you."

"It can't," Emrys said quietly. "The Ninth Regiment was never deployed."

"No," Braydon agreed. "It wasn't. Never deployed. Never reinforced, either. Funny how much a regiment that doesn't exist can bleed."

The ward sounds went distant, the way sound does when the blood leaves your head. Emrys reached into their pocket without deciding to, fingers finding the small hard shape they carried everywhere now—a button, brass, stamped with a number that officially belonged to no campaign, no engagement, no history at all. Tomas's button. The boy clutched its twin and would not let anyone touch it.

Emrys set it in the sergeant's open palm.

Braydon looked at it for a long moment. Then he closed his fingers over it, carefully, the way one closes a hand over a coal.

"Where did you get this?"

"A child," Emrys said. "Left at our door. He doesn't speak. He holds the other one."

The sergeant breathed out, long and slow, and some tension left him—not relief exactly, more the loosening of a knot tied too long. "Then God is more careless than they say, sending him here. Or more deliberate." He turned his head and looked out past Emrys, down the row of cots, at nothing. "Sit properly, nurse. And listen. I've got perhaps an hour of dictation in me, and I mean to spend every minute of it."

* * *

"The Ninth went out in October," Braydon said. "Two hundred and six men, four officers, a full baggage train. You will not find us on any order of march, because we were not meant to be anywhere. We were a correction. We were the thing that happens after the thing that isn't supposed to happen has happened."

Emrys took out the ledger. Their hands were steady—they were always steady, that was the terrible joke of them—but inside, something had begun to move like water under ice.

"Dictate," they said.

"Fourth Company, Ninth Regiment of Foot. Sergeant Elias Braydon, service number—" he gave it, and Emrys wrote it, and beneath it the date of his enlistment eleven years gone, and beneath that, in the column Emrys had ruled off for this purpose alone, the words he spoke next.

"Ordered to hold the pass at Kellin's Drift. Ordered to hold it until relieved. We were told relief would come within thirty-six hours." He paused, gathering strength, and Emrys saw the effort of speech in his throat working like a pump. "We held it nine days."

"Who gave the order?"

"That's the heart of it, isn't it." Braydon's mouth twisted. "We never saw the man. That was deliberate too—you don't put your signature where it can be shot at. Orders came down in writing. Written orders, nurse, do you understand what I'm telling you? Somebody sat in a warm room with a pen and committed us to that pass on paper, and that paper went into a file, and when we didn't come back the file went into a fire, or so I assumed." His eyes came back to Emrys, bright and terrible. "But files are copied. Clerks copy things. It's the one honest habit the army has."

"You saw the order itself."

"I saw the last one. Day eight, when we were down to sixty men fit to stand and the ammunition was being weighed by the handful. A runner came up with written confirmation: *hold position, reinforcement imminent.* Signed." He stopped. Swallowed. "I won't tell you I read the name. I was busy dying at the time. But I'll tell you what I know, and what I know is this: no colonel writes that order. Holding a pass with no relief isn't tactics. It's arithmetic. You send men somewhere to be subtracted, and only one kind of officer does subtraction with other people's regiments—the kind who needs a disaster that can't be traced to a name. The kind with a promotion coming."

The ward tilted slightly. Emrys gripped the edge of the cot frame.

"There's a major," Emrys said, very evenly. "Corvane."

Braydon studied them. "You've met him."

"He inspects the infirmary."

"Then you've met the man who owns my death." He said it without heat, the way one states a debt. "Yes. Corvane. I never had proof, nurse—I had weather, and guesswork, and the shape of a thing. But you're holding a child who walked out of a village that Corvane reported as empty of civilians before it burned, and I'm lying here with a bullet the army will record as *received in action, location undisclosed*, and between us we've got most of a truth. What we haven't got is time. Mine's spent. Yours—" he glanced at the ledger, at Emrys's careful hand, "—yours is just beginning, and it will be shorter than you think if you write it carelessly. So write it carefully."

And then, for nearly an hour, while the winter light crawled along the canvas seams and the ward breathed around them, Sergeant Elias Braydon dictated.

He gave names. Not all of them—he was honest about the gaps, honest in a way no official report would ever be honest—but he gave forty-one, rank by rank, service number by service number, and where he knew them the small human particulars the ledgers of armies never carry: Private Wren, who could imitate every bird in the county; Corporal Ashby, who'd promised his girl a piano; young Tull, seventeen, who lied about his age so badly the recruiting sergeant kept the lie out of pity. Emrys wrote until their wrist ached and their eyes burned and the pages filled, and when Braydon faltered Emrys read back what was written and he corrected it, fussy and precise as a man balancing accounts he intended to leave square.

Forty-one names. Forty-one men who had held a pass for nine days and died in a place the maps had been ordered to forget.

"Why?" Emrys asked at last, when the list was done and the silence had weight again. "Why does it matter to you? You're dying either way. The army will bury you as unknown. Why spend your last hour on paper?"

Braydon was quiet for a while. Outside, wind moved the guy-ropes, and somewhere down the ward a man moaned in his sleep and was quieted by a comrade's hand.

"When we'd been holding four days," he said finally, "and it was clear even to the stupid ones what we were there for, Tull—young Tull, seventeen, bird-bones and big eyes—came to me in the dark and asked me if we were going to be all right. And I told him yes." His voice did not break. It simply stopped carrying weight for a moment, like a rope going slack. "I told him yes because that's what sergeants say. And he believed me, and he died believing me, and the difference between those two facts is the whole of my life now. If nobody says their names, nurse—if nobody stands up in some room someday and says *these men existed, and they were lied to, and they died anyway*—then what I told Tull in the dark becomes true. Then it's fine that they vanished. Then forgetting is just tidiness." He turned his head with great effort and looked directly at Emrys. "I need you to make sure the universe stays untidy. Can you do that?"

Emrys found they could not speak immediately. When they could, they said, "I have done it for twenty-two names already. I will do it for forty-one. I will do it for however many there are."

"I know," Braydon said. "I watched you walk the rows at night. A man learns what people are by watching what they do when nobody's paying them." He shifted, wincing, and pressed the button back into Emrys's hand, folding their fingers over it. "Give that back to the boy. Tell him—" he stopped, and started again, choosing the words like footing on broken ground. "Tell him the men who wore these stood where they were told to stand. That's all. That's the eulogy. We stood where we were told, and it wasn't our fault, and somebody should write that down."

"I'm writing it down," Emrys said. "Every word."

"Good." He settled back against the blanket, and something in his face eased, the way a landscape eases at dusk. "Good. Then I'm done. You go on. There's others needing hands, and I've got an appointment with the obvious." The ghost of the smile again. "Leave the book where I can see it a moment longer, though. Just a moment. I'd like to watch it exist."

Emrys left it open on the blanket beside his hand, the ink still drying on forty-one lives, and Braydon looked at it the way starving men look at bread.

* * *

Sergeant Elias Braydon died at quarter past four in the afternoon, with the snow still falling and the ward lamps just being lit. He went quietly, between one breath and the next, in the manner of men who have finished what they stayed for. The orderly assigned to the dead asked what name to enter in the register, and Emrys gave it—full name, rank, regiment—and the orderly blinked, because everyone knew the Ninth had never been deployed, and Emrys met his eye and repeated it, and after a moment the man wrote it down. Small rebellions, Emrys thought. They start with a clerk's pen and they end somewhere no one predicts.

By evening Emrys was back at the corner cot with the ledger open, transcribing the day's entries into fair copy, checking each service number twice against Braydon's slow dictation still ringing in memory. Forty-one names. Twenty-two before, forty-one now. Sixty-three reasons, and a boy asleep in the linen store clutching a twin of the button in Braydon's palm, and a major with a promotion pending and polished boots and a habit of cataloguing faces.

It was Agnes who brought the news, near midnight. She came down the ward without hurrying, which Emrys had learned was her way of making bad news walk slower.

"Courier came in with the evening post," she said, low. "Garrison orders for the new year. Honours list, promotions, transfers." She paused at the foot of the cot, and her face, which had survived three wars by revealing nothing, revealed something now. "Corvane's commission comes through December the thirty-first. Brigadier. With authority to close all operational records predating his command."

Emrys looked down at the ledger. Sixty-three names in candlelight, ink the colour of old wounds.

"Eleven days," they said.

"Ten," said Agnes. "And a river checkpoint, and a rail yard, and the governor's gate between here and anyone who'd listen." She straightened. "Sleep if you can, Brightwater. Tomorrow you'll want your wits, and I suspect you're going to need every one of them."

Outside, the snow went on falling, patient and white, covering everything equally—the graves and the paths and the lies alike—as though the world itself had joined the conspiracy of forgetting. Emrys blew out the candle, slid the ledger beneath the floorboard, and sat in the dark a long while, listening to the ward breathe, doing the only arithmetic left.

Ten days. Three checkpoints. One child.

One chance.
