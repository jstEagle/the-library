## 7. Three Checkpoints, One Night

The candle had burned down to its last thumb's width when Emrys finally drew the map.

They had chosen the dispensary for it—the small stone room at the back of the infirmary where laudanum bottles lined the shelves and the window was shuttered with a board nailed crooked, so that no light escaped in a clean line but only in broken slivers that might pass for moonlight on frost. Sister Agnes stood at the door with her ear tilted toward the corridor, listening to the night sounds of the hospital: the moaning of the men who would not see Christmas whole, the squeak of an orderly's boots, the wind worrying at the canvas of the new tents. Below them, in the ward, Tomas slept in the cot nearest the stove, curled around the regimental button as though it were warm and breathing.

"Draw it properly," Agnes said without turning. "If we are going to hang for this, I should like it done neatly."

Emrys dipped the stub of pencil into the candle smoke, held the point in the black ribbon of soot above the flame until it darkened, and began to draw upon the back of a requisition form—one of Corvane's own, stamped QUARTERMASTER'S OFFICE along the top, which struck Emrys as a small and private joke worth the risk. The smoke-blackened lead moved slowly, deliberately: the river first, a long gray stroke curving across the upper third of the page.

"The ferry crossing," they said. "Twelve miles northeast, where the road from the valley comes down to the water. There is a checkpoint on the near bank—a timber gate, a hut, a patrol that walks the bank on the hour. They stop every cart. They check papers."

"They checked mine twice last month," Agnes murmured. "Boys younger than the sentries doing the checking. That one worries me less than the boat."

"There is no bridge for forty miles either direction. Everything crosses by ferry—grain, coal, wounded men going home to die in their mothers' houses. The ferryman is called Odran. He took my brother's coffin across that river, years ago." Emrys did not look up from the page. "He owes me nothing. But he hated the major before any of this, and hatred is a currency that does not devalue."

"And if his hatred has been priced out by army silver?"

"Then we drown in the river or we hang in the square, and either way it is over quickly." Emrys said it flatly, and heard how it sounded, and wished they had not. The candle guttered. Smoke curled upward, and for a moment the shadow it threw against the shelf of bottles looked like a man standing very still, watching.

*You are being foolish,* Emrys told themselves, the way they had told themselves every night since the child had appeared at the door. *You are a nurse. You mend what the war breaks. You are not meant to carry wars out the back gate in a laundry basket.* But the thought would not take hold, because it had never truly taken hold—not since the ledger's first name, not since the day Emrys had understood that mending was only half the work, and that the other half was remembering, and that remembering, done honestly, was itself a kind of war.

Three checkpoints. One night—that was not literal, of course; the journey could not be made in a single night, twelve miles to the river, then two days by rail if the freight schedules held, then the last approach to the capital through country that grew flatter and more watched with every mile. But it must be *as* one night: continuous, unbroken, each crossing feeding into the next, so that there was never a moment when Emrys and the boy sat still long enough for Corvane's net to close around them. A hare does not pause between hedges. A hare pauses once, and is eaten.

"The river first," Emrys said, writing it in small, smoke-dark capitals. RIVER CHECKPOINT — FERRY — NIGHT OF THE 27TH. "Then the rail yard at Marren Junction. That is the second gate."

*

The rail yard troubled Emrys more than the river did, and they said so while Agnes brewed chicory over a spirit lamp and the soot-map grew a second set of marks: a sprawl of sidings, a long rectangle for the goods shed, a crosshatch where the military siding ran behind wire.

"A rail yard is not a gate you talk your way through," Emrys said. "It is a town built for the army's convenience, with one road in and one road out, and soldiers at both, and clerks—clerks are worse than soldiers, they count things. Every wagon has a manifest. Every manifest has a destination. You cannot charm a manifest."

"You could forge one."

"I could forge a pass. I have forged passes before." Emrys thought of the sickening ease of it, the way the quartermaster's signature had come off their pen looking more confident than anything Emrys had ever signed honestly. "But a pass gets a person through a gate. It does not get a person onto a freight train bound for the capital, hidden among cargo, past men whose entire trade is noticing what should not be aboard."

Agnes poured the chicory into two chipped cups and pushed one across the table. Steam rose between them, ghosting the map.

"Then you do not hide," she said. "At the river you can hide—in barrels, under nets, whatever Odran allows. At the yard you walk on. Two travelers going home for the New Year. The trains will be full of them. Every regiment wants its men seen to be festive; even Corvane's kind believe a soldier at Christmas looks better in dispatches than a soldier in a trench." She sipped, wincing at the bitterness. "Who staffs the yard gate?"

Emrys hesitated. This was the part they had turned over and over in the dark hours, the part that lay in the chest like a swallowed stone. "I don't know," they said, which was a lie, and Agnes knew it was a lie, because she had nursed beside Emrys through two winters and could read a falsehood the way she read a fever chart.

"Lieutenant Hale," she said quietly.

The name sat on the table between the cups.

"He is adjutant now," Agnes went on, merciless in her gentleness. "Adjutants get posted wherever the major wants eyes. And the major will want eyes at Marren Junction, because it is the only rail line to the capital and he is not a stupid man, whatever else he is. If anyone stands at that second gate, Emrys, it is your oldest friend wearing Corvane's colors."

Emrys looked down at the map. In the smoke-darkened ink the rail yard looked like a trap drawn by a child—all straight lines and corners, no way through but the way in. They thought of Hale as he had been at seventeen: lanky, laughing, keeping count of everything the way boys do—runs scored, birds counted, pennies owed—Hale, who had once walked six miles in the rain to bring Emrys a book of poems because Emrys had mentioned wanting it exactly once. Hale, who had shaken hands with Emrys's brother the week before the march that killed him. Hale, who wrote letters that said *duty* where other men wrote *honor*, meaning something quieter and truer by it.

And Hale, lately, who had stopped writing altogether.

"If he recognizes me," Emrys said at last, "he must choose. In the space of a breath, with his corporal standing behind him, he must choose what he is. I cannot make that choice for him. I can only decide whether I am willing to put him in the place where it must be made."

"Are you?"

The candle flame bent sideways in a draft, righted itself. Emrys thought of the forty names in the ledger, written in a hand so small it might have been a moth's tracks. They thought of Tomas asleep below, dreaming whatever children dream after villages burn.

"I don't know," Emrys said. "God forgive me—I don't know. Ask me again at the gate."

*

The third mark went at the bottom of the page, beneath everything else, the way the capital sat beneath everything else: the whole war, the whole winter, the whole weight of the year pressing down toward that single point. Emrys drew the city wall as a heavy line, and the gate—a great arched mouth in it—as a small circle, and inside the circle they pressed the pencil so hard the soot-blackened lead snapped.

Agnes handed them a knife to re-sharpen it and watched the governor's gate take shape.

"That is the one that kills you," she said. It was not a question.

"It is the one that kills everyone. By the thirtieth the city will be thick with holiday traffic—farm carts, gentry carriages, officers coming down for the garrison balls. And Corvane knows that traffic is his last chance to catch us, because on the thirty-first his commission is gazetted. After that—" Emrys tapped the page. "After that, the records pass under his authority entirely. Officially sealed pending inquiry, inquiry indefinitely postponed, and every document I might need becomes a piece of paper he owns. A dead thing. He does not even have to burn them. Ownership is slower than fire, but it is just as thorough."

"So the gate on the thirtieth."

"On the thirtieth. And they will search everything, Agnes. He will have doubled the guard; his patron will have lent him men. They will turn out every cart, open every trunk, run their hands along every lining—because by then, if he is half as frightened as I hope he is, he will know there is a ledger. He may not know its shape, but frightened men search linings." Emrys sat back and looked at the finished map: river, rails, gate. Three gates, and behind each one some version of the same arithmetic—papers or luck or love, and no guarantee of any.

For a long moment neither spoke. Then Agnes leaned forward and did something Emrys had not expected. She put one blunt finger on the little circle of the governor's gate and dragged it slightly aside, redrawing it in her mind rather than on the page.

"You're thinking like a smuggler," she said. "Smugglers hide things. Hiding is for objects that look guilty. Tell me—what does a nurse look like, walking into the capital two days before New Year?"

Emrys stared at her.

"A nurse looks like duty," Agnes said. "A nurse with a child at her skirts looks like charity, and no sergeant in this army wants to be the man who turned away charity at Christmas in front of a crowd. You will not sew the ledger into a false bottom. You will sew it into the boy's coat—into the lining, flat, page by page, where a hand pats but doesn't pry—and you will walk through the middle of the gate in broad daylight with your chin up and your papers real, and you will let them see exactly what you are showing them, which is nothing at all except a tired woman and a quiet child." She withdrew her finger. "Boldness reads as innocence. My mother taught me that at market. She also taught me never to buy fish on Mondays, but the first lesson served me better."

Emrys laughed—a short, startled sound, the first laugh in days, and it hurt somewhere under the ribs like a stitch from running. "Sister Agnes," they said, "you have survived three wars by seeing nothing."

"I have survived three wars," Agnes said, "by seeing precisely what I choose. It is a subtler discipline. Now—" she turned the requisition form toward herself, studying the smoke-drawn map with the eye of a woman who had provisioned hospitals through blockades, "—rations. You'll need four days' food that doesn't spoil, and you'll eat it cold, and you'll hate it. Money—Odran will want coin whether his heart is in it or not, and the yard porters expect tipping the way clergy expect tithes. A compass, because the shepherd paths north of the river are easy to lose in snow. And a story."

"A story?"

"For the boy. Not for the guards—for him. Children smell fear the way horses do, and worse, they announce it. Give him a part to play. Make it a game: *we are going to visit my aunt, and at each gate you must be sleepy, because sleepy children are boring children.* Boring is armor." Agnes drained her cup and stood, joints cracking like green wood. "I'll see to the food tonight. The rest is yours."

She paused at the door, hand on the latch, and did not turn around.

"When I was a girl," she said, "my village flooded. River took the bridge and eleven houses. What saved people wasn't strength—it was knowing the ground. Knowing which field went under first, which lane stayed dry. Maps, Emrys. The ones in the head." She opened the door a crack, letting in the ward's dim warmth and the sound of Tomas turning in his sleep. "You have drawn a good map. Sleep now. Tomorrow I teach you to sew a coat lining so no judge's clerk will ever find the seam."

*

Alone, Emrys did not sleep. They sat with the map a while longer, reading it as one reads a letter from someone feared dead—over and over, hunting for what might have been missed.

RIVER CHECKPOINT. Ferryman whose grief might be coin. A patrol on the hour. Searchlight if the gunboats were patrolling the reach—and they had been, twice this month, their lamps sweeping the water like slow white questions.

RAIL YARD AT MARREN JUNCTION. A gate, a manifest, a friend. *Ask me again at the gate.*

GOVERNOR'S GATE. Boldness reading as innocence. A coat with a seam that would hold a war inside it.

And above all three, written in the corner of the page where a date belongs on official correspondence, Emrys wrote the number that governed everything: **31**. December the thirty-first. The hour the records died and Corvane ascended, sealed and untouchable, promoted out of the reach of every name Emrys had ever written down. Four days. Four days and three gates, with a silent boy and a stolen truth, against a man who commanded soldiers, clerks, and the particular lethal patience of institutions protecting themselves.

Emrys passed the pencil stub through the candle flame until it caught, and touched it to the corner of the map, and watched the fire crawl inward from the date like winter eating a field—because a map drawn in candle smoke must not survive to be found, and because there was a strange comfort in watching the plan burn *after* memorizing it, the way a prayer burns after being spoken. The paper curled. The river vanished first, then the rails, then the gate. Ash drifted down onto the table like the first gray flakes of the snow that had begun, beyond the shuttered window, to fall.

Emrys brushed the ash into their palm and carried it to the stove in the ward, and stood a moment over Tomas's cot. The boy slept with one fist closed tight, and even in sleep his fingers had arranged themselves around the absent button, guarding the small round fact of it. His face, slack with sleep, looked impossibly young and impossibly old at once—the face of everyone in the ledger, and of no one in it, because he alone had come back.

"Four days," Emrys whispered, in the tone of a vow or a wager.

Below the floorboard, in its hiding place, the ledger waited with its forty names and room for more. Above it, in the corridor, boots were coming—measured, unhurried, wrong for the hour—and stopped outside the dispensary door, and knocked twice, softly, the knock of a man who did not wish to be heard by others but needed Emrys to hear him now.

Emrys knew that knock. They had known it across ten years and two wars, through every letter answered and every letter lately unanswered, and their heart went cold and hot at once, because it was the knock of Lieutenant Hale—Corvane's own adjutant—standing in the falling snow at midnight on the wrong side of every line Emrys had just drawn.

Emrys opened the door.
