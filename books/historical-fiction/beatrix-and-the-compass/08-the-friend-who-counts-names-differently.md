## 8. The Friend Who Counts Names Differently

Lieutenant Edmund Hale had learned to count in three currencies during his years under Major Corvane: men, minutes, and mistakes. The first he spent freely, at the major's direction. The second he hoarded jealously, because Corvane's staff meetings began and ended on the stroke of the hour and lateness was the only sin the major punished faster than incompetence. The third—the mistakes—he collected the way other men collected debt, quietly, in a ledger of his own that existed nowhere but behind his eyes.

It was the ledger in Emrys Brightwater's possession that occupied him now.

He had discovered it by accident, which was how all the worst things in his life arrived. Three days past midwinter, Corvane had sent him to the infirmary with requisition orders for morphine and chloroform—paperwork, pure paperwork—and Hale had arrived to find Emrys kneeling at the open floorboard beneath the third cot in the recovery ward, a slim black book in their hands, their face wearing an expression he recognized from twenty years ago, from the orchard behind the Brightwater farm, from the day they'd buried a songbird together and Emrys had refused, absolutely refused, to pretend it hadn't mattered.

Emrys had looked up. He had seen the book. Emrys had seen him see it. And neither of them had said a single word, then, because Sister Agnes had been coming down the ward with a tray of dressings, and some silences are themselves a kind of language—one that old friends speak fluently even after years apart.

Now it was two nights later, and Hale sat at the desk in his quarters with a lamp turned low and Corvane's correspondence spread before him like a losing hand of cards. He should have been drafting the major's year-end report. Instead he was doing arithmetic.

The arithmetic went thus: if he reported what he had seen—a nurse concealing an unauthorized document in hospital property, in wartime, under the nose of the commanding officer—then by regulation he must also report the document for seizure and inspection. If the document was inspected, its contents would be read. If its contents were read, then forty names—or however many names there were—would pass through Corvane's hands before New Year's Eve, when the major's commission came through and every record in this garrison became his personal kingdom, sealed and stamped and legally beyond the reach of any inquiry. Hale knew how Corvane treated inconvenient paper. He had watched it go into the stove in the major's office more than once, fed page by page with the unhurried care of a man stoking warmth into his own conscience.

And if Hale did not report it—if he kept Emrys's secret—then he was no longer merely an adjutant keeping his head down. He was an accessory. He was a man who had stood in Corvane's office while the deployment reports were drafted and signed them himself, *E. Hale, Lt., adjutant*, in his neat clerk's hand, and told himself each time that signatures were not orders, that ink was not blood, that he was only the pen and not the hand.

He was thirty-nine years old and he could no longer remember whether he believed that.

* * *

The trouble with Emrys, Hale thought, walking the perimeter road that evening with his collar up against the wind, was that they had never let him be comfortable. Most people, given a friend, wanted the friendship to be a soft place. Emrys had always wanted it to be a true place, and the two were rarely the same.

They had met as children over the body of that songbird, struck by frost from the hedge row. Tomas—no, that was another name entirely, one he did not yet know he would come to know—anyway: Emrys had been eight and already fierce about small things, cradling the frozen bird in cupped hands as though it might wake, and Hale had been the practical one, the one who said *it's dead, Emrys, it's only a bird*, and Emrys had looked at him with those grey eyes gone flinty and said, *Then we'll say so. We won't say it fell.* Even at eight, Emrys understood the difference between what happened and what got written down.

Hale had forgotten that for twenty years. Or perhaps he had not forgotten it; perhaps he had spent twenty years building his life precisely on the forgetting.

His commission, his posting, his clean quarters and steady pay—all of it rested on his willingness to count men the way Corvane counted them: as entries, expenditures, acceptable losses. A regiment ordered to hold a position that would never be reinforced was not a tragedy; it was a line item. A village burned in the aftermath was not a crime; it was a consequence. And a boy missing from that village was not a witness; he was a loose thread, and Corvane had asked him only yesterday, in that mild voice the major used for his most dangerous sentences, whether Hale thought it likely a child could survive alone in this country in December.

*I shouldn't think so, sir,* Hale had said, and heard his own voice say it, smooth as a signature.

*No,* Corvane had agreed, smiling faintly at the window. *I shouldn't think so either.*

* * *

Hale found Emrys where he knew he would find them: the linen store, between the second shift and lights-out, where the smell of lye soap and cold starch thickened the air enough that even the wind outside seemed muffled. It was the one room in the infirmary without patients, and Emrys had claimed it years ago the way cats claim windowsills—by simply being there so consistently that everyone else forgot it had ever been otherwise.

They looked up when he entered, and their face closed like a ledger snapping shut.

"You've come to ask what you saw," Emrys said.

"I've come," Hale said, "to tell you what I saw."

"Those aren't the same thing."

"No," he admitted. "They aren't."

He took off his cap. It was an officer's habit, standing bareheaded in a linen store like a man at a graveside, and he supposed that in a way he was. There was a version of this conversation in which he arrested Emrys Brightwater on suspicion of theft of military documents, and there was a version in which he turned and walked out and said nothing, and he had considered both versions all day with the honest diligence he brought to everything, and found both of them intolerable. So here he was, in the third version, the one he had no plan for.

"There's a search coming," he said. "Corvane's requested authority from division. It'll be here within the week—sooner, if the child is found anywhere near your infirmary."

Something moved behind Emrys's eyes—fear, quickly mastered, and underneath the fear something harder that Hale recognized with a sinking heart. Not panic. Purpose.

"You don't know what's in that book," Emrys said.

"I can guess."

"You can't." Emrys stepped closer, and the lamplight from the corridor caught their face, thinner than he remembered, older than thirty-two ought to look. "You sign papers, Eddie. I write down what the papers do. Do you know what I mean by that? Sergeant Okafor, who held the ford at Kessel with a broken arm because the order said hold and no order ever came that said go. Private Lund, sixteen years old, listed as died of fever when the field surgeon will swear to you he took shrapnel in the retreat. Forty-one names, Eddie. Forty-one men whose deaths have been unwritten, and every one of them unwritten by the same hand." They stopped, breathing hard, as though the words had cost physical effort to carry. "There's a boy asleep upstairs with a button from a regiment that was officially never deployed. You signed that deployment report. I've seen the copy."

The silence afterward was very long. Somewhere down the ward a man moaned in his sleep, and the sound rose and faded like weather.

"Yes," Hale said finally. "I signed it."

He had expected saying it aloud to feel like falling. Instead it felt like setting down a weight he had carried so long he'd mistaken it for his own body.

"I didn't know," he said, and heard immediately how thin that was, and pressed on anyway, because Emrys deserved to watch him fail honestly. "I didn't want to know. That isn't better. I'm not asking you to say it's better. But Emrys—listen to me—" and he crossed the space between them and gripped their shoulders, feeling how slight they were, how stubbornly unbowed, "—if Corvane finds that book, he doesn't just burn it. He burns the person who wrote it. Quietly. The way he does everything. You'll be a fever death or a desertion or a regrettable accident, and I will spend the rest of my life counting that among my mistakes, and my arithmetic is already ugly enough."

Emrys did not pull away. That was the worst of it—that they stood there and let him hold their shoulders and looked at him with something worse than anger, which was grief.

"Then help me get it out," they said quietly.

"I can't."

"You can. You're his adjutant. You know his movements, his passes, his—"

"*I can't.*" His voice cracked on it, and he was ashamed of the crack, and more ashamed of the sentence. He released them and stepped back and stood in the middle of the linen store like a man whose legs had been taken from him. "If I help you openly, we both hang, and the book hangs with us. If I do nothing, he finds it within the week. Those are the true numbers, Emrys. I've run them all day. Everything else is wishing."

Emrys's jaw set. He knew that jaw; he had known it across school desks and sickbeds and one long argument at a railway platform eleven years ago that had ended their speaking acquaintance until the war threw them back together in worse circumstances. When Emrys set their jaw like that, the world was about to lose an argument.

"Give me your hand," Hale said.

"Why?"

"Because I'm going to do the one thing left to me, and I'd rather you understand it before you hate me for it."

Slowly, warily, Emrys extended their hand. Hale took it in both of his, and from inside his tunic he drew out a key—a small brass key on a length of cord—and pressed it into their palm, and closed their fingers over it.

"The floorboard under the third cot has a lock," he said. "Old pattern, half-tumbler. I fitted it myself last spring, when Agnes complained the medicine cabinet couldn't be secured. There are two keys. One is in your pocket right now, I expect. This is the other."

Emrys stared at the key, then at him, uncomprehending.

"I'm taking it," Hale said, and his voice was steadier than he had any right to expect. "Not tonight. Before the search. I'll lift it from wherever you've hidden it, or you'll give it to me, but either way it goes with me—and when Corvane tears your infirmary apart looking for proof, he will find a locked box under a floorboard with no key anywhere on the premises, and no lock that anyone on his staff can pick quickly, and he will stand there hating every minute of it because the one thing he cannot afford is to break it open in front of witnesses and reveal that he cares what's inside." He swallowed. "It buys you days. Maybe only two. Possibly three. It is not rescue, Emrys. It is a merchant buying you time at the counter, and I am charging myself everything I have."

"And what does it cost you?" Emrys asked, though he could see in their face that they already knew.

"A duplicate key to restricted garrison hardware, in the hands of an officer under suspicion of nothing yet. If it's traced, it's my career. If the wrong person traces it, it's my neck." He smiled, and was surprised to find the smile almost easy. "But here's the part I need you to hear, and then I need to leave before anyone sees us. I am not helping you. Do you understand? Whatever you're planning—and I know you, Emrys Brightwater, I know you've been planning since the day that knock came at your door—I am not helping you, and I will deny knowing anything about it, and I will do it convincingly, because I have spent six years learning to lie to that man in a polished uniform and I am very good at it now."

"Then why take the key at all?"

"Because if I don't," Hale said, "you'll keep it on your own body, and the night they search *you* instead of the infirmary, it will hang you." He closed Emrys's fingers again around nothing, for he had already taken the key back, holding it up between them so the brass caught the corridor light. "Whereas if it sits in my pocket, it is one officer's small indiscretion, discoverable by nobody unless somebody looks at me very hard indeed. And nobody," he added, with a bitterness that surprised them both, "looks at the adjutant."

For a moment Emrys searched his face, reading it the way they had read faces across twenty years, looking for the flaw in the arithmetic. Then their expression broke—not into forgiveness, exactly, nor gratitude, but into something rawer and older, the look of someone watching a friend walk into weather and unable to call them back.

"Eddie," they said. "When this ends—whichever way it ends—you'll have to account for yourself too. You know that. What you signed. What you saw."

"Yes," Hale said. "I've done the sums."

* * *

He walked back across the parade ground under a sky the color of unpolished pewter, and the snow began somewhere above the darkness and came down fine and dry, settling on his shoulders without weight, the way guilt settles when you've carried it long enough.

In his pocket, the duplicate key lay against his palm like a coin for a ferryman.

He thought about Corvane at his desk, mild-voiced, feeding pages to the stove. He thought about the boy upstairs in the infirmary, whom he had not yet seen but whose existence had already rearranged the major's entire calendar. He thought about the report on his desk, unfinished, and about the morning's routine that would begin in seven hours, and about how simple loyalty had seemed once—how it had felt like a coat that fit, and how he had never noticed it tightening until tonight.

At his quarters he paused at the door and looked back toward the infirmary wing, where one lamp still burned in a ground-floor window. Emrys would be awake. Emrys was always awake, writing down the names of the dead so the dead would stay written, doing the work he had refused to do with a pen he had sharpened himself.

"They'll say you chose wrong," he said aloud, to no one, to the snow. "One of us did."

He went inside, sat down at his desk, and did not touch the major's report. Instead, on a scrap of paper he would burn within the hour, he wrote out the thing he had never written anywhere: *Kessel Ford. Ordered to hold. No relief authorized. My signature below.*

Then he folded it small, and held it over the lamp, and watched his own confession curl and blacken—because he was not ready, not yet, not quite—but he memorized every word first, the way Emrys had taught him, without meaning to, twenty years ago beside a frozen bird:

We'll say so. We won't say it fell.

Three days later, the search warrants arrived from division headquarters bearing the major's seal, and Lieutenant Hale, rising from his desk to receive them, felt the duplicate key shift in his breast pocket like a small, stubborn heartbeat—and knew, with perfect clarity, that whatever came next, the counting had only just begun.
