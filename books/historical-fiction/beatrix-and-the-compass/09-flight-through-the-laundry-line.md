## 9. Flight Through the Laundry Line

The knock came at half past four in the morning, and it was not the knock of a messenger.

Emrys had learned to read knocks the way sailors read weather. The soft double tap of the night orderly asking for laudanum. The brisk rattle of the adjutant's man with his requisition forms. This one was flat, heavy, deliberate—the knock of men who did not need to ask for anything. It landed against the infirmary door like a stone dropped down a well.

They were already awake. Emrys had not truly slept since Major Corvane's inspection three days past, and Tomas had not slept in Emrys's presence since the night before that, when he had begun, without explanation, to drag his blanket off the cot each evening and fold himself into the narrow gap between Emrys's bed and the wall, where the shadow was deepest. Emrys had stopped fighting him on it. There were things a child's body knew before a child's mind could say them.

Sister Agnes was already in the corridor when Emrys opened the cot-room door. She stood in her shawl with her grey hair pinned so severely it looked like armor, and her face—usually arranged into that careful blankness she called professional composure—was tight as a drumhead.

"Lanterns," she said. "At the gate. Six of them. And they didn't sign the visitors' book."

Emrys's hands went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the winter draft under the door.

"Where is he?"

"Where you put him. Under the floor." Agnes's eyes flicked toward the linen room, then back. "Brightwater. Listen to me, because we will not have this conversation twice. The ward must look like an ordinary morning. Men coughing, bandages being changed, water carried, all of it boring enough to drown a man. You will do your rounds as though your heart is not trying to climb out of your throat. And I—" her voice caught, just once, a hairline crack in old plaster, "—I will let them in."

* * *

The next hour was the longest performance of Emrys's life.

They came through the door with the particular courtesy of armed men, six of them, boots scraping snow-melt across the scrubbed boards, and behind them a seventh: a corporal with a clipboard and the apologetic expression of a clerk asked to search a church. He presented papers Emrys did not read. He said words like *routine* and *inventory discrepancy* and *nothing personal, Sister*, and all the while his eyes moved over the ward the way Corvane's had—not seeing patients but counting shapes.

Emrys changed a dressing. That was the whole of their strategy: change a dressing, badly if necessary, with hands that only shook a little. Private Okafor lay under their fingers with a wound in his thigh gone angry at the edges, and he watched Emrys's face with the shrewd patience of a man who had been lied to by officers long enough to recognize fear when it bent over him wearing a nurse's apron.

"Steady on," he murmured, so low only Emrys could hear. "Whatever it is. Steady on."

Emrys pressed the fresh lint down and did not trust themselves to answer.

The soldiers worked outward from the dispensary. They pulled drawers and shook out rolls of bandage with a thoroughness that was almost reverent—they had clearly been told exactly what they were looking for. A ledger. Papers. Names written by a hand that should not have been writing them. Emrys kept their eyes on Okafor's wound and performed arithmetic in their head: how long to strip the floorboard, how many paces from the linen room to the dispensary, whether the corporal's clipboard meant he would record every item or only the missing ones. Somewhere beneath their feet—or rather beneath the linen room floor, two rooms away—forty-one names slept in ink, and a boy slept above them, breathing as quietly as a child could who had learned that quiet was survival.

One soldier pushed open the linen room door.

Emrys did not turn their head. This required effort of a magnitude they could not have explained; it was as though the whole weight of their body had been poured into keeping the spine straight and the hands busy. From the corner of their eye they saw Agnes materialize in the linen room doorway ahead of the soldier, arms crossed, voice pitched to its flattest register.

"Mind the shelves. If you mix my sizes I'll have you rewashing them yourself, and the surgeon will have you boiled besides."

It was perfect. It was nothing. It was the complaint of a woman who had survived three wars precisely by making herself too tedious to bother with, and the soldier grumbled, and knocked a basket of clamps anyway, and moved on to the storeroom.

The floorboards held. Whatever instinct or informer had brought the soldiers, it had not given them the board under the third shelf, the one whose nail Agnes now stood upon in her sensible boots, quite literally, shifting her weight onto it as she supervised the shuffling of her own stores. Emrys understood then, with a clarity that felt like cold water, what the older woman had done in offering the supply route, the cart, the laundry line—she had chosen a side after thirty years of choosing no side at all, and she was standing on her choice.

By dawn light the corporal apologized again, recorded something, and left. The lanterns regrouped at the gate and moved off toward the officers' quarters, and the infirmary exhaled, sixty wounded men pretending they had noticed nothing, which was its own kind of solidarity, and Emrys went to the linen room, knelt, and lifted the board.

Tomas looked up at them from the dark, eyes enormous, one small fist closed around a regimental button.

"It's time," Emrys said. "Isn't it."

The boy nodded once. He had not spoken in nineteen days. He did not speak now. But he climbed out of the hiding place on his own, folded his blanket—he had brought it down into the dark with him—and waited to be told what came next, with a patience that broke Emrys's heart in a place they hadn't known was still whole.

* * *

Agnes had the cart ready by seven.

It was the supply cart, the one that went down to the town market twice a week, driven by whoever could be spared, waved through the river checkpoint on the strength of the hospital's stamp alone because even armies need their linens washed. Except today it would not go to the market. Today it would go, according to its manifest, to the rail depot with a consignment of boiled sheets for the transport ships, and between the layers of clean linen—folded with more care than Emrys had ever given any fabric in their life—a child would ride north.

"The laundry line first," Agnes said, walking Emrys through it while she loaded, her hands never stopping, her voice never rising. "You'll take the cart out the yard gate as always. Hobb will drive—he owes me more than money can count, and he can't read the manifest anyway, which has saved us both trouble for years. You walk beside it. You are going to the depot to supervise the consignment. That is true, so say it as though it is. Liars rehearse; honest people are merely bored."

"And if they stop the cart?"

"Then they stop the cart, and God help us both." She set a covered basket in the front, on top of the linens. Bread, cheese, a clay jug of water, a twist of paper Emrys did not ask about. "But they won't. Not today. Today they're looking at roads and gates, because Major Corvane believes everything important happens at gates. He'll think of carts tomorrow, or the day after, and by then—" She stopped herself. Straightened a fold that was already straight. "By then it will be someone else's problem to be brave."

Emrys took her hands. They were work-worn and cold and they did not tremble, and Emrys understood that the trembling had been rationed elsewhere, into Emrys's own knees, which felt like water.

"You saw everything," Emrys said. "All these years. You said once you survived by seeing nothing."

"I lied," Agnes said. "It's the only lie I've told since the Crimea, and I tell it daily." Her grip tightened, brief and fierce. "Go before I change my mind and hide you under the floor myself. I've grown fond of that floorboard."

Tomas emerged from the shadows of the wash-house dressed as a bundle of mending, which is to say wrapped in a sheet, hooded, and instructed—by signs, by the language of looks they had all developed around his silence—to lie still and breathe slowly and believe, if he could, in the goodness of folded cloth. Before he climbed into the cart he turned and looked up at Agnes for a long moment. Then he did something he had never done in nineteen days: he reached up and touched her hand, one finger, briefly, and let it fall.

Agnes made a sound low in her throat that she converted, mid-birth, into a cough.

"Off," she said. "Both of you. Off."

* * *

The yard gate was nothing. A sentry glanced at the cart, at the hospital stamp on the manifest Hobb held upside down, and waved them through into the white morning, and Emrys walked beside the wheel with their heart hammering so loudly they were certain it would frighten the horses, and nothing happened. Nothing happened, and the road unspooled ahead of them, rutted and frozen, lined with bare poplars, and still nothing happened, and Emrys discovered that the absence of catastrophe was its own vertigo, like a stair that isn't there.

They passed the first patrol half a mile on. Two soldiers, bored, breath steaming, eyes on the river. One nodded. Emrys nodded back. Their legs carried them forward on pure animal momentum while some distant part of the mind observed, with detached interest, *I am smuggling a child past the army of a nation at war, and I am doing it beside a cart of bedsheets, and the most dangerous thing so far is the ice.*

Hobb drove in silence. He was a stooped grey man with a face like a closed shutter, and he drove as though transporting contraband children were simply this week's variety of errand, which, Emrys reflected, in thirty years of Agnes's errands, it possibly was.

Halfway to the depot, Tomas shifted under the linens.

It was the smallest movement—a rustle, a settling—but it went through Emrys like a rifle shot. They made a sound they hoped resembled clearing their throat and began, loudly, to complain about the price of soap, and Hobb took the cue without looking and answered at equal volume about the price of oats, and the two of them conducted a full and furious argument about commerce while beneath the canvas a small boy laughed.

Emrys heard it and nearly stumbled.

He was laughing. Silently, mostly—a shaking of the cloth, a hitch of breath—but laughing, and Emrys thought: *nineteen days of silence, and it takes a farce about soap.* Something loosened in their chest that they had been carrying tight since the night of the note on the doorstep. *Forget the face.* Well. The face had not forgotten itself. The face was laughing under a pile of laundry, and whatever else happened at the depot, at the checkpoints, at the governor's gate—whatever Major Corvane and his polished menace and his silver ambitions brought down upon them—that laugh had already happened. It could not be unwritten. Emrys had spent years learning that some things survive even when people try to erase them. Here was the proof, giggling into boiled cotton.

* * *

The rail yard rose out of the flats like an argument the landscape was losing: sidings and smoke and freight cars furred with frost, soldiers on the platforms, and—Emrys's stomach dropped—more soldiers than there should have been. Checking papers at the yard gate. Turning over luggage. The manifest trick would not carry them through this; the yard was visibly not routine anymore.

Hobb saw it too. He drew the cart up in the shadow of a grain warehouse, set the brake, and spoke for the first time in an hour, quietly, to the horses' backs.

"Gate's thick. Won't thin before the noon freight, if then." He turned his head; the shutter of his face opened a crack, and underneath it his eyes were kind and very old. "There's another way in. The laundry line."

And he pointed—with his chin, discreetly—at the line itself: a long wire strung from the warehouse gable across the yard fence to the washing sheds beyond, hung with hundreds of sheets, hospital sheets, ship sheets, snapping pale and ghostly in the wind, a curtain of white wide enough to swallow a cart, a woman, a hidden boy, and every plan Emrys had made.

"Agnes sends two hundred sheets a week down that line," Hobb said. "Nobody counts sheets. Nobody ever has." He reached under his seat and produced, of all things, a laundry ticket, stamped and genuine. "Take the consignment through the sheds. The yard gate will wave you out the far side because you'll already be inside the counting. It's not clever," he added, almost apologetically. "It's just laundry."

Emrys lifted the linens. Tomas looked up at them, awake, composed, button in fist, and Emrys saw their own terror mirrored and mastered in the child's face, and felt the strangest thing bloom through the fear—not comfort exactly, and not courage, but exhilaration, bright and improper as a bell rung in church. For eleven years they had been invisible: the nurse who noticed, the quiet one, the name no officer bothered to learn. Invisibility had been armor and prison both. Now it was burning off them like morning fog, and underneath it Emrys Brightwater was still standing, still holding a child's hand, still—God help them—glad.

"In we go," Emrys said. "Walk slow. Look bored."

They slipped through the fence gap behind the sheds, into the fluttering white corridor of sheets, and the wind took the linen and hid them and showed them and hid them again—soldiers ten feet away, a sergeant's voice, the hiss of steam from the shed—and Emrys counted paces and breathed and felt, absurdly, alive in a way that frightened them more than the search parties did.

They were halfway across when the sheets parted ahead of them, deliberately, held back by a gloved hand, and Lieutenant Hale stood in the gap with a ledger of his own under his arm and a look on his face that Emrys had seen only once before, twenty years ago, the night Emrys's brother's name was read wrong at the memorial service and Hale had been the only one who said so out loud.

"Don't," Hale said softly. "Not another step until we talk."

Behind Emrys, under the sheet in the barrow, Tomas went utterly, practicedly still.
