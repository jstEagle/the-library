## 10. The River Checkpoint

The river announced itself before they saw it—a smell of wet iron and rotted reeds rising out of the dark like a warning whispered through cupped hands. Emrys felt it in their chest before their mind caught up: *the water. We have reached the water.*

They had walked since the small hours, following the laundry route Sister Agnes had traced for them with a flour-dusted finger—canal path, then hedgerow, then a disused drover's road where the mud held hoofprints of cattle long gone to slaughter. Tomas had ridden Emrys's back for the last hour, his head heavy against their shoulder blade, his breath coming slow and even through the wool of the borrowed scarf wrapped twice around the lower half of his face. A sleeping child looks like no child at all from a distance. From close up, he looks like exactly what he is.

The ferry landing crouched at the bottom of a slope of black grass, a single lantern hanging from a crooked post, its flame guttering sideways in the wind that came off the water. The river itself was invisible—just a vast breathing darkness on the far side of the landing, wide enough that the far bank might have been another country. Which, Emrys thought grimly, adjusting the strap of their medical satchel, in every way that mattered this winter, it was.

*One checkpoint down,* Agnes had said. *Then the rail yard, then the gate.* As if the saying of it made it possible.

The ferryman was waiting. That was the first miracle, and miracles, Emrys had learned, were never free.

He was an old man shaped by decades of rope and current, shoulders like a yoke, face seamed and brown as a saddle left out in all weathers. He sat on an overturned crate mending a net, and he did not look up when they came down the slope, which was itself a kind of courtesy—or a kind of theater. Emrys could not tell anymore which things in this country were genuine and which were performed for whoever might be watching. Perhaps the distinction had stopped existing everywhere. Perhaps performance was simply what survival called itself now.

"You'd be the nurse," the ferryman said, to his net rather than to them. His voice was gravel dragged over gravel. "Agnes's friend."

"I would."

"Pass?"

Emrys produced it—the forged travel authorization sewn into the lining of their coat, signed with the name of a quartermaster who had died of fever in October and could not object to lending his hand. The ink was good; Agnes had obtained it from a clerk who owed her more than he could repay in any honest currency. The paper trembled very slightly in Emrys's fingers, and they willed it still, and it mostly was.

The ferryman took it, held it to the lantern for so long that Emrys counted their own heartbeats—twelve, thirteen, fourteen—and then grunted, folded it, and handed it back without ever having appeared to read it.

"Tide's kind till three," he said. "After that, the patrol boat comes up from the weir on the half hour. You want to be across and in the trees before then."

"And the sentry?" Emrys asked, because Agnes had warned them: *the army posts a boy at the landing now. They took the older men for the front. What's left is children guarding doors that shouldn't need guarding.*

The ferryman spat neatly into the reeds. "Down the bank, warming himself at the brazier like a matchstick somebody stood up. He'll want to see your pass too. He'll want to look important. Let him." He rose at last, joints cracking like green wood in fire, and jerked his chin toward the flat-bottomed ferry moored below, low in the black water, stacked with cargo barrels lashed under tarpaulin. "You'll ride amidships. The boy rides—" he tapped one barrel with his boot, a hollow wooden sound, "—there. The tall one's empty except for straw. There's air in the top third, if he keeps his head down and doesn't sing."

Emrys's stomach turned over slowly, deliberately, like something heavy shifting in the hold of a ship.

"He's five years old," they said. "He's never once in my hearing sung."

"Then you've less to fear than most." The old man began uncoiling the mooring rope, unhurried, a man performing a task he had performed ten thousand times and expected to perform ten thousand more. "Fifteen minutes' crossing if the current holds its temper. Get him in before I light the second lamp. The second lamp means paying passengers, and paying passengers get looked at."

---

Getting Tomas into the barrel was the single worst thing Emrys had done since the night they found the ledger and understood what it was for.

Not because he fought it. That was the terrible part. He climbed the little rope ladder of Emrys's clasped hands and stepped over the barrel rim as obediently as a child stepping into a bath, and when Emrys arranged the straw around him and explained—again, softly, again—*you must not move, you must not sneeze, if you hear my voice talking to someone you must be a barrel, barrels don't breathe loudly, can you be a barrel?*—Tomas had only looked up at them through the dimness with those enormous dark eyes and nodded, once, with the grave solemnity of a soldier accepting orders.

That obedience was worse than screaming. Screaming would have meant he didn't understand. The nod meant he understood perfectly: that the world he had been born into was one where five-year-olds hid in barrels while soldiers passed, and that this was simply how things were done, the way harvests were done, the way winters were done. Somewhere in the burned village whose name Emrys refused to say aloud even inside their own skull, some adult had taught him to hide quietly. Emrys thought of the note tucked against the child's chest that first morning—*forget the face*—and wondered who had written it, and whether that person was alive, and whether forgetting was a mercy or just another order nobody should have had to follow.

"I'll be ten feet away," Emrys told him. "The whole time. Ten feet. You could throw a stone and hit me."

Tomas reached up out of the straw and touched Emrys's wrist, briefly, two fingers, the way you check a pulse. Then he pulled the tarpaulin shadow down over himself and became cargo.

Emrys stood in the dark of the landing with their heart going like a trapped bird and discovered that guilt is not a feeling but a geography—it has regions, elevations, weather systems of its own. Here was the guilt of hiding a living child among barrels as though he were contraband. Here, adjacent, the colder guilt of knowing the alternative: that Corvane's men had reported a missing child from a burned village in the same flat administrative tone they used for missing ammunition, and that what happened to found children in military custody did not bear the word *custody* honestly. Here, coldest of all, the memory of forty names in a hidden ledger, and the young faces those names had belonged to, and the arithmetic that kept insisting, cruelly, that every boy in the ledger had been somebody's Tomas once.

The ferryman lit the second lamp.

---

The sentry could not have been more than seventeen.

He came down the bank at the ferryman's hail, rifle slung with the careless discomfort of a boy who had been issued it rather than chosen it, and stood in the lantern light stamping his feet against the cold. He had the raw, unfinished face that the war had been printing copies of for two years now—cheeks still soft, jaw promising a man it had not yet delivered, eyes doing their best to look bored instead of frightened. His greatcoat was one size too large. His gloves had been repaired at both thumbs with different-colored thread.

*Private Alfred Wexler, age nineteen, listed as twenty-two.* The name surfaced in Emrys without permission, one of the forty, one of the pages sewn beneath floorboards a hundred miles behind them now. *Died of wounds, officially returned to duty, actual burial site unknown.* Every boy in the ledger had been this boy once. Every boy in the ledger had stood at some checkpoint or infirmary door with his thumb-threads showing, believing the paperwork that described him, trusting the institution that would erase him.

"Pass," the sentry said, and then, remembering some drilled sequence, added, "and state your business and destination."

"Medical personnel, attached transport," Emrys said, and heard their own voice come out level, unhurried, almost bored—boredom being the safest emotion anyone could perform at a checkpoint, boredom meaning *I am nobody, I have crossed a hundred crossings, do not waste your cold fingers on me.* It astonished them, distantly, how easily the lie came. Once—not so long ago—they had been a person who could not lie about a late dose of laudanum without blushing. The war had taught them many useless things and one essential one: the truth is a luxury, and luxuries are the first casualties.

The sentry read the pass. Or held it. His lips moved slightly over the printed header, and Emrys watched him arrive at the dead quartermaster's signature and find nothing suspicious in it, because why would he? Boys believe signatures. Boys believe the whole vast apparatus of stamps and seals and countersigns, because they have been raised on the faith that the machinery is honest, that somewhere above them are careful men doing careful work.

Emrys had seen what those careful men did with ledgers.

"Your cargo?" The sentry's breath hung in the lantern light. Beyond him, out on the black water, the ferryman busied himself with his pole, giving them privacy or alibi.

"Agricultural supplies and medical stores. You're welcome to inspect, Private, but the morphine crates are sealed and I'd have to note the seal numbers in my report, and Sergeant Maddox at the rail yard does love a discrepancy." Emrys let the smallest pause fall, the pause of a professional mildly inconvenienced by procedure. "It's your evening, of course."

It worked because it was built to work: the invented sergeant, the implied paperwork, the gentle suggestion that diligence here meant forms later. The sentry's ears went pink. He looked at the tarpaulined barrels, at the dark shapes of them, and Emrys felt the moment balance on its point like a coin spun on edge—because ten feet away, under that middle stretch of canvas, a five-year-old was being a barrel with all his small might, and if the sentry lifted one corner, if curiosity beat caution even for a heartbeat—

"Carry on," the sentry said, and handed the pass back, and his gloved fingers brushed Emrys's, and his thumb had a thread on it too.

Emrys boarded the ferry without running, which was the hardest physical act of their life.

---

The crossing was fifteen minutes of hell measured in water.

The river took the flat-bottomed boat into its grip almost immediately, a slow muscular shrug that set the barrels knocking gently against their lashings, and Emrys stood amidships pretending to be cargo themselves—still, dull, a shape the eye would slide off. The far bank was a rumor. The near bank shrank to a smear of lantern light, and the sentry on it shrank to a matchstick somebody stood up, and Emrys found themselves unable to stop watching until he was a pinprick, until he was gone, until he was safe, and then hated themselves for the relief of it. *He'll live tonight,* they told themselves. *Tonight he lives.* It was the best prayer they had.

Halfway across, the searchlight found the river.

It came around the bend downstream like a second moon gone wrong—a white blade sweeping the water from the patrol boat's mast, swinging in long patient arcs, touching the surface and turning it momentarily to hammered silver. The ferryman did not stop poling. He did not change rhythm. But his voice reached Emrys low, without turning his head:

"Routine sweep. They see a working ferry with a lamp, they log it and move on. Stand natural. Don't cover your face—only guilty people cover their faces."

*Only guilty people cover their faces.* Emrys stood in the open, hands loose at their sides, medical satchel plain on their shoulder, and let the light come for them. It seemed to take a year. The beam crawled along the water, touched the bow, climbed the gunwale—

—and swept over them, a brief cold blindness, the smell of river and lamp oil and their own sweat gone suddenly vivid and particular, the way senses sharpen when the body believes it is ending. In the white wash of it Emrys thought: *if he moves, if Tomas coughs, if a barrel shifts—*

The light passed on. Swept the stern, swept the empty water behind, swung away downstream toward nothing. The patrol boat's engine muttered on into the dark, a dog checking a scent it had decided not to trust, and the river closed black over the place the light had been.

From under the tarpaulin, very faintly, came the sound of a small boy exhaling through his nose. Barrels don't breathe loudly. But they breathe.

Emrys knelt by the tall barrel as casually as a passenger adjusting luggage and slipped two fingers inside the rim. Small fingers found theirs instantly and gripped—two fingers on the wrist, checking the pulse again, his ritual now—and did not let go until the keel ground onto the far shore's shingle and the ferryman said, "Trees, quick now, the tide turns," and Emrys lifted Tomas out of the straw stiff and blinking and silent, and set him on solid ground on the far side of the river.

One checkpoint down. One river that could not be un-crossed. Emrys looked back once at the near bank, at the smudge of lantern light where a boy with mended gloves was logging a working ferry in his patrol book, believing in the machinery—and then turned north, toward the rail yard, toward Hale, toward whatever waited in the arithmetic of an old friendship.

Tomas walked beside them, small hand swallowed in theirs. And after a hundred yards of shingle and frost-brittle grass, in a voice rusty and small as a hinge that had not moved in months, the boy said his first word since the infirmary door:

"Whistle."

Emrys stopped. The wind moved in the bare trees. Tomas pointed back the way they had come—back across the river, back toward the burned village, back toward something in the dark only he could see—and said it again, louder, as if Emrys were the one who had failed to understand the world:

"*Whistle.*"
