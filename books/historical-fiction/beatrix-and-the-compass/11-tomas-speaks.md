## 11. Tomas Speaks

The road was long and it went up.

Tomas counted his steps because Mama had taught him counting and because if he counted then the road could not be infinite. Infinite was a word the priest used at the village church before the church was black. Infinite meant it never stopped. The road stopped at the top of the hill. He could see it stop. So the road was not infinite, and that was good.

One hundred and four. One hundred and five.

The nurse walked in front. The nurse had a name — Emrys — but names were dangerous now, so in his head Tomas called them the Nurse, the way you'd call a thing what it mostly was. The Nurse carried a big brown bag and did not complain about how heavy it was even though their shoulders were pulled down like the bag was full of stones. Once, when they thought Tomas was asleep under the hedge, they had opened the bag and taken out not bread and not blankets but papers. Papers wrapped around a flat dark book. They had looked at the book for a long time by no light at all, just the moon, moving their lips.

Tomas thought maybe the book was a Bible. But Bibles didn't make people look like that. Bibles made people look like the priest, soft and far away. This book made the Nurse look like someone holding a hot coal and refusing to drop it.

One hundred and forty. One hundred and forty-one.

His boots were too big. They had come off a soldier who didn't need them anymore, the ferryman's wife said, and she'd laughed a small hard laugh when she said *didn't need*, and the Nurse had looked at her sharply and the woman had stopped laughing and given Tomas wool to stuff in the toes. He kept the wool. Wool was important. In the night his toes were the only part of him that stayed warm without help.

He had not talked since the fire. Not on the boat with the barrels that smelled of fish, not in the linen cart with the sheets folded over him like snow, not through all these hills. Talking was the thing that got people found. He had learned that. When the soldiers came to the village, Papa said *be quiet, be a stone,* and Papa pressed him down into the root cellar and put the board over the door, and Tomas was quiet, quieter than he had ever been, so quiet he was nearly a stone himself, and being a stone is why he was still here.

Stones don't talk.

But stones also get heavy. And the words inside him were getting heavier every day, piling up like snow on a roof, and somewhere along this high gray road, between one step and the next, Tomas felt the roof begin to bend.

* * *

They stopped at noon in the lee of a stone wall where the wind couldn't get its whole hand on them. The Nurse broke bread and gave him the bigger half, which was wrong, grown-ups should have the bigger half, and he pushed it back and they traded and neither of them smiled about it but something passed between them anyway, warm as the wool in his boots.

"Your feet," the Nurse said. "Let me see."

He let them see. His feet were blue-white and one of them had a crack in the heel that had bled earlier and gone sticky. The Nurse worked at them with strong careful fingers, rubbing until the color came back, wincing as if the feet were theirs.

"You should say if it hurts," the Nurse said. "You never say anything. You don't have to be brave with me, Tomas. I'm not one of the ones you need to be brave against."

Tomas looked at them. Up close the Nurse's eyes were tired in a way that wasn't about sleep. It was the way Papa's eyes had gotten after the harvest failed twice. Tired of knowing things.

"I can keep a secret," the Nurse said softly. "I'm very good at it. I've been keeping a whole book of them." Then they stopped, as if they'd said more than they meant to, and busied themselves with the bandage.

Tomas looked down at his coat. His coat had gotten heavier two nights ago in the shepherd's hut while he slept, and he had noticed, because he noticed everything now, noticing was another thing stones were good for. Someone had sewn something into the lining. Flat pages. He had felt the Nurse's fingers working by candlelight and pretended harder than he had ever pretended.

The book. The Nurse had put the book in *his* coat. Which meant the book was safest next to him. Which meant — and his mind went around this slowly, the way you go around a hot stove — which meant that he was the most important thing on this road, or the book was, or both, and both of those were the same thing, somehow.

If he spoke, and the soldiers heard, the book would burn with him.

But if he never spoke, then nobody would ever know what the man with the whistle did. And the words would stay piled on the roof of him until the roof came down.

* * *

That evening the sky did the thing it always did here — went red from edge to edge, like the inside of a shell, like the night the village burned seen from inside the root cellar through the gap in the boards. Tomas sat with his knees up watching it and the redness went into him and stirred everything around, and the first word came out before he had decided anything at all:

"He blew it three times."

The Nurse went very still. Still like an animal that knows sudden movement will break whatever is happening.

Tomas kept looking at the sky. It was easier to talk to the sky.

"The whistle. Silver. Shiny. He held it up so everybody could hear it over the shouting, like he was proud of it. He blew it three times and then the shooting started." He turned his hands over in his lap, studying them. "Before that it was only yelling and fire. Fire first. The barns. You set fire to barns so everyone runs out where you can see them. That's what I figured out later, listening through the boards. First they're loud and scared, running everywhere. Then the whistle. Three times. Then it goes organized."

"Tomas—" the Nurse began, and their voice cracked in the middle of his name like ice under a boot.

"It's all right," he said, surprised to find it was almost true. Saying it was like letting water out of a dam one spoonful at a time. It didn't drown him. It made room. "You want to know. I can tell you know things already. You look at me sometimes like I'm a letter you're afraid to open."

The Nurse made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been something sadder, and sat down beside him against the wall, close enough that their shoulder was a wall too.

* * *

"The soldiers came after dark," Tomas said. "Not our soldiers. Our soldiers had left two days before. Papa said they were going to hold the pass, and he wasn't worried, because Papa believed things people told him. Mama didn't believe things so easy. Mama packed the flour and the icons the night ours left, and she made me try my boots on twice."

He paused. The boots. Too big. Full of dead soldier's wool.

"These aren't my boots," he said. "Mine burned. These are fine. I just wanted you to know that, because you keep looking at them."

"I'll stop," the Nurse whispered.

"The new soldiers came fast. Horses first, then men on foot, and they weren't shouting yet, that was the strange part, they were quiet, doing things in order, like chores. I saw them from the cellar gap. A man poured something from a jug along the side of the barn and then there was light where light shouldn't be. Then everybody woke up. Dogs. People running with pillows, thinking pillows would matter. Old Ferenc ran out with his sword from above the mantel, the one he polished for shows, and they shot him almost politely, quick, like swatting."

Tomas's hands had gone tight together, knuckle on knuckle. The Nurse didn't touch him. They seemed to know touching would break it.

"Mama tried to bring me up out of the cellar. She changed her mind halfway down the ladder. She kissed my head through the hair and said, *count, count till morning, count and don't come out till the birds.* And then she went back up and put the board over the door herself, from outside, so nobody would see it. She was smart about the board. That's why I'm here. She was smart about the board."

He said that firmly, as if correcting a mistake in the ledger of the world: *she was smart about the board.*

"Through the gap I saw the yard. I saw Mrs. Kollar on the ground by her own doorstep and I saw the well and I saw — " He stopped. Started again, smaller. "I saw things I'm not going to tell you all of. Not because I'm keeping secrets from you. Because some of it isn't shaped like words yet. Maybe when I'm big."

"Maybe never," the Nurse said quietly. "Some things don't have to be told to anyone. Ever."

"Some of it does," Tomas said. "The rest doesn't. That's why I'm telling you the middle part. The middle part has to be told."

* * *

"The man with the whistle stood on the low rock by the trough, where the herdsman stands in summer. He had a coat nicer than the others and he wasn't doing any work, he was *watching* the work, and when it got messy he did the whistle." Tomas lifted his hand and mimed it, three short rises of an invisible silver thing to invisible lips. "Three times. Like church bells but colder. Everything stopped. The men stood straight. And he talked to them — I couldn't hear the words, just the shape of them — and then he did one more blow, long, and that's when it stopped being messy and started being thorough."

He heard the word land strangely in his own mouth. *Thorough.* The priest's word. The Nurse flinched as if he'd thrown a stone.

"There was another officer, younger, standing near the horses. When the long whistle blew, the young one turned away and was sick behind the water barrel. The man with the whistle saw. He wrote something in a little book while he watched the young man be sick, and then he put the little book away and whistled again — two times, different — and the men went down toward the far fields, and that's when I understood they were going to do the fields too, and the far houses, and that Papa was at the far mill."

Tomas stopped talking for a while. The sky had gone from red to purple to a deep bruised blue, and the first star came out and sat there, indifferent and perfect.

"Papa was at the mill," he said finally. "So I don't actually know what happened to Papa. Isn't that funny. I saw everything in the village and I don't know the one thing." His voice wobbled and he hated it and pressed it flat. "Mama either. After the board went on, I heard her voice one time, far off, saying something over and over — I think it was *no, no, we are neutral* — and then I didn't hear her anymore, and I counted. I counted to a number so big I don't know its name. The birds came. I came out. Everyone was smoke. Except me, because of the board."

The Nurse's breath had gone ragged. When Tomas glanced over, there were tears on their face and they weren't hiding them, which confused him; grown-ups always hid them, that was practically the definition of a grown-up.

"Why are you crying?" he asked. "It happened to me."

"Because," the Nurse said, and their voice took a moment to find its feet, "because I have a book, and in the book I write the names of everyone the army pretends never died. And your village — your village isn't even *in* it, Tomas. There was no battle reported. No village. Nothing. As if the smoke went up and history just — stepped around it." They wiped their face with the heel of their hand, angry at their own tears. "I cry because you are the only page that escaped the burning, and you are seven years old, and that is the most terrible arithmetic I know."

Tomas considered this.

"The button," he said.

The Nurse stared at him. "What?"

"The button I wouldn't give the sisters at the infirmary. You found it in my fist when I was fevered. You kept it. I've seen it in your pocket-book." He held out his hand, palm up, the universal gesture of *give*, and after a heartbeat the Nurse drew out the small brass disc and laid it there, warm from riding against their body.

"My papa's regiment," Tomas said. "The 44th? That's what the number is. Papa's buttons were brass, 44, and he shone them with tallow on Sundays. When the board went on the cellar door I was holding one, because Papa gave it to me for luck when ours marched out, and I held it the whole time I counted, and I decided — down in the dark — that if I got out, I would give the button to whoever asked me about it properly. Nobody asked properly. The sisters just wanted me to *talk, talk, poor lamb, talk.* You never asked. You just waited." He closed his fingers over the button. "So here's what I'm giving you instead. The 44th held the pass. Papa said so. And the pass is twenty miles from my village, and my village needed no holding. So somebody moved them. Or somebody never sent them. Either way — " he frowned, reaching for the shape of a thought too big for his mouth, and landed on it plainly, the way children land on truths — "either way, the man with the whistle knew the 44th wouldn't come. He whistled like a man who has read tomorrow's newspaper."

The silence after that was enormous. Above them the stars thickened. Somewhere downslope a fox screamed once and fell silent.

Then the Nurse said, in a voice scraped down to bare wire: "Tomas. The man with the whistle. His face. Could you—"

"I never forget faces," Tomas said simply. "Mama said it was my gift. Faces and numbers." He looked at the Nurse, and in the dark his eyes were very old and very young at once. "Why? Do you know him?"

The Nurse did not answer right away. When they did, they answered by taking his hand — the hand with the button in it — and folding his fingers tighter closed, and pulling him gently to his feet, and shouldering the terrible brown bag.

"Tomorrow," they said, "we reach the rail yard. And the day after, the governor's gate. And somewhere past that gate there is a judge, and judges have rooms where a story like yours gets written into books that cannot burn." They paused at the crest of the rise, where the road fell away toward a distant scatter of lamplight, and Tomas heard them swallow. "But tonight you sleep, and tomorrow you walk, and you keep the button and the face safe one more day. Can you do that? One more day?"

Tomas looked down at the valley, at the lights, at the world that had whistles in it.

"One more day," he said. "But then I want it back. The talking. I'm not putting the board back on."

Far below, at the black seam of the rail line, a lantern swung — once, twice — in a pattern that made the Nurse's grip tighten around his hand, and they drew him back from the crest, off the road, into the dark of the gorse.
