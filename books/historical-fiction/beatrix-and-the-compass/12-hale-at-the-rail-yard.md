## 12. Hale at the Rail Yard

The rail yard at Kesselbruck smelled of coal smoke and frozen iron, and Lieutenant Edmund Hale had been standing on Platform Three for four hours pretending to inspect the loading manifests.

It was, he had to admit, a convincing pretense. He had a clipboard. He had a pencil behind his ear and a greatcoat with major's braid conspicuously absent from its shoulders—Corvane had wanted him in dress uniform for the holiday security rotations, and Hale had pleaded the cold. The cold was real. His fingers had gone numb around eleven o'clock, and by one in the afternoon he could no longer feel the difference between the clipboard and his own hand. None of it mattered. He wasn't reading the manifests. He hadn't read a manifest all day.

He was watching the road that came down from the hills.

Emrys would come that way. They had to. The river crossing at Weller's Ford would have cost them a day if the ice held, two if it didn't, and Emrys Brightwater had never in their life been early for anything except other people's suffering. But the rail yard—the rail yard was the only way east before the new year. Two trains a week left Kesselbruck for the capital: the mail run on Tuesdays and the freight on Thursdays, carrying colliery output and army provisions under bills of lading no one audited until spring. Today was Thursday, the twenty-eighth of December. Two days before Corvane's commission vested and every document in the southern command became legally indistinguishable from truth.

Hale knew Emrys would come through this yard the way he knew the tide would come in—not because they had told him so, but because he had spent thirty years learning how one particular mind moved through the world, and that mind moved like water: patiently, along the path of least resistance, wearing down whatever stood in it.

He had not reported the ledger.

That was the fact he kept turning over, the way a tongue returns to a cracked tooth. Three nights ago, in Corvane's office, he had found the duplicate key where Emrys had once hidden it—in the seam of the old field desk they'd shared as children, brought south when Hale took the adjutancy—and he had opened the strongbox and seen the second book inside, the fair copy Emrys didn't know existed, the one Hale himself had begun keeping six months ago in a hand disguised as a clerk's. Forty-one names in Emrys's ledger. Thirty-eight in his. Between them, he suspected, complete.

He had closed the box. He had turned the key. He had gone back to Corvane's office and drafted the deployment summary the major required, the one that described the Third Weslarian Regiment's movements in October as *redeployment pending*, and his pen had not hesitated, because hesitation was a thing Corvane could smell the way dogs smelled fear, and Hale had survived three years as adjutant precisely by never hesitating where anyone could see.

But he had not reported the ledger. And this morning, when the wire had come through—*fugitive nurse, abducted child, all stations alert, shoot-on-sight authorized*—he had read it twice, folded it into his pocket, and told the duty sergeant there was nothing actionable for Kesselbruck yet.

Now there was perhaps an hour of daylight left, grey draining out of the sky like blood from a wound, and down the hill road Hale saw a cart.

* * *

It was a laundry cart, which almost made him laugh, and almost made him weep, and he did neither, because Sergeant Doyle was ten feet away checking the couplings on the freight cars and Doyle missed nothing that happened on Platform Three.

The cart came down the switchback road at the pace of a woman going nowhere—slow enough to seem aimless, steady enough to seem routine. Hale recognized the driver only because he had memorized her file three days ago along with every soul attached to the infirmary: Marta Ostrava, laundress, forty-six years of service, widowed, no family in the territory. A woman whose face would appear on no wire and in no report. Behind her walked a second figure in a porter's coat two sizes too large, cap pulled low, carrying a wicker basket against their chest the way porters carried baskets, except that porters did not carry them the way one carries a child one loves.

Emrys.

Even bent, even diminished into the slouch of manual labor, even with their hands wrapped in sacking and their face smudged to anonymity, they were unmistakable to him. He had known them since he was nine years old, when they had both stood at the same dockside window in Greyharbour watching their fathers' ship go out and not come back, and Emrys had said, with the terrible practicality of a ten-year-old, *Someone should write down who was on it.* He had thought then it was a child's nonsense. He had spent the rest of his life learning it was a vocation.

They were forty yards away when they saw him.

He watched it happen—the fractional stumble, the basket clutched tighter, the whole body deciding between flight and performance. And then Emrys did the bravest thing Hale had ever witnessed them do, which was to keep walking. Straight toward him. Toward the checkpoint. Toward the man whose duty it was to stop them.

*Loyalty versus conscience,* Hale had written in his own ledger once, in a moment of self-dramatizing despair, and now the phrase arrived again and revealed itself as a lie. There was no versus. There was only arithmetic, and he had been doing the sum wrong for years.

* * *

"Manifests," Hale said loudly, to Doyle, to the platform, to the fiction of the afternoon. "These colliery weights are all off. I want every car re-checked before coupling."

Doyle straightened, unhappy. "Begging pardon, sir, train's due out at six."

"Then you'd best be quick about it." He made his voice bored, official, the voice of a man thinking about his supper. Then, lower, as Doyle trudged off: "Take the far end. Start with twelve."

Sergeant Doyle went. The laundry cart rolled up to the platform edge and stopped, and Marta Ostrava climbed down without being asked, and for one long moment Hale and Emrys Brightwater stood close enough to touch and did not look at each other at all.

"You're in my light," Hale said. "Porter. That freight car needs ballast loaded. Go."

Emrys looked at him then. Under the cap brim their eyes were the same eyes that had watched the ship go out—grey, level, and full of something he had never seen in them before, which was fear for him rather than from him.

"Hale," they said, very quietly. Just the name. It contained, in three syllables, everything either of them had failed to say for three years.

"Don't." He kept his gaze on the clipboard. "Don't thank me, don't apologize, don't say my name again where the wind can carry it. Load the ballast. Car fourteen, the one with the tarpaulin. There's a gap behind the crated stoves where the heat pipe runs—it'll be warm and it won't be found unless they empty the car entirely, which they won't, because the manifest says stoves and nobody checks a stove twice." He tore a page from his notebook, folded it small, and set it atop the basket as though adjusting a strap. "Departure is six. Water stop at Marren Siding, eleven minutes, don't leave the car. After that, nonstop to the capital."

"The wire," Emrys murmured. "Shoot on sight."

"I wrote the distribution list. Your name isn't spelled correctly anywhere it matters." He heard how it sounded—the coldness, the bureaucratic ease of it—and understood that Emrys was hearing it too, hearing what three years in Corvane's office had made of him, and he hated them for it, briefly, and loved them more. "There's bread in the basket already. Don't argue. Eat it."

From inside the basket came a sound—a small shifting weight, a breath. Hale looked down despite himself.

A boy's face looked back up at him from beneath a blanket: perhaps seven years old, thin, dark-eyed, watchful as a bird. The child did not cry or whimper. He simply studied Hale with the grave attention of someone who had learned, recently and permanently, that adults decided things, and was waiting to learn what Hale had decided.

Something in Hale's chest turned over and would not turn back.

"They say he's stolen," Hale said. His voice had gone strange. "The wire says kidnapped. Abducted from protective custody."

"He's not," Emrys said. "You know he's not. You've read what I've read."

*Forty-one names.* Hale had read them all, in Emrys's hand, in the lamplit back room of a house in Greyharbour a lifetime ago—or last month, it amounted to the same thing. Names, ranks, dates of death that appeared in no official record, causes of death recorded as *fever* or *desertion* or *returned to depot, whereabouts unknown*. The Third Weslarians, erased. This boy was the regiment's whole surviving muster.

The child was still watching him. Slowly, gravely, the boy raised one small hand from under the blanket and held something out—held it *up* to Hale, deliberately, the way one presents credentials.

A button. Brass, regimentally stamped, worn nearly smooth.

Hale knew the stamp. Every officer in the southern command knew the stamp, and knew better than to know it. He looked at the button for a long moment, and then he looked at the boy, and the boy looked back with those old dark eyes, and Hale understood that he was not being begged from. He was being *asked*. Formally. By the smallest witness in the empire.

"Yes," Hale said, to the child, quietly. "All right. Yes."

Then he straightened, and the officer's voice returned like armor buckling on. "Ballast detail! Are you deaf as well as slow? Car fourteen. Now."

Emrys shouldered the basket and went. Hale watched them cross the platform with a porter's rolling gait they could not possibly have learned in three days—no, he corrected himself; Emrys had grown up hauling their grandmother's washing in Greyharbour; the gait was authentic, the disguise was grief—and climb into the shadow of car fourteen, and vanish among the crates.

Six minutes to departure. Five. Doyle was working his way back along the couplings, swearing at the cold. Marta Ostrava stood by her empty cart, patient as furniture.

Hale opened his notebook to a fresh page and began, in his neat adjutant's hand, the report that would end him:

*Sighting report, 28 December, 1540 hours. Reliable civilian informant reports individual matching description of fugitive nurse observed at Weller's Ford crossing, moving north along the towpath. Informant credibility assessed as moderate; description possibly confused with itinerant peddler reported at the ford on the 27th. Recommend patrol dispatch to Weller's Ford sector. No sighting at this station.*

He dated it, signed it, sealed it. At 1758 hours, after the freight had pulled out in a shriek of couplings and coal smoke, he handed it to the signals clerk for transmission to divisional headquarters, and watched the clerk encode it, and knew precisely what he had done.

A false sighting at Weller's Ford would pull patrols west, toward the river, for a minimum of eighteen hours—time for the train to clear Marren Siding, time to put fifty miles of track between the boy and the net. Eighteen hours bought with a document bearing his signature, his rank, his name. When the truth surfaced—and it would surface; Corvane burned everything eventually but paper, and paper convicted everyone—the report would sit in the signal archive like a confession with a timestamp.

*False sighting.* Three words. In the army's grammar, they meant cowardice or incompetence or treachery, and Hale found, examining himself with the detached interest of a man reading his own obituary, that none of those words fit. What he had done was closer to bookkeeping. Emrys counted the dead. Someone had to count what the dead were owed. This—this forged entry, this small treason dressed as diligence—was simply his column in the ledger. His name added while he could still sign it.

*Someone should write down who was on it.*

He stood on Platform Three as the dark came down and the lamps flickered alight, and he felt, unexpectedly, lighter. Lighter than he had felt since the day he'd taken the adjutancy, since the day he'd first signed his name beneath Corvane's and felt the small click of a lock turning somewhere inside him. The lock was open now. What remained on the other side—ruin, court-martial, perhaps worse—was merely weather.

"Lieutenant?" Doyle, stamping his feet, breath steaming. "Cars checked and coupled, sir. Train's gone. Nothing on your manifest after all."

"No," Hale agreed. "Nothing here at all."

* * *

At nine o'clock the reply wire came to the Kesselbruck station office, decoded and delivered to Hale personally, as divisional adjutant-to-station-officer correspondence was required to be.

He read it standing at the counter, and the lightness drained out of him the way warmth leaves a body.

*Sighting report received. Ford patrol dispatched, negative result. NOTE: Major Corvane departed capital 1800 hrs by special engine, destination southern command, arriving via Marren Siding approx. 2300 hrs 29th. All station commanders to hold themselves available for personal inspection. Corvane has been informed of irregularity in freight manifest, car fourteen, Kesselbruck departure. He considers the coincidence significant.*

He considered it significant too. Of course he did. A man like Corvane did not survive on orders and patronage alone; he survived on pattern, on the instinct for the loose thread, and Hale had just handed him one—had pulled it gently across the table like a card player conceding a trick to hide the rest of the hand.

Corvane would be at Marren Siding by eleven tonight. The freight train, delayed on the single line, might reach the siding within the hour after. Eleven minutes' water stop. One special engine standing on the adjacent road. One man stepping down onto the snow with a lantern and a silver chain glinting at his throat, walking the length of the freight, tapping the tarpaulins.

Hale set the wire down on the counter and found his hands were steady, which surprised him, and his mind was already moving, which did not. There was exactly one faster route to the capital than the night freight, and it belonged to the divisional commander, and its pilot was warm, and Hale had the authority to order it fueled.

There was, he reflected, a certain symmetry in it. He had spent three years opening doors for Corvane. It was fitting that he spend his last free hours trying to outrun him through one.

He took his coat and went out into the falling snow to steal a general's train.
