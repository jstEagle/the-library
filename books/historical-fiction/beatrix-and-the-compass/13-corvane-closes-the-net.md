## 13. Corvane Closes the Net

Major Corvane had always believed that civilization was, at its root, an act of editing.

History did not forgive; it forgot, and forgetting had to be arranged by someone with the stomach for it. He had the stomach. He had discovered this about himself years ago—not with horror but with the quiet satisfaction of a man finding that a coat fits him better than he'd expected. Others flinched at the necessary arithmetic of empire. Corvane performed it in his head without moving his lips.

He sat now in the infirmary's administrative office, which he had commandeered within an hour of arriving, and let the room tell him what it knew. Small, scrubbed, smelling of carbolic and boiled linen. A ledger shelf—empty ledgers, requisition books, the dull accounting of bandages and deaths. A cold stove. Two chairs, one behind the desk and one before it, and Sister Agnes in the latter, hands folded atop her apron like a woman waiting for tea to be poured.

She had survived three wars. He knew this from her file, which he had read twice on the journey over, and he respected it the way he respected any well-executed campaign. But survival left traces, and he intended to read hers.

"Sister," he said pleasantly. "Thank you for receiving me at such short notice."

"You are welcome in God's house of healing, Major." Her voice was flat as a slate. Dutch-accented, or something older than that—a border accent, the kind that belonged to no single country and therefore to all of them.

"I'll be brief. A child has gone missing."

"Children go missing constantly in wartime. It is one of war's chief products."

The answer was insolent and entirely deniable, and he felt a flicker of professional appreciation. "This child is particular. A boy, perhaps six or seven. Dark-haired. Reported seen in the vicinity of a village that—" he consulted a paper he did not need to consult, "—suffered an unfortunate fire in October. You understand how rumors travel. Someone claims to have seen him here. At your infirmary."

"Then someone should write to his mother."

"His mother is dead."

"Then someone should write to God." She did not blink. "Major, I have forty-one men in wards behind me who will die or won't die according to how much sleep I get. If you intend to arrest me for insolence, do it quickly, so I may be replaced by someone equally insolent and less efficient."

There it was—the armor of usefulness. He had watched a hundred administrators hide behind it. In her case, he suspected, the armor was also true, which made it stronger.

"I have no intention of arresting anyone," he said warmly. "I intend to find a child. You've nothing to hide from a man looking for a child, surely."

"Nothing," said Sister Agnes.

He studied her face the way he studied terrain. The map of it said *nothing to declare*, but maps lied by omission; that was their nature and their use. He noted the way her eyes did not flick toward the floorboards, nor toward the stovepipe, nor anywhere in particular—and it was precisely the absence of any telling glance that told him something. An innocent woman would have looked at the door by now, wanting him gone. This woman wanted nothing visible at all.

"You run a supply line," he said, changing key. "To the capital. Medical stores, correspondence?"

"We do."

"How often?"

"Fortnightly. More if there's cause."

"And when does it next run?"

Her pause was small—half a breath, a heartbeat's hesitation—but he had spent twenty years listening for half-breaths. "After the new year," she said. "The roads are poor. And you have made them poorer."

*After the new year.* Either she was lying, or she was telling the truth in a way designed to sound like a lie, or she was lying in a way designed to sound like truth. It hardly mattered. What mattered was the shape of her caution, which confirmed the thing he had already decided to believe: the child was here, and the nurse Brightwater—who had been asking too many questions about casualty returns since autumn, oh yes, he kept a private ledger of his own, a ledger of people who kept ledgers—was the hinge on which it all turned.

He rose, straightened his tunic, and smiled at her with genuine warmth, because warmth disarmed better than threat and cost no more.

"Keep your wards running, Sister. That's all I ask of you. Efficiency is its own form of loyalty, and yours is exemplary." At the door he paused, as though remembering something trivial. "Oh—one matter of routine administration. I'll need your admission records. All of them. The army requires a full audit of patient intake since October. Names, dates, circumstances. Standard procedure after an incident like the fire."

"The audit can wait until my patients stop bleeding."

"It can't, I'm afraid. Procedure is procedure. My clerk will collect them within the hour."

He heard her breath go shallow behind him and did not turn around. There was no satisfaction in it—or rather, there was a kind, the thin administrative pleasure of a lock clicking shut, and he had long ago learned not to mistake that pleasure for cruelty. Cruelty was indulgence. He was not an indulgent man.

* * *

The records burned beautifully.

He had them brought out to the yard in three canvas sacks—admission books, treatment registers, the daily logs in Sister Agnes's cramped, continent-spanning hand—and he fed them into a brazier himself while his clerk recorded the destruction with bureaucratic solemnity. *Destroyed by order of Major H. Corvane, pursuant to Directive 114, being documents of compromised provenance.*

Compromised provenance. It was good phrase. It meant nothing and excused everything.

The infirmary staff were drawn up along the far wall to watch. He insisted on witnesses for these things; an act done in secret bred rumor, but an act done openly bred resignation, and resignation was the more obedient child. He saw the nurses' faces—shock, fear, the particular anger of women who had written those pages in exhaustion and now watched them curl into black lace. He addressed them over the flames, reasonably, almost gently:

"These records contained irregularities. False names, false dates, entries inconsistent with official casualty returns. Such discrepancies, in wartime, constitute a security risk. They are not destroyed because anyone did wrong"—he looked directly, briefly, at the tall figure of Nurse Brightwater, whose face was composed into perfect professional blankness—"but because wrongness must not be permitted to look like truth."

One of the younger nurses began, softly, to cry. He did not reprimand her. Grief, properly unaddressed, curdled into silence eventually. It always had.

Page by page the fire took them. He watched a name flare and vanish—a name he could not read, belonging to some soldier whose death had been entered honestly by some tired hand, and he felt nothing about that, which was itself a comfort. Sentiment was a tax on command. He had paid it once, early, at a place whose name he never allowed himself, and he had resolved never to pay it again. Order required forgetting. Not callousness—he resented the word callous—only proportion. A surgeon amputates without weeping. He edited without weeping. The alternative was a country that remembered everything, remembered every error and every necessity, until memory itself became a wound that would not close, and men like him would be replaced by men worse than him. That was the argument he carried in his breast pocket like a photograph. He took it out often. It still fit.

When the last register had gone to ash he dismissed the staff, then lingered alone at the brazier, warming hands he did not need to warm, and thought about Emrys Brightwater with the focused patience of a man watching weather build.

Brightwater. Quiet, competent, invisible—which was to say, exactly the sort of person who turned out to be dangerous, because invisibility was a form of ambition that had merely chosen its target late. The casualty-return queries. The questions about the 41st's deployment status asked of clerks who reported everything for pocket money. And now a child matching the description of the sole loose thread from that whole regrettable October afternoon, present in Brightwater's infirmary, vanishing the same week his audit arrived.

It was almost elegant. He could admire it even as he moved to crush it.

*Forget the face.* The note had been recovered from the abandoned bundle—no, that wasn't right either; the note had been described to him secondhand, and descriptions were unreliable, and yet the phrase lodged in him like shrapnel because it was, in its way, an accusation aimed at his entire philosophy. *Forget the face.* As if faces could be forgotten on command. As if forgetting were not the most laborious work in the world, requiring fires and directives and loyal adjutants and, when all else failed, men willing to stand in snowy yards burning paper while nurses wept.

He would find the child. He would find Brightwater. And the matter would end before the thirty-first, quietly, in whatever ditch or doorway such matters ended in, and on the first of January he would be a general, and the past would be past in the only sense that phrase had ever truly meant.

* * *

The orders were drafted by lamplight in his quarters and signed before midnight.

**To all checkpoint commanders, river patrol, and gendarmerie, effective immediately:** A military nurse, E. Brightwater, was wanted for questioning in the abduction of a minor. The child, Thomas, age approximately six, was considered abducted and possibly drugged or coerced. Personnel encountering either party were to detain them; in the event of resistance or flight, lethal force was authorized.

He read the paragraph back twice. *Kidnapper and stolen child.* It was very nearly true, which was the best kind of lie—the kind that merely rearranged furniture inside a room that already existed. A child taken from lawful custody. A nurse fleeing an investigation. Any court reading the file in five years would see a rescue operation, tragically misfired. He had known officers who falsified battle maps; he falsified nothing so crude. He simply chose which facts to carry forward and which to leave in the burned yard, cooling.

Lieutenant Hale brought the fair copies for signature and stood at the desk longer than the task required.

Corvane signed without looking up, but he was aware of Hale the way he was aware of all his instruments—including the ones that had begun, faintly, to hum at the wrong pitch. Hale had been Brightwater's friend once; everyone knew it; Corvane had posted him to the rail yard partly despite it and partly because of it, because a man watched hardest the door he feared, and because if Hale was going to be a weakness, better to know precisely where the weakness stood.

"Something on your mind, Lieutenant?"

"No, sir. The distribution list—shall I add the governor's gate garrison?"

"You shall. Priority. And the rail yard commander is to personally inspect all freight leaving before the thirty-first. Every car. Every manifest."

"Yes, sir." A pause, professionally measured. "And if the nurse offers no resistance, sir? At a checkpoint?"

"Then they will be detained, treated courteously, and questioned." Corvane set down the pen and finally met his adjutant's eyes. "You ask as though you hope for that outcome, Lieutenant."

Hale's face did what it was trained to do, which was nothing. "I ask so that the orders are unambiguous, sir. Ambiguity at checkpoints gets soldiers court-martialed."

"Spoken like a staff officer." Corvane smiled and slid the papers across. "Unambiguous, then: I want the child found before the thirty-first. After the thirty-first it stops mattering whether we find anyone at all—do you understand? The commission is signed, the record closes, and this entire sorry business becomes a rumor about a fire. Until then, we hunt. Discreetly, thoroughly, and without sentiment. Send the copies tonight."

Hale saluted and went. Corvane listened to his boots recede down the corridor and catalogued the sound under *pending*, the way he catalogued everything unresolved.

Alone, he poured a measure of brandy he would barely drink and stood at the window. Snow had begun again, fat and slow, filling in the tracks of the day, erasing the yard, the brazier's grey scar, the wheel ruts—erasing everything, impartially, the way the sky erased things, without motive or mercy or paperwork. He found the snow restful. It was the only honest editor.

Somewhere out in that white blindness, a nurse and a stolen child were either hiding in his net or already through two of its knots. His instinct said the latter—instinct sharpened by the small hesitations of Sister Agnes and the too-smooth blankness of Lieutenant Hale, both of whom had lied to him today in different dialects of the same language. Fine. Let them run. Roads funnel. Rivers funnel. Railways above all funnel, and a fugitive with a child cannot travel fast, cannot travel quiet, and cannot, in the end, travel anywhere except toward the places where other people travel.

He drank the brandy at last, one swallow, and set the glass down with a click like a stamp on a document.

On the desk lay the morning telegram from his patron, all reassurance and one thin warning at the bottom: *the press has a nose for fires. Conclude matters cleanly.*

Cleanly. Yes. That was the word for it. He would conclude matters cleanly, and the snow would finish the editing, and the new year would open on a world that remembered exactly as much as it needed to.

Which was nothing at all.

He did not know—could not, standing warm at his window while the wires carried his orders out into the dark—that the freight schedules had already been read by other eyes, that a duplicate key was at that moment changing pockets, and that somewhere east of the pass, in a shepherd's hut buried to its windows in the same indifferent snow, a child was sleeping with forty-one names sewn against his small chest, closer to his heartbeat than to the cold.

The net was closing. But nets, Corvane had forgotten, are woven by hand, and every knot is a place where a finger once worked—and fingers remember what files do not.

By dawn, the storm would make its choice for all of them.
