## 14. Snowbound

The storm found them an hour after the shepherd's hut did.

Emrys had seen the low stone shape from the road—a grey smudge against a whiter sky—and had begun to run before understanding why, some animal instinct in the spine reading the horizon and pronouncing judgment: *you will not survive the open country tonight.* Tomas had run too, small boots punching through the crusted snow, one hand locked in Emrys's grip so tightly that the fingers had gone numb long before they reached the door.

The hut was hardly more than a cairn with a roof—dry-stone walls furred with lichen inside, a hearth blackened by years of peat smoke, a straw pallet, a three-legged stool, a shelf holding a clay jug of water gone cloudy and half a loaf of bread wrapped in cloth. Whoever lived here had left it stocked against just such emergencies, as shepherds do; there was even a stack of cut turf by the hearth and a box of matches under an overturned bowl. On the wall hung the compass, taken from the dead officer's coat two checkpoints ago, its glass face fogging immediately in the cold room's sudden warmth of breath.

Emrys shut the door, wedged the stool beneath the latch, and stood listening to the wind arrive.

It came across the high ground like something with intent—not howling at first but *pressing*, a great soft weight thrown against the stones, then withdrawing, then pressing again, testing. Within the hour it had found its voice. Snow did not fall so much as fly sideways, and the single window, a foot square with no glass, only a wooden shutter, ticked and shuddered in its frame like something alive trying to get in or trying to get out.

Tomas sat on the pallet with his knees drawn up to his chin, watching Emrys with those dark unblinking eyes that had watched everything and explained nothing for eleven days now. Eleven days since the laundry cart. Nine since the river checkpoint and the young sentry whose face Emrys still saw sometimes when closing their eyes. Two since the shepherd, Dafydd, had fed them mutton broth and pointed through the storm toward the capital road, saying nothing about why he was helping, asking nothing either, which was itself a kind of eloquence.

"We're not moving tomorrow," Emrys said, and heard how strange their own voice sounded after a day of wind. "Perhaps not the day after."

Tomas said nothing. He almost never did. But he nodded once, gravely, the way he did everything—like a man much older wearing a boy's body, like someone who had already learned that words were expensive and spent them sparingly.

Emrys built the fire small. There was no more turf than what was stacked, and they might need this hut another night, and heat could be rationed the way bread could. They set the kettle—the shepherd's, dented tin—to warm, divided the remaining bread into portions with a cold-eyed arithmetic that made their chest ache: enough for two days if neither of them was honest about hunger. Then they unwrapped the bundle they carried everywhere now, checked it for dampness, and retied the string, and felt the familiar weight settle back against their ribs where it rode beneath their coat like a second heart.

The ledger. Forty-one names now, since the sergeant. Forty-one men who had been born, had mothers, had laughed at bad jokes and complained about their boots and died in a valley that officially did not exist, in a battle that officially never happened, under orders that officially bore no signature.

Outside, the storm thickened until the world beyond the window was simply white noise given texture.

* * *

The first day passed the way days pass in hiding, which is to say slowly and all at once.

They slept late because there was nothing else to do with the darkness before dawn. Emrys woke to find Tomas already awake, sitting cross-legged by the banked fire, feeding it splinters of turf one at a time with the concentration of a surgeon, keeping it alive without ever letting it grow large enough to smoke. The boy had learned caution somewhere, Emrys thought. Learned it in the way children learn things—permanently, in the body.

They ate. Half a portion each, though Emrys quietly shaved their own thinner than the boy's and pretended not to notice the way Tomas noticed anyway.

Then the long middle of the day, which had to be survived rather than lived. Emrys tried teaching the boy a card game with a deck they didn't have, playing both hands in the dust of the hearthstone with marked pebbles, and discovered that Tomas understood strategy instantly but would not, under any circumstances, make the sound that went with winning. He'd point at his winning hand. He'd look up. And then he'd wait, expressionless, for confirmation that it was permitted to have won.

"You won," Emrys told him, the first dozen times.

The thirteenth time, something cracked open slightly in the boy's face—not a smile, nothing so extravagant, but a loosening around the mouth, a brief unclenching—and Emrys felt it land in their own chest like a struck bell.

In the afternoon the wind changed key and Emrys went out into it, because the water jug was empty and because a body that stays indoors too long starts believing indoor thoughts are the whole world. The two minutes at the stream behind the hut were among the worst of Emrys's life—the wind stripping meaning from the air, the snow so dense it erased the hut twenty feet away—but they filled the jug, and staggered back, and slammed the door on the white roar, and stood dripping and gasping while Tomas looked up at them from the pallet with an expression Emrys needed a moment to identify.

It was worry. The child had been worried for them.

"Well," Emrys said, absurdly pleased, setting down the jug. "I survived. We'll note it in the record."

* * *

The second night, the storm grew bored of mere violence and turned contemplative, settling into a deep steady hush that somehow made the isolation more complete rather than less. The fire was fed generously—it seemed safe now, the storm having proven it would keep them regardless—and the hut filled with amber light and the smell of burning peat and wet wool.

Tomas sat close, closer than he had ever voluntarily sat, his shoulder just barely touching Emrys's arm. Not leaning. Just touching. A point of contact maintained with the same deliberate care he gave everything.

Emrys took out the ledger.

It was habit more than necessity; there was nothing to add, nothing to check. But the hands wanted their work, and the work was the book, and perhaps also—and here Emrys was honest with themselves, alone in a stone hut with a silent child and a storm for company—perhaps they were tired of carrying it invisibly. Tired of being the only one who knew what it weighed.

"This is why we're walking through the cold," Emrys said, holding the ledger where the boy could see it. "This is the whole reason. Do you understand?"

Tomas looked at the worn cover. Then, slowly, he reached out and touched it—one finger, resting on the leather—and left it there.

"Names," Emrys said. "Men your—" *your father served with*, the sentence began, and Emrys stopped it, because they had promised themselves they would not interrogate the child, would not harvest him for evidence the way Corvane harvested villages for reports. "Men I knew," they finished instead. "Some of them I knew."

That was true. Not of the forty-one—all names gathered secondhand, from dying men and deserting clerks and Sister Agnes's prodigious memory—but of others, older names, names that lived in Emrys on a different shelf of the mind entirely. And the storm, and the dark, and the small warm weight of the boy's shoulder against their arm did something that eleven days of fear had not done. It loosened the tongue.

"I had a brother," Emrys said.

The wind moved around the eaves. Tomas's finger stayed on the book.

"His name was Rhys. He was four years older than me, which he considered the most important fact in the universe." Emrys heard their own voice go strange—lighter, performing the memory, the way one performs memories that are too heavy to lift plainly. "He could whistle through his fingers so loud it stopped birds. He taught me to swim by the simple method of throwing me off the jetty and hauling me out by the hair. He was going to be a surveyor. He liked maps the way other men liked drink—all his wages went to charts, and he'd spread them on our mother's table and trace roads with his finger and say, *here, Em, here's where we'll go.*"

The fire shifted. A knot in the turf collapsed in a small shower of sparks.

"He joined the regiment because the recruiting sergeant had a good coat and Rhys was nineteen and beautiful and stupid in the way beautiful nineteen-year-olds are. Our mother cried for a week and then made him take a scarf she'd knitted, army-grey, wrong wool entirely, and he wore it the whole campaign under his uniform because he was sentimental under all the swagger, that was the truth of him, and nobody knew it but me and her."

Emrys turned the ledger over in their hands, though there was no name in it for Rhys. Rhys was in a different ledger, an internal one, kept longer.

"The engagement was small. A skirmish, the dispatches called it later, which is a word officers use when the numbers embarrass them. His company was mispositioned—someone's error, never named—and when the line broke, the survivors were scattered across six miles of country. Rhys was found two days later, according to the man who found him, sitting against a wall with the scarf around his throat, dead of his wounds, his rifle unloaded beside him. The man buried him and marked the grave with his rifle and bayonet, the old way, and reported it."

Emrys had not told this story aloud in nine years. It came out with the strange smoothness of a stone worn by water—all the rough edges polished off by private repetition.

"And the army wrote home to my mother that he had deserted."

The word sat in the hut. Tomas's finger pressed harder on the ledger cover, as if he understood the word's weight without knowing the word.

"Desertion. Rhys, who carried our mother's portrait in a locket and policed the camp for stragglers on his own initiative because he couldn't bear the idea of anyone being left. Deserted. Which meant no pension. Which meant no official death, no closure, no grave with a stone. Which meant that when our mother died—two years later, and I will always believe it was of exactly this—she died the mother of a deserter, in a town that knew how to be polite about it, which is the cruelest way to know anything."

Emrys looked at the fire.

"A clerk's pen," they said. "That's all it took. Some captain wanted his casualty returns tidy and his own conduct clean, and he moved a pen across a page, and a living breathing whistling boy became a shameful absence. Nobody killed him twice. But they buried him twice. The second burial was deeper."

The storm hushed against the roof. In the quiet, very quietly, Tomas leaned—not suddenly, but by degrees, the way a tree leans in slow wind—until his head was against Emrys's shoulder. Fully leaning now. Trusting the structure to hold.

Emrys put an arm around him and looked at the top of his dark head and understood, with the simplicity of arithmetic, that this was why. This was the whole answer to the question people would ask later, if there was a later—*why risk everything for a book of names?* Because a name in a ledger is a person who cannot be buried twice. Because every entry is a small resurrection. Because somewhere a mother deserves to die knowing the truth about her son, even if the truth arrives too late to save her, because truth is owed to the dead precisely because they cannot collect it.

"When I started the ledger," Emrys said softly, into the boy's hair, "it was spite. I'll admit that. Spite is good fuel; it burns hot. But it doesn't burn long. Somewhere along the way it became this instead—" they tapped the cover under Tomas's finger— "a promise. Every name I write down is a name Major Corvane cannot erase. That's all it is. That's all it needs to be. He has the guns and the promotions and the patron in the capital, and I have forty-one names, and I believe—I have to believe—that in the end the names weigh more."

Tomas lifted his head then. And in the firelight, with terrible slowness, he opened his mouth and pointed one small finger into it, toward his teeth, and shook his head—*no*—and Emrys realized, with a lurch, what the boy was trying to say.

He wasn't refusing to speak. Or—he was, but not from shock, not the way the nurses whispered. He had decided it. Somewhere in that burned village, in whatever days had followed, Tomas had concluded that words were dangerous things, that saying things made them real, that the last order he'd heard spoken aloud—

"It's all right," Emrys said quickly. "You don't have to. You never have to."

But Tomas shook his head again, insistent, and then he did something else. He reached into his shirt and drew out the regimental button, the brass one from the unit that had never been deployed, the one he had clutched through eleven days and three checkpoints, and he pressed it into Emrys's palm and closed Emrys's fingers around it with both his small hands.

Then he pointed at the ledger.

Emrys's throat closed. *Add it,* the gesture said. *Make it real. Make it count.*

"Tomas," Emrys whispered—the first time they'd used his name aloud in days, hoarding it like the bread. "When we reach the capital, when a judge asks you—will you tell them? Then? With the whistle-man in the room?"

The boy studied Emrys's face for a long moment, firelight moving in his eyes, doing whatever fierce private calculation children do when they are deciding whether the adults can be believed. Outside the storm was beginning, very faintly, to lose its grip—the wind dropping by degrees, the pressure easing, the silence between gusts growing longer.

Then Tomas nodded. Once. A promise, given as gravely as any oath sworn on scripture.

Emrys drew him back down against their shoulder and held him as his breathing slowed toward sleep, and sat watching the fire, the button warm in their closed fist, and let themselves feel—for one hour, one stolen storm-bound hour—the dangerous comfort of believing they might actually arrive.

* * *

They woke to stillness so complete it rang.

Emrys was on their feet before conscious thought caught up, hand on the ledger-bundle, listening—and then understanding. Not silence. *Snow-silence.* The storm had blown itself out in the night, and the light through the shutter was different: thin, hard, blue-white, the light of a world freshly buried.

Tomas was already awake, dressed, standing by the door with his boots in his hand.

Emrys opened the shutter and looked out on a landscape erased and remade—the track they'd followed gone, the fences gone, only the shapes of the high hills against a sky scrubbed clean and pale, and above them, absurdly, one line of geese crossing south, unhurried, as if wars and ledgers and majors were matters of no concern to geese.

Two days lost. Two days of margin, spent on weather. The deadline stood unmoved on the far side of the storm: December 31st, when Corvane's commission would seal the records like a tomb. Today was the 28th. One checkpoint remained—the governor's gate, thick with holiday traffic, thick with Corvane's men—and beyond it the capital, the judge, the end of the road in every sense.

Emrys dressed, divided the last of the bread, and left half the turf stack and a carved thank-you on the hearthstone for the shepherd whose name they hadn't thought to ask.

At the door, Tomas tugged Emrys's sleeve and pointed at the compass hanging on the wall. They took it down, checked it out of old habit—and froze.

The needle was behaving strangely. Trembling. Not settling north but swinging, hesitating, as if confused by the storm-disturbed iron of the earth or—or by something nearer. Emrys remembered, with a cold slide of the stomach, that magnetism answered to iron, and iron answered to men, and that a compass trembling near a door could mean nothing at all.

Or it could mean riders.

Through the clear post-storm air, faint across the snow, came a sound that was not geese and not wind: the distant, rhythmic complaint of hooves on frozen crust.

Emrys snuffed the fire, swung Tomas onto their back, and slipped out the door into the blinding white, heading not for the road—for the road belonged to whoever owned the hoofbeats—but for the shallow fold of the hills, where the snow lay undisturbed and the compass needle, held tight against their chest next to the ledger, slowly, slowly steadied.
