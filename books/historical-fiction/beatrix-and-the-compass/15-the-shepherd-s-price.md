## 15. The Shepherd's Price

The storm broke sometime before dawn, and with it went the illusion that the world outside the shepherd's hut had simply agreed to forget them.

Emrys woke to a silence of a particular quality—the hush that follows heavy snow, when sound lies buried under white and even the wind seems embarrassed to speak. For a moment they lay on the narrow pallet beneath the window and let themselves believe, foolishly, that stillness meant safety. Then they heard the dogs.

Not barking. Whining. A low, pained complaint from the stone byre attached to the hut, the kind of sound animals make when strangers put hands on them.

Emrys was upright in an instant, bare feet finding cold floorboards, crossing to the window in three steps and pressing their face to the frost-furred glass. The storm had spent itself against the mountainside and left behind a world blinding and blue-edged, drifts sculpted against the hut's low walls like frozen waves. And coming up the track below—two riders first, then a cart, then four more men on foot in the grey-blue of military police, their breath rising in columns, their boots punching through the crust.

They were not lost. Lost men did not move with that unhurried certainty, that air of errand rather than accident.

"Tomas." Emrys crossed the room and shook the boy gently. He slept as he always slept, curled small, one fist closed around nothing—as if the button he'd surrendered weeks ago had left a ghost in his palm. "Tomas, wake up. We have to be quiet, and we have to be quick."

He woke the way he always woke now: without crying out. That silence had been the first thing Emrys had learned about him, and it had taken until the road past the river checkpoint to understand it wasn't stubbornness but survival—a child who had learned that noise summoned the man with the silver whistle. He looked at Emrys's face, read whatever was written there, and slid off the pallet without a word, gathering his shoes in his arms rather than pausing to lace them.

Old habits. Good ones, God help them both.

Emrys dressed in the dark, fingers working buttons by memory, mind running faster than their hands. The hut belonged to a man called Aldric Vey—or just Vey, because shepherds this far up needed no other name—who had found them half-dead with cold at the mouth of his track two days ago and had asked precisely no questions. He had fed them barley broth and hard cheese, given them his own bed and taken the hay, and each morning had climbed through the snow to see whether the passes toward the capital were open yet. He had a face like weathered oak and hands that shook slightly, and when Emrys had offered him money from the small store sewn into their coat hem, he had refused it with a look that suggested the offer itself was mildly obscene.

"We owe him," Emrys whispered, mostly to themselves.

Tomas nodded solemnly, though Emrys suspected he didn't fully follow. Children understood debts differently—in bread, in warmth, in stories told across two nights of storm. In those currencies they were already rich.

Emrys pressed an eye back to the window.

The soldiers had reached the byre. One of them dragged open the gate and the dogs spilled out—not driven, *thrown*, stumbling into the snow—and another man began turning the hut's store shed upside down with methodical, officious violence. Feed sacks slashed. Tools scattered. The whole economy of one man's winter strewn across the white ground like entrails.

And there, coming around the corner of his own byre with a lantern he'd evidently gone to fetch for their benefit, lantern raised helpfully against the dark that no longer needed it—there was Vey himself, in his nightshirt and greatcoat, saying something. His voice didn't carry, but his posture did: the flat, uncomprehending stance of a man being asked questions that assume guilt.

An officer stepped forward. Even at this distance Emrys recognized the type: young, ambitious, wearing the armband of Corvane's provost detail—the men who had been spreading along every road since the rail yard, since Hale's false report had bought them hours and no more. The officer held up a piece of paper. Read from it, loudly enough now that the words came thin and shredded across the frozen air:

*"—aiding and harboring fugitives sought under standing orders—kidnapper and stolen child—"*

The paper fluttered. Vey stood very still.

Then two soldiers took him by the arms, and he did not resist, because a man alone on a mountain learns that resistance is a currency spent all at once, and Vey was too old and too canny to spend everything on a losing hand. But as they turned him toward the cart, he looked up—not at the officer, not at the soldiers, but directly at the hut. Directly, it seemed to Emrys, at the window. At them.

His face did not accuse. That was the unbearable thing. It was calm, and tired, and faintly rueful, the face of a man watching hail flatten his crop and thinking, *well, there it is.* As if to say: *this is what sheltering costs. I knew the price when I opened my door.*

Then the lantern dropped from his bound hands into a drift and went out, and they loaded him into the cart like a sack of meal, and the column turned back down the track toward the valley road.

* * *

Emrys sat on the cold floor with their back against the wall and Tomas beside them, and for a long time neither of them moved.

The fire had burned to grey ash. Outside, the dogs circled the ruined yard, sniffing at the slashed feed sacks, unwilling to settle, unwilling to leave. Snowlight filled the hut with a pale, sourceless brightness, the kind of light that made everything look already remembered rather than present—artifacts in a museum of what they were about to lose.

"He helped us," Tomas said at last. Three days of speech had not yet worn the wonder from it; every sentence he produced seemed to surprise even himself. "We should help him."

"Yes," Emrys said. "We should."

It was remarkable, how easily the mouth could agree while the mind ran the arithmetic underneath. Emrys had done this work too long not to know the sums by heart. Two days of storm, and the passes barely opening. The governor's gate tomorrow, December the thirtieth, with the city thickened by holiday traffic and Corvane's men checking every cart. New Year's Eve, when the promotion came through and the records became legally untouchable—when forty-one names in a hidden ledger would stop being evidence and become merely grief.

And down that valley road, at this moment, a cart carrying one old shepherd toward whatever the provosts did with civilians who chose the wrong guests.

Rescue meant pursuit. Rescue meant armed men, meant abandoning the high path for the roads where checkpoints clustered like flies on a wound. Rescue meant—if it succeeded at all, which Emrys's professional estimate of their chances declined to rate—arriving at the gate on the second or third of January with testimony that had expired like milk.

Go on meant walking away from a man whose only crime had been hospitality.

"Emrys?" Tomas's small voice. They realized their hands had made fists without consulting them.

"I'm thinking, love."

"You're doing the thing where your face goes far away."

"Do I?" Emrys managed something that wanted to be a smile. "Well. I've come back from farther."

But they hadn't, quite. There was a country inside them they visited more often than they admitted—the one where their brother Jonath sat eternally at a camp table writing a letter home that some clerk would later file under *desertion*, that tidy little lie that had started all of this, that had taught Emrys what armies did with inconvenient truth. Every step of this journey, they understood now, had been walked out of that country and back toward it. And the price of passage was never paid by the traveler alone. Sister Agnes risked her infirmary. Hale had sealed his ruin with a stroke of false paperwork and a freight car door left unlatched. The ferryman had taken coin, yes, but coin could be traced. And now Vey, who had asked for nothing, was paying in full.

*Survival always bills someone.* If Emrys lived to be old, they thought they might carve it above a door somewhere. It deserved to be architecture.

Except—

They stood. Pacing helped; pacing had always helped. Three steps to the window, three steps back, the boards complaining softly beneath them. Because the ledger taught another lesson too, if you read it honestly. It taught that no one survived alone, that every erased name had been saved by someone willing to notice—that the whole fragile apparatus of remembering was built out of ordinary people doing small, deniable kindnesses in sequence, none sufficient, together sufficient entirely. Agnes seeing everything and saying nothing for thirty years until saying something became the braver arithmetic. The ferryman. The judge-to-be, perhaps. Hale.

A network. They had one. They had built it without meaning to, stitch by stitch, the way one builds anything under occupation.

You did not tear the network apart by rushing its weakest node yourself, unarmed, through a cordon of provosts. You used it.

Emrys stopped pacing. Tomas watched them with those grave dark eyes, waiting, as he always waited, for adults to finish being incomprehensible.

"We're going to get him back," Emrys said slowly, feeling the shape of the plan assemble as they spoke it, "but we are not going to do it stupidly. Which means we do the hardest thing I know how to do, Tomas. We walk away. This minute. Up the pass, toward the gate, while they think we're still hiding in these hills. And meanwhile—" they knelt so their eyes were level with his, "—we ask someone else to hold the rope."

* * *

The pass above Vey's land was marked on no map Emrys had ever seen, which was precisely why it existed in their plan at all. Vey himself had described it the first night, unprompted, in the tone shepherds use for geography—with the intimacy of a man describing family. Over the shoulder of the mountain, he'd said, there is a drover's road. Steep, ugly, impassable with carts. But a man on foot with a good stick and no pride could make the ridge by dusk, and from the ridge the whole upper valley lay open like a palm, including the military road, including the prison cart's route to the garrison at Marrenholt.

Including, Emrys realized as they climbed, the telegraph line that followed the military road like a black stitch through snow.

That was the part that decided it. Not rescue—word. Word, sent ahead through hands Emrys could trust, to the only person they knew who had spent three wars mastering the art of acting when observation would destroy her.

They gained the ridge as the light went long and gold, and the valley opened below exactly as promised: the river iced to a dull pewter, the military road a dark seam, and there—small with distance, crawling—a cart, moving at the pace of exhausted horses toward the garrison's lights. Behind and above, Emrys and Tomas were two specks against a white sky, invisible unless someone knew to look.

Tomas sat obediently behind a boulder, chewing the last of the cheese Vey had pressed on them, watching Emrys work with the absorbed attention of a child memorizing how adults solve problems.

Emrys had bought the materials in the market town before the rail yard, telling themselves they were for emergencies: paper, a stub of pencil, and—crucially—a pigeon. Not theirs. A bird purchased from a fancier who sold to anyone and asked nothing, riding in a wicker creel slung at Emrys's pack, eating their grain for five days, an absurdity they had almost released at the hut. Now they took it out, felt the small frantic heartbeat through the wicker, and wrote fast on a scrap of paper folded to the size of a stamp:

*A. — Vey of the high pasture taken by provosts this morning, south road to Marrenholt garrison. Harboring charge only. He never saw the ledger, knows nothing worth knowing, but will hang anyway because kindness has no defense in court. You once told me you survived by seeing nothing. I am asking you to see this. Use whatever you have — infirmary supplies, the chaplain, the quartermaster's debts, anything. He has dogs and no one else. — E.*

They read it twice, added nothing, and tied it to the bird's leg with hands that were steadier than they had any right to be. Then they opened the creel and threw the pigeon skyward.

It exploded upward in a clatter of wings, banked once against the gold-lit cloud, and became a dot, and became nothing—gone south over the valley at sixty miles an hour, faster than any cart, faster than any cavalry, carrying three sentences toward a woman who kept her hospital's supply ledgers balanced to the farthing and knew, therefore, exactly which officers owed her which favors.

"It will find her?" Tomas asked.

"Pigeons find their homes," Emrys said. "Whether Agnes finds her conscience is the wager. But I'd stake the ledger on her." They paused. "I already have, come to think of it."

Tomas considered this. "Is that why we're not following the cart?"

"That," said Emrys, "and because the bravest thing in war isn't charging. Anyone can charge. The brave thing is trusting someone else to fight while you keep walking." They heard their own voice thicken and were annoyed at it. "Someone taught me that. I'm choosing to believe it again right now, and I'd thank you not to argue."

The boy didn't argue. He leaned instead against Emrys's side, small and warm as a coal, and together they watched the cart dwindle along the valley road until it passed behind a spur of the mountain and was gone.

* * *

They walked until full dark, down the far side of the ridge where the drover's road descended in switchbacks through pine, the snow squeaking underfoot, the cold finding every gap in their clothing with the thoroughness of an auditor. They ate walking. They did not light a fire. Twice they heard wheels on the military road below and stood motionless among the trees until the sound died.

Somewhere past midnight they reached the floor of the next valley and a hamlet of six houses where the plan's next link waited: a carter named Doss who owed the infirmary for his mother's lying-in, and who ran goods to the capital twice a week and had, according to Agnes's careful note sewn into Emrys's coat lining, *a cart with a false bottom he believes no one knows about*. Emrys knocked the agreed pattern—two, pause, one—and after a long interval a shutter cracked, lamplight leaked out, and negotiation began.

It concluded near dawn. Doss would take them the last leg, leaving tomorrow evening to reach the governor's gate in the deep holiday traffic of the thirtieth, mixed in among forty other carts hauling festival goods into a city that preferred, this season especially, not to look too closely at anything. In exchange: nearly everything Emrys had left, and a promise extracted with unexpected firmness that the old woman in the back bedroom would never learn fugitives had slept under her roof.

Which left one day. One day of hiding in Doss's hay barn while the valley's church bells rang for the last Sunday of the year, and Emrys lay in the straw listening to them and doing the accounting they had been avoiding since the ridge.

Vey's face, rueful and calm in the lantern light. Agnes's pigeons, real and metaphorical, flying south. Hale somewhere ahead of them, ruined and unknown. And tomorrow the gate, where Corvane's men would check every cart, and beyond the gate a judge who needed muster rolls Emrys didn't have, and beyond the judge a New Year that would either open the graves or seal them.

*Survival bills someone,* Emrys thought, staring up through the barn's broken slats at a sky going from black to iron. *So pay what you owe. Keep the accounts. Never let the bill go unpaid just because it landed in someone else's lap.*

That was the whole of the ledger, really. That was the entire philosophy, compressed. Everything else was courage, and courage was merely bookkeeping performed at speed.

Near midday, Tomas shook them awake with an urgency that turned Emrys's blood to ice water before they were even fully conscious—but the boy wasn't frightened. He was pointing through the barn boards toward the valley's southern rim, where, impossibly high and impossibly small, a bird was circling down out of the grey.

It came straight for the barn. Straight for the creel Emrys had left open on the windowsill as Doss had instructed, because Doss's cousin kept fancy pigeons and the bird knew a loft when it saw one.

Emrys untied the scrap from its leg with fingers that had finally, at long last, begun to shake.

Agnes's hand, cramped and furious and unmistakable: *Vey released on my surety this morning — the colonel owed me his gout and I called the debt in. He sends his dogs' whistles as thanks. Now stop reading and MOVE — gate reports say Corvane doubled the patrols yesterday. He knows you're coming. — A.*

Below the signature, in a different, shakier script, one added line:

*Tend them well. Both of you. — V.*

Emrys read it three times. Then they folded it small, tucked it next to the ledger pages sewn into Tomas's coat, and turned to the boy whose eyes were fixed on their face, waiting to learn from it what the news meant.

"He's free," Emrys said, and found they could say it only in a whisper. "Tomas. The shepherd is free."

And then, because the second sentence could not wait, because the second sentence was already burning its way up through the relief: "But Corvane knows we're coming. Tomorrow we walk through his gate."
