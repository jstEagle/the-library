## 16. The Governor's Gate

The needle went through the wool with a sound like a small breath.

Emrys had stolen it from the infirmary in another life—three days ago now, though it felt like three years—along with a spool of black thread and a thimble that did not fit their thumb. They sat by the window of a room above a chandler's shop in the river district, where a woman named Mrs. Okafor rented beds to travelers who asked no questions, and they sewed the ledger into the lining of a child's coat while the last light of December 30th drained out of the sky.

Forty-one names. Forty-one entries written in Emrys's careful, compressed hand, each one dated, each one witnessed where witness had been possible, each one a small act of rebellion against men who believed that ink could be commanded the way soldiers could. The pages had been removed from their binding two nights before, folded into eighths, oiled with tallow from a candle to make them shed water. Now they lay against Tomas's back like a second spine, sewn flat between the outer wool and the cheap cotton lining, distributed so that no single bulge would betray them. Emrys had worked by feel more than sight, remembering the shape of every page, and their fingers—fingers that had dressed wounds for eleven hours at a stretch without trembling—shook now like leaves.

*It is only paper,* they told themselves. *It is only names.*

But that was the lie of the century, wasn't it. Names were exactly what men hanged for.

Tomas sat on the narrow bed watching them, chin propped on his fists. He had spoken perhaps forty words since the road out of the river valley—the whistle, the man with the silver whistle, the fire—and he spent his silence now the way other children spent noise: completely, exhaustively, with his whole body. His eyes followed the needle everywhere.

"Will it hurt?" he asked suddenly.

Emrys looked up. "What?"

"The book. In my coat."

"No, little one." Emrys set down the needle and turned to face him fully, because some things deserved to be said to a face rather than over a shoulder. "It won't hurt. It will just sit there, quiet as you like, while we walk through the gate. You won't even remember it's there."

"You'll remember," Tomas said. Not a question.

"I will always remember," Emrys said, "even if every page in the world burned."

Tomas considered this with the terrible thoroughness of children, then nodded once, satisfied, and returned to watching the needle. He was six, or near enough that it made no difference. He had walked two days in borrowed boots. He had watched his village burn and kept the only button of it left in the world clenched in his fist, and now he trusted a stranger with thread and needle to keep him safe because there was nobody else left to trust. Emrys thought, not for the first time, that the whole weight of the empire rested on precisely this kind of trust, extended blindly and repaid so rarely, and that if Major Corvane could have seen the boy's face at this moment he might have understood what he was trying so hard to erase.

Emrys picked up the needle and finished the last seam.

* * *

They left before dawn on the thirty-first—not for the gate itself, but to be near it when the crowds came. Mrs. Okafor accepted their coin without counting it and told them, in the flat voice of a woman who had helped many people leave many places, that the Processional Road would be thick by nine o'clock. "New Year's traffic," she said. "Half the province comes through the Governor's Gate to see the illuminations. Carts, wagons, pilgrims, peddlers. Soldiers hate holiday crowds. Too much like work." She paused at the door of her shop and added, without turning around: "Walk slow. Look bored. Boredom is the best disguise ever issued."

Emrys carried the advice like ammunition.

They reached the Processional Road as the sun came up red behind the smoke of the capital, and found it already filling. What Emrys had imagined as a gauntlet—a corridor of bayonets through which two fugitives must crawl—was in fact a fair. Farmers' carts loaded with winter cabbages and crates of squawking geese. Merchants' wagons draped in bunting for the illuminations. Families on foot, children riding shoulders, a man selling roasted chestnuts from a tin brazier whose smoke smelled of home for no reason Emrys could name. Church bells somewhere ahead, practicing for midnight. And threaded through all of it, in ones and twos and knots of four, the grey-green coats of Corvane's men.

Every cart was being stopped. Emrys saw it immediately: a checkpoint set across the road a hundred yards short of the great arched gate itself, with barriers, lanterns still burning against the morning grey, and soldiers pulling aside wagon after wagon, thrusting bayoneted fingers into hay, opening crates, running hands along floorboards. A sergeant with a list checked faces against something. Beyond the barrier, past the gate, the capital opened up in banners and chimney smoke—so close Emrys could smell the bread of its morning markets.

Two hundred yards of open ground. That was all that stood between them and sanctuary.

Emrys drew Tomas into the shadow of a grain merchant's wagon and crouched, pretending to retie the boy's bootlace, and let themselves think.

The plan—they had made plans, Agnes's route had carried them this far, and God rest and bless her for the map drawn in candle smoke—but every plan had assumed carts. Cargo. Anonymity in bulk. Emrys had imagined bribing a carter, hiding beneath onions, holding their breath through a search. But the soldiers were searching carts precisely because carts were what hid things. Every farmer's wagon was being pulled apart in the cold while its owner swore and stamped and lost an hour of trade. A cart was a confession. *I am cargo. Search me.*

And a woman walking openly with a child—

Emrys straightened slowly, and the idea arrived the way dangerous ideas do: fully formed, absurd, and obviously correct.

*Boldness reads as innocence.*

Corvane's men were hunting a kidnapper and a stolen child. That was the charge Hale's warning had described: posters, descriptions, a woman alone with a boy of six, traveling furtively, avoiding checkpoints. Everything about the wanted notice assumed flight. Assumed concealment. Assumed fear. So give them nothing of it. Walk toward the gate at mid-morning, in the middle of the holiday crowd, a tired nurse leading a sleepy child by the hand. Carry a medical satchel openly—nurses traveled constantly; the war had made them invisible precisely where soldiers were most visible. Answer questions before they were asked. Meet every eye.

And the ledger? The ledger rode into the capital sewn into a six-year-old's coat lining, because no soldier alive would undress a child in front of a crowd of New Year's pilgrims, and because if the worst came—if Emrys was taken—then the evidence must be taken too, carried onward by the boy's small stubborn body even if Emrys's ended here.

That was the part Emrys did not say aloud. But Tomas looked up at them as they rose from the bootlace, and something in his face suggested he had heard it anyway.

"Remember," Emrys said, kneeling to his eye level, arranging the scarf Agnes had knitted so it hid the pale line of new stitches at his collar. "You are Thomas Wren. Your mother is a nurse. Your father died of fever. You are cold, you are hungry, and you want your breakfast. All of those things can be true. If anyone asks you anything else—" Emrys held his gaze. "You may be shy. Shy children look at their shoes. Can you look at your shoes for me?"

Tomas looked at his shoes. He was, Emrys thought with a lurch of fierce pride, a very good actor.

They joined the stream of people.

* * *

The hundred yards took a lifetime.

Emrys had expected terror to arrive like a wave and break. Instead it settled in as a permanent condition, a second heartbeat, a cold precision that sharpened everything: the smell of chestnuts and horse dung, the particular grey of the morning, the exact number of paces between them and the barrier. Twenty soldiers. No—eighteen; two were wrestling a goose crate out of a farm cart and enjoying it no more than the goose. The sergeant with the list was older, moustached, bored in the deep professional way Mrs. Okafor had prescribed as though she'd known him personally. He waved through a family of five. He waved through a priest. He stopped a young man with a canvas bag and went through it item by item, holding each object up to the light like a jeweler.

Emrys walked at the pace of the crowd. Not faster—that was flight. Not slower—that was hesitation. The pace of a woman with sore feet and a long day ahead. Their free hand swung the medical satchel. Their other hand held Tomas's, and the boy's small fingers gripped back with a strength that would have surprised anyone who hadn't seen what he'd survived.

Fifty yards.

A soldier peeled off from the barrier and began walking toward them, and Emrys's heart performed a maneuver that should have been fatal. Don't stop, they told themselves. Stopping is what guilty people do when they see a soldier coming. Keep walking. Let him come to you.

He was young. That was the cruelty of it—he couldn't have been twenty, cheeks chapped raw, greatcoat a size too large in the shoulders, and Emrys knew his face. Not his particular face, but the face: the same face that had filled page after page of the hidden ledger, the farm-boy face the empire consumed by the thousand and then declined to write down. This boy might already be in the ledger under someone else's careless arithmetic. He might be in it next month. He smiled at Tomas—an automatic softening, the reflex of a man who had younger brothers—and then remembered himself and squared up with his rifle.

"Mornin'. Business in the city?"

"Nursing business." Emrys produced the pass before being asked—Agnes's old hospital travel warrant, amended by three days of careful forgery, naming a sister charity ward in the capital that Emrys had verified existed. "St. Brendan's. I'm transferring for the New Year staffing. We've been on the road since the river crossing." A small sigh, expertly pitched between weariness and grievance. "He's been a soldier about it, haven't you, love. Better than half the convalescents I've nursed."

The soldier glanced at the pass. Glanced at Tomas. And here it was—the moment Emrys had rehearsed in the dark for three nights, the moment where the whole mad architecture either held or collapsed, because the notices said a child was missing, and here was a child, and all the soldier had to do was ask the boy his name and compare it to a list.

"He yours?" the soldier asked.

"My sister's boy. She's on night duty at the ward; I brought him along for the illuminations tomorrow. First time he's seen the capital." Emrys rolled their eyes with the perfect intimacy of shared adult exhaustion. "He thinks the geese are the most exciting thing he's ever seen. He told a man back there his goose was going to eat him."

"That true?" the soldier said to Tomas. "Goose after you, was it?"

Tomas looked at his shoes. Then, with a timing no coach could have taught him, he peeked up—just once, just enough—and gave the smallest possible nod.

The soldier laughed. It burst out of him before he could stop it, a young man's laugh, and he smothered it instantly with a cough and a glance at his sergeant, and in that laugh Emys heard the checkpoint open like a door.

"Right. Pass is in order." He handed it back. Then, quieter, with a glance at Tomas: "Mind the crowd come evening, miss. There's talk of pickpockets working the illuminations."

"There's talk of worse," Emrys agreed pleasantly, and walked through the barrier.

No hand fell on their shoulder. No voice called out. The crowd closed around them like water around a stone, and ten steps on, twenty, fifty, the great arch of the Governor's Gate rose overhead, carved with the imperial arms and the motto about order and prosperity, and Emrys passed beneath it into the roar and steam and bell-sound of the capital with a child's hand in theirs and forty-one names pressed against that child's spine.

They made themselves walk normally for another quarter mile. Past the chestnut seller again—there were two, apparently, the whole city smelled of chestnuts tonight. Past a church hung with evergreen. Past a newsboy shouting something about the New Year honours list, and Emrys did not let themselves hear the name that was surely in it, did not let their step change, because the honours list meant the commission, and the commission meant midnight tomorrow, and the race was not over. It had barely begun.

Then they turned down a quiet street of solicitors' offices, saw that it was empty, and stopped, and put a hand against a wall, and breathed.

"We did it," Tomas said.

"We did." Emrys crouched, checked him over with a nurse's hands—warm, whole, unharmed, the coat hanging right, the seams invisible—and then, finding no injury to treat, simply rested their forehead against his for a moment. "You were magnificent. The goose. Where did you learn comic timing?"

"From you," said Tomas, who had apparently decided that flattery was a game worth learning, and Emrys laughed—a real laugh, startled out of them, the first in longer than they could measure—and realized as it left them how close to the surface terror still lived, how thin the membrane was between this joy and ruin.

Because they had walked through the gate, yes. Openly. Boldly.

And boldness cut both ways. Every soldier at that barrier had seen their face. The young one with the laugh would remember the nurse and her nephew if anyone came asking—and someone would come asking, because Corvane did not post notices and burn records and issue orders out of idle habit. By tonight, by tomorrow morning at the latest, the description would circulate in this city too: a woman, a boy of six, arriving from the river road. They had traded invisibility for speed, and the bill would come due.

Emrys straightened, resettled the satchel, and took Tomas's hand again.

"Where now?" Tomas asked.

"To see a judge," Emrys said. "An old man who owes me for a bad lung and three broken ribs, and who is going to pay his debt whether he likes it or not." They looked up the street, at the smoke and spires of the capital, at the last full day of the old year burning away above it. "Come along, Thomas Wren. We have until midnight tomorrow to end Major Corvane—and I intend to spend every hour of it."

They turned east, toward the Inns of Court, and did not see the mounted patrol turn into the street behind them until the hoofbeats had already begun to slow.
