## 17. An Old Debt in the Capital

The capital did not look like a city at war. That was the first cruelty of it.

Emrys had grown accustomed to a landscape measured in mud and bandages, where the war announced itself in every sound—the groan of stretcher wheels, the whistle of shells too distant to dread properly, the coughing of men whose lungs had given their arguments to the smoke. Here, on the last morning of December, the boulevards were swept clean, the shop windows dressed in holly and ribbon, and gentlemen in good coats strolled past newspaper stands whose headlines concerned nothing but the New Year's honours list. Somewhere in that list, Emrys knew, would be a name with a major before it, soon to be a general.

Tomas's hand was small and cold inside theirs. They had walked since dawn from the rail yard, through streets gone grey with sleet, and the boy had not complained once. He complained about nothing, ever. It was the silence of a child who had learned early that complaint invited attention, and attention—Emrys understood now—had once come to his village wearing uniforms.

"We're nearly there," Emrys said, though they said it quietly, because a woman walking with a child and speaking to him in a low urgent voice drew no eyes; a woman who looked frightened drew every eye in the street. "Do you remember what I told you?"

Tomas nodded. *If anyone asks, we are going to visit my uncle. You are tired from the train.* The lie sat in Emrys's mouth like a coin of false metal. They had lied to Corvane once now, in the infirmary, and discovered with something like horror that they could do it—that the tongue which had fumbled over every social pleasantry of their life could, when the stakes were blood, move smoothly enough. Perhaps all virtue was a muscle. Perhaps lying was too.

Judge Aurelian Vance lived on Corder Street, in a row of narrow townhouses whose facades had been fashionable forty years ago and had since settled into dignified shabbiness, like old officers at a regimental dinner. Emrys had never been to the capital before, but they had carried the address in their memory for eleven years, the way other people carried a saint's medal. *Aurelian Vance, 9 Corder Street.* Written on the flyleaf of a prayer book he had pressed into their hands at a fever hospital in the river provinces, when Emrys was nineteen and he was sixty and dying by inches of pneumonia contracted in the flood relief camps.

*You have kept me alive past any reasonable claim,* he had said, hoarse, while the water rose outside the ward windows. *That gives you a creditor's right over me. Remember it. Men like me forget our debts the moment we can stand again.*

Emrys had remembered. Eleven years of remembering, through nursing school and three postings and a brother's death filed under a word that meant *coward* — eleven years, and now here they stood at number 9, with a stolen child, a coat full of sewn-in evidence, and a face that Corvane's men had begun, they were certain, to know.

They knocked.

* * *

The housekeeper who answered was suspicious in the efficient way of household staff who have served a great man long enough to become his gatekeeper. Emrys gave the name Brightwater and watched it land on the woman without meaning—and then watched her expression change entirely when she relayed it up the stairs and came back down with her hands wringing her apron.

"He says—" she began, and stopped, and started again. "He says you're to come up directly, and forgive the state of the study, and that if you're the same Brightwater he owes his pulse to, he'll thank you to sit near the fire because the cold gets into his chest now."

The stairs smelled of beeswax and old paper. Judge Vance was smaller than Emrys remembered—all men they had saved were smaller afterward, diminished by the years they should not have had—but his eyes were unchanged, pale grey and quick, set in a face folded like an old letter. He was standing when they entered, one hand braced on the desk, and he did not offer to shake. He looked at Emrys for a long moment, then past them, at the doorway, where Tomas stood half-hidden.

"Shut the door," Vance said. "Marta will bring tea and ask no questions because I shall tell her to ask none, but walls in this city have better hearing than servants." He lowered himself into his chair with the care of a man negotiating with his own ribs. "Brightwater. I heard you'd gone out to the colonies. Nursing."

"Yes."

"And I hear things, from time to time, about what happens out there that doesn't get printed. I am retired, you understand. Retired men are permitted to hear things." His gaze went again to Tomas, who had crept forward to stand at the fire, hands out, coat still buttoned to the chin—buttoned over forty-one names written on paper against his small chest. "Sit down, child. Sit down, both of you. Now tell me why a field nurse crosses three hundred miles in winter to call in a debt."

So Emrys told him.

They told it plainly, because they had learned on the journey that plainness survived retelling best: the ledger begun in boredom and finished in fury; the names written by candlelight under floorboards; the note pinned to a sleeping child—*forget the face*—and the button from a regiment that had officially never existed; Sergeant Brody dictating his testimony with the last of his breath, the order that had left forty men holding a ridge that was never reinforced, the signature at the bottom of that order. They did not say Corvane's name aloud. Instead Emrys took the judge's pen and wrote it on the back of a memorandum, and turned it so he could read it, and watched his face while he read.

Vance did not gasp or go white. That was the terrible thing about powerful men, Emrys thought; even their shock was administratively contained. He read the name, set the paper down, and put both hands flat on the desk as though steadying it—or himself.

"I know this man," he said quietly. "I dined at his father's table twenty years ago. His patron is Lord Ashcombe, and Ashcombe sits on the defence council, and Ashcombe has wanted this promotion gazetted for two years. If it is gazetted on the thirty-first, the man's service record becomes—how shall I put this—not sacred, precisely, but armored. Any inquiry touching a serving general becomes a political question, and political questions in this country are buried faster than soldiers." He looked up. "You want me to hear testimony. Very well. I hear it. I believe it—you'll forgive an old man's vanity, but I have spent forty years listening to liars and I know the ring of truth when it sits at my fire with its coat still wet. But belief, Brightwater, is worth exactly nothing in a courtroom."

"It's worth something," Emrys said. Their voice came out harder than they intended. "It's worth everything, where I've come from. Out there, whether an officer believes a nurse's word decides whether a man gets morphine or a grave."

"I don't doubt it." Vance's voice gentled, and that gentleness frightened Emrys more than any harshness. "But you did not cross three hundred miles for morphine. You crossed it for justice, and justice in the capital is a machine, my dear—a magnificent machine, gears and levers and stamped papers, and it does not run on belief. It runs on corroboration, admissible documents, and jurisdiction. Let me show you what your case looks like from inside the machine." He began counting off on his fingers, and each finger was a door closing. "One: a handwritten ledger of unknown provenance, compiled by a person of no official standing, in a theatre of war governed by military regulations the civilian courts cannot reach. Two: a child witness, seven years old perhaps, who cannot be sworn and whose trauma would be cited to dismiss anything he said. Three: a dead sergeant's statement, hearsay, taken by the same nurse, uncorroborated. Four"—and here his voice dropped—"*four*: the accused is not yet accused. He is a decorated officer days from elevation, whose patron controls the appointments that would decide who investigates him and how. Do you see? Every road your evidence might travel passes through ground he holds."

The fire ticked. Tomas had gone very still in his chair, watching the judge with the fixed attention he gave to everything—cataloguing, Emrys thought, the way children in ruined places catalogue the adults around them, sorting friend from weather.

"So it's hopeless," Emrys said. They had not meant to say it. It came out flat, emptied.

"No," Vance said. "Hopeless is what it would be if you had only come to me a week ago. What you have, however, is a wound in the machine's armour, if you're quick enough to find it." He rose, slowly, and went to a cabinet behind his desk, and took from it a rolled map of the colony's military administration—out of date, Emrys guessed, by a decade, but the structure of things changed slower than the dates. "Inquiries into conduct in the field require two things to convene with binding force: a presiding officer of sufficient rank who is independent of the accused's chain of command, and *original documentary evidence*. Not copies. Not recollections. The muster rolls. The deployment orders. The requisitions that show forty sets of rations issued to men who never appear in any after-action report, the telegraph logs, the reinforcement request that was received and refused. Paper, Brightwater. The machine cannot ignore its own paper. It is the only language it truly speaks."

"The originals are in his office," Emrys said slowly. "At divisional headquarters. Under lock."

"Yes." Vance rolled up the map and did not look at them. "I imagine they are."

The silence stretched. Outside, church bells rang ten o'clock, and somewhere below Marta's footsteps crossed the hall and retreated toward the kitchen, and Emrys sat with the arithmetic of it turning over in their mind. To testify was not enough. To be believed was not enough. Somewhere behind them, across the frozen roads they had bled to travel, lay the true evidence—and between that evidence and this room stood headquarters gates, sentries, and a man who had already begun closing accounts.

"There's someone," Emrys said at last, and hated the hope in their own voice, because hope had become a thing they rationed. "Someone close to him. Someone who—if he could be made to understand—"

"You mean the adjutant." Vance turned. "Yes. I know about Lieutenant Hale. One hears these things. A decent man, they say, which in that headquarters makes him either useless or dangerous, depending on the week." He came back to his chair and sat, and for a moment the judge fell away and Emrys saw only an old sick man who owed them his pulse. "Understand what you'd be asking of him. If Hale brings out those rolls, he is finished—court-martialled, imprisoned, perhaps worse, because a man like Corvane does not prosecute theft quietly when ruin can be arranged instead. You would be trading a friendship for a document."

"I've already traded more than that," Emrys said. "I traded the infirmary. I traded my name—there are posters now, I'm told, describing a kidnapper. Sister Agnes is risking her neck, and a shepherd I never learned the full name of is sitting in a cell because he gave us bread. The ledger has cost everyone who touched it. Why should it stop billing at Hale?" They heard themselves, and were startled by the bitterness, and then recognized it: it was the ledger's voice, the voice they wrote names in by night. "But that's not the reason I'd ask him. The reason is that Hale counts names differently than I do—I've known that since we were children—and maybe he's been waiting his whole career for someone to show him the correct sum."

Vance regarded them for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he smiled—a thin, dry, real smile.

"Eleven years," he said, "and you have not gotten softer. Good. Softness is for after; there must always be people hard enough to make the *after* possible." He pulled a sheet of paper toward himself. "Now. Here is what I can do, and what I cannot. I cannot convene an inquiry myself—my commission lapsed with my health. But I know the man who can: Justice Olembe, of the appeals bench, who owes me a very old favour and dislikes Ashcombe for reasons going back to the flood years. If you bring me original muster rolls bearing that order—if you bring me *paper*—I will have Olembe convened before the thirty-first, and I will sit in that room and make the machine choke on its own documents." He dipped his pen. "Testimony first. Tonight, while it's fresh, I'll take your statement and the boy's, informally, witnessed. Then—"

A knock at the study door. Everyone in the room froze; even Judge Vance's pen stopped mid-word. Marta's voice came through the wood, apologetic: "Begging your pardon, sir. There's a gentleman at the door asking after visitors. Army coat. Says he's making inquiries about a missing child, and—sir, he's asked may he search the street, and there's another one standing at the corner by the lamppost, and I don't care for the look of either."

Tomas made no sound at all. That was the worst of it—he simply drew his knees up and became furniture, and Emrys's heart broke a little further along a crack that had been widening since the infirmary door.

Vance met Emrys's eyes across the desk. Slowly, deliberately, he picked up his teacup, which was empty, and raised his voice into the crisp register of a man annoyed at being interrupted.

"Tell the gentleman," he said, "that Judge Vance receives callers by appointment only, that there are no children here but the neighbours' brats, and that if the army wishes to search a retired judge's street, it may apply in writing to the magistrate—which, incidentally, will be me." He set the cup down and lowered his voice. "Back stairs, behind the linen press on the landing. They lead to the mews. Take the boy and go to the address I'll give you—a printer's on Chandler's Lane, ask for the blind compositor, tell him Aurelian sent a reader. Stay there until dark. And Brightwater—" he was already writing, fast, his pen scratching like a mouse in the wall— "when you've caught your breath, tell me everything you know about divisional headquarters. Its locks, its routines, and most especially its adjutant. Because unless I've misread the whole of your story, you have until midnight on the thirty-first to make a thief out of an honest man—and I suspect you're going to need a better plan than hope."
