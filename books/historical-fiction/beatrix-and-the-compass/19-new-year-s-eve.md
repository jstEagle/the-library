## 19. New Year's Eve

The judge's house smelled of beeswax and old paper, and Emrys had spent the better part of the afternoon trying to decide which of those smells they trusted less.

It was a narrow townhouse wedged between two grander ones on Vellum Street, its windows fogged with the breath of a city preparing to celebrate. Somewhere beyond the glass, cart wheels and church bells and the shriek of children with paper horns announced the dying of the year. Emrys stood at the drawing-room window with Tomas's coat over their arm—the coat with forty-one names sewn into its lining—and watched the lamplighters work their way down the hill, one flame at a time, like a fuse being lit.

Tomas sat cross-legged on the hearth-rug, feeding split kindling to the fire with the grave concentration he brought to everything. He had not spoken much since the governor's gate. He did not need to. The boy had learned what Emrys had learned long ago in the tent-wards: that silence could be a kind of watchfulness, a way of holding still while the world decided what it meant to do with you.

"Judge Harrow says there will be cake," Tomas said at last, in the tone of one delivering intelligence gathered behind enemy lines.

"Then we should be polite to Judge Harrow."

"I am always polite." A pause. "Except to Major Corvane."

Emrys turned from the window. "You don't have to see him again. I want you to understand that. Whatever happens tonight, you won't have to see him."

Tomas considered the fire. "But he'll have to see me," he said, and put another stick of kindling in, precisely, as though laying a stone in a wall.

Emrys had no answer for that. There were moments—more of them now than Emrys cared to count—when the child said something so clean and so terrible that all the adult machinery of comfort ground uselessly against it. *He'll have to see me.* That was the whole strategy, wasn't it? The whole mad wager of the last month: that a face could outlast an army, that a small boy standing in a lamplit room could do what regiments and petitions and the dead themselves could not.

Harrow appeared in the doorway, his robe exchanged for a plain grey coat, his hands clasped behind him in the manner of a man who had spent thirty years listening to arguments and had stopped believing most of them. He was past seventy, slow of gait but not of eye; Emrys had pulled him back from fever eleven years ago in a colonial hospital he'd been touring as a reform commissioner, and had never once mentioned it until three days ago, when there had been nothing left to trade but gratitude.

"The muster rolls are catalogued," Harrow said. "I've signed them into evidence under my own seal. Hale is sleeping in the study—I couldn't talk him out of the cellar, but I got him above ground at least. His hands are still blue."

"Will it be enough?" Emrys asked. "You said witnesses alone wouldn't hold."

"And I stand by it." Harrow came to the window, looked out at the hillside streets with their scattered lights. "Corvane's patrons can buy a witness or bury one. But paper—" he tapped the ledger where it lay on the sideboard, wrapped in oilcloth, "—paper signed into record before midnight is a different animal entirely. At midnight his commission vests. After midnight, unmaking him means unmaking the men who made him. They will fight hardest in these last hours, Miss Brightwater. Or Mr., forgive me—they never did settle on how to write your name in the ward records."

"They didn't," Emrys agreed. It seemed, absurdly, the least important thing about tonight.

"Then expect them before midnight." Harrow's eyes went to Tomas, small and orange-lit by the fire. "There is brandy in the decanter and a pistol in the desk drawer, and I would take neither if I were you. What we do in this house must look exactly like what it is. Testimony. Nothing more."

*Nothing more.* Emrys thought of Sister Agnes's hands folding linen, of the shepherd's empty hut, of Hale's face in the rail-yard gaslight as he stepped aside and sealed his own destruction with a clipboard and a lie. Nothing more. Every quiet thing in the world cost something loud.

At half past nine, the bells began to ring wrong.

Not the New Year peals—those were hours off—but a short, urgent pattern from the river parishes, the alarm-toll Emrys had heard only twice before: once for a warehouse fire, once for the mutiny. Then boots. Not marching—the boots on Vellum Street made no attempt at order. They came fast and spread wide, and Emrys was moving before conscious thought caught up, snuffing the hall lamp, gathering Tomas off the rug, pressing the child's coat into his arms even as Harrow's housekeeper began hammering bolts into doors below.

"Front and back both," Harrow said calmly, from the middle of the room, as though remarking on weather. "Well. They learned that from me, at least."

The knock, when it came, was almost gentle. Three raps, evenly spaced, the knock of a man who has never once needed to force a door.

"Judge Harrow." The voice through the oak was warm, cultured, unhurried. Emrys knew it instantly and felt the old cold slide down the spine. "Major Corvane. Might I trouble you at this late hour? It seems I've lost something, and I'm told you're a man who finds things."

Harrow looked at Emrys. Emrys looked at the ledger on the sideboard. Tomas's hand found theirs and gripped—not the grip of a frightened child, Emrys noticed, but the deliberate grip of someone choosing where to stand.

"Open it," Emrys said.

Harrow raised an eyebrow. "You're certain?"

"He knows we're here. Bolts only tell him we're afraid. And fear—" Emrys thought of every ward round, every inspection passed, every lie told straight-faced to a man with medals, "—fear is what he feeds on. Let's give him something else."

* * *

Corvane filled the doorway the way winter fills a valley. He had dressed for the occasion—a greatcoat with a collar of astrakhan, gloves of grey kid, the silver whistle glinting at his breast on its chain like a decoration he had awarded himself. Behind him, filling the street from curb to curb, stood perhaps twenty soldiers in campaign dress, their breath rising in a single pale cloud, their rifles held with the studied neutrality of men who had been given orders they were still deciding how to interpret.

"Gentlemen of the Twelfth," Corvane said pleasantly, stepping into the hall, stamping snow from his boots. "Thank you for seeing me without an appointment. Judge. Nurse." His eyes found Emrys and lingered there with something that was almost fondness, the way a cat watches a bird that has been very clever and is now very much in the room. "We really must stop meeting like this. People will talk."

"You have no warrant," Harrow said.

"I have a report of kidnapping. A stolen child. A fugitive nurse with seditious papers." Corvane removed one glove, finger by finger. "And a warrant signed at six o'clock this evening by an officer whose name, I'm afraid, outranks yours. But please—don't let the law intrude on hospitality. I'd hate to disturb your evening." He smiled at Tomas. "Hello, little man. Do you remember me?"

Tomas did not answer. He stood beside Emrys with the coat clutched to his chest, and he looked at Corvane the way he had looked at nothing else in all their weeks together—levelly, without blinking, memorizing.

"No?" Corvane's smile thinned by a degree. "Children are forgetful. It's a mercy of nature. Which is rather the point of tonight's errand." He turned to his soldiers. "Sergeant, search the house. Bring me any papers that mention names."

"Sergeant," Emrys said.

The word carried further than Emrys intended—or exactly as far. The sergeant, a weathered man with a corporal's chevron worn thin and a face like a folded map, stopped with one hand on the banister.

"You were at Kessel Ford," Emrys said. "In September. You carried a private named Aldous Wren to my tent on a stretcher, and you asked me three times if he would live, and I lied to you three times, because that is sometimes the nurse's job." Emrys drew a breath and reached for the oilcloth. "His name was Aldous Wren. He died on the fourth of October. And he is not the only one."

"Enough," Corvane said softly.

But Emrys had already unwrapped the ledger, and the room had already changed shape.

It was not a dramatic thing. That was the strangest part, the part Emrys would remember all their life: how quiet it was, how ordinary—a fat account-book with a cracked spine, its columns in a nurse's careful hand, each entry no longer than a prayer. Emrys opened it to September and read aloud into the hall's poor lamplight.

"Wren, Aldous, private, Twelfth Regiment, died fourth of October, Kessel Ford. Cause entered in official record: fever. Actual cause: wounds received holding the ford under orders to await reinforcement that never came."

Silence. The soldiers' breath-cloud hung in the street like a held note.

"Cutter, Bram, corporal, same regiment, died sixth of October. Official record: deserted post. Actual: killed at his post, witnessed by myself."

"Nnadi, Joseph, private—"

"I would not continue," Corvane said, and now the warmth was gone from his voice entirely, leaving something machined and precise beneath. "Nurse Brightwater. You are a woman of—of discipline, of duty. You of all people understand that records exist to protect the integrity of an institution. That grief must sometimes yield to order. Read enough names and you will find some of mine among them—that is what command *is*. I have written letters to mothers whose sons I sent to die, and I sleep, nurse, because the alternative is a war run by sentiment." He advanced two paces into the hall. "Give me the book. Give me the boy. Go home to whatever life is left to you, and by morning none of this will ever have happened. That is the last merciful sentence I will speak tonight."

Emrys looked at him—at the whistle, at the grey kid glove, at the face that had ordered a regiment held at a ford to drown in its own blood and then filed them away as fever and desertion—and understood, with a calm that surprised them, that they were not afraid anymore.

"No," Emrys said, and kept reading.

"—Nnadi, Joseph, private, died ninth of October. Official record: not deployed. Actual—"

"Stop them," Corvane said to the sergeant. "That is an order."

And the sergeant—weathered, fold-faced Sergeant Dane, who had asked three times if Wren would live—did not move.

He stood in the doorway with his hand fallen from the banister, and his lips moved silently around a name. Perhaps his own brother's. Perhaps only Wren's, whom he had carried on his shoulders through four hundred yards of mud. Around him, one by one, the soldiers of the escort had gone still, and Emrys saw the thing Corvane had built his whole career on forgetting: that orders travel downward, but memory travels sideways—from man to man, from tent to tent, from the living to the ones who carry them.

"—Okafor, Daniel, private, died tenth of October—"

"Seize her!" Corvane's voice cracked at last, the polish splitting to show the panic underneath, and it was the most human sound he had made all night. "By God, I will have every one of you court-martialed—"

"Private Lundt," Emrys read on, steady as a pulse. "Private Marchetti. Private Okonkwo. Corporal Reyes—"

No one moved. Twenty soldiers stood in the falling snow of Vellum Street and listened to their dead read aloud, and not one of them raised a hand against the reader. Somewhere in the back rank a man began quietly to weep, without shame, the way men weep in field hospitals at three in the morning when there is no one left to pretend for.

Corvane looked around him and saw, perhaps for the first time in his career, that his weapon had come apart in his hands—not seized, not broken, simply set down. He reached inside his coat, and Emrys saw his fingers close on the silver chain, and saw the moment he understood that pulling out the whistle now, in front of these men, in front of this child, would confess everything it had ever commanded.

It was Tomas who ended it. The boy walked forward—three steps, four, small boots on the judge's polished boards—and stopped an arm's length from the major, and looked up, and pointed one mitten at the glint of silver at his breast.

"That's the man with the whistle," Tomas said clearly, to the whole street. "He's why nobody came."

The words fell into the silence like the first stone of an avalanche.

And then—boots again, but measured this time, marching, and lantern-light swinging up Vellum Street, and the voice of Lieutenant Hale, hoarse and frost-burnt and carrying like a bugle: "Provost detachment, halt! In the name of the inquiry commission, Major Corvane, you are relieved of command and requested to accompany us. Your patron," Hale added, with the flat satisfaction of a man delivering paperwork, "has withdrawn his protection as of nine o'clock tonight. Something about the optics."

Corvane stood very still amid his unraveling. Then, slowly, he took the whistle from around his neck—and held it out, not to the officers, but downward, to the child.

Tomas did not take it. He let it fall into the snow between them, where it lay gleaming under the lanterns, small and worthless and finished.

As the provosts closed around the major and led him down the hill toward the river, the bells of the city began at last to ring out the old year—one great rolling peal from a hundred towers, drowning the boots and the weeping and the wind. Emrys knelt and gathered Tomas up, and the boy buried his face against their shoulder, and over his head Emrys met Sergeant Dane's eyes in the doorway.

"Read them all," Dane said hoarsely. "Every name. We'll stand for them."

The bells thundered on toward midnight, and Emrys lifted the ledger from the hall table, and opened it, and began.
