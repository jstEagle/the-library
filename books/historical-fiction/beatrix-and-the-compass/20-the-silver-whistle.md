## 20. The Silver Whistle

Tomas knew the sound before he knew the name.

It came back to him the way dreams did—not all at once, but in pieces, like a plate dropped on a floor and swept up into a dustpan. A curve of tin. A hole for the mouth. The little cold weight of it swinging against a coat button when the man walked. Tomas had been small enough, that night, to be at the height of the man's belt. He remembered looking up at it. The whistle had caught the firelight from the burning houses, and it had shone like a fish's belly.

He was sitting in the judge's house now, in a chair too tall for him, with his feet not quite reaching the floor. Emrys stood behind him with one hand resting on his shoulder, and the hand was steady even though Tomas could feel, through his coat—the new coat, the one with the stiff seams where something papery had been sewn in and then cut out again—that Emrys was not steady at all underneath.

The room smelled of lamp oil and old books and snow coming off people's wet boots. There were more people in it than Tomas liked. A fat man with white eyebrows who everyone called Your Honour even though he wore no uniform. Lieutenant Hale, who looked worse than any soldier Tomas had seen come off a stretcher, grey-faced and shivering, wrapped in a blanket that someone had given him and that he clutched like a child would. Sister Agnes was there too—she had arrived two days ago smelling of river water and train smoke, and when she saw Tomas she had held his face in her big hands and said, *there you are*, as if she'd been looking for him her whole life.

And by the window, apart from everyone, stood the man with the silver whistle.

Tomas hadn't seen him arrive. But he knew him anyway. He knew him the way you know a smell from your own house.

*There you are,* Tomas thought, and his stomach turned over.

* * *

Emrys had taught him what would happen tonight. They had practised it in the shepherd's hut while the storm screamed outside, using a brass button on a string as the whistle, using each other as the whole courtroom.

"You only have to say what you saw," Emrys had told him, kneeling so their eyes were level. "You don't have to say anything else. Not one word more."

"What if I forget?"

"You won't forget." Emrys's voice had gone strange then, low and fierce. "That's the whole point of you, my love. That's why they can't ever win. You remember."

Tomas hadn't understood it all. He understood pieces. He understood that the ledger—the book Emrys carried like other people carried a heart inside them—had names in it, forty-one names now, because Sergeant Blythe had added his own at the end, dictating it with a voice like wind through a broken window until he couldn't dictate anymore. He understood that the muster rolls were papers that proved the men had been sent somewhere and left there, and that Hale had carried them across half the country inside his coat, frozen nearly white doing it, and that Hale had said, when he laid them on the judge's table like a man laying down a wound, "I signed the false report. It's all here. My hand wrote it."

And he understood that the man by the window was the reason the village was quiet now. Empty-quiet. The way a house is quiet after everyone has died in it.

* * *

"Major Corvane," the judge said, and his voice was careful, the voice of a man walking on ice over deep water. "You were not invited to this house."

"I invite myself wherever the security of Her Majesty's forces requires it." Corvane's voice was smooth. Warm, almost. That was the thing about him that Tomas remembered most, later: how warm he sounded. Like a stove in a cold room. Like the kind of voice you'd want to trust. "There is a stolen child in this house, Your Honour. There are stolen documents. There is an escaped kidnapper who will answer for—" he glanced at Emrys without hurrying, the way a man glances at furniture "—a great many things. And there is a witness, a mere infant, who cannot possibly understand the gravity of what is being put upon him."

"He understands more than you think," Emrys said quietly.

"Children repeat what frightened adults feed them," Corvane said. "It is practically a scientific principle. I have here—" he drew a folded paper from his breast pocket, unhurried, elegant "—a medical assessment, prepared this morning, attesting that a child of his age cannot distinguish memory from suggestion. Whatever story he has been told, whatever he has been coached to say, it will not stand. And I should mention—" the warmth in his voice cooled by one degree, precise as a tap being turned "—that Lord Ashcombe sends his regards, Your Honour. He asked me particularly to convey his confidence that this unfortunate business will conclude before midnight, cleanly, and without embarrassment to anyone's career."

Tomas didn't know who Lord Ashcombe was. But he watched what happened in the room when the name was spoken. He watched the judge's white eyebrows come down like a shutter. He watched Hale, in his blanket, go very still. He watched Emrys's hand tighten on his shoulder—not squeezing, just settling, the way you'd settle a bird before a storm.

Then he stopped watching all of that, because Corvane moved toward the chair, toward him, crouching down to bring his face level with Tomas's face—and there it was. Swinging forward out of the man's collar on its chain, catching the lamplight, shining like a fish's belly.

The silver whistle.

"There now," Corvane said gently. "You're quite safe, son. These people have been very unkind to you, filling your head with stories. Why don't you come along with me, and we'll find you some supper, and—"

"It's yours," Tomas said.

The room went quiet the way a forest goes quiet.

Corvane smiled. It was a good smile. Practised. "What's that, lad?"

"The whistle." Tomas pointed at it. His finger did not shake, which surprised him. "It's yours. You blewed it that night. You blowed it three times and then everybody started shooting."

* * *

Afterward, Emrys would tell him that he had spoken clearly, bravely, perfectly—but Tomas remembered the moment from the inside, where it didn't feel brave at all. It felt like falling. Once he had opened his mouth the words came out the way water comes out of a cracked jug, and he couldn't have stopped them if he'd wanted to, and he didn't want to, and that was the frightening part.

"That night," Corvane repeated softly. He had straightened up. The smile was still on his face but it had gone flat, like a picture of a smile instead of a real one. "Gentlemen. This is precisely the tragedy I described. A child, fed a fiction, pointing at a decorated officer—"

"You wear it round your neck," Tomas said. "On a chain. It's got a dent in it. On the side. Where it hit the ground when you dropped it, when the shooting started, because you were running to the horses, and a man picked it up and gave it back to you and you hit him with your glove."

Silence.

Tomas hadn't known he remembered that either. It came out of him the same way, spilling. "His name was Petar. He had a red scarf. You hit him and then you blowed the whistle and then—" His throat closed. Emrys's hand tightened, just once, and loosened again. Then he made himself finish, because Emrys had said he only had to say what he saw, and he had seen this. "Then everybody shot everybody. And you went away on the horse. And my mama pushed me in the flour bin."

Nobody moved.

Then Corvane laughed—a single, easy, contemptuous note. "A child's nightmare, stitched together from nothing. A dent? Half the whistles in the army have dents. Shall we hang a major on the testimony of a dream?" He turned to the judge, palm open, reasonable. "Your Honour, I have indulged this theatre long enough—"

"Show me the whistle," the judge said.

Corvane paused. Something flickered behind his eyes—the first crack in the polish, thin as a hair in porcelain. "I beg your pardon?"

"The whistle around your neck, Major. Remove it and place it on this table. If the boy is dreaming, this costs you nothing. If you refuse—" the old man's voice went dry as paper "—refusal will be entered into my record, and my record does not stay private. Ask anyone in this city."

For a long moment, the only sound was the clock on the mantel, ticking toward eleven.

Then Lord Ashcombe's name saved no one, because Corvane reached up, unhooked the chain, and laid the silver whistle on the table with a small bright click.

* * *

Tomas slid down out of the chair and crossed the floor. His legs felt far away. The grown-ups parted for him without seeming to decide to. He picked up the whistle—it was heavier than he remembered, colder than he remembered—and he turned it over in his small hands until his thumb found the place.

The dent.

"I dropped it," he heard Corvane say above him, smooth again, recovering, already rebuilding the wall. "In the mud, during the engagement. Any soldier—"

"Tomas." The judge leaned forward. "Look at the top of it. Is there anything written there?"

Tomas squinted. There were letters, tiny, scratched into the silver near the mouthpiece. He sounded them out slowly, the way Sister Agnes had taught him with the biscuit wrappers. "C... O... R..."

"Enough." Corvane's voice cracked like ice underfoot.

"...VANE," Tomas finished. "It says your name."

Engraved presentation whistles were standard issue for senior officers; Hale had explained this to Emrys in the hut, and Emrys had explained it to Tomas. But no explanation was needed in that room. Everyone understood at once, in their own way, what sat gleaming on the judge's table: a piece of the night itself, worn every day since around the neck of the man who ordered the guns, engraved with his own name, kept polished by his own hand, close enough to his lips for two years to warm it.

Hale rose from his blanket. His voice was hoarse but it carried. "The muster rolls are before you, Your Honour. Signed orders countermanding the reinforcement, in Corvane's hand. My confession, also in my hand. And now the whistle." He swallowed. "I was there that morning, sir. I signed the report that said the regiment was never deployed. I have been counting that lie ever since. It comes due tonight."

The judge looked at the whistle. At the papers. At the boy standing beside the table with his hand still resting on the cold silver, because letting go of it felt dangerous, like it might climb back onto its chain and disappear.

"Lord Ashcombe," the judge said at last, mildly, to no one in particular, "is evidently unavailable for comment. How unfortunate for the Major." He took up a small bell and rang it once. Footsteps in the hall. Two military policemen entered, caps dark with melted snow. "Gentlemen—you are placed under my authority as of this hour. Major Corvane, you will consider yourself in custody pending formal charges: the wilful abandonment of His Majesty's forces, the falsification of official records, and the murder of such persons as the inquiry shall determine. Do not speak. I have heard enough of your voice to last the year."

* * *

They took him past Tomas on the way to the door.

Later, people would ask Tomas what Corvane's face looked like at that moment, and he would never be able to explain it properly. It wasn't angry. It wasn't afraid, exactly. It was the face of a man whose arithmetic had stopped working—who had spent years adding up soldiers like sacks of grain, subtracting witnesses, carrying the ones, borrowing against the future—and suddenly the numbers wouldn't come out anymore, and he could see, plain as day, that they had never been going to come out.

He stopped in front of Tomas. The policemen let him, because he still had the shape of authority on him and men obey it out of habit.

"You'll forget," Corvane said quietly. Only Tomas heard it. "Everyone forgets. Give it ten years. Give it five. It goes soft, like everything else."

Tomas thought about it seriously, the way he thought about everything, turning it over like the whistle in his hands.

"No," he said. "I keeped it." He didn't have the right word yet—Emrys would give it to him later, *kept*, and he would hold the word carefully ever after—but he had the thing itself, which mattered more. "I keeped it the whole time."

Something moved behind Corvane's eyes then that Tomas would spend years trying not to understand: the look of a man seeing, in the smallest mirror in the world, exactly what he was.

Then they led him out into the snow.

* * *

Outside, the church bells began.

At first Tomas thought it was midnight already, but Emrys checked the mantel clock and shook their head—only a quarter past eleven, the bells were ringing in some other occasion, some ordinary service, some ordinary year ending in some ordinary town. But everyone in the judge's house stood up anyway as if midnight had struck, because it had, in every way that counted.

Sister Agnes gathered Tomas up and held him so hard his ribs squeaked, and she said things into his hair that he mostly couldn't hear and didn't need to. Hale sat down again slowly, staring at his own empty hands as though he expected the muster rolls to still be in them, and Emrys knelt beside him and said something low that made the lieutenant close his eyes.

And the judge stood at his window, watching the military escort's lanterns shrink down the snowy street, and murmured, half to himself:

"They will call it irregular, of course. A child's evidence. Papers of dubious provenance. They will fight every line of it." He turned, and his white eyebrows lifted, and for a moment he did not look severe at all. "But I have sat in that court for thirty years, and I will tell you what wins. Not patrons. Not promotions. A boy who remembers a red scarf, and a book full of names, read aloud, slowly, until the room hears every one of them. The inquiry opens Thursday. Bring your ledger, Nurse Brightwater." His mouth twitched. "Bring all forty-one names."

Emrys looked down at Tomas, and their eyes were shining and wet, and they were smiling like Tomas had never seen them smile, like something breaking open in the good direction.

"Well," Emrys said softly, taking his hand. "Thursday, then. Shall we go and wake the dead, my love? It's high time somebody listened to them."

Below the window, the bells rang on, and the snow kept falling on the year that was dying, and in Tomas's coat pocket—where Emrys had placed it for safekeeping, where it would stay until the inquiry clerk logged it into evidence with his own small signature beneath—the silver whistle lay cooling toward the temperature of the room, warming no one's lips ever again.
