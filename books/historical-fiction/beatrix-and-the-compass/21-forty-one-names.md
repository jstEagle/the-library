## 21. Forty-One Names

The inquiry chamber had once been a ballroom. Emrys could see it in the bones of the place—the tall windows meant for candlelight and dancing, now fitted with frosted glass that turned the January morning the color of old milk; the parquet floor scuffed by a hundred years of boots where it should have known only slippers; the gallery rail where young officers' wives might have leaned, now crowded with clerks and journalists and one elderly woman in mourning black who had not missed a single session since the doors opened.

Emrys sat at the witness table with their hands folded on the wood, the way Sister Agnes had taught them twenty years ago: *still hands, Brightwater. A nurse with still hands tells everyone in the room that nothing here is out of control.* It was good advice for an inquiry too. The room wanted to see whether they would tremble.

They did not tremble. But beneath the table, where no one could see, Tomas's small hand gripped theirs, and Emrys held on as if to a rope thrown across dark water.

The president of the inquiry was a thin man named Justice Aldous Wren, who had spent three days listening to Major Corvane's counsel explain that wartime records were necessarily imperfect, that the fog of war obscured all manner of administrative detail, that no reasonable officer could be expected to account for every name on every roll. He said *fog of war* eleven times before lunch on the first day. Emrys had counted. Counting was what they did.

On the fourth day, the ledger was entered into evidence.

A clerk carried it forward on a wooden tray, as though it were something fragile—a relic, a bone of some saint—and set it before Justice Wren, who put on his spectacles and opened it with both hands.

It looked so small from a distance. A common ledger, army-issue, its cover stained with mud and iodine and something Emrys preferred not to identify. The spine was cracked from being opened by candlelight, night after night, in a hospital where sleep was a rumor. Forty-one names written in a hand that had begun the winter steady and ended it cramped and slanting, because the last entries had been made with fingers too cold to feel the pen.

"Mr.—" Justice Wren paused, consulting his papers. "Nurse Brightwater. Is this your hand?"

"It is."

"And can you tell the court what this book contains?"

Emrys rose. Still hands. Steady voice. They had rehearsed this moment in the shepherd's hut, in the freight car, in the judge's guest room while Tomas slept—rehearsed it the way one rehearses anything one cannot afford to fail.

"It contains the names of forty-one soldiers of Her Majesty's forces who died in the campaign between October and December of 1899," Emrys said, "whose deaths do not appear in any official record. Some are recorded as deserters. Some as transferred. Most are recorded nowhere at all."

A murmur moved through the gallery like wind through wheat. The woman in mourning black closed her eyes.

"How did you come to learn these names?" Wren asked. "You were not attached to the regimental staff. You kept no official duty that would bring such information to you."

"No, sir." Emrys let the pause sit a moment. "I was attached to the dying. They told me."

* * *

They read the names aloud.

Justice Wren had ruled on the third day that each name omitted from the muster rolls would be entered individually into the public transcript—not summarized, not appended, but spoken, in full, by the witness who had recorded them. Corvane's counsel had objected; it would take hours, he said, it was theatrical, it was prejudicial. Wren had looked at him over his spectacles and said, "Sir, the prejudice is against the dead. We will proceed."

So Emrys stood at the table, hour after hour, and read.

*Private Edmund Calloway, Fourth Battalion, died the fourteenth of October, wounds to the abdomen. He asked for his mother in Leeds.*

*Sergeant Thomas Rennie, died the nineteenth of October. He gave me his paybook and asked that his sister be told he was not afraid, which was not true, and I wrote down instead that he was brave, because she deserved a kindness even if it was not the truth.*

*Private Aloysius Finch, died the second of November, fever. He had a whistle his brother sent him from home, and when the fever broke him he whistled until near the end.*

At that name, Tomas's grip tightened. Emrys did not look down. Later, in the corridor, the boy would say, *That's like the silver whistle,* and Emrys would say, *Yes,* and that would be all either of them could manage for a while.

The names went into the record one by one, transcribed by clerks whose pens scratched like mice in the walls. Each name was a small stone laid on a scale. No single stone weighed much. But the pile grew, and the room grew quieter, and somewhere past the twentieth name even Corvane's counsel stopped shuffling his papers and simply listened, because there is a sound a list of the dead makes when it is read slowly enough, and no man trained in argument can talk over it forever.

When Emrys reached the thirty-ninth name—*Sergeant Bram Hollis, died the eleventh of December, ordered to hold the ridge at Kessel Ford and never reinforced*—the woman in mourning black stood up. She did not speak. She simply stood, straight-backed, her gloved hands at her sides, and remained standing until the fortieth and forty-first names were read, and then she sat down again.

Afterward, in the corridor, Emrys learned her name was Mrs. Hollis, and that Sergeant Bram Hollis had been her only son, and that for fourteen months she had received letters addressed to a deserter, and had refused to believe them, and had been called a foolish woman for it.

"You wrote he was not afraid," she said to Emrys. Her voice was dry as paper. "In your book. Did you write that?"

"I wrote what he told me to write."

She nodded once, twice, as though confirming an arithmetic of her own. Then she took Emrys's hand in both of hers—her gloves were worn through at the fingertips—and said, "Then he was not forgotten after all. Only late." And she walked away down the corridor with her back straight, and Emrys stood there holding a hand that still felt held.

* * *

Lieutenant Hale's testimony came in the second week.

He arrived under escort, having surrendered himself voluntarily—a fact the newspapers found irresistible, and the sketch artists drew him looking noble, which was generous, because by then he looked mostly hollowed. He wore his uniform without insignia. He had surrendered those too, along with everything else.

He confessed to signing the false deployment report. He confessed to knowing of the order that left Hollis's company unreinforced. He confessed to discovering the ledger's existence and warning Emrys instead of reporting it, and to taking the duplicate key, and to what he had done with the key—stolen the original muster rolls from Corvane's office on the night of the thirtieth, carried them half-frozen through the snow to Judge Merrow's house, and given himself up beside them.

He answered every question in a flat, careful voice, like a man reading his own inventory. When Corvane's counsel suggested he had acted from ambition or resentment, Hale said only, "No, sir. I acted from arithmetic. I counted the names differently than the major did, and I could not make my sums agree with his."

Sentencing would come later, after the inquiry concluded. Everyone understood what it would likely be. A career ended. A prison term, perhaps years. Dishonor, the kind that follows a man into rooms after he has left them.

Emrys visited him once, in the cells beneath the courthouse, where the light came through barred windows high on the wall and lay in pale stripes on the floor. Hale sat on the bunk with his back against the stones, and for a long moment neither of them spoke, because there are friendships that survive everything except the truth, and they had already survived the truth, and what remained was stranger than friendship and harder to name.

"You look well," Hale said finally, "for a person who was nearly shot in December."

"You look terrible."

"I know." He almost smiled. "I find I don't mind it. Isn't that absurd? Fifteen years building a record, and I ruined it in one night, and I've slept better since than I have in a decade." He turned his hands over, studying the palms as though expecting to find the ink still on them. "Do you remember, when we were nineteen, you told me you'd become a nurse because someone had to keep the count?"

"I don't remember saying it."

"You said it. You were drunk on bad wine and furious about your brother, and you said, *someone has to keep the count, Hal, or they'll file us all away as weather.*" He looked up. "I thought it was sentiment then. I've revised my opinion."

"They'll give you time, Hal."

"Yes."

"Are you afraid?"

Hale considered this honestly, the way he considered most things, which had always been his best quality and his worst. "Of prison? Somewhat. Of myself, if I had stayed silent? Far more." He leaned back against the stones. "Tell Tomas—" He stopped. Started again. "Tell the boy the man at the rail yard is sorry he hesitated. Even a minute of hesitation, in that yard, could have cost him everything."

"He knows what you did. All of it. Agnes told him the whole story while I was giving testimony." Emrys paused. "He made me a drawing. Of the freight car. There's a figure standing on the platform letting them board, and he's drawn it rather larger than the others. He said that's how important people look."

Hale laughed—a short, startled sound, quickly swallowed, as though laughter were contraband in that place. But his eyes were wet when he said, "Larger than the others. Well." He cleared his throat. "Tell him thank you. And tell him—" He shook his head. "No. That's enough. That's more than I've earned."

It was not enough, and they both knew it, and they let it stand anyway, because some debts are paid in installments across years, and the first installment had been made.

* * *

Sister Agnes did not attend the inquiry.

She sent word instead, in her flat, unsentimental hand, that hospitals did not run themselves, that the infirmary had been burned of its records but not its walls, and that walls could be restocked faster than reputations. By the third week of January she had sixty beds standing where Corvane's men had left ash and splinters, and by the fourth she had nurses—some returned, some new, drawn by the strange fame of a hospital that had hidden a ledger inside its own floorboards and gotten away with it.

Emrys traveled out once, on a gray Sunday, to see it. The new floorboards had not yet been laid in the east ward; the joists showed bare, and Agnes walked Emrys across them without ceremony.

"I'm having them leave a gap," she said. "Under the third bed from the window."

"A gap."

"For whatever comes next. Something always comes next." She said it the way she said everything, as though reporting the weather. Then, softer, in a voice Emrys had heard perhaps three times in twenty years: "I saw everything, Brightwater. Three wars. I saw everything and said nothing, and I told myself it was survival, and it was, and I am not sorry, because the ones I kept alive needed me alive." She looked out the empty window frame at the muddy yard. "But I was tired of surviving as nothing but a pair of hands. You gave me one chance to be more than that. I intend to keep the gap in the floor as a reminder."

"The shepherd," Emrys said. "Have you heard—"

"Released yesterday." Agnes permitted herself the ghost of a smile. "The inquiry summoned his arresting officer to explain the charge of *aiding fugitives*, and the officer explained that the fugitive in question had subsequently been vindicated as a witness of the Crown, at which point the charge collapsed like wet paper. Old Pieter walked home through the snow. He says his sheep missed him more than the soldiers ever frightened him." She paused. "He also says you owe him a lamp. The one you broke in the hut."

"I'll buy him two."

"He knows. He's counting on it. He's a shepherd, not a saint."

They stood together in the unfinished ward, two survivors of the same long winter, and Agnes put one hand briefly on Emrys's shoulder—heavy, brief, like a stone placed and lifted—and said, "Go home. Your boy is waiting, and I have beds to fill."

*Your boy.* Emrys walked back toward the coach thinking about those two words, and how easily she had used them, and how little they had cost her, and how much they were worth.

* * *

The verdict on Corvane came at the end of January, and it was not the end of anything, which Emrys had known it would not be.

His patron in the War Office withdrew protection—quietly, without statement, the way rats leave a ship that has begun to list. The promotion was rescinded before it could take effect. The commission that would have sealed the records on New Year's Eve was dissolved, and the muster rolls Hale had carried through the snow were entered into evidence beside the ledger, and the two documents agreed with each other in every particular, which no amount of fog-of-war rhetoric could survive.

Corvane was taken into custody on charges that would take years to try. His counsel promised a defense. The newspapers promised a spectacle. Emrys promised nothing to anyone, and went home each evening to a small rented house near the river where a boy was learning his letters and a compass hung by the door.

But justice, Emrys learned that winter, is a thing that arrives the way spring arrives in northern countries—late, incomplete, grudging, and never quite covering the ground it ought to. Forty-one names entered the public transcript, and forty-one families received letters that began, *It is my painful duty to inform you*, and some of those families wept with relief and some with rage, because fourteen months of *deserter* is not undone by a paragraph, however true. Mrs. Hollis framed her letter. Another mother burned hers. Both responses seemed correct to Emrys.

And the inquiry itself ground on, week after week, calling officers and clerks and quartermasters, establishing timelines and responsibilities, distributing blame in careful portions among men whose portions would never equal their crimes. Emrys testified nine more times. Tomas testified once, in a closed session, in a voice so quiet the president had to lean down from the bench to hear him, and when he described the silver whistle the room was so still that Emrys could hear the frosted windows ticking as the sun warmed them.

Afterward, walking home through the slush with the boy's hand in theirs, Emrys asked, "Were you afraid in there?"

Tomas considered this with the seriousness he brought to all questions now that he had words for things. "A little. But you were there. And the man writing everything down—he wrote my words exactly. I watched him." The boy swung their joined hands. "If it's written down exactly, it's real, isn't it? That's what you do. That's why you did the book."

"That's why I did the book."

"Then I wasn't afraid very much."

That night, after Tomas was asleep, Emrys sat at the kitchen table with a new ledger open before them—blank pages, good paper, bought with the first money anyone had ever paid for testimony. On the first line, in a steady hand, they wrote:

*Pieter the shepherd — released. Owed: one lamp, repaid twice over.*

Below it: *Mrs. Hollis — her son's name is in the transcript now. She stood up for him.*

Below that: *Lt. E. Hale — awaiting sentence. Sleeps better than he deserves. Visits permitted monthly.*

Not the dead this time. Not the erased. A different count entirely—a ledger of the living, of debts acknowledged and repairs begun, of everything the winter had cost and everything, improbably, that it had returned.

Emrys dipped the pen and sat looking at the blank space below the third entry, wondering what would come next, what always came next. Outside, the river ice groaned and shifted in the February dark, breaking up at last, moving toward the sea.

There was a knock at the door—soft, uncertain, the knock of someone who hoped no one was home.

Emrys closed the ledger, crossed the cold floor, and opened the door onto the night.
