## 22. Beatrix and the Compass

One year later, the winter came again, but gentler.

Emrys felt it first in the mornings—in the way the light through the kitchen window fell differently on this table than it had on the infirmary's long benches; in the fact that there was a kitchen window at all, with frost ferns growing up its panes in white gardens that Tomas liked to breathe on and redraw with his fingertip. The cottage sat a mile outside the capital, close enough to hear the city bells on Sundays, far enough that no one knocked without reason. Emrys had chosen it for that distance. A year ago, every knock had been a threat. Now they were mostly the grocer's boy or Mrs. Halloran from the lane, come to trade gossip for bread.

The kettle was on. The porridge—Tomas took his with honey now, an extravagance Emrys had stopped arguing against sometime around midsummer—was keeping warm. And on the table beside the salt cellar lay the new ledger, open to a fresh page, because it had become habit: before the day began, one entry.

Emrys read over yesterday's while the tea steeped. *Mrs. Halloran — knee better since the frost broke. Walked to market unaided twice.* Below that, from three days past: *The shepherd Aldric — letter received, written by his granddaughter's hand. He is planting winter wheat where he said he would not farm again. Sent him wool socks. He sent back cheese. The arithmetic of debts continues.*

And below that, the entry Emrys found themselves returning to more than any other, the one written in October when the inquiry's final report came through:

*Lieutenant Hale — sentenced to two years, remitted to eighteen months for cooperation. He writes monthly. His letters are dry as army reports until the last line, which is always about the weather. I think the weather means something else. I have begun to hope it does.*

Sister Agnes had laughed at the new ledger, the first time Emrys showed her. They had met in the ruins of what remained of the infirmary—a year ago she had stood in its doorway with her arms crossed while soldiers carried out the burned records in buckets, and she had said nothing then, but a month later she had appeared at Emrys's lodgings with a cart, a cat, and the announcement that she was retiring somewhere quiet within visiting distance. "A ledger of the living," she'd said, turning the pages with her cracked thumbs. "You always did count wrong-way-round, Brightwater." But she had read every page. She had wept at the entry about the shepherd, though she'd claimed it was dust in her eye, and Emrys had known better than to say so.

* * *

Tomas came down the stairs the way he always did now—too fast, one hand on the rail, announcing himself by the thunder of his feet rather than his voice. That was the change no one who hadn't known him before would believe: the boy who wouldn't speak, whose silence had been a fortress with forty-one names buried under it, now narrated everything. His dreams. His lessons. The imaginary war between the sparrows at the fence and the crows in the elm, conducted daily with terrible casualties on both sides and full honors rendered to the fallen.

"The frost garden grew a ship," he reported breathlessly, skidding into the kitchen. "On the window. With sails. I think it's going to the ice country."

"Then eat quickly," Emrys said, sliding the bowl toward him, "so you can rescue it before the sun melts the sea."

He ate. He was seven now—or nearly eight; they had settled on the birthday in spring because he remembered daffodils near his village, and it seemed right that his years should begin with something that returned. He wore his hair cropped short in winter and long in summer because he could never decide, and he had a scar above his left eyebrow from falling off the garden wall in September, which he displayed to visitors like a medal. He still kept the regimental button, but not clenched in his fist anymore—it lived in a small tin on his shelf, alongside a gull feather, three marbles, and the silver whistle.

The whistle. Even now, Emrys looked at the shelf and thought of that night at the judge's house, the candles guttering, Corvane's face going the color of old tallow as a seven-year-old held up a scrap of silver and said, in a voice that filled the room like cold water: *"That's his. That's the man who told them to hold."*

Corvane's trial had taken eleven days. His patron had withdrawn first, then his patron's allies, the way men step back from a fire that has jumped the hearth. In the end the major—the former major, the prisoner—had not raged. That was somehow worse, Emrys thought. At the sentencing he had stood very straight and said that history would judge him kindly, and the judge had replied, mildly, that history would do nothing of the sort, because history now contained forty-one names read aloud into the public record, each one spoken slowly, twice, by Emrys's own voice while Corvane sat listening in the dock. Emrys had watched his hands on the rail of the dock. They had not moved. But something behind his eyes had gone out, quietly, like a lamp carried down a long corridor.

"Can I take the ship's crew outside?" Tomas asked, mouth full. "Before the sun gets them."

"Boots first. Coat second. Porridge third."

He thundered back upstairs. Emrys poured the tea and let the quiet settle the way it had learned to—not the held-breath quiet of hospital nights, but the kind that fills a house where someone is expected back through the door by luncheon.

That was the word, really. Not safety, exactly, though there was that. Not peace, though the nights were whole now, undivided by footsteps in corridors. *Home.* It had taken Emrys most of the year to be able to think the word without flinching, the way a hand remembers a burn.

The adoption had gone through in June. There had been a clerk, a form, a question about the child's surname, and Emrys had sat with the pen hovering for a long moment before writing *Beatrix*—their mother's name, the woman who had died when Emrys was nine and who had survived since then only in a pressed flower in a Bible and a story told once too often: that she could find her way anywhere, that she'd once navigated home through fog by the smell of the river alone. *Brightwater* belonged to the ledgers and the inquiries now, to testimony and transcript. *Beatrix* belonged before all of that. It seemed right that the boy should carry forward the part of the family that predated the war—that had existed when names were only names.

So: Tomas Beatrix. Who was currently descending the stairs at a speed that violated at least two of the instructions.

* * *

They walked out to the gate together after breakfast, because the post came late on Tuesdays and Tomas had declared himself responsible for its retrieval, a duty he performed with the gravity of a man collecting state secrets. The lane was iced over and they crunched along it side by side, their breath hanging in the still air. Beyond the hedgerows the fields lay white and blue-shadowed, and the elms stood with their black branches full of crows conducting the morning's maneuvers.

"Hale's letter might come today," Tomas said. It wasn't a question. Hale wrote the first Tuesday of every month, and Tomas had appointed himself the reader-aloud of the final lines, the weather lines. Last month's had said *Frost early this year; the chestnuts in the yard of my present accommodation are holding on longer than expected,* and Tomas had studied Emrys's face carefully after delivering it, waiting for the translation. Emrys had translated honestly: *I think he means he is holding on.*

"He might," Emrys said. "Or Wednesday."

"I hope the chestnuts keep holding."

"So do I."

At the gate, the postbox yielded a single envelope—the cheap paper of government stationery, which made Emrys's stomach tighten for half a second before they saw the franking: the Ministry of Records, not the Ministry of War. The difference mattered. Everything about those two buildings differed now; one had spent a year handing over documents it swore did not exist, and the other had spent the same year learning to apologize.

Tomas carried the letter back up the lane at a ceremonial pace, and inside, warming their hands at the stove, they opened it together.

It was brief. The muster rolls of the Forty-First were being formally transferred to the national archive—not sealed, not shelved in some back room where paper went to die, but catalogued, indexed, available to any widow or orphan or historian who asked. A public reading of the names would be held at the memorial on the anniversary of the massacre, in February. The Ministry requested—*requested*, the word underlined twice in someone's considerate hand—whether Nurse Beatrix would attend and open the reading.

Emrys set the letter down on the table, next to the ledger, and found that their eyes were stinging.

"They want you to say the names," Tomas said. He knew about the names. He knew all of it now, as much as a child could carry—and perhaps more than some people thought a child should, but Emrys had made a promise early on, in a snowbound hut with the wind screaming, that this boy would never again be protected out of his own history. He was the witness. Witnesses got to know what their witness meant.

"Yes," Emrys said. "If you'll come with me."

"Do I have to wear the uncomfortable coat?"

"You may wear the comfortable coat."

"Then yes." He considered the letter further, with the seriousness he brought to matters of ceremony. "Will Sergeant Wrenn's sister be there? The one who sent you the letter?"

"She writes that she's coming. From the coast. Three days by train."

"Good." He said it with fierce satisfaction. He had a memory like a filing cabinet for the families—Wrenn's sister, Okafor's widow, the twins of Private Lindqvist, born after their father sailed. When the compensation notices came, Tomas insisted on being told each one, and each time he would go quiet afterward, not the old silence but a different one, a settling, like snow finding its level. "Because they should hear them in person. Their person. Saying the name."

"Yes," Emrys said. "That's the idea."

* * *

The afternoon Emrys spent on errands and the small husbandry of the place: splitting kindling, banking the fire, turning the compost that Mrs. Halloran swore by and Emrys maintained mostly out of deference to her authority in all matters rural. The compass came down off its nail while Emrys worked, knocked loose by a sleeve, and they caught it against the wall and stood a moment with it in their palm.

It had been a good instrument once—brass-cased, given to Emrys by a quartermaster in the first desperate week who owed them a debt of the old kind, the kind incurred over a leg saved or lost. It had guided the flight: river checkpoint, rail yard, governor's gate, the needle steady through all of it while Emrys's own pulse refused steadiness. Afterward, Emrys had meant to put it away. Instead it hung by the door, and one evening that first spring, Tomas had noticed the needle pointing not north but inward—toward the house, dragged by some iron nail or hinge-screw in the doorframe—and had announced, with a child's absolute certainty, "It points home."

Emrys had checked. The physics were ordinary: a bent hinge, a stray bolt in the wood. But the boy's version was better, and truer in the way that mattered, and so it stayed: the compass by the door, pointing not north but home, and every visitor eventually remarked upon it, and every time, Emrys gave the same answer: *My son fixed its direction.*

*Son.* The word still startled sometimes, arriving unbidden in the middle of ordinary sentences. But less often now. Less every month.

Emrys hung it back on its nail and went in to start supper.

* * *

Evening came early, as it did in December. By four the lamps were lit and Tomas was at the table doing sums—he had decided, after meeting the judge again in October, that he would be a magistrate when he grew up, "so no one can burn the papers"—and Emrys sat across from him with the new ledger open, making the day's entries while the boy's pencil scratched in companionable rhythm.

*December 19th. Frost. The window-ship melted by noon; crew rescued in time. One letter from the Ministry: the rolls will be public. The reading in February—I have said yes. Tomas has approved the coat.*

Emrys paused, pen lifted, and then added, because the ledger had taught them that the smallest facts were the load-bearing ones:

*The compass still points home. The hinge remains unrepaired.*

Below it, the running tally that had grown all year, the list Emrys turned to whenever the old fury stirred—the fury that had once filled a hidden ledger with the dead and now needed somewhere to go:

*Sister Agnes — visited twice monthly, never once on the appointed day, always on the day after, which is her way. Teaching Tomas cards; cheating; he knows; neither says.*
*Aldric the shepherd — free, farming, sends cheese.*
*Mrs. Halloran — knee improving.*
*Hale — eighteen months. Letters monthly. Weather holding.*
*Forty-one families — compensated, named, remembered. Some will not forgive. That is theirs to keep.*
*Tomas Beatrix — speaks. Laughs. Does his sums. Afraid of nothing except the crows winning.*
*Emrys Beatrix — home.*

The fire ticked. Outside, the first flakes of the evening's snow were coming down soft and unhurried, the gentle kind, the kind that meant the world intended to let them sleep. Tomas finished his sums, closed his book with the decisive slap he'd learned from Agnes, and announced that if the snow kept up, tomorrow's ship on the window might be a fleet.

"Then to bed," Emrys said, "so morning can get on with it."

But at the foot of the stairs the boy stopped, and came back, and put his arms around Emrys's waist—the quick, hard embrace of a child who has learned that affection, like everything else worth having, should be given while there's time.

"Goodnight," he said into Emrys's coat. Then, pulling back, suddenly grave, suddenly eight-going-on-eighty: "In February, when you say the names—I'll be standing next to you."

"Yes," Emrys said. "You will."

"And after the names, can we visit Hale? Mam said—" he stopped, corrected himself with the ease of a child whose corrections no longer hurt, "—Agnes said his chestnuts would be down by then. I want to tell him they held."

Emrys's throat tightened. They knelt, brought their face level with his, and saw in it—as they did in certain lights, certain angles—no ghost of any burned village, no shadow of silver whistles, only a boy: stubborn, warm, entirely here.

"We'll tell him they held," Emrys said.

He thundered up the stairs. A door closed. The house settled around its warmth, and Emrys stood a moment in the hall with the lamp low, looking at the compass on its nail—needle steady, pointing its impossible, domestic direction—before carrying the ledger to the table for one last line before bed.

They dipped the pen. And in the quiet, under the scratch of snow at the window and the small sounds of a sleeping child overhead, Emrys wrote the entry that had waited a full year to be true:

*We kept the faces. We kept the names. And the keeping did not, in the end, cost us everything—it cost much, it cost Hale his freedom and the shepherd his winter and me the self I was, but what it bought cannot be entered in any column. It walks upstairs to bed. It calls me by my name. Tomorrow it wants honey in its porridge and a fleet upon the window, and God willing, tomorrow it will have both.*

*The count stands. All present.*

Emrys blotted the page and closed the book, and outside the snow went on falling, softly, on the whole forgiving dark.
