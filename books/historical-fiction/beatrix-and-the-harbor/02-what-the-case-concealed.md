## 2. What the Case Concealed

The master clock's case came apart the way old secrets do: reluctantly, and then all at once.

Elodie had worked an hour to reach that point—first the hood, lifted free of its pins and set against the balustrade where the harbor light could fall across the dial; then the weights, lowered on their cables with a care that made the observatory's night watchman, dozing in his chair by the stove, murmur something in his sleep and settle again. Now the case itself stood open, its side panel resting in her hands, and she was looking at the inside of the thing she had been hired to heal.

It smelled of cedar and old oil and, beneath both, the cold mineral breath of stone—the observatory's tower breathed through every joint of it, salt rising from the harbor below in slow exhalations. The movement hung in its cradle like a held breath: great wheels of brass going green at the teeth, the pendulum's rod gleaming dull silver down through the case's throat, the escapement ticking with the patience of something that had counted out thirty years of tides and did not intend to stop for her.

She had introduced herself to it, in the wordless way her father had taught her before his hands failed. *You and I will be honest with one another.* It was the first rule of the bench. A mechanism lied only when it was broken, and even then it told you precisely how.

The trouble was not in the escapement. She had known that within minutes of standing before the dial, listening. The tick was clean, the tock a half-tone lower, exactly as it should be. The trouble was higher up, in the strike train, where the hours had begun arriving late—not much, four seconds in a fortnight, but enough that the astronomers had complained to the harbor trust, and the trust had sent for a clockmaker, and the trust's letter had been addressed not to Lockridge & Son but to *Beatrix*, clockmaker, of no street and no number, whose bills were paid in cash and whose existence was attested by nothing except the excellence of the work.

Four seconds in a fortnight. In most clocks that would have meant dirt, or a worn pallet, or the slow sag of a century settling into a foundation. But this was a regulator, built to be corrected rather than cleaned, and when Elodie put her loupe to the strike train she found instead a shim—a thin leaf of brass, wedged with great delicacy between the warning wheel and its stud, positioned to retard the release by just so much. Not damage. Adjustment. Someone had tuned this clock to run slow on purpose, and recently, within the year, because the brass of the shim was bright where the older metal around it was dulled.

She removed it with tweezers, held it to the window light, and turned it over.

A stamp sat in one corner. Small, struck deep, professional: a monogram she did not recognize—two letters interlocked, an O threaded through a V—and beneath it, almost too fine to read, *Vasse et Fils*.

Odile Vasse. Her father's rival since before Elodie could hold a file. The woman whose shop windows on Cordwainer Row displayed movements so finely finished they looked ashamed of their cases. What Odile Vasse's shim was doing inside the observatory's master clock, retarding the official time of the harbor by four seconds a fortnight, was a question Elodie filed away with the careful part of her mind—the part that did not panic, that catalogued and waited—because there was something else now, something that had stopped her breath.

Wedges between the case's floorboards. Two of them, pine, hand-cut, holding a gap of perhaps two fingers' width above the base board. And in that gap, lying flat where someone had slid it and wedged it out of sight behind the pendulum's cradle, a packet wrapped in oilcloth and tied with waxed cord.

Her first thought, absurdly, was of mice. Her second was of the watchman ten feet away, snoring gently, his stove ticking as it cooled, and how easy it would be to set the panel back in place, finish the honest repair, collect her fee in cash, and walk out of the tower as Beatrix, having seen nothing. That was the whole art of her life, after all. Seeing nothing. Being nobody. The Lockridges had survived twenty years of harbor politics by being too small to notice, a failing workshop on a lane the carts avoided, taking repair work under a name that belonged to no registry, no guild roll, no inquiry.

But her hands were already moving. That was the truth she would think about later, in darker rooms than this: her hands had decided before she did, drawn by the same instinct that opened stuck cases and found the fault no one else could find. Things hidden inside mechanisms were placed there by craftsmen. And craftsmen hid things either to destroy them or to keep them.

The cord was knotted with a sailor's grip, wet-stiffened once and dried again. The oilcloth crackled as it unfolded—a sound enormous in the tower's hush, though the watchman did not stir—and inside was a letter.

Not paper she recognized. Heavy stock, the kind magnates used, watermarked when she tilted it toward the grey morning coming through the lantern-room above: a fleur-de-lis and the letters *H.T.* Harbor Trust. The seal had been broken and reseated with fresh wax, carelessly, someone in a hurry, and the red had dripped a thin tail down onto the fold. Addressed on the face of it, in a clerk's copperplate, upright and unhurried:

*To the Master of the Observatory Clock, or whomsoever opens the case in the proper discharge of his duty.*

And beneath the address, underlined twice in ink gone brown with age:

*Lockridge.*

The tower seemed to tilt. She put her hand flat against the case to steady herself, and the case, indifferent, ticked on.

Not *Beatrix*. Not even her father's given name. Simply the name—*Lockridge*—the way an indictment says it, the way a debt collector says it, the way no one had said it aloud in this harbor for years without lowering their voice. Dated—she turned it toward the light again—dated eleven years back. She had been nine years old. Her mother had still been alive. The workshop had still employed three men besides family, and the harbor had still pretended, in its official publications, that the Lockridge signature meant anything other than what certain people wanted it to mean.

She should not open it. Every instinct of trade and survival said so: a sealed letter is a sealed fate, and whatever was inside had been hidden by someone who chose the inside of a clock precisely because clocks are opened only by people paid to open them, rarely, and quietly. Whoever left it had trusted the trade itself to guard it. There was a strange integrity in that, and it argued for leaving the wax unbroken and the packet back behind the cradle and the whole discovery unwitnessed.

But the letter said *whomsoever opens the case in the proper discharge of his duty.* It had been waiting for a clockmaker. It had been waiting, perhaps, for exactly one kind of clockmaker—one who worked under a false name, one who knew about shims and compartments and the difference between damage and design. Her hands, which had never once in her life obeyed anyone but her, unfolded it the rest of the way.

The handwriting changed mid-page. The copperplate address gave way to a cramped, hurried script, the pen pressed hard enough to groove the paper, as if the writer feared interruption.

*I write this on the night of the ninth of March and I do not know if I will have the courage to deliver it tomorrow, so I place it where only honesty will find it.*

*What is said of the Lockridge firm is false. I was present at the Meridian Warehouse on the nights in question. The cargo marked in the Trust ledgers as consigned to Lockridge was never theirs. The signatures that bind them to it are forgeries—I watched them made, three of them, cut from a tracing of an old bill of lading, and I am ashamed to say the hand that guided mine was not unkind to me, and I was young, and I wanted what he offered.*

*They will hang this affair on the smallest neck they can find. They have already chosen. I have seen the list. If this reaches any officer of inquiry, let it be known that the Lockridge name was selected as the vessel for others' crimes precisely because it was respectable enough to be believed and small enough to be drowned.*

*Names are in the ledgers still. The Coriolis carries more than cargo.*

That was all. No signature. The ink stopped as if the writer had heard footsteps.

Elodie stood in the tower of the observatory with the letter trembling very slightly in her hands, and below her the whole harbor lay out in the early light—the masts in their hundreds, the smoke of the fish-curing sheds, the cranes standing over Quay Four like patient gallows—and she understood, in the wordless way she understood a broken mechanism, that she had lived her entire adult life inside a lie that was constructed to protect her and had instead been built to bury her. Beatrix was not a shield. Beatrix was the lid of a box. Someone, years ago, had arranged for the Lockridge name to disappear into smallness, invisibility, unimportance—not out of mercy, or not only out of mercy—but because a drowned name cannot testify. Her father's poverty, her own alias, the careful shrinking of the workshop, the cash payments, the absence from every register: none of it had been the natural decay of a failing trade. It had been architecture. Someone had built their ruin, brick by patient brick, and called it misfortune.

Eleven years. She had been proud of their survival. She had polished the brass of their smallness every night of her life and called it virtue.

The watchman stirred, muttered, and slept on. The stove ticked. Far below, a bell buoy clanked in the swell, keeping its own unreliable time.

Elodie folded the letter along its original creases, wrapped it in the oilcloth, and then—her hands moving with the automatic precision that fear sometimes produces, finer than calm ever manages—she did something she could not afterwards fully explain even to herself. She took her smallest driver from the roll at her hip, loosened two screws at the back of the strike-train bridge, and slipped the folded packet flat into the space beneath, where the brass of the plates would shield it and the weight of the movement would hold it fast. Then she tightened the screws, wiped her prints of oil from every surface, replaced the shim question in her mind beside the Vasse monogram, and closed the case.

Let it live inside the clock, she thought. Clocks keep things better than people do. Clocks remember everything and tell only the time.

She finished the honest work first, because the work was the only thing she had never doubted: cleaned, oiled, the escapement trued, the strike train released from its deliberate lameness so that the great dial above the harbor would once again tell the true time to every ship's chronometer and every fishwife's memory of noon. When the pendulum settled into its restored rhythm—tick and tock married again into one clean beat—she stood a moment with her palm resting on the warm wood of the case, like a hand on a shoulder.

Then she went down the tower stair, past the sleeping watchman, out into the wind, and stood at the head of the cliff stair looking down at the harbor with the letter's words repeating in her skull like an escapement that would not release.

*They have already chosen.*

Chosen eleven years ago. Which meant whoever wrote that letter might be alive. Might be dead. Might be the person who had wedged it into the case—or, the thought arrived cold, might be the person who had wedged the Vasse shim into the strike train, retarding the official time of the harbor by four seconds a fortnight, for reasons that suddenly looked less like error and more like appointment-keeping. Ships arrived on schedules. Tides kept appointments. Ledgers balanced at particular hours. Four seconds, multiplied across a season, could mean the difference between which pilot boat reached a vessel first, which inspector was still at his dinner, which warehouse door stood unlocked.

Someone had been adjusting time itself, and the harbor's one honest clock, and had used a Lockridge to do it—had sent for *Beatrix*, no street, no number, payable in cash, traceable nowhere—had summoned the invisible name to service the visible lie.

She descended the stair slowly, her toolbag heavy on her shoulder, and at the bottom, where the cobbles began and the fish-smell rose thick as wool, she made herself two promises. First: she would say nothing to her father yet, because she needed to watch his face before he knew what she knew, and she could only watch it clean. Second: there was one man in this harbor who understood how paper became power, who spent his days among the registries where the past was manufactured, and who owed her nothing—which meant he was the only safe person to ask.

Ambrose Pell, clerk of record, third window from the corner of the Custom House, keeper of the harbor's memory.

The tide was turning; she could see the line of it move out across the basin, smooth as a hand passing over a bedsheet. Somewhere out on that water lay answers with her family's name forged on them, sailing in hulls she could not name, signed by hands she could not see.

But names, the letter had said, are in the ledgers still.

She turned toward the Custom House and walked, for the first time in her life, toward the record instead of away from it.
