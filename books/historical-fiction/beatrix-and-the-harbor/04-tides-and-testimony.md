## 4. Tides and Testimony

Sable Ferren had learned to read the harbor the way other people read letters.

The water told her things first. When the tide came in fat and early, there was money moving somewhere upriver. When the gulls went quiet over Pier Nine, something had been unloaded that nobody wanted seen. And when the *Coriolis* was moved from her usual berth at the coal quay to the deep-water berth beside the customs house — that was a message written in rope and anchor chain, plain as print, for anyone who knew the alphabet.

Sable knew it. She'd been spelling it out since she was nine years old, hauling tarred line for her father until her palms went hard as saddle leather.

So on the morning after the fog lifted off the harbor like a sheet drawn back from a corpse, Sable stood on the customs house steps with a coil of hemp over one shoulder and watched three men in good coats direct four men in bad ones as they carried document crates up the *Coriolis*'s gangway. The crates were new pine, banded in iron, and each one bore a stenciled mark she didn't recognize: a stylized anchor wrapped in laurel, the kind of emblem that got designed by someone paid too much to make theft look like civic virtue.

She counted the crates. Eleven. Then two more came out of the customs house under tarpaulin, and the count became thirteen, and the men in good coats stopped talking and started watching the watchers, which meant the cargo mattered.

Sable shifted the coil on her shoulder and did what she always did with dangerous knowledge: she carried it down to the one person alive who might know what to do with it.

* * *

Elodie Lockridge was not at the workshop when Sable arrived, which was itself information. The Lockridge shop sat at the crook of Rope Walk where the street bent away from the water like a man avoiding eye contact, and its windows were the dirtiest on the lane — deliberately so, Sable had long suspected, because Elodie's family had made an art of being overlooked. But this morning the shutters were open and the smell of oil and brass drifted out, and when Sable leaned in the doorway she saw Master Ivo alone at the bench, his back to the door, working some small mechanism with the concentration of a man pretending very hard that nothing else existed.

"Morning, Master Lockridge."

He didn't turn. "If you're looking for my apprentice, they're not here."

"I can see that." She let the silence stretch just long enough to be rude. "Where are they?"

"Errands." The word was clipped, filed smooth at the edges like a gear tooth ground down to hide a flaw. Ivo Lockridge had hands that could make a watch movement sing, and Sable had noticed years ago that he used them the way other men used alibis. "Harbor business. Clocks don't wind themselves."

"The observatory clock," Sable said. "I heard it stopped. Heard they sent for a Lockridge to fix it."

Now he turned, and his face was arranged into mildness so quickly that the arrangement itself was the tell. "Apprentice work. Good practice. Nothing more."

"Right." Sable tapped the doorframe twice with her knuckles, an old dock habit, a knock against wood against luck. "Well. When they're back from their errands, tell them the tide brought news. They'll know what I mean."

"They won't," said Ivo, but his eyes flicked — just once, just a hair's width — toward the back room where the family kept its records, and Sable filed that away too, in the same drawer where she kept everything else about the Lockridges: carefully, and with a growing unease she couldn't yet name.

* * *

She found Elodie an hour later on the seawall below the observatory hill, sitting with their boots nearly touching the black-green water, feeding gulls pieces of a roll they clearly couldn't afford. That was Elodie all over. Generous with the last of what they had and secretive about the first of it.

"You look," Sable said, dropping down beside them, "like someone who found something in a clock."

Elodie's hand stopped mid-throw. A gull screamed its outrage and wheeled off over the masts.

"Sable—"

"Don't say nothing. Don't explain. Just listen, because I've got news and you've got a face, and between the two of us we're going to figure out how bad this is." Sable pulled her cap off and turned it in her hands — an old habit, giving her fingers something honest to do while her mouth worked. "The *Coriolis* is loading at the customs berth. Thirteen crates, iron-banded, marked with the magnates' new seal. She sails on tomorrow's morning tide."

"Sails where?"

"That's the thing." Sable looked out at the water, at the light lying on it like hammered tin. "I asked around. Quietly. Old Maren on the pilot boat says she's cleared for the capital, papers signed by Magistrate Corven himself. She's carrying documents, Elodie. Testimony, ledgers, depositions — everything the inquiry needs to decide what happened in this harbor these past ten years. Everything the magistrates will read when they sit down in the capital and decide who the smugglers were and who the honest men were and whose names go in the record forever."

The gulls wheeled. Somewhere behind them the observatory dome caught the sun, white and blind as a dead eye.

"And whose names are in those crates?" Elodie asked quietly.

"That's the question, isn't it." Sable turned the cap again. "Whose names, and who wrote them there. Because I'll tell you what I know about the men in good coats directing that loading. Two of them work for Harrow & Vane. One of them is the customs assessor who signed off on every shipment that ever came through Pier Nine without inspection. And none of them—" she looked sideways at Elodie, and gentled her voice despite herself, because some things deserved gentleness even when they were knives, "—none of them are going to write down anything that hurts them. Whatever goes into those crates is going to come out of the capital as gospel. It's going to be the truth, official and stamped and bound in leather, because history isn't what happened, Elodie. History is what gets shipped."

Elodie was silent a long moment. The water slapped the seawall stones with a sound like slow applause.

"There's a letter," they said finally. Their voice was flat, controlled, the voice of someone holding a heavy weight at arm's length. "Hidden inside the master clock's case. Sealed. It has my family's name on it, Sable. Dated six years ago. It names Lockridges in the smuggling runs — consignments we never touched, signatures that aren't ours, or weren't ours until somebody made them ours on paper."

Sable felt the cold go through her the way harbor fog went through wool. "Six years ago."

"Before I was even apprenticed. Before any of it. Someone put our name on paper and then hid the proof of it inside a clock, like — like evidence in a vault only a clockmaker would ever open." Elodie laughed, and there was no humor in it, only a thin wire of fear pulled taut. "And now the magistrates are coming, and the ship that decides everything sails tomorrow morning, and I'm sitting here feeding gulls because if I go back to the workshop I'm going to have to look my mentor in the face and ask him what he knows, and I am terrified, Sable, of what his face will do."

The confession hung between them. Sable understood its weight. Elodie Lockridge trusted no one — had been trained to trust no one, raised in a family that survived by being invisible, by keeping its name small and its windows dirty. Every word cost them. Sable could see the cost in the set of their shoulders, in the way their hands had gone still in their lap, still as stopped clocks.

So she answered the only way that mattered.

"What do you need?"

Elodie blinked. "What?"

"You heard me." Sable jammed the cap back on her head and turned to face them fully. "I've hauled cargo for Harrow & Vane for eleven years. I know which clerks drink, which guards sleep, which doors stick and which locks are painted-on. I know the tide tables better than the harbormaster does, and I know that the *Coriolis* draws too much water to sail before half past seven tomorrow, which gives whoever's interested a whole night to be interested. So I'm asking. What do you need? Because whatever's in your face right now — that look like you're measuring whether I'm safe to want — you can stop measuring. I'm offering. Freely. No price on it."

"Why?" The word came out raw. "Why would you? You barely know me. You know Beatrix, maybe — the apprentice who fixes the observatory clock. You don't know what being tied to the Lockridge name costs right now. The enforcers are already asking questions on the piers. If the letter surfaces, anyone near us—"

"If?" Sable laughed, short and hard. "Elodie. Look at me."

They looked. Sable held the look, let them see what was in her own face, no arrangement on it, no filed edges.

"My father worked Pier Nine his whole life. Died with a hook scar through his palm and a pension of nothing, because the magnates' books said his years of service belonged to a company that dissolved conveniently the week he got sick. My brother took the fall for a short-weight scheme he didn't run — served eighteen months in the capital prison while the man who ran it sits on the harbor board now, wearing a good coat, probably directing the loading of thirteen crates full of lies. I have spent my entire life watching other people write the record of this harbor, and every page of it has been a lie with a stamp on it." She reached out and gripped Elodie's shoulder, feeling the tension in it, the coiled readiness to run. "You think I'm helping you because we're friends. We are friends. But I'd do this for a stranger, if the stranger was standing where you're standing. Somebody has to put the true thing in front of the magistrates before the ship sails. Somebody has to get the real account into hands that can't be bought. And it turns out the person holding the evidence is the only person in this harbor I'd trust to hold it."

Elodie's throat worked. For a moment they looked younger than their years, and more frightened, and — underneath both — hungry in a way Sable recognized, because she'd been hungry for it herself her whole life: the hunger to be believed.

"I don't know how to do this," Elodie said. "Hiding, I know. Repairing, I know. But fighting — placing the truth somewhere it counts — I don't know the machinery of it."

"Then we learn the machinery." Sable stood, pulling them up by the shoulder, and pointed down the harbor with her chin. "You start with the clerk of record. Ambrose Pell, customs house, third floor, the little office that smells of ink and disappointment. He knows how a thing becomes official — how testimony gets certified, how a document enters the registry and can't be quietly disappeared afterward. If there's a way to get your letter and your name into the permanent record before that ship takes the lie to the capital, Pell knows it. Everyone forgets Pell. That's exactly why he matters."

"A clerk," Elodie repeated doubtfully.

"The most powerful man in this harbor," Sable said, "if you understand what power actually is. Magnates buy magistrates. They can't buy the registry — not entirely, not cleanly, not without traces. Paper remembers, Elodie. Even when people don't want it to." She grinned, sharp as a boarding axe. "And lucky for you, I know which nights Pell works late."

The wind came off the water then, carrying the smell of pitch and brine and the far-off groan of the *Coriolis*'s hull settling against her berth, impatient, already leaning toward the open sea. Elodie stared down the harbor at the ship — at the crates going aboard one by one, each one a brick in the wall of the official story — and Sable watched something shift in their face. Fear was still there. It would always be there; it was bred into the Lockridge bones. But beside it now sat something harder, something with edges.

"Tomorrow morning tide," Elodie said slowly. "Half past seven."

"Half past seven," Sable confirmed. "Which means whatever we're going to do, we do tonight."

Elodie nodded once, and turned back toward the town, toward Rope Walk, toward the workshop and the mentor waiting there with his carefully arranged face — and Sable fell into step beside them, matching their stride, already running the harbor map in her head, already counting doors and locks and hours.

Behind them, the gulls settled back onto the seawall, screaming at the empty air where the bread had been.

And above the rooftops, half-hidden by chimney smoke, the observatory dome gleamed — and Sable, glancing up at it, saw a figure in the gallery window watching them go. Not Ivo Lockridge. Someone smaller. Someone with ink-stained fingers pressed to the glass.

Someone who, Sable realized with a cold drop in her stomach, had been listening long enough to hear every word.
