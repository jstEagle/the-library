## 5. The Master's Hands

The workshop smelled of brass polish and yesterday's tea, and to Elodie it had always smelled of safety.

That was the trouble with coming home after learning something unbearable: the rooms did not change to match you. The benches stood where they had stood for forty years, scarred with the initials of Lockridges dead and gone. The window over the finishing bench still rattled in its frame when the harbor wind came up the hill, still let in a bar of light that fell across the lathe at the same hour every afternoon, as faithful as any escapement. The great case of the regulator clock — the one their grandfather had built for the shipping exchange, the one that had made the name *Lockridge* mean something once — still dominated the back wall, its pendulum swinging with the slow patience of a heart that had never learned to hurry.

Elodie stood in the doorway a long moment, hand still on the latch, and let all of it look at them: the smell, the light, the pendulum. *You are still here,* all of it said. *Nothing has changed.*

*Everything has changed,* Elodie answered silently, and came inside.

Ivo was at his bench, of course. He was always at his bench; it was the one constant of Elodie's life more reliable than weather or meals. He sat the way he always sat, spine curved like a question mark bent over the movement pinned before him, loupe screwed into his right eye, tweezers poised with the delicacy of a heron over water. He was working on a carriage clock for the harbormaster's wife, a pretty thing with an enamel face and a broken repeater, and he did not look up when the door opened, because he never looked up. Recognition, in this workshop, was an act of hearing: the specific creak of the floorboard, the weight of a particular pair of feet. *That is Elodie,* the sound said. *Safe. Family.*

"Master," Elodie said. "I'm back."

"Mm." The tweezers lifted a click-spring the size of a fingernail paring. "And the observatory clock?"

"Repaired. It's keeping time within two seconds a week." Elodie hung their coat on the peg, watching their mentor's hands in the mirror of the polished brass lamp housing — an old trick, learned from him. "There was wear in the third wheel. And the pallets needed dressing."

"And?"

The word hung there. *And?* — as though he knew there should be something else. As though the sentence were not finished without it.

Elodie crossed the room slowly, unhurried, the way one crosses ice. "And nothing. It runs."

Ivo grunted, satisfied or performing satisfaction, and went back to the carriage clock.

Elodie sat down at their own bench, took out the repair ledger, and pretended, for a quarter of an hour, to enter figures. The lie of it was easy. The hands knew the motions even when the mind was elsewhere; that was the whole philosophy of the craft, wasn't it — that a trained body could be trusted when the heart was unfit for duty. In. Out. Escape. Lock. Their pen moved across the page recording hours worked, parts replaced, and none of it was true, because the true entry would have read: *Found a sealed letter hidden in the master clock's case, bearing our name, dated eleven years ago, and I have not slept since.*

Eleven years ago, Elodie had been nine. Eleven years ago, Ivo had begun paying for this workshop outright, quietly retiring the mortgage no one outside the family had known existed. Eleven years ago, Ivo had stopped taking apprentices from the harbor guilds and started insisting — gently, reasonably, in ways that had seemed like prudence then — that the Lockridges keep to themselves. Fewer contracts. Smaller jobs. *Invisible*, though he'd never used the word. A family drawing inward like a snail into its shell, and Elodie had grown up believing the shell was simply the shape of a careful life.

They knew better now. Shells are built by things that have been hurt. Or by things that are hiding.

"Master," Elodie said, keeping their eyes on the ledger. "While I had the case open. There was a letter."

The tweezers did not slip. That was the first wrongness. Ivo Lockridge's hands did not shake — forty years of fine work had burned steadiness into his nerves deeper than bone — but they *paused*. A hitch, half a second, no longer than a badly cut pinion catching a tooth. Anyone else would have missed it. Elodie had spent nineteen years learning to read these hands the way sailors read water.

"I know," Ivo said.

Elodie set down the pen. "You *know*?"

"I put it there."

The room went very quiet. Even the pendulum seemed to hold its breath at the top of its swing.

"You—" Elodie turned in their stool. "You put it there. You hid it. In the observatory clock. For eleven years."

"Yes." Ivo removed the loupe from his eye and set it down beside the carriage clock with terrible care, aligning its edge parallel to the bench's front rail, which was what he did instead of wringing his hands. He did not look at Elodie. He looked at the movement in front of him, at the enamel face of the harbormaster's wife, at anything that was not his apprentice's eyes. "It seemed the safest place. No one opens a master clock who doesn't know what they're doing. And no one with sense robs the observatory."

"That's not— Master, that isn't the point." Elodie heard their own voice climb and forced it flat. "The letter names us. It names the *family*. It says Lockridges signed cargo manifests for goods that never touched a customs house. It's dated three years before I was born and it reads like a confession waiting to be found. And you've known about it the whole time. You built your whole life around me around—around *hiding it*—and you never said a word."

"I said I hid it to protect you."

"You said you hid it. You didn't say why. Those aren't the same sentence."

For the first time since Elodie had come through the door, Ivo looked up. His eyes were pale gray, the color of the harbor in February, and Elodie had spent a childhood believing there was nothing in them but tiredness and precision. Now they saw something else, or thought they did — something moving under the surface the way eels move under dock planking, present and unseen.

"The letter is a forgery," Ivo said. "Or a lie, or a trap, I don't know which, and neither do you. It names us in a business we never touched. If I had shown it to you, what would you have done with it? You were a child. Then you were a young person who worried about everything and showed it to no one, which I counted as my one success as a teacher. A paper that accuses us does not become less dangerous by being read. It becomes more dangerous. Paper wants to be found, Elodie. It's the nature of paper. So I put it somewhere paper could rest."

"In a clock I was sent to repair."

"Yes." A pause, thin as foil. "That was unfortunate."

"*Unfortunate.*"

"The guild doesn't consult me about work assignments." Ivo's voice remained level, reasonable, infuriatingly gentle. He picked the tweezers back up. "You did well, actually. Better than I expected. The third-wheel wear — did you fit the spare from the blue box?"

"Don't." Elodie was standing now; they had no memory of standing. "Don't ask me about the third wheel. Don't sit there with the harbormaster's clock and talk about spare parts. Who wrote that letter, Master? Who signed our name to smuggling manifests eleven years ago, and why did you decide the answer was silence?"

"I decided the answer was silence because silence keeps workshops standing and families breathing." Now his voice did change — a hairline fracture running through the reasonableness, there and gone. "You don't remember those years. You were a baby when the harbor changed hands. Men were ruined in a single tide, Elodie. Hanged, some of them, after the inquiry — hanged on paper. Not deeds. Paper. I watched men walk to the gallows because of what was written about them, not what they did. When a letter like that appears in your world, you do not wave it about. You bury it where only a Lockridge will ever dig."

"And if a Lockridge digs," Elodie said quietly, "and finds it, and reads it, and starts asking questions?"

"Then the Lockridge should stop digging."

The words landed between them like a dropped tool. Neither moved.

* * *

Elodie went to the cold hearth and stood there with their back to the room, because it was easier to think facing away from him. The fire irons leaned in their stand. Above the mantel hung the framed certificate of the Lockridge guildmark, granted in 1841, its wax seal dull with age. *To Lockridge & Son, Clockmakers, for excellence of craft and honesty of dealing.* Honesty of dealing. Their grandfather had paid for that plaque with sixty years of true weights and kept promises, and some stranger had written their name into a smuggler's ledger with a flourish, and their own master had chosen to answer forgery with concealment.

Except.

Something had been bothering Elodie since the moment they walked in — a wrongness below the level of thought, the way a clock can be running correctly and still sound wrong to the ear that knows it. They had catalogued the workshop a thousand times over nineteen years, without meaning to, the way one memorizes a face. And now, facing the dead hearth, they let the inventory play itself forward and waited for the discrepancy to surface.

It surfaced all at once, cold and complete.

Elodie turned around. "Your files."

Ivo's shoulders did not move. But they *stiffened* — a stillness that was itself a motion, the way held breath is a sound.

"Your pillar files," Elodie said, crossing back toward the benches. "You keep them in the rack behind the lathe. Third slot, fourth slot, fifth. You've kept them there since before I could reach the bench. You used to say a craftsman's tools should live where his hands expect them, because in the dark, in a hurry, the hand must find truth without the eye." They stopped beside his bench. The rack stood behind the lathe, exactly as always. Except the files were not in the order of a lifetime. The fifth file — the fine-cut, the one Ivo used for escape wheels, the tool he handled more than he had ever handled any living creature — sat in the first slot. And the first slot's coarse rough file sat fifth. Swapped. Deliberately, or carelessly, or —

or by a man who had opened that drawer in the dark, in a hurry, recently, and put everything back almost perfectly, and almost was a word that did not exist in this workshop.

"When did you last open that drawer?" Elodie asked.

"For what purpose?"

"To take out the fine-cut. Within the last four days. Because it's in the wrong place, Master. It's been in the third slot since I was six years old. I know this room the way you taught me to know a movement, and something has been *turned* in it, and you're sitting there telling me about paper wanting to be found while your own tools confess that you've been searching for something." Elodie's voice shook, and they let it. "What did you take out of here, Ivo? What are you carrying in your coat pocket right now that matters more than the truth?"

For a long moment, master and apprentice regarded each other across the bench, across the small enameled clock with its broken repeater, across nineteen years of shared lamps and shared silences. And Elodie understood, with a grief that arrived whole like a bell-crack, that whatever came next, the workshop would never again smell entirely of safety. That door, once opened, does not close true again. Every clockmaker knows it. A cracked pivot can be polished and it can be oiled, but it carries the flaw forever, and it will fail — the only question is under what load.

"Ivo," Elodie said. Not *Master*. Just the name, bare, the way one calls across a widening gap.

He closed his eyes. Opened them. And for one unguarded instant, before the shutters came down, Elodie saw what lived behind the reasonableness — not guilt exactly, and not shame alone, but fear, old and worn smooth with handling, a stone he had carried so long it had taken his palm's shape.

"There are things," Ivo said slowly, "that I kept from you to keep you. I want you to believe that, because it is true, and because it may be the only thing I have left that is." He began, with perfect deliberateness, to reassemble the carriage clock, fitting the repeater's spring into its seat, his beautiful hands steady as stones. "The letter is real, Elodie. The signatures on it are not ours, but the letter is real, and it was given to me by someone who is dead now, and I have spent eleven years being grateful he was. Leave it buried. Some clocks are better left unwound."

"And if the magistrates unwind it?" Elodie asked. "Sable says there's an inquiry coming. Says the magnates have hired themselves a historian. If the past is going to be opened anyway, Master, wouldn't you rather be holding the key than the lock?"

Ivo's tweezers hovered above the movement and did not descend.

That night Elodie did not sleep. They lay in their narrow bed under the eaves listening to the regulator tick in the workshop below, counting its beats the way other people say prayers, and around two in the morning they heard the street door open — softly, so softly — and close again twenty minutes later. They heard Ivo's boots on the stair, his step in the workshop, the particular scrape of the third floorboard. And then, unmistakably, muffled by floor and distance but clear enough to a ear raised on mechanisms: the sound of the strongbox under the stairs, its lock yielding to a key, its lid coming up, and — after a long silence — closing again.

When Elodie crept down at dawn, the strongbox stood open and empty except for the deed to the workshop and their mother's wedding ring.

Every paper Ivo had ever hoarded — the old invoices, the guild correspondence, the letters in his cramped hand that Elodie had never been allowed to read — was gone. Taken out in the night, in the dark, in a hurry. Carried somewhere. Toward someone. And Elodie stood in the gray light with the empty box gaping before them and understood that whatever their master meant to trade, or burn, or bury, the record of it was walking out of their reach minute by minute, and the tide would not wait.

They knew of only one place in the harbor where a man might go to make papers disappear — or to find out what papers others wanted disappeared.

The harbor office kept its ledgers locked, but locks, Elodie had been told since childhood, are just opinions rendered in brass.

They went to find Sable.
