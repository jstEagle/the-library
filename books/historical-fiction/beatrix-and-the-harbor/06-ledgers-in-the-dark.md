## 6. Ledgers in the Dark

The harbor office stood at the end of Quay Street like a man with his back to the sea, all shoulders and no windows facing the water. Sable Ferren had walked past it ten thousand times in her life—carrying crates, carrying messages, carrying other people's weights—and had never once thought of it as anything but what it appeared: a squat brick box where paper went to sleep. Now, standing in the alley behind it with fog rolling off the harbor in slow gray ropes, she understood that buildings could lie the same as people.

"You're certain about the watchman," Elodie whispered.

Sable kept her eyes on the office's rear door, its iron handle furred with rust. "Tobias Crane. He drinks at the Anchor and Chain every night from eleven until one, and he drinks until he can't find his own boots. He'll be snoring in the coal shed behind the tavern till dawn. I've seen him there myself more mornings than I care to count."

"And you're certain you want to be part of this."

It was a fair question. Sable turned it over the way she'd turn a bitt of driftwood in her hands, feeling its weight. She was a dockworker. Her whole life was lifting things for other people, moving goods whose purposes she was never told, signing manifests for cargo she would never see again. Nobody had ever asked her opinion on anything larger than whether a crate should go bow-first or stern-first.

"I've been part of this harbor my whole life," she said. "Might as well know what it's been carrying."

The lock gave to Elodie's picks faster than Sable could have believed—not quickly, exactly, but surely, each small click arriving like a word in a sentence being read aloud. The door swung inward on hinges that did not creak, which struck Sable as wrong somehow. Offices like this ought to protest. This one simply admitted them, and that felt like complicity.

Inside smelled of tallow, dust, and old ink—the smell of paper that had outlived the men who wrote it. Elodie struck a match and cupped it, and the light found shelves rising floor to ceiling, hundreds of ledgers bound in canvas and leather, their spines labeled in a clerk's careful hand. *Inbound. Outbound. Wharfage. Tonnage.* Years stacked upon years like sediment.

"Where do we even start?" Sable breathed.

"The Coriolis." Elodie moved along the shelf, finger walking the spines. "If she's been running magnate cargo under false papers, it'll be in the outbound tonnage books. Signatures, cargo descriptions, consignees. Paper always repeats itself if you make it talk long enough."

Sable stationed herself at the window, watching the alley. That was the arrangement: Elodie read, Sable watched. But she found herself turning back again and again, drawn by the sight of Elodie Lockridge in the guttering matchlight, moving through those books like someone reading scripture. There was something in it that frightened her—not the breaking and entering, which was honestly the least of her worries now, but the look on Elodie's face. It was the face of a person finding their own name written somewhere it should never have been.

"Here." Elodie's voice had gone flat. "Eighteen ninety-three. The Coriolis, outbound, manifest signed... signed 'Elias Lockridge.'"

"That your father?"

"My uncle. Dead six years. And this—" Elodie's finger trembled against the page. "This is dated three months after he took to his bed. He couldn't hold a pen. Sable, he couldn't hold a *spoon*."

* * *

They copied by matchlight and moonlight, taking turns, because Sable insisted—one set of eyes on the ledger, one on the street. Elodie wrote fast and small, reproducing entries into a notebook borrowed from Odile Vasse's shop, dates and tonnages and signatures rendered in an apprentice's precise hand. Sable watched the alley fog shift and listened to the scratch of the pencil and felt something building in her chest that she didn't have a name for yet.

She had grown up on stories of the great smuggling years. Every dock kid had. The magnates' ships ran untaxed silk and untaxed brandy and worse through the harbor while the customs men looked the other way, and when the inquiry finally came—a whole decade ago now—it had swallowed a few small names and spat out nothing else. The Ferrens of the world kept their heads down and their backs bent and were grateful for the work, even the work that wasn't clean. Especially that work.

And here, in a dead man's signature on a live man's ledger, was the machinery of it all, still oiled, still turning.

"There's a pattern," Elodie said softly. "Look. Every third shipment, the Coriolis clears with Lockridge papers. Not just my family—here, the Hallowell marks, the Prynne consignments. All of them old families, all of them respectable, all of them—" the pencil stopped, "—all of them *uninvolved*. Names borrowed. Signatures forged."

"Why forge names of people who aren't in on it?"

"Because that's the point, isn't it." Elodie's voice cracked on it. "If the trade ever comes to light, the blame lands on families too proud or too dead to defend themselves. The magnates get their profits, and when the fire comes, they hand the magistrates a list of other people's names to burn."

Sable came away from the window and looked over Elodie's shoulder. The ledger lay open under her friend's cupped hands, and in the poor light the columns of figures seemed to move, tide-like, pulling. She saw it then—the sheer size of it. Not one ship, not one season. Years of it. A harbor built on a lie so large nobody had thought to look for it, because who hides an ocean?

Her exhilaration, which had been singing in her blood since they slipped out of Dock Row, curdled all at once into something cold and slow. She thought of Tobias Crane drunk in the coal shed. She thought of the enforcers who walked the quay in pairs after dark. She thought of how many men drew wages from the men whose signatures sat at the bottom of these pages.

"Elodie," she said. "We need to go. Now."

"One more entry—"

"Now." Because down the alley, between two warehouse walls, Sable had seen a lantern swing. Not carried—swung, casual and confident, the walk of a man making rounds who expected to find nothing. "Lamp on the lane. Come on."

Elodie closed the ledger with the gentleness of someone laying down a sleeping child, and they went out the way they'd come, and Sable pulled the door shut until the latch caught, and pressed herself flat to the cold brick as the lantern rounded the corner.

Footsteps. Unhurried. A whistle—an enforcer's two-note signal, low then high, the sound Sable had heard all her life and never once feared until tonight.

The steps paused at the rear door.

Sable held her breath. Beside her, she could feel Elodie doing the same, both of them pressed into the shallow dark beneath the loading stair, close enough that she could feel her friend's heart hammering through two layers of coat. Above them the fog moved. Below them, the puddle at their feet held a trembling coin of lamplight.

The man tried the handle. It held. A grunt. Then—worse than anger—a soft chuckle, as though he'd expected nothing anyway, as though whatever the office contained belonged to him and his masters so completely that there was no need to guard it closely. The footsteps resumed, receded, and the lantern's glow slid off across the quay like water finding its level.

They waited a long time anyway. Long enough for Sable's knees to ache against wet cobblestone. Long enough for the cold to reach her bones and settle there like a tenant.

When they finally moved, they didn't speak until they were three streets away, in the shadow of the fish market where the smell covered everything and no one respectable ever came.

"He laughed," Elodie said.

"I heard it."

"He wasn't checking on anything. He was *walking his property*." Elodie's hands were shaking now that shaking was permitted, and Sable reached out and steadied them without thinking, the way you'd steady a crate on a swaying deck. "Sable, do you understand what we copied? It isn't one letter. It isn't one forged name. It's the whole spine of this harbor, and every vertebra of it is owned by four or five men who can buy magistrates the way I buy lamp oil."

"Aye." Sable's mouth was dry. "I understand it."

"Do you? Because an hour ago I wanted proof. Now I have proof, and all I can think is that proof is only as strong as the hands that hold it, and ours—" Elodie looked down at those hands, ink-stained, calloused at the fingertips from files and pin-wires, "—ours are apprentice hands. They break easy."

Sable let go and stepped back, and the market's darkness folded around them both.

Here was the thing she couldn't say, or maybe wouldn't: she had spent her whole life believing the harbor worked the way it looked, that the ships came and went honestly, that her labor bought honest bread. Tonight she had learned otherwise, and the knowledge fit her like a coat cut for somebody bigger. There was room in it for fear, yes. But there was room in it, too, for something else—for the strange, terrible freedom of knowing exactly whom to hate and exactly why.

"My dad hauled rope on the Coriolis for nine years," she said at last. "Broke his back for that ship. Died still owing the chandlery money, and the magnate who owned her sent a wreath to the funeral and docked his final wages for the time he missed dying." She heard her own voice go hard and flat, a wharf-woman's voice, a voice for counting. "So don't talk to me about what our hands can bear, Beatrix. I've been bearing things since before you learned to hold a file."

Elodie flinched at the alias—Sable saw it land, saw the old flinch of a person wearing a name like borrowed clothes—but she didn't look away, and after a moment she nodded, once, like a clock accepting its winding.

"Then we need more than copies," Elodie said. "Copies can be called forgeries. We need someone who keeps records for a living. Someone who understands how paper becomes truth."

Sable thought immediately of the clerk. Ambrose Pell, small and gray and precise, who ruled his registry room the way a lighthouse keeper rules a rock—humbly, totally, and without any illusion about who respected him. Whether Pell was one of the owned or one of the free, she couldn't say. Nobody could, which might have been the highest praise a harbor man could earn.

"Pell," she said. "The clerk of record."

"Pell," Elodie agreed.

Above them the sky was beginning the long gray negotiation that passed for dawn in this city, and somewhere beyond the breakwater the Coriolis rode at anchor, her holds full of other people's crimes and other people's names. Sable pulled her cap lower and started toward Dock Row, and behind her she heard Elodie fall into step, and ahead of them the harbor lay waiting, vast and patient, keeping its account.

But as they crossed the empty market square, a figure detached itself from the doorway of the shuttered coffeehouse—a woman in a workman's coat, arms crossed, waiting with the stillness of someone who had been standing there long enough to grow roots.

Even in the half-light, Sable knew the set of those shoulders, the exactness of that posture. Odile Vasse. And she was looking at Elodie with an expression that said, as clearly as words:

*I know where you've been. And I know what you carry.*

"We need to talk," said the rival clockmaker. "About the harbor office. About what you took. And about what I want."
