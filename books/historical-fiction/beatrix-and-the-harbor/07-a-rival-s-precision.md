## 7. A Rival's Precision

Odile Vasse had always believed she could tell a great deal about a person from their hands.

It was the first thing she taught her own apprentices, back when she could afford apprentices: *watch the hands before you listen to the mouth.* A hand that hesitated at a lock had stolen before. A hand that flinched from cold metal belonged to someone raised in comfort. And a hand that moved through another workshop's drawers with reverence rather than greed—lifting each tool as if it might wake—that hand belonged to a craftsman, whatever else it belonged to besides.

The hand currently working its way through her shipping ledgers, by the light of a single shaded lamp past midnight, belonged to someone very careful indeed.

Odile stood in the doorway of her own back room and did not move. She had come down for the oil she'd forgotten—her left wrist ached on damp nights, an old injury from a press that had taken a finger's worth of skin and most of her patience—and she had heard, instead of silence, the whisper of paper. Now she watched the intruder in the mirror of her polished bench plate: a slight figure hunched over the ledger of harbor manifests, copying by pen into a notebook, lips moving faintly with the numbers as though tasting them.

She knew those shoulders. She knew that coat, darned at the elbow with thread two shades wrong. The apprentice who called herself Beatrix—the one who had beaten Odile's bid for the observatory contract with work so fine it had made Odile laugh alone in her shop, one short bitter bark at midnight—was sitting in her chair, at her desk, stealing from her ledgers.

Any other night, any other year, Odile would have gone straight for the constable. Harbor theft was harbor theft, and reputation was the only currency a clockmaker could not repair with a file and a steady hand. She had built her name out of exactly this kind of precision: every gear cut true, every promise kept to the minute, every grudge maintained like a fine movement, oiled and dusted and running.

And she had grudges enough. That was the trouble.

She let the floorboard speak for her—one deliberate creak, placed the way she would place a counterweight—and watched the figure go still as a stopped clock.

"Close the ledger," Odile said. "Gently. It's older than both of us."

Beatrix turned slowly. In the lamplight the face was younger than Odile had remembered, and more afraid, and beneath the fear something harder and more interesting: not guilt. Calculation. The look of someone measuring the distance to the door and finding it, correctly, too far.

"Madame Vasse." The voice was low, pitched carefully between what it wanted to be and what it was. "I can explain."

"You are in my shop at the second hour of the morning, copying harbor manifests through a locked door you opened without breaking—which tells me you have skill, or a key, or both." Odile came forward and set the oil flask down on the bench with a small, deliberate click. "So explain. But understand first that I have already noticed which pages you chose. Not the customs rolls. Not the duty schedules. The Coriolis consignments. Three years of them." She tilted her head. "A common thief reads money. You read names."

* * *

The girl—for she was hardly more, Odile saw now, now that the lamplight had done its honest work—did not run. Odile noted that with something adjacent to respect. She closed the ledger first, gently, exactly as told, and squared its edges with the others, and only then lifted her chin.

"If I tell you the truth," Beatrix said, "you'll have me arrested anyway. But you'll arrest me knowing it, and I find I'd rather be arrested for the truth than for a lie I told badly."

"That," said Odile, "is the first sensible sentence anyone has spoken in this room all week. Sit."

There was only one other chair, a stool she kept for clients who wouldn't leave. Beatrix sat on it as though it were an escarpment's edge. Her notebook lay on the desk between them, and Odile picked it up without asking—let the girl watch her do it—and turned the pages.

The copying was immaculate. That annoyed her almost more than the theft would have. Column after column of manifest entries in a small, exact hand, dates cross-referenced, tonnages circled where they disagreed with dock receipts, and beside certain entries—perhaps a third of them—a notation in a finer stroke: initials that were not initials. *E.L.* written small and slanted, again and again, like a signature repeating itself under interrogation.

"Lockridge," Odile said.

He had expected denial. She watched the girl prepare it, watched the lie assemble itself behind the eyes the way a movement assembles in a case—wheel, pinion, escape—and then watched it be taken apart again, unused.

"The signatures on those manifests aren't mine," Beatrix said quietly. "They're good. Better than good. Someone studied Lockridge hand for years to make them. But I know the family hand the way you know your own presses, madame, and they are not ours. They were never ours. And in three days—" her voice caught and steadied, a wheel catching a tooth "—in three days the Coriolis sails with the evidence, and the inquiry in the capital will read those signatures and conclude that my family smuggled half the harbor's contraband through a war's end, and no one will ever ask who held the pen."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above, gulls argued over the harbor's leavings, the way they did even at this hour, even in the dark.

"Your family," Odile said.

"Beatrix is a harbor name. A working name." The girl's hands were flat on her knees, pressed down as if to keep them from shaking; Odile recognized the technique, used it herself before difficult clients. "My name is Elodie Lockridge."

There it was, then. Odile sat down slowly across the desk, and for a long moment neither of them spoke, and the space between them filled with everything a harbor says at night: rope creaking against pilings, the far iron clang of the night shift, water worrying at stone.

She should send for the constable. She thought it clearly, dutifully, the way one thinks of winding a clock: because it was the mechanism, because it was what one did. A Lockridge, caught red-handed—or red-penned—in her shop. If she reported it, she would be owed a favor by men whose favors were worth having. Halloran of the shipping trust. Merrick of the wharf company. Men whose portraits would hang in the new exchange building when it opened, smiling their painted smiles over a harbor they owned down to the silt.

Men who had ruined her.

* * *

Her father had not been ruined all at once. Ruin, Odile had learned, rarely announced itself; it arrived the way damp enters a wall, quietly, through joints nobody inspected, until one morning the plaster came away in your hand and the whole face of the thing was hollow.

Edouard Vasse had been the finest marine chronometer-maker on the coast. Not the richest—the magnates saw to that—but the finest, and the harbor office had kept its standard clocks by his hand for thirty years. Then the trust had needed a convenient failure. There had been an inquiry into a foundered ship, the *Meridian*, lost with eleven souls off the shoals, and questions asked about harbor timing and tide tables, and Edouard Vasse's certification had been produced, examined, and declared irregular. Irregular. The word had done its work in a week. By month's end the harbor office had new clockmakers, new contractors, new friends of the trust, and Edouard Vasse had a hearing he could not win because the documents against him had been altered before he ever saw them—altered expertly, in a hand indistinguishable from his own.

He died eighteen months later. His heart, the physician said. Odile had stood at the graveside in the rain with eleven other families who had lost people on the *Meridian*, and she had looked at the faces of the magnates in their carriage at the cemetery gate, come to be seen being solemn, and she had understood something that had organized her life since:

The truth did not need to be destroyed to be defeated. It only needed to be filed incorrectly.

She had spent nine years proving she could outwork them. Nine years of perfect escapements and punctual deliveries and a reputation built gear by gear, telling herself that precision would eventually be mistaken for justice, that the harbor would notice, that quality was its own revenge. And tonight a stranger had broken into her shop to show her how wrong she was—because the strangers' family had been defeated by the same weapon, the same forged hand, the same filing cabinet, and the strangers had never even been invited to lose.

"You knew my father's work," Odile said at last. It was not a question. The girl's eyes had gone to the chronometer on the side shelf—the old man's last piece, the one Odile would sell when hunger outran sentiment and not one hour sooner.

"He rated our observatory regulators against it," Elodie said. "Before. He came to the workshop when I was small. I remember his hands." She hesitated, then added, with the terrible frankness of the young: "I remember thinking he handled tools the way other people handle children."

Odile laughed. It escaped before she could inspect it—short, rusty, real. Nine years since anything in this room had made her laugh.

"Yes," she said. "That was him." She rose, crossed to the shelf, and brought the chronometer down. Its case was scarred, its brass gone the color of old honey. She set it on the desk between them like a document entered into evidence. "The harbor office declared this instrument irregular too, after he died. An audit. They sent a boy to do it, an apprentice with soft hands and a printed checklist, and he wrote *irregular* because he was told to write *irregular*, and that word is still in the record. Anyone who looks can find it. Anyone who looks finds my father named careless beside eleven drowned men." She turned the chronometer so the girl could see the movement through the crystal. "It has never lost four seconds in nine years. I check it every Sunday. Do you understand what that means, Elodie Lockridge? It means the record is not merely wrong. It runs backward. It contradicts the object in front of it, and the object loses, because paper outweighs brass in a courtroom, and men who control the paper know it."

Elodie's hand had come up to hover over the crystal—not touching; Odile noted the discipline of that—and her expression was one Odile recognized from her own mirror: grief being converted, right before your eyes, into something with edges.

"The ledgers," Elodie said. "The Coriolis manifests. The signatures that aren't mine." She swallowed. "Madame, they're the same hand."

"I know," Odile said. "I've been looking at it for three years."

The girl stared at her.

"You think I don't read the harbor records?" Odile allowed herself the thin smile that had frightened more than one client. "Every quarter I read the customs publications, the shipping notices, the inquiry abstracts. Looking for mistakes. Looking for my father's name misused, or the *Meridian*'s papers surfacing, or—" she touched the manifest page, the repeated signature "—or this. Because I would know that hand anywhere, Lockridge. It certified my father's ruin. It appeared in the harbor office the same season the trust hired its new clerks. It has signed things for Halloran and Merrick for a decade under six different names, and every one of those names is clean, and every family it borrowed is dirty." She leaned back. "You are not the first family they forged. You are simply the first to notice while there is still a tide to catch."

The lamp flame bent and recovered. Elodie Lockridge sat very still, and Odile watched her arrive somewhere—watched the fear and the fury fuse, the way solder fuses, needing exactly the right heat and no more.

"Why didn't you report it?" the girl asked finally. "If you've known for three years. Why stay quiet?"

"Because knowing is worthless." Odile said it flatly, without self-pity; the pity had been spent years ago. "Because a lone clockmaker who accuses the harbor trust of forgery is a lone clockmaker with a grievance and no standing, and grievance is easily filed away—I know how easily. And because until tonight I had nothing but suspicion. Suspicion is smoke, Lockridge. It needs a fire under it or it dissipates." She tapped the notebook. "But copies. Certified copies, witnessed, sealed, entered into registry before the Coriolis takes the originals beyond any subpoena's reach—that is not smoke. That is a record. And a record, once it stands, cannot be unfiled by any man's money. I learned that from them. They taught me exactly how strong a filed document is. I intend to return the lesson."

"They'll destroy you," Elodie said. "Us. They'll—"

"They will try what they tried before, yes. Forged words, filed words." Odile stood, went to the window, and looked out at the harbor, where the masts of the Coriolis were visible even now, a thin black forest against the grey edge of the night, loading by lantern light. "I have considered it. Nine years of considering it. Here is what I concluded: they can take the workshop. They took my father's name once already; mine weighs less than his did. But they cannot forge what is already certified and copied and scattered, and they cannot sail away from a registry entry. The law is their weapon, Lockridge—but the law is also a machine, and machines can be operated by anyone with the nerve to put their hands on the wheels. I have nerve. My hands are very steady."

When she turned back, Elodie was watching her with an expression Odile hadn't seen from her before—wariness giving way, reluctantly, joint by joint, the way rust gives way to oil.

"And what do you want?" the girl asked. "No one does this for free. I know about bargains. I've already been offered one this week that smelled of interest."

Good, Odile thought. Distrust her; distrust everyone; it would keep them all alive long enough to be useful. "What I want is in the record," she said aloud. "Vasse, restored beside Meridian. Eleven names read aloud in the capital instead of buried in an appendix. And the hand that forged us all, named." She extended her own hand across the desk—ink-stained, scarred at the press, steady. "Beyond that? Nothing of yours. Your workshop is your own affair, and your alias, and your mentor's sins, whatever they turn out to be. I deal in documents, not in families. Keep your secrets; just put them in order."

Elodie looked at the offered hand for a long moment. Then, instead of taking it, she pulled the notebook toward herself, tore out the final blank leaf, and laid it on the desk, and uncapped the ink.

"Witness this instead," she said. "Tonight's date, your mark, and what we found here. If we're doing documents, madame, let's do them properly, from the beginning. Everything dated. Everything signed." A ghost of a smile, sharp as a freshly cut tooth. "Everything in order."

Odile Vasse looked at the girl—at the ruined family's last apprentice, copying evidence by lamplight in a rival's shop at the second hour of the morning—and felt something she had not felt in nine years, and had to name it carefully before trusting it.

Allies. The word was dangerous. It weighed exactly as much as the person offering it.

She took the pen, wrote her mark, and dated it to the minute.

It was nearly three when they finished the first fair copy. Odile was reaching for the sealing wax when a knock came at the street door—soft, twice, then twice again, a pattern—and Elodie went white as unbleached linen and said, in a voice gone suddenly young,

"That's Sable's knock. Something's happened at the harbor office. Someone's reported the break-in—the magistrate has my name."

*Odile sets down the pen.*

*The wax is still warm.*
