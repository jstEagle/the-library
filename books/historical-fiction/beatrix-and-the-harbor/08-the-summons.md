## 8. The Summons

The envelope arrived on a Wednesday, folded into the workshop's letter slot sometime between the last stroke of midnight and the first gray suggestion of dawn. Elodie found it when she came down to stoke the forge, lying face-up on the cold flagstones as though it had drifted there like a leaf, and she knew before she touched it that the ordinary rhythm of her days had ended.

It was not the paper that told her, though the paper was fine—heavier stock than any shop in Lockridge Row could afford to buy. It was not the wax seal, crimson and unbroken, stamped with the harbor magistracy's anchor-and-scales. It was the address.

*To Beatrix, clockmaker's apprentice, at the observatory of record.*

Not *to whom it may concern*. Not care of Master Ivo Lockridge, or the workshop on Chandler's Lane where the sign had hung for three generations and the shutters had not been repainted since Elodie was nine. To *Beatrix*—the name she had invented out of thin air four years ago, a whisper of nothing, an alias worn so long and so lightly that she had almost forgotten it was a costume at all.

Someone had written that name down. Someone had carried it to a magistrate's clerk and spoken it aloud in an official room.

Elodie stood in the doorway with the envelope in both hands while the forge ticked and cooled behind her, and outside the window the harbor came awake in its usual order—the first gulls, the first carts, the low bell of the tide-buoy—and none of it, not one wheel or weight or pendulum of the vast machine she loved, had changed at all. Only she had changed, in the space of reading eleven words. She was no longer invisible. She had never been invisible. She had only been unwritten, and now someone was writing.

She broke the seal with her thumbnail, carefully, the way she loosened a rusted screw—no haste, no force—and unfolded the single sheet within.

*MAGISTRACY OF THE HARBOR DISTRICT*

*Beatrix, apprentice of the clockmaking trade, is hereby summoned to appear before Magistrate Corven Hale at the Hall of Records on Thursday next, at the hour of ten in the morning, to answer questions touching upon the mechanism of the observatory master clock, which is alleged to have been altered, tampered with, or otherwise interfered with by hands unauthorized. Failure to appear will be construed as admission.*

*Given under seal, this ninth day of October, 1898.*

*Altered. Tampered with. Interfered with.*

Elodie read the words three times. On the third reading they stopped being ink and became something with teeth. She had repaired the master clock—that was all, that was everything—but repair was the whole of her trade and the whole of her innocence, and yet here it was, dressed in the language of accusation: *alleged to have been altered.* As if a clock could be mended without being opened. As if opening it were itself a crime.

And beneath the fear, colder and quieter, another thought turned over like a gear finding its mesh.

*Ivo's tools had been moved.*

She had noticed it days ago, in the week after she'd pulled the sealed letter from the clock's case and hidden it inside her bodice like a coal she couldn't put down. The master's loupe had been on the wrong hook. His pin-vice lay in the second drawer instead of the first, and Ivo Lockridge was a man whose drawers were arranged with the devotion other men gave to scripture. She had told herself it meant nothing—that grief made hands careless, that age did it, that any number of innocent explanations existed. She had wanted very much to believe one of them.

But now: a summons. Delivered in the dark. Addressed to a name that only three living people should have known how to spell.

* * *

"You look," Sable Ferren said, "like a woman who's swallowed the mainspring."

They stood at the edge of the fish market, where the cobbles ran slick with brine and the morning's catch lay silvered on beds of ice, and Sable was pretending to inspect a crate of haddock with the profound professional interest of a dockworker who had absolutely nowhere else to be. It was their arrangement: never seen speaking twice in the same place, never seen speaking long. Elodie had thought such caution paranoid once. She held the summons against her ribs now, wrapped in oilcloth inside her coat, and understood that paranoia was simply foresight wearing its work clothes.

"Not a woman," Elodie said. "A summons."

Sable's hands went still over the crate. Around them the market roared on—gulls screaming their eternal grievance, a porter swearing at a handcart, the ice-man's bell—and in the middle of all that noise, the two of them had become an island of stillness.

"Corven Hale?" Sable asked, and when Elodie nodded: "That's Vane's man. Magnates' man. He doesn't pull a thread unless the loom tells him to." Sable glanced up then, and Elodie saw something she had never seen in that broad, weathered face: fear, and worse, fear *for her*, which sat differently on Sable Ferren than fear for oneself would have. Sable wore fear for others like a coat two sizes too large, uncomfortable and unaccustomed. "Questioning about the clock. What did you do to the clock?"

"Fixed it. That's the whole of it. Escapement, mainspring, the strike train—it hadn't been serviced in years, it was losing four minutes a day, and an observatory clock that loses time is a scandal all by itself. I did my work properly. Better than properly." Elodie heard her own voice tighten and could not loosen it. "I signed nothing. I left no mark. I went as Beatrix, through the water-door, at hours when no one honest was watching."

"And somebody was watching anyway." Sable picked up a haddock, examined it, set it down—a man performing normalcy badly, because his mind was elsewhere. "Or somebody didn't need to watch. Somebody already knew you'd be there, because somebody sent you there." A pause, delicate as a pallet dropping into its notch. "Who else knows about Beatrix?"

The question landed exactly where Elodie had been trying not to let it land.

"My uncle," she said. "Master Ivo. And the observatory keeper who engaged me—though he thinks Beatrix is simply an apprentice from up the coast, no surname attached. And—" she hesitated, and hated the hesitation, "—and whoever hid that letter in the case knew the family well enough to choose that hiding place. If they also knew about Beatrix..."

"Then Beatrix isn't your shield anymore." Sable said it flatly, the way one states the weather or the state of the tide, because stating terrible things plainly was Sable's way of taking their power away. "Beatrix is a hook. Someone dangled the alias out where the magistrate could bite it, and Hale bit, and now there's a paper trail leading straight to a girl who doesn't officially exist." Sable finally abandoned the pretense of shopping and turned fully toward her, lowering their voice until it was barely above the sound of the ice shifting in its crates. "You can't go alone."

"I have to go alone. It's *my* name on the summons. Absence is confession, remember. And if anyone comes with me—if a dockworker famous for knowing every crate on every quay walks into the Hall of Records beside a nonexistent apprentice—we might as well hang a sign around our necks."

"Elodie—"

"Sable." She said it quietly, and the quiet did what shouting could not; Sable closed their mouth. "I've spent four years being careful. Let me spend one more morning being careful, and afterward we'll know more. Whoever reported the tampering will want something. People always want something. And whatever it is they want, the shape of the wanting will tell us who they are."

Sable looked at her for a long moment. Gulls wheeled overhead; somewhere down the quay a winch began its groaning song. Then Sable reached out—not a handshake, nothing so formal—and gripped Elodie's shoulder once, hard, through the coat, through the oilcloth, through the summons itself, as if to say: *whatever happens in that room, this hand exists, and it is yours.*

"Ten o'clock," Sable said. "Thursday. I'll be at the corner of Rope Walk from nine. You don't have to see me. Just know I'm there."

* * *

Wednesday passed in a fever of small preparations that were really prayers in disguise.

Elodie cleaned the workshop until it gleamed, not because cleanliness would help but because her hands demanded occupation the way lungs demanded air. She oiled every lathe. She squared the drawers Ivo had disarranged—or tried to; some part of the disorder resisted her, as though the tools had been moved back but moved back *wrong*, returned by someone working from memory rather than habit, and every time she noticed this her stomach dropped another inch.

She rehearsed. That was the truth of it. She stood at her bench after Ivo had gone up to bed, lamplit, surrounded by the sleeping brass of half-repaired clocks, and interrogated herself aloud in Magistrate Hale's imagined voice.

*State your name.* — *Beatrix. Only Beatrix. An apprentice travels light, in name as in luggage.*

*How long have you been employed at the observatory?* — *Since spring. Engaged by the keeper, Mr. Aldous Wren, upon recommendation.*

*Recommendation from whom?* — and here the trap yawned, because the honest answer was *from my uncle, who wished the work done under a name that wasn't ours,* and honesty in that room would be indistinguishable from suicide.

She wound answers around the trap like wire around a post. She built half-truths the way she built wheels—each one true at its rim, false at its hub, spinning smoothly enough to pass an inspection conducted at speed. *Recommended through the guild network. My master prefers his apprentices' names kept private; harbor clients can be superstitious about family associations.* Every word technically survivable. Every word a small betrayal of the truth, and she hated each one, and made them anyway, because the alternative was the gallows-adjacent future the magnates had been building for the Lockridge name since before she was born.

Near midnight she heard Ivo's tread on the stair—slow, deliberate, the tread of a man who wanted to be heard coming—and she swept the summons off the bench and into the drawer before the door opened.

He stood in the doorway a moment, candle in hand, and the light made hollows of his face. He had aged years in weeks; the letter's discovery had done something to him that even their ruinous fortunes had not. Or perhaps, Elodie thought now, watching him watch her, it was not the letter's discovery that had aged him. Perhaps it was the letter's *existence*, carried secretly all these years, and the discovery had merely transferred the weight from his shoulders to hers.

"The observatory sent round a note today," Ivo said. "Wren. Asking whether you'd been taken ill. You missed your appointment to regulate the strike train."

Had she? She counted backward through the fog of the last days and found that yes—Tuesday, the strike train, forgotten entirely. "I've been occupied."

"With the letter." Not a question. "Or with what it means." He set the candle down on the bench, and its flame stood perfectly still between them, and in that stillness Elodie watched her mentor—her uncle, her father in everything but signature—struggle with something. When he spoke again his voice was very quiet. "There are questions being asked in certain rooms. About the clock. About who worked on it. If anyone should come asking you things—"

"Then I'll answer them."

"*Carefully,*" Ivo said, with an emphasis that cracked like a snapped pivot. And then, softer, almost pleading: "Elodie. There are ways of surviving inquiries. There are things I did, years ago, that you cannot understand yet, and if you would only trust that they were done out of love—"

"Love," Elodie repeated. The word tasted strange, like a coin bitten and found to be base metal. "Uncle, someone wrote the name Beatrix down. Someone carried it to a magistrate. There are perhaps three people alive who knew that name, and one of them has been moving your tools."

His face did something then—too fast to read, too slow to hide. A flicker, a flinch, the smallest possible recoil, there and gone like a spark from a grinding wheel.

"You've been going through my drawers," Ivo said.

"You've been *rearranging* them."

Silence. The candle flame leaned, recovered, leaned again. Somewhere upstairs a clock struck the three-quarter hour, its voice thin and faithful in the dark house.

"Go to bed," Ivo said at last, wearily, gathering up the candle. "Whatever tomorrow brings, meet it rested."

And it was only after his footsteps had faded above her, after his door had closed with its familiar soft click, that Elodie understood the sentence's full shape. *Whatever tomorrow brings.* Not *if* anyone comes. Not *should.* Tomorrow.

He knew about the summons. He had known before the envelope ever slid through the slot.

* * *

Thursday dawned the color of old pewter, and the Hall of Records rose against it like a headstone for something that had once been beautiful.

Elodie walked there through streets she had known all her life and experienced them as a stranger. The chandler's, where she'd bought tallow as a child. The corner where Odile Vasse's workshop windows threw their cold precise gleam. The fish-gutters calling their prices in voices like gulls. All of it ordinary, all of it continuing, and she moved through the middle of it carrying her heartbeat like contraband.

At the corner of Rope Walk, as promised, a figure leaned against a bollard with the studied idleness of a man waiting for a ship. Elodie did not look directly at Sable Ferren. She allowed herself the knowledge of that presence the way a clock allows itself the knowledge of its weights—unseen, but felt, holding her steady—and she climbed the Hall's steps alone.

Inside, the building smelled of dust and lamp oil and the particular dry rot of paper that has been stored too long by men who do not read it. A clerk with ink-stained cuffs took her name—her *only* name, given evenly, given as if it had always been hers—and led her down a corridor where the gaslights burned behind green glass, turning everything including Elodie's own hands a faint drowned shade.

The room beyond was small and cold. A table. Two chairs. One window, high and barred, admitting a blade of harbor light that lay across the table like something laid deliberately, like evidence.

Magistrate Corven Hale sat behind the table with a file folder open before him, and he did not rise, and he did not offer his hand. He was younger than Elodie expected—fifty perhaps, silvering at the temples, with the neat compressed features of a man who kept himself filed as carefully as his papers. He wore no robe. That frightened her more than a robe would have. Robes were theater; this was meant to feel informal, offhand, the prelude to something. Magnates' men preferred conversations to proceedings. Conversations left no record.

Except, she reminded herself, settling into the offered chair and folding her hands as if they were resting on a workbench—except with this particular clerk, this Ambrose Pell, who believed records were sacred. Except if the right documents reached the registry in time. Except if the tide cooperated.

"Beatrix," Hale said, trying the name the way one tests a coin. "Curious appellation. No family uses it in this district. No guild register lists it. It appears—" he turned a page without looking down at it, a flourish of casual power— "from nowhere, works on the most sensitive timepiece in the harbor, and returns to nowhere. You must forgive my interest in provenance. It's a professional habit. Everything in this city has a history, Miss Beatrix. Even names. *Especially* names invented recently."

"The name was chosen for privacy," Elodie said. Her voice came out level. She was distantly proud of it. "Harbor work attracts superstition. Clients prefer apprentices unencumbered by local association."

"Do they." Hale smiled without warmth, the smile of a man watching an escapement tick and counting its beats. "We'll come back to names. First, the clock." He drew a paper from the folder and laid it flat, rotating it so she could read it upside down, which was itself a message: *I decide what you see and how you see it.* It was a workman's inventory of the observatory mechanism, dated the previous month, itemizing the escapement, the great barrel, the strike train—and beside the entry for the strike train, in a clerk's meticulous hand, a notation:

*Plate bears tool-marks inconsistent with original manufacture. Alteration suspected. Date of alteration unknown.*

"You repaired the master clock," Hale said. It was not a question, so she did not answer it. "Tell me, Beatrix. When you opened that case—when you removed the plates and handled the strike train—did you find anything unexpected inside?"

The room went very still. High above, muffled by stone, the harbor bell rang the half hour, and Elodie felt each stroke pass through her body like a pulse.

Because he was not asking about tool-marks. Tool-marks were the pretext, the legal scaffolding. He was asking about *the letter*. He knew—or believed—there had been something hidden in that clock, and he wanted to know whether she had found it, and whether she still possessed it, and his entire manner—informal, patient, smiling—was the manner of a man fishing in dark water who had just felt the line go taut.

She thought of the letter sewn into the lining of her coat at home, warm as a heartbeat. She thought of forged signatures in shipping ledgers bearing her family's name. She thought of Sable at the corner of Rope Walk, keeping faith without being watched, and of Odile Vasse's cold bright windows, and of Ambrose Pell saying *records become history, and history belongs to whoever files it first.*

"No," said Elodie Lockridge, in the voice of Beatrix, steady as a escapement, "nothing unexpected. Clocks are simple things, Magistrate. They contain only what people put into them—and what people take out."

Hale regarded her across the table for a long, silent moment. The harbor light shifted on the wood between them. Then he closed the folder with a soft, decisive snap, like a case shutting over its mechanism, and said:

"We'll continue this conversation tomorrow. Same hour. Do plan your answers overnight, Miss Beatrix. I find that answers improve with rehearsal—" and here he smiled again, and this time it reached his eyes, which were the pale flat gray of a winter harbor, "—though rarely enough."

She was shown out through the green-lit corridor, past the ink-stained clerk, down the steps into the pewter morning, where at the corner of Rope Walk a figure pushed off a bollard and fell into step beside her without a word.

They walked three streets before either of them spoke, and then it was Elodie, staring straight ahead, her breath coming short and fast as a runaway train of gears.

"He's not investigating the clock," she said. "Sable. He's hunting the letter. And he gave me one night to decide what I'm going to tell him I did with it."

She stopped at the mouth of Chandler's Lane, where the workshop's darkened windows waited like held breath, and turned to Sable with a clarity that felt almost like calm, the way the moment before a mechanism engages feels almost like stillness.

"Tomorrow night," she said, "we're going to need a printer."
