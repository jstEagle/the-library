## 9. Questions in a Cold Room

The summons came at half past seven in the morning, folded into the hands of a boy who smelled of tar and did not meet Elodie's eyes.

*Beatrix, clockmaker, to appear before Magistrate Corven at ten o'clock, Harbor Offices, regarding the observatory clock. Come alone.*

Elodie read it three times by the light of the workshop's single window, the paper trembling not from any wind but from the small earthquake happening somewhere behind the sternum. The words did not change on the second or third reading, though Elodie wished they would. *Regarding the observatory clock.* As if the clock were a witness that had complained. As if the mechanism itself had turned informer.

Ivo was not yet downstairs. The forge was cold; the lathes slept under their oilcloths like patient animals. Elodie stood in the grey half-light of the shop where four generations of Lockridges had cut and filed and measured, and understood with sudden, terrible clarity that the alias had stopped protecting. A name invented for safety becomes, in time, a second self—and second selves can be arrested just as thoroughly as first ones.

There was no question of not going. *Come alone* meant that not coming alone would be noticed, and being noticed was the one thing Beatrix had never allowed. But beneath the fear, another feeling was already assembling itself, gear by gear, tooth by tooth: the cold, familiar calm of a difficult repair. When a movement refuses to run, you do not shake it. You do not force the mainspring. You open the case slowly, and you listen.

Elodie dressed carefully. Not too fine—the harbor knew Beatrix as a working tradesperson with ink-stained cuffs and a habit of keeping the left sleeve rolled. Not too shabby either, because Corven would be reading clothes the way a jeweler reads hallmarks. The coat with the mended elbow. The boots resoled twice. Everything chosen to say: *unremarkable, honest, of no interest.* A costume of invisibility, worn so long it had become skin.

At five minutes to ten, Elodie climbed the stone steps of the Harbor Offices, where the salt air had pitted the balustrade into something resembling coral, and gave the name *Beatrix* to a clerk whose pen scratched it into the day-book without ceremony—one more small transaction in a building built of small transactions.

The corridor beyond was long and poorly lit, and the sound of Elodie's boots came back off the walls a half-beat late, as if someone were following at a careful distance.

* * *

The room was cold in a way that had been arranged.

Not merely unheated—arranged. It sat on the north side of the building where no sun ever reached, and its single window had been opened an inch despite the October damp, so that the air carried the smell of the harbor at low tide: weed, brine, the sourness of exposed pilings. A table of scarred oak. Two chairs, placed so that whoever sat in the further one faced the window's grey light while whoever sat nearer the door sat in shadow. Elodie noted this the way one notes the layout of an escapement—automatically, professionally, because arrangement is never accidental in machines or in rooms.

Magistrate Corven was already seated when the door closed behind Elodie. He was smaller than his reputation, a compact man of perhaps fifty-five with dry, precise hands and the kind of face that had been handsome in a severe way before decades of disapproval had weathered it into something like driftwood. He wore no robes. That, too, was a message: this was not yet a trial, his lack of ceremony said, but it might become one, and he wanted Elodie to do the arithmetic of that possibility without help.

"Beatrix," he said, tasting the name. "Sit."

Elodie sat in the chair near the door, which meant accepting the shadow, which meant refusing to be seen clearly, which was either surrender or strategy depending on how one chose to spend it. Elodie chose to spend it as strategy.

"You repaired the observatory's master clock," Corven began, "in the last week of September."

"I did." Keep answers short. Short answers leave fewer edges to catch on.

"On whose authority?"

"The Warden of the Observatory wrote to the Lockridge workshop requesting a clockmaker. Master Ivo Lockridge was engaged elsewhere. He sent me."

"With what qualifications?"

"Seven years' apprenticeship. Competency in escapements, remontoire, and strike work. The Warden saw my master's letter of introduction and was satisfied." All true. All beside the point, and Elodie knew they were both aware it was beside the point, which made the exchange feel like two clocks ticking at each other across the table, each waiting for the other to lose time.

Corven opened a folder—actually opened it, deliberately, letting Elodie watch him handle whatever papers it contained without showing them. "Describe the fault."

"A worn pallet arbor. The pivot had scored its bearing, and the recoil was irregular. The clock was losing nearly four minutes a day. The astronomers require better than that or the whole harbor's chronometry drifts."

"And you replaced the arbor."

"Cut a new one, polished the pivots, rebushed the bearing. Cleaned and oiled the train while the movement was down." Elodie heard the steadiness of the own voice with a kind of distant pride. It sounded like a person discussing gear trains, because that was what it was pretending to be.

"And in the course of this cleaning," Corven said, and here he let the pause stretch until Elodie could hear the water moving in the harbor below the window, "did you find anything unusual?"

There it was. The whole interview turned on that question the way a whole movement turns on the escape wheel—everything upstream of it stored energy, everything downstream dependent on how it was released.

Elodie had rehearsed this in the walk along the quay, measuring out possible answers the way one measures a spring, coiling each one and testing how it would unwind. The truth—that a sealed letter naming the Lockridge family had been hidden in the case for years—was a spring under full tension; released carelessly, it would destroy everything it touched, beginning with Elodie. A flat denial—*nothing unusual*—was brittle; if Corven held even one corroborating fact, denial would snap and take credibility with it.

So Elodie gave him the half-truth, machined to fit.

"The case had been opened before," Elodie said. "There were old tool marks on the interior panelling—someone had removed the back board years ago and refitted it. The joints had been re-cemented with the wrong hide glue. Sloppy work. I noted it because sloppy work offends me, not because it seemed significant. Clock cases get opened all the time. People hide keys, letters, gambling debts. Superstition mostly. I re-cemented the joint properly and thought no more about it."

"Did you tell anyone? The Warden? Your master?"

"No. It wasn't worth anyone's time." A pause, then, deliberately, the smallest offering: "Though I confess I wondered about the workmanship. Whoever did it knew joinery but not clockmaking. They went through the case rather than the movement. A clockmaker hides things inside movements. There's far more room, and no one suspects a going train."

Why give him that? Because a half-truth with a professional flourish in it reads as whole; because volunteering expertise makes a witness look like an enthusiast rather than a conspirator; because Corven would discover the case had been opened whether Elodie admitted it or not, and it was better that he hear it framed as a craftsman's idle grievance than dig it out himself and imagine it a secret worth killing over.

Corven's dry hands folded together on the folder. "You know a great deal about hiding things in clocks, Beatrix."

"I know a great deal about clocks, sir. Hiding things is only a use people put them to."

For the first time, something moved behind the driftwood face—not warmth, nothing so generous, but the flicker of a man recalibrating an instrument. He drew a paper from the folder and slid it across the table. It was a list, written in a clerk's hand, of names. Elodie read them upside down, which took no effort; years of reading engraved serial numbers backward across a bench had trained the eyes for inversion.

Harrow & Sons. Vasse. Coriolis Shipping Concern. Pell. Ferren. And there, fourth from the bottom, in the same ink as all the rest but somehow heavier, as though the clerk had pressed harder: **Lockridge**.

The room did not change temperature, but Elodie felt it grow colder anyway, the way metal feels colder than wood at the same degree because it drinks the heat out of you faster.

"Do you know these names?" Corven asked.

Carefully. Escapement-slowly. One tooth at a time.

"They're harbor names. Anyone who works the quays knows them. Harrow ships grain. Vasse is a clockmaker—I've competed with her for repair contracts since I was licensed. Pell keeps the registry. Ferren works the east stairs. Lockridge—" and here Elodie's voice did exactly what it had been trained to do, which was nothing at all, "—is the workshop that employs me."

"You've been with them long?"

"Seven years."

"Long enough to know them well."

"Well enough to file their gear teeth and stay out of their arguments."

"And what arguments are those?"

"Workshop arguments. Money, mostly. A failing business argues about money the way a sick engine knocks—you can't stop it, you can only hope it holds together till payday." Let him hear precarity. Precarity is pitiable, and pity makes men careless interrogators; suspicion sharpens them. Better to be a poor relation of the name than its heir apparent.

Corven studied Elodie for a long moment, and in that study Elodie became aware of a terrible new fact assembling itself out of the morning's details: the summons had come at half past seven, but someone must have written it the night before. Someone had reported the tampering—or the finding—or both—to the magistrate within hours. Only three people had known the case had been disturbed: the Warden, who was old and incurious; Sable, who would sooner have swallowed the anchor; and—

No. Elodie put the thought down gently, the way one sets down a part that might still be needed. Not now. Now there was a man across the table with dry hands and a folder full of names, and the name at the bottom of the list was pressing against the paper like a hand under a sheet.

"Tell me what you know of the harbor affair," Corven said. "Nine years ago. The Coriolis manifests. The bonded warehouses."

"Nothing, sir. I came to the harbor eight years ago, and clockmakers don't sit at the tables where those things are discussed. We're summoned when something stops turning and dismissed when it starts again. If you want the affair, ask the shipping concern. Ask the registry." *Ask Ambrose Pell*, said the voice behind the voice, and Elodie prayed the prayer did not show.

"The registry," Corven repeated softly. "Yes. Everyone says that. Everyone points at everyone else's ledgers." He closed the folder. The sound it made was very small and very final, like a click at the end of a winding. "You'll remain available, Beatrix. The inquiry may have questions about the observatory clock. Its provenance. Its... previous maintenance."

"Its history is in your registry, sir. Every service in nine years is signed in the observatory's log."

Something crossed the magistrate's face then—quick, quickly mastered, but Elodie caught it, because catching such things is the trade. Not surprise. Confirmation. He had known about the log already. Perhaps he had read it. Perhaps he had noticed, as Elodie had noticed only last night lying sleepless, that the entries for the years before the affair were written in a hand that changed character precisely once—in 1889, the year the conspiracy was said to have begun—and that every signature after that change, though spelled identically, sat differently on the line.

*I. Lockridge*, all of them said. But ink has fingerprints, and Elodie had spent a lifetime learning to read them.

He suspected the name. Not Beatrix—Beatrix was weather to him, a detail. He suspected *Lockridge*, had suspected it perhaps for months, and had summoned the observatory's clockmaker hoping the clockmaker would lead him up the garden path to the family that had, rightly or wrongly, been planted at the bottom of his list like a bulb someone wanted grown.

"You may go," Corven said. Then, as Elodie rose: "One thing more. The Lockridge workshop. Is it true the premises are mortgaged?"

"Yes, sir. To Harrow. Since 1893."

"Thank you. You've been most precise."

Precision, Elodie thought, walking back down the long corridor with its echoing footsteps, is what a good movement and a good liar have in common: everything meshes, nothing catches, and the truth of the thing is entirely in the adjustment.

* * *

Outside, the harbor was doing its ordinary brilliant violence—gulls screaming over the fish stalls, winches groaning, the tide climbing the green-slicked steps. Elodie walked past it all wearing the face of a clockmaker who had spent an uneventful hour discussing arbors, and let the crowd fold around and conceal the shaking that had started in the hands and would not stop.

It was not fear of Corven, or not only. It was the arithmetic he had forced. *Is it true the premises are mortgaged? To Harrow. Since 1893.* Two years before the letter was sealed in the clock. Which meant Harrow had held a lien on the Lockridge workshop since before the frame-up hardened—which meant the magnates had never needed the Lockridges guilty. They had needed only the name, and a debt to collect against it. The workshop was not at risk because of the inquiry.

The workshop was collateral that had been posted years ago, by enemies, without signature.

Elodie stopped at the mouth of Tanner's Lane, where the shadow of the observatory dome fell across the cobbles like the gnomon of some vast dial, and understood that the day had shifted underneath everything. There was no longer a question of whether to go quietly. There was only the question Corven's cold room had asked without asking: *when the account is finally written, will your name appear in it as author, or as exhibit?*

Down at the east stairs, a familiar figure in a dockworker's jersey was coiling rope, and looked up as Elodie passed. Sable's eyes asked the question before her mouth could.

"The magistrate knows the name," Elodie said quietly, not stopping. "All of it. We have less time than we thought."

Sable dropped the rope where she stood.
