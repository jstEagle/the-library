## 12. Sable's Price

The enforcers came for Sable Ferren at the change of tide, when the quay was thinnest of witnesses and the fog had settled in thick as wool over the water. Sable had been expecting them for two days—ever since the night at the harbor office, ever since the ledgers were copied—and there was a strange comfort in the waiting ending. Fear, Sable had long ago learned, was a rope that went slack when you finally took its weight in your hands.

They were three of them, big men in oilskins with their collars turned up, and they did not bother with introductions. The biggest one, whose face Sable knew from the magnates' piers—a man called Dree with knuckles like mooring bollards—simply said, "Walk with us," and the tone made it clear that walking was the generous version of what might happen instead.

Sable set down the crate being loaded. "I'm on the clock."

"Not anymore." Dree smiled without warmth. "You're off the clock. You're off every clock."

*That,* Sable thought, letting the first blow land on the shoulder rather than the face because turning away invited worse, *is almost poetry.* The second blow caught the ribs and the third found the kidney, and after that the poetry stopped and there was only the cold cobbles coming up fast, and the taste of iron and harbor brine, and the peculiar intimacy of boots—knowing each boot by its weight before it arrived.

They were professionals. They did not go for the eyes or the teeth, nothing that would show in daylight among honest dockworkers and invite questions from the kind of people who still believed the harbor belonged to everyone. They worked the body, where bruises bloomed under cloth like ink spilled inside a sealed letter. A message written to be read by one person only.

"Tell the clockmaker's whelp," Dree said, crouching close so the words could find Sable's ear through the ringing, "that curiosity sinks faster than ships. Tell them the next copyist we catch won't be able to hold a pen. Or a spoon."

Sable spat blood onto the stones between them, which was not brave so much as it was all that remained in the mouth to give. "I'll tell them," Sable managed. "Word for word. Including the part where three grown men needed a full minute to handle one stevedore."

Dree laughed, genuinely, and that was somehow the worst of it—the laugh of a man doing a job he found pleasant. Then he stood, and the boots stopped, and the fog closed over the three of them like water over a dropped stone.

Sable lay on the quay for a while longer than necessary, taking inventory. Ribs: two cracked, probably, maybe three, on the left side where the loading hook scar ran. Wrist wrenched but not broken. Face mostly spared. Everything else: bruised so thoroughly that the body felt less like a body than a ledger of debts, entry upon entry in fading ink.

The tide turned. Somewhere out in the gray, the Coriolis's bell rang the hour, indifferent and precise.

"Right," Sable said to no one, and began the long business of standing up.

* * *

Elodie Lockridge opened the door of the workshop at the second knock and went white as unbleached calico.

"What happened to—" Elodie stopped, because the answer was obvious, and because saying it aloud would make it true. "Come in. Sit. Don't sit—stand, I need to—"

"You need to breathe first," Sable said, leaning in the doorframe. "Then fetch whatever passes for spirits in a clockmaker's house. I'm told clockmakers keep good spirits. All that patience."

It was meant as a joke and landed as a croak, and then Sable's legs announced that the walk from the quay had spent the last of something, and the room tilted gently sideways until Elodie's hands—small, precise, astonishingly strong hands—caught Sable under the arms and steered everything toward a chair near the stove.

"I'm fine," Sable said.

"You are visibly not fine."

"I'm *structurally* fine. Load-bearing walls intact."

Elodie made a sound that was half laugh, half something else entirely, and then became all business, and Sable watched with a kind of dazed admiration as the apprentice transformed. Shirt off—it hurt more going over the head than the beating itself had—and then warm water, clean cloth, a bottle of something amber and vicious that Elodie said was for cleaning movement plates and would do for flesh well enough.

The lamplight caught Elodie's face as the apprentice bent close, working the cloth along the worst of the bruising with the same absorbed attention given, presumably, to escapements. Sable had seen this look before, at the observatory, at odd hours, but never aimed so directly, never aimed at *Sable*, and there was something in being looked at that way—like a mechanism worth understanding down to its smallest wheel—that made the ribs ache for reasons that had nothing to do with boots.

"Dree," Sable said, when the breathing allowed speech. "And two others. Magnates' men, regulars from the east piers. They wanted me to carry a message."

Elodie's hands went still against Sable's side. In the silence Sable could hear the workshop's clocks ticking, a dozen small hearts keeping different times, and beneath them Elodie's breathing gone shallow and quick.

"The message is for you," Sable said. "Not me. I'm just the paper it's written on."

"Don't."

"It said: tell the clockmaker's whelp that curiosity sinks faster than ships. It said the next copyist won't hold a pen again. It said—" Sable hesitated, then gave it whole, because a warning diluted was a warning wasted. "It implied you'd been identified. That Beatrix is known."

Elodie stepped back, and the lamplight showed something terrible happening in that careful face: the slow collapse of an architecture. The alias had been a wall, and Sable could see the wall coming down brick by brick behind Elodie's eyes.

"They know who I am," Elodie said quietly. Not a question.

"They know someone copied ledgers. Whether they've matched the hand to the name yet, I couldn't say. But they're close enough to spend money on warnings, and men like that don't spend money early."

Elodie turned away, and for a long moment stood among the clocks with their backs straight and their shoulders very still, the way people stand when standing still is the only thing holding them together. Sable knew that posture. Had worn it often enough. Dockworkers learned it young: grief and terror both get carried upright, because sitting down on a quay means drowning eventually.

"I brought this on you," Elodie said at last.

"That's the stupidest thing I've heard since Dree tried to tell me the tide schedule was his personal invention."

"Elodie—"

"No. Listen to me." And here Elodie turned around, and Sable saw that the collapse had finished and something else had been built in its place—something harder-edged and lit from within, like a forge seen through a shuttered window. "You didn't ask to be beaten. I asked you to help me, yes. Two nights ago I asked, and you said yes, freely, and I have been sick with knowing what that yes might cost ever since. But *brought it on you*? No. The men who own the piers brought it on you. The men who forge dead families' names onto cargo manifests brought it on you. The men who hired a historian to write the harbor's story with the dockworkers scraped off it like barnacles—they brought it on you. You're not the cause of anything. You're the only person in this whole rotten port acting like a free citizen."

Sable blinked. The speech had cost Elodie something; the apprentice was breathing hard, and one hand had curled into a fist against a thigh.

"Well," Sable said, when the moment had aged enough. "Remind me never to argue with you when you've got a cloth in one hand and cleaning spirits in the other."

"Bind your ribs," Elodie said, tossing the roll of linen across. "Tightly. And drink water. And then listen, because I've done arithmetic while you sat there bleeding, and the sum has changed everything."

* * *

The binding hurt, and the water helped, and the stove ticked as it cooled, and outside the fog had thickened until the window showed nothing but gray and the yellow smudge of the harbor lamp. Sable wrapped the linen methodically, watching Elodie move around the workshop collecting things: paper, the copied ledger pages folded into a leather wallet, a jeweler's loupe on its chain, the sealed letter with the Lockridge name on it, handled like a live coal.

"Odile Vasse came to me," Elodie said. "After the copying. She caught me at it, actually—watched me the whole time and let me finish, which should have frightened me more than it did."

"She let you finish?"

"She's got her own account to settle with the magnates. Her father's workshop, the patents, the way they strangled her trade licenses. She'll tell it herself if she chooses; it's not mine to spend." Elodie laid the papers out on the bench in an order that clearly meant something. "What matters is this: she has presses. She can print certified copies of everything—the letter, the forged signatures, the manifest pages. Enough copies that burning any one of them accomplishes nothing."

"And the cost?"

"There's always a cost." Elodie's mouth twisted. "My family's workshop, held as collateral. If the record fails—if the inquiry buries us—the Lockridges lose everything anyway, but this way Odile gets something out of the wreck. At least that's how she framed it. I think there's more loyalty in her than she wants anyone counting."

Sable tested the binding with a breath. Pain, but bearable pain, pain with edges rather than depth. "So the plan is real now. Copies, certification, Pell's registry, before the Coriolis sails."

"On the morning tide. Three days."

"Three days." Sable looked at the fogged window, toward the harbor that could not be seen but could be heard—the groan of hulls, the slap of water, the far bell of the Coriolis marking time like everything else in this city marked time, faithfully, for whoever owned the hour. "Then hear me, Elodie Lockridge, and hear the price I paid tonight, because it wasn't the ribs. The ribs are rent. The price is this: they know now that someone stands beside you. Which means whatever comes next, they'll aim at the people, not just the papers. They beat me to prove that helping you has a tariff."

"I know." Elodie's voice had gone very quiet. "I've been standing behind other people's courage since I first put the name Beatrix on. Behind yours. Behind Pell's office. Behind Odile's presses. Every step of the way I told myself invisibility was safety, and tonight a man with bollard fists wrote the correction to that theory into your back." The fist uncurled, flattened against the bench. "No more. If they come again, they come for me. I file the record myself, under my own name, and let them learn what a Lockridge signature looks like when it's true."

Something moved in Sable's chest that the ribs had no part in. "You'd sign it? Your name? Knowing what they do to names?"

"My name is the only thing they haven't managed to steal yet." Elodie smiled, and it was not a gentle smile. "I intend to spend it."

Outside, deep in the fog, the Coriolis's crew could be heard hauling at some late cargo, voices carrying flat and strange across the water. Three days. The morning tide waited like a held breath.

"Then we'd best not waste the evening," Sable said, reaching for a shirt. "Because if I know anything about magnates' men, Dree doesn't consider tonight a message delivered. He considers it a deposit. And tomorrow they'll be watching the clerk's office, the quay, and every door in this district." Sable pulled the shirt down over the binding, wincing, and met Elodie's eyes across the lamplight. "Which means we move tonight. Fetch Odile. Wake her presses."

Elodie was already pulling a coat from its hook.

But at the door, pausing with a hand on the latch, Elodie said, quietly, "Thank you. For taking the message and bringing it to me anyway. For standing."

"Stand somewhere useful," Sable said. "The quay. Before the tide. That's where I'll be."

And together they stepped out into the fog, toward the lights of Vasse's workshop burning late across the harbor—though whether those lights meant an ally already at work, or trouble that had gotten there first, neither of them could yet say.
