## 13. The Escapement

The observatory at night was a different instrument entirely.

By day it hummed with clerks and astronomers, the scrape of chalk on slate, the bellows-voice of the great refractor being slewed by hand. But after midnight it emptied, and what remained was the building's true conversation: timber settling in the salt air, wind worrying the copper dome, and beneath it all, the heartbeat—the master clock, ticking in its case like something patient and buried alive.

Elodie worked by lamplight with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, hands black to the second knuckle with lamp-black and old grease, hair pinned up so carelessly that strands had long since escaped to cling to her neck. She had been at it since dusk. The escapement lay disassembled before her on a cloth of white flannel, each part ranked in order of removal: pallets, escape wheel, crutch, anchor. Eleven pieces. She could have named them blind, could have drawn their shapes in the dark, because she had grown up falling asleep to drawings of them—her grandmother's hand, her mother's hand, her own childish copies in the margins of ledgers that were now, possibly, evidence.

*The clock itself becomes a witness.* Odile had said it as a joke, half a joke, the way a blade is half a tool and half a wound. But standing here with the movement opened like a chest for surgery, Elodie felt the truth of it settle coldly into her stomach. A clock recorded nothing and everything. It kept faith with every second it was given. If someone had hidden a letter in its case—and someone had; she had held that letter, had read her family's name written on it in a hand she did not know—then the clock had kept that secret the way it kept time. Perfectly. Without opinion.

She picked up the escape wheel and turned it under the lamp.

It was wrong.

Not broken—nothing so obvious. Wrong the way a portrait is wrong when the eyes have been repainted by a lesser hand. The teeth were true, the wheel concentric, the crossings elegant. But the *arbor*—the steel spindle at the wheel's heart—was too new. Every other component in this movement carried decades of handling, a patina like the inside of an old violin. The arbor shone. Someone had replaced it, or re-turned it, within the last ten years, and had done the work well enough to fool a casual eye.

But Elodie Lockridge was not a casual eye. She had spent nine years learning to see the difference between a thing that was whole and a thing that had been made to look whole. Her entire life was that difference.

"Hello," she murmured to the wheel, softly, the way one greets a nervous animal. "What are you carrying?"

* * *

She should have gone straight to Sable. That would have been the sensible thing—the agreed thing. They had made promises to each other on the quay outside the harbor office, breath fogging in the dark after the ledgers: *no more alone, Elodie. Whatever we find next, we find together.* And she meant to keep it. She truly meant to keep it.

But the arbor came out of the plate with a whisper, not a fight, and once it was free she saw the end of it was threaded—finely, beautifully threaded, a watchmaker's thread, the kind of work you only learn in Geneva or in a house that had trained someone who had trained in Geneva—and the hollow revealed inside it ran down through the center of the wheel like a well shaft, and her heart was already hammering before her fingers had decided anything at all.

Her hands, traitorous and faithful, went to work.

She told herself it was caution. The magistrate's summons hung over her like weather; anyone who found her here with the clock dismantled needed a defensible story, and a story required the repair to be real, the parts laid out in honest order. That was true. It was also true that some part of her had suspected since the letter that the first hiding place was not the only one. People who hide things well hide them twice. Ivo had taught her that—not in words, never in words, but in the way he'd kept duplicate tools, duplicate keys, a duplicate ledger with innocuous entries that existed solely so that an inspector would find *a* ledger and stop looking.

Ivo. Whose denial had been too smooth, whose tools had moved on the bench like animals scenting a storm.

The threaded cap at the wheel's hub resisted, then gave with a click she felt in her teeth. Inside the hollow arbor, packed tight as a seed in its husk, was a roll of paper no thicker than her little finger.

She did not touch it for a full ten seconds. She stood there listening to the dead clock—she had stopped it to work, and the silence of the observatory without its heartbeat was enormous, a held breath the size of the harbor—and she understood, with terrible clarity, that whatever happened next began now. Once she drew the paper out, there would be no version of events in which she was only Beatrix, only the quiet apprentice from the workshop with the fading sign, only a girl good with gears who asked no questions. The paper knew her name. Everything in this harbor lately seemed to know her name.

"Alone," she said aloud to the empty room, mocking herself gently, and then she took the long brass tweezers and drew the roll out into the light.

* * *

It was not a letter this time. It was smaller—a single sheet folded to the width of a pencil, and older, the paper gone amber and brittle at the creases. She unrolled it with the tweezers on the white flannel, weighting the corners with four brass nuts from her tray, and bent close until the lamp threw her own shadow across it and she had to shift aside to see.

A signature.

One signature, alone on the page, in brown-black iron-gall ink gone the color of dried blood. Below it, a date. Nine years past. And above it, a fragment of heading torn away so that only the last words remained: *—ment of Cargo Received, Quay Three.*

*E. Lockridge.*

Her own initial. Her family name. Written in a flowing, confident commercial hand with the elaborate crossed capital that her grandmother had used on every bill of sale, every warranty, every certificate that had ever left the workshop—except that her grandmother had been dead two years when this page was dated.

Elodie did not realize she had stopped breathing until her body overrode her and dragged in air.

This was the original. This was the source from which all the forged entries in the harbor office ledgers had been copied—she was certain of it, certain the way she was certain about an escapement's beat. The forger had practiced here. Had cut this signature from its document, rolled it, and sealed it inside the very spine of the observatory's clock, where no constable would ever think to look, where time itself stood guard over it. Whoever had done this had not merely framed the Lockridges. They had *archived* the frame. Preserved it like a reliquary preserves a saint's finger bone.

Why? The question turned slowly in her mind, catching light.

Because a forgery can be denied. A copy can be dismissed as a copy. But the original, cut and kept, was leverage—it was proof against the forger's own confederates, insurance in a conspiracy among thieves. Someone had held onto it the way a moneylender holds a promissory note. And someone else—her mind went, unwillingly, to Ivo's face, to his too-smooth denial, to his tools that moved on the bench—someone else had found it, or known of it, and chosen to leave it sleeping in the clock rather than destroy it.

*I hid the letter to protect the family,* he had said.

He had hidden the letter. He had said nothing of a signature. And Elodie remembered now, with the particular horror of a thing seen sideways rather than straight, the way his eyes had gone to the clock case behind her when she'd told him what she'd found—as if checking whether she had found *all* of it.

* * *

She sat back on her stool and let the observatory be silent around her for a long moment. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above, wind moved through the dome's shutter slit and made a sound like slow breathing.

Then, because grief and terror are poor craftsmen but she was a good one, she went back to work.

That was the mercy of the trade, the thing that had saved her a hundred times since childhood: hands know how to proceed when the heart wants only to stop. She cleaned each piece of the escapement in turn, oiled the pivots with one drop at each, seated the escape wheel with its hollow, guilty arbor—but with a plain replacement arbor from her own kit, the original wrapped now in wax paper and tucked into the inner pocket of her waistcoat, flat against her ribs where she could feel it with every breath. Let the clock keep honest time with honest steel. The false heart would travel with her.

By three in the morning the movement was whole again. She raised the weights, set the pendulum swinging, and watched the escapement take up its ancient rhythm—tick, and tock, and tick—with a precision that made her eyes sting. There. Eleven days of labor ended, and the master clock of the harbor observatory would keep time better than it had in twenty years, and no one would ever know what it had been carrying in its chest, and no one would ever know who had drawn it out.

Except that they would. Someone had reported her tampering once already; the summons proved it. Someone watched this place, or watched *her*, and tomorrow or the day after, questions would come again—harder ones, in colder rooms. And she would have to decide, in some window between now and the morning tide, whether the signature in her waistcoat went to Ambrose Pell's registry, or into the harbor deep, or into the fire along with every hope she had of walking away from this with her name intact.

Her name. She looked at it on the torn page under the lamp—*E. Lockridge*, in her grandmother's stolen hand—and felt the strange doubling of it, the way she had felt it since taking the alias *Beatrix*. Two names, neither of them safe. The forged one that bound her family to a crime. The assumed one that kept her invisible while the forged one did its work.

And beneath both, the third one, the one she had barely dared use aloud for years: *Elodie.* Waiting.

She closed the clock case and latched it. She cleaned her tools and rolled the flannel around them. She trimmed the lamp low. And as she gathered her coat, her eye fell one last time on the ledger clipped to the observatory's notice board—the repair ledger, open to the current page, where every craftsman who touched the master clock was required to enter the work performed and sign it.

Beatrix, she would write, as she always had. Beatrix, of the Lockridge workshop. The alias that had protected her right up until the moment it became a noose.

She took out the wax-paper packet and pressed it flat against her chest through the cloth, feeling the small hard geometry of the folded page within, the ghost of her grandmother's flourish, the weight of nine years of lies stacked like coins.

Then she blew out the lamp, and in the dark the clock ticked on beside her—faithful, repaired, and emptied of its secret at last—and Elodie slipped out into the wet night, toward the harbor, toward Sable, toward the tide that was coming whether she was ready or not.
