## 14. Odile's Bargain

Odile Vasse had learned long ago that the truth of a person could be read in their hands.

Her father taught her that, back when his hands were still steady enough to hold a jeweler's loupe without trembling and his workshop still bore the family name above the door instead of a receiver's notice. *Watch the hands,* he would say, turning a customer's pocket watch beneath the gaslight while Odile sat cross-legged on the counter, ten years old and hungry for the secrets inside small machines. *The mouth will tell you what it wants you to hear. The hands will tell you what they cannot afford to say.*

She thought of this now, watching Elodie Lockridge across the scarred oak of her workbench, because those hands were telling her everything the quiet voice would not. They rested on the bench's edge—long-fingered, ink-stained at the cuticle, nicked with the small pale scars of a life spent among brass edges and sprung mainsprings—and they were not still. The right thumb traced the grain of the wood, over and over, a circuit as regular as an escapement. Counting. Measuring. Buying time by the second, the way Odile herself had once bought bread by the farthing when there was no more credit to be had anywhere on Vasse Street.

The lamp between them guttered and steadied. Rain worked at the windows of the Vasse workshop, a fine persistent hiss, and beyond the glass the harbor lay invisible except for its lights—a scatter of lanterns down the quay, the twin beacons of the mole, and somewhere out in all that black water, riding at anchor, the dark shape of the *Coriolis*.

Two days now until the morning tide. Two days until the evidence ship sailed for the capital with the magnates' version of history lashed in oilcloth below its waterline.

"You've come to me," Odile said, "because you have nowhere else to go."

It was not a kindness, the way she said it. It was not cruelty either. It was simply the shape of the thing, stated plainly, because she had found that plainness disarmed people faster than warmth ever had. Warmth made them calculate. Plainness made them listen.

"I came," Elodie said, "because you caught me copying stolen ledgers and did not call the watch."

"And we've established what that cost me. Or might." Odile leaned back in her chair, which creaked like ship's rigging. Her workshop was smaller than the Lockridge establishment—not the grand corner premises near the Customs House with its gilded sign and plate-glass windows, no; hers was two rooms above a rope-walk on the wrong side of the harbor, where the smell of tar worked itself into everything and the ceiling beams were low enough that a tall man stooped. But every surface was hers. Every tool hung in its place according to a system no one else could read. She had built this small kingdom from nothing after her father's bankruptcy, and she guarded it the way other people guarded children. "The magnates know I have a grievance. If they learn how deep my grievance runs, they will not send enforcers next time. They will send magistrates."

Elodie's thumb stopped its circuit. "Then why am I here?"

"Because," Odile said, "I want something the magnates have, and you are the only person in this harbor who can get it for me."

She rose, and crossed to the back room, and let Elodie follow, because some things had to be seen to be believed.

* * *

The press room had been her father's pride, before. Even bankrupted, even stripped of the retail shop and the client list and the house on Chandler's Row, he had refused to surrender the presses—the two flatbed iron monsters, salvaged from a printing concern he'd held shares in during the one prosperous year of his life, hauled here piece by piece on a handcart through a February of sleet. He said a Vasse should always be able to make many things from one thing. He died before he proved whether that philosophy could feed anyone, but the presses remained, oiled and wrapped in canvas, occupying most of the back room like sleeping livestock.

Odile pulled the canvas from the larger of the two.

"They print," she said. "They print well. I keep them in working order the way you'd keep a clock in working order—not because there is demand, but because the day the mechanism fails is the day you need it." She ran her palm along the cold iron of the platen, an old affectionate gesture. "Paper, type, ink—I have all of it. And I have something else the Lockridge workshop does not have, though your family would never deign to admit such a thing has value."

"What?"

"A commission." Odile turned. In the lamplight her face was all angles and patience. "Notary Marrec. An old client of my father's who owes me a debt he'd rather pay than remember. He can certify copies. He can witness signatures. He can take a printed page and make it into a *record*, the kind the clerk of registry is bound by statute to accept, the kind that survives an inquiry, the kind the historians cannot quietly lose in a drawer." She let that sit a moment. "You have ledgers. You have testimony. You may even have whatever it is you've been so carefully not mentioning since you walked through my door—an hour ago, shivering, smelling of rain and fear. What you do not have is the means to make any of it *official*. Truth without form is just rumor with better manners."

Elodie looked at the press for a long moment. Odile watched the calculation move behind those eyes—she had expected reluctance, argument, some scruple about the ethics of demanding payment—but instead she saw something rarer: a craftsman assessing another craftsman's work. The girl was checking the platen's alignment by eye, noting the condition of the screw threads, the tautness of the canvas wrapping, the absence of rust. Approving.

"It's true," Elodie said at last. "I can't do this alone. Pell explained it—records become history only if someone places them, properly, in the machinery that keeps them. And the machinery requires—" A breath. "Certification. Witnesses. Copies distributed beyond the reach of any single fire or theft. I know. I've known since the clerk's office. What I don't have is the means."

"No," Odile agreed. "You have a name. A good name, an old name, a name currently being fitted for a noose by men who never earned theirs. Names are worth a great deal in this harbor." She smiled, thin as a feeler gauge. "Which brings us to price."

There it was. Odile saw Elodie's shoulders set before the word was even finished—the flinch of a person who had known the kindness was too large to be free but had hoped, just this once, against all sense, to be wrong about the world.

"Name it," Elodie said.

"The Lockridge workshop."

The rain filled the silence for three full seconds. Somewhere below, on the rope-walk, a workman's boots splashed past.

"That's not mine to give," Elodie said quietly.

"It will be, if you win. And if you lose, it won't matter whose it is, because the magnates will strip it for the brass in the door handles." Odile kept her voice level, almost gentle, because cruelty would have been easier and she refused the easier thing. "Understand me, Lockridge. I am not doing this for you. I am doing it for my father, who signed papers he couldn't read fast enough, in a room full of smiling men, twenty-two years ago this March. The same men. The same families. Harlan Gorse's father held the notes on ours. When the crash came—engineered, I have never doubted, engineered to clear the small shops off the waterfront—the elder Gorse called in every debt at once and bought the whole street for pennies. My father went to his grave believing he had failed. He had not failed. He had been *harvested*. There is a difference, and I intend to see it entered into the record."

Elodie had gone very still. Not the stillness of fear—Odile knew that stillness too well from her own mirror—but the stillness of someone recalibrating everything. Rebuilding a model of the world around new information.

"You want the workshop," Elodie said slowly, "as collateral."

"As collateral, yes. If the record stands and the inquiry clears your family, you buy back your premises at whatever sum we agree tonight—fair, written, witnessed by Marrec himself, I'm not a thief. If the record fails—" Odile shrugged. "Then I will have spent my presses, my paper, my notary, and my safety on a lost cause, and I'll want something to show for it besides ruin. Sentiment does not print pages, Lockridge. Iron does."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you should leave now, and kindly forget the location of my door, because if you're followed here we are both finished." Odile moved to the workbench, began squaring a stack of blank paper that did not need squaring—a habit, a tell, her own restless escapement—and made herself say the rest of it. "I'll tell you what I told Sable Ferren, when I caught the two of you in the harbor office and chose blindness over duty: I have spent fifteen years being precise in a small room, repairing other people's shoddy work, taking the jobs no respectable firm wants, and telling myself that precision was its own reward. Precision is not its own reward. Precision is a weapon that rusts if you never fire it. I am forty-one years old and I am tired of keeping my powder dry."

* * *

They negotiated for two hours, and Odile would remember those two hours for the rest of her life with something dangerously close to fondness.

Elodie did not beg. That was the first surprise. The girl—no, not a girl; Odile kept having to correct herself; an apprentice grown into something harder, a maker in her own right whatever the guild rolls said—sat across from her at the bench with the rain drumming overhead and bargained like a dockside merchant. Terms were proposed and countered. The repurchase sum was argued down, then up again, then fixed at a figure both of them hated equally, which Odile privately considered the definition of a fair price. Clauses emerged: the agreement void if Odile disclosed anything before filing; the agreement doubled in obligation if Odile's presses failed mid-run; the agreement binding Odile's own premises if she abandoned the work unfinished—Elodie's addition, sharp as a graver, and Odile had laughed out loud, actually laughed, for the first time in a month, at the sheer nerve of it.

"You realize," Odile said, dipping a pen, "that you're drafting a contract with the same care you'd cut a gear train."

"You realize," Elodie answered, not looking up, "that I'm trusting the only printing press in the harbor not owned, directly or indirectly, by the men we're trying to bury."

Fair. Odile wrote the clause in anyway.

By the third hour the document lay between them, four pages in Odile's narrow upright hand, terms initialed in the margins. All it lacked was the notary. All it lacked, Odile thought, staring down at the signature lines, was the one thing that could not be drafted: trust.

"There's one more term," Elodie said. "Not in writing."

"Those are usually the dangerous ones."

"If we win—if the record stands and the inquiry clears us—you'll have the workshop. Legally. And I'll honor it, every clause, to the letter." Elodie looked up, and in the lamplight her eyes had the flat bright quality of polished steel. "But I want you to tell me now, honestly, what you'll do with it. Because my father's father built that bench my mentor works at. And I need to know whether I'm mortgaging my family's grave or selling it."

The question hung in the damp air. Odile set down the pen. Outside, the rain had thickened toward the steady harbor downpour that drowned sound and turned every lantern into a smear of gold, and she thought about the answer that would be clever, and discarded it, because this girl would smell cleverness the way she smelled a badly cut pinion.

"I don't know," Odile said. "That is the honest answer. I want it because wanting it is the only clean feeling I have left about this harbor. Whether I'd keep it, sell it, or stand in the middle of the shop floor and weep like a fool—I genuinely cannot tell you. What I can tell you is this: I have watched the magnates run businesses for fifteen years, and I have watched your family keep one, and there is not one practice, one standard, one habit of the Lockridge workshop that I would change except its address." She picked the pen back up. "If I ever do hold those keys, the benches stay. The standards stay. Perhaps that is worth more than ownership. I've never been able to afford finding out."

For a long moment Elodie said nothing. Then she drew a slow breath, and nodded once, the way a craftsman nods when a part seats true.

"Marrec," she said. "Tomorrow. Before the magistrate's office opens, before anyone is awake to wonder why a Vasse and a Lockridge are standing together on the same side of a street."

"First light," Odile agreed. "And Lockridge—" She hesitated, then reached beneath the bench and produced a small parcel wrapped in oilcloth, setting it on the table between them. Inside, when Elodie unwrapped it, lay a set of punches and a burnisher, secondhand but immaculately maintained, the steel still bright. "For the copies. If we're forging nothing, we're certifying everything, and certified documents want proper seals pressed clean. Yours have been reground; I noticed, the night I caught you, that you were working with worn tools. Precision deserves better."

Elodie held the parcel as though it weighed far more than it did.

"Why help me at all?" she asked, and it was not suspicion in her voice now—it was something rawer, almost bewildered, the question of someone who had lived so long in concealment that generosity registered as error.

Odile looked at her—the ink-stained hands, the exhausted eyes, the terrible young certainty that the world could still be put right if only the parts were machined true enough—and felt, briefly and unbearably, the full weight of her own forty-one years.

"Because they took my father's name off that door," she said, nodding toward the street below, toward the rope-walk and the rain and the harbor beyond, "and nobody printed a single page to say it shouldn't have come off. This time, somebody will."

She blew out the lamp. In the sudden dark, the presses stood like patient animals, and beyond the streaming glass the harbor lights burned on—and among them, low on the black water, the riding lamp of the *Coriolis*, swinging gently at anchor, counting down the tide.

"Get some sleep," Odile said into the darkness. "Tomorrow we make history official. And the morning after—we find out what your master Ivo has been doing with that letter, because I promise you, Lockridge, men like him never let a valuable paper sit still for long."

She heard Elodie's breath catch in the dark, and knew the words had landed exactly where she'd aimed them.
