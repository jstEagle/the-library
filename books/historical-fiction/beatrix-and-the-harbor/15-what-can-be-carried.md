## 15. What Can Be Carried

The workshop had never been quiet before. Even at night there had been the tick of a dozen unfinished clocks, the breathing of pendulums out of step with one another, the small restless industry of mechanisms waiting to be finished. Now I had silenced them all—every weight let down, every escapement stopped—and in that manufactured silence the room felt less like a workshop than like a body laid out for viewing.

Odile's bargain sat on my chest like a lead weight in a clock case. Her presses against our name. If we failed, the Lockridge workshop passed to Vasse & Daughters, and everything my grandfather had built with his hands would be signed away by mine. There was no time to grieve it properly. The warrant was coming; the tide was coming; and between them lay one narrow night in which I could decide what a family was allowed to carry.

So I began to sort.

I started at the bench nearest the window because it was where Grandmother Lockridge had worked, and because the light came earliest there, gray and pearl-colored off the harbor, and I wanted to do this part while the light was kind. Her tools lay in their velvet grooves: burnishers worn to the shape of her thumb, a pair of pliers whose jaws no longer quite met, tweezers fine enough to lift a hair-spring without bruising it. Each tool carried the ghost of its use. A craftsman's tools are not objects so much as arguments—a record of ten thousand decisions made in steel.

I wrapped the burnishers in flannel. My hands knew the work even as my chest ached. You wrap a tool the way you'd bandage a wound: snug, but not so tight you crush what's delicate beneath.

* * *

By midmorning I had made three piles on the long workbench, and the piles told me more about myself than I wanted to know.

The first pile was *value*—the brass stock, the jeweled bearings, the finished movements we might sell or trade. It was the smallest pile and I felt ashamed of how quickly I'd assembled it, as though some practical stranger lived inside me and took over in moments of crisis.

The second pile was *sentiment*—Grandfather's first apprentice piece, a lopsided carriage clock with a door that never closed true; Mother's engraving tools; the framed certificate from the Guild of Horologists, its gold leaf gone brown at the edges. This pile was too large. Sentiment always is. I stood over it a long while, touching things, putting things back, taking them up again.

The third pile was empty, and it took me until nearly noon to understand what belonged in it. Not what could be carried. What *should* be carried. What mattered past the losing of it.

I was still standing there when Sable Ferren came through the door without knocking, which was her way, carrying two bread rolls and a face like weather.

"You look," she said, "as if you're holding a funeral."

"Something like that." I nodded at the bench. "Dividing an inheritance."

She set the rolls down among the sentiment pile, which seemed right somehow, and picked up the lopsided carriage clock. She turned it over in her big dockworker's hands with surprising gentleness.

"Ugly little thing."

"It was his first. He kept it on his shelf his whole life. Never sold it, never fixed the door." I heard my own voice go thin. "Sable, if Odile takes this place, they'll strip it in a day. Lathes to the scrap man, ledgers to the fire. That's what happens. I've watched it happen to other names."

"Then take what matters and let the rest go."

"That's exactly what I'm trying to learn how to do."

She put the clock down and looked at me with those flat harbor eyes that missed nothing. "You've been looking at the wrong piles, then. Show me what you're keeping."

I showed her. Tools, some stock, the certificate, a photograph of my parents that I hadn't known was in the third drawer until this morning, when grief had sent me digging through places I hadn't opened in years. Sable studied the collection the way she studied a cargo manifest—quickly, completely, without mercy.

"And the letter?" she said.

I blinked. "What?"

"The letter. The one that started all of this. The one with your family's name forged on it like a brand." She tapped the bench. "You've got your grandfather's ugly clock and your mother's tweezers, and you haven't once looked at the thing that's actually worth something. The record, Elodie. The letter, the ledger pages, the signature from the clock case. That's your inheritance. Not this—" she gestured at the benches, the dead clocks, the dust hanging in the pearl light— "this is just the house the inheritance lived in."

I wanted to argue. Some stubborn part of me—the part that had spent years being Beatrix, invisible, careful, nothing—wanted to say that the tools were real and paper was paper. But I had spent weeks now learning otherwise. Ambrose Pell had taught it first, in his office that smelled of sealing wax and cold tea: records become history, and history is written by whoever bothers to write it down. And Odile had taught it again, pressing certified copies like coins fresh from a mint: truth is worthless until it is witnessed.

"I know," I said finally. "I do know. It's just—" I picked up Grandfather's carriage clock and turned its broken door in my fingers. "He used to say a clockmaker's whole life is in her hands. That you could read a person's character off their work the way you read a hallmark off silver. If the workshop goes, what's left to read?"

Sable was quiet a moment. Outside, a gull argued with the morning, and somewhere down the quay a winch began its slow complaining turn.

"You're reading it backwards," she said. "It isn't what he left in the shop. It's what he left in *you*. The patience. The measuring. The way you can hold a spring up to the light and see whether it's true." She shrugged, embarrassed by her own eloquence, and reached for a bread roll. "Nobody can confiscate that. Though knowing you, they'd try."

* * *

I worked through the afternoon with a new discipline. Not sorting anymore—*selecting*. Everything went into three crates borrowed from Sable: one for tools that fit my hands as though grown there, one for documents wrapped in oilcloth against damp, and one, deliberately half-empty, for whatever I could not yet foresee needing. I left the brass stock. I left the lathe. I left the certificate, after a long moment, because I realized the Guild's seal meant nothing next to what we were about to file with Pell, and because carrying it felt like carrying a letter of introduction to a world that had already decided who we were.

The hardest thing was the wall of drawings.

Three generations of Lockridges had sketched their designs directly onto the plaster between the benches—escapements, gear trains, a striking mechanism Grandfather had dreamed of building and never had. They covered the wall like a mural, like scripture, forty years of graphite rubbed into lime wash. You cannot crate a wall.

I stood before it a long time with a candle stub in my hand, because the light had failed and I hadn't noticed.

Then I did what any cowardly, sensible, heartbroken apprentice would do. I took tracing paper from the flat drawer and a soft pencil, and I copied them. All of them. My hand shook at first and the lines wobbled, and then—somewhere around Grandmother's detached-lever escapement, drawn with a delicacy that still made my breath catch—the shaking stopped, and I was simply working, simply measuring, simply doing the one thing in the world I knew how to do, and the grief poured itself into the pencil line and became something else. Purpose, maybe. Or the shape purpose leaves behind.

Line by line, the wall came down onto paper. Line by line, the workshop became portable.

When I finished, my neck was rigid and the candles had burned to pools, and Sable was asleep in Grandfather's chair with her boots up and her mouth open, having refused twice to leave me alone with my ghosts. I woke her, gave her the second bread roll she'd been saving, and asked her to help me nail the crates shut.

"One more thing," I said.

I went to the master clock—not the observatory's great regulator, but our own shop clock, the tall case that had governed my childhood, whose chime had marked supper and curfew and every punishment and every pardon. I opened its case the way I'd opened the observatory clock weeks ago, with that same mixture of reverence and dread.

Empty. Of course it was empty. Our clock held only time.

But I knelt there a moment with my hand flat against the wood, and I thought about what I'd found in the other one. A sealed letter. A forged name. Evidence hidden by a mentor who claimed love and delivered concealment. *Everything hidden rots,* I thought. *Everything buried turns traitor.* Whatever we carried out of here tomorrow would be carried in the open—filed, sealed, witnessed, stamped. Inheritance by daylight. That was the whole difference between us and Ivo, between us and the magnates, between us and every victor writing history in his own hand.

"Sable," I said. She looked up from the crate lid, hammer in hand. "If we lose tomorrow—if the warrant comes and Odile takes the shop and the Coriolis sails with their poisoned evidence—what will you do?"

She considered it honestly, the way she considered everything, weighing cargo.

"Find you," she said. "Wherever you land. Then start over." She swung the hammer. "A dockworker carries her trade on her back, same as you carry yours. Shops burn. Ships sink. Names are the only cargo worth insuring."

The hammer fell. The nails bit home.

* * *

Near midnight, with the crates stacked by the door and the workshop stripped to its bones, I made a discovery that stopped my blood.

The flat drawer—Mother's flat drawer, the wide shallow one where she'd kept her engravings—was lighter than it should have been. I'd inventoried it that morning. I knew its contents the way I knew my own teeth. And something was missing.

I pulled it out entirely and set it on the bench under the last candle. The tracing papers were there. The gravers were there. The small tin of rouge was there. But the space at the back, where Mother had kept her guild commission certificates and, folded into them, the original deed to the workshop—

Gone.

I told myself a dozen explanations. I had moved it during the sorting. Sable had shifted it. Paper migrates; drawers lie; memory is a bad witness. I went through every crate twice, then every drawer, then the sawdust bin, kneeling in the debris of my dismantled childhood with candlelight jumping across the floor.

The deed was nowhere in the workshop.

And then another absence announced itself, quieter and colder: the sound of the house above. Ivo's tread, which I had tracked my whole life through the floorboards—heavy at dawn, measured at noon, restless after dark—had been absent since evening. His coat hook by the stair held nothing. His good boots, the ones he wore only for business, were gone from their pair beneath the bench.

I stood in the middle of the ruined workshop with the candle guttering and understood, with the terrible clarity of a balance wheel settling into beat, exactly what was missing, and why, and where it was going.

Not the deed alone. The deed, and perhaps the letter, and perhaps everything—traded tonight, on the eve of the tide, for whatever immunity a frightened old man could buy with his family's name.

"Sable," I whispered, and she was awake instantly, reading my face in the dark. "Wake up. We have to reach the quay."
