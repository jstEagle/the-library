## 17. Night of Copies

Odile Vasse had spent twenty years learning that a press never lies. It takes the form set upon it—the letters, the spacing, the ink worked into the grooves of type—and it reproduces that truth or that falsehood with perfect fidelity. A press does not care which it carries. She had printed reward notices for the harbor magnates, invoices stamped with their seals, congratulatory broadsheets praising men she had watched cheat widows of their warehouses. Her hands had made their lies legible. Tonight, at last, her hands would make something else.

She lit every lamp in the workshop before they arrived. Not from extravagance—oil was dear, and her margins thinner than anyone suspected—but because she wanted no shadows in this room tonight. Whatever was made here would be made in full light, witnessed by three pairs of eyes, and if the magistrate's men kicked down her door before dawn, she wanted them to see everything plainly: the type, the paper, the evidence of her own guilt in honest illumination.

The workshop smelled of oil of turpentine, hot metal, and the tallow candles she kept for emergencies. Her presses stood in their familiar ranks—two iron hand-presses from her father's day, a composing stone worn to a shallow dish by decades of thumbs, cases of type arranged in the order she could read blind. Above them, hung on nails, were the wooden patterns she used for ornamental borders: acanthus leaves, ships' prows, the crossed anchors of the harbor authority. She had forged that anchor more times than she could count, on permits and clearances, always at the magnates' instruction, always technically sanctioned, always a small violence against the truth.

She heard them on the stairs before she saw them—Sable Ferren's boots, heavy and uneven, favoring the ribs the enforcers had cracked two nights past; and beneath them, lighter, quicker, the careful step of Elodie Lockridge, who walked like someone perpetually measuring the distance between herself and other people.

They came in carrying the world between them. Sable had the satchel of ledger pages copied in the harbor office by candlelight, wrapped in oilcloth and strapped against her chest like an infant. Elodie carried the letter itself, and the brass fitting pried from the observatory clock—the hidden compartment, the forged signature pressed into wax older than Elodie's apprenticeship—and a folio of Odile's own making: certified copies of shipping manifests that Odile had printed years ago at Halloran & Mott's request, manifests she had kept proofs of precisely because some instinct in her had whispered *someday*.

"Lock the door," Odile said. "Both bolts and the bar."

Sable threw the bar across with a sound like a coffin closing. "How long do we have?"

Odile consulted the regulator clock on her wall—the one fine instrument she owned, kept to the second because a printer's reputation lived and died on deadlines. "Warrant issues at six, if it issues at all. Pell won't say when he'll be asked to countersign the seizure order until he's standing in front of it." She let that sit a moment. "So we assume we have until dawn, and we assume dawn comes early."

Elodie set the letter on the composing stone with the reverence of a craftsman laying out a movement's finest wheel. In the lamplight her face looked carved, all planes and shadow, exhaustion drawn into the lines around her mouth. Younger than Odile by ten years, perhaps twelve, and carrying weight that would bend a dockside crane. Odile had spent most of her life disliking the Lockridges on principle—rival workshop, rival name, Master Ivo's delicate movements that fetched higher prices than anything the Vasse presses could produce—but principle had died two nights ago in this same room, when Elodie had shown her the letter and Odile had read her own father's name in the list of ruined suppliers, and understood that the conspiracy she had serviced with ink had swallowed her family long before it swallowed the Lockridges' good name.

"We print three copies," Elodie said. "One for Pell's registry. One sealed and held apart—if the registry is seized, the second survives. One—" she hesitated.

"One for the capital, sent by whatever ship isn't the Coriolis," Odile finished. "I've thought of that too. The *Merrow*, out on Thursday, carries mail. A captain who owes me for four hundred funeral notices I printed at half price when his wife died." She pulled her apron from its hook and tied it. "Nothing given freely, Lockridge. You know our terms. But within those terms, we work like the devil is reading over our shoulder. Because he is. He wears a magistrate's chain."

* * *

They began with the letter.

It was the smallest document and the most dangerous—a single folded sheet, sealed with plain wax, naming the Lockridge family as parties to a smuggling arrangement they had never joined, dated eleven years back, written in a hand Odile recognized the moment she saw it under her lens. Halloran's own. The harbor magnate had the penmanship of a man who signed things without reading them, all flourish and no pressure, and his signature appeared in the lower corner as witness to an agreement that existed nowhere else in the record except—she now knew—in forged entries scattered through eleven years of shipping ledgers.

"It has to be reproduced exactly," Elodie said. "Every flaw in the paper. The foxing, there, at the fold. If we print a clean copy, they'll say we fabricated it. It has to be imperfect in precisely the way the original is imperfect."

"That," said Odile, "is the hardest kind of printing there is." She was already pulling her facsimile type—the closest thing to forgery equipment she owned, purchased legitimately years ago for reproducing antique playbills—and her block of soft transfer wax. "Give me an hour with it alone. Meanwhile, Sable, the ledgers. We set them as tables, exact figures, exact dates, and beside each entry we set the corresponding manifest line where the signature differs. The eye will catch what lawyers take hours to explain."

Sable lowered herself onto the bench with a hiss of pain, one arm cradled against her side, and began unwrapping the oilcloth with fingers swollen but steady. Odile watched her a moment—the dockworker's broad hands moving among the delicate pages like a bear handling moth wings—and felt something shift in her chest that she had not permitted herself in twenty years of solitary work.

"You're hurt worse than you've said," Odile said.

"I'm hurt exactly as much as I've said, and no more, and it doesn't slow my reading." Sable flattened the first page against the stone. "Numbers don't care about my ribs."

"They will when you drop a stack of them."

"Then you'll pick it up." But Sable's mouth twitched, and Odile caught it, and for a moment the two women regarded each other across the composing stone with the wary amusement of soldiers sharing a trench. Then Sable bent to the pages, and the room settled into the rhythm of work.

It was a rhythm Odile knew better than prayer. The clack of type being set, letter by letter, into the composing stick. The tap of the mallet driving quoins tight. The rasp of the press bed as it rolled, the wet kiss of ink on dampened paper, the pull of the lever that required her whole body's weight. Around midnight she sent Elodie to the dampening tub and took over the letter reproduction herself, working the transfer under glass, matching the foxed patches with diluted sepia applied by brush to the stone, breathing through her mouth so her hands would not tremble.

At some point she became aware that Elodie was watching her.

"You've done this before," Elodie said quietly. "Copied documents. Made old things look new and new things look old."

"I've done what my customers paid for." Odile did not look up. "Halloran needed a lost lease reconstructed after the fire that conveniently destroyed his office. Mott wanted his grandfather's founding charter refreshed for the anniversary banquet. Every time, I told myself the original existed somewhere, that I was only restoring a truth, not inventing one." She lifted the roller, inspected the ink coverage, and pulled the first proof of the forged letter—Halloran's treachery rendered in faithful detail, every stain accounted for. "Tonight is the first time I've pointed these tools at the men who gave me work. I find I have no hesitation whatsoever. That surprises me less than it should."

Elodie took the proof and held it beside the original for a long minute, comparing under the lamp. Then she did something Odile did not expect: she nodded, once, the precise nod of a clockmaker approving an escapement, and said, "It would fool me. And I've been living inside that family's paper since I was seven."

"Your mentor taught you to read documents that way?"

"He taught me to read mechanisms that way. Paper is a mechanism. So are ledgers. So are conspiracies." Elodie laid the proof down. "Gears and springs, Odile. Everything moves according to what someone built into it. Ivo taught me that much truth even while he was—" Her jaw tightened. She did not finish.

Odile let the silence stand. There was nothing to say about Ivo Lockridge that either of them had not already thought. The man had taken the letter meant to destroy his family and hidden it inside a clock rather than confront the men who wrote it, choosing eleven years of quiet survival over one day of open war—and then, when the war finally came to his door, had taken the letter to the quay to trade it for himself. They had intercepted him there. Elodie had not spoken of it since. Odile recognized the wound because she had one shaped like it, left by a different coward in a different decade.

"The third copy," Elodie said at last, changing the subject the way one releases a mainspring slowly, controlled. "You said a fourth thing earlier. When we planned this. You said—"

"I said we'd need witnesses." Odile moved to the second press and began damping fresh paper. "Not just our testimony. Certificates. This city trusts seals, not people. So we'll seal each copy ourselves—not with the harbor anchor, God forbid, though I confess the temptation—with my workshop mark and your signature, both, plus Sable's mark. Three names. Three trades. Clockmaker, printer, dockworker. Let the inquiry try to argue that the whole working harbor conspired to forge one letter."

From the bench, without looking up from the ledger columns, Sable said, "My mark's just an X."

"An X witnessed and sworn before a clerk who risks his office to register it," Odile said. "Ambrose Pell will notarize at dawn, before he presents the warrant, or not at all. Which brings us to the hour." She glanced at the regulator. Half past one. Outside, the rain that had threatened all evening finally arrived, drumming on the skylight in long silver sheets, and beneath it, fainter, the harbor bell counting the quarter with its patient bronze voice.

* * *

At two o'clock the presses found their stride, and Odile stopped thinking in words.

This was the state she loved—had always loved, had chosen over marriage and comfort and her father's blessing—the place where the body becomes the mechanism. Left hand feeds the dampened sheet onto the tympan. Right hand pulls the frisket. Weight forward onto the bar, the platen descending, the impression biting clean and deep. Release. Lift. The finished page goes to the drying line, the next sheet takes its place. Two hundred impressions an hour if the arms hold, and hers had held for twenty years and would hold tonight or die trying.

Elodie worked the type, and Odile marveled at it. The girl composed like she must repair clocks—with total absorption, breath slowing, fingers finding each character in the case without looking, eyes fixed on the stick. Between them and Sable's tireless checking of figures against figures, the true account assembled itself page by page across the stone floor: the letter, facsimile and transcription; the ledger tables with their damning parallel columns; Odile's original manifest proofs, annotated in her own hand certifying date and circumstance of printing; and a statement of narrative, drafted by Elodie in a copperplate so disciplined it looked typeset, telling the whole affair from the observatory clock to the quay at nightfall—Ivo's flight, the interception, the surrender of the original letter into Elodie's keeping.

Sable read that statement three times and finally said, "You wrote yourself smaller than it happened."

"I wrote what can be proven."

"You wrote what a frightened person would write." Sable's voice was gentle, which somehow made it worse. "Put the rest in. Put the fear in. Put the part where you almost ran. History that's all clean corners is a lie, Elodie. Nobody will believe it because nobody ever lived it."

Odile pulled the lever, set a sheet, and said nothing, because the dockworker was right and the clockmaker knew it, and after a moment Elodie took back the draft and added two paragraphs in her careful hand, and the account grew teeth.

Rain intensified toward three. Water found a seam in the skylight and began a thin, musical drip into a bucket near the drying lines, and the sound wove into the rhythm of work until it seemed part of the mechanism. Once, boots passed in the street below—heavy, several men, walking with the unhurried confidence of patrols that expect to find nothing—and all three of them froze, mid-motion, a tableau of stillness lasting thirty heartbeats, until the boots faded toward the customs house. Sable exhaled slowly through her nose. Elodie's hand resumed setting type without appearing to have stopped at all. Only Odile admitted the moment, wiping her palms on her apron: "That's the warrant's advance riders, or I'm a Dutchman. They check the district before dawn so the seizure comes as a surprise. Which means Pell was asked for the district survey tonight, which means he's already decided how to answer." She allowed herself a thin smile. "Quiet little man. I'd have bet a year's profits he'd fold. I owe him an apology, if any of us live to give it."

At four, disaster nearly came anyway, and came—as disasters prefer—through the smallest channel. The transfer wax for the final facsimile impressions ran out with nine copies still unmade. Nine copies short of completeness, and no shop in the harbor sold such wax at four in the morning.

Elodie solved it. Of course Elodie solved it; Odile was coming to expect that the way one expects the tide. The girl went to the clock on Odile's wall—the regulator she prized above all her possessions—opened its case with a borrowed movement wrench before Odile could protest, and extracted from the movement a small disc of sealing compound that served to bed the crutch wire, softened it over the lamp flame with a clockmaker's absolute control of heat, and rolled it flat between two plates of glass to a thickness the original wax could not match.

"That compound is better than commercial wax," Elodie said. "It holds impression for fifty years. Your copies will outlast the originals."

"You've desecrated my regulator."

"I've improved it. The bedding was dried out. I'll recut it before I leave." A pause, and then, with the ghost of a smile that Odile had never seen on her before tonight: "Consider us even for the workshop clause."

Odile laughed then—a short, rusty sound, an instrument played after years in storage—and returned to the press, and the nine remaining impressions came off the bed clean and deep and true.

By five the drying lines hung full, forty-five copies in three identical sets, and the certification began. This was the slow sacrament: Elodie signing each statement page, Sable pressing her inked thumb and drawing her X, Odile striking her workshop seal into a wafer on every copy—the Vasse comet-and-wheel, the mark her father had registered when printing was still an honorable trade in this city. Sign, press, sign, press. The bucket caught the drip in steady time. The sky beyond the rain-streaked skylight turned from black to the color of wet slate.

When the last seal cooled, they divided the sets. One went into a strongbox for Pell. One went into the false bottom of Odile's paper cabinet, behind twenty years of innocuous jobbing work. The third Elodie wrapped in oilcloth, and her hands slowed as she tied the cord, and Odile understood that the girl was holding the weight of everything—the workshop she would lose if this failed, the name she was spending like her last coin.

"It will be enough," Odile said. "It has to be constructed correctly, and it is. I build arguments the way I build pages, Lockridge. Nothing in this record is loose."

"Records aren't gears," Elodie said quietly. "People read them. People can be bought."

"People can," Odile agreed. "But Ambrose Pell cannot, and the registry cannot, and morning cannot be stopped. Come. Dawn in forty minutes. We should be standing on his doorstep when he unlocks it, so the warrant arrives second."

They doused the lamps but one. At the door, Sable paused, looked back across the workshop—at the drying lines strung with the true account like laundry washed clean of eleven years of lies—and said, to neither of them and to both, "My grandmother used to say the harbor keeps what's honest and sinks the rest. Tonight it kept us."

Then they were on the dark stairs, and the rain had thinned to mist, and the east showed the first gray seam of the day they intended to survive.

But when they reached the street, Odile stopped.

At the far corner, where the lane opened toward the harbor road, a carriage stood waiting with its lamps lit—a private carriage, crested, and beside it three men whose silhouettes she knew the way she knew bad type: enforcers, and behind them, stepping down onto the wet cobbles with a lawyer's care not to soil his shoes, the magistrate's own clerk, holding a paper that even at this distance Odile would have known anywhere.

The warrant. An hour early, and aimed—not at Pell's office at all.

At them.
