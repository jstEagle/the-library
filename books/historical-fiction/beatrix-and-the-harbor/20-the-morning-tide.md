## 20. The Morning Tide

The tide turned at eleven minutes past four, and Elodie felt it before she heard it—a long exhalation beneath the pier, as though the harbor itself had been holding its breath through the whole of the night and could finally let go.

She stood at the rail of the customs quay with Sable's blood still dried under her fingernails, because there had been no time to wash properly, only a hurried scrubbing at the pump in Odile's yard that had left her knuckles raw and pink in the cold. The lamps along the quay burned low and orange against a sky that was not yet any color at all, merely the promise of one, a gray so pale it might have been spun from clock glass. Below her, the *Coriolis* rode at her moorings, riding higher in the water than she had the evening before.

That was how Elodie knew, before anyone confirmed it, before Pell's clerk came stumbling down the wet stone steps with a lantern swinging wild in his hand—that they had won. A ship carrying evidence rides heavy. This one floated like an emptied purse.

"They're not loading," Sable said beside her. Her voice was rough as rope end; the bruise along her jaw had gone purple-black in the hours since the enforcers had finished with her, and when she spoke her lip split again at the corner, a thin line of red welling up that she wiped away with the back of her wrist as if it were nothing, as if rain had touched her. "Look at them. They don't know what to do with their hands."

It was true. On the deck of the *Coriolis*, the magnates' men moved in the jerky, purposeless way of people whose instructions have evaporated. One stood at the gangway holding a strongbox that Elodie knew—knew the way she knew the weight of any mechanism—was meant to hold the letter, the forged signature, the false ledger pages that would have sailed to the capital on this morning's tide and become, by noon next Thursday, the official truth of the harbor affair. The box swung from his hand like something dead. Every few minutes he looked toward the harbor office, and every few minutes the office windows stayed dark, because Ambrose Pell was upstairs sealing the registry with wax and his own trembling, unbreakable oath, and he would not come down for anyone until the record was done.

Elodie's legs ached in a way she had never felt before—not the ache of standing at a bench for fourteen hours over a movement too delicate to rush, but a deeper one, bone-deep, as if she had been winding herself all night with no governor to regulate the spring. She put her hand flat on the quay rail to steady something, even if only herself.

The rail was slick with spray. Beyond it the water moved in that slow muscular way it had before dawn, black shot through with the last lamplight, and the smell was of tar and salt and the green rot of pilings—the smell she had breathed her whole life under the alias Beatrix, invisible, unrecorded, safe. It smelled different now. It smelled like a place she might be seen in.

"Do you feel it?" Sable asked. "Under your feet?"

"The tide."

"The tide." Sable nodded slowly, wincing as the nod pulled at her ribs. "My father used to say the tide doesn't take sides. Comes for everything eventually, high water or low. But I think—" she stopped, and Elodie saw her work her jaw, testing whether speech still worked properly around the swelling. "I think this morning it came for them."

* * *

The magnates arrived at half past five.

Elodie knew two of them by sight alone, as everyone at the harbor did: Halloran of the shipping lines, broad and silver-haired, a man who wore his wealth the way a battleship wears armor plate, and young Merrick Stave, whose family owned the warehouses and whose face always suggested a man listening to music no one else could hear. They came down the quay steps together in the first true light—which was to say, the light had stopped pretending to be gray and committed instead to a sickly pearl—and behind them came their secretary, and behind the secretary came two enforcers who slowed, visibly, when they saw the dockworkers along the rail.

Because the dockworkers were still there. That was the thing Elodie kept returning to, turning over in her mind like a gear whose teeth she wanted to count. The enforcers' warning beating had been delivered to Sable twelve hours ago, brutal and efficient, meant to scatter loyalty like sparrows. And instead the quay this morning held forty of them, fifty, silent men and women with their hands in their pockets and their caps low, lined along the customs rail in the cold, watching the *Coriolis* the way a jury watches a defendant enter the room. No one had called them. No one had paid them. Sable had limped through Odile's workshop door at midnight, spat blood into the sink, and said only: *the quay, before the tide*, and apparently that word had traveled the way tide-words do, wharf to wharf, lamp to lamp, faster than any printed page.

Halloran reached the foot of the steps and stopped. His gaze went along the rail—measuring, Elodie thought, the way she herself measured a case for its movement, assessing what would fit and what would not—and then went up to the harbor office windows, which now, finally, showed lamplight. Then back to the ship that floated too high in the water.

"Where is my manifest cargo," he said. He did not raise his voice. Men like Halloran never needed to; the harbor raised it for him.

The secretary unfolded a paper. His hands were not steady. "The strongbox was delivered to the office last night for certification, per the new filing requirements—"

"There *are* no new filing requirements."

"There are now, sir." The secretary's throat moved. "Clerk Pell entered it into the registry at four o'clock this morning. Sealed. Witnessed." A pause in which the gulls seemed very loud, wheeling and crying over the masts. "It's been countersigned, sir. By three witnesses. Under his official seal."

Elodie watched Halloran absorb this, and what struck her was not rage but arithmetic happening behind his face. She recognized the expression. It was the expression of a man opening a watch case and finding the escapement altered—not broken, nothing so crude as broken, but altered, regulated by someone else's hand, keeping someone else's time. A man could shout at a broken watch. An altered one required study.

"And the letter," Halloran said quietly. "The Lockridge item."

"Entered also, sir. Same hour. Same seal."

Somewhere along the rail a dockworker shifted his weight, and the small sound carried in the hush like a ratchet clicking.

Merrick Stave laughed—one short syllable, bitten off. "Pell," he said. "Ambrose *Pell*. Twenty-two years pushing ink in that closet of his, and he chooses *this*—" He caught himself, glanced at the dockworkers, at the enforcers who would not meet his eye, and Elodie understood that he had heard it too, the arithmetic underneath: you cannot dismiss a clerk who has done precisely, procedurally, unimpeachably his duty. You cannot fire the law for following the law. The machinery they had built to protect themselves had been turned, gently, exactly, against them, and there was no lever in the world that pried loose a sealed and witnessed registry entry.

"Who," Halloran said, to no one, to the water, and then louder, turning at last to face the rail full of watching workers: "*Who* carried the papers? Who certified them?"

No one answered. The gulls wheeled. The tide ran under the pilings, patient, impartial, taking everything a minute at a time.

And Elodie stood among them—Beatrix no longer, though the alias had not yet been formally buried, only outgrown, the way a movement outgrows its temporary holding plate—and said nothing, and had never in her life felt more visible, and had never minded less.

* * *

The *Coriolis* cast off at six minutes past six.

There was almost nothing to it. That was the strange, deflating truth of the moment Elodie had raced for across five sleepless days: the casting off of a ship is a small thing. A whistle. Lines coiled by deckhands who did not know and would never know what their vessel had failed to carry. The tug boats nosed her bow around, and the great hull swung slow as a door closing, and the water between ship and quay widened hand-span by hand-span, and that was all. History, Elodie thought, does not announce itself. It slips its moorings in the fog with a whistle and a wash of foam, and the people it saves and the people it condemns stand on the same wet stones breathing the same cold air, and mostly they cannot tell, yet, which they are.

But she could tell. She watched the *Coriolis* gather way under her canvas and head for the harbor mouth, and she knew with a certainty that settled into her like ballast: the ship rode empty of the lie. Somewhere below the waterline, in a hold fitted for crates of testimony that would have buried the Lockridge name forever, there was nothing but ballast and empty dunnage. The letter lay instead in a registry vault sixty feet behind her, under seal, entered at four o'clock, indexed in Ambrose Pell's narrow upright hand: *Deposition, correspondence, and supporting ledgers in the matter of harbor shipping signatures, submitted by E. L., certified copies appended.*

*Submitted by E.L.* Not Beatrix. When Pell had asked her, pen poised, what name should appear on the submission, the question had hung in the lamplit office like a struck bell still ringing, and she had heard herself say *Lockridge* in a voice that did not sound like hers, and Pell had written it without hesitation, and the world had not ended. The world had, in fact, continued to turn at its usual rate, indifferent and reliable, like good clockwork.

Beside her, Sable exhaled—a long, slow breath that seemed to empty her entirely, shoulders dropping, and for a moment Elodie thought she might sway, and got a shoulder under her friend's arm.

"I'm fine," Sable said, which was a lie, and they both let it stand, because some lies are load-bearing.

"You should be in a bed."

"I should be on that ship," Sable said, watching the sails fill, "if the world were fair. Carrying the account myself, walking it up the capital steps. Instead it goes by post and paperwork, and I get a bruise the size of a dinner plate." She grinned with half her mouth, the unsplit half. "Still. Paperwork. My father would die laughing. Ferrens don't file things, Elodie. We haul them."

"You hauled plenty."

"Mm." Sable was quiet a moment. The ship grew smaller, a dark shape against the brightening east, where the sky had begun—at last, grudgingly—to admit the possibility of gold. "The others'll want telling. The ones on the rail. They stood here all night for this; they'll want to know they won."

"They know."

"They know the ship left. They don't know what it means. There's a difference." Sable turned her head carefully, favoring the unbruised side, and looked at Elodie with an expression she had never directed at her before—or rather, an expression she had, but never this openly: respect worn on the outside where anyone could read it. "You'd better tell them, then. Whatever it is you're going to be, now. Beatrix told time. The rest is yours."

* * *

Odile Vasse found them at the harbor office steps an hour later, when the sun had fully committed and the quay had begun, cautiously, to resume being a quay—crates rolled, bells rang, the ordinary commerce of the morning flowing around the spot where extraordinary things had happened, as water flows around a piling.

She came down the street with her coat collar up and her hands in her pockets, and Elodie saw at once that she had not slept either; there was ink on her fingers to the second knuckle, the particular stubborn ink of press work, and smudges of it along one jaw where she had pushed back her hair with clean intent and dirty hands.

"It's done, then," Odile said, stopping before them. "I heard the whistle from my shop. I printed two hundred copies of an event I couldn't see." She looked out at the harbor mouth, where the *Coriolis* was now a suggestion, a dark fleck at the edge of the world. "Two hundred certified copies, Elodie. In your name. If we've guessed wrong—if Pell's seal doesn't hold, if the inquiry in the capital decides to prefer the magnates' version after all—"

"Then you take the workshop." Elodie said it steadily, though the words tasted like cold iron. "I know our bargain. I haven't forgotten."

"That's not why I mention it."

"No," Elodie said. "But it's why you can't stop mentioning it. I understand. I'd be the same." And she found, examining the feeling honestly, that she meant it without bitterness, which surprised her. Odile Vasse had taught her something in that midnight print shop, amid the smell of oil and damp paper: that a person can be ambitious and honest at once, that justice does not require purity, only precision. The presses had run till four. The copies were exact. That was all Odile owed anyone, and all she had promised.

Odile studied her a moment, that appraising clockmaker's stare, checking tolerances. "Pell will send the notification today," she said. "Formal notice of the registry entry, by fast packet. The inquiry commissioners will have it within the week." A pause. "They'll want the submitting party. To testify. To *authenticate*. That's how these things proceed—I've read the procedure. The person who placed the record has to stand behind it, in the capital, in open session, under oath."

The words landed in Elodie's chest one at a time, like weights being loaded onto a scale.

The capital. Not the harbor, where she could be Beatrix in a crowd, anonymous behind a workbench. The capital, where inquiries sat under lamps with stenographers taking down every syllable, where names were spoken aloud into the official air and became permanent. Where she would have to rise in front of magistrates and commissioners and Halloran's lawyers and say, clearly, her full name, and mean it.

"I know," she said.

Sable shifted beside her. "One thing at a time. Let the man eat first. Or at least sit down." She looked at Elodie properly then, and whatever she saw—the shaking that had started somewhere around dawn in Elodie's hands and had not yet consented to stop, the exhaustion coming off her like heat off a forge—softened something in her battered face. "Truly. Go home. Sleep. The tide's gone; nothing else leaves today."

But home, Elodie thought, walking back up the hill with the sun climbing warm on her neck at last, home was the workshop. Home was Ivo's bench and Ivo's tools, returned to their places or not, she didn't know; home was the man himself, wherever he had gone after the quay last night, after they had taken the letter from his hands and watched him understand what he had tried to do. She had not seen him since. She did not know which she dreaded more: finding him, or finding the workshop empty.

She stopped at the crest of the hill and looked back at the harbor, whole and glittering and ordinary, the fishing fleet out, the bells marking the quarter-hour all over the town in voices she had tuned or cleaned or built—the hidden city of ticking that was her family's real monument, scattered through every wall and tower, keeping faith even now.

The cost was not yet paid. She knew that with the same certainty with which she'd known the ship rode light. There would be a reckoning for Pell and his office; there would be questions about a warrant and a magistrate and a cold room with one chair; there would be Ivo, someday, wanting words she did not have to give him. And above it all, arriving within the week like weather: a summons west, to the capital, to stand and speak her own name into the record.

Let it come. She turned up the lane toward the workshop. She had spent her whole life learning that a mechanism keeps honest time only when every part is willing to bear its load—and she was done hiding the parts that carried the most.

At the workshop door, she found it already unlocked, and inside, seated at the bench in the morning light with his hat on his knees, waiting: Ivo.
