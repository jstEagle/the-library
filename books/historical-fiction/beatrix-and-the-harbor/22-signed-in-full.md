## 22. Signed in Full

The observatory at dawn was a different country from the one Elodie had first entered under another name.

Light came through the eastern windows in long amber panes, catching the dust that the winter had shaken loose from the dome's timbers, and the great master clock stood where it had always stood—patient, upright, indifferent to what it had witnessed. Its brass pendulum swung with the same unhurried authority it had kept through scandal and inquiry and the sailing of the *Coriolis*, as though time itself had declined to take sides in the affair of the harbor. Perhaps it hadn't. Perhaps that was the whole of what Elodie had learned: that time did not judge; it only recorded, faithfully, whatever was placed before it.

The repair was small. A worn pallet in the strike train, a hair of backlash in the third wheel, the kind of work an apprentice would do without ceremony and forget by supper. But Elodie did not intend to forget it. The oil was warmed, the tools laid out on green baize in the order of use—each one familiar as a hand, each one carrying the ghost of Ivo's grip over their own. That ghost no longer hurt the way it had in the cold room, or on the quay, or in the weeks after the inquiry's first summons. It had faded into something more bearable: a lesson written in steel about the difference between protecting a name and preserving a truth.

Elodie worked slowly. There was no tide to race now, no warrant bearing down, no ship standing off the mole with its hold half-empty and its captain furious. The morning was only a morning, gray-gold and salt-smelling, gulls arguing over something on the breakwater. The harbor below the cliff moved in its ordinary rhythms—crates swinging, hawsers creaking, men calling numbers that meant nothing to anyone but them. The harbor had chosen its side once, on this quay and every other, and it seemed content now to return to its own affairs.

The strike-train pallet came free with a whisper. Elodie dressed the contact face against the Arkansas stone, counting strokes the way Ivo had taught—*even pressure, even breath, let the stone do the remembering*—and found, to their quiet surprise, that the memory of those lessons could be held without flinching. He had taught well. That was true, whatever else was true. The hands that had hidden the letter in the case thirty years ago were the same hands that had guided Elodie's around a file for the first time, and both facts belonged in the record, because both facts were real. The certified copy in the capital's registry said so. It said everything, now, in Elodie's own arrangement of testimony and ledger-line and the old letter itself, flattened under glass like a specimen: the conspiracy they never joined, the signatures forged in a hand that mimicked their father's, the cargo the *Coriolis* had carried out to sea empty-handed while the true account stayed home.

The magnates had learned, too late, that paper is heavier than cargo.

Elodie fitted the pallet back into its lever and let the escapement taste it—one tick, two, three—the little metallic heartbeat steadying into its old rhythm. The clock approved. Clocks were honest that way; they could not be flattered or threatened, only maintained or neglected. Elodie had spent years believing invisibility was the same as safety, hiding behind *Beatrix* the way the letter had hidden behind the case-wood. But Beatrix had been an illusion someone else maintained, just as the false Lockridge signatures had been an illusion someone else maintained—and illusions, however lovingly made, always served the person who built them.

No more of that. Not today.

* * *

The gallery above the clock room had filled without Elodie noticing—that particular trick of absorbed attention, the world narrowing to the movement under the hands. Now, setting down the tweezers, Elodie heard the floorboards overhead and looked up through the rail.

Sable Ferren sat on the front bench with forearms on knees, boots planted wide as if braced against weather that wasn't there. The bruises along her jaw had gone the mottled yellow-green of deep healing, and she still favored her left side when she laughed, which she was doing now at something Odile Vasse had said. Odile stood at the rail's edge precisely, hands folded, wearing her good coat—the one with the ink-shadowed cuffs of a woman who ran presses—and her expression was the one Elodie privately called *inspection-grade*: the look she gave a movement she intended to find fault with and might, grudgingly, admire.

"You've let the fourth wheel run dry," Odile called down, without heat.

"I haven't. I oiled it Tuesday."

"Then your Tuesday oil has gone somewhere. I'd check your oiler."

"My oiler is fine. Your presses are crooked."

"They're level within a hair," Odile said, "which is more than can be said for your family's humility."

Sable's laugh cracked across the room like a whip of pure delight. "God preserve me from clockmakers. Are you nearly done down there, or are we going to stand here all morning being measured?"

"Nearly." Elodie closed the strike train's access panel, seated the screws, and gave the case door a last wipe with the chamois. The brass gleamed dully, honestly—not the parade-ground shine of a thing dressed for company, but the working luster of a machine that had a job to do. Through the open dial aperture the hands stood at four minutes to nine.

Four minutes to nine. Elodie knew exactly what those minutes were for.

"Come down, then," Elodie said. "Both of you. This part isn't mine alone."

Boots on the stair, unhurried. Sable arrived first, filling the doorway, smelling of tar and the coffee she pretended not to love; Odile followed, precise as a rack of type. They took up positions on either side of the clock case, and for a moment none of them spoke, because the moment understood itself and needed no help.

On the clerk's desk by the far wall—borrowed from Ambrose Pell that morning, carried up the cliff road by two dockworkers who refused payment and refused explanation in the same breath—lay the observatory's maintenance ledger, open to the current page. Years of entries marched up it in a fair round hand: *cleaned, adjusted, regulated*, and beside them, in a smaller, careful script, *B. — repaired per instruction*. Dozens of them. Every visit Elodie had ever made, signed with a name that had never belonged to anyone at all.

Beatrix. An alias chosen for safety, kept for habit, worn so long it had begun to fit like skin. And beneath its protection, all those years, the letter sleeping in the case three feet away, keeping the Lockridge name hostage in the dark.

"You don't have to make a speech," Sable said quietly. "I know how you hate speeches."

"I wasn't going to make a speech."

"You were going to make a *small* speech. I've seen you wind up to one."

Odile said nothing, but her eyes went to the ledger, then to Elodie, and in them was the whole of chapter fourteen's arithmetic: the workshop lost, the bargain kept, the presses that had run through two nights printing certified copies while enforcers pounded on shutters three streets away. Odile had demanded collateral and gotten it—the Lockridge bench, the tools, the sign with the painted dial. She owned it now, by law and by honor, and she had said only one thing when the inquiry's findings came down: *You'll want a place to work. Mine has room.* Nothing given freely; everything given anyway.

Elodie picked up the pen.

The nib took ink with a small, familiar sound, and Elodie's hand paused over the line—not from fear, but the way a hand pauses at the top of a descent it has chosen deliberately. Underneath, the entry waited: *Master clock, strike train pallet renewed, third wheel backlash corrected, regulated to mean solar time.* True words. Verifiable words. Words a magistrate could read aloud in the capital and no one could contradict, because the record behind them was sealed and stamped and sworn.

Elodie wrote the date. Then, in a hand that shook only slightly and only once:

*Elodie Lockridge.*

The letters looked strange on the page and entirely correct, the way a repaired escapement feels strange in the fingers for an hour and then simply feels like itself. Elodie set down the pen and blotted the name with care, as if it were wet paint rather than wet ink—as if it might smudge, might be unwritten, might be taken back.

It could not be. That was the point. Records did not take things back; that was why Ambrose Pell guarded his registry like a man guarding a heart, and why the magnates had wanted their historian to write the account instead, and why a forged signature had nearly cost a family everything. What was entered stayed entered. Elodie had spent a year learning that the hard way, and one minute choosing it on purpose.

"There," Sable said softly. "Now you exist."

"I always existed." Elodie closed the ledger. "I just wasn't on the record."

"Same thing in this harbor," Sable said, and grinned, bruise and all, "and don't let any historian tell you otherwise."

* * *

They climbed together to the gallery afterward, because the clock was due to strike and none of them wanted to miss it, and because Elodie wanted—needed—to hear the repaired pallet do its work with witnesses present. Below, through the dial's aperture, the minute hand gathered itself toward the hour.

Odile stood at the rail. "The capital post came yesterday," she said, in the tone she used for matters of inventory. "A letter. Official paper. Blue seal."

Elodie's stomach performed a small, skilled maneuver. "From the inquiry?"

"From the *Astronomical Society*." Odile drew it from her coat—an envelope heavy with rank, addressed in copperplate. "They've read the registry record. All of it. Apparently a certain clerk of record sent them a certified copy, unprompted, on his own initiative, at considerable risk to his office." Her mouth did something at one corner that on another face would have been called a smile. "Ambrose Pell, of all people. The quiet ones. Every time."

"What does it say?"

"It says the Society requires a horologist of proven integrity to certify instruments for the new tidal observatory at the capital. It says the inquiry cited your evidence as 'exemplary precision under adverse conditions'—their phrase, not mine, though I may have it engraved. And it says—" Odile turned the envelope over, savoring the delay the way she savored a well-set line of type, "—it says the position includes a workshop. Provided. In the city. With presses nearby."

The clock struck nine.

The new pallet caught the wheel cleanly, and the note that rose through the case and the floorboards and the soles of their feet was rounder than Elodie had ever heard it—a full, warm bronze tone, unbroken, the sound of a mechanism with nothing left to hide. Nine strokes. Each one landed like a signature.

Below in the harbor, the morning tide was turning, water sliding gold-gray past the mole where the *Coriolis* had once stood loaded with lies. Dockworkers looked up at the observatory windows—some of them the same ones who had formed the wall on the quay, shoulder to shoulder, and dared the enforcers to try—and a few lifted hands in salute.

Sable leaned on the rail beside Elodie, close enough that her voice came private under the fading hum of the bell. "So. The capital." She said it lightly, and not lightly at all. "Big city. Terrible weather, probably. Nobody who knows how to fix anything."

"Their clocks will be in appalling condition."

"Tragic. Somebody ought to go save them." She watched the harbor, jaw yellow-bruised, eyes bright. "Somebody ought to bring a dockworker who knows the way of things. And a rival with crooked presses."

"Level within a hair," Odile said from the rail's far end, not turning around.

Elodie looked out over the water, over the roofs and rigging and the slow gold spreading east, and measured it all the old way, out of habit—the weight of things, the cost, what could be carried. The workshop was Odile's now, benches and sign and all, and it would go on making clocks under a different name, which was right; names should belong to the living. The tools that mattered most fit in one case, and the case would travel. Everything else—the fear, the alias, the mentor gone down the coast road in disgrace with his confession on file and his shame in his pockets—all of that could be left standing exactly where it fell.

What remained was portable after all, Elodie thought, and lighter than expected: a name, clean; a record, sealed; three people who had decided, separately and then together, that the truth was worth the freight.

"Well then," Elodie said, and picked up the blue-sealed letter, and turned it over to write the answer on its back the way dockworkers tally cargo—plainly, where anyone could read it. "Someone had better tell the clerk we'll want the registry's first certified copy of *this* signature too."

The clock ticked on below them, steady as a promise kept, and the tide ran out shining all the way to the open sea.
