## 2. A Stranger at the Tide Gate

The fog came in off the Greyhaven channel the way it always did in the last week of autumn — not arriving so much as remembering itself into place, filling the streets from the harbor end until the whole city seemed drawn in graphite and left unfinished. From the drafting room's tall window, Petra Amberdale could see perhaps forty feet: the tide gate below, its black timber posts slick and glistening, the lamplighter already out with his pole, coaxing small orange suns into the murk one by one as though he could hold back the season by sheer stubbornness.

She did not look up when the bell at the shop door rang. Customers were rare this close to closing, rarer still in weather like this, but rare things had a way of arriving on the worst days, and she had learned long ago that the world preferred to interrupt her at the delicate parts. Her nib was mid-stroke along the coastline of the northern archipelago — the Skerries, Master Corvane had called them, though no chart in the city gave them a proper name — and the line required a steadiness of hand that seven years of apprenticeship had finally taught her body even when her mind was elsewhere.

"I'm closed," she said, without lifting the pen.

"Then I'll be brief."

The voice was wrong for Greyhaven. That was the first thing she noticed, before she even saw him. The city had a particular music — vowels flattened by salt wind, consonants softened by generations of speaking through rain — and this man's speech carried none of it. It came from across the water, from somewhere with colder winters and harder borders, and it landed in the warm little shop like a stone dropped through ice.

Petra finished her line. She let the nib rest in its well, blotted the coast with sand, and only then raised her head.

He stood just inside the door, dripping onto the flagstones in a spreading dark corolla that any other day would have earned a sharp word about the floors. He was younger than his voice suggested — thirty, perhaps thirty-two — with the lean, weather-cured look of someone who had spent more time on decks than in rooms. His coat was a good one gone bad, wool once fine now shabby at the cuffs, and under it he wore the particular posture of a man who kept track of exits. She knew that posture. She wore it herself some days.

"You're closed," he agreed, and did not leave. Water ran from the brim of his hat and pooled around his boots. "I'm looking for a mapmaker. Corvane. Master Aldous Corvane."

The name went through her like a needle finding the seam of a garment.

Seven years. Seven years since they had pulled what the sea gave back from the shallows below the lighthouse — not enough of him, never enough, the coroner had said gently, for an open casket but enough, God help them, for certainty. Seven years since Widow Amberdale had stood at the graveside in her good black dress with her hands folded like a woman waiting for a coach that would not come. Seven years since Petra had inherited a half-finished atlas, a household of strays, and a grief she had learned to fold away each morning the way one folds away bedding — neatly, efficiently, without looking at it too closely.

And here was a soaked stranger saying the name as though it were an appointment.

"There is no Master Corvane here," Petra said. Her voice came out level. She was distantly proud of that. "This is the Amberdale map house. I am the mapmaker. If you have cartographic needs—"

"I don't." He moved further into the shop, and she marked the way he did it: unhurried, deliberate, positioning himself where he could see both the counter and the door, the way sailors stand on unfamiliar ground. His eyes — grey, unglamorous, tired — swept the shelves of rolled charts, the brass instruments under glass, the great drafting table where the atlas plates lay weighted beneath linen cloths. Something in his face tightened when he looked at the table. Recognition, or its performance. "I need him."

"Then you need a man who has been dead seven years," Petra said. "Drowned. There was an inquest. I can direct you to the records office if you enjoy reading about tragedies."

*Lie smoothly*, Corvane had told her once, when she was fifteen and had misquoted a surveyor's figures to a difficult client rather than admit uncertainty. *Not because truth is cheap, Petra-girl, but because sometimes the truth in your keeping isn't yours alone to spend.* She had thought then that he meant fudging a latitude. She had learned since how large the lesson actually was.

The man studied her. She let him. Under his gaze she felt the familiar itch at the base of her skull — the awareness of everything in this room that must not be noticed — and she kept her shoulders loose and her face arranged into the mild, faintly bored expression of a tradeswoman dealing with a wasted journey.

"Dead seven years," he repeated. Not a question. A weighing.

"The seventh of Lastmoon. The sea below the old lighthouse took him during the equinox gales. They found—" She stopped herself, precisely, at the edge of the detail only family would know, the way one stops at a property line. "There is a grave in the churchyard of Saint Ferren's. You're welcome to visit it."

For a moment he said nothing. Rain worked at the windows. Somewhere down the lane the lamplighter's bell chimed his progress, a small patient sound, absurd against the weight in the room.

Then the stranger reached inside his coat.

Every muscle in Petra's body went quiet and ready. She had a letter knife on the counter, a brass rule within reach, and a heavy paper-press that could break a man's fingers if it needed to; she catalogued all three in the space of a breath, and hated, as she always did, how quickly such arithmetic came to her.

But what he drew out was folded oilcloth, tied with waxed cord, and from the oilcloth a letter. He set it on the counter between them with the care of a man laying down a card he knows wins the hand.

"Read it," he said. "Then tell me again about the grave."

She should not have touched it. Some cold, sensible part of her — the part that was Corvane's best student, the part that had survived her own childhood by learning exactly which doors stayed shut — said *don't*. Touching it made her part of whatever story this letter belonged to. Hands left marks. Involvement was a debt that collected interest.

Her fingers untied the cord anyway.

The paper was good stock, travel-worn, creased from folding and refolding. The handwriting hit her before the words did, the way a smell hits before a memory: the strong forward slant, the peculiar hooked *d* that looked always as if it were leaning into weather, the tiny precise numerals tucked into the descenders like birds sheltering under a cliff. She had spent eleven years copying that hand. She had wept over samples of it. She had inked the final atlas plates these last months deliberately altering her own natural script toward it, because the work was his and should be signed in his spirit if not his signature.

It was his hand. No forger alive could produce that hooked *d* with such unconscious ease, such fatigue in the downstrokes. She would stake every plate of the atlas on it. She would stake the house.

*Corvane,* the letter began, without honorific, as though its writer were too hurried or too proud for ceremony, and then three lines in cipher — a cipher she recognized, oh yes, the alternating substitution they had used for client corrections, childishly simple, maddeningly his — followed by a date.

The date was the eleventh of Firstharvest.

Three months after the drowning.

The floor of the world tilted, very slightly, the way a ship's deck tilts when a swell passes underneath that you cannot see. Petra became aware of her own heartbeat, of the rain, of the stranger watching her face with the attention of a man reading a chart for hazards.

"Well?" he said.

"It means nothing." She heard her own voice as if from another room — steady, cool, a professional assessing an anomaly. "Handwriting can be learned. Grief makes people credulous. Someone is selling you a dead man's letters, sir, and you've paid too much for them."

"He wrote it to me."

"People write to the dead all the time. It doesn't raise them."

His jaw tightened. "You didn't read the cipher."

"I read it," Petra said, and had the satisfaction of seeing surprise flicker across his face before it hardened again. "Three lines. Coordinates and a tide table. Which proves nothing except that whoever wrote it knew Corvane's habits — as his apprentices did, as his colleagues did, as any number of people might who wished him to be alive or wished you to believe it."

"Why would anyone wish me to believe it?"

"That," Petra said, folding the letter along its original creases with hands that wanted very badly to shake, "is the first intelligent question you've asked. And the answer is not my business."

She held the letter out to him. Every nerve in her arms screamed at her not to. Behind her calm, her mind was moving fast and low, a smuggler's boat running dark: *the date, the cipher, the coordinates — those coordinates are Skerry waters, waters only Corvane charted, waters that appear nowhere in the commercial atlas because they belong to the other work, the unnamed work, the work whose existence she had deduced years ago from gaps in the files and never spoken aloud, not even to the widow, not even to herself in the dark.*

If the letter was genuine — and the hand was genuine, the hand was *his* — then the drowning was a lie, and the grave was a lie, and the widow's folded hands at the graveside had been something other than grief, and seven years of Petra's mourning had been spent on a fiction. And if it was false, then someone across the water knew enough of Corvane's private mathematics to counterfeit them, which meant the private mathematics had leaked, which meant the *other* work was blown, which meant—

Which meant either way, the carefully mapped world she had inhabited for seven years had just developed its first true fault line, and faults, she knew better than anyone, do not stay closed once opened. They widen. They swallow roads.

The man did not take the letter.

"Keep it," he said quietly.

"No."

"Keep it." He pushed it an inch toward her across the counter, and there was something new in his voice, something that had been under the hardness all along and now surfaced: exhaustion. The bone-deep weariness of a courier at the end of a road he no longer understands. "Because you'll read it again tonight anyway. Because you'll hold it under a glass and compare the *d*s, and check the tide tables against your almanacs, and by morning you'll know what I knew by the second page. And when you know it," — he picked up his hat, and water sheeted off the brim — "I'll come back, and you'll tell me where he is."

"And if I don't?"

"Then I'll have crossed the water for nothing," he said, "which wouldn't be the first thing I've done for nothing." At the door he paused, half-turned, silhouetted against the fog behind him so that he seemed to be standing inside the weather. "Elias Vane," he said. "I'll be at the Gull and Anchor. They say it's the cheapest bed in Greyhaven. I imagine that's true."

The bell rang. The door shut. The fog leaned briefly into the shop, tasting the warmth, and withdrew.

* * *

Petra stood at the counter for a long time after the footsteps faded down the lane.

The lamp gutted once and recovered. On the drafting table behind her, the Skerries lay half-inked under their linen, a coastline waiting patiently for a hand to finish it. Through the ceiling came the muffled sounds of Sable singing wordlessly to herself in the kitchen — the cook's songs had no words, having never needed them — and beneath that, Tam's uneven footfalls as he hauled coal up the back stair, and beneath everything, the widow's clock in the front parlor marking time in its slightly limping measure, a beat late, always a beat late, like a heart that had learned caution.

Her family. Assembled, like her, from leftovers and luck and one dead man's stubborn mercy. She counted them the way she had counted them every night for seven years, a rosary of names: Sable, who spoke with her hands and fed them all like an army; Tam, who had appeared one frozen January with no history and no questions asked, because asking questions was not how this house did its arithmetic; Widow Amberdale, who had lost a husband to the sea and gained, somehow, a daughter and two strays and a boy, and who never once in seven years had let Petra see her cry.

*The house holds together*, Petra had written once, in a private notebook, *because everyone agrees never to pull on any thread.*

And now a soaked stranger had walked in out of the fog holding a thread in his fist.

She looked down at the letter, still lying on the counter where he'd left it, innocuous as an invoice. Slowly, she carried it to the lamp and unfolded it, and this time she did what she had promised herself she would not do: she fetched the loupe, the heavy jeweler's glass Corvane had bought in her third year, and she held the paper flat and examined the *d* in *dead*, the hooked *d*, leaning into weather, and the numeral 7 tucked into its descender like a bird under a cliff.

Eleven years of practice, and her own hand still could not reproduce that hook without hesitation. Hesitation was forgery's fingerprint. This letter had none.

She checked the tide tables next, because she had promised herself she would, because rigor was the discipline that kept trembling useful. The coordinates put a point two miles northeast of the Skerries, at slack water, on the night of the twelfth of Firstharvest — the night after the letter's date. A rendezvous. Whoever wrote this letter had expected to be somewhere, in the flesh, in the dark, in waters that appeared on no chart sold in any shop in Greyhaven.

The almanac confirmed the tides were correct. Correct to the minute.

Petra set down the loupe. Her hands were shaking now, openly, and she watched them shake with a strange detachment, as though they belonged to an apprentice she was responsible for training. *Steady linework*, she reminded them. *Whatever else fails, the line stays true.* But the lines were all redrawn now. Seven years of grief, carefully inked, and somewhere beneath the vellum the true coastline ran differently than anyone had mapped it.

*He's alive.*

The thought arrived whole and terrible and did not leave. Alive — and drowned in every record, buried under a stone in Saint Ferren's yard, mourned by a household that had perhaps known and perhaps not and which possibility frightened her more. Alive, and writing coded letters to strangers across the water. Alive, and hunted, or hiding, or both, because men do not stage their own deaths for reasons that keep them safe.

And this Elias Vane — Vane, of course, a name like a flag of convenience — had come to her door asking after a dead man with the persistence of a man who had been *sent*. Sent by whom? With what authority? People who wanted Corvane found fell into two kinds, and Petra had spent enough years understanding the shape of her master's secrets to know that one kind wanted to find him to embrace him, and the other kind wanted to find him to end properly what the sea had failed to finish.

She could not yet tell which kind stood dripping at the Gull and Anchor, three streets away, warming himself on cheap gin and patience.

What she could do — what she was trained to do, what she had been made for, though she'd only ever understood that in retrospect, the way one understands a map only after crossing the country it describes — was control what happened next. Control the tempo. Send him to his inn and let him wait, because waiting cost him information and cost her nothing she wasn't already losing. Give him a lie smooth enough to sail on: *no Corvane here, madam is the mapmaker, good evening.* Let him report home, if he reported anywhere, that the apprentice was ignorant, incurious, safe.

And meanwhile, tonight, while the fog thickened and Sable sang her wordless songs and Tam dreamed boy dreams under the eaves, she would go down into the archive room where the master's private papers were boxed in cedar, and she would open boxes that had been sealed for seven years, and she would begin — with a tremor she would hide the way she hid all of them — to find out what else about her master's death was a fiction.

Petra folded the letter along its creases. She did not tie it back in its oilcloth. Instead she slipped it inside her bodice, against her heart, where it sat like a coal.

Outside, the fog had swallowed the streetlamps entirely; only the tide gate's black posts remained visible, rising out of the white like the masts of a drowned fleet. Somewhere in that blindness, Elias Vane was finding his inn. Somewhere beyond the fog, past the Skerries, past the edge of every chart she had ever finished, a man with a hooked *d* and a talent for dying was still writing letters.

The bell above the door gave a soft, unprompted chime — settling, just the settling of old glass in its frame — but Petra turned toward it with her heart hammering, and for one wild moment half-expected to see the dead walk in.

Only fog. Only the ordinary, merciful, lying fog.

She banked the lamp, covered the atlas plates, and went downstairs to lie to her family about why dinner was late.
