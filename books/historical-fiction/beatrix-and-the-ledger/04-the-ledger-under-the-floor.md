## 4. The Ledger Under the Floor

The floor of the drafting room had always spoken.

Petra had learned its language as a child, before she learned her letters properly — the groan of the third plank from the hearth when the weather turned damp, the hollow knock of the boards near the north window where the joists had shrunk away from their pegs. Master Corvane had made a game of it once, when she was eleven and homesick for a home she could not remember. *Every house keeps its own map,* he had said, tapping his boot heel against the boards. *Learn to read it, and you will never be lost in the dark.* She had thought it a whimsy then, one of the small entertainments he conjured for frightened apprentices. She had not thought of it in years.

She thought of it now, at half past two in the morning, kneeling on that same floor with a lamp turned low and Elias Vane's letter folded into a square no larger than a playing card, pressed against her ribs through her shirt like something stolen.

She had come down intending to work. That was the truth of it, or near enough — she had lain in her narrow bed above the kitchen and listened to the rain combing the roof tiles, and the letter had lain on her chest of drawers with its impossible date, and sleep had become an untenable country. So she had dressed, taken the lamp, gone down the stairs she knew by heart, and sat at Corvane's old desk to ink the final plates of his atlas, because work was the only prayer she had ever learned.

But her hands would not steady. Twice she spoiled a coastline — the western shore of the Sea of Marrows, where the estuary forked like a snake's tongue — because her wrist kept that fine tremor she hid all day in long, deliberate strokes. The ink pooled where it should have flowed thin. She blotted it, cursed softly, scraped the vellum clean with a razor, and began again, and again the line wandered.

It was in setting down the razor that she heard it. Or rather, that she failed to hear it.

The third plank from the hearth had not groaned under her knee.

Seven years she had worked in this room. Seven years, and she knew the floor's voice better than her own. She shifted her weight deliberately, and there it was — the groan, faint, correct. She moved back toward the hearth, and the sound died beneath her, replaced by something else: not a groan but a knock. A hollow knock, but wrong. Not the loose rattle of shrunken joists. Something deeper. Something with air beneath it.

Petra knelt very still, the lamp guttering beside her, and let herself feel the fear first — because Master Corvane had taught her that too, though he had never named it as a lesson. *When the map surprises you,* he said, *stop drawing and start looking. The surprise is the territory telling you your assumptions were wrong.*

Her assumption, seven years deep and mortared with grief, was that this house held nothing she did not already know.

She set the lamp aside and worked by touch, running her palms flat along the boards, pressing each one, listening with her hands the way Sable listened with her eyes. Near the hearth, beneath the third plank's stubborn silence, she found it: a seam so fine her thumbnail crossed it twice before she felt it catch. Dust lay thick over the whole stretch of floor except here, where someone had recently swept — recently, because the grain still showed the faint drag-marks of a brush, and the household swept this room only on Saturdays, and today was Thursday.

Someone had opened this floor within the past five days.

The household kept its silences, and Petra had catalogued them all — the widow's avoidance of the harbor road, Tam's refusal to speak of the winter he arrived, Sable's habit of standing at windows facing the water. But there was one silence she had never questioned, because grief had sealed it shut: the silence around Master Corvane's effects. His desk untouched. His instruments in their roll of oiled leather. His room above the drying loft kept locked, dusted by the widow herself, entered by no one.

And now a floorboard, brushed clean within the week.

She fetched the tool chest from the alcove, chose the thinnest blade, and worked it into the seam. The plank resisted, then gave with a sigh of old wax, hinging upward on a leather strap she had never seen because it had been oiled to invisibility. Cold air rose out of the dark beneath, smelling of cedar shavings and tallow and something else — iron, perhaps, or the memory of iron. The cavity below was deeper than a floor had any right to be. It had been cut between the joists, boxed in with cedar, and it went down past the reach of the lamplight.

She lowered the lamp on its handle, arm straining, and saw the shape of a book.

* * *

It came up heavier than any atlas she had ever handled — a ledger bound in grey calfskin gone soft as chamois with age, its corners worn to the color of bone. No title. No clasp. Just the swelling of pages so numerous the covers would not close upon them, and a cord of plaited hemp knotted around the middle like a belt.

Petra carried it to the desk with both hands, the way one carries a sleeping child or a loaded weapon, and set it down in the circle of lamplight, and did not open it for a long moment. The rain filled the silence. Somewhere below, in the kitchen, Sable's banked stove ticked as it cooled. The house breathed around her, full of sleepers who trusted her, and she understood — dimly, the way one understands a wave has lifted the boat before one sees the shore receding — that whatever was written in this book would change what she owed them all.

She untied the cord.

The first page was a commercial ledger, or wore the face of one: shipments of copper plate, purchases of vellum, sums paid to a paper merchant in Lowbridge, all in the cramped, upright hand she knew better than her mother's — if her mother had ever written to her, which she had not. She turned the pages slowly. Accounts. Accounts. An inventory of inks with marginal notes on their lightfastness. And then, a third of the way in, the accounts stopped being accounts.

A column of figures appeared — quantities of cargo, weights, prices — but the cargoes had names that were not cargoes. *Twelve bales, grey wool, the Merrow's Daughter, tide of the dark moon.* She knew the *Merrow's Daughter*. Everyone in Greyhaven knew her; a coastal trader, unremarkable, that ran wool and salt fish to the river towns. But wool was weighed by the sack, not the bale, and no wool shipment needed a tide table annotated to the quarter hour.

She turned faster now, and the book shed its disguise the way fog sheds at noon. Pages of routes — not drawn, for there was scarcely a diagram among them, but *written*, rendered as instructions, as sequence, as grammar. *From the cooper's yard on Bracken Stair, take the stair down past the third buttress; the door is chalked with a fish. Below, follow the culvert west by torchlight until the sound of the sea comes from the left, not the right. Do not carry a lamp after the second turning.* Pages of names — some plainly written, most in cipher, initials and numerals braided together in columns that repeated and varied and repeated, a litany. Pages where a single name was struck through in red, gently, almost tenderly, and beside it a new name added in a different hand.

And threaded through everything, crowding the margins, climbing the gutters, filling the spaces between entries like ivy through a fence — annotations. Corrections. Warnings. *The Salt Gate watch changes at compline now, not matins — tell them.* *Old Nan is dead; the attic room passes to her granddaughter, who can be trusted for two years, no more.* *Do not use the Marrow culvert after the spring floods; I have redrawn it, see plate—*

See plate.

Petra's hands had gone cold. She looked at the margin note a long time without moving. Then she rose, went to the flat cabinet where the finished plates of the atlas lay stacked in their tissue, and drew out the plate for the western approaches to Greyhaven — the very plate she had been failing to finish tonight, the coast with its estuary forking like a snake's tongue. She carried it back to the desk and laid it beside the ledger, and compared.

The atlas showed a beacon. The ledger referenced a culvert. She measured with her dividers, translating scale, and set the point of the compass where the annotation said the culvert ran — and found it fell precisely where the atlas marked nothing at all. Not nothing. Worse than nothing. The atlas marked a sandbar, a shoal, a hazard to shipping — a place no boat would go, no searcher would look.

Unless one knew that the shoal was a lie.

She sat back on the stool, and the breath went out of her slowly, and for a moment the room seemed to tilt the way a deck tilts. Every plate. Her mind ran ahead of her, plate by plate, the ones she had inherited and the ones she had finished herself, working from Corvane's surveys, his notes, his maddeningly precise instructions. The sandbars off the Kindly Narrows. The drowned quay that appeared on no chart but his. The churchyard of Saint Ferrell, mapped with such loving attention to its boundary stones, though the boundaries of churchyards interested no buyer of atlases. Seven years of work assembled itself behind her eyes into a different edifice entirely, stone by stone rearranged, and she saw it now whole: not a commercial atlas, or not only, not chiefly — but a key. A map of the city's hidden arteries, drawn in plain sight, distributed into the world in fine bindings for gentlemen's libraries, waiting to be read correctly by those who held the reading.

Those who held the ledger.

And the handwriting in the margins —

She had told herself, in the first mad minutes, that these were old notes, that Corvane had kept this book for years before his drowning and simply never shown her. It was even partly true; the earliest entries were faded, browned, the ink of another era. But the margins were not old. She bent close to the lamp and looked with a craftsman's eye, and her craft told her what her heart had already begun screaming. The ink of the later annotations was fresh. Not weeks old — days. The last entry, squeezed into the bottom corner of the final page in a hand grown hurried, the downstrokes trembling where his never used to tremble:

*They are counting berths now. Three remain. Tell P. the shoals are true who know to doubt them. Keep the plates wet until the tide.*

Tell P.

The lamp flame stood perfectly still, and Petra did not. Her hand rose and laid itself flat against the page, over the letters, as if touch could test them the way it tested paper weight and vellum grain — and the paper was real, and the ink was real, and the hand was his, and the hand was *recent*, and the man whose funeral she had attended, whose grave she had weeded every Sunday for seven years, whose atlas she had given her twenties to finishing — that man had written *tell P.* within the season, within the month, perhaps within days of Elias Vane appearing soaked at the tide gate with a letter dated three months after the drowning.

He was alive. The word arrived without drama, flat and enormous, like a bell falling down a stair. He was alive, and he had let her grieve him. He had let the widow wear black for seven years. He had let Tam arrive one winter fatherless and never asked about, and let Sable cook in his kitchen, and let Petra believe that the last thing he trusted her with was his unfinished work rather than himself.

Or — and her mind, trained to hold two contradictory charts in view at once, laid the second course alongside the first — he was alive because he had to be, hunted, ruined, unable to come home, writing to her across the water the only way left to him. Drowned men do not choose their funerals. Perhaps the drowning itself had been the choice. Perhaps dying — being seen to die — was the only way a man carrying this book could stay useful to the people in its pages.

Why he had to die, or pretend to. The thought completed itself with terrible ease, and once completed could not be undone. This ledger was worth more than the map house, more than the atlas, more than any of their lives singly. It was the difference between flight and capture for every name in its columns — and Greyhaven was full of people whom Magistrate Hollis's men counted, registered, summoned, taxed into shadows. She had grown up among them without naming them. The household itself was made of them.

She looked at the struck-through names in red and wondered, with a coldness that surprised her, how many of those gentle strikings were deaths, and how many were escapes, and whether anyone would ever strike her name through in kindness.

Somewhere above her a floorboard creaked — the widow's room, turning in sleep, or Sable rising for the bread. Petra blew out the lamp and knelt in the dark, heart hammering, feeling foolish and then not foolish at all, because the ledger had been hidden from the world for good reason and she had dragged it into the light. She waited until the house settled. Then she relit the lamp, low, shielded with her cupped hand, and read until the window over the north bench turned from black to iron to the pale grey of gull-hour — read the routes, the tides, the litany of names, the margins filling with a dead man's living hand — and when at last she closed the book and knotted the cord and lowered it into its cedar dark and seated the plank and swept the dust back into its honest disorder, she knew three things with the certainty of surveyed ground.

That the atlas must be finished. That the three berths must be found and filled. And that Elias Vane had asked her for Master Corvane by name, with a letter in his hand, and she had lied to him smoothly and sent him to the Salt and Anchor inn, believing herself the only keeper of this house's silences.

She stood in the greying room, her knees aching, the letter still pressed against her ribs like a coal, and heard in memory the exact timbre of his voice asking, and understood suddenly — the way one understands a current by the way it bends the weed — that a man does not cross cold water on a rumor. He had come for the ledger. He had known to ask. And she had told the first stranger in seven years to walk straight past her door exactly where to wait while she decided what this family could afford to lose.

Outside, the fog was coming in off the harbor, swallowing the tide gate, and somewhere inside it Elias Vane was awake too.
