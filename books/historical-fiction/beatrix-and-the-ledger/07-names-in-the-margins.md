## 7. Names in the Margins

The cipher did not yield to cleverness. It yielded to patience, and patience was the one inheritance Master Corvane had left me in abundance.

For three nights after the bargain with Elias Vane, we sat in the drafting room with the ledger open between us like a body on a table — his long fingers hovering over the columns of figures that were not figures, my eyes tracing the marginalia that were not marginalia. The lamp burned whale oil down to its dregs. Rain came against the shutters in long grey sheets, the kind of rain Greyhaven saved for the end of autumn, as if it meant to wash the whole town out to sea and think better of it by morning.

"It's arithmetic," Elias said for the fourth time, tapping the page. "Look. Every entry balances. Debts, payments, interest — he built it like a merchant's book."

"He built it *disguised* as a merchant's book," I said. "There's a difference, and the difference is where the truth lives." I turned back to the earliest pages, the ones written in a hand younger and more confident than the later entries. "My master taught me that every map lies a little. The trick is knowing which lie is load-bearing."

Elias looked at me across the ledger, and for once he didn't argue. He had a face made for suspicion — hollow-cheeked, quick-eyed, a man who'd learned young that rooms have exits — but when he forgot himself, which was rare, there was something almost boyish underneath, something that wanted to be told how things worked.

"Show me," he said.

I spread my drafting tools across the table: dividers, the brass protractor Corvane had worn smooth as river stone, a rule graduated in chains and furlongs. "He was a mapmaker before he was anything else. If he hid something in numbers, he'd hide it the way a cartographer thinks — in scale, in bearing, in distance." I set the dividers to the first entry: 47.3. "This isn't money. It's a latitude fragment, or a bearing read off a compass rose, or—" I stopped.

Or it was a page number and a line.

I went still. Elias noticed; he always noticed. "What?"

"The atlas," I said. My voice came out strange, flattened, the way sound goes flat under fog. "The commercial atlas. The one I've spent seven years finishing. Forty-eight plates. He wrote the ledger *against* it. Every figure in this book points somewhere in that one."

* * *

We worked until the rain stopped and the gulls began their quarrels over the fish market refuse, and what we found in those hours I will set down plainly, because plainness is the only honest way to record a thing that rearranges the floor of your life.

The first cipher was simple once you saw its spine — most locks are, from the inside. Each ledger entry contained three numbers disguised as sums. The first gave a plate number in the atlas. The second gave a coordinate on that plate, measured in Corvane's own idiosyncratic scale, the one he'd invented for measuring coastlines no official chart bothered with. The third gave a letter-count into the plate's engraved title cartouche — those flourished banners of text that every buyer admired and no buyer ever counted.

Plate twelve. A cove two miles north of the Gullstone light, marked on the finished chart as unsafe anchorage, shoaled, avoided. The cartouche yielded letters at the given counts: *T-I-D-E-K-I-N-D-L-Y*. The Kindly Tide. A ship I had never heard of, named in a book that was supposed to be about wool tariffs and harbor dues.

"She's real," Elias said quietly. He said it the way men say a prayer they're not sure they believe in. "The Kindly Tide. She runs the southern circuit — I've seen her name on manifests in three ports. Always small cargo, always odd schedules." His jaw worked. "I thought she was smuggling brandy."

"She is smuggling people," I said, and the words tasted like iron filings.

Because here is what I understood then, sitting in my dead master's chair with his dead master's lamp guttering between us: the atlas I had mourned over, bled over, inked plate by plate through seven years of grief — it was never an atlas. Or rather, it was an atlas the way a glove is a hand. It had been shaped around something living all along, and I had been finishing its skin without ever knowing it held bones.

Every safe cove. Every unmarked channel. Every "dangerous water" that was dangerous only to magistrates' cutters because Corvane had surveyed the gaps in their patrol lines himself and drawn them closed on paper while leaving them open in fact. Seven years of plates, and each one a door.

"He was moving them out for years," I whispered. "Before the drowning. Before any of it."

Elias said nothing. He was copying the decoded entries into his neat courier's hand, and I watched him do it and felt the old animal wariness rise in me — this stranger, this bought man, holding now in his handwriting the whole architecture of my master's mercy. But we had made our treaty, imperfect as all treaties are, and a treaty is only as good as the moment you decide not to break it. I decided. Not gladly. Deciding is rarely glad work.

It was near dawn when we reached the entries that changed everything.

They came early in the ledger — pages three through nine, written in ink so dark it seemed fresher than the rest, though it must have been the oldest. Three entries, set apart, each followed by a symbol I had taken for a merchant's mark: a small anchor drawn with a curved fluke, like a bird settling on water.

"Three entries," Elias murmured. "Three berths?"

But when I ran them through the cipher, they did not decode to coordinates. They decoded to names.

* * *

The first name was *Merren Halloway*. I knew it. Everyone in Greyhaven of a certain age knew it — the chandler who had vanished eleven years ago after the Salt Riots, whose shop the magistrate seized and whose family scattered like sparrows. There was a story that he'd drowned himself off the mole. The entry beside his name gave a date and a single word decoded from the cartouche system: *SAILED*.

"He got out," I said. Eleven years dead in every mouth in Greyhaven, and the ledger said he had walked up a gangplank in a fog so thick it might have been tailored for the purpose.

The second name made Elias go pale — genuinely pale, the blood leaving his face in a tide. *Odile Vasseur.*

"You know her," I said.

"She's not a name. She's a rumor." He rubbed his mouth with the back of his hand. "In the ports south of here, they tell stories about a woman who moves papers — real ones, unforgeable, magistrates' seals and all. Half of them say she's dead. The other half say she's a network all by herself." He looked at the ledger as if it had spoken in a voice he recognized from childhood. "Corvane trained her. He put her on the Kindly Tide years ago and she became — God. She became the far shore. The whole route depends on her."

The third entry took longer, because the cipher shifted on that page — Corvane had grown more careful as the years passed, or perhaps more frightened. But dawn was coming up grey through the shutters, and my hands knew his habits better than anyone alive, and at last the third name surfaced from the numbers like a face rising through dark water.

*Tam.*

Just that. No surname. The ledger's only name without one, and beneath it, in Corvane's cramped marginal script — the same script I had seen fresh in the hidden compartment, the script of a living man — a single line:

*The boy appeared in the winter of the hard frost. No one asked where from. That is why he will be safe: no one can trace what no one has ever sought. One berth. Held in trust. Tell him nothing until the tide tells him.*

I read it twice. Then a third time, though the words did not change and neither did the cold spreading through my chest.

Tam. Our Tam. The boy who had appeared at the Amberdale door one frozen January with frostbitten ears and no history, whom Widow Amberdale had fed without questions and Sable had taught to knead bread dough and I had taught — God help me, I had taught him to grind pigments and hold a pen, because a household teaches its children what it knows. The boy who slept in the loft above the drying room and stole plums and laughed at his own jokes before he finished telling them.

A berth. Held in trust. On a ship that would sail in six weeks.

"He's a child," I said. My voice cracked on the second word, and I hated it, and let it crack anyway.

"He was a child," Elias said gently. "He's fourteen now, maybe fifteen. Old enough that the berth matters." He hesitated, and I saw him choose honesty over comfort, which I would learn was the hardest thing Elias Vane ever did. "Petra — berths on a ship like that aren't seats at a table. They're whole lives purchased in advance. Papers, passage, a name waiting on the far side. Corvane paid for this boy's escape before the boy could even read."

"Why?" The question tore out of me raw. "Why Tam? Why spend that on a stray nobody asked about?"

And then I heard myself, and stopped.

Because that was what the Amberdale house *was*. Strays nobody asked about. A widow who kept a dead husband's secrets. An apprentice bought cheap from a workhouse and never sent back. A deaf cook whose silence everyone mistook for emptiness. A boy with no past. We were all of us entries in somebody's ledger of the untraceable, and Corvane had simply done for Tam in advance what he had done for all of us in practice: held a place. Drawn a door on a map and left it unlocked.

"He promised," I said at last. "That's why. He promised a child a way out, years ago, and he wrote it down because he intended to keep it even if keeping it killed him." I pressed my palms flat against the ledger's cold page. "And now he's gone — or gone enough — and the promise has fallen to whoever inherits his ink."

"That's you," Elias said.

"I know that's me."

* * *

Widow Amberdale found us there when she brought the morning bread, because she always brought the morning bread and because she missed nothing, having spent thirty years married to a man whose profession was concealment. She set the basket down, looked at the ledger, looked at my face, and did not ask. Instead she poured tea from the pot Sable had sent up — strong, black, sweetened against the morning chill — and sat with us in the grey light like a woman attending a funeral she had long expected.

"You've found the names," she said. Not a question.

"Three berths," I said. "One belongs to Tam."

Something moved behind her eyes — grief and relief braided together, the particular agony of a mother who learns her child will be saved by being taken from her. She smoothed her apron over her knees twice, a habit she had when her hands wanted to shake and her dignity forbade it.

"He used to sit where you're sitting," she said, nodding at my chair. "Ambrose. Nights, after I'd gone up. I'd hear the pen scratching and pretend I believed him when he said it was commissions." Her voice was steady as a level. "I knew what he was keeping in this house, Petra. You don't marry a man like Ambrose Corvane and stay ignorant unless ignorance is a gift you give him. I gave him mine every day for thirty years."

"Widow—" I began.

"Hush. You'll want to tell me I didn't have to carry it alone. I did, and I did, and it's done." She looked at the ledger with an expression I couldn't read — tenderness and fury occupying the same features, like two portraits hung over one another. "Six weeks. That's what the last entry says, isn't it? Six weeks, and the Kindly Tide, and three names."

"How do you—"

"Because I dusted this room for thirty years, girl, and Ambrose talked in his sleep." She rose, gathered her basket, and paused at the door with the morning light making a thin gold seam of her silhouette. "You should eat something. Both of you. Cracking codes goes better on bread." And then, quieter, at the threshold, not turning around: "When you tell the boy — and you will have to tell him, sooner than you want — bring him to me first. Some things a household does together."

She left. Elias exhaled slowly, a man surfacing from deep water.

"There's a problem," he said, which was the most Elias thing he could possibly have said.

"There's always a problem."

"Three berths. Held in trust since before the riots." He tapped the first entry, Merren Halloway's, sailed and settled. "But look at the dates, Petra. These trusts were written eleven, ten, nine years ago. People change. Halloway may be dead in truth by now. Vasseur may not need rescue — she *is* the rescue. And Tam—" He stopped, choosing again. "Tam may not be able to leave. Not cleanly. A boy with no papers, no history, no name but the one your household gave him — getting him aboard is the hardest boarding of the three, not the easiest. Hollis's clerks will be looking for exactly that shape of passenger."

I stared at the third entry until the letters swam. *Tell him nothing until the tide tells him.* Corvane's caution, Corvane's kindness, and now a weight settling onto my shoulders with the slow inevitability of fog rolling into a harbor — because the tide was six weeks away, and tides do not wait for children to be ready, and someone would have to sit a boy down in the lamplight and say: everything you are, everything safe and ordinary and warm, has been provisional since the night you knocked on our door. Someone would have to watch his face do what faces do when the floor gives way.

Someone. Meaning me.

Outside, the gulls wheeled over the grey water, screaming their hunger at a sky just beginning to lighten, and down in the kitchen I could hear Sable's kettle beginning its low whistle, and overhead Tam's floorboard creaked as he rose to fetch water — the small sounds of an ordinary morning, each one now numbered, each one now precious, each one ticking toward a departure none of them knew was coming.

Elias was watching me. "What will you do?"

I closed the ledger. "First," I said, "we find out whether Odile Vasseur is alive. Because if she is, she holds the far end of everything my master built — and if she isn't, then three berths are about to become two."

And it was that morning, while the bread was still warm, that a knock came at the tide gate — soft, deliberate, three times — and Sable, wiping flour from her hands, opened the door to a woman in a fishwife's shawl who looked at me across the threshold with eyes I had seen before, in miniature, in the engraved cartouche of plate twelve.

"I'm told," the woman said, "that Ambrose Corvane's apprentice has been reading his mail."
