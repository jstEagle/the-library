## 8. The Magistrate's Polite Men

They came on a Tuesday, in the grey hour between the morning fog's retreat and the noon bell, when Greyhaven's streets ran with fish water and the gulls argued over the leavings of the tide. Elias saw them from the window of the map house before they reached the door — three men walking in step without meaning to, the way soldiers do, or men who had once been soldiers and now wore clerk's coats to hide it.

He knew the type. He had been the type.

The tallest carried a leather satchel under his arm, squared at the corners like a hymnal. The second was younger, with ink-stained fingers and the patient eyes of a man who copied things for a living — confessions, mostly, though he would not have called them that. The third hung back half a pace, hands folded behind him, and said nothing at all, which told Elias more than anything the others would say. Every delegation has one man whose only function is to be witnessed.

"Visitors," Petra said from the drafting table, not looking up. Her pen did not pause over the coastline she was inking. "Widow Amberdale is at the fish market. Sable is out."

"I know." He had watched all three leave within the hour, and he did not believe for a moment that it was coincidence. The widow had kissed her nephew's cheek at the door, looked down the street once — a long, measuring look, north to south, the way she read weather — and then gone about her errands as if the day were ordinary. But nothing in this house had been ordinary since the letter, and Elias had begun to understand that the Amberdales conducted their ordinariness the way other people conducted ceremonies: deliberately, with everyone knowing their part.

"Then I'll answer the door," he said.

Petra's pen stopped. She raised her head, and for a moment the lamplight caught her eyes — grey-green, tide-colored, the eyes of someone who charted coasts because she did not trust them. "You don't have to."

"I know," he said. "I'd like to."

It was not entirely a lie. It was also not entirely true, and the distance between those two things had become, over the past weeks, the country where he lived.

* * *

The knock came precisely as the youngest clerk stepped onto the stoop — a knock calibrated to the arrival, so that no time elapsed between approach and permission. Elias opened the door to three polite faces and the smell of wet wool and lamp oil.

"Good morning." The tall one smiled. It was a good smile, well-made, the kind that had been practiced in front of a mirror or learned from a superior who owned one. "Master Quill. Master Fenwick. And this is Mr. Sorrel." A gesture toward the silent man, who inclined his head by a degree. "We're clerks attached to the office of Magistrate Hollis. We've come about an atlas."

Elias leaned against the doorframe with the easy slouch of a man with nothing to hide and nowhere better to be. "You've come to the right street, then. This is a map house. We're overrun with atlases."

Something flickered across Master Quill's face — amusement, perhaps, or its professional counterfeit. "Not just any atlas. An atlas compiled by Aldous Corvane. We understand he worked here. We understand he drowned." A small pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "And we understand that before his death, he was engaged upon a work of some... ambition. A grand survey. Perhaps you've heard mention of it?"

"You understand quite a lot," Elias said. "For clerks."

"We are paid to understand, Mr.—"

"Vane. Elias Vane. Journeyman engraver. Late of Vellmarrow, if the accent troubles you." He gave them the truth in the shape of a confession, because he had learned long ago that the cheapest way to disarm suspicion was to volunteer what could be verified. His papers were genuine. That had cost him nearly everything he had left, and it occurred to him, standing in the doorway with Hollis's men smiling at him, that the documents might be worth exactly nothing now — that the men before him might hold, somewhere in their ledgers, the very authority that had issued them.

"The accent doesn't trouble us, Mr. Vane. Vellmarrow is a long way off."

"Everything is a long way off from Greyhaven."

Quill laughed — warmly, generously, a laugh meant to include everyone present — and produced from his coat a card, cream-colored, edged in the magistrate's blue. "We won't keep you. We're making inquiries up and down the harbor. When a man of Corvane's reputation dies, there are always... interested parties. Collectors. Rivals. People who invested in his work." He let *invested* sit in the damp air between them. "If such an atlas exists — even partially — the magistrate's office would consider it a matter of civic importance that it be preserved. Catalogued. Brought somewhere safe."

"Somewhere safe," Elias repeated.

"The magistrate's archive is very secure." Quill's smile widened by a hair. "Nothing kept there is ever lost again."

Behind the tall clerk, the young one — Fenwick — had drifted half a step sideways, and his eyes were moving over the doorframe, the sill, the joinery of the lintel with the absent, cataloguing attention of a man trained to notice how buildings were put together. And taken apart. Elias felt the observation land on the back of his neck like a cold fingertip. They weren't looking for the atlas in the room. They were looking for the room in the atlas.

"Corvane drowned seven years ago," Elias said. "Whatever he was working on went into the harbor with him, near as anyone can say. Widow Amberdale sold off his plates years back. You're welcome to ask her."

"Oh, we intend to." Quill tucked the card into Elias's hand, folding Elias's fingers over it with two dry, careful taps. "She'll be home directly, we expect. The market closes early when the herring run fails." Another smile. "We'll wait, I think. If it's no trouble. Mr. Sorrel gets restless standing in streets."

* * *

They waited in the front parlor, which the widow kept furnished for exactly this purpose — a room designed to be seen and never truly entered, holding a tea service that had never poured, books shelved spine-inward, a fire laid but unlit because a lit fire invited lingering. Elias made tea anyway, because refusing hospitality was itself a message, and he wanted them reading the wrong book. Petra moved through the room with the tray, serene and slightly vague, playing the apprentice who understood her station and resented it, and Elias marveled at her even while his stomach knotted. She lied the way water lay in a harbor: smoothly, at whatever depth was required, conforming to every shore it touched.

"So you're new to the household," Fenwick said. He had positioned himself near the window, ostensibly admiring the fog, actually counting windows — Elias was almost certain of it. "How long?"

"A season."

"And before that? You engraved in Vellmarrow?" Fenwick turned, mild as milk. "There was trouble in Vellmarrow, wasn't there. Some years ago. A great many people left."

"People leave everywhere," Elias said. "That's why maps sell."

The joke earned him a thin smile, and underneath the smile he watched the young clerk file the exchange away — the date, the deflection, the accent — into whatever ledger he kept inside himself. These men did not interrogate. They accumulated. Each visit added entries; each entry narrowed a route; and one morning a household woke to find that every road out of it had been quietly closed, entry by entry, without a single door ever being forced.

Quill, meanwhile, had risen to study the framed charts on the parlor wall — old ones, harmless ones, harbor surveys fifty years stale. "Beautiful work," he murmured. "The old school. Corvane trained under Hesselman, didn't he? Before the war. There's a precision in that lineage you don't see anymore." He touched the glass above a coastline, lightly, with one finger, the way a man touches a face through a shroud. "A man like that doesn't stop working just because the sea takes him. In my experience, Mr. Vane, the dead are the most industrious people in any city. They finish everything."

"That's a grim philosophy for a Tuesday."

"It's a Tuesday philosophy," Quill agreed pleasantly. "Grimness keeps the hours light."

* * *

It happened while Petra was refilling cups. Quill set down his saucer, straightened his cuffs, and said, in the tone of a man remarking on weather, "Of course, the magistrate's interest isn't purely archival. There are parties who claim the atlas isn't a survey at all. Who claim it's a ledger dressed as a survey. Routes, names, safe passages." He glanced up, benign. "Sedition, essentially. Drawn beautifully."

Elias heard his own heartbeat. Once. Twice. The room went very quiet around the sound, the way deep water goes quiet.

"That seems unlikely," Petra said, before he could find his voice. Her tone was perfect — faintly bored, faintly offended on the dead master's behalf. "Master Corvane sold coastal charts to grain merchants. He invoiced the harbormaster twice for the same vellum because he was careless, not clever. If he hid a rebellion in an atlas, it's the first thing he ever finished."

Fenwick laughed — a real one, startled out of him — and Quill shot the young man a glance that was not unkind but was definitely a warning. Then Quill returned his attention to Elias, and the warmth drained out of his face so gradually and completely that Elias understood he had been watching a performance all along, and was now watching the stagehand strike the set.

"Here is my difficulty, Mr. Vane." Quill spoke softly now, conversationally, as though the words were only for him. "My employers tell me a stranger arrived in Greyhaven some weeks ago. Ferry manifest says Elias Vane, engraver. Papers in order. Very good papers." He examined his own fingernails. "But the same employers tell me this stranger asked questions at the Tide Gate about Aldous Corvane. Asked after him *by name*, seven years dead. And then took a room at the Gull and Anchor, and visited this house daily, and has been seen — this is my favorite detail — carrying parcels wrapped in oilcloth. Heavy parcels. Map-sized."

The fire that was never lit might as well have been roaring. Elias became aware of Petra beside the tea tray, utterly still, her stillness itself a kind of speech — *say nothing, follow me, trust me* — and of the third man, Sorrel, who had not spoken or moved in an hour and whose hands remained folded behind his back as though he were holding something there, patiently, that he would very much like an excuse to use.

"Papers can be in order and still be bought," Quill continued. "That's not an accusation. Half the honest documents in this country started life dishonestly. I merely observe that you are either exactly what your papers say you are—" the smile returned, thin as foil "—or you are a thief who came to steal what my employers already own. And I am paid to determine which. So I will ask you plainly, once, and I will pretend afterward that I never asked. Where is the ledger, Mr. Vane?"

* * *

Later — much later, after the clerks had departed with courtesies and a promise to return after speaking with the widow, after the door had closed and the bolt slid home and Petra's composure finally cracked into a long exhale against the jamb — Elias stood in the middle of the parlor and understood, with the slow sick certainty of a man watching land sink beneath a receding tide, what he had spent these weeks failing to see.

His employers were not collectors. They were not rivals, not scholars, not the frightened network of sympathizers he had allowed himself to imagine when the mission grew uncomfortable enough to require imagining. The money that had bought him — cheap, they had known he was cheap, a man crossing water with one bag and no past worth mentioning — that money had come from the magistrate's office, filtered through two names he had never thought to trace because tracing names was how men like him ended up in harbors.

He had not been sent to rescue the ledger. He had been sent to destroy it. To find it, burn it, and burn, if necessary, whatever sheltered it — and he had walked into the Amberdale house carrying that commission like a lantern, eating their bread, learning Tam's letters alongside him, drinking Sable's broth, sleeping under the roof of the woman whose husband had died — or pretended to die — to build the thing Elias had been paid to reduce to ash.

"Elias." Petra was watching him from the doorway. She had watched him go white, and she had said nothing while the clerks were in the street, and now she said his name the way she drew a coastline: carefully, knowing the shape beneath. "What did they take from you, just now? I saw it land. What was it?"

"They didn't take anything." He sat down heavily on the arm of the chair that was never sat in. "They gave me something. They showed me whose signature was on my orders." He looked up at her, and found he could not manage the smooth lie, could not manage even a partial truth shaped like comfort. "I was sent to destroy it, Petra. Not save it. Not buy it. Burn it. And everyone connected to it, if burning required."

The silence held for a long moment. Outside, the fog was returning off the water, folding the street away fold by fold, and somewhere down toward the fish market a bell rang the hour — four o'clock, ordinary as bread, indifferent as tide.

Petra crossed the room and stood over him, arms folded, and he braced for the fury he deserved. Instead she said, quietly, "Do they know you've failed? Or do they still think you're theirs?"

"They think I'm theirs."

"Then," she said, and something in her face shifted — not softening, nothing so simple, but a decision being drawn in firm lines, "then as far as they know, you haven't reported yet. Which means, Elias Vane, that for the first time since you stepped off that ferry, *you* are the one holding the pen." She held out her hand. "Come upstairs. The ledger won't decode itself, and we have six weeks — no. Less than that now. They'll be back, and next time they won't bring tea."

He took her hand. It was warm, ink-stained, steady — and rising from the chair, following her toward the stairs, Elias understood that he had just chosen a side, and that the choice had been made for him weeks ago, at a table, over bread, by people who had trusted a stranger for no better reason than that he was hungry.

Above them, in the locked drawer of the drafting desk, the ledger waited — and somewhere across the grey city, in an office that smelled of lamp oil and wet wool, a clerk was already writing his name.
