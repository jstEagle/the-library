## 9. What Elias Carried Across the Water

The map house at midnight was a different country than the map house by day.

Elias had learned its geography in the dark hours, in the six days since he and Petra had struck their uneasy bargain. He knew now that the third floorboard from the hearth sang when stepped on, that the widow's rooms went quiet at half past ten but her candle burned until eleven, that Sable rose before the gulls and moved through the kitchen below like a tide no one had asked to come in. He knew the smell of the place — size-stiffened paper, tallow, the iron bite of ink, and under all of it the salt that came up through the very bones of Greyhaven as though the sea had been mixed into the mortar.

He sat across the drafting table from Petra Amberdale and watched her hands, because looking at her face while he said what he had come to say would be unbearable.

She was working on a plate. Of course she was. She seemed to work always, the way other people breathed, her burin moving through copper in fine sure strokes, and he had come to understand that the steadiness of those hands was not calm. It was discipline laid over something that shook. He recognized the architecture of it because he had built the same structure in himself, stone by stone, on a different shore.

"I'm going to tell you," he said, "what I told no one."

The burin stopped. She did not look up immediately. When she did, her eyes in the lamplight were the color of the harbor in November — grey with something green buried deep, and just as cold to swim in.

"Go on, then."

* * *

He began where all confessions begin, which is farther back than the confessor intends.

"My country you'd find on none of your charts. That's not poetry. It's simply too small, too poor, and too far east for anyone with ink to bother." He turned his cup on the table, not drinking. "There was a river dispute. There is always a river dispute; it's how borders argue when they're bored. My father farmed land on the wrong side of whatever line the last treaty drew, and one autumn soldiers came to explain to him which flag he owed his taxes to, and my father explained back, with his fists, the way he explained everything."

Petra said nothing. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the sea worked at the seawall with patient criminal hands.

"He was hanged from the bridge over our own river," Elias said. "My mother and sister took ship west within the month. I stayed long enough to bury what could be buried and burn what shouldn't be found — papers, mostly. Then I followed. I was seventeen."

"You were younger than I am now."

"Yes. And I arrived in this city with nothing but a coat that fit two boys ago and an ear for languages, which is the only inheritance my family ever left me." He smiled, and knew the smile was wrong, and let it be wrong anyway. "Do you know what a man without papers can sell in Greyhaven? His ear. His eye. His hand. His ability to stand in a room and remember everything in it. I sold all four, piecemeal, to whoever paid. I copied letters for a shipping concern. I read documents aloud to a man who could not read and pretended not to understand them. I carried messages whose contents I made myself not know, because not-knowing was worth more money than knowing."

"And eventually," Petra said quietly, "someone bought your whole self instead of pieces."

"There it is." He set the cup down. "A man called Merrow. You won't have heard the name; he keeps it behind three clerks and a wall of respectability. He buys things for other people. Information, mostly. Passages, occasionally. People who are useful and deniable." He met her eyes then, forcing himself to. "He bought me the way you'd buy a wet dog at market. Cheaply. Because no one else wanted me, and because a thing nobody wants can be pointed at anything."

"What did he point you at?"

"The atlas." The word came out of him flat as a coin laid on a dead man's eye. "He told me a story, and I want you to understand that I believed it, because believing it required nothing of me except appetite. He said Master Corvane had compiled an atlas of commercial routes — soundings, channels, harbor depths along the western coast — and that it had been stolen from his firm after Corvane's death. That they had bought the plates fair and been robbed. That a woman named Amberdale was holding stolen property and selling it piece by piece to competitors." He paused. "That if I recovered it intact, there would be passage money enough to take me home across the water."

The silence that followed was long enough to drown in.

Petra rose from the table. She did it slowly, the way a fire rises through a house — first the chair pushed back with terrible care, then the standing, then the turning away toward the window where the fog pressed against the glass like something begging to be let in. Her hands hung at her sides and Elias saw, for the first time since he'd known her, that they were trembling. Not slightly. Openly. As though the discipline that governed them had been withdrawn all at once like a tide.

"Stolen property," she repeated.

"Yes."

"You came into this house. You sat at this table. You ate Sable's bread—" Her voice cracked and she mastered it, jaw tightening, and he watched her master it and hated himself for being the reason. "You drank tea with a widow whose husband drowned for that *stolen property*, and you counted our doors."

"I did. Yes."

"And if Merrow's story had been true — if it had been only a matter of harbor soundings and shipping fees — you'd have taken the atlas and gone home." She turned from the window. "Wouldn't you?"

It would have been easy to lie. She expected the lie; her whole body was braced for it, the way a person braces for a blow they've already decided to forgive. And he understood, suddenly and completely, that whatever happened in the next thirty seconds would decide not the bargain they'd signed six days ago but the real one, the unwritten one, the one that mattered.

"No," he said.

Her eyes narrowed.

"I'd have tried," he amended, because a lie of the other kind would be worse. "I'd have told myself it was only paper. Only commerce. I'd have wrapped it up in justifications the way I've wrapped up every ugly job I've ever done, and I'd have done it, and I'd have spent the rest of my life hearing your voice asking me this exact question." He stood. "But I don't think I'd have managed it. From the first night I saw what this house is. What you keep in it. A deaf cook who reads lips through walls. A boy nobody asks about. A widow who has buried more than one husband, I think, if widowing means what I've come to believe it means here." He took a breath. "You cannot steal from a thing like this house. You can only desert it. And I have deserted enough things, Mistress Amberdale. I won't do it again."

"You don't know that I believe you."

"No. But you'll watch me, and belief will come or it won't, and either way I'll have said it plainly, which is more than anyone has ever gotten from me before."

* * *

She did not forgive him quickly. He hadn't expected her to.

What she did instead was furious in a way that had nothing to do with shouting. She pulled the ledger from beneath the floor — their ritual now, the board lifted, the oilcloth unwrapped — and slammed it open on the table between them, and began reading names aloud.

"Do you know these people?" she demanded. "Because I'm learning them. This one — Aldous Ferryman — hanged himself in the Kirkwall cells rather than name the route he used. This family, the Serrs — four names, crossed out, and Corvane wrote *taken* beside them in the margin, and I used to think *taken* meant lost at sea. It didn't, did it. These aren't cargo routes, Elias. These are people. This atlas is a door out of a burning building, and your Mr. Merrow sent you to steal the door off its hinges and give it back to the fire."

"I know."

"And Hollis—" She stopped herself on the magistrate's name the way one stops oneself on a curse. "Hollis's men pull weavers out of their shops for having the wrong grandfather. They took the Rennick brothers in March. Nobody's seen the Rennick brothers since March. And you stood in my kitchen and let Sable pour you tea."

"Sable," Elias said carefully, "pours tea to everyone she hasn't yet decided about. I noticed. I also noticed she decided about me somewhere around the fourth day, and I still don't know which way."

Something flickered at the corner of Petra's mouth — not quite humor, but the ghost of where humor lived in her. It died fast.

"You should have told me from the first night."

"Yes."

"Why didn't you?"

And there it was, the true question, and he sat back down slowly because the answer required sitting. "Because telling the truth has never once in my life made me safer," he said. "I have survived seven years on the principle that everyone lies, that lying is simply the tax you pay for breathing, and that the man who tells the truth first is the man who hangs first at the border. I looked at you — a girl holding a dead man's atlas together with her own two hands, guarding a house of strays with a ferocity I've only seen in soldiers — and every instinct I own said: *this one would kill you for the truth, and be right to.*"

"So you lied."

"So I lied beautifully. I lied the way I've lied to customs men and press gangs and two wives of two merchants whose husbands wanted better spies than honesty could buy." He spread his hands. "And then I watched you lie to Widow Amberdale about why you were awake at three in the morning. I watched you lie to Tam about why he couldn't play on the harbor wall anymore. I watched you lie the way I lie — smoothly, instantly, out of love rather than greed — and I thought: *she knows the shape of my survival. She's worn it.*"

Petra had gone very still.

"Don't," she said.

"I'm sorry?"

"Don't flatter me by comparison. Don't make us mirrors." But her voice had changed key, and they both heard it. "You don't know what you're talking about."

"Then tell me I'm wrong. Tell me you weren't taken in as a child. Tell me the Amberdales found you in a cradle with a christening blanket and a legal name." He kept his voice low, almost gentle, and hated the gentleness even as he used it, because gentleness was a tool too and he had never owned a tool he didn't use. "Tell me you've never once lied to protect someone in this house and hated yourself for how good at it you'd become."

The lamp guttered. Outside, a ship's bell tolled the half hour, muffled by fog, counting time for a city that mostly ignored it.

"I was eight," Petra said at last, and her voice was the flattest he had ever heard it, flat as surveyed ground. "My mother left me at the tide gate with a chart case strapped to my back and instructions not to follow her. I never learned if the instructions were mercy or annoyance. Master Corvane found me trying to draw the harbor from memory because I thought — I genuinely thought — that if I drew it well enough, someone would pay me enough to eat. He laughed. First thing I ever saw him do. He laughed and said, *You've put the sandbar where the channel goes, child; you'll drown honest sailors,* and he brought me home to feed me, and I never left." Her hands had gone quiet on the table. "So yes. I know exactly what it costs to arrive nowhere with nothing. I know what a household of strays is, because I am the founding stray. And that is precisely why I am so angry at you — not because you lied to me, Elias Vane. I'd have forgiven the lie by morning. But because you believed Merrow's story about this house. Stolen goods. Hidden treasure. A widow hoarding value." Her eyes came up and they were bright and terrible. "You looked at the only kind of home I've ever known and you saw *inventory.*"

The words landed in him like stones dropped down a well, and he waited a long time for them to hit bottom, and they never did.

"You're right," he said.

"I know I'm right."

"And I'll carry it. Not as penance — I don't believe in penance, it's a luxury for people with fixed addresses. I'll carry it the way I carry everything. Practically." He leaned forward. "Here is what I can do with what I am, Petra. I know Merrow's methods, his couriers, his schedule for reports. I know how his clerks verify a recovery, which means I know exactly what a forged destruction report must contain to satisfy them. I know how Hollis's people ask questions because I've been the man asking them. Everything I did before I walked through your door — every lie, every theft, every piece of myself I sold off cheap — I can spend it now against the people who taught me to do it. That's the only apology I have that's worth anything. Not remorse. Use."

Petra studied him for a long moment. The fog had thickened against the window until the world beyond the glass was only lamplight and suggestion, and inside the circle of light the ledger lay open between them with its coded routes and crossed-out names, the dead and fled testimony of everyone who had ever trusted her master.

"If you betray us," she said, "I will not scream and I will not run. I will simply know, the way Sable knew by the fourth day, and I will put you where you can't hurt this house. I've been learning where such places are. Corvane's margins are educational."

"I believe you," Elias said, and discovered that he did, and that believing it felt strangely like safety.

"Good." She pulled the lamp closer and turned the ledger toward him, and her hand — steady again, disciplined again, but he would never again mistake the steadiness for peace — tapped a page dense with cipher. "Then start here. Third section. If your Mr. Merrow's clerks need convincing paperwork, I want to know what convinces them, and you're going to teach me while we break this together." A pause. "Alliance terms. Final offer."

"Alliance," Elias agreed.

They bent over the page together, two liars and their shared alphabet, and outside the fog rolled in off the water carrying the smell of the sea and the far-off clang of the harbor bell, and neither of them heard the stair creak, or saw the thin line of candlelight under the door where someone had been standing, listening, for some time.

Above them, in her room, Widow Amberdale blew out her candle at eleven o'clock exactly, as she always did, and sat in the dark smiling like a woman whose last trap had finally caught what it was baited for.
