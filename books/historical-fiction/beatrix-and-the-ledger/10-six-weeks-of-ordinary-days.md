## 10. Six Weeks of Ordinary Days

There is a particular quality to a morning that you do not yet know is numbered. It arrives like any other: gulls arguing over the fish market scraps, fog pressing its grey cheek against the windows of the Amberdale map house, the smell of Sable's bread climbing the stairs like something alive and looking for a warm place to sleep. I woke into it on the first of those six weeks without knowing it was the first, and that is perhaps the only mercy the season granted us — that none of us could count the days as they spent themselves.

I had begun keeping two ledgers.

The first lay open on my drafting table, innocent as a hymnbook: corrections to the coastal plates, tide tables for the atlas, a note about the smudge on plate fourteen that Master Corvane would have made me burn and begin again. The second lived beneath the loose board under my stool, wrapped in oilcloth, and it held the decoded fragments — one route, then two, then the beginnings of a third — each name and passage coaxed out of Corvane's cipher by lamplight while the household slept. By day I was an apprentice finishing her master's commercial atlas. By night I was reading his true testament, one page ahead of my own fear.

The cipher was beautiful. That is the thing no one warns you about secrets built by careful hands: they are lovely from the inside. Corvane had hidden his routes in the marginalia of ordinary navigation — soundings that were not soundings, bearings that pointed at nothing in the sea but everything in the city. A depth of seven fathoms meant the seventh house on a street; a shoal marked with a particular hatching meant a cellar door that opened onto another man's property. He had written a map of mercy disguised as commerce, and he had hidden it inside my work, because he had known — even then, even before the drowning — that if anything happened to him, the only person patient enough to finish the plates would be me.

Some mornings I hated him for that confidence. Most mornings I simply missed him, which is its own kind of hatred when it has nowhere to go.

* * *

Elias Vane had been in the house nine days when the rhythm settled over us like weather. He slept in the narrow room off the chart room that had once stored paper stock, and he had learned — with the quickness of a man who had survived by learning — the household's small laws. Sable's kitchen was sovereign territory; you entered only to fetch, never to advise. Widow Amberdale took her tea at six and did not speak before the second cup. And I did not like to be watched while working, though Elias watched anyway, in the sideways manner of a man cataloguing exits.

We tested the cipher pages in the afternoons, when the light through the south windows went honey-colored and honest. I would spread a plate proof across the table — some innocuous stretch of coastline north of Greyhaven — and Elias would read the ledger's coded column aloud in a murmur, and together we would hunt the correspondence between what the map pretended to be and what it actually said. He was good at it. Better than I wanted him to be. There is a discomfort in discovering that a stranger's mind fits alongside yours the way two plates of a fold-out map fit — precisely, and with a crease where they meet.

"The Saltrow tenements," he said on the fourth day, tapping a hatched line. "This isn't a channel. Channels don't dead-end behind a tannery."

"It's a path," I said. "Roofline to roofline. You'd cross at night, when the tanyard dogs are fed and stupid."

"You've walked it?"

"I've drawn it." I heard how that sounded and did not correct it. Some part of me had walked every route in that ledger, night after night, in the country behind my eyes where the ink was still wet.

Elias looked at me for a moment longer than the sentence required. Then he wrote *Saltrow — roof crossing* in his careful foreign hand, and we went on to the next line, and the fog outside thickened, and the kettle sang in the kitchen, and it was almost possible to believe we were only two cartographers at a table, doing what cartographers do.

Almost. Because every decoded route had to be tested against the living city, and so every afternoon ended the same way: one of us walking down to the harbor with a market basket or a mended net or some other prop of ordinariness, counting doors, confirming that the seventh house still stood, that the cellar door had not been nailed shut, that the world Corvane had mapped from exile still matched the world as it was. We took turns. We never went together. Two people from the same house lingering at the same corners is a pattern, and patterns are what Magistrate Hollis's clerks were paid to find.

The harbor gave us nothing those first weeks — or rather, it gave us its usual everything, and we had to read it carefully to know whether anything had changed. The fishing fleet came and went. The customs skiff made its rounds with the same bored diligence. Coal barges, timber rafts, a brig out of the southern ports riding high and hungry. But I began, in that season, to watch the harbor the way Sable listened to a room — not to what was said, but to what was absent. Where were the navy's tenders? Why was the pilot boat repainting its hull in a week when it usually took a month? Small silences, arranged around a larger one. I wrote them in the second ledger and tried not to hear them accumulating.

* * *

It was in those weeks, too, that I taught Tam to read charts.

He was eleven that autumn, or twelve — he did not know, and none of us asked, because asking questions about Tam's before was against the household's unwritten law. He had appeared one hard winter with frostbitten ears and an appetite like a debt collector, and Widow Amberdale had fed him once, then twice, and by spring he slept in the box room and called the widow nothing at all and everyone else by their names as if he had always had us. That is how the Amberdale house assembled itself: not by decision but by drift, the way driftwood builds a breakwater.

He came to the drafting table because there was nowhere else to put him on rainy afternoons, and I set him to copying coastlines as children have been set since maps were invented. But the ledger sat beneath my feet like a second heartbeat, and one afternoon — I still cannot say whether it was kindness or preparation, and I have stopped pretending there is a difference — I turned the practice sheet over and drew him a proper chart instead. Greyhaven harbor. Buoys, channels, the tide gate, the lighthouse standing its lonely vigil on the point.

"Read it to me," I said.

"That's easy." He traced the fairway with a grubby finger. "Deep water here. Rocks there. That's the tide gate, and that's old Hollow Light."

"Depth marks. Good. Now — if you were coming in at night, in a small boat, in fog, where would you go?"

He frowned at it, tongue between his teeth. "The fairway. It's deepest."

"Deepest is where the customs men look. Try again."

I watched him work it, and I will not lie: my heart conducted its own private weather while he did. Because this was not a lesson, whatever I told myself. This was a boy whose name was written in a dead man's margins, being taught the shape of the water he might have to cross in the dark while men with lanterns searched for him. Every child should know how to read a chart, I told myself. Every child in this house especially. The reasoning was sound. The reasoning was a coat I wore so I would not feel the cold underneath.

"The creek," Tam said at last, pointing to the shallow inlet east of the gate. "It dries out at low water, so boats run aground, so nobody patrols it. But a flat-bottomed skiff could come in on the flood tide and sit in the mud till dark."

I let the silence sit for exactly as long as Master Corvane would have let it sit.

"That," I said, "is the best piece of navigation anyone has shown me all year."

He glowed like a signal fire. And then he leaned over the chart again, absorbed, and I looked at the top of his head — the cowlick, the salt-and-pepper of winter nits he'd never quite been rid of, the ordinary miracle of it — and thought: *your name is in the ledger, Tam. Third page, third berth, in a hand you will never see.* Three berths on a ship called the Kindly Tide, promised years ago by a man who drowned, to a boy who didn't even know he needed saving. I had six weeks to make sure the promise kept itself.

"Again," Tam said, pushing the chart toward me. "Do the north shore. Harder."

So I did the north shore. And outside, the rain came down on Greyhaven like a curtain drawn between two acts, and neither of us knew which act we were in.

* * *

Sable's birthday fell in the third week, on a Thursday that smelled of herring and woodsmoke, and the whole house bent itself around the celebration the way iron filings bend around a magnet.

No one knew how old she was. Sable herself, when asked, would hold up one hand and rock it — *somewhere between* — and laugh her silent laugh, which was more felt than heard, a shaking of shoulders and a crinkling of the eyes that always pulled the room after it. She had been deaf since a fever in girlhood, or so the widow said; she spoke rarely, in a voice roughened by disuse, and she signed in a private language she had built across thirty years with the widow, hands moving quick as finches. She ran the kitchen the way a harbormaster runs a harbor: absolutely, invisibly, and with total knowledge of everything that entered.

Widow Amberdale declared that a woman who refused to state her age deserved a cake of correspondingly uncertain magnitude, and so Sable baked — over her own vigorous objection, signed in furious little circles — the largest cake I had ever seen, and then presided over its distribution with the grim satisfaction of a general accepting surrender. Tam ate three slices and was sick quietly behind the coal shed, a development he attempted to conceal and failed. Elias, who had been with us barely a fortnight and who I suspect had not sat at a celebratory table in years, held his single slice as though it might be confiscated, and ate it slowly, and looked at the candle smoke on the ceiling as if it were writing something there he needed to memorize.

And I — I sat in the warm clutter of that kitchen, flour dust settling, the widow telling the story of the great pie disaster of some fifteen years past with escalating inaccuracy, and I felt the thing I felt most dangerously in those weeks: happiness. Not the loud kind. The quiet, structural kind, the kind that gets into the joists of a house. And underneath it, steady as a sounding line dropped into black water, the knowledge of the expiration date. Six weeks minus nineteen days. Every ordinary hour now came stamped, the way Corvane's charts bore the map house's seal. *This sweetness certified genuine. Best before.*

Sable caught me looking at nothing. She was across the table, mid-laugh at something the widow had signed, and yet her eyes found mine and held them, and for a moment the noise of the party seemed to pass around us like current around a stone. She tilted her head. She signed something small and swift — I knew only a handful of her signs, borrowed from the widow's vocabulary across the years — and I caught maybe half of it: *eat*, and *now*, and something I did not know but understood anyway, the way you understand a tone.

*Be here,* I think it meant.

I ate my cake. I laughed at the pie story. I was here. And the whole time, beneath the table, my foot rested on the floorboard that hid the ledger, and I could not have said whether I was guarding it or leaning on it.

* * *

The arguments started in the fourth week, which is how I knew the pressure was finding us.

They were small arguments, the domestic kind, but they arrived with unusual sharpness. Tam left brine-stained boots on the drafting room stairs and I snapped at him with a violence the offense did not warrant. The widow and I quarreled over whether the atlas's northern plates should use the older soundings or the new survey — a technical dispute that somehow acquired, by the end of the evening, the emotional tonnage of a war. Elias moved a stack of plate proofs from the chart room to the parlor and I moved them back, and he looked at me with an expression I chose not to translate.

"We're fighting about shelving," Elias observed mildly, on the fifth such evening.

"We're fighting because it's raining."

"It's been raining since I got here."

"Then we're practicing."

He almost smiled at that, and I almost threw an eraser at him, and both of us understood the truth underneath, which was this: fear needs somewhere to go, and a household is a convenient drain. We could not fight Hollis yet. We could not fight the waiting. So we fought each other in miniature, safely, the way a boiler vents through a whistle, and every night Sable fed us all as if feeding were a form of forgiveness, and every morning we woke and did it again.

But between the arguments, the work went forward with a strange tenderness. Elias finished decoding the third route and immediately began the fourth without being asked; I noticed he had taken to leaving the decoded pages on my side of the table, never his, so that possession of the dangerous papers would fall to me alone if anyone came. The widow walked the fish market every morning and came back with cod and gossip in equal proportion, and twice she mentioned, in her scattered way, that the harbor master's office had new clerks, young ones, very polite, very curious. I added it to the second ledger. The second ledger was becoming a history of our caution, and caution, I have since learned, reads exactly like dread when you review it later by candlelight.

* * *

On the last evening of the sixth week — though I did not know it was the last; the calendar of those days only revealed itself afterward, the way a reef reveals itself to a sinking ship — I sat up late finishing plate twenty-one, the final plate, the one Master Corvane had left blank except for a corner inscription in his cramped, beautiful hand: *For the ones who follow.*

The house was asleep. Rain worked softly at the glass. My lamp made its small golden territory on the desk, and inside it I engraved the last lines of the last coastline, and when it was done I sat back and looked at the completed atlas — all of it, bound proofs stacked and squared, a book that could pass any inspection in the kingdom — and I felt the pride and the grief arrive together, twins, holding hands.

Beneath the floorboard, the second ledger waited. I lifted it out and set it beside the atlas, the true book next to the false-fronted one, and I did what I had done every night for six weeks: I checked the cipher pages against the routes, confirmed the berths, counted the names. Three names. Three berths on the Kindly Tide, sailing soon, watched waters. Tam. A name I had not yet fully deciphered, its letters stubborn in the margin. And the third berth's name, which I had saved for last every night, the way you save the hardest sounding for the end of the day — because the cipher around it was dense, layered, guarded more carefully than anything else in Corvane's hand, as if he had hidden that one name deeper than the others on purpose.

That night, for the first time, the letters resolved.

I stared at them. I sounded them out again, the way I had sounded out letters for Tam at this very table. I checked the key twice, three times, my hands gone strangely cold in the warm room.

The third berth on the Kindly Tide belonged to someone who had been sitting across from me at supper every night for six weeks. Someone whose silences I had catalogued, whose signs I had only half-learned, whose birthday we had celebrated with a cake of magnificent and disputed size.

Someone who, I suddenly understood with the certainty of a plumb line finding bottom, had been watching me decode the ledger for far longer than six weeks — and who had never once let her face show it.

Downstairs, very faintly, came the sound of the kitchen door easing shut.
