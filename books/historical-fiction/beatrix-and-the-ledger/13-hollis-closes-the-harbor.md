## 13. Hollis Closes the Harbor

The notice went up on the harbor wall on a Tuesday, nailed at each corner by a man whose hammer rang against the old stone like a bell calling the faithful to something other than prayer. Widow Amberdale heard it from the fish market, where she had gone to buy cod she did not need and to speak to people about nothing in particular, which was, these days, the most important work she did.

She let the crowd carry her toward the wall. She let her shoulders stoop. She let her hand tremble as it rose to shade her eyes — a tremble she had perfected over forty years of being underestimated, first as a merchant's daughter, then as a mapmaker's wife, and now as an old woman whom nobody troubled to watch.

HARBOR INSPECTION ORDER, the notice read. BY COMMAND OF MAGISTRATE HOLLIS. ALL VESSELS SUBJECT TO SEARCH. REWARD OFFERED FOR INFORMATION LEADING TO THE RECOVERY OF CERTAIN SEDITIOUS DOCUMENTS.

Below that, in smaller print, the reward: fifty crowns. Enough to keep a family fed for two years. Enough to turn a hungry neighbor into an informer, a debtor into a traitor, a nephew into — but she did not finish that thought. She had learned long ago not to finish the thoughts that frightened her most. Unfinished thoughts could be set down like heavy bundles and picked up later, when one had the strength.

*Fifty crowns,* she thought instead, and almost laughed. Corvane had drawn coastlines worth more than the whole city and given them away to strangers who needed them more. He would have found the price of his atlas insulting.

"Terrible thing," said the fishwife beside her, not quietly enough. "Searching honest boats."

"Hush, dear," the widow murmured, loud enough for others to hear, and patted the woman's arm with her papery hand. "You know how the magistrates worry. Old men worry so." She smiled the vague, drifting smile she kept folded in her pocket alongside her shopping list, and shuffled on toward the herring stalls.

Behind her faded eyes, she counted. Three clerks copying names at the tide gate. A new skiff flying the magistrate's pennon, patrolling between the moles. And the notice itself — the wording mattered. *Seditious documents*, plural, unnamed. Hollis did not know precisely what he was looking for yet. That was worth knowing. That bought them days.

She bought her unneeded cod. She paid with small coins counted out slowly, apologizing twice for her slowness, and while the fishmonger wrapped the parcel she mentioned, in the idle way of the very old, that her niece's boy was sailing Thursday on the *Gull's Fortune* and wouldn't it be a shame if the inspections delayed him, he was such a nervous traveler. The fishmonger, who owed her thirty years of quiet kindnesses and knew exactly what her rambling meant, nodded sympathetically and said he'd tell his brother on the wharf, who'd be sure to mention it to the *Gull's Fortune*'s master, who might do well to sail Wednesday night instead.

The warning would travel that way — fishmonger to wharfman to captain — unwritten, deniable, dissolved into the ordinary gossip of the market like salt into broth. It was how she had passed word during the bread riots, during the press-gang years, during every season when Greyhaven's powerful decided that some of its people were inconvenient. Her husband had drawn the routes; she had carried the whispers. The ledger under the floor had never surprised her. She had only ever wondered what had taken Corvane so long to build it.

* * *

At home, the house smelled of ink and frying onions, and Petra was hunched over the drafting table with the particular rigidity she wore when she had been working too many hours — spine straight as a plumb line, pen moving in strokes so controlled they looked carved rather than drawn. The girl grieved with her hands, the widow had observed. When the sorrow got too large she made it smaller by making lines exact.

Elias sat across from her, cutting quills. He had been cutting quills for an hour, far more than any household needed, because quill-cutting was what he did when he wanted to be near Petra without speaking. The widow had raised three children of her own, buried two, and watched a great many hearts in her kitchen, and she knew courtship when it took the form of feather shavings scattered across a table like pale snow.

"The harbor's closed," she announced, setting down her cod.

Petra's pen stopped mid-stroke. "Closed?"

"Inspected. Every hull, every hold, every passenger questioned at the tide gate. And there's a reward now." She lowered herself into her chair by the stove, letting the effort of the market show in her face, though the effort had been mostly theater and the tiredness mostly real. "Fifty crowns for seditious documents. Unnamed. Which means our good magistrate knows there is an atlas and does not know its face."

"He'll know it soon enough if we're careless," Elias said. He had gone still over his quills — the stillness of a man recalculating, the widow noted, not the stillness of a man panicking. Whatever else the stranger was, whatever he had been sent here to do, fear did not rule him. That reassured her more than his charm ever had.

"Then we will not be careless," she said. "But hear me, both of you. The reward changes things. Fifty crowns buys a great many silences, and silence has never held a fixed price in this city. Anyone we have spoken to, anyone who has seen the girl's plates drying in the window—" she gestured at the finished engravings pinned along the sill, beautiful and damning, "—is now holding fifty crowns in their hands and calling it loyalty."

"We've spoken to no one," Petra said.

"You've spoken to everyone," the widow corrected gently. "You buy bread. You buy candles. You laugh with the chandler's daughter. Loyalty is a muscle, child, and hunger is very good at atrophying it. We must assume the walls have ears that are counting coins."

Silence settled over the kitchen, broken only by Sable stirring something in the corner, the wooden spoon knocking the pot's rim in the little rhythm the cook used to say *I am here, I hear everything* without saying it at all. The widow caught the older woman's eye and received back a look that contained an entire conversation: *How bad? Worse. Then we move faster.* Twenty years of shared cooking had given them a language richer than speech.

"Grandmother," Petra said — she had called her that since her second winter in the house, though no blood connected them, and the widow had privately decided that blood was the least reliable ingredient in any family — "you shouldn't go to the market again. Not until we understand what Hollis knows."

The widow looked at her for a long moment. Then she began, deliberately, to laugh — a cracked, wheezing old woman's laugh, and she let it run until Petra's ears went pink with confusion.

"Oh, child." She dabbed at her eyes. "You think they'd suspect me? That is precisely why it must be me. Let me show you something."

* * *

Magistrate Hollis's clerks came the following afternoon, as she had known they would, because the reward notice had named the Amberdale map house in the third paragraph — *premises formerly associated with the deceased* — and Hollis was thorough the way dull men are thorough, grinding forward without imagination but also without pause.

There were two of them: a senior clerk with ink-stained cuffs and a junior one carrying a leather folio, both standing in her parlor among the doilies and the daguerreotype of her dead sister, both wearing the particular discomfort of young men who had not expected their administrative careers to include confronting the elderly.

"Madam Amberdale." The senior clerk bowed. "Forgive the intrusion. We are conducting inquiries regarding your late husband's professional papers."

"Corvane?" She said the name the way she always said it now, with a soft, distant wonder, as if retrieving it from very deep water. "My husband has been dead seven years, young man. Drowned off the mole in the autumn storm. They never even found—" she paused, pressed her hand to her collarbone, and let her voice wobble. "They never found the body. But drowned men rarely come home whole. I have made my peace with the sea."

"Of course, madam. Our condolences." The clerk shifted. "We understand your husband was compiling an atlas. A survey of the northern coasts. We are attempting to locate his drafts, his notes — anything of scholarly value that might have been left behind."

"An atlas," she repeated, and blinked at them slowly. Then she brightened, terrible and sweet. "Oh! You mean the maps! Yes, he drew maps, such maps. Little ones, big ones. He used to pin them all over this room, and I would dust around them for years." She chuckled and reached for the teapot, pouring with a hand that shook just enough to make the cups rattle. "Would you like milk? I forget. Do I know you, young man? Have you been here before?"

"We haven't met, madam."

"That's what my niece says too, and then it turns out I baptized her eldest." She laughed at her own foolishness and pushed the cup toward him. "Drink. You're thin. All you official men are thin; it's the sitting."

While the senior clerk drank tea he did not want, the junior one drifted toward the hallway, toward the stairs, and the widow watched him in the polished surface of the teapot, her mind running quick arithmetic beneath her smiling vacancy. The drafting room was upstairs. The floor compartment was sealed and carpeted. Petra's false-bottomed portfolio stood in the wardrobe. There were risks, but she had prepared the ground for weeks — every surface up there arranged, every loose plate hidden, the room dressed as a shrine to a dead man's commerce: charts of shipping lanes, tariff tables, half-finished commissions for grain merchants.

"May we examine his study?" the senior clerk asked. "We will disturb nothing."

"Study? Oh, you may look at anything, dear, if it helps you find his lovely atlas. He worked so hard on it." She rose with visible effort, gripping her cane, and led them up the stairs at a pace designed to test the patience of saints. On the landing she stopped to catch her breath three times and once to point out, at length, a water stain shaped like a rabbit. "He saw it too. He said the house was haunted by a very shy rabbit." She giggled. The junior clerk's jaw tightened pleasantly.

In the drafting room they searched for the better part of an hour while she sat in Corvane's old chair, hands folded, occasionally offering commentary. "That drawer sticks. No — the other way. Those are invoices from 1841; I keep meaning to burn them, he never threw anything away." The invoices were genuine. So were the grain merchant's commissions. Deceit, she had found, was easiest when nine parts true.

They found nothing, because there was nothing to find, and when they came down again the senior clerk wore the expression of a man composing a report that would disappoint his superior.

"Madam, if you recall anything — papers arriving unexpectedly, strangers asking after your husband's work—"

"Strangers ask after everything these days," she said vaguely. "Yesterday a man asked if I wanted my chimney swept. Was that what you meant?"

"No, madam."

"Well. If a stranger brings me an atlas, I shall be sure to send for you." She smiled her drifting smile and showed them out with many small courtesies, and stood waving from the doorway until they turned the corner, and only then let her hand fall and her face settle into its own true lines — which were, if anyone had cared to look, not frail at all.

* * *

That evening she walked to the fish market again, leaning on her cane, wrapped in a shawl, an old woman taking the air. Marta the herring wife saved her the best of the catch and complained loudly about the price of salt. Two stalls down, a boy was selling cockles and singing to himself, off-key. Nobody looked at them twice.

"Marta," the widow said, "my joints say rain coming. Terrible weather for boats."

"Aye," Marta agreed, wrapping the herring. "My cousin's lad says the *Kindly Tide*'s master is fretting over the inspections. Says he may delay his sailing till the officials grow bored. These things pass, he says. Officials always grow bored eventually."

"Do they," the widow said. "Well. You tell that master that boredom is a luxury, and the sea keeps its own schedule. Some cargo doesn't wait well. Perishables." She tapped her nose. "Fish spoils. People too."

Marta's hands did not pause in their wrapping, but something moved behind her eyes — acknowledgment, receipt, transmission guaranteed. By morning, the message would reach the network through channels Hollis's clerks could not imagine: fishwives and guttersmen and a lighthouse keeper's daughter, all of them bound to the widow by decades of small mercies, none of them holding anything written down, none of them worth fifty crowns to anyone because none of them existed, officially, at all.

She walked home slow, because slow was her costume, and thought about Corvane as she went. Seven years of believing him at the bottom of the harbor, and grief had become to her a coat she wore indoors, comfortable as an old slipper — and now it turned out the coat had been lined all along with secrets, that her careful mourning had been the final and finest layer of his disguise. She ought to be angry. Perhaps she would be, later, when there was time. For now there was only this fierce clarity: he had spent his life buying strangers their futures, and she would spend her remaining years making sure the bill got paid.

At the door, Petra met her, anxious. "Two clerks came while you were out—"

"I know. I served them tea and confused them thoroughly." She handed over the herring. "One of them looked in the wardrobe, dear. Do stop leaving your cipher pages in your boot."

Petra went pale, then went crimson, then — the widow noted with satisfaction — went steady. The girl was learning. Grief and fear made fine teachers, but so did an old woman's patience.

Inside, the kitchen was warm, and Sable knocked her spoon twice against the pot — *all clear* — and Elias was cutting quills again as though the harbor weren't closing around them like a fist.

It was later, hanging her shawl on its peg, that the widow noticed the letter on the hall table. Grey paper, salt-damp at the corners, no seal. And on its face, in a hand she had known for forty years — thinner now, hurried, the letters leaning as if written by someone walking —

a second letter that should not exist, addressed not to Petra, not to the household,

but to *her*.
