## 14. Tam Runs

The soldiers came into the Salt Quarter on a Tuesday, when the fish carts were still slick from the morning market and the fog had not yet burned off the harbor. Tam saw them first from the roof of the cooperage, where he went sometimes to be alone, because a roof was the only place in Greyhaven where nobody could ask him where he had come from.

There were twelve of them. He counted twice, because counting was the one thing Master Corvane had taught him that never failed to steady his hands. Twelve soldiers in magistrate grey, moving down Brine Street in pairs, their boots striking cobbles in a rhythm too neat for the quarter. They stopped the fishwives. They stopped the rope-walk men. One of them held up a sheet of paper, and even at that distance Tam knew what it was without reading it — he had learned the shape of official paper the way other children learned the shapes of clouds. It was the paper that meant *someone is being looked for*, and the looking had come close enough to touch.

He should have gone home. That was the thought he would carry afterward, turning it over like a coin worn smooth: *I should have gone home.* Home was four streets east, behind the blue door on Ledger Lane, where Widow Amberdale kept the kettle always full and Sable hummed songs she had never once heard aloud. Four streets. He had run farther than that before, in another life, in a winter he did not talk about.

But his feet took him the wrong way. His feet took him down the cooperage ladder and along the quay, because he had seen something else from the roof, something that mattered more than sense: two of the soldiers had peeled off toward the tide gate, and the tide gate was where the small boats landed, and if they were watching the tide gate then they were watching for people arriving or leaving, and either way it concerned the house on Ledger Lane, and Tam Amberdale — for he had given himself the surname himself, quietly, the way you pocket something that isn't yours — Tam was not going to let anything concern that house without warning it first.

So he ran.

* * *

Petra saw him cross the Fish Gate square at half past ten, moving with the particular speed of a boy who does not want to look like he is running. She was coming back from the paper merchant with a roll of rag stock under her arm, and she had learned long ago to read the streets of Greyhaven the way she read a plate engraving — every line meaningful, every shadow placed. So she read Tam at a glance: the too-bright eyes, the set of his shoulders, the way he touched the wall at the corner as though the wall might steady him.

She also read what he was running from, because the square had gone quiet in the way squares do when soldiers enter them, and two figures in magistrate grey stood at the mouth of Cooper's Row with a list between them.

*Tam,* she thought, and the name went through her like a nail driven flush. *Tam, don't.*

But he had already seen her, and seeing her changed his course — she watched it happen, watched the boy swerve toward her the way water finds a channel it trusts. He reached her breathless, grabbing her sleeve, and for a moment he was eight years old again instead of nearly thirteen, all the winter he'd arrived in written across his face.

"They're at the tide gate," he said. "Two of them. And there's more by the fish market, asking after papers, asking who comes and goes. Petra, they had a list."

"I know." Her voice came out level. She had spent seven years training her voice to come out level; it was nice to know the training held even when her stomach did not. "How many did you count?"

"Twelve. Maybe fourteen." He swallowed. "They're checking papers at the Fish Gate now. I came through the shambles."

"You came through the shambles alone."

"I came fast."

Yes, she thought. You came fast, and fast is about to be the death of us. Across the square, one of the soldiers looked up from his list. Not at her. At Tam. Perhaps it was nothing — boys were everywhere in the quarter, boys were furniture — but the soldier's head turned again, and this time he nudged his companion, and Petra felt the whole day tilt beneath her like a deck taking water.

"Tam," she said softly. "Walk with me. Slowly. Toward the chandler's."

"Why the chandler's?"

"Because it has a back door and Mr. Petch owes me for a chart." She did not say *because a man in grey has noticed you*, and she did not say *because your name may be written on that list*, though she suspected both, and the suspicion sat in her chest like swallowed stone. They walked. Behind them, boots on cobble found a pace to match theirs, unhurried, patient, the pace of men paid by the hour who knew that everything in Greyhaven eventually ran out of street.

* * *

Tam heard the boots behind them and told himself not to turn around. Turning around was an admission. He had survived one winter alone in this city by learning its grammar — who could be looked at, who must not be, which doors opened for a knock and which for a bribe — and the first rule of that grammar was *never confirm that you have been seen.*

But his heart was going like a gull in a sail loft, and the boots did not pass them, and when the voice came it was polite in the way that made his skin crawl, because he had learned young that politeness from power was just cruelty wearing gloves.

"Young sir. A moment."

The soldier was older than Tam expected, grey at the temples, with a clerk's soft hands and a list folded into one of them. His companion drifted a step wide, cutting the angle to the chandler's door, and Tam understood with cold clarity that this had been done before, many times, to many boys.

"Name?" asked the soft-handed one.

"Amberdale," Tam said, and blessed the name even as fear curdled it, because it was true, in the way that mattered. "Tam Amberdale. My mother's expecting me."

"Your mother." The soldier consulted his list without appearing to. "And where does young Master Amberdale live?"

"Ledger Lane."

"Ledger Lane," the soldier repeated, pleasant as porridge. "Near the map house."

Everything in Tam went still. He thought of the ledger under the drafting floor, of the names in its margins, of the word *Kindly Tide* whispered once between Petra and Elias when they thought he was asleep — and he thought, with terrible grown-up clarity, *they are not fishing. They know the pond.* He made the mistake then, the mistake he would relive for weeks: he hesitated. Half a heartbeat. Long enough for the soft-handed soldier to smile, long enough for the companion to shift his weight forward, long enough for the whole encounter to change species — from question to trap, from trap to net.

"There's no hurry," the soldier said gently. "You can come tell the magistrate yourself where Ledger Lane is. He's very interested in maps."

Tam ran.

He did not decide to. His body decided for him, the old winter wisdom seizing command — *away, away, low and fast* — and he was three steps gone before the shout went up, before the square's quiet tore open, before Petra's voice cracked across the cobbles crying something he couldn't hear over his own blood. He ran the way he had run at nine years old, down the gap between the chandler's and the ropewalk, over a fence slick with herring brine, into the labyrinth of drying-yards where the nets hung like the sails of drowned ships. Behind him: boots, a whistle, the flat clatter of a man vaulting what Tam had scrambled.

He knew these yards. He had played in them, worked in them, hidden in them from Widow Amberdale's wrath and Sable's kindly surveillance alike. But the nets were wet and heavy today and the fog was folding back in off the water, and at the far end of the second yard, where the lane opened onto Quay Street, a third soldier stepped out of the white with the casual timing of a man who had been placed there, and Tam understood that he had not been running away.

He had been running *toward.*

A hand closed on his collar. Wool, and beneath the wool, iron. Tam twisted, dropped — the trick the dock boys used — and got half free, and then the net of them closed, and he was shouting, he realized, shouting the only thing worth shouting:

*I live on Ledger Lane! I live at the map house! Somebody—*

* * *

Petra came around the corner of the ropewalk at full sprint, rag stock abandoned somewhere behind her, her mind doing what it always did under catastrophe: making maps. Three soldiers, one boy, one lane of egress north, one yard of nets south, fog thickening. Elias beside her — he had appeared at her shoulder within seconds of the shout, appearing the way he always did, silently, from a direction that made no sense — and she had time for exactly six words.

"He's ours. Get him clear."

Elias didn't answer. He was already unbuttoning his coat.

What Petra saw next she would remember in fragments, the way she remembered engravings — each element precise, the whole assembled later. Elias walking into the yard not running but strolling, hands visible, face arranged into an expression of bureaucratic grievance so perfect it was almost beautiful. The soldiers turning. Elias producing from inside his coat a document — a document Petra had watched him forge three nights ago at the kitchen table by candlelight, complaining mildly about the quality of the seal wax — and presenting it with the weary authority of a man whose patience for clerks was famously short.

"Harbor master's survey," she heard him say, crisp as a ledger line. "Under Writ of Inspection, Section Nine. Every vessel's crew list goes to the magistrate's office by nightfall, and I am missing the afternoon tide arguing with three gentlemen about a shipwright's apprentice."

The soft-handed soldier hesitated. Petra watched him hesitate, watched his eyes flicker between the paper and Elias's face, and she understood the fulcrum on which the moment balanced: not the forgery itself — Elias's work was good but seals could be checked — but the *manner*. Men who worked by lists feared above all things the man who might be *on* a better list. Authority, real or forged, was mostly costume, and Elias wore it like he'd been fitted for it at birth.

"You can verify the writ at the customs house," Elias continued, ice-cold, holding out the paper. "Take it. Go now. And while you walk, consider explaining to Magistrate Hollis why his inspection schedule slipped a day."

That did it. She saw the exact instant the arithmetic tipped — the soldiers' faces closing into the blankness of men choosing the safer obedience — and the hand left Tam's collar. The boy stumbled free, and Petra was moving before anyone thought to stop her, gathering Tam against her side with an arm that shook only after it was safely around him.

"Apprentice," Elias said to the soldiers, jerking his chin at Tam with theatrical disgust. "If he runs off again before he delivers my papers to the chandler's, I'll see he's flogged by his own master. My apologies for the disturbance, gentlemen. The tide waits for no writ."

And the soldiers went, boots receding into the fog toward Quay Street, leaving the drying-yard silent except for the drip of the nets and the three of them standing very still among the hanging ropes like actors abandoned mid-play.

Nobody spoke until the last boot-sound died. Then Tam's legs simply stopped working, and he sat down hard in the mud of the yard, and Petra went down with him, both knees in the muck, holding his face in her hands.

"Look at me. Look at me, Tam Amberdale. You're here. You're whole. Breathe."

He was shaking. So, she discovered, was she — a fine continuous tremor from wrist to shoulder that she clenched her jaw against, because one of them had to hold steady and it had always, always been her job.

"I shouldn't have run," Tam said. His voice came out small and ancient. "I hesitated and then I ran and they were waiting, they were *waiting*, Petra, they knew—" He stopped. She watched him decide whether to say the rest, watched him decide to say it. "They asked about Ledger Lane. About the map house. Not the street. The *map house.*"

The words settled over the yard like the fog itself. Petra looked up at Elias, who stood with the forged writ already disappearing back into his coat, his face unreadable, his breathing — she noted it, filed it — not calm at all but controlled, the calm of a man holding a door shut from the inside.

"They weren't fishing," she said quietly.

"No," Elias said. "They're dredging."

Tam looked between them, and Petra watched the boy do the thing she had done at eleven, at thirteen, at every hard age since: watch the adults' faces to learn how afraid to be. She owed him better than that arithmetic. She gathered him up, mud and all, and put her coat around his shoulders even though his own was nearly dry.

"Then we go home," she said, "and we tell the household everything, tonight, all of it — no more silences. Silences are how a boy ends up in a net yard alone." She said it loudly enough for Elias to hear, and meant it as both promise and accusation, and saw by the flicker in his eyes that he received it as both.

They went home through the shambles, three abreast, Tam in the middle. And all the way, Petra's mind was already working ahead the way it always did, laying lines and soundings: the soldiers knew the map house; Hollis was closing his hand; whatever time she had believed she had was shorter than mapped.

Behind them, in the fog among the nets, one of the soldiers paused and unfolded a scrap of memory he could not quite place — the accent of the harbor surveyor, perhaps, or the cut of his coat — and then let it go, because the tide was called the tide, and the tide waits for no writ.

But Elias Vane did not let it go. Walking home with his forged papers warm against his ribs and a boy's trembling weight leaning into his elbow, Elias understood, with the clarity of a man reading his own chart upside down, that he had crossed a line in that yard and could not find it behind him. He had acted not for pay, not for his employers — his employers, he now knew beyond all doubt, had sent those soldiers. He had acted for the boy, for the household, for the impossible patched-together warmth of the blue door on Ledger Lane.

Which meant, he thought grimly, that he was now a traitor to the only people who knew where to find him. And traitors, in his experience, needed to choose their side quickly and completely.

By the time they reached the blue door, he had chosen.

"I need to tell you what my employers actually sent me to do," he said to Petra, quietly, on the doorstep, while Tam's knock echoed inside and Sable's footsteps hurried to answer. "All of it. Tonight. Because there's something I carried across the water that you need to hear — and something I think you already know."

The door opened. Fog leaned in behind them like a listener.
