## 16. The Forging

The first night of the forging began with a funeral.

Not for a person — for an atlas. Petra laid the finished plates of Corvane's great work out on the drafting table in the order they would be bound, all forty-two of them, and stood over them with her hands flat on the wood as though taking a pulse. The lamp guttered once and steadied. Rain worked at the shutters the way it always did in Greyhaven, patient and without malice, like a debt collector who knew you'd pay eventually.

"We don't copy it," she said. "If we copy it, Hollis's men will know within a page. They've seen enough of his work to smell a forgery." She looked up at me across the lamplight, and there was something in her face I hadn't seen before — not the guarded apprentice who had negotiated with me like a border commission, but a craftsman about to do something difficult and enjoying the difficulty. "We make it *adjacent*. The same hand learning to be his hand. A sister atlas. One he might have made."

"And the route?"

"Real-looking. Wrong." She pulled a plate toward her — the eastern quarter, all its canals and warehouses rendered in Corvane's fine, confident hatching. "Every safe passage in the true ledger ends at the water. Every false one will end here." Her finger came down on a block of buildings just inland of the customs wharf. "Hollis's own warehouse. The one he seized from the salt merchants two years ago and never filled."

I understood it before she said it, and understanding it felt like swallowing something cold. "You're going to send him hunting his own property."

"I'm going to let him find what he's already looking for." She said it lightly, but her finger stayed on that block a moment longer, pressing hard enough that the nail went white. "He wants the refuge to be somewhere he can reach. We're only being generous."

Sable arrived at midnight without anyone letting her in — she had her own key and had used it for years, apparently, long before I knew there was a door to unlock. She hung her wet cloak on the peg by the stove, set a wrapped parcel of bread and hard cheese on the corner of the table where no ink could ever reach it, and then stood looking down at the spread of plates with her arms folded, her whole body doing the work of a sentence.

*You're sure,* that posture said.

"No," Petra answered honestly.

Sable nodded once, as if *no* were the only acceptable answer, and pulled up the third stool.

That was how I learned the shape of the work. Petra would draw; Sable would age; I would write. Three hands, one object. I remember thinking, somewhere in the small hours when my eyes had gone gritty and the lamp oil smelled like a struck match, that I had spent my whole life being hired for one skill at a time — this man's fists, that woman's papers, this smuggler's memory for faces — and that I had never once been part of a thing that required more than any single person could carry. It was not a comfortable feeling. It was better than comfort.

* * *

My task was the worst one, and everyone at that table knew it.

Forging a living hand is theft. Forging a dead one is séance. For three days I sat with Corvane's letters and ledger pages pinned around my board like saints' icons, and I tried to become a man I had never met — a man whose hand I knew only through the evidence of what it had made, a man who was, by every account but one, at the bottom of Greyhaven harbor with the eels.

His capitals leaned left, into the wind of the page, as if leaning away from whatever he'd just written. His lowercase was smaller than it should have been, cramped, a man conserving something — paper, breath, time. He crossed his t's with a slight downward flick, quick, dismissive, and he almost never lifted the pen mid-word: the ink told you where he had paused, because pauses pooled dark. And he had a habit — I found it on the ninth letter, buried in a marginal note about tide tables — of letting the last letter of a sentence trail off into a hairline, as though finishing the thought had become suddenly unimportant compared to starting the next one.

"You've got the slant," Petra said over my shoulder on the second morning, and her voice had gone strange. "You've got the slant exactly."

"Is that good or bad?"

She didn't answer right away. When I turned, she was looking at my practice sheet the way you look at a door you thought was locked swinging open on its own. "It's unsettling," she said. "Keep going."

Sable taught me aging. This surprised me — I had assumed the cook's contribution would be food and silence, and I had been guilty of underestimating her the way everyone underestimated her, which was precisely her protection and, I think now, precisely why Corvane trusted her with everything. But Sable knew paper the way a fisherman knows weather. She showed me how tea stains differently depending on whether the paper has been sized with hide glue or starch; how to crease a folio along its true spine so the fibers break honestly rather than artificially; how smoke damage near a candle should bloom outward in one direction, because fire comes from somewhere, it doesn't hover. She rubbed ochre dust into the gutter margins with the heel of her hand until the page looked like it had been consulted nightly for years by fingers that had just come in from the harbor.

When she was satisfied with a sheet, she would hold it up between us and look at me through it, backlit by the lamp, checking the fiber the way an honest woman checks a coin. If it passed, she'd set it down and tap it twice. If it didn't, she'd tear it in half without ceremony and hand me fresh paper, and somehow that tearing — brisk, unsentimental, without blame — kept me working harder than any criticism could have.

Petra built the poisoned route itself, and she built it beautifully, which is to say she built it the way a spider builds a web: every strand correct, every strand a lie. The false refuge sat behind the magistrate's warehouse in her rendering of the eastern quarter, approached by a canal that did exist and a gate that did not, guarded by nothing, generous with its exits. She copied Corvane's cipher marks onto it faithfully — the little anchor-and-bridge glyph that meant *shelter*, the double stroke that meant *trusted keeper*. Then, in the margin, in my best dying fall of a hairline, we wrote the words that would finish it:

*Safe house. Ask for none. Knock twice and wait.*

Anyone following that route would knock on the warehouse of Aldous Hollis and wait to be taken.

"It's cruel," I said, on the second night. I hadn't meant to say it. My hand ached; the lamp was low; I had been writing another man's hopefulness for eleven hours.

Petra set down her pen. "Yes," she said. Not defensive. Just true. "And the people who follow it will be Hollis's own men, following a map Hollis sent them to find. Cruelty to cruelty. I can carry that." She picked the pen back up. Then, quieter, without looking at me: "Can you?"

I thought about the people who had bought me across the water — cheap, they'd said, he was cheap — and about the checkpoint where I had put Tam's collar up and my forged pass into his fist and lied to a soldier with a rifle until lying felt like the only honest trade left. I thought about the fact that I could not remember my mother's handwriting at all.

"Yes," I said. And found, checking, that it held.

* * *

The third day was the day everything nearly broke, and I mark it here because afterward no one ever spoke of it, the way households swallow their worst hours whole and keep only the shape.

Near dusk, boots on the lane. Two pairs, unhurried, the walk of men paid by the day rather than the errand. Sable heard them first — of course she did; she read the street through the floorboards better than any of us read it through the window — and she moved before the rest of us had stopped breathing. Plates into the press-bed. Ledger into the floor. Petra doused the forging lamp and lit the ordinary one and was grinding pigment for a client's survey chart, a real commission, real work, her hands steady as a mason's, by the time the knock came.

I was the problem. I had no reason to be in a mapmaker's house at dusk, and the pass I carried named me as an agent of nobody. So I became furniture: coat off, sleeves rolled, hauling the widow's delivery crates from the back hall to the kitchen while Sable directed me with sharp little gestures that were half theater and half genuine contempt for my crate-carrying. The clerks came through. One of them was the polite one, Mr. Fenn, with his soft voice and his eyes like a scale finding its balance point.

"Widow Amberdale," he said, "still awake at this hour. The work keeps you young."

"The work keeps me," said the widow, drifting in from the parlor in her shawl, and I watched her do it again — the tremor coming up through her hands on cue, the sweet confusion settling over her face like fog over the harbor. "Is it Thursday? I never know, since Aldous— since my husband passed. These girls keep all my days for me."

They looked at charts. They looked at Petra, inking her legitimate survey with the mild, slightly bored competence of an artisan with nothing to hide. They looked at me exactly once, saw a laborer sweating over crates, and looked away, having filed me somewhere below their interest. Fenn thanked the widow for her patience with the city's troubles. The door closed. The boots receded.

Nobody moved for a full minute. Then Sable crossed the kitchen, took the last crate out of my arms, set it down, and patted my cheek twice — the same double-tap she gave an approved page — and went back to the stove as though nothing in the world were unusual.

That night we worked until the birds started. Somewhere past three, Petra fell asleep sitting upright, mid-stroke, her pen leaving a small comet-tail of ink across a practice sheet. I watched Sable ease the pen out of her fingers, wipe them, and settle a blanket around her shoulders with a tenderness she would have denied under oath. Then Sable caught me watching, and pointed at Petra, and pointed at me, and made a gesture I understood without translation: *this is what we are doing all this for.*

I went back to Corvane's hand. The capitals leaned into the wind. The sentences trailed into hairlines, a running man's economy, a hiding man's hope. By the fourth hour I stopped translating him and started hearing him — the particular patience in the letterforms of a person who believed, against all evidence, that careful work could save careless lives. I had been forging his script for three days. Somewhere in that dark morning I realized I had also, without meaning to, absorbed his argument: that a map is a promise, and a promise is worth the making even if you never see who keeps it.

At dawn Petra woke, looked at the table, and counted. Forty-two plates aged, annotated, bound in the false spine Sable had stained with walnut and beeswax to match the original. The poisoned route gleaming faintly gold where she'd laid the refuge glyph. The final annotation in my hand, dated six days after Corvane's drowning, in an ink Sable had faded to the exact brown of old oak gall.

Petra held the finished decoy in both hands and turned it over, once, slowly, like a mother checking a sleeping child for fever.

"It's beautiful," she said, and her voice cracked on it, and I understood that this was the hardest thing she had ever made, harder than truth.

"It needs one more thing," Sable said.

We both looked at her. Sable spoke perhaps ten sentences aloud in a week, hoarding her voice like coin, and when she spent it she expected attention. She took the decoy from Petra, opened it to the final plate, and pointed at the bottom corner — the blank margin where a master signs off his completed work for his apprentice to witness.

"He always signed," she said. "And Petra always countersigned. Hollis will know that. Everyone who ever saw the atlas knows that." She set the book down in front of Petra and stepped back.

For a long moment Petra didn't move. Then she took up her pen, dipped it, and wrote her master's name beneath the final route — the lean of the capitals, the wind-blown slant, the trailing hairline like a breath let go — and beneath it, in her own small, steady, entirely honest hand: *witnessed, P. Amberdale.*

Two signatures. Two truths. One object.

Sable tapped the cover twice. Approved.

We stood there in the grey beginning of the day, three forgers around a lie the size of a city, and I felt something I had no language for yet — the specific weight of being trusted with the worst job and forgiven for having taken money to do a version of it. Found things, I wanted to say. This is what found means. Not discovered. Assembled.

It was Petra who broke the silence, because it was Petra who heard what I had not: wheels on the lane again, early, too early, and this time a carriage — not clerks' boots but magisterial iron-rimmed wheels, stopping directly outside the map house door.

She set the false atlas down on the table between us, open to the poisoned route, gold glyph shining.

"They're not waiting for the atlas to be lost," she said quietly. "They've come for it now."
