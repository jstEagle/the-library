## 18. Three Berths, Five People

The message came at dawn, folded inside a mackerel that Sable bought from the fish market with the same coins she had used for twenty years, and by the time I had wiped the brine from the paper my hands had already begun to shake in that particular way they do when a number goes wrong.

*Departure advanced. Kindly Tide slips her moorings tomorrow night, flood tide, fog willing. The berth list is fixed. No additions. No exceptions.*

No signature. There never was one. But the hand was small and slanted and pressed hard enough to emboss the page, and it was not Corvane's.

"It moved up," I said, though Sable could not hear me. She read it in my face anyway, the way she read everything — in the angle of a shoulder, the speed of a breath — and set down her knife on the cutting board with a care that frightened me more than any noise would have.

She signed, quick and economical: *How long?*

"A day. Perhaps less, if the fog comes early."

Sable looked out the kitchen window toward the harbor, where the morning mist was doing exactly what mist does in Greyhaven in autumn: gathering itself against the rooftops like a debt collector who knows he will be paid eventually. Then she signed one more word and went back to her fish, and the word was *Good,* which told me she had been expecting this since before I was born.

I took the message up to the drafting room, where Elias sat hunched over the ledger with a lamp already lit, because for six days none of us had slept so much as a full night and the lamps had simply stopped being put out. The widow was there too, in her chair by the south window where the light was best for mending, pretending to mend. She had been pretending very well all week. She could pretend anything. She had spent three days being senile for Hollis's clerks and had enjoyed it, I think, in the way a duelist enjoys an opponent who underestimates her.

I laid the paper among Corvane's pages and watched them read it, and then I said the thing we had all been refusing to say.

"The ship leaves tomorrow night. Three berths. Five of us who need to go."

Nobody answered immediately, because there was nothing to answer with. Arithmetic does not negotiate. It sits on the table like a stone and waits for you to pick it up.

* * *

Let me lay the sum out plainly, because I have learned that plainness is its own kind of courage.

Tam must sail. His name has been in the ledger's oldest pages since before he could write it himself; Corvane promised him passage years ago, and whatever else my master did or failed to do, he kept his promises, and I intend to keep them for him. Tam is also fifteen, known to two of Hollis's informants by face, and the reason soldiers were searching the Saltmarket checkpoints last month. He cannot stay. That is not sentiment. That is the ledger.

Sable must sail. The third berth is hers, has been hers for longer than I knew — she showed us the entry herself, in her own careful notation, having understood Corvane's cipher years before either Elias or I cracked it. She has been feeding the network from this kitchen for nearly a decade, passing warnings through the fish market wrapped in the language of prices and catch. If Hollis takes her, he takes the whole western route with her, and worse — he takes the only person alive who knows every safe house between Greyhaven and the northern passes. She must sail. Again: not sentiment. Necessity wearing sentiment's coat.

The third berth — the one Hollis's men do not know to watch, because the nephew sold the map house and the ledger's existence but not the ship's full passenger list, either because he didn't know it or, God help me, because some scrap of loyalty made him keep that one thing back — belongs now to Merrit. The widow's nephew. The man who came to us eight months ago with soft hands and a story about a failed shipping concern, who ate at our table, who mended the garden gate, who copied out three pages of the ledger for thirty crowns of debt relief and a promise of more.

Three berths. Tam, Sable, Merrit.

Which leaves me, Elias, and the widow standing on the quay when the Kindly Tide goes out, and the truth is that of the three of us left behind, I am the only one Hollis has never managed to name. Elias he knows as a foreigner with forged papers. The widow he watches the way a cat watches a hole it is sure contains a mouse. But me — I am the mapmaker's apprentice, the grieving orphan girl, the harmless ink-stained creature his clerks patted on the head twice while asking their courteous questions. Staying is dangerous for all of us. It is least dangerous for me.

That is the arithmetic. I want to be honest that I arrived at it honestly — that I did not bend the numbers toward the answer I wanted, though God knows I checked them four times hoping they would change.

They did not change. Numbers are loyal to nothing.

* * *

The argument happened in the drafting room with the shutters closed, which is where all our arguments happened that week, and it lasted the better part of an hour.

"He stays," Elias said. "That's the whole of it. You don't give a berth to a man who sold you."

"He didn't sell *me*," I said. "He sold a fragment. A location and a rumor. And yes, Elias, I know how that sentence sounds, I have been listening to myself say versions of it for two days and every version sounds like treason against my own judgment."

"Because it is." Elias paced the length of the drafting table, three steps each way, a habit I had come to find steadying and now found maddening. "Petra. The man copied pages of the ledger. Whatever he kept back about passengers, Hollis knows the atlas exists, knows this house produced it. When the false atlas lands on Hollis's desk and he discovers the poisoned route — and he will discover it, within days — he will tear Greyhaven apart looking for whoever built it. Anyone connected to this house becomes a target overnight. Merrit included. Especially Merrit, because Merrit is the loose thread he can pull."

"Then he hangs with the rest of us," the widow said, without looking up from her mending. Her needle did not pause. "That is what family means, in my experience. It means hanging together."

"That is what *stupidity* means," Elias snapped, and then caught himself, because you did not speak to Widow Amberdale that way, not quite, and the silence that followed had the texture of an apology neither party wanted.

The widow set down her sewing at last. She looked at her nephew's empty chair by the cold stove — he was locked in the storeroom below, at his own terrified request, as if a door with a latch could protect him from what he had done — and her face did something complicated that I will spend the rest of my life trying to describe.

"He was nine years old when his father drowned off the Herring Light," she said. "I stood at his mother's side at the funeral. I held his hand. He cried into my coat until it stiffened with salt." She picked the needle back up. "You are both so young. You think betrayal is a character. It isn't. It's a circumstance, wearing a person's clothes. Debt is a circumstance. Fear is a circumstance. Hollis's people found a man drowning in his own obligations and they offered him a rope with a hook in it, and he grabbed it, because drowning men grab things. It is the most forgivable terrible thing I have ever watched someone do, and I have watched several."

"And if he betrays the berth too?" Elias asked quietly. "If he walks up the Kindly Tide's gangway and, once safely at sea, decides his best protection is proving his usefulness? He knows the route. He knows the network. He knows everything Merrit knows, which is more than any of us can afford to have in Hollis's pocket."

"Yes," the widow said. "There is that."

"There is also this," I said, and I heard my own voice come out in the flat register I reserved for linework and other irreversible things. "If we leave him, Hollis takes him tomorrow. Not next week. The moment the house empties, or the moment his creditors squeeze again, or the moment one of the clerks remembers his name. They will question him. He has already proven he talks. Everything Elias just said — the route, the boarding point, the false atlas — he hands over in an afternoon, because he handed over less than that for thirty crowns. So the choice is not whether Merrit is dangerous. The choice is whether he is dangerous here, in our hands, or there, in theirs."

The lamp guttered. Outside, gulls argued over something dead on the tide flats, the way they always did, indifferent as arithmetic.

"You're saying mercy is tactics," Elias said.

"I'm saying I can't tell the difference anymore, and I've stopped believing I should have to." I looked down at Corvane's ledger, at the coded routes running down the margins like veins under skin. "My master drew escape routes for strangers for twenty years. People he owed nothing to. People who, for all he knew, would have sold him for less than thirty crowns. He did it anyway. Not because he was certain they deserved it. Because the ledger doesn't ask whether you deserve a way out. It asks whether you need one."

Elias stopped pacing. He stood at the window with the shutter cracked an inch, and through the gap I watched his face take the grey light, and I thought — not for the first time, but for the first time without flinching — that I knew exactly what he was seeing, because I had seen it too, in him and in myself and in every stray this house had ever taken in: the specific loneliness of a person calculating, constantly, whether today is the day they get put out.

"My employers will kill me if the atlas fails," he said finally. "Or worse. And you're proposing we hand a berth to a man who proved he'll sell anything for enough rope."

"I'm proposing we hand a berth to a man who is going to be on that ship either way," I said. "The only question is whether he sails knowing why."

* * *

I went down to the storeroom alone. Elias and the widow let me go without argument, which was its own kind of verdict.

Merrit was sitting on a crate of obsolete charts with his elbows on his knees and his head in his hands, and he looked up when the door opened with the expression of a man who has spent hours rehearsing for a trial and finds, when it arrives, that he cannot remember a single line.

"I didn't tell them the ship's name," he said, before I could speak. "Whatever they've told you — whatever you've decided about me — I never gave them the Kindly Tide."

"I know," I said.

He blinked. "You know?"

"Elias worked out that your fragment couldn't have contained it, from what the clerks asked when they came. They asked about the atlas. They asked about the house. They never once asked about departures. If you'd given them the ship, they'd have had the harbor watched weeks ago instead of instituting general inspections." I sat down on the crate opposite him. Up close he smelled of fear, which has a smell — sour, close, like a room where a fire has just gone out. "You kept one thing back, Merrit. One. I'd like to know why, before I decide what it buys you."

For a long moment he stared at his hands. They were shaking. I remembered that they had been soft when he arrived, hands that had never held a pen knife or a scrub brush, and I thought about all the ways a man's hands can tell on him.

"Because of Aunt Verity," he said finally. "Not — don't misunderstand me, not out of love, I wasn't protecting her, I'm not that good a man." His laugh came out broken in the middle. "Out of shame. Do you understand the difference? Love would have kept me honest altogether. Shame only ever keeps a little of you honest, the corner where it happens to live. I sat at her table eating her food and I copied those pages and I told myself it was survival, everyone steals to survive, the world is a hard market — and then I got back to my rooms and I saw her handwriting on the copy paper, because I'd traced over her inventory notes by accident, her little looping hand, and I thought: she taught me my letters. On this table. When I was seven. And I had just sold her house."

He looked up at me, and his eyes were red and ordinary and human, and I hated him, and I understood him, and the two feelings sat together in my chest the way two colors sit in a wash and stain each other.

"So when it came to the passenger list — the one thing that would actually drown people, actually put bodies in Hollis's cells — I found I couldn't write it down. My hand wouldn't do it. Not virtue. Just... a limit. Everyone has a limit. Mine turned out to be exactly one step short of murder, which I grant you is not an impressive distance."

"No," I said. "It isn't."

"But it's a distance."

"It's a distance," I agreed.

I let the silence work on him for a moment, because I am my master's apprentice and silences are tools like any other. Then I said: "Tomorrow night, when the fog comes, a ship leaves this harbor with three berths on it. Tam has one. Sable has one. The third is yours — if you want it, and if you accept the terms."

His mouth opened. Nothing came out of it.

"The terms are these. You sail under the name in the ledger, not your own. You do not speak to anyone aboard about this house, this atlas, or this network — not from gratitude, not from fear, not ever. You go wherever the ship puts you and you start over somewhere far away with soft hands that will have to learn calluses, and you will owe a debt you cannot repay, and the payment plan is the rest of your life spent being the kind of man whose hand won't write down the thing that kills people. Do you accept?"

"Yes," he said, instantly, hoarsely. "Yes. Petra —"

"Don't thank me." I stood. My legs had gone stiff from sitting and my voice, mercifully, held. "I'm not doing this for you. Understand that plainly. I'm doing it because leaving you here makes everyone I love less safe, and because a dead man drew routes for people who needed them without auditing their souls first, and I am trying, very hard, to finish becoming his apprentice." At the door I paused, because there was one thing more and it would not keep. "And because in a week or a month, when Hollis finds out his beautiful new atlas is poison, someone in this city is going to pay for it, and I would rather every person who helped build it be beyond his reach — including the one who helped him find us. That's the arithmetic, Merrit. You're not forgiven. You're *managed.* Learn the difference. It might save your life someday."

He nodded, small and jerky, a man accepting a sentence, and I climbed the stairs back toward the lamplight feeling precisely nothing, which I understood even then was the mercy — that the heart, presented with an impossible sum, sometimes simply declines to feel the answer until afterward.

* * *

We told Tam at supper.

Sable cooked the good fish, the ones she'd bought that dawn, and she cooked them with the butter we had been saving, and none of us explained why except that Sable explained why, in her way, by putting the plate in front of Tam and signing at him slowly and deliberately, so there could be no mistake:

*Eat well. Big water tomorrow.*

Tam looked at the plate. Then at her. Then around the table — at Elias, at the widow, at me — and I watched him do the arithmetic faster than any of us had, because children who appear one winter and are never asked about learn to read rooms the way deaf women read faces and orphans read silences, and he had been reading ours for six years.

"Both berths?" he signed, meaning his and Sable's, and Sable nodded, and Tam put down his fork and put his head down on the table beside his plate and cried the way boys cry when they have practiced not crying for years — silently, violently, with his shoulders doing all the talking.

Sable got up and stood behind him with both hands on his shoulders and did not sign anything at all, and the widow ate her fish with wet eyes and perfect posture, and Elias looked at the ceiling beams as if consulting them, and I sat with my hands flat on the table and felt the tremor finally leave them, because the decision was made now, the numbers were final, and there is a horrible calm in that which I suspect I will carry to my grave.

Afterward, when the plates were cleared, Tam came to me and tugged my sleeve and signed a question I had not prepared for:

*You come too?*

And I smiled at him — a real smile, I think, or near enough that it served — and signed back the first lie I had ever told him, and the truest thing I said all day:

*Someone has to draw the maps home.*
