## 20. The Master in the Lighthouse

The fog came off the water that night like something poured rather than risen, thick enough to hold a lantern's glow at arm's length and turn it to a bruise of yellow. Petra climbed the salt-slick path to the old lighthouse with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest and every instruction Sable had pressed into her hands before she left — *tap twice, wait, tap once* — repeating itself under her breath like a prayer she didn't believe in.

The lighthouse had been dark for eleven years. Its lamp room stood at the top of the promontory like a severed head, glass panes starred with salt and bird-strike, and below it the keeper's house sagged into its own foundations, held up mostly by ivy and habit. The city had stopped maintaining it the year the new harbor light went in; the year after that, it had become the kind of place respectable Greyhaven crossed the street to avoid. Which was, Petra understood now, precisely why Corvane had chosen it.

She tapped twice on the keeper's door. The wood was soft with rot and her knuckles left faint crescents in it.

She waited. She tapped once.

Somewhere inside, a board creaked under weight. Then another. Then a voice — roughened, lower than memory, but arranged in an accent she would have known in the dark, in a crowd, at the bottom of the sea — said: "You counted the taps right. He taught you well."

Her hands would not move to the latch. She stood on the threshold with fog beading in her eyelashes and thought, absurdly, of the first day of her apprenticeship, when she had been nine years old and so thin her wrists looked borrowed, and Master Corvane had set a pen in her fingers and said, *Your hand will shake. That's fine. Everything true is drawn trembling.*

She opened the door.

He was sitting at a table beneath a shuttered lamp, and the sight of him did what seven years of grief had not: it broke her composure cleanly, the way a keel breaks water. He was thinner. His hair had gone entirely white at the temples and gray everywhere else, and his left arm rested in a sling made from what looked like a flour sack, and there was a scar through one eyebrow that had not been there when he drowned. But his eyes were the same — quick, brown, patient, the eyes of a man who had spent his life reading other people's maps while pretending to draw his own.

"Petra," he said.

"You're supposed to be dead," she said, and hated how young her voice came out, how it cracked in the middle like a child's.

"I am," he said gravely. "It's been very convenient. Sit down, girl, before you fall down."

* * *

The keeper's house smelled of lamp oil, old paper, and sickness — that last one faint but unmistakable to anyone who had nursed a household through fevers. There were charts pinned everywhere along the walls, and Petra's professional eye catalogued them before her heart could recover: coastal surveys of the Reach, soundings tables, tide calculations in a hand she knew better than her own. And among them, half-hidden behind a chart of the Greyhaven shoals, a copy of a page she recognized. One of hers. From her atlas. Drawn by her hand within the last month.

"You've seen my plates," she said.

"I've seen everything you've done." He poured tea from a pot crusted with mineral scale, one-handed, awkward, refusing her help with a small motion of his shoulders. "Every proof you've pulled. Every cipher layer you added to chapter four — that was elegant, by the way. I'd have used a different modulus, but elegance isn't security, is it? It never has been." He set a cup in front of her. "You finished the atlas, Petra. I only ever dreamed of finishing it."

"You drowned." She said it flatly, because if she let any other tone in she would weep, and she had not come here to weep. "I identified your coat. Widow Amberdale identified your body. We buried—"

"You buried a coat," Corvane said. "I'm sorry for it. It was a cruel thing to do to you, and I have paid for cruelty these seven years in ways I won't bore you with." He flexed his slung arm slightly, and she understood, suddenly, what the sling was hiding. "Hollis's men came for me on the ninth of Marrow-month. Not for questions. For the ledger. I had perhaps two hours' warning from friends whose names you'll never know, and two hours is exactly enough time to drown a master mapmaker and put a stranger's corpse in his coat, provided you have a tide and no conscience. I had a tide. The conscience took longer."

"And then you left us." Her voice was steady now, which surprised her. Steady and cold as a sounding line. "You left her. You left a widow in that house with debts and suspicions and a household of strays, and you let us think — seven years, Master. Seven years she lit a candle in the front window every night. Did you know that? Did your friends in the network tell you that?"

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, wind worked at the loose glass in the lamp room, a low organ note.

"Yes," he said quietly. "I knew."

* * *

He told her the rest of it in the flat, unhurried voice he had used for lessons, and she sat with her tea going cold and made herself listen the way she had once made herself listen to corrections.

The ledger under the floor had been his work for twenty years — before the widow, before the atlas, before Petra. A network of safe houses, forged passes, coded routes out of Greyhaven for the people the magistrate's court devoured: debtors, dissenters, the wrong kind of foreigner, the wrong kind of faith. The atlas had always been its twin — a commercial work, beautiful, sellable, respectable, and underneath its engraved plates, readable only with the ledger, a complete map of every bolt-hole and backwater and bribed customs lane on the coast.

"When Hollis took office, the arithmetic changed," Corvane said. "Before him, the network could run slow and quiet and survive. After him, it needed to run fast. Fast means mistakes. Mistakes mean the ledger gets found, and if the ledger gets found, three hundred names go to the gallows or the galleys." He turned his cup slowly on the table. "So I died. A dead man's work gets overlooked. A dead man's apprentice gets pitied and left alone. And I ran the network from out here like a ghost, which is what I deserved to be."

"And the Kindly Tide. The berths."

"Mine. All of it mine — the arrangement, the ship, the six weeks, the timing chosen around the equinox fogs." Something moved in his face, the first expression that wasn't controlled. "I built the whole escape and then couldn't take it myself, because a dead man can't book passage. So I stayed here and steered, and sent Elias Vane across the water to make sure the ledger reached the right hands." He paused. "That part went differently than I intended. I'm told. Vane has a talent for ending up somewhere other than where he was sent."

"He has a talent for staying," Petra said, and heard what she'd said a half-second too late, and saw that Corvane heard it too, and filed it away in his quick eyes with all the rest.

* * *

"Now," he said, and there was something new in his voice — a gentleness that frightened her more than anything else he'd said, because she had only ever heard him use it for bad news dressed as kindness. "The second ship."

"There is no second ship."

"There is now. The *Merrow's Grace*, out of the northern quays, four days from tonight, riding on papers I've been cultivating for two years for exactly this eventuality. Two berths. One for me." He leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the ruin the last seven years had made of him, and made it briefly noble again. "One for you."

The words landed in the room like a dropped weight. Petra sat very still.

"Me."

"You've finished the work I abandoned. You cracked ciphers I designed to outlive me. You kept a household alive under Hollis's nose for six weeks, and from what Vane tells me — and he tells me a great deal more than he thinks he does — you forged a master's hand well enough to fool clerks who study documents for a living." He smiled, and it was his old smile, dry and warm at the edges. "I am offering you my berth, Petra. Out of everything I have ever stolen or smuggled or salvaged, you are the one thing I made that I'm proud of. Take it. Go south where the ink doesn't freeze. Draw maps of places that have never heard of Greyhaven. Live past thirty."

Outside, the fog pressed against the shutters like a palm. She thought of the harbor, black and patient below the promontory. She thought about saying yes. She let herself actually think it — the warm southern light, a workshop with a view of water that wasn't grey, hands that grew old drawing coastlines instead of escape routes, sleep without listening for boots on stairs. For three full breaths she let herself want it, honestly, the way her master had taught her to measure a coastline: completely, without flinching.

Then she thought of the front window candle, burning seven years for a man who was sitting in front of her drinking bad tea. She thought of Tam's name in the ledger's margin, already sailing east on the *Kindly Tide* with Sable's steady hands beside him. She thought of the pages still coming back through the fish market, the families who hadn't gotten out yet, the ones who would need the atlas in the spring when Hollis's inspections tightened and the last honest harbormaster lost his post.

"No," she said.

Corvane closed his eyes briefly, as if she had confirmed something. "Petra—"

"The atlas isn't finished." She stood, and found her legs steadier than she expected, and began gathering the oilcloth bundle back against her chest. "The final plate is cut but the key annotations aren't transferred, and the southern route through the marshes changed when they rebuilt the weir — I know, because I redrew it, and you'd know if you'd seen this month's proofs instead of last month's. Half the network's guides are people who can read a chart and nothing else. If I leave, the atlas leaves with the only person who can finish it, and the people who stay get a beautiful book full of beautiful lies."

"It will kill you," he said simply. "This work. Staying kills people slower, but it kills them."

"Then I'll die slowly," Petra said, "among people who know my name." She looked down at him — at her master, her ghost, the author of half her grief and all of her craft — and felt the strange, level calm of a decision already made. "You taught me that a map is a promise. Every line says: someone can live here. Someone can pass here. You made a promise to this city twenty years ago and then you died to keep it, and I honor that. But I don't have to repeat it. I can keep the same promise alive. That's what apprentices are for — to do the next part."

For a long moment the only sound was the wind in the lamp room and the sea working the rocks below, patient as arithmetic.

"You sound like your mother," Corvane said at last, roughly, and Petra went still, because he had never once, in all her years, mentioned her mother. "She told me the same thing, the day she put you in my arms. *I can't go where the map goes,* she said, *but she can. Teach her the next part.*" He set down his cup with care, as if his hands had become untrustworthy. "I have spent seven years being a dead man, and it turns out dying was easier than this. Take the berth, girl. That's the last order I will ever give you."

"No," Petra said again, gently this time. "It isn't. Your last order was: *everything true is drawn trembling.*" She crossed to him and, carefully, avoiding the hurt arm, embraced her master goodbye — feeling how slight he had become, how much of him the seven years had taken, how his good hand came up and gripped her shoulder like a man holding a railing in a storm. "I'm leaving you the proofs of chapters five through eight. They're in the bundle. Finish checking them and send them through the fish market by Thursday, the way Sable taught you — yes, I know she taught you, she told me. And when the atlas is distributed and Hollis is holding nothing but a beautiful forgery and his own fury—" she pulled back, and smiled at him with tears running freely down her face and her voice perfectly level, "—you can buy me a drink in some harbor town yet. If the fog lets you find one."

At the door, she paused with her hand on the latch.

"Master."

"Apprentice."

"Burn the wall charts before you sleep. The ones of the Reach. You've grown careless living alone." And before he could answer, before the moment could soften into something she couldn't carry back down the cliff path, she stepped out into the fog and pulled the door shut behind her.

The fog swallowed the promontory whole. Below her, far out in the black water, she could just make out the running light of the *Kindly Tide* — a single point of gold, moving east, carrying three people she loved toward mornings she would never see. Behind her, the dead man's candle burned in the shuttered lamp room. Ahead, the city lay under its fog with three hundred names hidden in it like seeds under snow, waiting for spring, waiting for pages, waiting for her.

Petra tightened her grip on the oilcloth bundle and started down the path, walking steadily, drawing her line trembling.

And she did not see — no one in Greyhaven saw — the figure detach itself from the customs shed at the foot of the cliff road, note the direction of her coming, and turn at an unhurried pace toward the magistrate's house, where lamps were still burning past midnight, and where a clerk was awake to receive him.
