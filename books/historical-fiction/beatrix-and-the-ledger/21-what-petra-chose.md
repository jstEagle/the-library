## 21. What Petra Chose

The Kindly Tide went out on a Tuesday, into a fog so thick the harbor bells seemed to ring from inside Petra's own skull. She stood at the tide gate with ink still under her fingernails — she had been working on the false atlas's final plate until the hour before, and no one had thought to tell her to wash — and she watched the ship's lantern dissolve into the grey like a coin dropped into milk.

She did not cry. She had expected to. She had braced for it the way one braces for a wave, shoulders set, breath held. But what came instead was something quieter and stranger: a feeling like a chart being rolled closed. Tam was at the rail — she had found him there, small and sharp-eyed, his hand raised not waving but *measuring*, because she had taught him to measure everything, masts and distances and the width of the world. Sable stood beside him with her shawl over her hair, and when the fog took them Sable turned and signed one last thing across the widening water, a sign Petra knew from the kitchen: *eat something. rest.* Even leaving, the cook had worried first about feeding people.

And the nephew — Aldous, whose debt had nearly killed them all — stood apart at the stern, hat in his hands, looking back at the three of them on the quay with an expression Petra could not name and did not try to. Mercy, she had learned in the last month, does not require understanding. It only requires the giving.

"He'll write," Elias said beside her. He said it the way he said everything now, low and even, as though loudness were a habit he had surrendered along with his old employment. "Tam will. The boy can't keep his thoughts behind his teeth any better than most people can keep rain off a sill."

"His spelling is terrible."

"I'll teach him." A pause. "From a distance."

The fog swallowed the lantern entirely. Petra stood a moment longer, committing the water to memory the way she committed coastlines — every seam of current, every place where the grey deepened toward black — and then she turned, and she and Elias walked back up through the sleeping streets to the map house where Widow Amberdale had left the lamp burning in the front window, which meant *come in, all is well* or possibly *the kettle is on* or possibly, simply, *you are expected; you belong to someone.*

Petra had lived in that house for eleven years and had only just learned to read its language.

* * *

The letters began in the spring.

The first came folded inside a bill of lading from a fish merchant in Saltmere, sixty miles down the coast, in Tam's terrible spelling: *Dear Petra the sea was very big bigger than your charts say wich is saying somthing. Sable says I am to eat more. I have learned to mend nets and also that I am never going back and also some days I want to anyway and Sable says that is normal. The stars here are the same ones. That helped more than I thought it would.*

Petra read it four times at the kitchen table while the widow pretended to mend a hem and Elias pretended to study a tide table, both of them listening with their whole bodies.

"The stars," the widow said at last, snipping her thread. "That's the boy entire. He always wanted the largest things made small enough to hold."

"He had a good teacher," Elias said, and looked at Petra, and Petra discovered that her eyes were stinging after all, weeks too late, over a sentence about stars.

The second letter came through the fish market channel the widow had kept running since before Petra was born — a widow's network of cousins and gutters-women and one extremely talkative oyster-man, through which passed herring prices, warnings, and now, occasionally, proof of life. Sable's message was dictated to a Saltmere schoolmistress and arrived in beautiful copperplate that was nothing like her: *Tell Petra the bread recipe was wrong all along, less water, hotter oven. Tell her also thank you, thank you, thank you. Tell her I am teaching the schoolmistress to sign and she is hopeless and it is the funniest thing I have seen in forty years.*

The third came from Aldous himself, and it was short, and it was not asking forgiveness, which Petra respected. *I work at a chandlery. I am honest here. It turns out honesty was never beyond my reach, only inconvenient. Do not write back. I do not deserve an answer. But know that I think of that night at the gate every day, and that I will spend however many years I am given being worth the berth you gave me.*

She read that one alone, in the drafting room, with the door shut. Then she copied it out in her own hand into a slim blank book she kept beneath the floorboards where the ledger used to live — because some things should survive fire, and this was one of them.

Winter came, and with winter, work.

The atlas was finished — the true atlas, the one that had cost Master Corvane his name and very nearly cost Petra everything else. It did not look like anything remarkable on a shelf: a folio of harbor charts, coastal profiles, soundings, the sort of thing any commercial house might produce. But read with the key — and the key lived now only in Petra's memory, Elias's memory, and the widow's long memory — the plates opened onto something else entirely. A route through the marsh cut marked *dangerous shoals* that was passable at half-flood by shallow boats that drew less than four feet. A fisherman's cellar in Lowbridge marked as a *storehouse* that held twelve people comfortably and twenty badly. The sequence of attic windows in the Weavers' Row, and which three stayed lit past curfew, and what that meant.

Corvane had built a city inside the city, a Greyhaven of mercy laid over the Greyhaven of law like a second coastline drawn beneath the first. And now Petra walked that hidden country weekly, delivering pages.

They did not give anyone the whole atlas. That was the first rule, and Petra had written it herself, in her own hand, on the first page of her own ledger: *No one carries what they cannot bear to lose.* Instead she copied single plates — a marsh route for the woman who ferried fugitives downriver; a Weavers' Row diagram for the tailor whose shop sat beneath the attics; the Lowbridge cellar for the cooper who owned it and had never known, until Petra told him, what his own beams had been sheltering for six years. Each copy was drawn fresh, coded differently, traceable to no other page in the world. If Hollis took one carrier, he took one page, and a single page of an atlas is a fragment of coastline: useless without the sea around it.

Elias handled the routes that crossed the water. He had a genius for harbors, for the grammar of manifests and the body language of harbor watchmen, and he had — Petra had come to understand this slowly, watching him work — a talent for being forgotten. Men remembered faces that wanted remembering. Elias had spent his whole life training his face to want nothing, and now that nothingness carried people to safety.

And Widow Amberdale — the widow played whist with the wives of two customs officers and a port assessor, lost gracefully, laughed at the right jokes, and came home twice a month with intelligence wrapped in gossip so thick it took Petra an hour to unwrap it. Which inspection houses had new men. Which clerks had debts. Which ships were taking an unusual interest in small craft.

As for Magistrate Hollis: Hollis had the forgery.

He had it locked, they believed, in the strongroom beneath the customs hall, and it was beautiful — Elias's forged annotations in Corvane's hand were indistinguishable even to Petra, and she had watched them being made. The poisoned route led to a warehouse on Cordwainer Staithe that had once, briefly, belonged to one of Hollis's rivals, and if the magistrate ever moved on it he would raid the property of a man who would make Hollis's life a prolonged and public misery. But Hollis had not yet moved. He sat on his prize like a man holding a winning card he could not quite bring himself to play, and every week he did not play it was another week the true pages traveled the marsh roads and the fish carts and the oyster-man's extremely talkative — and extremely discreet — mouth.

"Suspicion nothing yet," the widow observed one evening, dealing cards to no one, laying out patience in the lamplight. "That man suspects everything except the one thing worth suspecting. He thinks the atlas is a *thing*. A book you can lock up. Corvane understood it never was a book. It was a way of seeing." She turned over the queen of spades and studied it. "You understand that too. That's why he chose you, whatever he told you about your steady hands."

"My hands aren't steady," Petra said.

"No," the widow agreed placidly. "But your lines are. There's a difference, child. Steady hands belong to people who've never been afraid. Yours belong to someone who decided the fear doesn't get a vote."

* * *

On the first anniversary of the sailing, the fog came back.

It came the way it always did in Greyhaven, rolling up from the water in the late afternoon until the streetlamps burned like eggs in cups and the whole city seemed to have been dipped in milk and set aside to dry. Petra had finished the day's copying — a plate for the ferryman, a new route, her own cipher — and she climbed the stairs to the flat roof of the map house with a cup of tea going cold in her hand, because there was one thing she did up there each year on this date, and she preferred to do it unobserved.

Below her, Greyhaven glowed dimly through the fog. Somewhere down that way was the tide gate. Somewhere beyond it, past the horizon she could not see and could draw from memory to within a cable's length, was a fishing village where a boy measured stars and a deaf cook taught schoolmistresses bad signs, and a chandler's clerk paid his debts in honest coin.

"We've had word," Elias said from the roof door. He had followed her up; he always followed her up, and she had stopped pretending to mind. "Fish market channel. Tam's made journeyman — the net-mender took him on full. And there's a family coming through Saltmere next month. Six of them, from the eastern provinces. They'll need the marsh route and the Lowbridge cellar."

"Then I'd best draw the marsh route again," Petra said. "My pen is getting slow."

Her pen was not slow. Her pen had never been slower or finer. But Elias heard the ritual in it, as she had heard the ritual in *we've had word*, and he came and stood at the parapet beside her, and for a while they watched the fog together, saying nothing, which between the two of them had long ago stopped being silence and become simply another room they shared.

"Grief and peace," the widow had said once, describing marriage, or perhaps describing life; with her it had been hard to tell. "They're not opposites, whatever the poets claim. They're neighbors. They share a wall. Some nights you hear both of them moving about, and you let them be."

Petra felt them now, both of them, moving about in the rooms of her: grief for the master who had died a lie and lived a harder truth; grief for the berths she could not multiply, the arithmetic that no amount of cleverness had ever made kind; peace in the lamp-lit kitchen below, where the widow was cheating at solitaire and the kettle had just gone on; peace in the ledger under the floor with its growing pages in her own hand, her own codes, her own stubborn, unsteady, unshakeable line.

Belonging, she had thought once, was a place you escaped to — some shore over the horizon where the hunted could set down their names. She knew better now. Belonging was the tide gate in the fog. It was a lamp in a window meaning *you are expected.* It was a family scattered across a sea and gathered nightly at a table, and a city full of strangers' cellars and attics and marsh channels, stitched together page by patient page by a mapmaker's apprentice who had finally understood what her master had been drawing all along.

Not coastlines.

Refuge.

"Elias," she said, when the tea was fully cold and the fog had thickened until the harbor was only a rumor. "The family coming next month. Six of them, you said?"

"Six. Two children."

Petra nodded slowly, and in her mind the pen was already moving, laying down the first sure line of a new plate — and then she paused, because below them, in the fog-bound street, a figure had stopped before the map house. He stood in the lamplight from their window for a long moment, hatless, thin, looking up at the building the way a man looks at a house he once lived in and has no right to enter.

Then he raised his hand — not waving. Measuring.

Petra's heart stopped, started, and began to run.
