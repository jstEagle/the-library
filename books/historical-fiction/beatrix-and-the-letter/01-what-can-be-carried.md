## 1. What Can Be Carried

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width of tallow when Felix Galloway began to read.

She read the way she did everything else: quickly, completely, once. The dispatch lay open on the table before her, its seal already broken, its wax crumbs scattered like dried blood across the boards of the safehouse floor. Outside, Vessany City was coughing itself to sleep — the groan of cart wheels on wet cobbles, a drunk singing three streets over with the optimism of a man who did not know what winter was coming, and beneath it all, that new sound the city had learned this year, the one no one named aloud: the distant, rhythmic knocking of the armories running through the night shifts, hammering out bayonets for an army that was losing faster than it could be armed.

Felix read. She did not move her lips. She did not trace the lines with her finger. Nine years had taught her body stillness the way a river teaches its stones smoothness — not by force but by constant, patient insistence.

*Third Army repositioning from the Verel Heights to the Kostin Line by month's end. Ammunition reserves at Karst border forts to be drawn down and redistributed westward. The garrison commanders are not to be informed of the reason.*

She read it twice more, because the last line was the kind of sentence that wanted reading twice. *Not to be informed of the reason.* Somewhere in the ministry above her, in rooms she had never entered and never would, men were deciding which lies their own soldiers would die believing, and they had chosen her — not by name, because they did not know her name; none of them knew her name — but by function, to carry that decision east before the roads closed.

The roads closed soon. Everyone could feel it now, the way animals feel weather. The Republic of Vessany had been dying for years, but dying was a slow art, and lately it had begun to hurry.

Felix set the dispatch down. She closed her eyes.

And she recited it back.

This was the discipline that kept her alive, and like all disciplines that keep a person alive, it looked from the outside like nothing at all. A woman sitting very still in a cold room with her eyes shut. But inside her skull the words arranged themselves in their proper order, each clause finding its neighbor, the numbers settling into their places like coins into a purse — *Verel Heights, Kostin Line, month's end, drawdown, redistribution westward, garrison commanders uninformed* — until the whole document stood complete in her memory, weightless, unburnable, unstealable. It would live there now as long as she needed it to live. Longer, if she let it. Some of them stayed whether she wanted them or not. There were orders sewn into her sleep from four years ago that surfaced sometimes in dreams, troop strengths and supply figures drifting up through the dark like drowned men rising briefly to touch the surface before sinking again.

She opened her eyes and picked up the candle.

Paper burns best from the corner, held low so the flame climbs. She had learned this early, from a man whose name she had also burned, in a sense — her first handler, a gray, patient clerk who smelled of ink and boiled mutton and who told her, in the flat voice of a man reciting scripture he no longer believed in, the three rules.

*You are invisible. You are unattached. You ask nothing.*

He had said it the way other men taught children prayers. And like a child's prayer, she had repeated it ten thousand times since, until the words stopped being words and became simply the shape of her life, the way a key worn smooth by use stops being metal and becomes the shape of the lock it serves.

The dispatch caught. Fire walked up the page with small, greedy sounds, and the letters curled and blackened — *Kostin Line* folding in on itself, the numbers blooming orange and vanishing — and Felix watched with the particular attention she gave to every burning, because attention was the only mourning she permitted herself. Every document she destroyed was a small funeral. Not for the paper. For the version of herself that might have been someone who kept things.

When the flame reached her fingers she dropped the last scrap into the chipped basin on the table and pressed her fingertips against the cold iron, one hand then the other, until the sting faded. Her hands were good hands. Narrow, strong, unremarkable. A courier's hands. They had carried perhaps four hundred dispatches across nine years of war and collapse, and they bore no scars worth noticing, because a courier who collected noticeable scars did not remain a courier long.

The ash in the basin was gray and fine as moth wings. Later she would scatter it in the privy and flush it into the city's great gray bloodstream, where all secrets went eventually, and where even the most treasonous sentence in Vessany became indistinguishable from everything else people wanted gone.

She banked the candle and sat in the dark.

* * *

The safehouse was a room above a shuttered dyeworks in the Tanners' Quarter, rented under a name that belonged to a woman who had died of fever two winters past and would not be missed by anyone with the authority to ask questions. It contained a bed, a stool, a table, a basin, a cold stove, and a window that looked out on a courtyard where rain had spent the evening writing and rewriting the same long sentence across the flagstones.

That was all. That was everything.

Felix had lived in forty-one rooms like it. She knew this number precisely, because knowing it precisely was part of the discipline. Forty-one rooms in nine years, in eleven cities and towns, and she could have listed them in order if some tribunal had ever demanded it — the attic in Serevan with the wasps in the eaves, the canal-side loft in Ostmere where she'd heard a man drown one foggy night and had lain awake listening and done nothing, because doing something would have meant being seen, and being seen was death by inches. The farmhouse outside Pell with the leaking roof. The two weeks in a wine cellar in the capital's Third District, breathing yeast and darkness, waiting out a purge that took eleven couriers and both of the handlers who knew hers.

Forty-one rooms. One satchel. Three rules.

The satchel hung on its nail by the door, scarred leather gone dark with handling, its buckle replaced twice, its strap mended with thread that didn't quite match. Everything she owned fit inside it with room to spare. Two shirts. A wool dress. A knife, a flint, a spoon. A sewing kit, because a courier who couldn't mend her own gear couldn't mend anything. Papers — the blank ones, the beautiful blank ones, identity writs with seals but no names, the currency of her trade, documents that made her whoever the road required: a grain merchant's widow, a clerk traveling to see a sick sister, nobody, anybody, the woman who was no one at all. And at the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, the thing she had carried longest of all and never delivered anywhere: a child's shoe, small enough to sit in her palm, cracked with age, belonging to a girl who would be fourteen now if the world had allowed it.

She did not look at the satchel. She knew what was in it the way she knew the dispatches — completely, without looking. That was the whole architecture of her survival. Nothing carried that wasn't necessary. Nothing remembered that wasn't useful. Nothing loved that could be taken.

The stove was cold. She had decided against lighting it. Heat left traces — smoke smell in cloth, soot on the chimney, warmth in a room that a landlady's curious hand might find on a doorframe. Cold was safer. Cold was the price of the life, paid nightly, in full.

So she sat on the bed in her coat with her boots on — always the boots on; a courier caught bootless was a courier caught — and listened to the city fail.

It failed beautifully, in its way. That was the terrible secret of collapsing nations: they made more noise dying than living. The bells rang wrong hours because the bell-keepers had fled or been conscripted. The patrols in the streets called their challenges to each other in voices pitched too high, boys given rifles and authority in the same week. Somewhere north of the river a building was burning, and the glow of it stained the clouds a dull copper, and no engines came, because half the engines had no horses and the other half had no men. The Republic of Vessany, which had stood for two hundred years and printed its eagle on everything from treaties to bread wrappers, was going out like a lamp in a draft — flickering, guttering, and everyone in the room pretending not to watch.

Felix watched. Watching was permitted. Watching cost nothing.

She thought about the dispatch she had just eaten with fire. *Garrison commanders not to be informed of the reason.* In the morning she would carry the words east, thirty leagues through mud and checkpoints, and speak them into the ear of a colonel who would go pale and sign a receipt, and neither of them would discuss it, because discussion was not in her contract and comprehension was not in her nature. She was a throat, not a mind. Words passed through her and were not digested.

That was what she told herself, anyway. It was one of the stories she kept, the way other people kept locks of hair.

Because here was the truth she did not say, even to herself, even in the privacy of her own skull where no handler could burn it: she understood every word she carried. Had always understood them. Understood them the way a poor woman understands the arithmetic of bread — instantly, totally, without any education required. When she carried a requisition order that stripped a town's granaries, she knew what the town would eat that winter. When she carried a redeployment, she knew which villages would wake to find the soldiers gone and the enemy arriving by afternoon. Nine years of carrying the republic's decisions had made her a map of everything the republic was willing to lose, and the map grew larger every year, and lately it seemed to Felix that the map was nearly the size of the country itself.

She asked nothing. That was the third rule, and it was the deepest one, deeper than invisibility, because a person could hide her face easily enough but hiding her questions required a different kind of strength entirely. For nine years she had swallowed every question the way she swallowed the dispatches' meanings — whole, silently, without water.

*Why am I carrying this? Who wrote it? Who profits? What happens to the people on the other end of these words?*

Questions were attachments. Attachments were weight. And everything she owned fit in a satchel, and everything she remembered fit behind her eyes, and everything she felt fit nowhere at all, which was why she tried to carry as little of it as possible.

The rain in the courtyard went on writing its sentence. The copper glow over the northern rooftops dimmed as the fire found only wet ruins to eat. From the armories came the knocking, the knocking, the patient industrial heartbeat of a state manufacturing the instruments of its own defeat.

Felix pulled her coat tighter and rehearsed tomorrow's route in the dark. Bridge at Millen's Ford, open till dawn. The east road through Harrow Fen. First checkpoint at Ashford Gate — Captain there was said to be courteous, thorough, bored; boredom was the best emotion a checkpoint could contain. She laid the route out in her mind mile by mile, the way she laid out every route, and felt the old cold comfort settle over her like a second coat: the comfort of the plan, the comfort of the load measured and known, the comfort of a life that could be packed in five minutes and leave no dent in any bed.

This is what I am, she thought, in the phrase she used instead of prayer. I am what can be carried. Nothing more. Nothing heavier.

She slept the way she always slept: lightly, boots on, knife within reach, the day's burned dispatch standing guard in her memory.

* * *

The knock came three hours before dawn.

Felix was awake before the second knock — awake and off the bed and against the wall beside the window, breath shallow, listening. No knock was ever good. In nine years, no knock had ever once been good. Good news traveled by dead drop and chalk mark and folded paper under a loose brick; knocks were for soldiers, for informers, for the wives of arrested men come to ask questions that had answers no one survived giving.

The knock came again. Soft. Unhurried. Almost apologetic.

And beneath it, threading through the gap under the door of the room below, through the stairwell of the shuttered dyeworks, up into the cold of her room, came another sound.

A cry. Thin, furious, exhausted — the unmistakable complaint of an infant that has been crying long enough to have passed through grief and out the other side into pure outrage.

Felix stood very still against the wall, one hand on her knife, and felt nine years of discipline hold her in place like ice holds a stone.

*Invisible. Unattached. Ask nothing.*

The knock came a fourth time. Then silence, except for the crying, which did not stop, and the rain, which did not stop, and far away the armories, which did not stop either, hammering and hammering at the dark.

On the landing below, something small shifted against her door. Something that had been set down carefully, deliberately, by someone who had not waited to be answered.

Felix Galloway, who asked nothing, who carried nothing she could not burn, who had spent nine years becoming the shape of an empty satchel — stood in the dark and listened to a child cry on the other side of her door, and understood, with the cold clarity of a woman reading a dispatch by candlelight, that whatever waited down those stairs, it had been addressed to her.

Someone knew where she was.

For the first time in nine years, someone knew exactly where she was.
