## 2. The Weight at the Door

The crying did not stop. That was the first thing. Felix had expected — had hoped, in the way one hopes for small mercies without admitting to hoping — that the child would exhaust itself, that whoever had left it would return, that the whole arrangement would dissolve back into the ordinary machinery of her nights: candle, dispatch, memorization, flame.

But the crying went on, thin and relentless as water finding its way through a roof, and Felix Galloway stood on the stairs with her hand flat against the banister and understood that no one was coming. The stairwell held only her own breath and the sound from below, and beneath both, the river-hum of Vessany's last honest month, the city murmuring to itself the way cities do when they know something is ending and have agreed not to say so out loud.

She descended the last four steps the way she had been taught to descend every staircase in nine years of service: heel first, weight committed slowly, eyes moving over the landing before her body entered it. Old habits. The habits were all she owned outright.

The bundle lay against her door the way a package lies — squared, deliberate, arranged. Someone had set it down with care, tucking the blanket's edge under the child's chin, angling the small face away from the draft that came under the door like a slow gray animal. A folded paper was weighted against the blanket by a smooth stone, the kind of stone a child keeps, the kind that lives in pockets.

Felix did not touch any of it. Not yet.

She looked instead at the door itself, at the frame, at the hinges, reading them the way other people read letters. No scratch of forced entry. The wax she kept pressed along the jamb — invisible to anyone who didn't know to look for it, a hairline seam of gray in the gray wood — was unbroken. No one had gone in. Whatever this was, it had not come to take anything from her safehouse. It had come to give.

That was worse.

She crouched, keeping her weight on the balls of her feet, and looked at the child. A girl, she saw — though the swaddling made it nearly impossible to say, the blanket wound tight and competent, the work of someone who had done it many times or once with terrible concentration. The face was red with effort, eyes squeezed shut, mouth open around the cry, fists curled up beside her ears in the universal gesture of accusation. Newborn or near it. Days old. Perhaps a week. Small enough that Felix's first absurd thought was that she could carry her in the satchel, and her second thought, arriving hard on the heels of the first, was so obscene that she pressed it down the way she pressed down everything else.

*Someone knew where she was.*

Nine years. Nine years of rotating safehouses, of never sleeping two months in the same bed, of names worn like gloves and discarded before they wore through. Nine years of being the woman who was nobody, who asked nothing, who stood in ministries and garrisons and border offices while men with power glanced through her as if she were weather. And now, on the worst possible landing in the worst possible city, in the hour before the republic she served finished dying, an infant cried at her door with her name written somewhere near it — because surely it was written somewhere near it, because bundles did not simply arrive, they were *sent* —

She picked up the note.

Not the child. The note. She noted, distantly, with something that might have been self-knowledge, that she had chosen the note first, and filed the observation away in the place where she kept all her uglier truths.

Three words. A heavy cream paper, expensive, the kind that ministers wrote on and burned and wrote again. The handwriting slanted rightward, quick but controlled, the letters of a man accustomed to signing his name into law:

*forget the face*

Nothing else. No name. No instruction beyond those three words, which were not instructions at all but a verdict, a sentence handed down before the trial, before even the crime. Felix read it twice. Then a third time, though the words refused to change, refused to grow context around themselves the way words usually did. They sat on the page like three stones dropped in a well.

And the hand — she knew the hand.

She knew it the way a blind woman knows footsteps on her own stairs. Nine years of carrying sealed dispatches had given her no access to their contents, but it had given her their envelopes, their superscriptions, their seals broken and re-broken in her presence, and above all their cover sheets: *To the Commandant at Ashford Gate. To the Ministry of the Interior, by courier, urgent.* Hundreds of them. Thousands. All in that same rightward-slanting hand, that same peculiar hooked *g*, that same impatient crossing of the *t* that left a small spur like a thorn.

Minister Corvane.

Her stomach performed a slow, cold revolution. Corvane. The Minister for the Interior, whose dispatches she carried more than any other's, whose orders moved through her satchel the way blood moves through a body — invisibly, essentially, unquestioned. Corvane, who was said to be negotiating the armistice. Corvane, whose signature would soon be on whatever document decided whether Vessany continued to exist.

Corvane, who knew where she lived, because he signed the allowances that paid for it.

*Forget the face.*

Whose face? The child's? Her own? The imperative sat strangely, grammatically unfinished, as though a longer sentence had been written and then cut down — as though the minister had begun with something like *I could not keep her and I cannot look upon her and so you must* and then struck everything but the command, because commands were what he knew how to write.

The crying climbed a register. Felix became aware, with a distant irritation that surprised her, that she was cataloguing its variations the way she catalogued hoofbeats and footsteps — the exhausted wail, the hungry wail, the wail that was simply rage at existing. This one was hunger. She knew hunger when she heard it. She had heard her own, often enough, in the early years after the arrests, when invisibility meant learning to eat cheaply and rarely.

She should leave it. That was the correct answer, arrived at instantly, the way all her correct answers arrived. Leave the bundle on the step. Walk away. Request reassignment through the dead-drop at the cooper's yard, abandon this safehouse permanently, let the child become some neighbor's problem, some constable's, some charity's — anyone's but hers. A courier with a dependent was a courier with a leash around her own throat. Every rule she lived by said the same thing in the same voice.

But the rules had a flaw in them, she discovered, standing there in the dark with the note going soft in her grip. The flaw was that the rules assumed she felt nothing. And she had spent nine years building that assumption into a load-bearing wall, and now here was a child crying at her door with a minister's handwriting on the floor beside it, and the wall did not fall, exactly — but she felt it shift.

She looked up and down the landing. Empty. Below, the courtyard gate hung as it always hung, off its lower hinge. Above, the other tenants' windows were dark; the widow on the third floor slept like the dead and the students on the fourth had not come home. Whoever had left the bundle was gone, and gone cleanly, and had known enough about this building to choose the hour.

Which meant whoever left it knew about the widow. Knew about the students. Knew about the gate.

Knew her life the way her handlers knew her life.

Felix closed her eyes for precisely three seconds — she counted them, an old discipline — and when she opened them, the decision had already been made somewhere beneath her reasoning, the way decisions always were with her; the reasoning merely followed behind, writing the justification afterward like a clerk following an officer.

She gathered up the child.

The weight of her was nothing and enormous. Perhaps five pounds of warmth wrapped in wool, and yet Felix's arms, which had carried dispatch cases across three war winters without complaint, trembled slightly at the transfer, as though she were lifting something hot. The child's crying stuttered — startled by the change, the sudden altitude — then resumed, quieter now, hiccupping, indignant.

"All right," Felix said, and was disturbed to hear her own voice, low and unpracticed, saying a word she had not said aloud in this room in years. "All right."

Inside, she did not light the lamp. She worked by the street-glow through the shutters, by habit and by instinct, because a lit window was an admission and she admitted nothing to this city, not ever. She set the child on the table — the plain deal table where she memorized dispatches, where she burned them, where she ate her single meals standing up — and unwound the top fold of the swaddling just far enough to see.

A girl. Dark hair, damp at the temples. Fists still raised. Eyes opening now, unfixed, the color of storm water, looking at the ceiling and through it at nothing Felix could name.

And beneath the blanket's second fold, pressed flat against the small body where a mother might have tucked a warming stone, she found the paper.

It was folded eight times, compressed into a square no larger than a playing card, and it was not cream-colored. It was cheap stock, the gray-blue stock of ministry field offices, the kind used for cipher work — and Felix knew it on sight because she had carried hundreds of sheets exactly like it, sealed in oilskin, forbidden ever to be opened, forbidden even to be looked at too long lest curiosity show on her face where someone might read it.

Cipher sheets were never left lying in swaddlings. Cipher sheets were accounted for like ammunition. Their existence outside a ministry strongroom meant theft, or treason, or both.

She turned it over twice, feeling the faint ridges of the pin-drum impressions through the paper, the braille-like punctures of a working key. It had been used. Recently — the folds were crisp, unweathered. Someone had enciphered something on this sheet within days. Someone had then hidden it against an infant's body, wrapped the infant in wool, carried her through the dark to a courier's door, and written three words in a minister's hand.

*Forget the face.*

Felix stood very still in the unlit room, and the fear that had been sitting in her chest since the stairs underwent a change of state, the way water changes at the freezing point — same substance, new nature. Because there were only two readings, and she could see both of them now, laid out side by side like two roads on a map.

In the first, the child was a burden. A minister's inconvenient by-blow, smuggled out of some house before scandal could find her, delivered to the one person in the capital guaranteed to have no family, no connections, no questions. Cruel, but comprehensible. Powerful men had been disposing of embarrassments since power was invented, and couriers were convenient because couriers were invisible.

In the second, the child was a message. And the cipher sheet was its text. And *forget the face* was not a plea for secrecy but an order for destruction — the kind of order that followed evidence, that traveled ahead of witnesses, that ended in cellars and rivers and unmarked graves. In the first reading, Corvane had trusted her. In the second, Corvane had *used* her, had made her the last step in making a thing disappear, because who searches the invisible woman? Who follows the courier who asks nothing?

And either way — this was the part that made her hands go steady again, the cold professional core of her taking over the way it always did — either way, she was now holding a piece of Minister Corvane's business that he could not afford to have held by anyone. Which meant either way, her life had changed value overnight, and she needed to know which direction.

The child made a sound. Not a cry this time. Something smaller — a hiccough, a sigh, the tentative first syllable of a new complaint — and then a fist escaped the blanket's edge and flailed upward and caught Felix's finger.

Caught it. Gripped it.

It was nothing. Reflex, the midwives called it; every infant did it; it meant nothing at all. Felix knew this. She had nine years of evidence that knowing things was how she stayed alive, and among the things she knew was that a newborn's grip was a mechanical accident, a circuit closing in undeveloped flesh, signifying nothing.

The small fist did not signify anything. It simply held on — astonishingly strong for its size, the fingers surprisingly long, a musician's fingers maybe, or a forger's, or a soldier's, impossible to say — and held on with the whole uncomplicated conviction of a creature who had been alive for six days and had already decided that this particular warm thing was hers, that it would stay, that holding was sufficient.

Felix stood in the dark over the deal table with a treasonous cipher in one pocket and a minister's note in the other and a child's fist locked around her finger, and felt the fear in her chest do one final thing, the thing she would think about for the rest of her life: it curdled. Fear into something warmer and far more dangerous. Not love — she would not call it that, not then, not for a long time — but its rough draft. Its first sketch. The tenderness of a woman who had arranged her entire existence around touching nothing discovering that something had touched *her*, had reached out in the dark of an unlit room and taken hold, and would not, apparently, be letting go.

"You are," she said quietly, "the single worst thing that has ever been delivered to me."

The child opened her storm-water eyes and regarded the ceiling with the grave, unimpressed expression of someone reviewing a subordinate's report.

"And you," Felix added, "weigh almost nothing. That is the trouble with you. You weigh almost nothing, and you have changed everything."

Outside, far off toward the river, a church bell tolled the third quarter-hour, and beneath it — or perhaps she imagined it, or perhaps she did not, and this was the thought that finally sent her moving, banking the fire of her indecision — she thought she heard wheels. A carriage, somewhere in the wet streets, moving at the unhurried pace of a man who has finished his errand and is going home to sleep.

Felix took her finger back — the small fist released it reluctantly, renegotiating, gripping air — and lifted the cipher sheet into the window's thin light. Eight folds. A working key. Corvane's hand on the note above it like a signature on a confession.

Nine years of not asking questions. Nine years of burning what she memorized and walking away clean.

She sat down at the table with the child asleep against her shoulder, and unfolded the paper.
