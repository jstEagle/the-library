## 3. Nine Years Invisible

Nine years before the child came to her door, Felix Galloway learned that the surest way to survive a revolution was to become the kind of person no one remembered.

She had not always been forgettable. There had been a time—she could still find it if she pressed hard on the memory, like pressing on an old bruise—when she was the loud one of the Galloway children, the one who sang on the staircases of their house on Verren Street and argued politics with her father over supper until her mother threatened to serve dinner to the chairs instead. Her father had been a clerk in the Ministry of Provisions, a small man with ink-stained cuffs and a laugh that filled rooms he had no business filling. Her mother mended lace for the wives of men whose names appeared in the gazette. Her brother Tam wanted to be a soldier and practiced his salutes with a broom handle. Felix, at sixteen, wanted everything. That was the trouble. Wanting everything made you visible.

* * *

The night the soldiers came for her family, it was raining in the way Vessany rained that autumn—not falling so much as hanging in the air, a grey suspension that soaked through wool and wood and bone. Felix had been out. She would spend years arranging and rearranging that fact, testing its weight like a coin she suspected was clipped: *if I had been home, they would have taken me too; because I was out, I lived.* She had gone to hear a pamphleteer speak in the old tannery district, someone with a voice like a cracked bell railing against the Provisional Council, and she had stayed late arguing with strangers about whether the republic was dying or merely sleeping.

By the time she came home, the house on Verren Street had stopped being hers.

The door stood open on darkness. Rain had gotten inside and pooled on the floorboards, and in that water lay her father's spectacles, folded shut, one lens starred with a crack. She knew at once—before she saw the overturned chair, before she noticed the drawer of her mother's workbasket pulled out and gutted, spools of thread scattered like entrails—that they were not coming back. The state did not return what it took. Everyone in the capital understood this the way they understood weather: without needing it explained.

She stood in the doorway for a long time. A neighbor saw her there, Mrs. Halloran from two doors down, who had once given her plum cake and gossip in equal measure. Mrs. Halloran looked at her, looked through her, and drew her own shutters closed. Later Felix would understand that this, too, was survival—the neighbor's as much as anyone's—but that night it felt like watching her own funeral from outside her body. The city had already decided she was no one. It had simply neglected to inform her.

She did not cry. That surprised her afterward. She had expected grief to be loud, operatic, a tearing of clothes in the street; instead it arrived as a strange, ringing quiet, as though a bell had been struck inside her chest and was still vibrating somewhere below hearing. She gathered what she could carry: her father's spare spectacles, a knife, forty crowns sewn by her mother into a coat lining against exactly such a night, and a photograph she could not bear to look at and could not bear to leave. Then she walked out of the house that was no longer a house, and she did not look back, because looking back was how people got seen, and being seen was how people got taken.

* * *

The first winter she slept where she could—in a chapel that had been deconsecrated twice, once by fire and once by decree; in the luggage room of a bathhouse whose owner asked no questions because questions cost money; once, memorably, in the coal cellar of a magistrate who never learned of it. Hunger taught her things. It taught her that a person can walk past bread and not steal it if stealing means being noticed. It taught her that crowds are shelter but also exposure, that markets are full of eyes, that soldiers on patrol count faces without knowing they do it, and that the face that gives them nothing to count is the face they let pass.

She learned, above all, to be dull.

This was a discipline like any other, and she practiced it as rigorously as Tam had once practiced his salutes. She lowered her voice and flattened its music. She chose clothes the color of wet pavement. She developed a way of walking—unhurried, slightly stooped, the gait of a woman going somewhere uninteresting for reasons that were nobody's business—and a way of standing at counters, and a way of meeting eyes just long enough to seem honest and not a moment longer. She gave her name only when law required it, and even then she watched how the syllables landed: *Galloway*. The name was on lists now. She felt them sometimes, those lists, the way one feels a change in pressure before a storm. So she became Fen. Then, when Fen began to feel worn, simply *the woman from the lodging house*. Names were handles, and handles existed so that someone could take hold of you.

In the spring, the thing that saved her found her the way most dangerous things find people in collapsing states: through an acquaintance of an acquaintance, over weak tea in a room that smelled of mildew and printer's ink.

The woman's name was Sera Ilves—no relation, though the name would come to mean something very different to Felix years later—a grey, competent person with the manner of a schoolmistress and the eyes of an accountant. She represented nothing so grand as a ministry. She represented, she said, *continuity*. The Provisional Council was rotting from the head down, she said, but the body of the republic—the roads, the granaries, the garrisons, the clerks—still breathed, and something had to keep the blood moving between the parts while the doctors argued.

"You want a courier," Felix said.

"I want someone who can stand in a room full of frightened people and not be one of them." Sera turned her teacup slowly, not drinking. "You've been invisible for eight months. Don't look startled; we notice people who are good at not being noticed. It's the rarest skill in the capital right now."

"I'm not good at anything," Felix said. It was true in the way she meant all her statements then—true enough to pass any inspection.

"Good," Sera said. "Keep believing that. Believing it is half the craft."

What Sera offered was not glory. She was explicit about that. Couriers in Vessany's civil service died of fevers, of flooded roads, of summary executions at checkpoints manned by men who had stopped reading papers and started reading faces. What Sera offered was purpose with a low profile: sealed dispatches carried between ministries and garrisons and the rump offices of officials who no longer trusted the post. Payment in coin and, more valuable, in *legitimacy*—a courier's seal, blank identity papers stamped by three different bureaus, the right to pass where others were stopped.

Felix thought of her father's spectacles lying in the rainwater. She thought of Tam practicing salutes with a broom handle, saluting an army that had come for him in the end with its boots on. She thought: *they took everyone who was visible.*

"Yes," she said.

* * *

Training lasted six weeks and consisted mostly of unlearning.

Sera stripped away everything Felix had ever done to make herself interesting. She mocked her posture, her vocabulary, her instinct to answer questions fully. "A courier answers the question asked," she said, "and then stops talking. Silence is not rudeness here. Silence is armor." She taught her the seals and ciphers—not the elegant diplomatic codes but the ugly working ones, grease-pencil marks on laundry lists, pinpricks under letters, the fold of a paper meaning *burn me*, the absence of a seal meaning *memorize and deny*. She taught her to read a checkpoint captain's boots before his face, because boots told you how long he'd been standing and standing men were impatient and impatient men were careless. She taught her to burn a dispatch in a way that left no legible corner, holding paper over a flame until it blackened evenly, crumbling it fine, scattering it in running water.

And she taught her the three rules, which Felix wrote nowhere and kept anyway, engraved somewhere below memory:

*Carry nothing that is not yours to carry. Ask nothing that you cannot afford to know. Be no one, and you will live to deliver what matters.*

"The temptation," Sera said on the last day, "will be to matter. Someone will thank you. Someone will tell you that you're the only reason the eastern garrison has ammunition, or that your little satchel saved four hundred lives, and you will feel yourself swell up like bread in water, and you will want to be *someone*, finally, after all these years of being no one." She fixed Felix with those accountant's eyes. "The moment you matter, girl, you're a target. Every spy, every faction, every frightened minister will need to know whose side you're on, and the answer 'no one's' will stop being believable. Stay small. Stay dull. Let them think the dispatches move themselves."

Felix nodded and meant it and did not yet understand it. Understanding would come later, bought at prices she couldn't foresee. But the shape of the discipline settled into her that day like a skeleton settling into flesh: *invisible, unattached, unasked.* Three words. She would carry them nine years, longer than any dispatch, longer than any seal.

* * *

The years that followed had a texture she could never adequately describe to anyone who hadn't lived them—which was no one, since describing required attachment and attachment required being someone. Roads. That was the texture. Roads in thaw and roads in frost; roads washed out and roads held; checkpoints where a bored lieutenant waved her through and checkpoints where a nervous boy soldier made her unpack her satchel item by item in the snow while her heartbeat counted itself against her ribs. She carried orders that moved battalions and orders that starved them. She carried a requisition that, she later pieced together, had sent a grain convoy to the wrong province and killed a winter's worth of farmers. She carried a pardon for a man she passed on the road, walking the other way in chains, and she said nothing, because asking was forbidden and mercy was not in her inventory.

Once—only once—she broke toward visibility. In her third year, delivering medicine stores to a garrison hospital, she saw a clerk she recognized from Verren Street days, a soft man named Odell who had dined at her parents' table twice and laughed at her father's jokes. He was in the hospital yard, prisoner-laborer's grey, shoveling ash, and he looked up at her and his mouth opened with recognition, and every rule she owned screamed at once.

She looked through him. She performed the dullness she had perfected—the stoop, the flat eyes, the slight impatience of a woman with deliveries to complete—and walked past while his face collapsed into confusion and then into the particular despair of a man realizing he has been erased by someone he used to know. That night, in a waystation loft, she lay awake listening to rats in the thatch and understood, with terrible clarity, that she had chosen. Not between Odell's life and death—she could not have saved him, and perhaps looking back would only have signed them both away—but between the person she had been and the instrument she was becoming. The instrument had walked past. The person had stayed behind in the ash, watching.

She grieved for him, and in grieving discovered the engine beneath her discipline. It had never been caution. Caution was only the mechanism. Underneath, turning all the gears, was the grief itself—the ringing bell in her chest that had never stopped vibrating since the rainwater and the cracked spectacles. Grief had made her invisible, and invisibility had kept her alive, and staying alive was the last duty she owed the Galloways of Verren Street. As long as she carried things—orders, medicines, the dumb arterial traffic of a dying state—she was not useless, and as long as she was not useless, the taking of her family had not quite succeeded. They had wanted to erase a household. She remained, unbearably, unnoticeably, the last entry in its ledger.

So she stayed small. She stayed dull. When ministers' aides flirted, she was wooden. When officers offered her wine and their opinions, she poured and listened and forgot on schedule. When Sera Ilves retired—or vanished; in those years the words were synonyms—Felix accepted her new seals from a succession of grey intermediaries and asked nothing, per the second rule, though the first and third rules did most of the daily work. Nine years. Her hands grew scarred from rope and cold. Her face learned to arrange itself into pleasant nothing. People forgot her within minutes of speaking to her; she had watched it happen, watched eyes slide off her like rain off oiled canvas, and each time she felt the same double sensation—relief, and beneath the relief, a grief so old it had become structural, load-bearing, part of the wall.

There was a comfort in it. She would not pretend otherwise. A life measured in what fits in a satchel is a light life; it can be lifted and moved; it leaves no shadow long enough for anyone to cut. She had a room in three cities, none of them home. She had coins buried in four places. She had skills instead of friends and routes instead of memories. On clear nights on high roads, when the republic spread out below her—its watchfires dimming year by year as provinces went dark, its roads closing one by one like veins silting shut—she permitted herself the cold comfort of her own arrangement: *when it ends, whenever it ends, I will walk away from it the way I walked away from Verren Street, carrying what fits, owing nothing, known by no one.*

Nine years she believed that.

Then, on a wet evening in the ninth autumn, in the safehouse off Tanner's Row where she kept her spare seals and slept four hours a night, she heard a sound through the door that did not belong to any category her discipline had prepared her for.

It was small. It was furious. It was alive.

It was crying.
