## 4. The Hand I Know

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width by the time Felix dared unroll the cipher sheet again.

She had tried to leave it alone. That was the honest truth of the night, and she would carry the shame of it quietly afterward: for three hours she had done everything a sensible woman could do to pretend the thing did not exist. She had swept a floor that needed no sweeping. She had re-latched the shutters twice, tested the loose brick behind the stove where her emergency coin lived, counted her remaining dispatch seals as though arithmetic could save her. She had even, in a moment of weakness she did not forgive herself for weeks, considered folding the sheet back into the swaddling, wrapping the child warm, carrying bundle and evidence both down to the canal steps, and leaving them where some other fool would find them.

She did not do it. But she thought it. And the thinking of it sat in her chest like swallowed river stone for the rest of her life.

Now the baby slept in the bread oven's warmth — cold oven, door ajar, the closest thing her safehouse had to a cradle — and Felix sat at the table with the cipher sheet flattened beneath two saucers and a stub of tallow, and worked.

* * *

Every courier learned the ministry ciphers the way a sailor learns weather: not by study exactly, but by immersion, until the knowledge seemed less like something acquired than something grown into the bones. Nine years of carrying sealed dispatches taught you things you were never meant to know. You learned that ministers wrote in haste and clerks copied worse. You learned which seal impressions came from wax warmed over a lamp and which from an office fire, which secretaries pressed too hard on the pen and which barely touched the page. You learned the rhythm of official lies — the particular stilted formality that bloomed whenever a dispatch said nothing at great length.

And you learned, if you were careless once or twice in nine years, and Felix had been careless exactly twice, what the ciphers looked like beneath their seals.

The first time, she was twenty-two and new, and the wind took a half-burned fragment from her brazier before the ink had finished surrendering. She read four words before feeding the rest to the flames. Four words were enough to teach her the shape of the grid, the alternating rotation of the letter-rows. The second time, two years later, was no accident at all — a clerk in the eastern annex, drunk and weeping about his conscripted son, showed her the key-sheet for the autumn traffic because he wanted someone to know what he knew. He was arrested within the month. Felix never learned on whose word. She made certain there was never anyone to give one.

So her hands knew this work. That was the horror of it, she realized as she bent over the sheet — how quickly the old fluency returned, how *comfortable* the labor felt, like walking a road walked in childhood. Her eyes moved down the columns and the columns gave up their secrets almost gratefully, as though the cipher had been waiting nine years for someone who could actually read it.

The document was written in the third ministry cipher, the one reserved for foreign correspondence — which was already wrong, already damning, because third-cipher traffic should have been addressed to Karstine's ministry through proper channels, exchanged under flag, logged and witnessed. This sheet had never been meant for channels. It had been meant for one pair of eyes across the border, carried by hand, deniable, disposable.

Carried by hand. By someone with a courier's blank papers and a habit of asking nothing.

By her, if it had come to that. The thought arrived without permission and would not leave.

* * *

Outside, Vessany's capital was dying by inches. She could hear it through the shutter seams — not the artillery yet, the artillery had gone quiet in the way that quiet is worse than sound, but the smaller noises of collapse: wheels in the small hours, a woman's voice raised and cut short, the bells of the Foundling Quarter ringing the hour wrong because somewhere a ringer had fled his tower mid-peal. The republic had been coming apart for a year the way wet rope comes apart, strand by strand, each fraying too small to name until suddenly nothing held.

Felix had told herself, all these years, that her work held none of that. That she was a hinge, not a hand. Papers moved; she moved them. What was written was written above her station, decided above her pay, and her innocence was not cowardice but craft — the professional emptiness of a woman who survived because no one had ever found anything inside her worth searching.

She believed it the way you believe a floor will hold. Not consciously. Only by never testing it.

She tested it now.

* * *

The first section decoded cleanly and was terrible in its plainness. Garrison rotations — the northern line, the Ashford corridor, the river forts at Drennmark. Not the peacetime rota published in the gazette, but the true disposition: which companies were understrength, which redoubts had guns but no shot, which stretches of the border wall were patrolled by men paid three months late and deserting at a hundred a week. Felix knew some of those garrisons by reputation only, but others she knew intimately, because she had *carried their orders*. She had personally delivered the dispatch that rotated the Third Borderers out of Drennmark Lower and sent green replacements in. She remembered the colonel's face when he read it. She had assumed his gray look was fatigue.

Every number on this sheet was a door left unlocked. Every annotation — *unreliable*, *undermanned*, *no reserve* — was a map of everywhere a knife could go in.

The second section named the buyer. The Karstine envoy, Baron Vosse-Hallem, was referenced three times in the oblique honorifics the cipher reserved for accredited foreign representatives, and once, chillingly, in plain substitution: *payment schedule as agreed at the autumn meeting.* Agreed. Scheduled. This was not theft of documents, not espionage in any sense she had been trained to fear. This was commerce. Someone was selling the republic the way a farmer sells a field — and the someone had access to everything.

There were perhaps six men with access to northern dispositions at this depth. She turned the candle toward the final column, where the decoding grew strange — the letters resisted, because the writer had used a private variation, a personal overlay keyed to some phrase or date. It took her an hour of trial, working backward from the formal closing formula every ministry letter wore like a uniform, before the last block surrendered.

It ended with a signature block. Ciphers did not usually carry signatures — the cipher itself was the signature — but whoever wrote this had wanted no ambiguity in the record, had wanted the buyer to hold proof against him forever, which told Felix more about the arrangement than any number of troop tables. And the signature block, decoded, contained a name and a title and a phrase she had seen ten thousand times at the foot of ten thousand dispatches she had carried without ever reading:

*Given under my hand, for the good of the state. R. Corvane.*

Minister Corvane.

Her hands stopped moving.

* * *

She did not feel shock, precisely. Shock was quick, and this was slow — a long cold entering, like water finding the seam of a boot. Because it was not merely that Corvane was guilty. It was that Corvane's hand was the hand she trusted most completely in the world, though she had never met him, never seen him beyond engravings in the gazette. For nine years, Minister Corvane's dispatches had been the fixed stars of her trade. His seals were always perfect. His couriers were treated with a deference other ministries' riders envied. When the roads closed in the spring crisis, it was Corvane's authority that reopened them. Once, in her second year, she had carried a personal commendation bearing his countersignature — a blank promotion chit, effectively, worth her life if inspected — and she had kept the memory of that paper the way other people kept love letters.

He had signed the orders that kept her employed, alive, invisible. He had, in the most literal sense available to a woman like her, *made* her. Every mile of road she had survived had been a road his ministry opened and his ministry protected. Her loyalty to the republic had thinned over the years into something abstract, theoretical — republics died, she had watched them — but her loyalty to the *office*, to the machine whose gears she was, had remained fierce and simple and entirely unexamined.

And the hand at the top of the machine had sold the border. Had sold, among other doors, Drennmark Lower — where the green replacements stood. Where, very possibly, boys she had watched playing cards in a courtyard while she waited for a receipt now stood in trenches pointed at Karstine like fruit on a branch.

The candle guttered. She trimmed it with shaking fingers, steadied herself against the table, and made herself finish. A decoder who stopped at the signature was a decoder who deserved the firing squad.

The last undeciphered passage proved to be a postscript, written in a hurry, the substitutions sloppier, the overlay half-abandoned. It took her only minutes. It said:

*The matter of the child is concluded. No record exists. Forget the face.*

The same words. The note at her door, in the same hand, was not a fresh instruction but a copy — a reminder to himself, or to a confederate, that the loose end had been tied off. Except it hadn't been, because the child was asleep in her cold oven, breathing in the small whistling way that Felix had already, without deciding to, learned to find bearable.

No record exists. But a record existed; she was holding it. The cipher sheet was Corvane's insurance against his buyer — proof of the pact, signed, dated, complete. Which meant the infant had been wrapped in the one document in Vessany that could hang her own father. Wrapped and delivered to a stranger's door by someone who either loved the minister enough to betray him or hated him enough to, and had chosen a courier precisely because couriers asked nothing.

*Forget the face,* Felix thought. *You forgot the paper.*

Or — the colder thought, arriving on the heels of the first — he hadn't forgotten anything. Perhaps the note and the child and the cipher together were a message aimed outward, a dead-drop confession, a man trying to smuggle his own guilt out of the country before the republic's fall buried it. Perhaps she was holding, in one hand, a treason and its intended exposure both, and had no way of knowing which purpose the bundle had truly been meant to serve.

That was the moment her loyalty cracked — not with a sound, but with the specific silence of ice underfoot when you understand, all at once, that the lake has been thin for a long time and you have been running across it for years.

* * *

The child woke near dawn, hungry and furious about it, and Felix fed her the weak milk-and-honey mixture Marta had shown her before going home to pack, holding the small head at the practiced angle and watching the fierce little jaw work. She did this mechanically. Her mind was elsewhere, walking corridors.

If she burned the cipher, she was safe. If she burned it, the child became only an abandoned baby, unsellable, unremarkable, a ward for the Foundling Quarter or for whatever charity survived the armistice. Burn it and forget the face, exactly as instructed, and live. The republic was falling regardless; the border would be sold or lost regardless; one woman's knowledge had never once, in nine years of observation, changed the direction of anything. That was practically her creed. Knowledge was cargo, and cargo got people killed.

She sat with the sheet in one hand and the child in the other, and the stove ticked as it cooled, and outside the wrong-rung bells rang five.

She thought about Drennmark Lower. About the colonel's gray face.

She thought: *someone should be made to say it aloud. In front of people. With the paper in view.*

And she recognized the thought as the most dangerous thing she had ever carried — more dangerous than any dispatch, because it could not be memorized and burned, because it had already left the satchel and taken up residence somewhere behind her ribs, and it weighed nothing and everything.

She folded the cipher small and flat, and she did not put it in the stove. She unpicked the seam of the swaddling cloth's inner band — the wide feeding-shawl the child's unknown benefactor had wound around her — and slid the folded paper into the channel of fabric, and sewed it shut with stitches so fine they might have been factory work. Evidence slept next to the child's heartbeat. Anyone searching for contraband would find milk stains and a baby. It was, she admitted, the best hiding place she had ever devised: no soldier in Vessany would search an infant thoroughly, and no officer would dare.

Then the knock came.

Three measured strikes on the street door — the ministry pattern, the pattern that had summoned her to a hundred roads, unhurried, authoritative, indifferent to the hour. At dawn. Before the armistice sealed everything.

Felix went still, the needle still in her fingers, the child warm against her shoulder. Nobody knew about the child. Nobody should know about the child. And nobody, in nine years, had ever knocked on this door at dawn except to send her somewhere.

She laid Beatrix — no, she corrected herself, the girl, the package, the burden — laid her gently in the oven-bed and crossed the room on silent feet to look through the shutter seam.

A ministry rider stood below in the blue-gray light, holding a sealed folio, and beside his horse stood a second horse already saddled for her.

Whatever the dispatch said, it had been decided she would leave immediately. Through the checkpoints. Toward the border.

Toward Karstine.
