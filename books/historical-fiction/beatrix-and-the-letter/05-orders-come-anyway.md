## 5. Orders Come Anyway

The knock came at dusk, three soft raps and then a fourth, delayed—the rhythm of the ministry itself, patient as a debt.

Felix was at the window with the child against her shoulder, watching the last grey light drain off the rooftops of the Weavers' Quarter. Three days she had been in the safehouse, three days of pretending this was rest rather than paralysis. The cipher sheet lay where it had lain since the second night: folded into eighths inside the false bottom of her dispatch case, wrapped now in oilcloth as though oilcloth could make it less true. She had not deciphered more of it. She told herself this was prudence. She knew it was fear. A document read can be denied; a document understood must be answered for.

The baby stirred at the knock, her small fist opening and closing against Felix's collarbone like a question.

"Quiet," Felix murmured, which was absurd—she hadn't made a sound—and moved to the wall where the floorboard lifted.

The fourth rap settled it. Only one person knocked that way: the ministry's own runners, trained to signal completion rather than request entry. Which meant whoever stood below had been sent by someone who believed the roads still functioned, who believed there was still a ministry to be run, who did not yet know—or chose not to know—how much of Vessany now existed only on paper and in the mouths of men insisting upon it.

She opened the door on a boy of perhaps sixteen, rain-beaded, breath steaming, holding out a leather folio stamped with the triple-tower seal. His eyes went past her shoulder to the bundle, lingered half a heartbeat, and returned to her face with the careful blankness the ministry taught even its children. He had seen nothing. He would report nothing. That was the etiquette of the collapsing state: everyone pretending not to see the water rising around them.

"Signature," he said.

She signed. He waited while she broke the folio's wax—Corvane's seal again, the minister's crest pressed deep and clean, a man whose stamp never wavered—and read.

*To the courier designated G-7.*

G-7. Not her name; she had no name the ministry recognized, only a designation, a route number, nine years of sealed leather passing through her hands unopened. Seeing the designation written out in the minister's own upright hand produced in her a strange, cold flicker of the old comfort—she was still invisible, still a function—and beneath it, immediately, its opposite: *he knows exactly whose hands these are. He chose them.*

*You will carry one final dispatch under seal to the border commission at Verenne-Haut. You will depart within two days. You will pass the checkpoints at Ashford Gate, the Tollhouse at Marren Bridge, and the garrison post at Cail Serein, presenting this letter of passage at each. The armistice lines close in eleven days. After that, no civilian traffic crosses east of the river. Fail to arrive before the seals fall, and the dispatch dies with you.*

There was more—the usual clauses, the usual threats dressed as courtesies—but Felix had stopped reading with her eyes and started reading with the part of her mind that had kept her alive for nine years, the part that weighed paper like a moneylender weighs coin.

Verenne-Haut. The border assembly. The rump parliament-in-exile, the men who had fled east when the capital's politics turned cannibal, sitting now in a borrowed customs house within sight of the Karstine pickets, arguing over the shape of a country that might already be sold. Every road to Verenne-Haut ran east. Every checkpoint between here and there was manned by officers whose loyalty belonged, in whole or in part, to the faction that had arranged the selling.

And the dispatch in her satchel—this new one, this clean, innocent, triple-sealed folio—was to travel the same road as the cipher sewn into an infant's swaddling.

She looked up. The boy runner was watching the rain.

"You'll wait for receipt?" she asked.

"No need." He touched his cap. "Minister said you'd want to read it twice anyway." And he was gone down the stairs, boots quick on the stone, into the wet dark, carrying whatever else the evening required him not to have seen.

Felix closed the door and stood with her back against it.

* * *

She laid the two documents side by side on the table and lit both lamps, though the child was sleeping and light cost candle-tallow she could ill afford. On the left, the new dispatch, sealed and lawful, ordering her east. On the right, hidden in oilcloth, the old truth: troop movements, garrison strengths, rotation schedules—the flesh and skeleton of Vessany's eastern defense—copied in a clerk's careful hand and initialed, at the foot of the third page, by Minister Edouard Corvane himself. Sold. Or about to be sold. Or offered, tentatively, the way a man offers a knife handle-first, to the Karstine envoy whose name appeared in the margin in a different ink entirely.

Two documents. One courier. One road.

She sat down across from them as if they were guests who hated each other, and let herself think the thought she had been refusing for three days:

*He knows.*

Not suspected. Knew. A cipher sheet does not leave a minister's private office by accident. An infant does not arrive at the door of a specific safehouse—the G-7 safehouse, hers—by chance. Someone had carried the child through the Weavers' Quarter in the rain, someone who knew the address, someone who had been given instructions in that same upright hand: *forget the face.* And now, before she had even decided what to do with what she carried, the ministry had reached out and handed her a lawful reason to walk straight through every checkpoint between here and the people who most needed to see that cipher.

It was either a trap or a gift, and the terrible elegance of it—the thing that made her press her palms flat against the table until the wood grain printed itself into her skin—was that it was unmistakably both.

If it was a trap, it was beautifully built. Send the courier east under seal. Let her pass Ashford Gate, let her pass Marren Bridge, let her come to Cail Serein with the evidence strapped to her body like a second heart. Then stop her. Search her properly, not the theater of the routine inspection but the real thing, hands that knew where couriers hid things. Produce the cipher. Produce the child. Produce, at the end, the tidy arithmetic: *the courier G-7, found in possession of state secrets, attempting to flee to the enemy assembly.* Her death would be a footnote that closed the account. No one would ask what else the sheet contained. No one ever asked what else a dead courier carried.

But if it was a gift—

If it was a gift, then somewhere behind the minister's seal another hand had moved. A clerk who copied too much. A secretary who loved the wrong person. Corvane himself, in some hour of cowardice or grief she couldn't yet imagine, sending his own blood out into the rain with four words for a tombstone: *forget the face.* If it was a gift, then the road east was the only road in all of Vessany that led anywhere but a ditch, and the man who built the trap had—in his arrogance, in his certainty that no courier would dare turn his own orders into a weapon—left the gate standing open.

The trap and the escape were the same road. There was no third option. West lay the capital, where she was a designation with a face that could be matched to it. South lay the coast, closed since midsummer. North was Karstine territory already in everything but flag. East was the armistice line, the checkpoints, the assembly at Verenne-Haut—and the possibility, thin as winter ice, of setting the truth down in front of men who might still be forced to look at it.

Eleven days.

She got up and checked the window shutters, checked the door bar, checked the loose brick above the hearth where she kept the emergency purse: sixty crowns in mixed coin, enough to buy passage, bribes, or silence, depending on how the road felt about her. Enough for one. She counted it again, as though the coins might have bred overnight, and they hadn't.

One. Unless.

Her eyes went to the basket by the cold stove.

* * *

The child woke hungry at moonrise, and Felix fed her the thin gruel Marta—the widow from two doors down, the only soul in the quarter who had guessed at the bundle's existence and said nothing—had shown her how to soften with milk and sugar. The baby ate with the ferocious seriousness of the very young, both fists working, eyes fixed on Felix's face as though memorizing it for later testimony. When she finished she did not sleep. She lay in the crook of Felix's arm and studied the ceiling beams, the lamp flame, the shadow of Felix's hand moving on the blanket, cataloguing a world she had owned for barely a season.

"I should find you a family," Felix told her quietly. "A farm upriver. A woman with milk and no questions. People who never learn your face either."

The baby grabbed her thumb. It was becoming a habit between them, this small seizure, and Felix had stopped pretending she didn't wait for it.

"That's not an answer," she said.

It wasn't, and she knew why. Because a farm upriver meant papers, and papers meant names, and names meant questions asked by clerks who reported to ministries, and ministries reported—these days, in these weeks—to men like Corvane. Any record of the child anywhere was a thread, and Corvane had shown he could follow threads across a city in the rain. The only place the girl was truly safe was the place Felix could not reach without spending the very anonymity that kept them both alive: the assembly at Verenne-Haut, where proof could become something other than contraband. Where a cipher sheet could stop being a secret and start being a fact. Where a child stopped being evidence and became, if the world permitted such things, a person.

Which meant the trap-road was also the only mercy-road, for both of them. Corvane had built his snare so well that it had become a cradle.

She laughed once, short and without humor, and the sound startled them both.

* * *

She spent the next day doing what she had always done: preparation as prayer. She cleaned and re-oiled the satchel. She transferred the lawful dispatch into the inner sleeve where official papers rode, high on the back, first thing a courteous search would find—because the best hiding place for a real document was in plain sight, wearing its own authority. She unpicked three stitches of the swaddling cloth and considered, for the hundredth time, the cipher sheet lying flat against the child's warm back, and sewed them again with thread pulled from the hem so the repair would never show. She ate. She slept in shifts, light as a sentry. She wrote and burned two letters—one to no one, one to the version of herself who had taken this work at nineteen and believed invisibility was a kind of safety—and watched the ash curl up the flue into a sky the color of dishwater.

On the second morning, the letter of passage in her breast pocket and the child bundled against her chest beneath a plain traveling cloak, she stood in the doorway of the safehouse and looked back at the room that had held her for three days and, before that, in its dozens of identical siblings across nine years, had held the shape of her entire life. A cot. A stove. A table with two lamps. Nothing in it knew her name because nothing in it needed to.

"It's just another delivery," she said to the baby, and heard the lie for what it was, and picked up the satchel anyway.

The Weavers' Quarter was waking around her: shutters banging open, a cart horse complaining, the smell of coal smoke and wet stone. Somewhere a church bell rang the hour off-key, the way it had all autumn, no one paid to fix it. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a city going about its business on top of a fire.

Ashford Gate stood at the eastern mouth of the river road, and beyond it the Tollhouse at Marren Bridge, and beyond that the garrison at Cail Serein, and beyond *that*, if the seals fell kindly and the armistice clerks slept through her crossing, the customs house at Verenne-Haut where the last honest men in Vessany were running out of country to govern.

Three checkpoints. Eleven days. Two documents, one heartbeat away from each other, riding east on the body of a woman who had spent nine years learning never to be seen.

She turned her face into the wind and began to walk.

Behind her, faint under the bell and the cart noise, she thought she heard hooves—not following, not yet, just a rider taking the far street at a trot—but she did not turn around. Turning around was how couriers died. She fixed her eyes on the grey line of the eastern hills, shifted the child's weight to her hip, and stepped into the current of refugees and grain carts flowing toward the gate, one anonymous figure among hundreds, carrying enough truth to hang a minister sewn against a baby's spine.

At the toll arch, a bored sergeant glanced at her papers, waved her through, and never looked up.

The road east opened ahead of her, long and straight and wet with rain, running exactly where Corvane wanted it to run.

She walked it anyway.
