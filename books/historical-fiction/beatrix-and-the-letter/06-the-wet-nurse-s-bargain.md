## 6. The Wet Nurse's Bargain

The milk came in on the road north, which was the cruelest joke Marta's body had ever played on her.

She had left Vessany City with nothing but a bundle of linen, a copper pot, and the clothes she wore, and three days later, somewhere past the burned tollhouse at Kellin Ford, her breasts had swollen hot and heavy as though some child were waiting for her. There was no child. There had been a child—little Anselm, eight months old, gone with the fever in the spring when the wells of the lower quarter went foul—and her body had not received the news. Her body went on preparing for him, month after month, faithful as a dog at a grave. It was obscene. It was also, she would learn, worth money.

She learned it in the taproom of the Drowned Bell in Ashford, where she had stopped to beg a bowl of broth and been charged for it in a way she hadn't expected.

"You're leaking," said the woman at the next table.

Marta looked down. A dark bloom had spread across the front of her bodice, right over her heart, as if she'd been shot there. She pulled her shawl closed and felt her face go the color of tallow. "It happens," she said. "To women like me."

"To women who've nursed recently," the woman said. She was plain-featured in the way that made you look twice—there was nothing to hold your eye, no scar, no beauty mark, no crooked tooth, and so your eye kept returning trying to find what it had missed. Her hands were folded around an untouched cup of wine. Her boots were good but road-worn. She had the stillness of a person who had taught herself long ago not to fidget, and the watchfulness of someone who checked every door without appearing to.

"And how long since you've nursed?" the woman asked.

"None of your business."

"I'll make it my business if you like." She set a coin on the table between them—not a large one, but real silver, which after nine years of the republic's paper currency was its own kind of miracle. "There's a child. Three weeks old. Its mother can't feed it."

*Its mother.* The phrasing snagged something in Marta, some small fishhook of attention. Not *I have a child*. Not even *a friend's child*. *Its mother can't feed it*, spoken the way people spoke of weather or the price of grain.

"Where is this child?" Marta asked.

"Upstairs."

"Whose is it?"

The woman considered her for a long moment. Marta had spent her life reading faces—wet nurses lived and died by whether they could read the mother before the mother could read them—and this face gave her nothing. It wasn't blank the way an honest face is blank. It was blank the way a wall is blank. Somewhere behind the plaster there was a room, but she'd be damned if she could find the door.

"That's not a question I'm going to answer tonight," the woman said. "Ask me again in a week, if we're both still alive."

Most sensible women would have walked away then. But Marta Voss had stopped being most sensible women sometime around the morning she'd buried her son in the paupers' trench behind Saint Ellery's, in ground so cold the gravedigger had to light a fire under it first, and she had made herself a promise at that graveside that she would never again be sensible when need stood in front of her wearing any face at all.

"Show me the child," she said.

---

The room above the Drowned Bell was small and clean in a way that told Marta the woman had cleaned it herself, recently and thoroughly, the way soldiers clean things—everything squared, everything within reach, nothing decorative. A basin. A kettle. A chair by the window. And on the bed, in a basket that had plainly been made for a much finer nursery, an infant sleeping with her whole fists pressed against her cheeks as if bracing against some blow only she could see.

Marta looked at her for perhaps four seconds before everything in her chest that had been rusted shut since spring groaned open.

"She's starving," Marta said. Not asking. You could see it in the way the baby's mouth worked even in sleep, rooting at nothing, and in the faint yellow tint at the corners of her eyes. "Whoever's been feeding her has been giving her goat's milk or honey-water, and neither of those will keep a three-week-old alive. How old did you say?"

"Three weeks. Maybe less. Maybe more. I don't know her exact days."

"You don't know—" Marta turned. "You don't know her birthday?"

"No."

"You don't know her name?"

"No."

Marta stared at her. The woman bore the stare without flinching, which was itself an answer of sorts; liars flinch, eventually, and this woman had the patience of stone. "You found her," Marta said slowly. "You didn't get her from anyone. You *found* her."

"I'm offering you passage money," the woman said, as if Marta hadn't spoken. "Silver, paid each stage of the road. Food, lodging, your protection on the road as far as the border assembly at Halven Reach. All I ask is that you nurse her and keep her alive, and that between here and Halven Reach you don't ask me questions I haven't offered to answer."

"And if I do ask?"

"Then I'll lie to you, and you'll know I'm lying, and we'll both be worse off for it." Something moved behind the flat gray eyes—not warmth, exactly, but the ghost of warmth, the place where warmth had once been and might come back. "I'd rather not lie to you. I've gotten out of the habit of lying and I find I'm bad at it. So: don't ask."

Marta sat down on the edge of the bed, slowly, because her knees had opinions. The baby stirred. Her mouth opened, searching, blind and furious with hunger, and Marta thought of Anselm's mouth, Anselm's furious little rooting, Anselm at the end too weak to root at all, just opening his lips toward her breast like a flower toward a sun that wasn't coming.

"Give her to me," Marta said.

---

Feeding a starving infant is not the gentle thing the songs pretend. The baby fought the nipple at first, too weak to manage the letdown, choking, sputtering, and Marta held her the way she'd been taught twenty years ago in her first placement—upright, patient, letting the little one find her own rhythm—and after ten minutes of struggle the girl latched properly and began, for the first time in what was probably her whole short life, to actually drink.

Marta felt the pull of it all through her body, that deep strange ache that was half pain and half the oldest satisfaction there is. Her eyes blurred. She didn't wipe them. Let the woman see. If the woman was going to judge a wet nurse for weeping over a stranger's baby, better to know now.

But when Marta glanced up, the woman had turned away, toward the window, and was standing very straight with her back to the room, and Marta understood that the turning-away was its own kind of courtesy—the courtesy of someone who knew what it was to be watched while breaking.

They stayed like that a long while. The fire ticked. Downstairs the Drowned Bell's few patrons muttered about the armistice, about the roads closing, about the Karstine columns everyone swore were two weeks away or already past the hills, depending on whom you believed. Above it all the baby drank, and drank, and finally released, sighing, milk-drunk, her fists uncurling.

"There," Marta whispered. "That's it. That's the whole world, isn't it. That's everything."

"She needs feeding every two hours," the woman said, turning back. Her voice was level again. "Day and night. Can you do that?"

"I've done it for other women's children since I was sixteen years old. I can do it in my sleep, and frequently have."

"And you'll come with me to Halven Reach."

"On one condition." Marta shifted the baby to her shoulder and began the slow patting that brought up wind. "My brother comes too."

Something changed in the room. It was subtle—a stillness arriving where motion had been—but Marta caught it because she'd spent her life in rooms where mothers pretended not to notice things. "Your brother," the woman said.

"Tomas. He's in Ashford. He'll be looking for work on the road east; he's strong, he can drive, he can fix anything with wheels, and he doesn't drink more than the next man." All true. What Marta did not say: Tomas was also a deserter from the Fourth Vessan Line, hiding under their mother's maiden name since the retreat at Corvess Marsh, where he had watched his whole company walk into a kill-ground because some minister's map had been wrong—or bought—and where staying to die would not have saved a single soul but would have kept his name clean. He'd sent her word through a cousin that he was in Ashford. He'd sent it in code, the family code, because deserters who wrote plainly got hanged.

"He's not my business," the woman said carefully. "If he travels with us, he's your responsibility."

"He's my brother. That's the same thing." Marta met the gray eyes. "And here's what you're not saying: you need a man on this road. Whatever you are—and I've known wet-nursing employers for twenty years, madam, and none of them ever hired one alone—you're carrying something besides a baby, and the roads ahead are full of men with rifles and bad intentions. A driver, a nurse, and a woman playing the courier makes a family. Makes a story people believe. Alone, you're a target. With us, you're a widow going home to her sister with her late husband's sister's orphan. That's what I can give you that silver can't."

The silence stretched. Outside, rain had begun against the shutters, soft and steady, the kind of rain that made the whole world feel conspiratorial.

"You've done this before," the woman said at last. "Made up stories for checkpoints."

"I've crossed six provincial lines in the last year with other women's babies in my arms. Every checkpoint asks whose child. Every answer is a story." Marta smiled, and knew it was not a kind smile. "The trick isn't believing your own lie. It's believing it just enough to cry if they take the baby from you. They never take a crying mother's baby. It makes the soldiers uncomfortable, and uncomfortable soldiers wave you through."

For the first time, the woman laughed—one breath of a laugh, quickly suppressed, as if laughter were another thing she'd trained out of herself—but Marta heard it, and counted it a victory worth more than the silver.

"Bring your brother tomorrow," the woman said. "Before noon. We leave at midday, ahead of the crowd, through the Ashford Gate. After that—" she paused, and Marta saw her choose her words with visible care, laying each one down like a card she couldn't take back, "—after that, whatever happens on the road happens to all of us."

"All of us," Marta agreed. "One more thing." She looked down at the sleeping weight on her shoulder, the tiny chest rising and falling, the fine dark hair still damp from the feeding. "This one needs a name. I won't call her 'the child' for two hundred miles, and neither will you, whatever you're pretending not to know about her."

"She has a name," the woman said quietly. "I'm just not permitted to use it."

*Permitted.* Marta filed the word away with the others—*its mother*, the basket built for a richer house, the coin paid in silver, the woman who checked doors without looking. She was assembling a picture, piece by piece, the way she'd once assembled jigsaw puzzles of cathedrals with Anselm's father, God rest him, back when the world still rewarded patience. She did not yet know what the picture showed. But she knew it showed a powerful man somewhere, and trouble coming up behind them on the road like weather.

"Well then," Marta said, rising, tucking the blanket close. "Tomorrow I'll call her something. And by Halven Reach, so will you."

At the door she paused. "What do I call you? Since we're not asking questions."

The woman was silent long enough that Marta thought she wouldn't answer. Then:

"Felix," she said. "Just Felix."

"Just Felix," Marta repeated, tasting it. A courier's name, half a name, a name worn smooth from use. "Well then, Just Felix. Sleep while you can. Babies keep honest hours, and so do I."

---

Tomas was waiting in the stable loft behind the chandlery where he'd been sleeping, and he listened to her account with his jaw working, chewing the inside of his cheek the way he'd done since boyhood whenever he smelled danger.

"A courier," he said. "A ministry courier. Off the books. Hiring strangers off a taproom floor to carry—what? Tell me what, Marta. Because if it's what I think it is, every man on that road will be looking for it, and I am the last man alive who wants to meet the ones doing the looking."

"She's paying in silver."

"Marta."

"She's got a three-week-old starving in a basket and eyes like a woman who buried something, and I made a promise at a grave this spring that I'd never again walk past a thing that needed doing." Marta took his face in her two hands, rough palms against rough stubble, her little brother who had once fit entirely inside her forearm. "Come with me. Watch the road. Keep your head down and your name quiet—I know you can, you've done it a year. And when we reach Halven Reach, we split the silver and you disappear into the mountains and no one will ever connect you to anything."

Tomas looked at her for a long time. In the loft's dim lantern light his face seemed very young suddenly, younger than his thirty years, younger than the war had any right to leave anyone.

"The last time I trusted a stranger's plan," he said, "three hundred men died around me."

"This time it's a woman and a baby," Marta said. "And this time I'll be beside you the whole way."

He exhaled through his nose, the surrender-sound of every man in their family going back three generations. "Ashford Gate at midday," he said. "God help us, Marta. The roads close after the armistice, you know. Whatever's on that coach when it rolls through those gates—it's committed. There's no turning back past the first checkpoint."

"I know," Marta said, and found, to her own surprise, that the knowledge sat in her chest not like dread but like relief. For the first time in a year she was moving toward something instead of away.

Above them, through the floorboards, faint and insistent, the baby began to cry.
