## 7. First Checkpoint: Ashford Gate

The road to Ashford Gate ran through country that had forgotten how to be beautiful. Felix watched it from the coach window and catalogued it the way she catalogued everything—without attachment, without wonder, as terrain to be crossed. Burnt hedgerows. A church with its steeple sawn off at the shoulders, because a steeple was an observation post to one army and a target to another. A field of wheat gone to seed where no one had dared to harvest it, the stalks bleached bone-pale by weeks of rain, bending and straightening in the wind like a congregation of the drowned.

She counted milestones instead of praying. Four since the crossroads. Three. The driver had said Ashford Gate would come up on her after the second milestone, and drivers who lied about checkpoints were drivers she would not live to regret.

In the satchel on her lap sat the minister's dispatch, sealed in black wax, dull as a river stone. It contained nothing that mattered; she had memorized it in the capital and could have recited it backward while drunk. That was the trick of the last nine years: the thing they searched for was never in the case. The case was theater. The case was the prop that made the performance believable.

The real dispatch lay folded against Beatrix's belly, sewn into the second swaddling cloth with Marta's own needle and Felix's own thread, beneath the linen that would next touch the child's skin. A feeding cloth, the women called it—the absorbent pad that caught what infants leaked, that smelled of sour milk and needed changing four times a day. No soldier alive would search a feeding cloth. No soldier alive would want to.

Felix looked down at the bundle sleeping in Marta's arms across from her and felt the old familiar emptiness open under her ribs—but wider now, deeper, because for the first time in nine years she was not carrying only paper. Paper could burn. Paper could be swallowed, soaked, buried, denied. Paper did not wake at night with a cry that went through the chest like a nail.

*You are a fool*, she told herself, and then, because the thought would not leave: *You are a fool, and you are three miles from the first gate.*

"Ashford," the driver called down through the trap, unnecessarily. She could see it.

* * *

Ashford Gate had not always been a checkpoint. It had been a tollhouse once, back when roads were things people paid to use rather than things they died on—a low stone building straddling the highway like a farmer sitting on a fence, with a slate roof furred green with moss and windows that still kept their shutters. Someone had painted over the toll schedule on the wall and painted up instead the triple tower of the Republic and, beneath it, in careful block letters: ALL TRAVELERS DECLARE. THE REPUBLIC PROTECTS ITS OWN.

Someone else had scratched out *protects*. The scratch was older than the paint around it.

There were six soldiers at the gate, which meant six chances for everything to end. Two manned the barrier itself—a pine pole tipped with iron, balanced on a post like the arm of a scale—and two more walked the line of waiting carts with the unhurried gait of men being paid by the hour. One stood apart, holding a nervous horse. And one sat on a stool by the tollhouse door with a ledger open on his knees and a cup of something steaming beside him, and this last one wore a captain's knot on his shoulder cord, and he was watching the coach come down the road with an expression of mild, patient interest, the way a man watches weather he has already decided to walk through.

Felix made herself breathe. Nine years, and the fear never got smaller; she had only gotten better at folding it, the way she folded dispatches—smaller and smaller until it fit into some dark corner of her and stayed there, creased but quiet.

"Couriers sit forward," she said to Marta, low. "Back straight. You're tired, you're a widow, you hate me a little for what I can afford and you can't. If he asks your name, give the name we chose. Nothing more than he asks. Not one word."

"And if he asks the baby?"

"He won't." She hoped he wouldn't. "Babies aren't cargo yet. They're not worth a line in any ledger."

Marta shifted the child against her shoulder. Her face was doing what Felix had hired it to do: nothing. It was a broad, plain, weathered face, a face built for market queues and funeral bread, and it could go empty as a shut shopfront. Felix had seen her practice it against a window at the waystation, and had been quietly unsettled by how good she was—as though the whole war had been rehearsing her for this.

The coach rocked to a stop. The wheel nearest Felix creaked and settled. Somewhere ahead, a skylark was singing its heart out over no-man's-land, and Felix hated it for that.

"Good morning to you." The captain had risen from his stool without hurry. Up close he was fifty or so, gray at the temples, with a face like a map of somewhere gentle—soft hills, no contested borders. His uniform was clean but not new, brushed carefully, the buttons polished by years of thumbs. He held his ledger under one arm like a hymnal. "Traveling to?"

"Ferry Cross," Felix said. "On ministry business." She produced the courier's pass with two fingers, at the correct angle, at the correct speed—not eager, not reluctant. Bored. She let boredom sit in her voice like dust. "Third crossing this week. The roads get worse every time."

"They do that." He took the pass and read it more slowly than he needed to, which was either thoroughness or theater, and Felix could not tell which and hated not knowing. "Ministry of—ah. Dispatches." He glanced up. His eyes were pale brown, almost the color of weak tea, and entirely unremarkable, and they rested on her face for exactly as long as courtesy required. "Long way from the capital for a woman."

"The republic doesn't pay couriers to stay home, Captain."

That earned her the ghost of a smile. "No. I imagine it doesn't." He handed the pass back. "Names, for the ledger. Yours first."

"Felix Galloway. Courier, fourth class, seal number—" she recited the number she had worn for nine years like a scar, and watched him write it down in a hand that slanted leftward, unhurried.

"Marta Voss," Marta said from the window, in a voice flat as pond ice. "Wet nurse. Going where there's work."

"And the little one?"

Felix's heart performed a single enormous beat and then resumed, and she marveled from a great distance that her hands on her knees did not move at all.

"Nursed her since the mother died of childbed fever at the hospice in Corley," Marta said, and the lie came out of her smooth and round and terrible, a perfect egg of a lie. "Poor mite. I've grown fond. Where I'm hired, she goes. It's how it's done."

"It is how it's done," the captain agreed mildly, and wrote *infant, nursling* in the ledger, and Felix understood with dizzy clarity that she had just watched a stranger decide her fate on two words and a shrug, and that the coin he'd accepted them at was pity, or habit, or the simple human wish to be done before his tea went cold.

"Open the coach, if you would," he said. "It'll be quick. Corporal Renk will look through the luggage while we talk—it's the talk I mind about, honestly. These days everyone lies beautifully and nobody carries anything interesting."

* * *

Corporal Renk was twenty at most, with sunburned ears and the careful clumsiness of a boy handling other people's property. He laid Felix's satchel on the tailboard of the coach and opened it the way she had seen a hundred soldiers open a hundred bags: fast hands, slow eyes, looking for shape rather than substance. The false-bottomed compartment fooled him precisely as long as it was designed to—he found the ministry seal, the spare wax, the flint, the folded maps of roads that no longer existed, and he riffled each item with two fingers like a man dealing cards, and gave her back her life in eleven minutes.

The captain, meanwhile, talked.

He talked about the road ahead—the ford at Merle was up, he said, take the causeway even if the queue was long. He talked about the armistice, which everyone spoke of now the way farmers spoke of frost, as a thing certain to arrive and impossible to prepare for. He asked whether the ministry still paid in silver or only in promises, and Felix said silver, mostly, sometimes, and he laughed at that, a dry little laugh with no wind in it, and all the while his eyes went around the coach the way hers went around rooms: door frames, faces, hands.

They paused on Marta longer than they paused anywhere. On the bundle at her breast. On the small fist that emerged from the swaddling and waved at the world with the blind imperiousness of an emperor.

"Pretty child," he said.

"She eats well," Marta said, which was neither agreement nor disagreement, and pulled the swaddling a half-inch higher, a mother's reflex so ordinary that it was invisible. The cipher sat beneath that linen not eight inches from his eyes, stitched between two layers of cloth spotted faintly with milk, smelling of warmth and curd and life. Felix sat with her spine against the squab and her boredom arranged on her face like a shawl and thought, with a lucidity close to prayer: *he will look away now, please God, he will look away now.*

He looked away now.

"You'll want to declare anything of value before Ferry Cross," he said, closing the ledger. "The captains there are younger than me and twice as hungry. This used to be a good posting." He said it without self-pity, the way a man reads the date off a stone. "Well. Galloway, Courier Fourth Class. Your papers are in order, your nurse is tired, and your cargo—" he nodded at the baby, and for one lurching instant Felix thought he knew, he must know, the whole rotten edifice of her luck was coming down— "is the best-behaved traveler I've seen all week."

He raised two fingers from the ledger. The barrier arm went up.

"Give my regards to Ferry Cross," he said. "Tell them Ashford is still awake out here."

* * *

The coach rolled through, and Felix did not move for four hundred yards. She sat exactly as she had sat, bored spine, bored hands, and watched the tollhouse shrink in the window glass, and the burnt hedge swallow it, and only when Ashford Gate was fully gone from the earth did she discover that her body had been paying a bill her mind knew nothing about: her shift was soaked through at the small of her back, cold already, and her jaw ached from clenching, and her breath came out of her in a long unsteady ribbon like smoke off a doused fire.

Marta looked at her.

"Don't," Felix said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to."

Marta adjusted the sleeping child and offered, after a moment, the smallest possible shrug. "Feeding cloth wants changing before Ferry Cross," she said. "And the stitching should be checked. Milk gets into everything. Better I wash it tonight and resew it wet-damp, the way I showed you."

"Yes."

"That captain," Marta said, to the window, to the lark, to no one, "has hanged men. You could see it in his hands. Courteous hands are worse than cruel ones, my mother always said. Cruel ones tell you what they are."

Felix thought of the captain's soft map of a face, his dry laugh, his *tell them Ashford is still awake*. She thought about how the most dangerous thing she had met all morning was patience wearing politeness, and how he had written *infant, nursling* in a fair left-slanting hand, and how that line now existed in a ledger in a tollhouse on a road full of soldiers, and how words on paper were the whole reason she was in this coach at all.

Something passed over her then that she could not immediately name—a lightness, a wrongness, like a floor tilting half a degree. It took her another mile to recognize it, because it had been nine years since she had felt it.

Relief. Pride, even. The hot illicit taste of getting away with it.

She pressed it down the way she pressed everything down, but it would not stay pressed. It kept rising, warm and dangerous, and by the time the milestone reading FERRY CROSS — 9 came up out of the mist at the roadside, painted over with someone's idea of a joke—*LAST CHANCE TO TURN BACK*—she was almost smiling.

Then the driver reined in at the top of the rise, and Felix looked down at the valley, and stopped smiling.

Below, where the road bent toward the river, a rider in a gray traveling coat stood his horse at the edge of the queue for the ferry, and did not board, and did not leave, and was watching every coach that came down the hill with the still, patient attention of a man counting.

Not a soldier's coat. Not a courier's saddle. But she knew the set of those shoulders, and the way the man's hand rested flat on his thigh like something waiting to be drawn, and she had spent nine years learning what the ministry's shadow looked like when it fell ahead of her instead of behind.

"Marta," she said quietly. "Don't look up. We have a problem."
