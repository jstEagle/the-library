## 8. The Face She Was Told to Forget

The waystation at Hollowmere had once been a hunting lodge for men who owned the forests they hunted in. Now it was a coaching inn with a sagging roof, a hearth that smoked more than it warmed, and a landlord who asked no questions because questions cost more than answers. Felix chose it for exactly that reason, and for one other: it sat far enough from the Ashford road that the sound of wheels could not reach them, and close enough to the second checkpoint that she would need only an hour's warning when morning came.

She paid for the room at the back, up the stairs that groaned like a ship in weather, and paid again—a coin more than the room deserved—for the landlord's discretion. He took the coin the way tired men take everything, without ceremony or gratitude, and told her the well water was bad but the beer was worse, which was the longest sentence anyone had spoken to her in three days.

Marta fed the child by the fire in the common room below while Felix stood at the window and watched the road. This was habit worn into instinct: watch the road, count the riders, mark anything that moved against the wind. The road stayed empty. The wind smelled of wet ash from the burned crofts to the north, and underneath that, the green rot of autumn giving up. Somewhere beyond the hills, the armistice was being drafted in rooms she would never see, in a language of concessions she would carry in her satchel if anyone thought it worth sealing.

She carried it now—the dispatch from the capital, unbroken, unread, its wax seal intact beneath her thumb whenever doubt needed something to press against. And she carried the other thing, the thing that was not a dispatch at all, that had no seal and no sanction and no business riding in a courier's satchel except that Felix had folded it small and sewn it into the swaddling's inner hem where a feeding cloth might plausibly pad a infant's hips. The cipher sheet. Corvane's hand. Troop movements sold like grain futures to a Karstine envoy who signed himself with a merchant's flourish.

She had deciphered enough. Not all—she was a courier, not a cryptographer—but enough. Enough to know what rode against the child's skin every time Marta fed her, every time she cried, every time she did that astonishing newborn thing of gripping a finger with her whole furious strength.

Enough to know she was carrying treason sewn into baby clothes.

Felix went upstairs. She told herself it was to check the room, check the window, check the sightlines—courier's litany, the prayer of a woman who had survived nine years by checking. But her hands knew better. Her hands went to the satchel, and then to the swaddling bundle laid by the cold hearth where Beatrix—that was Marta's name for her already, spoken like a superstition, *she'll be lucky, this one*—had slept through the feeding, and Felix's fingers found the seam she had made herself, three nights ago, in a safehouse that stank of tallow and fear.

She unpicked it.

The cipher came out first, and she set it on the table as though it might bite. Then her fingers found something else in the hem. Something flat and hard and small, cased in shagreen gone soft with handling, that she had missed when she'd first bundled the documents away—or perhaps had not missed at all, perhaps some part of her had understood that she could only carry so many revelations in one night, and had simply declined to know.

She opened the case under the candle.

A portrait miniature looked back at her.

The painting was old-fashioned, done in the style favored before the war taxes emptied the studios—guilt and gilt, a girl rendered against a background of impossible blue. She was perhaps six years old in the image, with her hair dressed high in the capital fashion and a string of seed pearls at her throat, and she was laughing. Not smiling—laughing, caught mid-moment by a painter quick enough to see it coming, so that the picture held the exact instant before sound. Her eyes were dark and slightly crossed with merriment. One tooth was missing from her grin.

Felix knew the face.

Not from court circles—she had never been received in court circles. Not from the ministry galleries, where she moved like dust through light. She knew it from a mourning card, printed two winters past and distributed through every government office with the mechanical courtesy of state grief: *Lady Vessenne Corvane, beloved daughter of the Minister of State, taken in her seventh winter.* She had seen the card pinned above a clerk's desk, seen the same engraved face reproduced a hundred times in a hundred offices, always the same formal portrait, solemn, pearls, painted mouth. The republic had mourned its minister's daughter the way it mourned everything official: briefly, uniformly, and then never again.

But the engraving had been taken from a painting, and this was the painting—or its twin—and the girl who laughed out of it with her missing tooth and her crossed merry eyes was unmistakably, impossibly, the child asleep by the dead hearth.

Felix sat down without meaning to. The chair scraped. Below, faintly, she heard Marta humming to the baby, a tuneless peasant lullaby with no country attached to it, the kind of song that belonged to whoever needed it.

She looked from the miniature to the child and back again, and the looking changed nothing and changed everything.

It was not resemblance. Resemblance was for aunts and neighbors, for *doesn't he have his father's chin*. This was blood laid down twice, the same architecture of cheek and brow, the same particular darkness at the eye. A daughter returned. Or a daughter repeated—the way grief repeats itself, the way powerful men rebuild what they have broken and call it devotion.

*Forget the face.*

The note rose in her memory with the terrible clarity of things written in haste: four words in Corvane's cramped, downward-slanted hand, the hand she had memorized across nine years of sealed dispatches until she could have drawn it blindfolded. She had read it as an order. Of course she had; every line of Corvane's writing she had ever carried was an order, or the shadow of one, and orders were the water she swam in. *Forget the face.* Destroy the evidence. Erase the witness. The standard grammar of powerful men cleaning up after themselves.

She had built a whole architecture of dread on those four words. Treason, yes—but also murder, the tidy kind, the kind done to infants in swaddling and blamed on the chaos of a collapsing state. She had told herself she was saving the child from the minister's necessity. It had made the running simpler: flee the wolf, save the lamb, keep the cipher safe. Clean lines. A courier's morality, binary as a seal—intact or broken.

Now she turned the miniature in her fingers and the lines blurred.

Because this was not the work of a man erasing evidence. Men erasing evidence do not commission copies of their dead daughter's portrait and tuck them into the swaddling of the child they mean to disappear. Men erasing evidence do not choose, of all the hiding places in the world, the doorstep of a courier they know will run the last open road rather than fail a charge. Unless—

Unless the order and the gift were written by different hands inside the same hand. Unless *forget the face* meant something no dispatcher had ever meant by it.

Felix held the case up to the candlelight and saw, on the inner lid, tooled so faintly she had to angle it against the flame: a date. Not the daughter's death-date. A christening date. Last spring. And beneath it, in the same cramped slant as the note, a single letter: *V.*

The child had been born last spring. The daughter had died two winters past.

Felix did the arithmetic the way she did all her worst arithmetic, alone and fast and wishing she could burn the result. A minister in his sixties. A wife dead years before, of the long elegant decline that took ministers' wives. A household of servants whose silence could be bought, a city whose attention was fixed on its own collapse, and a grief so large it had stopped being respectable—the mourning cards, the withdrawal from court, the rumors that Corvane had grown strange, distant, that power had finally curdled him. She remembered the whispers passing through the ministry like drafts under doors: *the minister is not himself since the girl died.*

He had not been himself. He had been a man who found, or was brought, or bought—an infant. A child with his dead daughter's face, because blood does what portraits only attempt, and somewhere in the gray territory between a Karstine merchant's ledger and a minister's house there had been a woman paid to disappear, and a man mad enough, or hollow enough, to believe that the dead come back if you feed them.

And then the pact. The pact that would sell the garrisons, open the roads, end the republic in exchange for whatever Corvane had been promised—amnesty, escape, a life in Karst for himself and the child who could not be explained to any committee, any court, any successor. The child was not only evidence. The child was the reason. You cannot flee to an enemy nation carrying a living impossibility unless the enemy nation has agreed to hold their tongue as well as your purse.

*Forget the face* was not an execution order.

It was an instruction for survival, written by a father who knew he could not keep her and could not explain her and could not even name her, passed to the one person in the capital whose entire profession was not asking—who had spent nine years proving that she could carry anything without ever knowing, without ever speaking, without ever letting her own face become known. He had chosen her precisely because she was invisible. He had trusted her precisely because trust was beside the point; she was a mechanism, a route, a road that walked.

And he had hidden the miniature in the swaddling the way grieving men hide photographs, where no interrogator would search because no rational man would look for sentiment in evidence.

Felix set the case down. Her hands were not steady. Nine years of steady hands, undone by a tooled letter *V* and a missing tooth in a painted grin.

She should feel vindicated. That was what startled her most, sitting there while the candle guttered—she had expected, if she ever uncovered the human shape behind the treason, to feel the clean anger of the righteous. Instead she felt something colder and more complicated, something that fit badly, like a coat cut for another woman. Pity, maybe. Or recognition. Because she understood the arithmetic of disappearance. She had performed it on herself. She had erased her family's faces from her own speech nine years ago—never spoke of them, never wrote them, let the names go dim in her mouth until sometimes she could not conjure her mother's voice at all—and she had called it survival, and it was, and it was also a slow cowardice, and she knew both truths and carried both.

Corvane had chosen cowardice too. He had made a child to fill a grave, sold his country to protect the filling of it, and then, when even that collapsed under its own weight, he had done the one honest thing available to him: he had put her on a doorstep with a lie wrapped around the truth. *Forget the face.* Forget my daughter's face. Forget what she is. Let her be nobody, the way I cannot be nobody, the way I never learned to be.

Let her live invisible. Like you.

The candle spat. Downstairs the lullaby had stopped; the house settled into the creaks and ticks of old timber contracting in the cold. Felix rose, crossed to the hearth, and looked down at the sleeping child.

Beatrix—no, she corrected herself, the child had no name yet, the child was *the package*, the child was evidence, and yet Marta's nonsense word had already rooted itself in Felix's head like a seed in a wall. The baby slept with one fist curled near her cheek and her mouth working slightly, dreaming whatever infants dream, which could not possibly include ministers or borders or the price of garrisons. Her eyes were closed, but Felix had seen them open now, fed her now, watched that dark, slightly merry gaze fix on the world with an gravity far too old for it.

The daughter's eyes. The laughing girl's eyes, the ones the mourning card had rendered solemn because engravings cannot laugh.

*Forget the face,* Felix thought. *I was told to forget the face, and instead I am learning it. The way you learn a road. The way you learn your own.*

She knelt, repacked the hem with hands that steadied themselves through sheer professional stubbornness—cipher first, then the miniature, nested in the soft leather where it had ridden for months against a smaller, warmer body. She sewed the seam with thread from her repair kit, small stitches, a courier's stitches, the kind that pass inspection. When it was done she pressed her palm flat over the swaddling's curve and felt the faint, astonishing heat of a sleeping child through the wool.

Something had shifted in her and would not shift back. She examined it the way she examined roads: this passage open, that one watched. Before tonight, the mission had been abstract—the cipher, the corruption, the assembly at the border, justice spelled in documents. Now there was a painted laugh stitched against the treason, and the two rode together in the same hem, and Felix understood that when she stood before whatever remained of the parliament-in-exile, she would not only be delivering proof of a sold country. She would be delivering a father's confession, written in the only ink he had left: a child with a dead girl's face, and a note begging a stranger to make her no one so that she could be someone.

It did not excuse him. Felix held onto that with both hands, because pity is slippery and she could not afford to slide. Grief did not sell garrisons; a man's choices sold garrisons, and the choices were in the cipher in her own careful stitching, in Corvane's own signature. Whatever love had coexisted with the treason, the treason remained. But the love explained the shape of it—why the pact, why now, why the desperation that had made a minister gamble a nation on an escape route. Men do enormous evil for enormous reasons, but the enormous reasons are almost always small: a face, a name, a fear.

She blew out the candle and stood a moment in the dark, listening to the baby breathe, counting the breaths the way she counted riders on a road. Steady. Even. Alive.

Then she went to the window and pulled the curtain back a finger's width, because the litany did not stop for revelations—watch the road, count the lights—and the road lay empty under a thin rising moon, silver-gray, quiet.

Except.

There. At the far bend, where the Hollowmere track met the coach road, a light had appeared where no light should be: low, moving without bounce, unhurried. Not a farm cart—farm carts bounced. Not a patrol—patrols traveled in numbers and noise. This was one rider, walking his horse, keeping to the crown of the road where the frost had not yet formed, and stopping, just for a moment, at the fork where a man might consult a paper by shaded lantern.

Felix watched him consult it. Watched him fold it away. Watched him ride on toward the waystation, unhurried still, the way men are unhurried when they are certain of their quarry and paid whether the chase is interesting or not.

She let the curtain fall and stood in the dark with her heart going hard and slow, like boots descending stairs. Behind her, in the cold hearth room, the child slept on, wearing her dead sister's face and her father's treason, stitched and sewn and carried.

Whoever rode the moonlit road was not lost, and was not patrolling, and had not stopped at the fork to admire the frost.

Felix lifted the satchel, woke Marta with two fingers pressed to her shoulder and a whisper, and began, quietly, to dress the child for travel before the knock came.
