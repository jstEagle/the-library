## 9. Marta's Brother

The barn smelled of rot and rain and old blood, and Marta had slept in worse.

She said so to herself as she spread her shawl over the straw in the corner farthest from the door, arranging the baby first, then Felix's satchel beside her, then—last of all, an afterthought she did not quite believe—her own body. The roof was half gone. Stars showed through the broken slates in long silver seams, and where the seams ended, the dark began again, patient as debt. Somewhere beyond the far wall a wind moved through a drowned orchard, shaking water off the branches one tree at a time, like a slow hand counting.

"Two hours," Felix said. She stood at the door with her shoulder against it, watching the road they had come by, the road that ran back toward everything they were running from. "Then we move. I don't like stopping this close to the toll."

"You don't like stopping at all."

"No," Felix agreed, without apology.

Marta fed the baby by feel, in the dark, the way she had done for other women's children in three provinces now. The girl sucked greedily, one fist curled against Marta's breast like something clutching a rope. Outside, the rain thickened, and beneath the rain, faint but unmistakable, came another sound: boots. Many boots. Unhurried.

Felix heard it too. Marta saw her go still the way animals go still—not frozen but gathered, every part of her suddenly pointed at the sound. Her hand went inside her coat, not to a weapon; Marta had learned by now that Felix carried no weapon worth the name. It went to the satchel instead. To the child's spare swaddling. To the thing sewn there.

"Felix," Marta whispered. "How many?"

"Enough." Felix crossed the barn in four silent steps and crouched beside them. Rain-light made her face strange, half silver, half shadow. "Listen to me. If they come in—if anyone asks—you are a wet nurse going to your sister in Halven. The child is yours. Widow. Husband dead at Corvane's Bridge. You've told it before; tell it again."

"And you?"

"I'm nobody. Your cousin, if you need me to be. A woman traveling with you because the roads aren't safe alone." A pause, thin as paper. "Which is true."

Marta almost laughed. Almost. The boots crunched nearer, and laughter died in her chest like a struck match.

* * *

They did not come in through the door. They came through the collapsed wall on the north side, three of them first, then more, filtering into the barn with the weary economy of men who had stopped expecting shelter and merely accepted it. Deserters. Marta knew the breed the way farmers know weather: coats stripped of braid, boots wrapped in rags to quiet them, eyes that checked exits before faces. There were perhaps fifteen, maybe twenty, settling into the straw with the groans of men whose feet had outlived their loyalty.

A fire was coaxed up in the middle of the floor, small and shielded, burning damp wood that spat and smoked. In its light Marta counted faces: boys mostly, farm-boys and town-boys grown suddenly old, and a few veterans with the thousand-yard stillness that no age could give.

And one face she knew.

He sat apart from the others, near the broken wall, as if he had come in last and meant to leave first. Broad through the shoulders, narrow through the luck—a family shape she would have known anywhere, in any light, in any year, even though he'd been sixteen the last time she saw him and was twenty-two now, and even though war had planed him down to something harder than the boy who used to steal plums off the Sarello cart and swear he hadn't.

*Tomas.*

Her brother looked up. Their eyes met across a burning barn full of strangers, and Marta watched him go through, in the space of two heartbeats, everything she was feeling herself: disbelief, joy, terror, and under all of it, cold and rising, *fear*—because she was here, in a deserter camp, with a baby and a stranger and a satchel she never let out of arm's reach, and Tomas was clever enough, always had been, to wonder what that added up to.

"Marta?" He said it quietly, but the man beside him turned his head anyway.

She rose, keeping her voice easy, her face arranged into sisterly irritation. "Tomas Sarello. They told Mother you were dead at Vellin Ford."

"They told everyone that." He got up slowly. Up close he smelled of woodsmoke and unwashed wool and something sour underneath, the smell of men living on fear-rations. His eyes went past her shoulder—to Felix, to the bundle in her arms—and stayed there a moment too long. "Whose is that?"

"Mine." She said it flatly, the lie worn smooth already from the checkpoints. "My husband died. We're going to Halven."

"To Halven." He repeated it like a man tasting soup he suspected of being spoiled. Then, softer, in the old childhood tongue they'd shared over stolen plums, the tongue their father had beaten them for using because it sounded too much like Karstine: *"Who is she?"*

"A woman who paid for passage," Marta answered in the same tongue. "That's all you need to carry."

But she saw it in his face—the arithmetic beginning behind his eyes. Tomas had always been quick with sums. It was why Father had pinned such hopes on him, hopes Tomas had repaid by deserting a garrison that no longer existed to desert.

"Sit with us," Marta said aloud, before he could think further. "Eat. When did you eat last?"

* * *

The food was barley porridge gone to glue and a heel of black bread each, and Marta ate hers mechanically while the baby slept against her collarbone and Tomas talked—or rather, while the others talked and Tomas listened, which was worse, because listening was what he'd always been best at.

The talk was the talk of the collapsing republic, which by now had its own liturgy. The Third Army dissolved at Sorrel. The navy sold its hulls to pay officers who'd already fled. The capital eating its own ministries, one minister arrested every week, each arrest producing two more names, each name a smaller man climbing over a fallen bigger one. And through it all, threaded like wire through rotten cloth: the peace. The armistice being negotiated with Karst, the terms whispered about in every market from here to the coast. Terms that gave away garrisons. Terms that gave away rivers. Terms, one scarred veteran insisted, tapping his temple, that had been written *before* the fighting ever started, by men on both sides who needed the war to end only after it had made them rich.

Marta kept her face stupid and sympathetic. Around her, Felix sat in shadow with her hood half-drawn, hands folded, a woman of no consequence mending nothing, waiting for nothing. Only once did their eyes meet, briefly, across the fire—and Marta understood that Felix had recognized the danger in the room before Marta had, had marked Tomas from the moment he came through the wall, and had chosen to say nothing, trusting Marta's blood to hold or break on its own terms.

It was a kind of respect. It was also a kind of test. Marta wasn't sure which frightened her more.

"Tomas," she said quietly, when the fire burned low and the others had rolled into their blankets. "Walk with me."

* * *

They stood under the ruined eave where the orchard began, rain coming down fine as sifted flour between them. The baby slept in the sling against Marta's chest, a warm weight over her heartbeat.

"Sixteen months," Tomas said. "Since Vellin Ford. Do you know what happened at Vellin Ford?"

"I know what they printed."

"What they printed." He laughed without sound. "We held the ford for nine days. Nine days we were promised relief, ammunition, food. On the tenth day the order came to withdraw, and when we withdrew we found the road mined behind us. Our own engineers' work, laid a month before. Someone wanted the ford lost, Marta. Wanted it lost with the regiment in it. Two hundred of us walked out. Two hundred out of four thousand."

Marta closed her eyes. Rain ticked on the leaves above them.

"So you deserted."

"So I *survived*. There's a difference and I'll defend it." He stepped closer, and in the dark his face was young again, terribly young, the plum-thief's face. "I've walked eight hundred miles since. I've eaten things I won't tell our mother. And I'm telling you, sister, whatever you're mixed in with—" his eyes flicked toward the barn, toward the sleeping shape of Felix by the door, toward the satchel— "it isn't worth it. Nothing is. Get clear. Take the child and go east to Uncle Pieter's and let the republic finish dying without you."

"It isn't my choice to be clear of."

"Whose, then? Hers?" He said it softly, contemptuously, and Marta felt anger rise in her throat like heat. "You've known her a week. I'm your blood."

"She saved this child's life. What have you done lately but save your own?"

It was cruel and she knew it the instant she said it, knew it by the way he flinched, and she reached for his sleeve to take it back—but he pulled away, and when he spoke again his voice had changed. Gone flat. Gone careful. The voice of a man doing sums.

"There's an amnesty."

Marta's hand stilled on the empty air where his sleeve had been.

"They posted it at every crossroads south of the river," Tomas went on, in that flat careful voice. "Full pardon for deserters. All you have to do is bring information. Names. Positions. Anything useful to the provosts before the armistice makes all of us worthless." He was not looking at her. He was looking past her, through the rain, at the barn—at the glow of the buried fire, at the dark doorway where a courier slept beside a sealed satchel. "Anything useful."

"Tomas."

"A courier seal opens every gate on the road," he said. "Do you know what a man could buy with that? Not pardon. *Passage.* Papers. A ship. Out. Before the whole country goes under the ice." Now he did look at her, and his eyes were wet and hard at once, and Marta saw that he hated himself already and was going to do it anyway, or wanted her to stop him, or couldn't tell the difference anymore. "What's in her bag, Marta?"

"You don't want to know."

"That's exactly what people say when I should want to know."

Rain fell. The baby stirred against Marta's chest and settled. And Marta thought: *this is what the republic has come to. My brother, offering to sell a stranger's secret for the price of his own skin, and loving her the whole while.* She thought: *and how am I different? I took passage money from a woman I didn't trust, and called it necessity.* She thought, with a cold clarity that frightened her: *everyone on this road is selling something. The question is only whether you sell it cheap, or whether you finally find one thing you won't put a price on.*

"Tomas." She kept her voice low and level, the voice she used on feverish children. "Look at me. Whatever you think is in that satchel—it's bigger than an amnesty. Bigger than passage. If you sell it, you don't get out. You get *used*, and then you get buried, because the men you'd be selling to don't leave witnesses either. Ask yourself who posted that notice. Ask yourself who benefits."

"You don't know that."

"I know that our family taught you sums," Marta said. "So sum it. One pardon against—" she hesitated, and then, because lying badly to her own brother might be the one lie that saved him, she gave him the truth sideways— "against whatever a minister of the Republic of Vessany would kill to keep quiet. Because that's whose road we're standing on, Tomas. That's whose notice you read. Think about whose ink is on it."

He stared at her. The rain came down between them, fine as flour, soft as ash.

"The child," he said finally. His voice had gone strange. "Whose child is it? Truly?"

Marta held his gaze and said nothing, and her silence told him more than any answer, and she watched him understand that too, watch the sums rearrange themselves behind his eyes.

"God help us," he breathed. "You don't even know. You're carrying something you don't even—"

"Tomas!" A voice from the barn—one of the sentries, low and urgent. "Riders on the road. Coming up from the south. Uniforms."

Everything happened very fast after that.

The fire was drowned with a kick. Men poured out through the broken wall into the orchard like water finding cracks, silent, practiced, gone. Tomas gripped Marta's arm once—hard, wordless, a squeeze that was neither promise nor farewell—and shoved her back toward the barn, and then he was running with the others, swallowed by the trees, and Marta stumbled inside to where Felix was already awake, already standing, the baby's swaddling clutched to her chest with both arms, her face in the starlight absolutely calm.

"Riders?" Felix asked.

"From the south. Uniforms." Marta's heart was slamming. "Felix—they'll sweep the barn. They always sweep the buildings first."

"I know." Felix was moving, gathering, tying the satchel beneath her coat. "Back wall. There's a drainage cut—I saw it when we came in. Can you fit through it with her?"

"Yes."

"Go first, then. I'll follow." A pause, one breath long. "Marta. Your brother."

"He didn't do anything."

"He was going to."

"He *didn't*," Marta said fiercely, and found she was crying, rain or otherwise, and did not care. "Whatever else happens—he didn't."

Felix looked at her for a long moment. Something passed across her face that might have been understanding, or envy, or grief for a sister of her own, long since taken by the same state that had taken everything else. Then she nodded once toward the back wall, and the two of them went into the dark and the rain together, through the drainage cut and out into the flooded orchard, while behind them lantern light bloomed in the barn windows and voices called out—officer's voices, crisp and unhurried, the voices of men following orders.

And one voice, deeper than the rest, giving those orders in a tone Marta had heard only once before, at a checkpoint, through a coach door:

*"Search everything. She's carrying a package."*

Marta's blood went to ice. She tightened her arms around the child and ran.
