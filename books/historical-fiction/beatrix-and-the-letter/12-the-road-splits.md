## 12. The Road Splits

The ford announced itself before we saw it, in the way the road began to sing with water.

We had come down out of the hills since dawn, Marta walking beside the hired cart with her skirts kilted up and her boots black with mud, and all that time the river had been a rumor—a gleam between trees, a cold breath on the wind, the distant complaint of water worrying stone. Now the trees opened and there it was: the Vessa proper, wide and brown-green and moving with the patient muscle of something that had been doing this work for ten thousand years and would go on doing it long after the republic's name was a footnote.

The military road ran straight to the ford and across it. Two ruts beaten hard by army wagons, a plank bridge reinforced with iron strapping, and on the far bank—visible even at this distance—the squat stone bulk of a signal tower flying the tricolor of a government that no longer governed much of anything. From the ford to the border assembly was, by the milestone, thirty-one miles of good road. A day's drive. Half a day if the horses were rested.

Beside it, half-hidden in reeds, was the other way.

I had known it would be there. Every courier knows the map beneath the map—the smugglers' paths, the drovers' tracks, the ways that exist because soldiers cannot be bothered to look at them. The marsh path left the military road a quarter mile back, where a faded waymark leaned into the reeds like a drunk against a wall. It ran along the edge of the Veilmere, the flooded lowland that spread for six miles between the river and the hills, and it rejoined the road—if it rejoined anything—somewhere past the second bridge, assuming the water hadn't taken it this season. The dispatches I had carried over nine years mentioned the Veilmere exactly twice, both times in reports of drowned livestock.

Marta stood at the split with Beatrix against her shoulder, studying the two roads the way a woman studies two loaves at market, weighing what she can see.

"The bridge," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"The bridge," I agreed, and did not believe myself.

* * *

The trouble with the fast road was that it was fast in both directions.

We stopped at the drover's inn above the ford to rest the horses, and I told myself it was caution, not cowardice—watering animals, buying bread, doing the ordinary things that ordinary travelers do so thoroughly that no one thinks to look at them twice. Marta fed the baby in the shadow of the stairwell while I stood at the window with a cup of small beer going warm in my hands, and I watched the road.

I watched the road because for three days something had been following us. Not close. Not obviously. But at Ashford Gate the courteous captain had asked one question too many about where we'd crossed from the capital, and at the toll checkpoint the clerk's ledger had held, freshly inked, a description that might have been Marta or might have been any wet nurse in Vessany. And last night, sleeping in the loft of a barn, I had heard hooves on the far side of the hedge at an hour when honest men were abed, moving south to north—moving the way we moved—and by morning they were gone, which frightened me more than if they'd stayed.

Now, from the inn window, I saw them.

They came down the military road at a trot, four riders in the grey undress uniform of the Ministry Guard, and at their head rode a man I would have known anywhere, though I had seen him perhaps a dozen times and never once spoken to him. Captain Ilves. He had delivered Corvane's most sensitive dispatches to my handlers; he had signed the chits that authorized my routes; his handwriting sat in my memory beside Corvane's own, neat and unhurried, the hand of a man who never doubted what his orders meant. He sat his horse now the way he wrote—erect, economical, looking at the ford and the far tower and not at the inn at all, which was worse than if he'd looked.

He raised a hand. His riders spread along the bank in a practiced fan. And I understood, with the cold settling into the marrow kind of clarity, that they were not chasing us. They were waiting. The ford was the throat, and the throat was being closed.

"He'll question the ferryman first," Marta said at my shoulder. She had come up without a sound, the baby quiet now against her chest. "Then the innkeeper. Then anyone crossing with a child."

"Anyone traveling with a woman and an infant," I said.

"Then it's the bridge we don't take."

I made myself look at the other road. The marsh path entered the Veilmere through a stand of dead alders, and even from the inn window I could see the water standing black between the tussocks, still as poured tea, hiding its depth. There was a plank walk somewhere under the flood—I could make out a length of it, then a gap, then nothing but reed tops shivering in the wind. Six miles of that. With a child three months old. In the last week of autumn, when the nights came down like lids.

"It will kill her," I said. It came out flat, the way I stated distances and dates. That was how I kept my voice from breaking. "Cold gets into infants faster than into us. If she goes into the water—even once—" I didn't finish it. The image finished itself: the swaddling soaking black, the small face screwing up to cry and having no breath left for it.

Marta said nothing for a moment. She shifted the baby, and Beatrix turned her face out from Marta's chest and looked—at me, I thought, though infants look at everything and nothing—with those grave dark eyes that had no business being so steady.

"You've carried papers through worse," Marta said.

"Papers," I said, "don't breathe."

* * *

But that was the thing I could not stop turning over, all through the terrible hour that followed, while we paid the innkeeper for a room we didn't use and led the horses out the yard gate toward the alders like women gathering rushes. Nine years of carrying things, and every rule I had was built on one foundation: what can be carried must be worth less than the carrier. Papers burn. Seals can be forged. A courier who dies protecting her satchel has misunderstood her trade. I had watched a colleague hang for a dispatch case rather than surrender it, and I had admired him, and I had sworn I would never be so stupid.

And now here was the arithmetic turned on its head. The document was in the swaddling. The child was the satchel. And the choice the road offered was the oldest one in the world, dressed in mud and reeds: my survival, sure and swift, across a guarded bridge where I might talk my way through because I had done it a hundred times—or her survival, bought with six miles of black water and a fever risk I could not calculate, because I had never once in my life been responsible for anything that could die of cold.

Ilves was at the ford. Ilves had been sent. Which meant Corvane knew—not that a courier had left, but that this courier had left with more than paper. Mercy leaves fingerprints; so does fear. Somewhere behind us the minister had woken in the night and remembered exactly what he had set adrift at my door, and he had sent his neatest hand to fetch it back.

If I took the bridge, I lost. Not maybe. Not probably. Ilves did not fan his riders along a bank for a maybe.

If I took the marsh—

I looked at Beatrix again, and she was watching the water now, or the birds over it, her whole small attention given to the white gulls wheeling above the reeds, and I thought: she is the only thing I have ever carried that cannot be replaced. In nine years I had lost dispatches to fire and rain and once to a thief in Drennmark, and each time I had reported the loss and received a replacement and gone on, because that was the bargain of my life—nothing in the satchel matters more than the arm that carries it. I had lived by the belief that everything can be carried again.

She could not be carried again. There was no second copy of her.

"All right," I said to Marta, and my voice sounded like someone else's. "The marsh."

* * *

We sold the horses to the innkeeper for a third of their worth, because horses do not walk plank walks, and because a woman leading two animals back along the military road would be remembered forever. We bought oilskins, a boatman's pole from a lad mending nets behind the stable, and a flask of spirits that Marta insisted on and I pretended not to understand. We wrapped Beatrix in every dry layer we owned, and then Marta did something I would remember for the rest of my life: she took the feeding cloth from her own shoulders and wound it around the outermost swaddling, over the cipher's hiding place, so that the girl wore proof and milk-stains together, indistinguishable.

"No man searching for documents," Marta said, "searches here." She patted the cloth, matter-of-fact, and something in my chest turned over.

At the alder stand the path gave us its answer plainly. The first forty yards of plank walk stood above the water, sound enough. Then the flood took it. Beyond, the route showed itself only as a line of tussocks and dead reed tops, spaced like stepping stones for a giant, with black water lying between them at a depth none of us could guess. The boatman's pole would find the bottom or it wouldn't. The oilskins would turn the spray or they wouldn't. Six miles. The light was already leaning long and yellow across the Veilmere, and I knew—we both knew—that we would be out here after dark.

I tested the first tussock with the pole. Firm. The second. Firm, but it rocked. The water around it dimpled and closed, unbothered.

"Marta," I said. "You have passage money whether we cross or not. You have it whatever you choose. I want you to hear me say that."

She looked at me with an expression I couldn't read—half anger, half something older—and shifted Beatrix high onto her shoulder.

"I buried a daughter of my own milk last winter," she said. "Fever, in the baggage train outside Solm. Nobody chose anything. Nobody decided. She just went, because the world was careless with her." She stepped up onto the first plank, sure-footed, the baby riding above the black water like a lantern. "I'm not letting this world be careless with this one. Come on, or stay. But decide quick, because the light's going."

I looked back once, only once, at the military road. It lay empty in the evening sun, hard and straight and safe-looking, running to the ford where four grey riders sat their horses with the patience of men being paid to wait. The fast road. The sensible road. The road where I survived, because I had always known how to survive there—dull, invisible, unasked. Nine years I had loved that road the way a prisoner loves her cell door, and it was closing in front of me, and I found—to my astonishment—that what I felt was not fear.

It was relief.

I put the pole down into the black water and followed the lantern.

* * *

The Veilmere at dusk was a country that shouldn't exist. Sky below us and sky above, the flooded plain holding the sunset so perfectly that we seemed to walk on clouds gone bruise-dark, and the reeds sighing all around in a language just short of words. We moved tussock to tussock, Marta ahead with the child, me behind with the pole, sounding the water at every step—knee-deep, waist-deep, once nothing, the pole sinking into soft bottom until only a handspan of shaft remained and we doubled back fifty yards to find firmer ground. The cold came up out of the water through my boots like a living thing. Somewhere to the north a bittern boomed, once, twice, a sound like a great door swinging.

Twice Beatrix fussed, and twice Marta soothed her without stopping, a low continuous hum that I realized after a while was a lullaby sung almost entirely on one note, conserving breath the way a soldier conserves ammunition. And once—only once—the path failed us altogether.

A channel. The flood had found some old creek bed and widened it, eight feet of open black water between us and the next tussock, too deep for the pole, too wide to jump with a child. The light was nearly gone. Behind us, faint over the reeds, I could hear the ford road: a wagon, a shout, sounds of ordinary travel on the road we had refused.

Marta didn't hesitate. She knelt on the tussock, unwound the feeding cloth, and passed Beatrix to me—passed her into my arms, warm weight, furious red face, small fists already gripping my collar with a strength that had no right to exist in something so new—and I held her the way I had held nothing in nine years, both arms, whole body, all of me given to the single task of not dropping what could not be replaced.

Marta slid into the water.

It took her to the waist. I heard the breath go out of her between her teeth. Then she waded, hands out for the far tussock, gasping at each step in a rhythm she tried to make silent and mostly managed, and when her fingers closed on the reeds beyond she turned and held both arms up out of the water, and I stepped to the edge of the gap and gave her the child across the black space, and Marta lifted her high, higher than she needed to, so that the swaddling never touched the flood—so that the last thing between Beatrix and the cold water, all the way across, was air.

Then it was my turn in the water, and I will not describe it. Some things belong to the marsh.

On the far side we crouched together on a hummock while night finished falling, all three of us shaking, Beatrix screaming with the full outrage of her small body, which was the most beautiful sound I had ever heard, because screaming meant breath, and breath meant warm, and warm meant alive. Marta got her inside her coat against her skin. I got the flask into Marta's hands. Overhead the first stars were coming out over the reeds, indifferent and enormous.

"We're halfway," I said. I didn't know if it was true. It was the kind of thing one says.

Marta looked at me across the dark, water running off her braid, the baby's face just visible tucked beneath her jaw.

"You chose her," she said. Not accusing. Wondering, almost. "Back at the fork. You looked at the easy road a long time, and then you chose her."

I wanted to say something true and could not find it, so I said the next thing, which was also true:

"I've spent nine years learning how not to be seen. I think—" My voice did something unfamiliar. "I think I was waiting for something worth being seen for."

Marta snorted, which was as close to tenderness as the marsh allowed, and rose, and we went on into the dark, tussock by tussock, the pole finding the bottom, the reeds parting and closing behind us. And it was somewhere in that next black mile—cold to the bone, lost, listening to the bittern boom and the child's small grumbling breath against Marta's neck—that I heard the thing I had been refusing to hear since the ford.

Hooves. Not behind us. Ahead.

Out of the northern edge of the Veilmere, where the marsh path should have come up empty reed-bank and nothing else, a lantern swung—low, deliberate, the swing of a man dismounted and walking his horse through shallow water, coming toward us along the very track we needed. And with the wind came a scrap of voice, calling, not loudly, in a tone I knew from a hundred signed chits: patient, courteous, absolutely certain of being obeyed.

Captain Ilves had not waited at the ford after all.
