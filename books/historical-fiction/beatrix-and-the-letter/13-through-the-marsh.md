## 13. Through the Marsh

The water did not look like water. It looked like a road that had died and been buried, and the marsh had grown over its corpse—black in the hollows, brown-green where the reeds stood thick as a congregation, silver-skinned wherever the moon found a gap in the clouds and laid itself down flat. Marta stood at the edge of it with her skirts knotted above her knees and the baby strapped against her chest in the sling Felix had cut from a horse blanket, and she thought: this is what the end of a country looks like. Not fire. Not soldiers. Just a road nobody maintains anymore because there is nothing left on either side of it worth defending.

"Put your feet where I put mine," Felix said behind her. Her voice had gone thin since the ford—the voice of someone spending coins she could not replace. "The ground lies here. It will look solid for three steps and then open like a mouth."

"And you know this how?"

"I don't. A carter told me once, years ago, when I still asked questions." A pause, and then, drier than the marsh deserved: "I've regretted stopping ever since."

Marta almost laughed. Almost. The sound got as far as her throat and turned into something else, something that would have woken the child if the child were capable of waking, which lately she was not.

That was the thing Marta could not stop touching with her mind, the way a tongue returns to a cracked tooth: the heat. Little Lotte—no. Not Lotte. She had promised herself she would not name the girl, because names were nails, and everything nailed down in Vessany that autumn got pried up and thrown on a cart. But somewhere in the second hour of the crossing, with black water at her shins and the child burning against her sternum, the promise had broken, and the name had come out of her mouth as naturally as breath: *Bea.* Just Bea. A small sound, a stone you could keep in your cheek. She'd said it to the hot forehead, and the baby's eyelids had fluttered, and that was all, but Marta had felt it land somewhere below her ribs and stay there, lodged, permanent, like a splinter working its way toward the heart.

The fever had begun at the waystation, a flush and a fretfulness, easy to mistake for teething or fear. By the ford it had become a fact. The child's skin gave back heat like a brick wall at dusk. She cried without sound now, too spent to spend her voice, her small fists opening and closing against Marta's collarbone as though gathering handfuls of something invisible. And Felix—who read cipher sheets the way other women read love letters, who could stand before an officer and perform boredom so well that Marta herself half believed it—that woman had looked at the baby's flushed face and gone the color of tallow, and said, "We can't go back," and Marta had heard what she actually meant: *I cannot carry this decision alone.*

So they had taken the marsh path. The flooded one. The one the carter's ghost had warned about.

* * *

The first hour was merely miserable.

The mud took each foot with a kiss and released it with a complaint, a long sucking gasp that sounded, in the dark, obscenely like speech. Marta learned within twenty steps to lift her feet from the knee rather than the hip, to plant them wide, to trust the reeds more than the ground beneath them. Reeds held. Ground lied. There was a philosophy in that, she supposed, though she had never been a woman with much use for philosophy; her life had been one long sequence of things that needed doing before dark—milk, wood, water, wash—and thinking had always been the thing she did with whatever scraps of attention remained. Lately there were no scraps. All of her attention belonged to her feet and the bundle on her chest, and even so, even split two ways and fraying, part of her mind kept circling back to her brother, walking somewhere behind them in the dark with his courier's knowledge and his deserter's hunger, and she could not decide if she had done right by keeping his secret, or whether she had simply postponed the moment when it would cost somebody something. Everything cost somebody something. That was the whole of Vessany's arithmetic now.

Felix moved ahead of her like a heron, deliberate, angular, testing each step with a staff she'd cut from dead alder. Twice she went down to the hip in a hidden pool and came up silent, which frightened Marta more than screaming would have. Screaming would have meant panic. Silence meant practice. This was a woman who had trained herself out of every human noise.

"Talk to her," Felix said once, not turning around.

"She can't hear me."

"Then talk anyway. Fever patients hear through the skin. My mother believed it." A pause; the staff probed; the marsh swallowed and released. "She was wrong about most things. Do it regardless."

So Marta talked. Low, shapeless, the drone of a woman stirring a pot—nothing worth repeating, the names of villages she'd nursed in, the recipe for barley water, the color of her grandmother's kitchen door, which had been green, a terrible green, the green of pond scum, and how she had loved it. She felt foolish for ten minutes. Then she felt the small body against hers ease by one degree, the fists unclench slightly, the breathing go from sawtooth to ripple, and she stopped feeling foolish forever. Words were warmth. Who knew. She talked until her throat dried and the moon went behind cloud and stayed there.

In the third hour the leeches found them.

She felt the first one as a faint wrongness at her ankle, a coolness inside the warmth, and when she dragged her leg up into the sliver of light under the cloud-break, there it was: fat and glistening, banded like something precious, fastened to her just above the boot top and already swollen with her. She made a small involuntary noise—a human noise, the first in hours—and Felix turned back.

"Don't pull it."

"It's *drinking me.*"

"If you pull, the head stays. Salt or a knife edge or wait." Felix waded close, and by the moon's grudging return Marta watched her work the flat of her knife along the leech's body until it let go with what looked like reluctance, dropping away into the black like a coin returned to a purse. Then Felix crouched, balanced impossibly, and checked Marta's other leg, her hands brisk and impersonal, a nurse's hands, a soldier's hands. Three more leeches came off her calves. Two off Felix. One, when they paused on a hummock of firmer ground to check the child, they found already attached inside the sling's edge, gorged and sluggish, and Marta's stomach turned over as Felix flicked it away into the dark.

"They're only hungry," Felix said. "Everything out here is only hungry. It's almost restful."

"That isn't restful, that's terrible."

"Yes," Felix agreed, and Marta heard, beneath the dryness, something worn smooth as a riverstone. "It is."

They went on. Marta talked. Bea burned. The marsh breathed its cold wet breath over all of them, indifferent as God was rumored to be.

* * *

Somewhere past midnight the water deepened without warning, and the character of the crossing changed entirely.

One step the mud held her mid-shin; the next it was at her thighs, and Felix's staff found no bottom. Ahead, the reeds thinned into open black—flooded lowland, a drowned field maybe, or the marsh proper, miles of it, and the old road's raised bed was either ahead under the water or lost, and there was no asking anyone.

"We can't cross that with her in the sling," Marta said. The words came out flat with exhaustion. Fear needed energy; she had none left over.

"No."

"Felix."

"I'm thinking."

"You're standing in freezing water in the dark, thinking."

"I do my best thinking standing in freezing water in the dark. It's why I've survived." But her voice cracked on the last word, just barely, a hairline fracture, and Marta understood suddenly and completely that Felix Galloway was not afraid of drowning, or Ilves, or the checkpoints behind them. She was afraid of the weight in her arms going quiet. It was written all over her the way fever was written on Bea's skin. Nine years of invisibility, and this child had made her visible to herself, and she hated it, and she would die before giving it up, and she didn't know yet which of those things governed her.

"Give her to me," Marta said. "Both hands. You take the satchel and the papers and my bag. I'll hold her high."

"Marta—"

"She knows me. I've carried her since Ashford Gate. My arms are stronger than yours—you haul cipher sheets, I haul water buckets." She was already untying the sling. "And if I go down, you grab my hair. Don't be polite about it."

For a long moment Felix said nothing, and the marsh filled the silence with its small sounds—reeds knocking, water shifting, some bird crying far off like a hinge that needed oil. Then she took the child.

The transfer was the hardest work of the night. Felix lifted Bea out of the sling and passed her across the gap between their bodies, and the baby stirred at the change of arms and gave a thin, cracked cry, the first real sound she'd made in hours, and both women froze as though the sound might carry across the water to men with lanterns. It faded. Felix's face, in the moment before she handed the child over, was naked in a way Marta knew she would never be permitted to mention again—stripped of performance, stripped of the courier's blankness, just a woman holding a sick baby she had known for six days and would apparently burn down the world for. Then it closed again, like a shutter, and she became practical: satchel across her chest, bags distributed, staff in hand.

"Hold her above your head if you must," Felix said. "I'll find the road bed. Feel for the ridge under your feet—it's cobbled underneath, you'll know it when your sole finds stone."

Stone under mud under water under night. Marta stepped out.

The water rose. Waist. Ribs. The cold went through her like a confession, taking everything she'd withheld, and she gasped once and mastered it and kept Bea aloft with both hands, the child's head tucked under her chin, and walked. Each step was a negotiation conducted in darkness with a counterparty that wanted her dead. Her feet searched for the cobbled ridge Felix promised. Once, twice, she found it—a firmness, a rounded certainty under the silt—and followed it like a rope hand over hand. The water reached her chest. She tilted her chin back, lifting Bea higher, until the child rode above the surface entirely, a small hot weight held up on straight arms, and Marta understood that she would hold this position until her shoulders tore loose from their sockets, and that this information changed nothing whatsoever.

The fever-heat came through the wet cloth against her neck, absurd, impossible—a coal carried over an ocean. Bea whimpered. Marta put her lips against the damp hair and said the name again, because it was the only medicine she had.

"Bea. Bea, little one. I've got you up out of the water, see? Nothing touches you. The whole world's gone under and you're dry as Sunday. That's what I'm for. That's the whole job now."

She talked. She talked the way she'd fed children who weren't hers through two wars' worth of displacement—not because the words mattered but because the voice did, because a voice is a rope thrown to someone drowning, even when both of you are already in the water. She told Bea about the green kitchen door. She told her about her brother, whose name she hadn't spoken aloud in three days, telling it now as though confessing could keep him safe at a distance. She told her about a village with a bell that rang wrong, a half-tone flat, and how everyone had loved it anyway because it was theirs. The words ran out and she made sounds instead, humming, the tuneless lullaby of women with no talent for music, and the marsh took the sound and gave back nothing, and her arms burned, and she walked.

Twice she went off the ridge. The second time the water took her to the collarbones and her mouth filled with the taste of rot and old leaves, and for one full second she was certain—certain the way you are only certain at the very bottom of things—that she was going to let go, that her hands would fail, that the child would slide into the black. And then some animal below thought spoke in her, older than grief, older than countries: *not this one. Whatever else goes under, not this one,* and her elbows locked, and Bea swung up clear on straight arms, dripping, screaming now—a real scream, furious and loud, and Marta laughed with filthy water running down her face because it was the most beautiful sound of her entire life. A scream meant lungs. Lungs meant fight. Fevered and burning and furious, the child was fighting.

Felix's hand closed on Marta's hair, exactly as instructed, impolite as ordered, and hauled her sideways until her feet found stone again.

"Thirty more steps," Felix said. Her voice was wrecked. "There's a hummock. I can see it."

"There's nothing to see, it's night."

"I can see it."

Marta believed her. Later she would understand that this—belief, extended without evidence to a woman she'd known a week—was the true crossing, harder than any water. She counted steps aloud so Bea could hear the numbers, thirty, twenty-nine, twenty-eight, as though the count were a ladder they were climbing together, and the mud pulled at her legs like the hands of the drowned, and her shoulders screamed, and she held that child above the black water the entire way, and set her down on the hummock's coarse grass with arms that would not lower properly afterward, that stayed raised, trembling, for several seconds after they were empty, as though the body refused to accept the delivery.

* * *

They did not dare a fire. They made a nest of the horse blanket and the dry inner layer of Marta's bag, and Felix wrung out what cloth she could, and Marta held Bea skin to skin inside her own shawl, sharing what warmth she had left, which was not much. The fever peaked near dawn. Marta knew because the child stopped shivering and went rigid, and her breathing went quick and shallow as a bird's, and her eyes rolled back beneath lids that showed blue at the seams, and Marta's heart stopped with her.

"What do we do?" she whispered. "Felix. What do we do?"

Felix crouched over them. In the grey pre-light her face was all planes and shadows, and Marta watched her reach for competence the way a man reaches for a weapon he's not sure is loaded.

"Cool cloth. Wet her head, her wrists, the backs of her knees. Not cold—cool. Slowly." She tore a strip from her own shirt hem, soaked it in the seep of clean-ish water at the hummock's edge, wrung it, folded it, and laid it across the tiny forehead with a tenderness that seemed to surprise even her. "Talk. Keep talking. If she convulses, lay her on her side. Don't hold her down. Count the seconds aloud so I can hear them from wherever I am."

"Where will you be?"

"Getting us out of this marsh before full light. We're a mile from the toll road and there's a checkpoint on it, and we need to be near it when it opens, because after the armistice it won't open at all." She hesitated. Something moved across her face—doubt, offered like a gift. "Tell me to stay."

Marta looked up at her. Every instinct said keep the strong hands close, the knife-hands, the leech-hands, the hands that had hauled her out of the black by her hair. But Bea's breath was a moth's wing against her collarbone, and the truth was the truth: one of them had to think past this hummock, and Marta's whole mind had narrowed to the size of a burning forehead.

"Go," she said. "Get us out. I'll count."

Felix nodded once and went, wading into the greying mist, staff probing, growing less distinct until she was a rumor, until she was gone.

Marta counted. Seconds, then minutes, then the fevers' slow tides. She bathed the small wrists. She sang the flat-belled song of a village that probably didn't exist anymore. The sun came up the color of pewter behind the clouds and turned the flooded plain into hammered metal, and the mist burned off in rags, and somewhere out in it Felix was finding a road, because that was what Felix did—found roads through places that had none.

Near full light the fever broke. Not gently; it broke like a fever should, in a flood of sweat that soaked them both, the little body going limp and then restless and then, unbelievably, hungry. Marta fed her right there in the ruined grass with the marsh steaming around them, and the child nursed with the desperate competence of the truly recovered, and looked up at Marta with eyes that had cleared to a fierce ordinary blue, eyes that had no idea what they'd put everyone through.

"Well," Marta whispered. "Beatrix, then. Since you insist on surviving. Bea for short. Don't argue with me, I've earned it."

The name settled over the child like the shawl—light, warm, accepted. Somewhere beyond the reeds, faint but unmistakable, came the sound Marta had been waiting for and dreading: wheels on stone, a gate-bar lifting, the clipped morning voice of a soldier calling the first travelers of the day forward. The third checkpoint. Open. For now.

Marta gathered the child close, shouldered the bags, and started toward the sound.
