## 15. Ilves Closes

The granary rose out of the flatland like a broken tooth, its stone shell blackened from a fire two seasons old, its timber roof half-collapsed into the dark cavity where grain had once been poured in golden rivers. From a distance it looked like nothing—a ruin among ruins, one more casualty of the retreating army's scorched discipline. Up close it offered what Felix needed most: walls thick enough to muffle an infant's cry, a single doorway facing away from the road, and shadows deep enough to drown in.

She had been running on something that was no longer quite courage and not yet despair. The marsh had taken a full night and most of her certainty; the fever had taken hold of Beatrix somewhere in the black water, and now the child burned against her chest with a heat that had nothing to do with the swaddling. Marta walked beside her with her skirts stiffened to boards by dried mud, her hands cracked from hauling Felix out of a leech-pool when her legs had simply stopped answering. Neither of them had spoken in an hour. Speech cost breath, and breath was rationed now like everything else.

"She's worse," Marta said finally, because someone had to say it.

"I know."

"You keep saying *I know* as though knowing were a medicine."

Felix adjusted the bundle against her collarbone. Beatrix's breathing came fast and shallow, each exhale ending in a small cracked sound that went through Felix like a splinter working toward bone. Nine years she had carried things—sealed orders, cipher sheets, other people's wars—and never once had anything she carried been able to die if she set it down wrong.

The granary's doorway gaped at them, framed by smoke-stained stone. Inside, the smell of char still lingered under the wet rot: rye gone to ash, a harvest murdered before it could feed anyone. Felix stood at the threshold longer than she should have, listening. Wind moved through the broken roof in slow tides. Somewhere far behind them, too far to be sure and too close to be ignored, a dog barked twice and fell silent.

"We rest until dark," she said. "Then we take the field paths to Hallowmere. From there it's half a day to the third checkpoint."

"And after the checkpoint?"

"After the checkpoint," Felix said, "the road belongs to the assembly, and Ilves can't touch us without making himself a criminal in front of witnesses who matter."

Marta looked at her with the flat, unfooled gaze of a woman who had nursed soldiers through the lying hours between wound and grave. "You believe that."

"I have to carry something," Felix said. "It might as well be that."

* * *

Inside, the granary held a cathedral of wreckage. Half the roof had fallen inward, great charred beams leaning against one another like drunkards propping each other upright, and through the gaps overhead the afternoon sky showed in ragged pieces—pale grey wool shot through with one seam of hard blue. A drift of burnt grain had fused into a crust along the near wall; when Marta's boot caught it, it shattered with a sound like breaking biscuits. In the deepest corner, beneath the one section of roofing that had survived intact, someone had made a fire months ago. Ash in a ring. A rusted tin cup. The small archaeology of other fugitives.

They settled there as quietly as people settling anywhere can ever manage, which is to say badly. Beatrix would not stop crying—not loudly; her fever had burned her down to whimpers—but the whimpers carried in the granary's hollow acoustics, bouncing off stone, multiplying. Marta unbuttoned her shirt with fingers that shook, though whether from cold or fear Felix couldn't tell, and took the child to her breast. Beatrix rooted once, weakly, and turned away.

"She won't nurse," Marta whispered. "She hasn't since the water."

"Then we get her to a physician in Hallowmere."

"Hallowmere is a village of forty souls with one barber who pulls teeth."

"Then we get her past the checkpoint and across the border and—"

"Felix." Marta's voice was very low and very steady, the voice of a woman holding herself together at a seam. "Look at her. If we don't find help tonight, the checkpoint doesn't matter. None of it matters. You'll be carrying proof of treason to a parliament that will thank you, and you'll do it over a grave."

Felix did look. In the dimness the baby's face had gone from flushed to a strange translucent pallor, the lashes dark against cheeks that fever had painted with two bright, unnatural flags of color. Her small fists opened and closed on nothing. Felix had spent three weeks learning the weight of this child—the way her body redistributed itself in sleep, the particular gravity of trust—and now the weight felt wrong, lighter, as though something essential were already being subtracted.

"There's a farmstead," Marta said. "We passed it before the ford, remember? Smoke from the chimney. An hour back along the field path, maybe less if I run."

"With a fevered infant?"

"I ran from Vessany's cavalry with my brother over my shoulder and a musket ball through my hood. I can run for a physician."

"It's not a physician. It's strangers. It's names given away. It's—" Felix stopped, hearing herself. *Invisible. Unattached. Unasked.* The old litany rising up like a reflex, useless as a prayer to a god she'd stopped believing in. Every rule she had lived by for nine years told her to stay in the dark, to move at night, to trust walls over people. And every one of those rules had been written by a woman who had nothing alive to lose.

"The rules are why we're here," she said slowly, "and they're not going to save her."

"So I'll go," Marta said. "I'm faster than you, and the baby needs you calmer than you are, which isn't a compliment, it's arithmetic. Give me your last coin. If there's a herb-woman or a barber or anyone who's ever bled a fever out of anyone, I'll bring them back before full dark."

Felix dug the coin from the inner pocket where she kept the things that mattered—the coin, the miniature portrait with its small painted face she had been ordered to forget, the folded cipher sheet with its terrible arithmetic of sold garrisons. She pressed the coin into Marta's palm and then, without deciding to, held Marta's hand closed around it a moment longer than necessary.

"If you're not back by dark—"

"Then you go on without me," Marta said, "and you name the girl yourself, and you tell the assembly a wet nurse named Marta Kessler died of mud and honesty." She smiled, and it was the first smile in days that reached anywhere near her eyes. "But I'll be back. Trust is a wager, courier. I'm putting money on myself."

She buttoned her coat, pulled her hood up over her cropped hair, and slipped out through the doorway into the failing light. Felix listened to her footsteps fade along the field path—quick, even, unafraid, the footsteps of a woman who had decided something—and then there was only the wind in the broken beams and the small hot weight of the child, and the long grey silence of a country burning itself out.

* * *

Felix measured time by the sky through the shattered roof. The blue seam widened, thinned, began to color. Beatrix slept at last—a bad sleep, twitching, her breath rasping—and Felix sat with her back against the charred wall and let herself, for exactly sixty heartbeats, close her eyes.

She was asleep anyway. Exhaustion does not respect arithmetic. She woke to the sound of boots.

Not Marta's boots. These were heavier, shod with iron, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who had tracked something for days and finally heard it stop. Two sets. Maybe three. Coming around the granary's flank from the road side, which meant they had passed Marta on the path, or followed her, or—

*Marta.* Felix's blood went cold and stayed cold. They hadn't passed her. They had let her walk ahead of them, straight to the farmstead or wherever else she'd gone, because a woman asking for a fever remedy in a border district tells every listening ear exactly where the fever is sleeping.

"Courier Galloway." Captain Ilves's voice, from outside the doorway, pitched to carry without effort—the voice of a man who had never needed to shout and resented having to start. "I can see your silhouette against the wall gap. There's no point in the corner-and-knife routine. I've brought six men and I only need two of them."

Felix stood. Her legs ached; her mouth tasted of marsh water and old fear. She moved deeper into the shadow of the standing beams, one arm curled around the child, the other hand finding the knife at her belt—not because a knife against six men was hope, but because her hand wanted to hold something that had edges.

"You've been difficult," Ilves said. The doorway darkened with his silhouette: broad-shouldered, unhurried, his officer's coat brushed clean despite nine days of pursuit. He had always been fastidious. He had taught her half her tradecraft at the courier academy, years ago, in another republic. "The marsh was inspired. Stupid, with an infant, but inspired. Minister Corvane wants to know what you're carrying."

"Then he should have sent a better note with it," Felix said.

Something moved in the doorway—Ilves shaking his head, almost fondly. "Nine years invisible, Galloway. The best blind courier the ministry ever had. You ask nothing, you see nothing, you *are* nothing, and everyone trusts you precisely because of it. Do you know how many times I argued against your file being flagged? I said: that one will die of old age in a garret somewhere, having served her whole life without once becoming a problem." He stepped inside. His boots ground the burnt grain into the ash. Behind him, two soldiers fanned out to either side of the doorway, muskets loose in their hands, blocking the light. "And then one morning you steal a package worth a treaty and burn half the garrison roads getting away with it. What happened to you?"

"A door opened," Felix said. "There was something in it."

Ilves laughed, short and genuine, and it was horrible because some buried part of her had liked this man—had trusted the one instructor who never made the girls in his class feel like livestock. He advanced into the granary, his shadow sliding across the floor, and stopped ten feet from her corner with the easy stance of a man fencing with a student.

"Here is what happens now," he said. "You give me the documents. All of them. You give me the child—there are good families, paid families, waiting; the girl will want for nothing, that I promise on my mother's name. And you walk out of here with your satchel and your blank papers and your invisibility, whole. The republic falls in eleven days regardless. Corvane's peace is signed regardless. You can be nobody again, safely, forever. Or you can be a corpse in a burned building with a story about bandits that no one will trouble to check." He spread his gloved hands. "I am offering you your own life back, Galloway. It's a better offer than most people dying this month are getting."

For one moment—one long, treacherous heartbeat—it sounded like mercy. It sounded like the warm dark of anonymity, the satchel-shaped life, the blessed weightlessness of carrying things instead of being one. Nobody again. Safe forever. She felt the temptation the way you feel a bath when you have been cold for nine years.

Then Beatrix stirred against her chest and made her small cracked sound, and the spell broke on a fever-rasp no bigger than a sparrow's complaint.

"No," Felix said.

Ilves sighed, and all the warmth went out of him like a lamp shuttered. "Take them," he said to the soldiers, and the doorway filled.

What happened next happened in pieces, the way disasters do. The nearer soldier came forward with his musket angled to pin her against the wall; Felix moved left, into the lean of a fallen beam, and the beam groaned. The soldier shifted his footing on the crust of burnt grain, and the crust gave way under him with a crack like a snapped bone—he stumbled, swore, caught himself against the beam. And above them all, dislodged by that single stumble, a charred crossbeam high in the surviving section of roof let go of its rotted mooring and came down.

Felix would remember it forever as silent. Such mass, such fire-blackened timber, and yet no scream of wood, only the pressure of air parting and the world going white with dust. She threw herself sideways over the child. The beam struck where she had stood, drove through the weakened floor planking, and somewhere beyond the dust a man was screaming—the soldier whose leg had been pinned, his musket discharged wildly upward into the remaining roof, and now the whole surviving section of the granary's covering was shifting, shedding, beginning its long slow decision to fall.

Through the dust came a second shape. Not a soldier. Smaller. Moving fast, from the direction of the doorway, where Marta stood outlined against the last violet light of evening with a farmwife's shotgun still smoking in her hands—she had fired at the second soldier, or at Ilves, or at the darkness itself—and then she dropped the gun and ran straight into the collapsing heart of the granary because Felix and the child were in it.

"Marta, *down*—"

The roof finished its decision. Timber came down in sheets and spears, burning now where the musket ball had found dry rafter-dust, and the last thing Felix saw before she covered the child with her own body was Marta turning, not away, but *toward* the falling beam—turning to shove Felix clear of it, both cracked hands outstretched, her mouth open on words the noise swallowed whole.

The beam took Marta across the shoulder and back. The impact threw her sideways onto the grain-crust floor, and the fire that the falling timbers dragged down with them began, very calmly, to spread along the drifted ash and chaff toward the corner where the cipher sheet had fallen from Felix's pocket and lay open on the stones, its columns of sold garrisons pale in the growing orange light, beginning to curl.

Felix snatched it back with a bare hand and felt the paper's edge sear her fingertips, and didn't care. The roof-beam that had missed her by the width of a prayer lay across Marta's legs and lower body, and Marta was conscious—God help them both, she was conscious—and she was looking up at Felix with an expression of furious, betrayed astonishment, as though the beam had broken a promise.

"I put money on myself," Marta whispered. Blood came with the words. "Damn it. Damn it, I *won*—"

The fire climbed the fallen timber. Through the roaring doorway Ilves was shouting orders no one obeyed; his pinned soldier screamed; the second soldier lay where the shotgun had put him down. And Felix knelt in the ash between the woman who had lied beautifully for her at the toll of names and the child who burned with fever against her heart, and understood, with a clarity that felt like the beam itself, the price of survival made flesh at last: someone else had paid it. Someone had always been going to pay it.

"Lift it," Marta said through her teeth. "You lift the beam, courier, and I'll try to forgive you for being right about the rules."

Felix set the cipher and the miniature inside her shirt against her skin, tightened her arms around the crying child, and gripped the burning wood.
