## 16. What the Child Is Worth

The granary smelled of char and old wheat, of a fire that had happened years ago and never quite finished dying. Felix lay where she had fallen, half beneath the collapsed stair, her left side wet and growing wetter, and listened to the rain come through the burned roof in long gray threads.

It fell on the blackened beams and hissed. It fell into the dust of the threshing floor and raised the ghost smell of grain. It fell on the child, who had begun to cry — that thin, furious, newborn outrage — and it was the crying that told Felix she was still alive, because as long as Beatrix screamed there was breath in the small body, and as long as there was breath there was something left to carry.

Marta was not screaming. Marta was not making any sound at all, and Felix could not turn her head far enough to see her, only a dark shape against the darker wall, one arm flung out across the boards, unnaturally still, the way bodies go still when the animal in them has decided to spend nothing more on movement.

*She took it for me.* The thought arrived without feeling, flat as a ledger line. *She stepped into the line of it the way you'd step into a doorway to block a draft.*

Felix pressed her palm against her own wound — high on the ribs, below the arm, the kind of place that bleeds enormously and means either everything or nothing — and made herself count. Nine years of counting. Counting was what she had instead of prayer.

One: the cipher sheet was sewn into the swaddling, against the child's back, and the child was crying, which meant the swaddling was intact enough to hold her fury. Two: the miniature portrait was inside Felix's own bodice, against her skin, warm as a second heart. Three: Marta's brother had sold them once already and been paid in a bullet from the men he'd sold them to, because Captain Ilves did not buy traitors twice; he used them up. Four: Ilves was in the doorway.

He stood with the rain behind him and the pistol low at his side, not aimed, which was worse than aimed. Aimed meant hurry. Low meant leisure, and leisure meant he believed the outcome was already written.

"Courier," he said.

Felix said nothing. Her mouth was full of copper and straw dust. She watched him come into the granary the way a man walks into a church he no longer believes in — hat off, out of some old courtesy or some old habit, water running off the brim.

"The nurse is dead or near it," Ilves said. "The child is loud. You are bleeding into the floor of a building that's already been ruined once." He crouched, at a careful distance, and set the pistol across his knee like a farmer mending a harness. "I've ridden three days to say something to you, and I'd rather say it before anyone else starts shooting."

*Anyone else.* So there were others. She let her eyes move past him, and yes — shapes at the treeline, two, maybe three, patient as fence posts. Not soldiers. Soldiers would have come in. These were the other kind, the ones Corvane kept the way a man keeps knives he doesn't want his wife to find.

"You have something the minister wants buried," Ilves went on. His voice was even, almost gentle, the voice of a man reading terms aloud. "He sent me to bury it. That was the instruction. But I've known you nine years, Galloway. I signed your first travel chit. I've read your face across forty checkpoints when you gave me nothing, absolutely nothing, not even boredom — and I'll tell you what I told myself on the road: a woman who can be nothing for nine years is worth more than a regiment."

The rain filled the pause. Somewhere above, a beam settled with a groan, shedding ash.

"So here is what I am authorized to offer." He drew a folded paper from inside his coat but did not hand it over; he held it up, letting her read the seal upside down — the ministry's, the old one, the one that had signed away her family along with a thousand others in the purges she still did not talk about. "Full amnesty. Your name cleared of whatever they wrote down about your family — all of it, the record expunged. Passage anywhere. Money enough to stop running. And this—" he tapped the paper, "-is countersigned by three men who will still matter after the armistice, whoever wins. That last part matters. Read it again if you need to. Whoever wins."

Felix breathed. It hurt in a way that clarified things.

"And the price," she said. Her voice came out rusted, barely hers.

"The documents. The cipher, the miniature, anything sewn or hidden or memorized. Everything you carried out of the capital besides my dispatches." He paused, and then, because he was honest in the way cruel men are sometimes honest, he added: "And the child."

The word hung in the wet air between them.

"Not killed," Ilves said quickly, and she understood he needed to believe this himself. "Given over. There are families — good ones, loyal ones. She'd be raised as their daughter. She'd never know. In ten years she'd be a Karstine officer's wife or a Vessany magistrate's girl and none of this would ever have touched her." He leaned forward slightly. "You'd forget the face, Galloway. That's the whole order, the original order, the one that started all this. Forget the face. One sentence. Three words. You've carried worse sentences than that in your head for nine years. You've carried orders that got men shot and never learned their names. This is less than anything you've ever done."

And here was the terrible thing, the thing Felix would examine for the rest of her life in the small hours: for eleven full seconds, she wanted it.

She wanted it the way a drowning woman wants air — not elegantly, not with philosophical struggle, but with the whole dumb animal force of her body. She wanted the road with no name on it. She wanted the satchel light. She wanted to walk out of this burning-smelling ruin under amnesty, alone, unattached, unasked, invisible — invisible! — and let someone else's daughter grow up safe and ignorant somewhere warm while the wound in her side healed into one more scar among scars. Nine years of discipline rose up in her like a tide and offered, for the first time since her family was taken, an exit. Not survival. *Rest.*

Three words. *Forget the face.* She had spent her whole adult life forgetting faces — her mother's, going soft at the edges; her father's, gone entirely except the hands; the faces of the men whose death warrants she'd couriered, sealed before she could read them. Forgetting was her craft. She was the best in the republic at it. They were offering her a pension for doing the one thing she knew how to do.

The child cried on, thinner now, the fury guttering toward exhaustion.

Eleven seconds. Then Felix looked at the dark shape that was Marta — at the arm flung out, at the blood threading away from it through the floorboards toward the door, mixing with rainwater, traveling, as if the granary itself were trying to carry testimony out of the building — and the tide went out.

"No," Felix said.

Ilves closed his eyes briefly, as a man does when a horse refuses a jump he knows is too wide anyway.

"Galloway—"

"I carried your dispatches for nine years," she said. Each word cost her blood; she could feel it leaving with her voice. "I carried orders I didn't read and verdicts I couldn't stop and I told myself that was clean. Invisible hands stay clean. That was the whole religion. Do you know what I learned on this road?" She tried to sit up and failed, and hated the failing, and tried again, and got one shoulder off the ground, enough. "That the package is never a package. Every sealed thing is a person eventually. I just never opened one until now."

"The minister will—"

"The minister sold garrisons." The words came out steadier than she felt, and she let them, because a weapon should sound steady. "Karstine troop positions, winter supply routes, the rotation schedules for the river forts. Signed. Sealed. In his hand. And I have it sewn against a baby's back, and a copy of it in my head, and another copy already moving east with a woman who owed me nothing and paid everything." She saw the flicker cross his face — not doubt exactly, but the terrible arithmetic of a soldier realizing the numbers have changed. "You can shoot me, Captain. Shoot the child too, if your orders stretch that far and your stomach stretches further. But the paper exists outside this room now. Killing us is just tidying. It won't bury anything."

The rain came harder. Through the broken roof, through the hole where fire had won its argument with the rafters, the sky showed a thin bruised gray — nearly dawn, or nearly dusk; she had lost track days ago and no longer cared which.

Ilves stood slowly. He put his hat back on, and that small domestic gesture frightened her more than the pistol had, because it meant the conversation was over in his mind, one way or the other.

"I offered you the record wiped," he said. "Your family. Your name. A life."

"My family's name isn't mine to wipe," Felix said. "It's mine to *carry*. That's what I finally understand about it. Everything I ever carried — every sealed thing — I was carrying them. All of them. Every name on every page I never read." Her breath caught; she rode through it. "You want me to trade the child for my own erasure. Clean slate. No attachments. Captain — that's not a reward. That's the sentence. I've been serving it for nine years. I'm done."

For a long moment neither of them moved, and the child cried, and the rain fell, and somewhere beyond the treeline the other men waited with the patience of the paid.

Then Ilves did something she did not expect. He crouched again, close enough now that she could see the rain still beaded on his lashes, and said, very quietly:

"The garrisons. Which ones?"

And Felix, bleeding into the floor of a ruined granary with a dying woman behind her and a fatherless child beside her, understood that the road ahead had just forked again — and that the man kneeling in front of her had brothers stationed on the river, and had done the same arithmetic she had done at the ford.

* * *

Later she would remember the next minutes in fragments, the way a courier remembers a route — turns and distances, no scenery. Ilves standing. Ilves turning his back on her, deliberately, the way a man shows you his neck. Ilves saying to the treeline, in a voice pitched to carry: "Nothing here. Dead courier, dead nurse, no papers — the fire got them. We're riding on to the ford crossing before the water rises." And the shapes at the trees withdrawing, unhurried, obedient.

And Ilves coming back to her one last time, crouching, pressing something into her bloody hand — not mercy, he made clear with his flat stare, not forgiveness, just logistics: a field dressing, a flask, a slip of paper with a name on it. A surgeon, two villages east, who owed him.

"Forget my face," he said, standing, pulling his collar up against the rain. "That's an order, Galloway. The last one you'll take from me."

He walked out into the gray light, and Felix lay listening to the hoofbeats fade, and turned the slip of paper over in her fingers, and thought: *no.*

Not his face either. Nothing forgotten anymore. Nothing at all.

Behind her, faint but unmistakable — a sound she would carry the rest of her life — Marta groaned.
