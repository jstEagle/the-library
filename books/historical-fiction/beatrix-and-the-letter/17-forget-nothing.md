## 17. Forget Nothing

The granary roof had burned through in three places, and through those wounds the night sky fell into the room like spilled water—cold, moving, indifferent. Smoke still hung in the rafters in long grey banners that stirred whenever the wind found a gap. Somewhere beneath the ash and char, grain had fused into black glass; when Felix shifted her weight against the far wall, her boot crunched on it, and the sound was very loud.

Marta lay where she'd fallen. Not dead—Felix checked twice, pressed two fingers to the woman's throat with a care she usually reserved for documents—and breathing, but shallowly, each breath whistling through the wound below her collarbone as though it were being drawn through a reed flute. The baby, wrapped against Felix's chest inside her coat, slept. Infants could sleep through anything, Felix thought. Perhaps that was mercy. Perhaps it was simply that they did not yet know what things were worth.

Captain Ilves stood six paces away with his pistol leveled at her heart, and he did not lower it, and he did not fire.

He looked worse than she felt, which was remarkable, because Felix could feel her own pulse ticking in the gash along her forearm where a burning rafter spar had caught her as they'd run for cover—a bright, insistent line of pain like a signature written across the skin. Ilves's uniform, once immaculate enough to make checkpoints stand straighter, was grey with road dust and stained dark at one shoulder where the granary's collapsing door frame had struck him. His face, which she remembered from nine years of glimpses at ministry gates—young then, ambitious, Corvane's favorite hound—had gone the color of tallow candle wax.

"You were told," he said. His voice came out cracked. He cleared his throat. "You were told to forget the face."

"I was told many things." Felix kept her voice level. Nine years of practice: the courier's voice, dull as ledger paper. But something underneath it had come loose tonight, some bolt she'd spent a lifetime tightening, and what rose up through the crack was not fear. She examined the feeling with a kind of distant curiosity. It was anger. It had been waiting so long it had fossilized into something harder than rage—into certainty. "Most of them were lies."

"Put the child down."

"No."

"Felix Galloway." He said her name deliberately, tasting it, and she understood: this was meant to wound. Her name, spoken aloud by an officer of the Republic, stripped from her like a seal broken off a letter. For nine years no one had spoken it. For nine years she had been *the courier*, *the rider*, *the woman with the satchel*—a function, not a person, because functions could be trusted and people could be loved, and love was how the Ministry had taken her family. Hearing it now should have terrified her.

It didn't. That was the strange thing. It sounded, instead, like the first line of testimony.

"You know my name," she said. "Good. Then you'll want to remember it later, when they ask who let me through."

"They won't have to ask." His pistol hand steadied. "Because there won't be a later. Minister Corvane's instructions are precise. The documents are to be recovered. The courier is to be—" He stopped. Swallowed. The wind moved the smoke above him, and his shadow slid across the burned floor like something alive and ashamed. "Discreetly handled."

"Handled." Felix looked at the word as if examining a coin for clipping. "That's what we call it now. I carried dispatches for that man for nine years, Captain. I know every euphemism in the ministerial vocabulary. *Redeployed.* *Retired from service.* *Handled.* My parents were handled too. Did you know that? Eleven years ago. A knock at midnight, a wagon, a file that closed itself. I was sixteen." She heard her own voice do something it had never done in front of another living soul: shake. "I learned invisibility the way other children learn prayers. And now you're offering to handle me the same way. So before you do—before you spend that bullet—I'm going to tell you what's sewn into this child's swaddling, and you're going to listen, because you are the last man alive who can afford not to."

Ilves's jaw tightened. "Stalling."

"I don't need to stall. You haven't fired because firing makes noise and noise brings the deserters back, and because somewhere under all that polish you are a soldier who follows orders and has just realized the orders might be treason. I only need to talk." She drew breath. The smoke tasted of rye and ruin. "You searched me at Ashford Gate. You passed me through with a courtesy I've thought about ever since. You had brothers in the Third Garrison at Vellmark. I remember because your file crossed my hands once—couriers see everything, Captain, that's the whole disease of us—and because I remember faces even when I'm told to forget them. Two brothers. Older than you. Both commissioned. Both stationed, as of the spring muster, at Vellmark."

Something moved behind his eyes. Not fear yet. Recognition—the terrible early tremor of it, the way ground feels a half-second before it decides to open.

"The cipher," Felix continued, "lists the garrisons whose positions were sold to the Karstine envoy. Not rumored. Listed. With dates, with strengths, with the price per regiment. Corvane signed it himself—I've seen his hand ten thousand times, I could read his flourishes blindfolded, and it is his hand. Four garrisons. Vellmark is the second name on the page." She paused, and made herself say the rest plainly, without ornament, because ornament would have been a cruelty. "Your brothers' garrison is being sold, Captain. Their position. Their supply schedules. The dates their relief columns move. Every man under their command is already dead on paper—they just haven't received the news yet, because the news is traveling inside my satchel, and you've spent five days trying to catch it."

The pistol did not waver, but the man behind it did. It was a small thing, almost nothing—a settling of the shoulders, the way a house settles in cold weather—but Felix had spent nine years reading bodies the way other couriers read seals, and she knew what she was seeing. She pressed on, gently now, the way one presses on a wound to learn its depth.

"You've been telling yourself this was discipline. Retrieval of sensitive materials. Protection of the state in its final hours. I know the litany; I recited versions of it myself for years. But look at what you actually did. You left the capital quietly. No warrant. No escort. You burned a granary full of refugee stores to flush us out—refugees, Captain, women and children eating from the same stores you burned—and for what? For a bundle of paper and a baby who can't walk?" She shook her head slowly. "That's not discipline. Discipline doesn't smell like this room. That's a man doing dirty work because he believes the man who gave the orders is still worth obeying. So I'm asking you to look at the orders. Really look. Because I have, and here is what they say: *forget the face.*"

"*Forget the face,*" she repeated, and the words came out of her changed, transfigured by everything the road had taught her since that note appeared at her safehouse door. "Do you know what I found tucked in this child's wrappings at a waystation outside Drenholt? A miniature portrait. A little girl with dark hair and her mother's chin. Corvane's daughter—the one who died of the marsh fever eight years ago, the death the whole ministry pretended not to notice while he buried himself in work and began, that same season, meeting the Karstine envoy. This child is that girl's ghost given flesh. Some mother bore her, some father abandoned her at my door, and Corvane—the man who sold four garrisons, the man who will hand the Republic's eastern wall to its enemies for a seat at whatever table comes after—Corvane looked at this baby and couldn't bear it. Couldn't bear her face. So he wrote *forget the face* instead of *kill the child*, because even his mercy was cowardice, and he sent his best hound after her anyway, because even his cowardice was thorough."

She reached down, slowly—telegraphing every movement, palms wide—and lifted the swaddling-wrapped cipher free of her coat. The baby stirred but did not wake. The paper was scorched at one corner where the granary fire had kissed it; she'd pulled it from the flames with her bare hand, and the burn across her knuckles matched the burn on her forearm like punctuation.

"I could show you," she said. "I could unfold it right now and put your brothers' names under your eyes. But I don't think I need to. I think you already know. I think you've known since the ford, since the marsh, since you stood over Marta bleeding on this floor and watched me wrap a stranger's baby against my chest instead of running. I think that's why your hand shakes."

"It doesn't shake." But it did. A fine tremor, barely visible in the gunlight—there was no gunlight, only moonlight through the burned roof, and it showed everything.

"Marta took a bullet meant for me," Felix said. Her voice roughened. "She's a wet nurse I hired for passage money. She has a brother hiding among deserters whose name she wouldn't give me even to save her life, because loyalty was the one thing she owned. She carried this child through black water for a night and a day, above the leeches, above the fever, calling her a name nobody gave her because somebody had to. And she is lying on the floor of a burning granary in the middle of a dying Republic because a minister wrote *forget the face* on a scrap of paper and you believed the scrap of paper mattered more than she did." She stepped forward—one step, into the space between the pistol's muzzle and her heart. "So. Here is my offer, Captain. The only one I have."

Ilves's finger whitened on the trigger guard. "You're in no position to offer anything."

"I am always in a position to offer the truth. It weighs nothing. It fits in any satchel." Another step. Close enough now that she could see the sweat at his temple, the pulse jumping in his throat. "The border assembly sits at Harrowgate in three days. Rump parliament. Whatever's left of the lawful Republic, arguing about whether it deserves the name. Corvane arrives to negotiate amnesty—he'll arrive wearing his medals and his grief for his daughter like armor, and half the delegates will kneel to him because kneeling is easier than reading. Unless someone stands up and reads. Unless someone puts the cipher on the table and says the names out loud. Your brothers' names, Captain. Among them. Spoken in a hall where the record cannot be unwritten." She held his gaze. "Come with me. Or stand aside. Or pull that trigger and become the last officer of a Republic that died of exactly this—the willingness of good soldiers to carry bad orders in sealed envelopes and never once ask what was inside."

For a long moment there was no sound in the granary but the wind working through the burned roof, the small wet whistle of Marta's breathing, and the enormous silence of the sleeping child.

Then Ilves laughed. It was a terrible sound—short, involuntary, closer to a sob that had lost its way—and he dragged his free hand down his face, leaving streaks in the dust.

"Do you know what my brothers wrote me?" he said. "Last letters. Before Vellmark went quiet. Tomas wrote that the relief schedules had changed twice in a month and no one at the Ministry would say why. Anton wrote that he dreamed of white flags." His voice dropped to nearly nothing. "I told myself they were safe. I told myself the minister knew what he was doing. I have spent this entire ride telling myself stories, courier, and you—" He looked at her then, really looked, the way men look at a mirror they've been avoiding. "You're the worst story of all. You're the one where the servant knows."

"The servant always knows," Felix said softly. "That's the secret of the whole Republic. We carried everything. We simply never spoke."

"And now?"

"Now I speak."

He lowered the pistol. Not all at once—it came down by degrees, like a flag being furled, like something being surrendered rather than set aside—and when it hung at his side his whole arm seemed to hang differently, emptied. He turned the weapon in his fingers, studied it as though seeing it for the first time, and then, with a motion that seemed to cost him more than the march from the capital ever had, he slid it into his holster and buckled the strap over it.

"The swaddling," he said hoarsely. "Show me. I have to see it. If I'm going to do what I think I'm going to do, I have to see their names in his handwriting."

Felix hesitated only a heartbeat. Then she knelt beside Marta, eased the cipher from the folds of cloth where it had ridden through fire and flood, and unfolded it on the burned floor between them, weighting the corners with two stones of fused grain-glass. The moonlight through the broken roof fell across the page like a lamp held by a patient hand.

Ilves crouched. He read the way soldiers read maps—fast, hungry, hunting for themselves in the terrain—and Felix watched him find it. Watched his lips form the word *Vellmark*. Watched his finger stop beneath the second entry, beneath the numbers, beneath the date that was now eleven days past.

"He dated it," Ilves whispered. "He dated the sale. Like an invoice." His hand closed slowly into a fist atop the page—not crushing it, Felix noticed; shielding it, the reflex of a man protecting a map that meant home. When he raised his head, his eyes were wet and furious and, beneath the fury, terribly awake. "My brothers may already be—"

"Or they may have marched out ahead of the sale," Felix said. "The relief column moved early; I carried the order myself in the spring and never asked why. There's a chance. Chances are the only cargo I've ever hauled that appreciates in value." She folded the cipher with great care and returned it to the swaddling, to the warmth against the sleeping child, where it had lived so long it had learned her shape. "It goes to Harrowgate. All of it. The page, the portrait, the testimony. Mine included."

"Yours?"

"My name. Out loud. In front of the assembly." She almost smiled; it felt like a muscle she'd forgotten how to use. "Nine years invisible. It turns out invisibility was never protection, Captain. It was just a slower way of disappearing. Corvane taught me that. Everything he built taught me that. So I'm done being carried. From Harrowgate forward, I carry."

Ilves stood. He looked down at Marta, and something in his posture bent—he unslung his field kit without being asked, knelt, and began, with a surgeon's economy, to pack the wound properly this time, clean bandage and pressure and a splint of kindling wood, his hands steady again now that they had honest work. "She'll live if fever stays away. There's a farmhouse two miles east, abandoned since the exodus; I'll carry her there. You take the child and the road." He glanced up, and for a moment the young ambitious officer of the ministry gates looked out of his ruined face one last time, and then was gone. "There will be a patrol report by morning saying the package was destroyed in the granary fire. Unrecoverable. *Handled.* Let the minister believe it until he's standing in front of everyone he betrayed."

"And you?"

"I have brothers," he said simply, "whose names need speaking. I'll be at Harrowgate. If they let me in." A pause, and then, quieter, almost wondering: "If they let any of us in."

Felix rose. The baby breathed against her chest, small and warm and impossibly persistent, and outside the broken roof the sky had begun, very faintly, in the east, to consider the dawn. She gathered the satchel. She touched Marta's forehead—feverish but fighting—and Marta's eyes opened a sliver, found hers, and closed again in something like trust, which was more precious than any seal she had ever carried.

At the granary door, Ilves stopped her with one word.

"Courier."

She turned.

"What's the child called?"

"She isn't," Felix said. "Not yet. We'll fix that at the border."

She stepped out into the grey beginning of morning, onto a road that ran east toward Harrowgate and whatever waited there—toward a hall full of strangers who did not know her name, toward a minister arriving to buy his innocence, toward the moment when she would open her mouth for the first time in nine years and let everything fall out of it. The satchel rode light against her hip. The truth rode lighter still, sewn against a sleeping heart.

Behind her, faintly, she heard Ilves lift Marta from the floor with a gentleness that would have surprised them both.

Ahead, the road ran straight into the sunrise, and Felix Galloway walked it unhidden, unhurried, and—for the first time in nine years—entirely visible.
