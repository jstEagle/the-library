## 18. The Border Assembly

The road ended the way all roads end in wartime: not with a gate but with a crowd.

Felix had imagined the border assembly as something architectural—a hall, a wall, at least a door with a guard who would ask her business and give her leave to state it. Instead she found a fishing village called Sennet that had woken one morning to discover it was now the capital of what remained of the Republic of Vessany. Nets hung drying between requisitioned houses. A customs shed had been painted over in the tricolor, the paint still tacky enough that flies died in it. And along the quay, under canvas stretched between masts, sat the rump parliament: forty-one deputies of a body that had once numbered three hundred, arguing in the salt wind about quorum.

Marta slept against her shoulder in the cart, her bandaged arm propped on a coil of rope like cargo they were smuggling together. The wound had gone two days without turning bad, which Felix chose to read as an omen rather than luck. The girl—Beatrix, though no one had said the name aloud yet, not even Marta, who had begun saying it only to herself, Felix could see it moving behind her lips when she thought no one watched—slept too, in the crook of Marta's good side, her fist curled beneath her chin as if holding some small private dispatch she intended never to surrender.

The cipher was still sewn where it had always been, inside the swaddling's second layer, flat against linen that smelled now of marsh water and smoke that would not wash out. Nine days since Ashford Gate. Six since the granary. Two since Ilves had stood aside in the rain and let them walk past him, his face the face of a man reading his own name in a list he'd been told didn't exist.

Felix reined the cart to a halt where a bored militiaman waved down traffic, and understood, with a strange lurch, that this was the last checkpoint of her life—the first one she would pass through as someone whose business was true.

"Name?" the boy asked. He was perhaps nineteen, ink-stained fingers, a deputy's clerk pressed into gate duty because there was no one else.

"Felix Galloway," she said.

Her own voice sounded foreign in her ears. She had given false names at eleven checkpoints across nine years; she had given *no* name at thirty more, simply held out the blank courier's seal and watched men's eyes slide off her the way water slides off oilcloth. But the seal was gone—she had buried it in the marsh, wrapped in oilskin, because a blank thing in the wrong hands was worth more than any truth—and so there was nothing left to hide behind except herself, which turned out to be a very small shelter and a very large risk, and she found she did not mind it.

The clerk wrote the name down. Wrote it *down*, in a ledger, in ink, where anyone could find it. She watched him do it and felt something crack open in her chest that was either terror or relief, and could not for the life of her tell which.

"Business before the assembly?"

She looked at Marta, asleep, at the small rising fall of Beatrix's breath.

"Witness," Felix said. "I carry testimony."

* * *

They gave her a room above a smokehouse, because the assembly had no protocol yet for witnesses and simply housed people where there was space. The room smelled of herring and tallow. Marta slept through the afternoon with the fever-sweat of exhaustion drying on her brow, and Beatrix lay beside her, and Felix sat in the one chair by the window and did the thing she had trained herself out of for nine years.

She waited. In daylight. Where people could see her.

It was unbearable. It was also, she discovered, oddly like putting down a weight carried so long the shoulders had grown around it.

Word moved through the village faster than any dispatch she'd ever run. By evening everyone knew: Corvane was coming. Not summoned—*coming*, at the head of a delegation from the eastern garrisons, under a flag of parley, to negotiate terms. Amnesty for himself and his faction. The armistice was being drafted on tables fifty feet from where she sat, and the man who had signed away Vessany's border fortresses to the Karstine envoy intended to sign himself back into respectability at those same tables, and the forty-one deputies, hungry for peace and afraid of what peace cost, were inclined to let him.

"They say he arrives tomorrow," Marta said from the bed. Her voice was rougher than it had been; the fever had taken something from it, or maybe just revealed what was always there. "You knew he would."

"I hoped he wouldn't."

"Hoped." Marta laughed softly, then winced and pressed her bandaged arm. "You don't hope, Galloway. I've watched you for two weeks. You calculate. It's why we're alive."

Felix went to the window. Below, in the blue dusk, lamplighters were working the quay, and beyond them the black water of the estuary held the last grey light like a dish holds the last of a meal. Somewhere out on that water, or on the road east of it, Corvane was traveling toward the same door she had passed through that morning. Two couriers, she thought. Both carrying documents. His said the war was over and he should be forgiven. Hers said the war had been sold.

"He'll bring lawyers," Marta said. "He'll bring officers with polished buttons and statements prepared by clever men. What will you bring?"

Felix touched the swaddling, folded now and washed as clean as it would ever get, lying in the basket like an ordinary bundle of infant linens.

"A child," she said. "And the truth sewn inside her blanket."

Marta was quiet a long moment. Then: "Will they believe you?"

"I've been asking myself that since the granary." Felix turned. "I know what I am to them. Nobody. A courier. Couriers are furniture, Marta—we are the chairs messages sit on. Corvane is a minister with a signature half the republic has seen on grain allotments and school charters. When my word touches his word, his word weighs more."

"And?"

"And I spent nine years learning that paper doesn't lie. People lie *about* paper. Paper itself only does what hands made it do." She heard her own voice go flat and certain, the old courier cadence, and let it. "The cipher is in Corvane's hand. The envoy's countersignature is in Karstine cipher, and I can prove the key matches dispatches I memorized and burned in the capital two years ago—I remember every character, Marta, that was the whole of my trade. They will try to make me a liar or a madwoman or a Karstine plant, and every one of those attempts will be made by men who never once checked their own seals."

Marta studied her from the pillow, and something in her expression shifted—the wariness of a woman who had traveled with a stranger softening into something more dangerous, which was trust.

"You've changed," she said. "At the ford you were still hiding. Even in the marsh, even after the granary—you hid inside your own skull. Now you sound like someone planning to be seen."

Felix thought of the boy soldier at the third checkpoint, and how she had looked at him and could not perform indifference anymore, and how the whole architecture of her life had quietly come down around her somewhere on that road without her noticing the exact moment of collapse.

"I was told to forget a face," she said. "I'm done taking instructions."

* * *

Corvane arrived at midmorning under a sky the color of pewter.

Felix watched from the smokehouse window, and the sight of him struck her harder than she had prepared for—not because he was imposing, but because he wasn't. She had built him in her mind out of his handwriting: the sharp downstrokes, the impatient crossings, the hand of a man who wrote fast because the world was slow. She had imagined height, cold eyes, a presence that filled rooms. Instead a carriage of modest black stopped on the quay and out stepped a tired man of fifty-odd in a good coat gone slightly shiny at the cuffs, grey at the temples, moving with the careful stiffness of someone whose back hurt. He paused on the cobbles and looked out at the estuary for a long moment, and his face—

His face was ordinary. Kind, even, in its weariness. It was the face of a grandfather at a christening. It was, she realized with a cold turn of her stomach, a face that could belong to the miniature's subject—a father's face, softened by age, the girl's bone structure waiting inside it like a letter inside an envelope.

*Forget the face.*

She had thought it an order from a traitor destroying evidence. Then, at the waystation, finding the portrait tucked in the swaddling, she had reframed it as cowardice—a father's desperate, guilty love, sending his daughter into anonymity to save her from his crimes. Standing at the window now, watching the man himself, Felix felt a third reading settle over her like damp: that the order had been written to himself as much as anyone. That Corvane had looked at his infant daughter and seen the one witness who could never testify, and had begged her—in the only language left to a man who had sold everything—to be forgotten, so that she might live free of him.

It did not forgive him. It did not even much soften him. But it explained the note's trembling, which she had noticed and filed away and never understood until this moment, watching him stand on the quay looking at the sea like a man looking at the far shore of a river he could not cross back over.

Behind him came the delegation: six officers, four clerks, two lawyers whose silk made the fishing village look poorer than poverty already had. And at the rear of them, half a step apart from the others, walking like a man attending his own court-martial—

Captain Ilves.

Their eyes met across forty feet of wet cobblestone, and Felix did not move, and neither did he. Rain began, fine and needling. Around them the delegation hurried under umbrellas toward the customs shed; the villagers pulled their children indoors; carts rattled for cover. The two of them stood in it alone, the way people do at the center of events they cannot yet feel the edges of.

Ilves's mouth worked. Whatever he wanted to say—warning, apology, threat—he swallowed it. Instead he gave her something no officer gives a nobody: a nod. Small. Deliberate. The nod of a man acknowledging a debt he hopes never to have named aloud.

Then he followed his minister into the shed, and the door closed, and Felix went downstairs with her heart going like a hammer on a nail, to tell the assembly that a witness had arrived.

* * *

They nearly did not let her in.

The problem was procedure, which is what dying institutions use for a pulse. The assembly had rules for petitions, rules for envoys, rules for the reading of dispatches—but a courier arriving unannounced with testimony sewn into baby linen fell between every rule like a coin between floorboards. A deputy named Ostrand, heavy-faced and kind-eyed, heard her out in the anteroom while rain drummed the canvas overhead, and then said gently, "Madam, do you understand what you're claiming? You're asking us to unmake the armistice before it's signed. On the word of one woman and a document no one has verified."

"On the word of a document in Minister Corvane's own hand," Felix said.

"That is precisely what makes it impossible to receive casually. If it's genuine, it destroys the peace. If it's forged, it destroys *you*. Either way, madam, the timing—" He spread his hands. "Tomorrow the terms are ratified. You arrived one day late."

"One day early," Felix said. "You haven't signed yet."

Ostrand looked at her a long moment, and she saw the calculation move behind the kindness: the arithmetic of a tired man deciding whether truth was survivable today.

"The chamber convenes at dawn for final readings," he said at last. "I cannot promise you the floor. I can promise you that I will put your name before the chair, and that if the chair refuses you, I will refuse the chair, and there are perhaps eight others who will do the same. Nine votes, madam. That is what I can give you overnight."

Nine votes out of forty-one. Felix bowed her head in acknowledgment, and as she turned to go, Ostrand added, quieter:

"Galloway. The name in the ledger this morning. I looked—it's an old capital family, arrested in the purges, year of the bread riots. Any relation?"

"My family," Felix said.

Something moved across his face—pity, recognition, the particular grief of men who have survived purges and count the survivors. "Then you understand what Corvane's faction did to people like you."

"I understand exactly," Felix said, "what silence does."

* * *

That night she could not sleep, and near midnight she heard the rain stop and went out onto the gallery above the quay.

The customs shed was still lit. Through the fogged windows she could see shadows moving: the delegation working late, drafting, arguing, the shapes of men bent over papers. One shadow stood apart from the rest, motionless by a window, and she knew it was Corvane because it stood the way he had stood on the quay—like a man at a graveside, present for a burial he had arranged.

She wondered if he knew she was here. Ilves knew; Ilves had seen her; and whatever else Captain Ilves was, he served the man whose signature had bought garrisons, including—she had seen the names in the cipher, and watched Ilves read them in his own brothers' blood—garrisons with sons of his own in them. Would he tell? Had mercy left fingerprints, as she'd once thought, or had it left something worse: an ally Corvane didn't know he had, standing three feet behind him in the lamplight?

The shadow at the window raised its head, as if feeling her gaze across the dark water, and for a moment minister and courier regarded each other through glass and night, each carrying their document, neither yet knowing which of them would reach the floor of the assembly first.

Then a clerk crossed the room and handed him a paper, and Corvane turned away from the window, and the moment closed like water over a dropped stone.

Felix went back inside. Marta was awake, feeding Beatrix by candle stub, and looked up when she came in.

"Tomorrow?" Marta asked.

"Tomorrow," Felix said. She sat down across from them and watched the firelight move on the small face, on the fist that gripped the edge of the swaddling the way she had gripped Felix's finger that first night at the safehouse, holding on to a stranger as if strangers were the only honest things left in Vessany.

"Marta. If they don't seat me—if Ostrand's nine votes fail—"

"Then we go north," Marta said simply. "There are other assemblies. There's always another road. I've learned that from you."

"There's no other road. This is the last open one, and it closes at dawn whether I speak or not." Felix reached out and took the edge of the swaddling, and Beatrix's grip shifted to her finger, automatic and absolute. "So listen to me. If I fail tomorrow, take her north anyway. Don't wait for me. Don't come back for me. The document goes with her, and the document is the republic's memory, and memories survive better than witnesses. Promise me."

"No," Marta said.

"Marta—"

"I said no." Her voice cracked on it, the fever-husked voice splitting like dry wood. "I watched you carry her through black water for a night and a day. I watched you stand in front of a loaded pistol and refuse to forget. You think I'll walk away from the one person in this country who kept her word to a baby?" She shifted Beatrix in her arms, wincing, jaw set. "We both speak tomorrow or neither of us does. That's my price for coming this far, Galloway, and I've paid worse ones."

Felix looked at her—at this woman who had been passage money and a cover story and had become, somewhere on the flooded marsh path, the only person alive whom Felix trusted without a seal to guarantee it—and found she had nothing left to argue with.

"Dawn, then," she said.

Outside, the tide was turning. She could hear it against the pilings, the long slow exhale of the estuary, drawing back to expose the mudflats before it came flooding in again. In the customs shed the lamps still burned. In her basket the cipher lay flat against its linen, patient as paper always was, waiting for morning to learn what it had cost.

Dawn came gray and windless. The deputies filed into the net-loft that served as their chamber, forty-one of them, and Ostrand caught Felix's eye across the aisle and gave her the smallest of nods—*nine votes, madam*—and then the doors at the far end opened, and the ushers announced in voices cracked with ceremony:

"The Honorable Aldous Corvane, Minister of State, envoy plenipotentiary of the Eastern Compact."

And behind him, unannounced, unwashed, with rain still drying in her hair and a sleeping child on her hip and a dead woman's brother-in-law's debt unpaid in her pocket, Felix Galloway stepped through the same door.

The chamber went silent. Every face turned—not to the minister, she saw, but to her, because no one had seated her, no one had registered her, no one knew what a courier with no seal and a baby was doing walking into the last parliament of the Republic of Vessany.

From the front of the room, Corvane looked up.

Their eyes met. And in his face Felix watched the recognition arrive—the note-writer seeing the note's recipient, the father seeing the swaddling, the powerful man discovering at last, in front of forty-one witnesses and the whole turning tide, exactly what his mercy had cost him—and his mouth opened as if to speak her name, though he had never known it.

She spoke first.

"Honorable deputies," Felix said, and her voice did not shake, "I have testimony."
