## 19. Testimony

The hall they had chosen for the assembly had been a granary once, before the war took its roof and the exile parliament took its bones. Felix stood at the edge of it in the gray light of morning, watching dust sift down through the broken rafters like snow that could not decide to fall. Benches had been dragged in from three ruined churches, none of them matching, so that the delegates sat on a patchwork of other people's prayers. Above them, where a pulpit should have been, a clerk's table bearing a single brass bell.

Her hands would not stay still. She kept them folded in front of her, beneath the fold of her coat, and felt them trembling there like birds that had been caged too long to remember what wings were for.

Nine years of silence, and now she was to speak.

They had told her the procedure twice—once in the anteroom at the border, once again last night in the narrow room they gave her above the guardhouse, where Beatrix slept against her breastbone and Marta's bandages soaked through in the next bed over. State your name. State your office. Present your evidence. The words were simple enough. They were the simplest words Felix had ever been asked to say, and they sat in her throat like swallowed glass.

Because to state her name was to end everything she had built. Nine years of being no one. Nine years of walking through checkpoints with a face that officers forgot before they finished waving her through, a voice pitched to boredom, a body that made itself small and dull and forgettable as old newsprint. She had been invisible the way water is invisible—present everywhere, noticed by no one, alive because nothing bothered to look at it. And tomorrow, or in an hour, or in however many minutes it took the clerk to ring that bell, she would stand up in front of forty delegates and two hundred spectators and become someone. A name. A file. A target.

Corvane would know her then. Whatever happened in this hall, he would carry her name out of it in his ruined hands, and his faction would carry it into whatever shadows they scattered to.

*Forget the face*, the note had said. She had spent nine months obeying those three words as though they were scripture, first out of fear, then out of habit, and finally—she understood this only now, standing in the dust-fall of the granary hall—out of cowardice dressed up as discipline. Forget the face. Forget the child's face, yes, but also her own. Never be seen. Never be known. Never let the world fix you in place long enough to hit you.

And here was the thing she had learned on the road, in the marsh, in the burned granary where Marta had gone down with a bullet meant for her chest: invisibility was not safety. It had never been safety. It was only the safety of the already-dead, the ones the war had finished with early. The living got seen. The living got named, and aimed at, and sometimes shot—and Marta was upstairs with her shoulder bound and her arm ruined and her eyes open when Felix left, saying, *Go say it. Say it loud enough for both of us.*

The bell rang.

It was a small sound, that bell, bright and absurd in the vast cold hall, and yet it went through Felix like a blade through wet paper. The delegates settled. Somewhere behind her, the doors groaned, and a cold wind came in carrying the smell of the river and the smoke of the army camps outside, and with the smoke a different smell, fainter: horse and polished leather and ministerial cologne, a smell she knew from nine years of sealed dispatches, and she did not need to turn around to know that Corvane had entered.

She heard him anyway. His boots on the granary floor had always had a particular rhythm, unhurried, proprietary, the walk of a man who believed every room he entered belonged to him until proven otherwise. He had come to negotiate. That was the word being whispered through the assembly all week—*amnesty*, *reconciliation*, *the peace we cannot afford to lose*. Half the delegates wanted to believe him. The other half wanted to be done, and Corvane smelled like being done.

The president of the assembly—a hollow-eyed woman named Serrano whose son was buried in one of the garrisons whose coordinates Felix had memorized and burned—called the session to order and read the day's list. Petitions. Supply reports. The Karstine correspondence, such as it existed, which was to say not at all; the enemy's archives were sealed tighter than anything Vessany had ever managed.

Then: "Matters presented by citizens."

Felix stood up.

Her legs nearly failed her. It was a strange, humiliating, ordinary failure—the knees simply declining to do their work, the way they might on any staircase, in any year, in any life—and she caught the edge of a bench and held there for one breath, two, while two hundred faces turned toward the thin woman in the scorched courier's coat who had arrived three days ago half-dead and mostly silent.

*State your name.* She opened her mouth and found that her voice, when it came, did not sound like hers. It sounded older. It sounded like something that had been waiting in her chest for nine years, patient as a coal under ash.

"My name is Felix Galloway."

The sound of it in that hall was extraordinary. Not because anyone recognized it—no one did, that was the whole point of her, that had always been the whole point—but because saying it aloud felt like setting down a weight she had carried so long she had mistaken it for her spine. She said it again, because the first time shook too badly to be heard properly.

"Felix Galloway. Courier, fourth class, Ministry of Dispatches, nine years of service. I carried sealed orders through every checkpoint between the capital and the coast. I never read them. I never asked. Those were the rules, and the rules kept me alive, and I want the assembly to understand that I am breaking every rule I have." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she let it crack. "I am breaking them now because the rules themselves were the treason. A republic that asks its servants to be blind is a republic already sold."

A murmur moved through the benches like wind through wheat. At the clerks' table, Serrano leaned forward. "Courier Galloway. You have evidence to present?"

"I have evidence sewn into an infant's swaddling," Felix said, "which is where the Minister of Dispatches hid it, because he believed no one would search there."

The murmur became a roar. She walked forward through it, one step at a time, satchel against her hip—the scarred satchel, the one that had crossed three checkpoints and a flooded marsh and one burning roof—and she laid upon the clerks' table the things she had carried: the cipher sheet, unfolded flat, its columns of coordinates marching down the page like graves in rows. The transcript Marta's careful fingers had copied during the nights in the waystations, converting the cipher to plain words so that even if the original burned, the truth would survive in a wet nurse's handwriting. And last, wrapped in its scrap of embroidered linen, the portrait miniature: a girl of perhaps six, dark-eyed, solemn, painted by someone who had loved her very much and lost her anyway.

"Minister Corvane sold the positions of our border garrisons to the Karstine envoy," Felix said, and now her voice did not shake at all, and she wondered distantly whose voice that was. "Fourteen garrisons. Fourteen. I can recite the coordinates, because I carried the orders that emptied them, though I didn't know what I was carrying until the soldiers were already gone. The document before you is signed in his hand. The same hand that wrote the note pinned to a baby left at my door, instructing me to *forget the face*. This face." She held up the miniature, high, turning it slowly so the light caught the painted girl's eyes. "This is the face he told me to forget. His daughter's daughter. Because a father could not bring himself to erase her entirely, and a traitor needed the evidence somewhere no one would ever dare to look."

* * *

Corvane rose to deny it.

She watched him do it, and it was like watching a magnificent building discover, floor by floor, that it had been standing on nothing. He was good—he had always been good; she had seen him charm ambassadors and bully generals with the same half-smile—but the hall had changed around him and he had not changed with it. He called the document a forgery. He called her a deserter, a fabulist, a woman of no record and no account, and that word, *no record*, was supposed to destroy her, and instead it landed in Felix's chest like the last key fitting the last lock.

"That's correct," she said, and her voice carried to the far benches without effort now. "I have no record. That was my function. For nine years I was the blank page this ministry wrote its crimes on, because a courier who doesn't exist can carry anything. You chose me precisely because no one would believe me." She turned to the assembly. "But the cipher does not need my belief. Break it yourselves. Your cryptographers will confirm the coordinate system within a day—it is the ministry's own fourth-class field code, and I will sit with them and demonstrate it, every column, every key. And when you are finished, ask the Minister why the Karstine envoy's personal seal appears on the reverse of the sheet. Ask him why fourteen garrison commanders received withdrawal orders in his hand on the same night. Ask him—" and here she looked at him directly, this man whose dispatches she had carried and burned and carried and burned, whose handwriting she knew better than her own mother's—"ask him about the face he told me to forget."

He came apart quietly. That was the terrible thing, Felix thought afterward; she had expected thunder, denial, some grand performance, and instead Corvane simply sat down. His hands, which had signed death warrants with elegant economy, lay flat on the bench before him as though he had set them down to rest. Around him his faction rose one by one—not in solidarity, but like passengers abandoning a listing ship—and the emptying of the benches near him was itself a kind of confession, louder than anything he had failed to say.

Someone shouted for his arrest. Someone else shouted that the assembly had no authority to arrest a negotiating minister. Serrano rang the bell and it made no difference at all, and in the chaos Felix stood perfectly still at the clerks' table with the miniature in her hand, watching dust fall through the broken roof, feeling nothing so much as the strange weightlessness of a woman who has set down everything she owns and discovered she can still stand.

Corvane looked at her then. Across the hall, across the noise, across the wreckage of thirty years of ambition. And what she saw in his face was not hatred. It was recognition—the look of a man seeing, for the first time and far too late, the person who had been standing in his doorway all along, holding everything.

His lips moved. No sound reached her. But she read the words easily enough; she had spent nine years learning to read ministers.

*You were supposed to forget.*

She shook her head, once, slowly, and mouthed back the only answer that had ever been true:

*I forget nothing.*

* * *

They took him out through the side door, past the guards, and the hall emptied slowly after that, delegates clustering and arguing in the aisles, and Felix stood among them unnoticed for the first hour and completely seen for every minute of it. People looked at her now. That was the cost, come due at last, and she found it lighter than she had feared—a name was a heavy thing to carry alone, but she was not carrying it alone anymore.

Serrano found her at the doors. The president's face was grave, and tired, and underneath the tiredness something that might have been gratitude or might have been dread. "The cryptographers will begin tonight," she said. "You'll sit with them?"

"Tomorrow," Felix said. "There's someone upstairs who has been waiting to hear whether it worked, and a child who needs feeding."

Serrano nodded, and then, as Felix turned away: "Courier Galloway. What you did today—you understand it changes nothing by itself. Corvane has friends in every camp. Some of them will walk free no matter what your papers prove. The republic you've handed us is going to be messier than the one you imagined."

"I don't imagine republics," Felix said. "I carry what's true and put it down where it belongs. What gets built with it was never my job." She paused at the threshold, the cold river wind on her face, and allowed herself the smallest smile she had worn in nine years. "Though I find I'm curious to see."

Upstairs, Marta was awake, propped against the wall, her bound arm cradling Beatrix with her good one, and the child was laughing—that sudden, unearned infant laughter, aimed at the dust motes in the light, at nothing, at everything—and Felix stood in the doorway with her empty satchel hanging from her shoulder and thought: *this is what I was carrying it all for. I just didn't know the address.*

She crossed the room and sat down on the edge of Marta's bed, and Marta looked at her with those clear gray eyes and asked the question Felix had been dreading and longing for in equal measure:

"Well then. Did they believe you?"

Felix reached out and took her daughter's small hand, and began to answer.
