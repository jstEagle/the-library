## 20. The Price Paid

The hospital at Vellin Cross had been a granary once, and before that a chapel, and the smell of all its former lives clung to the rafters—dust and incense and rotting grain, layered now with carbolic and the sweetish undertone of wounds that were losing their argument against the body. I came every morning for nineteen days. I learned the sound of Marta's breathing from the doorway, before I saw anything: not the wet rattle I had feared on the road, but something slower, more deliberate, as though she had negotiated terms with her own lungs and intended to hold them to it.

She was awake on the twentieth morning.

I knew it because the rhythm changed. She had heard me come in—Marta heard everything, even drugged to the edge of sleep, even bleeding out in the mud of a burned granary with Ilves's pistol still smoking—and she turned her head on the pillow with that economy of motion she applied to everything, as if pain were a currency she refused to spend carelessly.

"You look terrible," she said.

"You've been shot," I said.

"I've *been* shot. You *look* terrible. These are different things." Her eyes went past me, searching the room, and when they found the wicker basket beside my chair—the small mound of blanket inside it, rising and falling—they settled, and something in her face unclenched that had been clenched, I think, since the marsh. "She's all right?"

"She's perfect. She has a cold. She sneezes like a kitten and the assembly clerk assigned to my household thinks this is the most charming thing he has ever witnessed."

"The assembly clerk," Marta repeated, tasting the words. "Felix. What did you do?"

What had I done. I sat down slowly in the chair by her bed, which was the wrong height for the bed and the right height for grief, and I told her. Not all of it—I have never told anyone all of it—but enough. The chamber with its broken windows papered over against the autumn wind. The members of the rump parliament sitting on benches meant for grain sacks, forty-one men and six women who had been mayors and schoolmistresses and one very confused bishop, waiting to hear what the courier had carried through three checkpoints while the republic burned behind her.

I told her how I had unwrapped the swaddling in front of them, layer by layer, the way I had first unwrapped it at my safehouse door a lifetime ago. The cipher sheet, unfolded along creases gone soft as cloth from handling. The miniature portrait, its painted face upturned toward the rafters where swallows had nested in some previous century. And then my voice—my own voice, which I had spent nine years making into nothing, into a courier's flat instrument good only for *yes, minister* and *no questions*—saying aloud: *My name is Felix Galloway. I carry things. This is what I carried.*

"You named yourself," Marta said. It wasn't a question. She had been there; she'd heard it secondhand within hours, because hospitals are villages and villages are gossips. But she wanted to hear me say it again, perhaps, or she wanted to watch my face while I said it.

"I named myself."

"And Corvane?"

I looked at my hands. They were clean. That was the strange part—I had expected, after everything, to look down and find them marked somehow, inked or bloodied or branded, some outward sign of what they had held and delivered. They were just my hands, chapped at the knuckles, the same hands that had burned dispatches over a hundred stoves without ever reading them.

"He didn't collapse, whatever they're saying in the coffeehouses," I said. "That's theater. He stood very straight and denied everything for eleven minutes—he counted, someone told me, he actually counted the minutes aloud, like an accountant objecting to a ledger. Then they read the cipher's fourth column aloud, the one naming the garrisons sold to Karstine, and he stopped counting, and he sat down, and that was all. No collapse. Just a man sitting down."

"Eleven minutes is long."

"It's longer than I lasted," I said, "for nine years."

* * *

The trouble with truth, I learned that winter, is that it does not behave the way dispatches behave. A dispatch goes from hand to hand and arrives intact or it doesn't; there is no third state. Truth arrives everywhere at once, in fragments, and every fragment gets rewritten in transit.

By the time the first provisional gazettes appeared—printed on the backs of army requisition forms, because there was no other paper—the story of the Corvane pact had grown a beard of details that had never happened. In one version I was a spy for Karstine myself, exposed in a counter-operation too clever to be declassified. In another I was Corvane's illegitimate daughter, which at least explained the miniature to people who preferred tidy explanations to true ones. In a third, more popular version, the child was dead, and my testimony was a fabrication born of grief, and Minister Corvane was a patriot destroyed by an anonymous woman's spite.

That last one found him sympathizers. That was the part I had not prepared for.

"They're calling for clemency petitions," Captain Ilves told me—not my Ilves, not the man at the granary, but his cousin, another Ilves, who had surrendered to the assembly with two hundred of the Third Garrison and now wore the new republic's cockade with the expression of a man wearing borrowed clothes. He had come to the hospital to deliver Marta's back pay—she had been enrolled, retroactively and absurdly, as a *civilian auxiliary of the courier service*, a title invented entirely so that someone would owe her money. "Four hundred signatures in Vessany city alone. Merchants mostly. Men whose loans he forgave."

"And the trial?"

"Is a hearing. Is a commission. Is—" He shrugged, uncomfortable. "There isn't a code of procedure anymore, Courier Galloway. There are forty-one people on grain-sack benches inventing law faster than anyone can break it. Some days they invent well. Some days they invent whichever way the wind is shouting."

He left the pay packet on the table and saluted, awkwardly, and I understood that the salute was not for me but for the story of me, which was already larger and better armed than I would ever be.

Some conspirators walked free. I want to write that plainly, because everything else about that winter got written crooked. Deputy Harsch, whose signature was on the second page of the cipher, claimed his inclusion was a forgery—and produced, God help us, genuine documents proving he had forged documents before, in the old ministry's service, which the commissioners accepted as exculpatory. He walked out of the hearing on the ninth of Frostmonth to applause from a crowd that had decided, in the absence of bread, that outrage was best rationed to one villain at a time. The Karstine envoy himself crossed back over the border under a flag of parley, carrying, I have no doubt, a fresh satchel full of promises, because borders change their minds faster than rivers freeze and no one had thought to declare him anything.

Corvane, at least, did not walk. But neither did he hang, and I will admit here what I admitted to no commissioner: I did not want him to hang. I watched him across that chamber—straight-backed, gray, counting his minutes—and I saw not a monster but a father who had tried to sell a country to buy a ghost, and hanging him would have made the arithmetic feel finished when it wasn't, would have let everyone believe the rot had lived in one man's chest instead of spreading through the whole timber of the state. I wanted him to sit in a quiet room for the rest of his life knowing exactly what he had carried, and what it weighed.

They gave him a house arrest in a country vicarage instead, which is nearly the same thing if you have any imagination, and nothing at all if you don't.

* * *

Victory, I discovered, smells like lamp oil and wet wool. The rebirth of the republic was not the clean dawn the pamphlets promised; it was a long, messy convalescence, a patient demanding to leave its bed too early. The armistice lines held in some places and leaked in others. Provincial councils declared loyalty to assemblies that hadn't asked for it and constitutions that didn't yet exist. In Marrowfield they lynched a tax collector who turned out to be the wrong tax collector, and the assembly sent a delegation to apologize to his widow, and the delegation was stoned, and the assembly debated for six days whether to send soldiers or bakers, and finally sent both, which satisfied no one and fed several hundred people, which is more than most governments manage.

And through all of it, Beatrix grew fat.

That is the sentence I could never put in any testimony, so I will put it here. While commissions convened and gazettes lied and petitions gathered names like snow on a sill, the evidence in the case of the Republic versus Corvane learned to laugh. She laughed at the hospital cat. She laughed at the sound of Velcro—no, at the sound of buckles, I am old but not that careless—at the sound of buckles being fastened, at Marta's songs, at her own fists. She gained weight in double chins. Commissioners' clerks fought over who carried her through the corridors of the assembly building, and the fiercest republican in Vessany, a tanner's daughter named Odile Voss who had once been sentenced to the galleys for printing sedition, taught the child to say *no* before she said *mama*, and defended this as pedagogy.

We lodged above a cooper's shop near the assembly hall, in two rooms that smelled pleasantly of oak shavings. The cooper's wife, Widow Sennet, pretended not to know who we were, which in that city was the highest form of courtesy. Every evening the knock would come—a different knock each night, a system Marta devised out of old smuggler habit—and one of us would count the intervals before opening, and every evening it was only neighbors, or clerks, or the boy soldier from the last checkpoint, who had followed us north after deserting his post rather than report what he'd seen, and who now split firewood for Widow Sennet in exchange for soup and refused, stubbornly, to tell anyone his real name.

Marta healed the way rivers heal after floods—slowly, leaving a changed course. The ball had gone through her shoulder and shattered the blade beneath, and the surgeon, who was also the cooper, told her frankly that she would never again lift a child with that arm, nor carry a pack, nor throw anything worth throwing. She received this news the way she received all news, by nodding once and immediately beginning to negotiate with it. Within a month she had relearned to cradle Beatrix against her left side, to pin swaddling with her teeth, to pour one-handed. But there were mornings—I heard them through the floor, so I know—when the arm simply refused its instructions, and she sat on the edge of the bed flexing her fingers and swearing with a fluency that would have embarrassed the marsh leeches, and those mornings she drank her tea alone and came downstairs afterward with her face arranged.

"You should write it down," she told me one evening, in the twelfth week, watching me mend the satchel. The satchel had taken a bullet-graze at the granary and a scorch-mark when the roof beams fell, and I could not bring myself to replace it, so I mended it, badly, with waxed thread. "All of it. The road, the checkpoints, the granary. Everything the gazettes got wrong."

"No one would print it."

"Then no one would read it, which is quieter." She shifted Beatrix on her hip—the left hip, always the left now. "You testified for them. Testify for her, someday. She'll ask where her scar came from—" the thin pale line below the child's ear, from a branch in the marsh, "—and you'll want to remember it properly. Memory is a courier too. It loses things in transit unless you seal them."

I told her I would think about it. I had already started. There was a notebook in the satchel's inner pocket, next to the blank identity papers I had not yet surrendered—the papers that said *COURIER, CLASS UNLISTED* in the old ministry's typeface, that described a woman with no name and no history and no face worth remembering. I had carried them for nine years like a second skin. Giving them up, I suspected, would hurt more than the granary had.

But that is a chapter for later. That evening I only sewed my bad stitches, and Marta sang low off-key, and outside the window the city argued politics in four dialects, and somewhere across the river Corvane sat in his vicarage counting minutes that no longer mattered, and Beatrix grabbed my threaded needle-hand in her whole small fist, the way she had gripped my finger at the safehouse door, holding on as if she had known me before I knew her.

Widow Sennet knocked—three quick, one slow—and called up the stairs that there was a letter.

Not a dispatch. A letter, on good paper, sealed with wax I did not recognize: a thistle pressed into crimson. Marta and I looked at each other across the lamplight, and Beatrix, sensing weather, went suddenly silent.

"From the assembly?" Marta asked.

"No," I said, taking it. The hand on the address was careful, educated, faintly trembling. "It's private."

I broke the seal, and read the first line, and sat down without meaning to.

It began: *You will not remember me, and you were wise not to—*
