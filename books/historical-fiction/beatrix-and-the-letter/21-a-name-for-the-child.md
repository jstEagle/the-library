## 21. A Name for the Child

*Felix*

The spring came late to the capital that year, and when it came it came all at once, the way truth does—slow for months and then everywhere in a single morning. The chestnut trees along the Ring Canal broke bud overnight, and the ice on the fountains cracked and shifted in the night like something waking from a long sleep, and by the third week of April the city smelled of wet stone and cut grass and bread baking in ovens that had stood cold all winter.

Felix noticed these things because she was learning, slowly, to notice things again.

For nine years she had trained herself not to. A courier who notices is a courier who lingers; a courier who lingers is a courier who is remembered. She had walked through cities the way water walks through a pipe—present, essential, leaving no trace. Now there was no dispatch in her satchel, no seal on her thumb, no route stitched into her memory in case her memory were taken from her. There was only a morning, and a canal, and a child on her hip who had learned, in the past month, to say *ga*, which meant everything, and *ba*, which meant bread, and one word more that she saved like a coin: *Ma*, sometimes aimed at Marta, sometimes at Felix, indifferent to the distinction, as though love were a single door with two handles.

The christening was set for the last Sunday of April, in the little chapel of Saint Aldric-on-the-Water, because Saint Aldric's register had survived the burning of the ministry archives—the rebels had burned the records of conscription first, and the amnesty rolls after, and the chapel registry had survived only because the priest, old Father Ilgen, had carried it out under his coat while the smoke was still coming off the shelves. A book of names, rescued by hand. It seemed to Felix the only proper place for what they meant to do.

She had not been inside a church since her family's arrest. She had not planned to feel anything about it. She stood in the doorway that morning with Beatrix dressed in the white gown Marta had sewn from a repurposed bedsheet—Marta's needlework was soldier's work, honest and crooked—and felt her chest tighten around something that had no name yet either.

"You'll crease," Marta said behind her, meaning the gown, meaning Felix's composure, meaning both.

"I don't crease," Felix said. "I fold."

"And that," said Marta, taking her elbow, "is exactly why we're doing this."

* * *

*Marta*

Marta had been married in a church, eleven years ago, before the fever took Hennig before the war took everything else. She remembered very little of it—a green dress, her mother crying, the smell of lilies too strong in a small room. She had promised herself she would not cry at this christening, because this one was not a funeral in disguise, and then Father Ilgen began the old words—*name this child before God and before the people gathered here*—and she felt her throat close anyway.

Because the people gathered here. That was the part that undid her. There were so few.

There was Felix, straight-backed in her one good coat, holding the baby with the careful grip of a woman who had once carried state secrets and now carried something heavier and warmer. There was Father Ilgen, whose hands shook but whose voice did not. There was Sergeant Orenne, the boy from the last checkpoint, come on two days' leave in a uniform that fit him better than it had in November—he had grown into it, or it into him. He stood at the back the way he must have stood at his post, watchful and shy, and when Beatrix was lifted up he smiled with his whole face, unguarded, a boy again for one moment.

And there was Tam, Marta's brother, sitting apart by the pillar, clean-shaven for the first time in two years, his amnesty paper folded in his breast pocket where he touched it every few minutes like a man checking a wound has stopped bleeding.

Her brother had almost killed all of this. He knew it; she knew it; Felix knew it. And Felix had spoken for him at the hearing anyway—one sentence, delivered in that flat courier's voice that could carry across a courtyard: *"He didn't sell me. He just wanted to go home."* It was more mercy than Tam deserved and exactly as much as anyone gets, and Marta had decided, somewhere in the marsh with black water rising past her wrists and a baby screaming above her head, that she would never again waste time calculating what people deserve.

"Beatrix," Father Ilgen said, and wrote it slow and large in the register, the nib scratching like a small animal. "*She who brings happiness.* An old name. Not a common one."

"It suits her," Marta said, before she could stop herself, and the priest looked up over his spectacles and said, gently, "It should. Names usually arrive after the fact. Hers arrived first."

The baby, as if on cue, sneezed—an enormous, indignant sneeze for such a small person—and the whole chapel laughed, even Tam, even Felix, and Marta felt the thing in her throat break open into something that was not grief, and she let it.

*Beatrix.* They had argued about it for weeks, in the low way they argued about everything now—not the old negotiation of need against need, but the new, softer haggling of two women who intended to be in each other's lives whether it was convenient or not. Marta had wanted a saint's name, something with weight and history. Felix had wanted nothing that could be found in any index; nine years of invisibility die hard. It was Felix, in the end, who had found the name, turning the pages of an old almanac one night while the baby slept on her shoulder. *Beatrix*, from the Latin. *She who brings happiness.* She had read it aloud half-mocking, and then not mockingly at all, and they had both gone quiet, listening to the small breath in the next room that had cost them a river, a marsh, a bullet, a war.

Happiness brought. Not found, not earned, not bargained for. Brought—by a stranger, to a doorstep, wrapped in instructions to forget her.

"There," said Father Ilgen, blotting the page. "Now she exists twice."

"Three times," said Felix quietly, and put her hand flat on the register, over the ink. "Once in us."

Marta did not say anything. Some sentences you let stand alone.

* * *

*Felix*

Afterward they walked home along the canal, the long way, because the day was warm and nobody wanted the afternoon to end. Orenne carried Beatrix for a stretch, holding her out at arm's length like something explosive until Marta adjusted his grip with a sergeant's patience, and Tam walked three steps behind everyone, near enough to belong, far enough to choose it.

At the bridge Felix stopped.

"Marta. Take her a moment."

Marta read her face the way she had learned to read weather on the marsh road—with attention and without alarm. She took the child, and Tam drifted after them toward the far bank, pretending interest in the swans, and Felix was alone at the center of the bridge with her satchel.

It was a poor satchel. That was the whole point of it, and always had been. Scuffed leather, mended twice, dull enough that no eye ever snagged on it. Inside it, for nine years, there had been whatever the republic needed moved without being seen: sealed orders, blank passes, cipher keys, and—most valuable of all—the identity papers. Her own. Issued blank, filled in by no hand, valid in any name she cared to write. *A courier is no one*, her first handler had told her, sliding the folder across the table like a plate of something unpleasant. *That is your armor. Never give it up.*

She opened the folder now, in the middle of the bridge, in full daylight, in front of swans and strangers and the rebuilt city.

Blank pages. Nine years of blank pages. Every road she'd ever run, every checkpoint she'd talked her way through, every name she'd worn for an hour and discarded—it had all been possible because somewhere under her coat there was a document that said, in effect, *this woman does not exist, and therefore cannot be caught.*

She had held onto it through everything. Through the marsh, through the granary, through the weeks in the hospital corridor outside Marta's surgery with her hands still shaking. She had told herself it was caution. The assembly was new; the peace was young; conspirators walked free; a woman might need to vanish again someday.

But that wasn't why she kept it. She kept it for the same reason Corvane had signed his treachery in his own hand—because power leaves fingerprints, and hers was the power to disappear, and some cowardly part of her loved it still.

Down the bank, Marta was showing Beatrix the swans. The baby had gone rigid with delight, both fists in her mouth, making a sound like a kettle deciding to sing.

Felix took the papers out—all of them, the blanks, the passes, the courier's seal with its worn republican device—and dropped them over the rail.

They did not flutter. They fell heavily, the way paper falls when it has been handled too much, and hit the brown water, and floated for a moment, white shapes on dark, and began to drink the canal in and grow heavy and translucent, and slid under one by one.

She watched until the last one went. Then she closed the empty satchel and found that her eyes were wet and that she was, unmistakably, smiling.

"Funny," she said aloud, to the water, to no one. "That's supposed to feel like dying."

It felt like the opposite. It felt like being issued, at last, to a single place and time—to a bridge, in April, in a city being reborn messily and imperfectly, with a wet nurse waiting on the far bank and a child who would grow up asking questions.

She crossed the bridge. Marta glanced up, took in her face, and asked nothing—which was, Felix thought, its own kind of vow.

"Well?" Marta said.

"She's mine now," Felix said, patting the satchel. "Empty."

"About time," said Marta, and handed her the baby.

* * *

*Marta*

That evening the four of them ate in the little room above the chandler's shop that Felix had rented with her witness's stipend—Tam included, at the table now instead of the pillar, progress measured in chairs. Marta cooked, because cooking was the one language in which she had never once been at a loss. Onions, the last of the winter potatoes, a chicken bought that morning from a man who had charged her double and then thrown in the giblets for free because the baby smiled at him.

Beatrix sat propped between them in a laundry basket padded with a blanket, supervising the meal with the gravity of a magistrate. Twice she banged her spoon. Once she achieved *ba*, and was rewarded with mashed potato, which she regarded as a betrayal but ate anyway.

Tam told a story about the amnesty office—a clerk who had processed his papers while eating a sausage, wiping grease on official forms—and Marta watched Felix laugh and thought: *there she is.* The woman underneath the courier. It had taken Marta months to find the seam in her, that first week on the road, when Felix had performed boredom at the Ashford Gate so well that Marta had genuinely believed her calm. She knew better now. She had seen the same woman hold a fevered infant above black water all night and say, at dawn, in a voice scraped down to the boards, *we are not stopping*. Calm was not what Felix had. What Felix had was a decision, renewed hourly, that the fear would ride behind her and not in front.

But tonight the decision was easy, and the laughter came up from underneath it like groundwater, and Tam—her wretched, lucky, living brother—laughed too, and outside someone in the street was playing a fiddle badly, and nobody stopped them, because it was spring and the war was over and the worst had already happened to all of them.

Later, when Tam had gone and the dishes were done and the baby was asleep in the drawer of the dresser that served as her cradle, Marta sat by the window with her mending and listened to Felix move around the room, putting things away. The satchel went into the bottom of the wardrobe. Felix shut the wardrobe door on it with a click that sounded, to Marta's ear, like a period at the end of a very long sentence.

"She'll want to know," Marta said, around a pin. "When she's older. Where she came from. Why Marta limps. Why you flinch at wax seals." She tied off the thread. "Children ask. It's the one thing they're born knowing how to do."

Felix came and sat down across from her. In the candlelight her face was tired and open, both at once, which was new.

"I know," Felix said. "I've been thinking about that. About how to tell it. All of it—the door, the note, the marsh, the granary, the assembly. Not the cleaned version. She deserves the whole dispatch."

"And?"

"And I think I have to start at the beginning, and I've never told anyone the beginning." She looked toward the dresser drawer, where the small slow breathing came from. "Not even you. My family. How I became—" she gestured vaguely at herself, at the space where the blank papers used to live— "no one."

Marta threaded her needle and said nothing, because there are moments when silence is the only courteous thing a friend can offer.

"The story's long," Felix said.

"We're not going anywhere," said Marta. "For the first time in either of our lives."

Felix looked at her for a moment. Then she reached over and turned the candle a little higher, and pulled her chair closer, and began.
