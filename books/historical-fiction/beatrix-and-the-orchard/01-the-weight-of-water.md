## 1. The Weight of Water

The sea did not remember my father, and I had learned not to ask it to.

That was the first lesson the water taught, and it taught it thoroughly, in its own patient grammar of salt and pressure and cold. The sea forgets. It takes a man whole on a Tuesday—my father went under on a Tuesday, between the second pearl bed and the shallows, between one breath and the next—and by Wednesday morning it has smoothed the surface to silk again, blue as a ledger's blank page, as though no entry had ever been made. The boats go out. The divers go down. Nothing is written anywhere except in the bodies that keep diving.

I was twenty-two years old that season, and I had been diving since I was nine, which meant I had thirteen years of practice at holding my breath and eleven years of practice at holding my tongue. Both skills were necessary. Only one of them paid.

The harbor at Meridian Point woke before the sun did, the way all harbors do—by sound rather than light. First the groan of hulls against pilings, timber complaining in its sleep. Then the chains, the winches, the cough of the early steam launches ferrying clerks out to the company ships. Then voices, layered: the dockhands shouting in three languages none of them shared but all of them understood, the fishwives haggling, the gulls conducting their endless litigation overhead. And beneath it all, constant as tide, the great mechanical breathing of the Meridian Trading Company—the whir of its counting machines, audible through the open windows of its headquarters even from the water, a sound like insects eating something important.

I slipped into the water off the north jetty while the sky was still the color of pewter, because the best light for finding stray pearls is no light at all. Strays, we called them—pearls missed by the harvest divers, left behind in the discarded shells or dropped into the silt when an oyster was pried open carelessly on deck. They belonged to no one, technically. The Company claimed everything with a value, including the seabed itself, but a stray found beyond the marked beds was a gray country, and the gray countries were where people like me lived. I sold them to Chao Wen at the back of his chandlery, who weighed them without looking at my face and paid me in small silver that went directly into the iron box the Company kept on my account. Every coin shortened the ledger line that bore my name. Every coin was a millimeter of freedom, measured out in nacre.

My father had taught me to dive. That was the arithmetic no ledger recorded: I was paying down a debt incurred by his death, in pearls recovered by the skill he gave me, in the waters that killed him. The Company called it indenture. My mother, before she followed him into the quieter country, had called it what it was, but she'd had the sense to say it only once, very softly, and then never again.

I went down.

* * *

There is a moment, just after the water closes over your head, when the world above ceases to exist—not fades, not dims, but simply stops having ever been real. The sounds of the harbor shear away. The light becomes a ceiling of hammered tin. And you are in the other country, the one that keeps its own records in silt and shell and bone.

I equalized my ears, tipped forward, and let the weight of me pull me down along the jetty's shadowed piles. The water in October carried a new coolness, a suggestion, a rumor of the storm season coming; the silt below had been stirred by the week's traffic and hung in the green like smoke from a fire far away. I worked by feel mostly, hands reading the bottom the way a clerk reads a page. There—a ridge of broken shell, the leavings of the harvest boats, three seasons deep. I fanned through it slowly, methodically, the way my father had taught me: *the sea pays the patient, little one, and cheats everyone else.*

His voice still came to me underwater. I don't know why. Above, on land, in daylight, I could go whole weeks without hearing it. But forty feet down, in the press and hush, he was always there, saying things he had actually said, in the tone he had actually used, as though drowning had been only a change of address.

*Feel, don't grab. The oyster knows the difference.*

I felt. My fingers walked the shell ridge, sorting by touch: oyster, oyster, oyster, trash, oyster, and then—the small dense wrongness that made the heart knock—one perfect roundness cradled in a half-shell some careless deckhand had split and tossed. A stray, all right. Small, the color of dawn before the dawn arrives, worth perhaps two days of my indenture. I closed my fist around it and kept moving.

Above me, faint through forty feet of water, the ships were leaving.

I knew their schedules the way other women knew saints' days. The *Coromandel Queen* on Thursdays, bound for the capital with the season's pearl shipment sealed in the Company's grey tins. The mail steamer every ninth day, carrying letters I never received and news I had learned not to need. Once a month, sometimes twice, the big white vessels with the red funnel band—those were the ones the dockhands lowered their voices about, the ones that took on cargo at night under lanterns hooded to seaward. Those ships carried things out. I had watched them from the jetty since childhood: crates stenciled with numbers instead of names, bales wrapped in oilcloth, sometimes—this I saw only twice, and both times pretended afterward I hadn't—long wooden cases the exact size and shape of a man standing up with his arms crossed.

Everything left from here. Everything worth keeping, everything worth burying, everything true or valuable or dangerous—it all went aboard and steamed over the horizon to *elsewhere*, that great bright country where history happened. Because history did happen there; of this I was certain. History was the thing in the newspapers that arrived three weeks late and concerned strangers. History was the war being fought up the coast, of which we saw only the prices it caused and, lately, the occasional ship with scorched paint. History was treaties and ministers and borders redrawn by men whose names I couldn't have pronounced properly. It was made in capitals, signed at tables, printed in languages I'd never speak, and then, like everything else, it was shipped.

Nothing history-shaped had ever happened to me. What had happened to me—my father's lungs filling with the sea he'd worked for twenty years, the Company clerk's pen scratching *widow's claim: denied, pre-existing contract* —was not history. It was weather. It was local. It was the small print of the world, and small print, I had understood from childhood, is never read aloud.

I tell you this so you will understand what I believed on the morning everything began, which was this: that the great currents of the world passed our port the way the deep current passes the pearl beds—we felt its pull, we bent in it, but it never touched us. We were not important enough to be touched. That belief was load-bearing. Take it from me now, and the whole house of my life comes down. But on that October morning it stood firm, and I dove comfortably inside it, a woman gathering strays from the margin of the world, watching the margins get shipped elsewhere.

I found four more pearls before my body called the morning's work done. The last was the finest—a button pearl, slightly baroque, warm-looking even in the cold water, resting alone in the sand as if set there deliberately, waiting. For a foolish moment I held it up to the failing light and thought of my father's other lesson, the one he only said when he'd been drinking: *Every pearl is a grave the oyster closed over. You dive among graves, little one. Be respectful.*

Then I kicked for the surface, because graves are for remembering and I had work to do.

* * *

Chao Wen's chandlery smelled of rope, tar, and dried cuttlefish, a combination that after ten years seemed to me simply how honesty smelled. He weighed my five pearls at the back counter by lamplight even though it was barely mid-morning—he kept the lamps lit year-round, claiming sunlight showed favoritism—and wrote the figure in his own book before speaking.

"Forty-one days," he said.

"Forty-one days" meant my remaining indenture had shrunk by the equivalent of forty-one days of contracted labor. I did the larger arithmetic automatically, the way you cannot stop yourself doing: forty-one days against a balance of nearly three years. Three years, minus luck. Minus storms, when diving stopped. Minus the Company's fees, which bred like flies in the account book: storage fee, registration fee, the fee for the fee-taker.

"You're getting faster," Chao Wen said. Not praise—an observation. He poured tea without asking whether I wanted any, because he had decided years ago that I wanted tea, and he was right.

"The beds were thin this week," I said. "Harvest crews are sloppy when the season ends."

"Sloppy men make rich finders." He slid the silver across the counter, and I watched it vanish into the receipt he stamped, the transaction already becoming paper, becoming abstract, becoming a number in a Company ledger where my name sat in a column with several hundred others. Thornbury, M. Indentured, term seven years, renewed. Balance owing.

My father had not been able to write. He had signed his contracts with an X, pressed hard enough to tear the paper, and I used to think—when I was young enough to think such things—that the X looked like a man crossing out his own name. When he drowned, the clerk read me the terms from the renewal: his debt, unpayable now, transferred. Orphaned minors of deceased indented divers could elect assumption of contract at age of majority or enter the Company orphan house. I elected assumption. I was fourteen and I chose the sea over the orphan house the way you choose the wolf you know.

"They say the armistice talks may come here," Chao Wen said suddenly, weighing nothing on his scale, eyes on the middle distance. "To the port. Delegates and their secretaries. Hotels will fill."

"Will they pay in silver?"

"History pays in silver," he said, "when it pays at all." He said it lightly, the way he said most true things, and moved the conversation on to the price of rope.

Armistice. The word meant nothing to me yet. It was a word from elsewhere, from the newspapers, from the war whose prices I felt but whose dead I had never seen. I drank my tea and thought about forty-one days and did not know—I could not have known—that within a year that word would be the axis my whole life turned on, that I would learn exactly what it cost and who paid it and in what currency. On that morning it was only weather from another country. Everything was.

* * *

I walked home along the sea road in the long gold light of afternoon, past the Company warehouses with their doors open to air the goods, past the registry office where the clerks were closing shutters against the glare, past the drying racks where the nets hung like the shed skins of enormous animals. At the top of the hill, where the road bent, I stopped—as I stopped every day, a ritual I would have denied under oath—and looked at the water.

The *Coromandel Queen* was working out on the evening tide, tugs fussing at her flanks like pilot fish. I watched her gather way, her wake unrolling white across the harbor's bronze, and I thought about what she carried: the season's pearls, sorted and sealed, the accumulated slow grief of a thousand oysters, going to the capital to become someone's inheritance, someone's scandal, someone's dowry. Things pulled from graves, shipped elsewhere, worn at tables where history was made by people who had never been forty feet down.

My father had pulled pearls from those same beds for the Company for twenty years, and the sum total of what the water gave back for him was a line in a ledger and a debt attached to his daughter's name. If there was a history of this port, of the bay, of the divers, of the burned villages up the coast that the newspapers mentioned only as coordinates—no one had written it. And no one ever would, I thought, because writing history required mattering, and we did not matter. We were the small print.

I turned up my street. Our row of divers' houses leaned together like tired old men sharing a joke, salt-eaten and stubborn, and mine was the last one, closest to the sea wall, close enough that on storm nights the spray ticked against the shutters like fingernails. I climbed my steps. I put my hand on the door.

It was not latched.

I stood very still. In twelve years of living alone in that house I had developed the animal habit of latching the door against everything—the wind, the neighbors' curiosity, the Company's inspectors, the whole loose-toothed world—and it was not latched now. It stood a finger's width ajar, and from inside, faint and rhythmic, came a sound I did not recognize and then, in the next heartbeat, recognized completely, though I had never heard it in my own home before.

A baby, crying.

The sea forgets, I told you. But apparently it also returns things. I pushed the door open, and there on my floor, on my one good blanket, lay a bundle wrapped in coarse linen, red-faced and furious with life, and beside it a folded note, and on the note—half-cracked, half-melted, but unmistakable to anyone who had ever bought rope, salt, or silence in this port—

the wax seal of the Meridian Trading Company.
