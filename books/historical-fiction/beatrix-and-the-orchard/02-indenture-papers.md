## 2. Indenture Papers

The Meridian Trading Company kept its ledgers the way the sea kept its drowned: in order, in darkness, and without comment.

Marguerite Thornbury knew this because she had spent the morning of her nineteenth birthday standing on the worn marble of the counting house, waiting for a clerk to finish his breakfast so he could renew the paper that owned her. The room smelled of lamp oil, sealing wax, and the particular sweetness of old paper left too long in salt air—a smell she had once found oppressive and now barely noticed, the way one stops noticing a stone carried in the shoe.

The clerk's name was Mr. Prosser, and he ate his kaya toast with the deliberate, unhurried motions of a man who had never once been made to wait for anything. He was pale in the way company men were pale—not from any northern country but from twenty years indoors, tallying the wealth that other men bled out of the water. His fingers were stained faintly yellow where they gripped his pen, and when he finally set down his plate and drew her file toward him across the desk, he did not look at her face. He looked at her number.

"Thornbury, Marguerite. Diver class two." He said it the way a man reads weather. "Contract originally executed—" his finger moved down the page, "—eleven years ago, upon default of paternal debt. Outstanding balance at last renewal: four hundred twelve dollars and sixty cents."

Her father's debt. Her father's death had not retired it; the Company had simply folded his funeral costs into the sum, with interest, the way the sea folds a body into its own weight.

"Payments rendered since renewal," Prosser continued, running his pen down the column, "diving wages credited against board and against equipment depreciation—your suit, your weights, your knife, replacement cord for the glass mask you cracked in March—" each item landed like a small stone dropped into still water, "—leaves an adjusted outstanding balance of three hundred ninety-eight dollars and fifteen cents."

Eleven years of work. Fourteen dollars and forty-five cents. She did the arithmetic before she could stop herself, the way her father had taught her to hold her breath: steadily, without panic, because panic spent air.

"Sign here," Prosser said, rotating the ledger toward her and tapping a line with a fingernail gone soft and ridged from paper-handling. "Renewal term is three years, standard conditions, diving wage unchanged at current schedule."

"My father died owing eleven dollars," Marguerite said. It was not the first time she had said it. It would not be the last. But she said it quietly, as one states a measurement, not a grievance. "You charged me for the boat that brought his body back. You charged me for the coffin. You charged me for the diver who went down to look for him before anyone knew he was dead."

"The costs of recovery were properly debited to the account," Prosser said, and there it was—the flat, unbothered voice of arithmetic, which never has to raise itself because it has never been argued down. He turned the ledger back around. "Sign, please. I have eleven more renewals before lunch."

She signed. Her name came out small and neat on the line beneath her number, and watching it happen—watching herself become, again, on paper, the property of the Meridian Trading Company—she felt the familiar sensation of being divided in two: the girl holding the pen, and the name lying flat on the page, weightless, legible, worth exactly what the column beside it said she was worth.

Three hundred ninety-eight dollars and fifteen cents. At the current rate of credit, that was nine more years.

She was nineteen years old and she owed the sea another nine years of herself.

* * *

Afterward she did not go straight back to the quay. This was habit, though she told no one about it, and habits unspoken become rituals, and rituals become something very like prayer.

The ledger room occupied the eastern wing of the counting house, behind a door of grey iron-banded oak that was locked at all hours except when a clerk went through it, and then only briefly, like a man slipping into a confessional. Through that door, somewhere among the shelves, lay every fact the Company believed was true about this port—who owed what, who had shipped what, who had been paid, who had been deducted, who had been debited for the cost of their own drowning. Eleven years of standing in corridors outside that door had taught Marguerite its geography the way a diver learns a reef: by patient attention, by small returns, by noticing what grew where.

There were four windows facing the courtyard, high and barred, north-facing, so the light stayed even and the ink did not fade. There was a second entrance off the records corridor, narrower, used for bringing in new files, and it had a different lock—older, brass, the kind that could be opened with patience and a bent pin if one ever had reason, which one did not. The shelves ran in eight long ranks, and the oldest volumes lived at the far end, near the outer wall, where the damp came through in wet seasons and the clerks complained and someone had propped a wedge under the last case to stop it settling. The coastal registries—the shipping manifests, the harbor dues, the village tax rolls for the settlements up the coast—were shelved nearest the second entrance, because they were consulted most often, and therefore wore out fastest, and therefore needed replacing fastest, and therefore were the least carefully guarded, because things replaced easily are things watched lightly.

She had never intended to learn any of this. That was the strange part, looking back. She had learned it the way water learns a hull—by finding, over years, every seam, every rivet, every place where pressure might get in.

Today, passing the records corridor on her way out, she noticed two things, and the noticing itself was new.

First: the door to the ledger room stood open, and inside, two junior clerks were hauling volumes off the coastal registry shelves and stacking them on a handcart—not the usual careful carrying, one or two at a time, but an untidy armfuls process, fast, furtive in the way men are furtive when they have been told to hurry and given no reason.

Second: Mr. Prosser stood at the end of the rank, holding a single ledger open in both hands, and he was not reading it. He was checking it against a list in his pocket, ticking items off, and his face wore an expression she had never seen on a clerk: fear, held very carefully, the way one holds a full cup while walking.

She slowed her steps. Nobody stopped her; she was a diver, class two, invisible in the hierarchy of the port—beneath the clerks, beneath the warehouse foremen, beneath the tally-boys who rode crates down gangplanks, beneath everyone except the other divers and the beachcombers and the women who sold drinking water by the cup. Invisibility had its uses. A person noticed walking past a door; nobody noticed a diver passing anything.

From the corridor she saw the cart stacked with perhaps thirty volumes, coastal registries among them, rolled toward the second entrance—and stopped short, because blocking the doorway stood a man she did not know, tall, narrow-shouldered, dressed in the same grey as the clerks but wearing it differently, closer-fitted, cleaner, with the air of someone whose authority came not from seniority but from function. He had spectacles and a long, serious face, and he was speaking to Prosser in a low voice that carried anyway, the way low voices carry when they are angry.

"—not to be removed, Mr. Prosser. Not culled, not consolidated, not *reorganized*. The registry stays whole until I certify it. If a single volume leaves this room without my signature on a transfer chit, I will note it in my audit report, and we will discover together whether Mr. Calloway's patience extends to sloppy inventory during negotiation season."

Prosser's answer was too quiet to hear. The tall man's was not.

"I don't care what the directive says. Directives come from gentlemen who have never smelled a ledger room in their lives. The record is the record. That is the entire point of me." He stepped aside, letting the cart pass—but only after pulling one slim volume from the top of the stack, holding it up, reading the spine. "Coastal registry, Kessel Bay section, years nine through eleven? Where is this going?"

"Consolidation," Prosser said, and his voice had the smooth, practiced texture of a man reciting something handed to him. "Duplicate copies. Standard review."

"There are no duplicate copies of original registries," the tall man said. "That is what *original* means." He set the volume back on the cart anyway—or rather, he hesitated over it, a hesitation so brief that only someone watching for it would see it, and then he placed it down and waved the cart through, and Marguerite understood that she had just watched a man lose an argument and pretend he hadn't.

Then his gaze lifted, and caught hers.

She had been seen. Standing in a public corridor, in plain view, doing nothing—she had been caught doing nothing, and it felt like being caught fleeing. The instinct to walk on arrived a half-second too late, and the delay itself was damning. He looked at her the way he had looked at the ledger spine: identifying, cataloguing. Then he inclined his head, precise and courteous.

"Good morning."

"Good morning, sir," she said, and walked on with the unhurried tread of a woman with nowhere particular to be and no interest in anything at all, and her heart was going like a diver's heart at depth, steady and hard and spending air.

* * *

The port explained itself to anyone who watched it long enough, and Marguerite had watched it all her life, from the water mostly, which gave her a vantage nobody else bothered with.

At the top sat the Company, which sat above everything the way weather sits above a coastline. Below it, the consular men and the trading factors in their white houses on the hill, shutters drawn against the heat, fans moving, ice shipped in at ruinous expense. Below them the clerks and archivists and surveyors—the paper caste, grey-suited, ink-fingered, harmless-looking, holding between them every fact worth having in this half of the coast. Below them the foremen and boat captains and the harbor watch. And at the bottom, holding the entire structure up without ever being counted as part of it: the divers, the dock laborers, the coal-heavers, the village people up the coast who sold fish and copra and, once a year at tax time, their silence.

Kessel Bay was one of those villages. She knew it the way you know a neighbor seen across water—a cluster of roofs and drying racks up the coast, boats with a distinctive blue paint on the bows, faces at market twice a month. Tomas Reyes sold oysters at the third stall, always the third stall, always with a story attached. She had bought his oysters for years. It occurred to her now, walking back down to the water, that she had never once asked him a question that wasn't about oysters.

The barges had come in overnight, flying the Company pennant alongside a flag she didn't recognize, and the hill above the harbor already had new soldiers' tents on it, white and orderly as teeth. Negotiation season, the tall man had said. She turned the phrase over. Armistice, the criers were calling it, peace commissioners expected within the month, history arriving in the port like a tide chart—scheduled, printed, happening to important people in Important Rooms.

None of it, she had assumed all her life, had anything to do with the bottom of the harbor where she worked.

But thirty volumes of coastal registry did not get hauled out of a locked room by frightened clerks for nothing. Original registries did not get called *consolidated* by men who knew what original meant. And a tall archivist with a serious face did not fight for a shelf of village tax rolls unless those rolls contained something somebody upstairs badly wanted changed.

She reached the quay. Her suit hung on its hook in the shed, sweating in the heat, and beyond it the water lay flat and bright and patient, holding whatever it held.

Marguerite stood a moment at the edge of the boards, and the resentment she had carried for eleven years—the dull, domestic, well-worn resentment of a woman billed for her own father's coffin—shifted its weight inside her, the way ballast shifts when a boat comes about. It was not pointed at her debt anymore. It was pointed at a door: grey, iron-banded, locked, holding four hundred pages of somebody's arithmetic about villages she had never thought to ask about.

Nine more years, Prosser had said, in numbers. But numbers were written by men, in rooms, in ink that dried. And ink, she thought, pulling on her suit, feeling the cold weight of the sea already reaching for her ankles—ink could be moved.

She went down the ladder and let the water close over her head, and below her, in the green murk, her hands began to search the sand—for pearls, she told herself. Only pearls.

Three days later, there was a bundle on her doorstep.
