## 3. A Bundle at the Door

The cry woke her before the gulls did.

Marguerite had lived above the drying loft on Salt Row long enough to know every sound the port made in the dark—the groan of hulls settling against the pier, the winch chains ticking in the wind like slow clocks, the drunk singing of stevedores three streets over when a payday ran late. She knew which noises meant nothing. This was not one of them.

It came again, thin and furious, from the bottom of the stairs. A cat, she told herself. Cats cried like that sometimes in the alleys, mewling over fish heads or territory or the simple cruelty of being alive. She lay still in her narrow bed and listened to the rain coming off the harbor in gray sheets, and she tried very hard to believe in the cat.

But the sound did not wander. It did not move off toward the fish stalls or dissolve into the general misery of the night. It stayed fixed at the foot of her stairs as though nailed there, rising and falling with a rhythm that was unmistakably, terribly human. Not the cry of someone in pain, exactly. The cry of someone *demanding*—insisting that the world correct itself immediately, that warmth and milk and hands arrive without further delay, that the injustice of existing be remedied at once.

She had heard that quality of insistence before, years ago, in the last weeks of her father's life. The sea took him on a Tuesday, and for three days before it the wind had moaned against the shutters in exactly that register—not asking to be let in, but demanding it, as if the storm had rights here too. Marguerite had been eleven. She remembered pressing her palms flat over her ears and thinking: if I cannot hear it, it cannot mean anything. It was the first lie she had ever caught herself telling on purpose, and it had not gotten easier since.

She rose. The floorboards were cold enough to feel through two blankets, and the single candle she lit guttered in the draft under the door before deciding, grudgingly, to live. Rain hammered the roof like fingers testing for weak places. She wrapped her father's old diving coat around her shoulders—heavy, salt-stiffened canvas that smelled even now of brine and tar and the underside of boats—and went down the stairs one careful step at a time, holding the candle ahead of her like a small, unreliable courage.

The door at the bottom was the door of the loft's entry, and beyond its warped planks the night waited. She put her hand on the latch and stood there a long moment, listening to the crying on the other side, feeling it travel through the wood into her palm the way she could feel the pull of current through a mooring line.

*History happens to other people.* That was the creed she had built her life on, plank by plank, the way her father had built his raft of spare timber behind the huts at Diver's Reach. Other people were conscripted; other people's villages burned in the interior where nobody from the port ever went; other people's names appeared in company ledgers as entries rather than faces. Her own name appeared in ledgers too—that she could not escape—but an entry is not a person. An entry is a way of not having to look. She had learned to live inside her entry the way a pearl lives inside its shell: closed, layered, small, and worth only what the market said.

She lifted the latch.

The rain came in first, a fistful of cold wet flung across the threshold, and then the candlelight found what stood on her step, and for a moment Marguerite's mind simply refused the information, the way it refused the fact of her father's drowning for nearly a year afterward.

A basket. Wicker, old, the kind used for carrying quinces down from the hill farms, with a lid propped half open. And inside it, swaddled in a man's shirt torn into strips, a baby—red-faced, black-haired, furious at the world, its small fists punching up out of the wrappings as though it intended to fight everything the century had planned for it.

No mother crouched in the shadows of the alley. No footsteps hurried away through the rain. Only the water falling and falling, and the child's voice going up and up, and somewhere far out in the harbor the low horn of a company freighter announcing itself to no one.

Marguerite looked up and down Salt Row. The other doors along the lane were shuttered and blind. A lantern swung somewhere near the chandlery, too distant to be any help. Whoever had left this bundle had chosen their moment well: the deepest hour of the night, the hardest rain of the season, the one street in the port where a woman alone would open her door quickly, because a woman alone who hesitates at strange sounds learns things about men that she does not want to know.

Then she saw the note.

It was pinned to the swaddling with a fishhook—a diver's touch, she thought absurdly, a touch she recognized—and the paper, though folded twice, was already going soft with damp. She drew it free and held it close to the candle, tilting it so the rain could not reach it, and read five words in a hand that was hurried but educated, the letters slanting forward as though the writer had been moving while writing:

*Forget this face. Please. Forget.*

Below the words, where a signature should have been, there was instead a seal. Wax, pressed into the fold of the paper, and half-melted—whether by rain or by flame she could not tell—but not so ruined that she could mistake it. The meridian line. The crossed arrows beneath. The device of the Meridian Trading Company, stamped into the one thing connecting this child to whatever had happened to it, as if whoever sealed the note had wanted her to understand precisely how large and how merciless the thing at the other end of that symbol truly was.

Or perhaps—as some colder, quieter part of her mind suggested even then—perhaps they had wanted her to understand that there was nowhere else the child could go.

Marguerite stood in the doorway with the rain blowing past her ankles and the note trembling in her fingers, and she felt two entirely different creatures wake inside her at once. The first was terror, old and practical, the animal that had kept her alive through nine years of indenture: *close the door, bar it, go back to bed, be an entry in a ledger and nothing more, whatever this is it is larger than you and it has a seal to prove it.* The second creature said nothing at all. It simply reached out.

The baby had stopped crying. In the cone of candlelight it had found her face—or found the light, or found the shape of warmth bending over it—and it stared up at her with eyes that were not yet any settled color, that dark blue-black of infancy through which all children regard the world before the world decides what they will become. Its mouth worked. One fist escaped the swaddling completely and grasped, with astonishing strength, the lapel of her father's coat.

She would think about that grip afterward. She would think about it many times over the years that followed: how the child held on to the coat of a drowned man as though it knew whose it was, as though it understood that everything in that coat's history—the cold water, the long breath, the pearls pried loose one by one to buy back days of a woman's life—had arrived at this exact doorstep for a reason.

"Where is your mother?" she asked aloud, and her voice sounded strange to her, roughened by sleep and something else. "Where is anyone?"

The baby blinked at the rain. It did not answer, babies being honest in a way the rest of the world never manages.

She looked at the note again. *Forget this face.* As if a face were a document that could be withdrawn from a registry. As if forgetting were a service one could perform on request, cleanly, like striking a line through a ledger entry. She thought of the clerk downstairs at the countinghouse, his pen moving in those swift, dry strokes, converting hours of her breath underwater into columns of figures, and she understood—with a clarity that felt like cold water closing over her head—that somewhere in this port tonight, someone was doing that same work to this child. Converting a face into a blank. A family into a null. A whole history into *forget.*

And the seal told her who *someone* was.

She should have closed the door. Nine years of survival spoke with one voice, and its counsel was unanimous. But terror and tenderness had collided somewhere beneath her ribs, and in the wreckage of that collision she discovered that her arms had already made a decision her mind had not authorized. She bent, and gathered the basket up, and stepped back inside, and drove the bolt home behind her with a hand that shook only a little.

* * *

Upstairs, by the fuller light of the lamp, the child became more real and more impossible.

She unwrapped the swaddling just far enough to count fingers and toes—a diver's habit, checking a thing's wholeness before trusting it—and found a girl, whole and loud-tempered and perhaps three months old, with a birthmark like a spilled drop of wine below her left ear. The shirt she was wrapped in had been fine once, good linen gone soft with washing, and stitched at the collar with initials that someone had tried, clumsily, to pick out with a razor. *E.V.—* no, *E.K.* The third letter defeated the attempt; a pale ghost of thread remained, a scar in the cloth where a name had been and was meant not to be.

Someone had been thorough. Someone had also been hurried. Both things were true, and both frightened her.

There was milk in the house because there was always milk in the house—condensed, tinny, bought with the small economies of a woman who dove for stray pearls and counted every cent against her debt. She fed the child from a spoon warmed in the lamp flame, and the baby took it with a desperate, gulping hunger that told its own story about how long it had been since anyone had answered its demands. Halfway through the feeding it paused, and looked up at her again with those unfinished eyes, and made a small sound—not a cry, not a word, something between—and Marguerite felt the second creature inside her tighten its hold and refuse, absolutely, to be reasoned with.

"You are evidence," she told the child quietly. "You are somebody's evidence, and somebody's grief, and I do not know yet which, and I am probably the most dangerous place in this port for you to be."

The baby finished the milk and fell asleep against her collarbone with the sudden totality of the very young, its breath quick and warm against her throat, one fist still knotted in the coat.

Marguerite sat by the dying lamp and did not sleep.

She turned the note over and over. The wax seal bothered her more each time she looked at it—not melted by rain, she decided now; the melt ran *inward*, the way wax runs when heat strikes it directly. Someone had softened the seal deliberately, half destroying it, wanting it legible but deniable. Wanting her to *know* and to be unable to *prove*. Which meant the person who left the child was afraid of the company, and also wanted the company's shadow to fall across the doorstep—either as warning to her, or as accusation, or as the only honest signature available in a port where everything official belonged to Meridian.

And the fishhook. She set it on the table beside the candle and studied it: bone-tipped, hand-binded with gut, the lashings worn smooth. That was no shop hook. That was a diver's hook, made the way divers in the outer settlements made them, the way her father had made them before the company's imported tackle priced them out of their own trade. Someone from the water people had carried this child. Someone from the water people had pinned a company-sealed note to its blanket, and that marriage—the hook of her father's world, the seal of the world that owned her—was the strangest and most alarming detail of all.

Outside, the rain slackened toward the gray beginnings of morning. Bells rang at the countinghouse, faint and indifferent. Somewhere out in the roads, she knew, ships were taking on cargo, and history was being lashed down under canvas and carried off to elsewhere, to boardrooms and ministries and signing tables in cities she would never see, where men she would never meet decided what had happened and what had not.

Until tonight, that machinery had never touched her door. It had taken her father, her lungs, her nine best years, and her future, all by slow arithmetic, without ever announcing itself. Now it had delivered itself to her step in a quince basket, weighing less than a sack of rice, screaming.

The baby slept. The note lay on the table, its five words darkening as the damp crept back into the paper.

*Forget this face. Please. Forget.*

Marguerite realized, with the calm of a woman watching her own footing give way on a descent, that she had already failed the note's only instruction. She had memorized the face—the stubborn jaw, the wine-dark mark below the ear, the ghost-letter in the linen. She would carry it now the way she carried her father's last morning, involuntarily, permanently, whether she wished to or not.

Which meant that whoever had written *forget* had badly misjudged her—had mistaken her for a woman to whom things merely happened. She looked down at the sleeping weight against her chest, at the small mouth working even in sleep, and heard herself say, in the level voice she used underwater:

"No."

The word hung in the cold room. Then, because the port was already waking and questions began at dawn, she did the practical things: banked the lamp, hid the note inside the false bottom of her father's lead weight box, tucked the child among the warm folds of the laundry hamper, and prepared a face for the day that showed nothing at all.

At half past seven, boots came up Salt Row in the measured cadence of a man who counted his steps, and a knock fell upon her door—three raps, precise, unhurried, official.

Through the window she watched a tall figure in a company coat consult a slip of paper, then look up at her windows, and she understood that the race she had not known she was running had already begun.
