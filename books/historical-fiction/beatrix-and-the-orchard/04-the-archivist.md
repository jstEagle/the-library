## 4. The Archivist

The registry room of the Meridian Trading Company kept its own weather. While the port outside sweated under a white sun and the harbor breathed its perpetual fog of salt and tar, the archive held a climate of its own making—dry as old bones, dim as a church, smelling of camphor and the particular sweetness of paper that had outlived everyone who had ever written on it. Elias Vane liked to say, though never aloud, that he was not employed by the Company so much as married to this room. He knew its moods. He knew which shelves leaned in the wet season and which drawers swelled in August until they required a specific combination of patience and profanity to open. He knew, above all, the sound the room made when it was correct: a vast paper silence, like snowfall heard through a wall.

It was the absence of that sound—or rather, the absence of one small part of it—that troubled him on the morning everything began.

He had been reconciling the coastal registry against the annual abstract, a task he performed quarterly with the devotion of a monk copying scripture. The work was simple in principle: every birth, death, indenture, and transfer recorded along the coast for the fiscal year ought to appear twice—once in the registry proper, once in the abstract compiled for the Board—and the two accounts must agree. When they agreed, the room hummed its paper silence. When they did not, Elias felt it in his teeth before his eyes confirmed it.

File K-114 was missing.

Not misplaced—he had checked misplacement first, because nine discrepancies in ten were only disorder wearing the mask of mystery. He searched the returns shelf, the pending shelf, the rebind drawer where damaged files waited for fresh linen tape. He walked the aisles running his finger along spines like a blind man reading faces. Nothing. K-114, *Kessel Bay — Coastal Registry, 1909–1910*, existed in the abstract as a tidy entry: forty-one households, two hundred and six souls registered, fishing and pearl concessions held under Company charter. And in the registry itself: nothing at all. A gap in the sequence where a file should sit, the way a mouth looks wrong with one tooth gone.

Elias stood before the gap for a long moment, listening to the room's imperfect silence.

There were innocent explanations. Files went to the magistrate's office for certification and came back late. Clerks borrowed them and forgot. Rats—though rats in his archive were an offense he took personally and punished with traps deployed like artillery. But something about this gap had a different texture. Files that wandered left traces: a withdrawal slip initialed in haste, a smudge of tea, a colleague's guilty shuffle when he passed their desks. This one had been lifted clean. No slip. No initials. As if the file had never existed—which was precisely what someone wished the world to believe.

He told himself it was professional pride that made him care. He was twenty-nine years old and already chief archivist of the port, promoted young because he was meticulous, because he could read four scripts and a fifth hand of terrible clerk's penmanship, because he understood—as few men of ambition ever do—that the empire's true power lay not in its gunboats but in its paper. Gunboats sank. Treaties burned. But a registry entry, certified and filed, could make a man free or unfree across three generations. Elias Vane kept the ledgers that decided who had officially happened.

So he cared about missing files the way priests care about missing saints' bones.

He began, quietly, to trace it.

* * *

The tracing occupied him for six days, and it taught him things he had not expected to learn.

First: the requisition log showed no withdrawal of K-114 in fourteen months. Second: the duplicate copy—the one sent inland to the regional office—had also vanished, according to a reply letter so blandly worded that its blandness became suspicious, the way an empty road in wartime is suspicious. Third, and strangest: Kessel Bay itself had, in the same fiscal year, simply stopped appearing anywhere. Trade manifests listed no landings from its jetty. Concession payments showed no remittances from its families. The tax rolls, which recorded even dead men who owed money, recorded nothing. Two hundred and six souls, entered in ink in January, erased from every subsequent account as if the sea had taken them—and the sea, whatever else it took, always left the paperwork behind. Drowned men still owed taxes here. That was almost a joke among the clerks.

Elias sat with the reply letter from the regional office in the lamplit evening, after the other clerks had gone home to their dinners and their wives, and read it again. *We regret that the requested duplicate cannot presently be located. We trust the matter is of no great urgency.* The hand was elegant. The phrasing was careful. Someone senior had drafted that letter more than once before sending it; you could see it in the way each sentence closed like a door.

He should have stopped there. He understood, in the abstract way all clerks understand such things, that some gaps are not accidents but architecture—that certain absences are built deliberately, load-bearing, holding up someone's career or someone's peace. A wise archivist lets those gaps lie. He files his curiosity in a drawer and closes it, and in ten years he is director of records, and in twenty he retires with a pension and a medal for discretion.

Elias pulled a fresh sheet of paper and began a private memorandum instead: *Inquiries regarding File K-114, commenced [date], sole purpose: reconciliation of registry.* Even his rebellion was documented. He smiled at himself without much humor, dipped his pen, and wrote down everything he knew, dated and initialled, because if the file could vanish then so could he, and he wanted—at least—one record that said he had existed and asked questions.

* * *

She came into the registry room on the seventh day, and he would remember afterward that she arrived at the exact hour he had given up pretending he wasn't waiting for something.

The afternoon light came through the high windows in slanted columns full of drifting dust, and she stood in it a moment, blinking, adjusting to the dark—a young woman in a faded blue cotton dress, salt-stiffened at the hem, her black hair pinned back with what looked like a fishbone comb. She carried herself with a stillness he'd seen in the divers who worked the harbor flats: people who spent their lives between breaths, who had learned economy from the water itself. There was a bruise-colored shadow of exhaustion under her eyes, and her hands, folded neatly at her waist, bore the fine white scars of shell and rope.

"Good afternoon," she said, and her voice had the careful neutrality of someone who has practiced being unremarkable. "I'm looking for information. About a village."

The desk clerks were at lunch; only Elias remained, which meant the question came to him. He set down his pen. "This is the coastal registry," he said. "Which village?"

"Kessel Bay."

Every instinct he possessed stood up at once.

He kept his face in order—it was a face trained by years of listening to men lie about cargo weights—and said, mildly, "Kessel Bay. May I ask in what connection? Property, family, concession?"

"Family," she said. "I thought—I hoped there might be records. Births. Who lived there." She hesitated, and in the hesitation he saw something flicker behind the practiced calm, quick as a fish turning in shallow water. "My father worked the oyster beds north of there, years ago. He knew families from the Bay."

"And your name?"

"Marguerite Thornbury." She spelled it without being asked, which marked her as educated enough to know how names got mangled in ledgers, and she met his eyes steadily while she did it.

He wrote the name down because writing things down was what he did, but his mind was moving fast and cold beneath the mild surface. Kessel Bay. The very gap he had spent a week circling. And now a diver's daughter—indentured, he would have wagered his pension on it, you could see the Company stamp on people like a watermark—walking in with soft questions about births and families in a village that officially contained neither.

Coincidence existed. Elias believed in it the way he believed in tide tables: as a general principle that individual events constantly betrayed. He asked a few more questions—her father's name, the years she meant, whether anyone had sent her—and she answered them all smoothly, too smoothly, each answer arriving exactly on time like a clerk reciting figures she had memorized rather than calculated. Her hands never moved from their fold at her waist. Only once, when he mentioned that the Kessel Bay file was not presently available, did her breath catch—a small hitch, quickly mastered, the sound a person makes stepping into cold water.

"Not available," she repeated.

"Under review." He let a beat pass. "Files are occasionally removed for audit. They return, usually."

"Do they," she said, in a tone that agreed with nothing, thanked him for nothing, and promised nothing. She asked, before leaving, whether records might exist elsewhere—inland, at the regional office—and he said truthfully that duplicates were sometimes held there, and watched her absorb that with the same controlled stillness. Then she thanked him in flawless, empty courtesy, and went out through the columns of dusty light, and the door sighed shut behind her, and the room resumed its paper silence.

Elias sat looking at the name on his memorandum pad. *Marguerite Thornbury.* He added, beneath it, in his smallest hand: *Asked after Kessel Bay families. Unprompted. Knows the regional office holds duplicates—or wanted me to think she didn't.*

Then he sat back and found, to his considerable irritation, that his suspicion would not hold its shape.

Because underneath the calculation, another thing had occurred, unbidden: he had noticed the exact gray-green of her eyes, the way the dust turned gold as it drifted past her shoulder, the scar across her right knuckle and the dignity with which she carried it. He had noticed that when she lied to him—and he was nearly certain she had lied, or at least withheld, with every polished answer—she had done it beautifully, without shame, the way a drowning man swims: not wickedly, but because the water demanded it. And he had thought, in the last moment before the door closed, that she was the first person in eleven months to walk into this room and ask after the missing village as if the missing village mattered—as if two hundred and six souls were people rather than entries.

That was dangerous thinking. He recognized danger when he catalogued it.

* * *

That evening he did three things, and the ordering of them mattered, though he wouldn't understand why until much later.

First, he checked the indenture rolls for Marguerite Thornbury, because her history was surely on file and reading files was both his sin and his sacrament. He found her easily enough: bound to the Meridian Trading Company at fifteen years of age, pearl-diving levy assessed annually, term of service extended twice—for debts, for damages, for the small administrative cruelties by which indentures lengthen themselves like shadows at dusk. Father: one Peter Thornbury, pearl diver, deceased by drowning, year of record 1904. Mother: blank. Next of kin: none. On paper, Marguerite Thornbury belonged to the Company the way the archive belonged to the Company—wholly, permanently, entry by entry. He looked at the ledger line a long time. Something in him resisted it—not the fact, which was ordinary, but the tidiness of it. Here was a woman reduced to a levy and a levy reduced to arithmetic, and somewhere in his chest an unprofessional voice observed that arithmetic had no business describing the color of anyone's eyes.

Second, he wrote to a contact at the regional office—an old instructor, half-retired, whose discretion was absolute and whose curiosity was even greater than Elias's own—asking, in the oblique language archivists use for matters that must never be named directly, whether the Kessel Bay duplicate had ever actually been requested by anyone, or only reported missing. The distinction mattered. A file lost was weather. A file reported lost that no one had ever asked for was a signature.

Third, he locked the registry room, walked out into the blue dusk of the port, past the customs sheds and the pearl-sacks stacked like sleeping animals, and found himself—by no route he admitted to choosing—passing the row of company lodging houses where the indentured workers lived. He did not stop. He did not look up at any window. He merely noted, with the part of his mind that never stopped taking inventory, which house was hers, that laundry hung late on its line, that a lamp burned in a lower window and was extinguished quickly, as though someone inside had seen him coming.

He walked home through the smell of brine and coal smoke and told himself, with the rigorous honesty that was his one vanity, that he had come by accident, and knew it for the first false entry he had ever willingly made in his own record.

* * *

Three days later the reply came from his old instructor, and it was short even by the standards of men who wrote nothing down that mattered:

*No requisition exists. The duplicate was withdrawn under seal, authority above my floor. Advise you reconcile elsewhere. Some files are missing on purpose, boy. Ask yourself who benefits from a village with no past.*

Elias read it twice at his desk in the morning quiet, the harbor bells sounding slow and thick through the shutters, and understood that he stood at one of the doors his profession warned him about—the doors that open off the corridors of records into rooms where decisions are made and unmade. Behind this particular door: a sealed authority, a vanished file, a village subtracted from existence, and now a diver's daughter asking gentle questions with drowned eyes.

He was an archivist. His whole life was arranged around a single conviction: that what is written down is real, and what is unwritten never happened. He had built his character on it the way other men build on faith. And now the machinery he served had shown him its other face—that the same power that preserved could erase, that a gap in a shelf could be a massacre's final act, committed not with fire but with flattery of the official record.

Ask yourself who benefits.

He took out his private memorandum, dated it, and wrote beneath his earlier notes: *Withdrawal under seal, regional office. No requisition. Registry falsified by omission at minimum. Continuing.*

His pen hovered. Then he added one more line, small and precise and entirely irregular:

*Subject M.T. returned today to the flats. Watched her dive for stray pearls below the customs wall for twenty minutes. Recovered three. She works like the water owes her money and she means to collect. Note: I have begun watching a person, not a file. Record this honestly.*

He blotted the page, filed it inside the false spine of a hymnbook he kept for exactly such purposes—he had always assumed the purposes would be innocuous—and sat in the great paper silence of the archive, listening to the gap where K-114 should be.

Outside, the noon bell rang across the water. Somewhere down on the pearl flats, a woman he had known for seven minutes was holding her breath on the harbor bottom, and he found he could not stop thinking about it: about her, surfacing somewhere below his window with shells in her fist and secrets in her mouth, while he sat here guarding a registry that had been taught to forget.

The next time she came asking innocent questions, he resolved, he would not simply answer them.

He would find out what she knew—and God help them both, he would decide afterward whether to report it, or help her hide it.
