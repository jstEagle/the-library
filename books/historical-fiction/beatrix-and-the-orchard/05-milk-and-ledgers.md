## 5. Milk and Ledgers

*MARGUERITE*

The knock came at the hour when the light turned to brine—neither day nor evening, that grey wash off the harbor that made everything look as though it were already pickled in memory. Marguerite knew the rhythm of it before she knew the reason. Company knocks were not like other knocks. Fishermen knocked twice and waited. The priest knocked softly, apologetically, as though the door itself were a sin. But the Meridian Trading Company knocked the way a scale drops: once, precise, final.

She had the baby on her hip.

That was the whole of her trouble, folded into seven words. Three days now since the bundle had appeared on her step with its half-melted seal of company wax, and already the child had colonized her body—the crook of her arm reshaped, her sleep cut into small watchable pieces, her breasts heavy and aching with milk she could not explain to anyone, least of all herself. Her body had made its decision before her mind consented to the arrangement. It was, she thought bitterly, exactly how indenture worked: the flesh signed what the spirit would not.

She set Aurelia—no. Not yet. She had not named her yet; naming was a thing you did in daylight, with witnesses, and this child existed only in the margins of the night—she set the baby down among the laundry heaped in the corner behind the drying screen, a mound of pearl-divers' smocks still stiff with salt, and pulled two of them over the small rising chest like a woman tucking a hillside over a spring.

"Hush," she breathed, not sure whether she was commanding the child or her own heart. "Hush now. You are laundry. You are nothing but wet linen and old sweat, and nobody in the history of the world has ever been interrogated by a smock."

The baby looked up at her with those dark, unblinking eyes—eyes that remembered something, Marguerite was increasingly certain, some last image of Kessel Bay or wherever the face came from—and did not cry. It was the child's single mercy, that silence, and Marguerite accepted it the way divers accept calm water: gratefully, knowing it would not hold.

The knock again. Once. Precise. Final.

She opened the door with the expression she wore for clerks and harbormasters, the one she thought of as her ledger face: every column balanced, nothing owed.

The man on her step was not a clerk. He was tall in the way of men who had grown up around tall furniture, with ink on his fingers that no amount of pumice would ever fully lift, and he held a leather folio against his chest the way other men held Bibles or weapons. His eyes went past her shoulder into the room with an efficiency that frightened her more than any uniform could have. Eyes that counted things.

"Marguerite Thornbury," he said. Not a question. "Contract 4471, dive-class, renewed last Tuesday."

"You have me at a disadvantage," she said. "You know my number."

"Elias Vane. Archivist, Meridian Registry." He produced papers without flourish, fanned them like a card player who wanted her to see every card. "Company statute fourteen. Quarterly audit of premises leased against company credit. Your quarters are leased against company credit."

"Are they?" She let genuine surprise touch her mouth. "I only sleep here. I hadn't considered the philosophy of it."

"May I come in?"

*There is an infant behind your drying screen,* said every voice in her skull at once. *There is a note with a half-melted seal sewn into your mattress ticking. There is milk leaking through your blouse this very moment, and you are about to invite a professional counter of things across your threshold.*

"Of course," said Marguerite Thornbury, contract 4471, and stepped aside.

* * *

*ELIAS*

He noted the laundry first because he was trained to note things in order of anomaly.

Quarters leased by a working diver—a woman, unmarried, twenty-three years of age according to her renewal papers—should contain: one cot, one table, one chair, one sea chest, diving gear in various states of repair, cooking implements, and clothing sufficient for one person. Elias Vane had audited perhaps two hundred such quarters in his six years with the Registry, and they were alike as shells from the same beach.

This room contained the cot, the table, the chair, the chest. It contained a small forest of mended nets. It contained a clay jar of ointment that smelled of lavender, which was an expense no diver's arithmetic justified. And it contained laundry—not a basketful, not a washing-day heap, but a *quantity*, piled high behind a drying screen in the warmest corner of the room, near the little iron stove whose fire had clearly been fed within the hour.

People heated rooms for reasons. People heaped clean linens near fires for reasons.

"Mind the nets," the diver said, moving ahead of him with the loose-limbed grace of someone whose body belonged to deep water. She lifted the kettle onto the stove as if he hadn't seen her do it—as if the stove's warmth were incidental, domestic, unremarkable. "I can offer you coffee that tastes like bilge, or tea that tastes like slightly better bilge."

"Tea," Elias said, and opened his folio on the table. "This is routine, Miss Thornbury. The Registry audits annually; we're simply late. There was a disruption in scheduling."

"So I've heard." She set a chipped cup before him. "Something about Kessel Bay."

It was said lightly, idly, the way one mentions weather three islands away. Elias kept his pen moving down his inventory sheet—cot, table, chair, chest—but some interior clerk in him made a mark beside the word *Kessel Bay* and underlined it twice.

"You'll forgive me," he said. "News travels slowly out here. What sort of disruption?"

"Fires." She shrugged, pouring her own cup. Steam curled between them, smelling of tar and leaf-dust. "Fires travel fast. That's rather their profession. Two burnings, they say, though the second one burned nothing you could see." She glanced at him then, and her eyes were the flat grey-green of water over a shoal—pretty, and hiding exactly how shallow the bottom was, or how deep. "But then, you'd know better than I. You keep the records. If a record burns, does the thing it recorded stop having happened?"

Elias set down his pen.

It was, he would reflect later, the moment the audit stopped being an audit. Not because she had said anything improper—her question was almost schoolgirl-philosophical, the kind of thing seminar men argued over port wine in cities he'd never see. It was because she had asked it *while looking directly at him*, and because somewhere beneath the idle curiosity in her voice lay a blade of genuine fury, honed and sheathed so quickly he might have imagined it.

"The record survives or it doesn't," he said carefully. "The event is indifferent to the paperwork."

"That's not what the company believes." She smiled, taking the edge off her own words, making them safe again. "Or you wouldn't be here counting my chairs."

"Cot, table, chair, chest," he agreed mildly, writing it. "And laundry."

"Ah." She followed his gaze to the screen, and Elias watched her do something extraordinary: she blushed. Not the performative blush of a woman caught, but a slow, mortified color climbing from her collar, entirely convincing, entirely wrong. "Yes. Well. You've caught me in a state of shame, Mr. Vane. Those aren't mine."

"They aren't."

"The Widow Okafor's boys. The older one works the shallows now, and she takes in washing, and her hands are bad with the salt-rheum, so I dry the loads here where the stove is, and—" She stopped, laughed at herself, pressed the back of her wrist to her cheek as though to cool the evidence. "You'll write that down and it will sound like exactly the excuse it is. Go on. Look, if you must. It's only linen."

*Only linen.* Elias rose, folio in hand, and crossed to the drying screen, and he was aware of her behind him—very aware, the way a man is aware of the sea's surface, knowing the weight of what moves beneath.

He lifted a salt-stiff smock. Linen. Another. Linen, patched, mended, redolent of brine and lye soap. A third. He was reaching for the fourth when he heard it.

Not a cry. Nothing so convenient. A sound like a kitten makes in its sleep, if kittens slept in mounds of divers' smocks—if kittens had lungs the size of plums and a heartbeat audible across a room to a man listening for exactly that frequency the way drowning men listen for bells.

Elias Vane stood with his hand full of laundry and did not move.

Behind him, Marguerite Thornbury poured tea she did not drink and said, in the pleasant, empty voice of a woman discussing weather, "My father used to say the sea keeps everything. That nothing sinks past remembering. Pearls, anchors, men. It holds it all, down in the cold. You just have to know where to put your hand."

Elias laid the smock back with exaggerated care, smoothing its fold as a sexton smooths a pall.

"Your father was a diver," he said.

"Drowned," she said. "Eight years ago. The sea kept him three days and gave him back at Marrow Point. So perhaps he was wrong about the keeping." She came to stand beside him, close enough that he could smell the salt on her skin beneath the lavender, and looked at the mound of laundry with an expression he could not read and never forgot—an expression like a woman standing guard over a grave that was also a cradle. "Well? Do you need to count the smocks individually, Mr. Vane, or will the estimate serve?"

*Say the number,* something urged him. *Lift the cloth. There is a child in this room and a missing file in your registry and a burned village whose coastal entries vanished from drawer nine eleven days ago, and this woman with her borrowed laundry and her dead father is either the coincidence of the century or the thread that, pulled, unravels the entire sleeve.*

He was twenty-nine years old and he had served the Meridian Trading Company since he was sixteen, faithfully, precisely, the way a ledgers serves its columns. He believed in order. He believed that paper was civilization's memory, that an archive was a promise made to the future. He believed the quarterly audits mattered because accuracy mattered, because a world recorded truly was a world that could be governed justly.

He believed these things the way one believes in a ship: right up until the moment one notices the water rising between the boards.

"The estimate will serve," Elias said. "Laundry, miscellaneous, one lot." He returned to the table and wrote it, and his pen did not shake, and he despised himself a little for how steady it was. Then, because his mouth had apparently decided to act independently of his training, he added: "The lavender ointment. For the rheumatism?"

"For the hands," she said, which answered nothing.

He closed the folio. "Your lease is in order. Your inventory is in order. Thank you for the tea." At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and heard himself say—in a voice pitched low, in a register he did not use with clerks or superiors or anyone—"Miss Thornbury. If you ever needed to ask the Registry an innocent question... the reading room is open to lessees on Thursdays. Drawer nine has been behaving strangely lately. Missing things. I mention it because inaccuracies trouble me. Professionally."

For one heartbeat the mask slipped—all of them, hers and his together—and what lay underneath was not innocence at all but two people recognizing each other across a great distance, the way ships recognize lighthouses.

"I'll remember that," she said. "Thursdays."

* * *

*MARGUERITE*

She listened to his boots descend the outside stair, counted to sixty, and did not go to the screen until ninety.

Then she knelt and drew back the smocks, and the baby looked up at her, awake and silent, having witnessed the entire performance from her nest of linen with the solemn attention of a judge.

"He knew," Marguerite told her softly. "Don't pretend otherwise. He lifted that third smock and he heard you breathe, and he wrote *laundry, miscellaneous, one lot* in his little book and walked out that door." Her hands, she noticed, were trembling now—now, when it was safe, the way her body always saved its fear for after, spending terror like a miser when no one watched. "Why would he do that?"

The baby yawned.

"Yes," Marguerite agreed. "Exactly."

She carried the child to the window. Below, the archivist was crossing the yard toward the registry road, folio under his arm, unhurried, and at the gate he stopped—just briefly—and looked back up at her window. He didn't wave. He didn't nod. He simply confirmed her position on the map of his attention and went on, and Marguerite understood, with a sensation like surfacing too fast, that something had changed in the architecture of her danger.

Three days she had been alone with the secret, and alone was simple: one wall between the child and the world, one pair of hands, one lie to maintain. Now there was a second wall—or was it a second breach? An archivist who knew and said nothing was either an ally or a fisherman, and fishermen always chummed the water first.

*Drawer nine,* he'd said. Behaving strangely. Missing things.

She had gone to the registry on Wednesday asking her innocent questions about her own renewal papers, and she had seen drawer nine standing open, and she had seen the young man's face as he searched it, and she had thought at the time: *there is a man who has lost something he cannot admit to losing.*

Now he had stood in her room, heard an infant breathe under a pile of linen, and chosen blindness.

Unless he hadn't heard. Unless he was simply a tired functionary with bad ears and a schedule to keep. Unless the whole of it was her guilt constructing conspiracies out of courtesy.

Marguerite fed the baby by the stove while the light failed, and turned the man over in her mind like a pearl held to the lamp, checking for flaws. Ink-stained fingers. Eyes that counted. A voice that lowered itself, unasked, when it offered dangerous gifts. *Inaccuracies trouble me. Professionally.*

"Well," she murmured into the small scalp, breathing in that smell of milk and warmth that undid her nightly and rebuilt her by morning. "We have ten days of his silence, perhaps. A fortnight if we're lucky. And then he'll decide what we are to him—witnesses, or evidence."

The stove ticked. Down in the harbor, a ship's bell rang the hour, and the sound rolled across the water the way history rolled across the world, indifferent, arriving whether or not anyone had finished preparing.

On Thursday, Marguerite Thornbury washed and braided her hair, put on her good blouse, tucked the note with its half-melted seal into the lining of her sleeve, and walked to the registry reading room to ask an innocent question of her own.

The archivist was waiting at the desk by drawer nine, and he had left two chairs out.
