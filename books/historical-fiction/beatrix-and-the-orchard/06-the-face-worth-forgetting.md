## 6. The Face Worth Forgetting

The child had been with me nine days when Old Sefa came to my door.

I remember the number because I had begun counting the way a prisoner counts—not toward anything, only to prove that time was still moving, that the days were distinguishable from one another. Nine days of milk fetched in secrecy from the widow Ocampo, who asked no questions because she was paid in pearls small enough to swallow without scandal. Nine nights of Aurelia—no, not yet; she had no name then, she was only *the child*, *the bundle*, *the little weight*—nine nights of her crying against my collarbone while I walked the floor and memorized the sound of my own heart going too fast.

Nine days, and I had already learned what every mother learns: that terror and tenderness are not opposites but twins, born together, holding hands.

Sefa found me at the washing basin in the yard, scrubbing the child's cloth through soapy water with the concentration of a woman defusing something. She came through the gate without calling out—that was her way, old-pearl ways, the silence of women who had spent their lives listening for the shift of tides and the warning scrape of a partner's fin. She was past seventy, hands folded into themselves like dried seaweed, eyes gone pale as the inside of a shell. The other divers said she was blind above water. They were wrong. She simply saw different things than the rest of us.

"You have a baby," she said.

"It's laundry day," I said. "One develops strange attachments to one's linens."

"Marguerite." My name in her mouth sounded older, heavier, the way it must have sounded on my grandmother's tongue before the company ever wrote it down. "Bring her out where the light is good."

*Her.* I hadn't told anyone the child was a girl.

---

I want to say I hesitated. I want to claim some prudence, some calculation, even cowardice. But the truth is simpler and more damning: I was so tired of hiding her that when Sefa held out her two crooked hands, I laid the baby into them the way you lay a hot coal down—relief and fear in the same motion.

Sefa carried her to the doorway where the morning came slanting through, gold and salt-hazed, and turned the child's face up to it.

The baby woke and did not cry. That in itself was unusual; she cried at everything else, at the gulls, at the creak of the stairs, at the particular pitch of my voice when I sang low. But in Sefa's arms she went quiet and stared up at the light with those grey-dark eyes that were not mine and would never be mine, and worked her small mouth as though tasting the air.

"Ah," said Sefa.

It was only a syllable. I have heard men scream with less meaning.

"What?" My voice came out wrong. Thin. "What is it?"

Sefa didn't answer at once. She turned the child's face slightly with one finger, gentle as a woman adjusting a pearl to catch the light, studying the line of the nose, the set of the brow, the small stubborn chin. Then she looked at me, and her clouded eyes went through me and out the other side, all the way to somewhere I couldn't follow.

"Where did you get this child?"

"She was left. At my door."

"I know what was left at your door," Sefa said. "I'm asking where she came from before that."

"I don't know. There was a note." I heard myself reciting it the way I had recited it to the dark all week: *Forget this face.* "No name. No mother. A wax seal, half melted. I thought—I thought if they wanted her forgotten, maybe forgetting was the kindest thing. Maybe whoever left her was saving her from something."

"Saving her." Sefa weighed the word and found it light. She sat down slowly on my doorstep, the baby bundled against her flat chest, and for a long moment she only breathed, in and out, the rhythm we divers learn first and forget last: full lungs before descent, never hold air when you rise, come up slow or the pressure will kill you. I think she was giving herself time to arrange whatever she had to tell me into an order I could survive.

"Marguerite," she said finally. "You dive the strays off Kessel Bay?"

Kessel Bay lay south along the coast, a half-day by boat, sheltered from the monsoon winds—a pearling ground my father had worked before me, shallow enough for free divers, rich enough that the Meridian Company kept a permanent station there. "I did. Before my contract moved me here. Why?"

"And you've been back since? This season?"

"You know I haven't. The company pulled the station's license in—" I stopped. I tried to remember when, exactly, the license had been pulled, and found that I could not. It had happened the way most things happened in this port: quietly, between ledgers, while everyone watched the weather. "Months ago. There was trouble, they said. Taxes unpaid. Some dispute about the concession."

"Trouble," Sefa repeated. The word sat in her mouth like a stone she was deciding whether to spit out or swallow.

"Sefa, you're frightening me, and you're doing it slowly, on purpose, and I'd thank you to stop."

She smiled then, a small terrible smile, and patted the step beside her. I sat. Below us the harbor was doing its morning business—lighters loading copra, a company steamer taking on water, the gulls conducting their eternal litigation over the fish market scraps. History, I thought then, as I had thought every day of my life, was happening somewhere out there, on the ships, in the offices, in the countries whose flags rotated over the customs house. Not here. Not on a doorstep. Not in a bundle of laundry-warm cloth.

"This child," Sefa said, "has the whole face of the Alvarado family. You see it? The nose—broad at the bridge, narrow at the tip. The chin that comes forward like it's arguing. Little Consolación Alvarado had that exact chin. And the eyes go grey in this light, but they're sea-colored really, blue-green, both branches of the family had it. The Alvarados and the Reyeses, intermarried three generations, half the bay carries that face." Her finger traced the air above the baby's brow. "This is a Kessel Bay face, Marguerite. There aren't many left to wear it."

The harbor sounds continued beneath us, indifferent. Somewhere a winch began turning.

"Burned," I said. I don't know how I knew the word was coming, except that I had been circling it for nine days without letting myself land. "The village burned."

"In the spring." Sefa's voice stayed level, diver's voice, conserving air. "They told us it was an accident. A cooking fire gone wrong in the dry season. Half a village lost, they said. Sad thing. The company sent blankets." Her mouth thinned. "My granddaughter's husband ran cargo boats down that coast. He came through Kessel Bay three days after. He saw the ash line, he said—the burn pattern. Cooking fires spread house to house, messy, patchy, you can read them like tide charts. This burned from the center out. Even. Like something poured. And the church records office, which was stone, which had no reason to burn at all—it was gutted to the walls. Nothing burns stone like that unless someone wants what's inside it destroyed."

"Why?" The word came out of me smaller than I meant it to. "Why would anyone—Sefa, it's a fishing village. Pearl divers and fishermen. What could they possibly have that—"

"Petitions," she said.

And there it was. The word dropped into the morning like a body into still water, and I watched the ripples spread backward through everything I had believed about my own life.

"The Alvarados were petitioners," Sefa went on. "Old Consolación's husband, Emilio, he could read and write—his father was schooled by the mission. For two years the families of the bay had been drawing up papers. Complaints. About the company's new concession terms, about divers dying of the bends working the deep beds the company opened, about the weighing scales at the station always reading light. They sent petitions to the colonial governor, to the courts, to newspapers in the capital. Signed documents. Names. Dates. Testimony." She looked down at the baby sleeping in her arms. "Paper, Marguerite. That's what that village had that was worth burning. Paper and the people who signed it."

---

I stood up because sitting felt suddenly like sinking. I walked to the edge of the yard where my washing line sagged under the weight of tiny garments, and I touched one of them—a sleeve no longer than my hand—and I made myself think it through, because thinking it through was the only way to keep from screaming, and screaming would bring questions, and questions would bring the company.

A village burned in the spring. The official story: accident. The burn pattern: deliberate. The records office: destroyed. The petitioners: dead or scattered. And now, months later, a company seal on my doorstep, half-melted as if it had been near flame or hurriedly broken open, and a note commanding me to forget a face—and the face belonged to the very family whose names had been inked onto those petitions.

Not abandoned. Erased. Or rather: erased *incorrectly*. One thread of the pattern left uncut. A survivor. An heir. A witness small enough to carry in two arms.

"They missed her," I said, to the washing line, to the harbor, to no one. "When they burned it all, they missed one person."

"Or someone didn't miss her," Sefa said behind me. "Someone with a conscience and a fast horse and one chance to get her out before the armistice people arrive and everything gets tidied. Someone who couldn't keep her and couldn't kill her and knew that an indentured woman with no family and no standing was the last place anyone would look." A pause. "That part's a mercy, Marguerite. Don't lose sight of it. Somebody loved this child enough to commit a crime for her."

"Somebody loved her enough to leave her with *me*." I turned around. "I owe the company four more years, Sefa. I live in a company row house. I buy my milk with pearls I pull out of company waters. If they find her here—"

"Then she can't be found," Sefa said simply, as though stating the depth of a lagoon before a dive. "Which means first you have to know exactly what it is you're hiding. Because right now you're hiding it blind, and blind diving kills."

The baby stirred and gave a thin complaining cry, and Sefa rocked her with the automatic economy of a woman who had raised ten children and buried six, and I watched the child settle against her, and I understood—with the cold specific clarity that comes just before a descent—that I was already past the point where I could give her up. Whatever she was, whatever danger she carried folded in her blankets like contraband, I had kissed that brow for nine mornings. The decision had been made without me, somewhere between the first feeding and the ninth night, in the territory of the body where choices are made before the mind files its report.

"What do I need to know?" I asked.

"That depends on what you want," said Sefa. "If you want to survive, you need to know nothing at all. You need to become a woman who found a baby and asked no questions, and you need to be that woman so completely that even you believe it. That's the safe road. I'd take it myself, at my age." She rose, stiff-backed, and put the child into my arms with great ceremony, the way you transfer something that has just become heavy with meaning. "But if you want to know—if some contrary piece of you needs to know, the way your father needed to know what was under every sandbar between here and the deep beds—then you find the survivors. There are always survivors, whatever the histories say. Fishermen who were out on the water that night. A clerk from the station who signed the false report and hasn't slept since. People who saw the second fire."

"The second fire?"

Sefa gathered her stick and turned toward the gate. Over her shoulder, in a voice pitched for me alone under the noise of the harbor:

"A village only burns once by accident, child. Kessel Bay burned twice. The second time, what burned wasn't houses."

She let herself out. The gate clicked shut behind her with the softness of a closing shell.

I stood in my small yard with my washing drying and my heart going like a diver's hammering pulse, and the baby looked up at me with her sea-colored eyes—Consolación's eyes, Emilio's eyes, the eyes of a whole drowned village insisting on itself through this one small surviving face—and I understood at last what had been left on my doorstep nine days ago.

Not a burden. Not a mystery. Evidence.

*Forget this face,* the note had said.

I looked down into it, memorizing it instead—the broad nose, the arguing chin, the grey-green light of the shallows caught in a gaze too young to know what it witnessed—and I swore, silently, in the language of women who work underwater and therefore trust nothing that cannot be held in both hands, that I would find out what this child had seen.

And that same evening, as if the world had been waiting for the oath, there came a knock at my door: a tall man in company grey, hat in hand, with dust on his sleeves and the careful eyes of someone who reads documents for a living—and who suspects, quite correctly, that some documents have gone missing.
