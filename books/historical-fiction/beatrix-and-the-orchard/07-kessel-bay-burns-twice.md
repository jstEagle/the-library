## 7. Kessel Bay Burns Twice

The first fire came from the sea.

That is the thing I want written down, before anything else, before anyone asks me how it began or who gave the order or what the flag looked like on the gunboat's mast. The first fire came from the sea. We were a fishing village; we knew every kind of weather the water could send us, and we had learned to read smoke the way other men read clouds — a smudge on the horizon meant a steamer running cheap coal, a thin grey thread meant the company launch out of Meridian Point. But this smoke came low and fast and wide, and it did not rise so much as *arrive*, the way a wave arrives, and by the time old Marta Serrano rang the chapel bell we could already hear the engines.

I was seventeen. I tell you that because everything I did afterward, I did as a boy does — without strategy, without understanding that some doors close behind you forever. My mother was salting fish in the yard. My father was at the drying racks with my uncle. My little sister Inés was chasing a goat she would never catch, because Inés never caught anything, she only chased, and the chasing was the whole joy of her.

"Reyes!" someone shouted — my name, not my father's, because I was the fastest runner in Kessel Bay and everyone knew it. "Run to Meridian Point! Run for the company office!"

I ran. God forgive me, I ran.

I have thought about that run every day of my life since. Not the fire — I will come to the fire, I promise you, I will give you the fire down to its last ember — but the run. Three miles of coastal path, my lungs full of salt, my feet finding the stones they had found ten thousand times before, and in me no thought at all except: *the company will stop them.* Because that is what a boy believes. That is what we all believed, then, in those years when the armistice was still being negotiated somewhere far away and the company men came through our bay buying pearls and dried fish and smiling at our children. The company was power, yes, but power on *our* side of the ledger. Power that kept order. A boy does not ask who pays for order, or what the payment is, or whose blood settles the account.

I reached Meridian Point in under an hour. I remember the clock over the company office said half past four. I remember because I stood beneath it gasping while a clerk with ink-stained cuffs looked at me through the window as if I were weather he had chosen not to go out in.

"The gunboats," I said. "They're shelling Kessel Bay."

"They're shelling *rebel positions*," the clerk said.

"There are no rebels in Kessel Bay. There are fish racks. There is the chapel. There is—"

"Then there is nothing to worry about," he said, and closed the shutter.

I stood there while the shutter latched. I heard the latch. To this day I can hear the latch — a small brass sound, almost polite, the most ordinary sound in the world — and I understood, in the way you understand a thing in your body long before your mind consents to it, that the shutter had not been closed against me. It had been closed against the window. Against anything the window might show. The clerk knew what the guns were doing the way I now know it: completely, in advance, by arrangement.

I ran back. Of course I ran back. What else is there in a boy but the going-back?

* * *

You want me to describe the raid, and I will, but first you must understand what Kessel Bay was, because the historians — the real ones, the ones who measure and weigh — will tell you it was nothing. A settlement of two hundred souls, unregistered land claims, a chapel without a resident priest. They will say it in that voice they use, the one like a ledger being read aloud, and they will make it small.

Kessel Bay smelled of salt and eucalyptus and fish oil and the woodsmoke of forty cookfires, each one different, because every family burned different wood and my whole life I could walk the lane at dusk with my eyes shut and know whose house was whose by the smoke alone. The Vargases burned guava wood, sweet. Old Marta burned driftwood, sharp and mineral. We burned mangrove, acrid, and my mother apologized for it every single evening of her life as though the trees had personally failed her. The bay itself was shallow and green-gold, and the pearl beds lay beyond the second reef, and the divers — my father among them, before his chest gave out and he took to the racks — brought up shells in woven baskets, and the shells went into the company's crates, and the crates went onto the company's ships, and none of us had ever seen the money that was supposedly coming back down that chain. But that was normal. That was everywhere. That was simply the shape of the world, and a shape is not an injury until someone shows you the outline of what you had.

The shelling lasted twenty minutes. Twenty minutes. I counted afterward, from the testimony of the survivors, piecing it together minute by minute like a diver counting his breaths on the way down. Twenty minutes of naval gunfire on an undefended village, and then the boats came ashore — not soldiers in the uniforms of any government I could name, though their rifles were new and their boots matched, which told you whose money dressed them — and they worked up the lane from the landing beach with torches.

Torches. After the guns, torches. As if the guns had been merely the opening argument and the fire was the verdict.

I came up the path from Meridian Point with the smoke standing over the bay like a black tree, and I could see the lane, and I will tell you what I saw and I will not dress it. I saw the Vargas house burning with the family inside it, because Señor Vargas had gone back for Inés Vargas — not my Inés, the little one, three years old — who had hidden under a bed when the shelling started, and the soldiers would not wait, and he would not leave without her. I saw old Marta in her doorway with the chapel bell-rope still in her hands, as though ringing it had been a thing one could keep doing, keep ringing, keep calling the village together, right up until a rifle butt ended it. I saw my mother's cookfire — *our* cookfire, mangrove smoke, acrid — and I knew before I reached the turn in the path that no one from my house had come down to the beach where the others had gathered, and I was right, and I will not describe the drying racks, except to say that my father died among his fish, and my mother died holding Inés, and neither of them had ever harmed a living soul, and the men who did it wore boots that matched.

Two hundred souls. When the counting was done — and the counting was done badly, quickly, by men who wanted a small number — they wrote down sixty-one dead. Sixty-one. I can give you names past a hundred, and I am not finished giving them, and I never will be.

But here is the part they do not put in any report, the part I have carried sealed inside me for years like a stone in the chest, and it is the reason I am telling you all of this now, at this late hour, to whoever will hold the paper steady.

They did not leave.

* * *

The soldiers stayed three days.

Understand: the burning took hours. The killing, the worst of it, was over by dark of the first night. If the object had been punishment — if some rebel had truly passed through, if a shot had truly been fired from our lanes, if there had been anything at all behind the official word *insurgency* — then the work was done and they should have gone. Armies that punish move on. Even armies that murder move on; murder has a rhythm to it, and the rhythm ends.

These men stayed three days, and on the morning after the fire, they began going through the ashes. Not looting. I know looting; the coast knows looting; looting looks like pockets and bundles and men glancing over their shoulders. This was different. This was methodical. They moved house to house, ruin to ruin, and they collected paper.

Ledgers. Letters. Baptismal rolls from the chapel — the chapel had burned, but the iron chest in the vestry had held, and they pried it open with axes and emptied it into sacking. Debt receipts from the company's own factor, who had kept an office above the boathouse and whose papers recorded eleven years of advances and repayments and interest owed by every diving family on the coast. Land claims — the old ones, the handwritten ones, from before the company standardized its forms, the ones our grandfathers had signed with X marks witnessed by priests who were also dead now. Correspondence. Petitions. The minutes of the village council, such as it was, going back forty years, in which our elders had complained, formally and politely and again and again, about the prices paid for pearl shell, about the water rights, about the company's surveyors moving boundary stones.

Every scrap of it went into sacking, and the sacking went into a boat, and on the third day a barge came from Meridian Point and the boat's cargo was transferred under guard, and I watched it happen from the rocks above the bay with my hands bleeding from gripping stone.

A boy I knew — Paulo, fifteen, half-mad with grief even then — started down toward the beach, and I caught him and held him and he fought me like an eel, and I whispered to him the only true thing I had: *"If you die here, no one is left who saw."* He stopped fighting. He cried into my shoulder without any sound at all, which is the worst crying there is, and we watched the barge take our history out past the second reef, over the pearl beds, into the deep channel.

That was the second burning. You understand now? The first fire burned the houses and the people. The second burning was quieter and more thorough, and it burned the *proof*. Because a massacre with documents is a crime that can be named, tried, answered. A massacre with no documents is a disagreement between accounts — and the men holding the pens hold the account.

I did not understand all of that at seventeen, crouched on the rocks. I understood it later, in pieces, over years. But I understood enough, that third day, to do the one clever thing of my life.

Before the soldiers finished, before the last sack was tied, I went back into what remained of the chapel vestry. The iron chest had been emptied, but chests have corners, and corners hold things, and there — wedged behind a rusted plate where the hinge had torn loose — I found what the soldiers' search had missed: the parish register's loose insert pages, the ones Father Anselmo used between bound volumes. Births. Deaths. Marriages. Eleven years of Kessel Bay's names in a dead priest's careful hand, including, dated fourteen months before the fire, a baptismal entry I will recite for you whenever you ask, because I made myself memorize it the night I found it, word for word:

*Baptized this day, in the chapel of Kessel Bay, an infant daughter of the family whose name is entered in the previous volume, witnesses present and signifying.*

Father Anselmo had grown cautious, near the end. He had begun writing around names instead of writing names — I believe he sensed what was coming, the way animals sense weather. Those insert pages were the only complete record of who lived in Kessel Bay that survived the second burning, and I sewed them into the lining of my jacket with sail thread, and I have worn them against my skin through six years and two changes of jacket, re-sewing each time, and I will wear them until they bury me in them.

* * *

Why am I telling this to you now? Because you asked, and because asking has become rare enough that I cannot afford to refuse it. Because the armistice is coming, they say, and with it the historians — the official ones, the ones with measuring instruments and government seals, who will arrive on ships and write down what happened to Kessel Bay in a hand that will outlive us all. And I know — I know the way I knew at the clerk's window, hearing the latch — what their hand will write. It will write *insurgent activity*. It will write *regrettable casualties*. It will write a number smaller than the truth, and it will not write a single name, because the names are ash and the ash is scattered and the registers are in a barge's hold or a company vault or a fire, wherever such things go to be unwritten.

Unless someone speaks.

Paper burns. I have watched it burn; I have helped carry it burning out of a vestry. But memory — memory must be killed, and I am difficult to kill, and there are others. Divers who were out past the second reef that morning and saw the gunboats' colors. A clerk at Meridian Point who kept carbons when the carbons were ordered destroyed, and who has not slept properly since. Fishermen who watched the barge and marked its heading. We are scattered and frightened and poor, and every one of us has been warned, in one fashion or another, that witnesses on this coast have short lifespans. I do not care. Sixty-one names — past a hundred names — sit in my chest beside the register pages, and they have waited six years for a hand willing to hold the pen, and I am done waiting politely.

There is a woman in the port town — they speak of her in the low voices people use for dangerous things — a diver, indentured, who has been asking questions. Asking them innocently, they say. Asking them the way a person asks who does not yet know what the answers cost.

If she comes to me, I will tell her everything I have told you, and more, and slower, and I will swear to it on the register pages themselves.

And if the historians arrive first — well. Let them bring their seals and their instruments. I have something their seals cannot stamp out. I have the smoke of forty cookfires in my memory, each one different, and I can walk that lane with my eyes shut still, and I will walk it aloud, name by name, for as long as there is anyone left to write it down.
