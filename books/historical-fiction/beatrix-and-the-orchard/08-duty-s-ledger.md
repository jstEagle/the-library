## 8. Duty's Ledger

The Meridian Trading Company kept its truths the way the sea kept its drowned: weighted, sunk, and formally declared lost.

Elias Vane had learned this in his first month at the registry, when he was still young enough to believe that a ledger was a promise rather than an alibi. His instructor then had been a dried stick of a man named Corbitt, who smelled of sealing wax and camphor and who taught him, above all else, one principle: *A record is only as permanent as the authority behind it.* He had repeated it like scripture. For six years Elias had recited it back to him on every audit, every certification, every quiet afternoon when the ledgers lay open before him like fields waiting for the scythe.

He no longer said it aloud. But he heard it still, in Corbitt's voice, in his own voice, in the dry rustle of paper — and on the morning that Mr. Halloran sent for him, he heard it so clearly he could almost taste the wax.

The director's office sat at the top of the company house, above the counting rooms, above the registry where Elias worked among the shelves, above even the balcony where the factors took their tobacco and watched the harbor with the fond attention of men watching money learn to swim. The stairs were teak, worn pale down their centers by decades of ascending ambition. Elias climbed them slowly, carrying his spectacles case in one hand and a folder of routine certifications in the other, though he knew — had known since the note arrived at dawn, unsigned, bearing only the director's initial pressed hard enough to tear the paper — that none of the routine would survive this meeting.

Halloran was at his window when Elias entered. The man had a back built for offices: broad, unhurried, the posture of someone who had never once needed to look over his shoulder because there was always someone behind him doing it professionally. Beyond the glass, the port glittered in its morning lie — masts like a winter forest, water the color of hammered tin, light lying across everything like a hand smoothing a bed.

"Vane." He didn't turn. "Sit."

Elias sat. The chair was harder than it looked, which he suspected was deliberate. Furniture in this building, like architecture in the colony entire, was designed to remind you of your temporary status.

"You've completed the coastal registry reconciliation?"

"Provisionally, sir. There are gaps."

"There are always gaps." Halloran turned then, and Elias saw that he was holding a single file, thin, tied with the grey ribbon reserved for closed matters. Closed. Elias's stomach performed a small, cold arithmetic of its own. "Kessel Bay."

"The Kessel Bay parish register," Elias said carefully. "Population rolls, baptisms, deaths. It came through my office two years ago when the coastal parishes were consolidated."

"It did." Halloran laid the file on the desk between them, but did not push it across. A courtesy of distance; you came to it yourself or you did not come at all. "It has since been reviewed. Amendments have been made. Your task is to certify the amended version as the original record of the parish."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, a clerk laughed at something; the sound rose through the floorboards thinned and strange, like a voice heard underwater.

"Certify it as the original," Elias repeated. It was not quite a question. Questions were permitted; repetition was merely comprehension, and comprehension was safe.

"The original suffered damage. Damp. Vermin. The consolidated copy is now the authoritative text." Halloran's voice had the flat patience of a man explaining tide tables to a passenger. "You'll note the discrepancies are minor. Some entries duplicated. Some struck. The population figures for the settlement have been adjusted to reflect—"

"Adjusted to reflect what, sir?"

A pause. Outside, a gull screamed once and thought better of it.

"To reflect accurate enumeration," Halloran said. "Prior counts were inflated. Squatters, transients, persons of no fixed trade. The amendment corrects an administrative error. That is all this is. That is all this will be remembered as." He pushed the file across the desk at last. "You are the certifying archivist of record, Vane. Your signature makes it true."

*Your signature makes it true.* There it was, Corbitt's catechism, worn smooth from handling. Elias looked down at the file without opening it. He could feel its thinness through the covering — how few pages remained inside compared to the register he remembered, that fat, salt-stained, lovingly irregular book kept by some parish curate with bad handwriting and worse spelling, who had recorded every birth at Kessel Bay for forty years as though each one mattered precisely because no one else would ever bother to.

"I'll need to compare against my working notes," Elias said. "For the certificate of provenance."

"Your working notes were also affected by the damp."

The sentence arrived without inflection, the way a knife arrives without music. Elias understood then the full shape of what was being asked, and understood too that Halloran knew he understood, and understood further — this was the true cruelty of it — that Halloran respected him enough not to insult him with pretense beyond a certain minimal thickness.

"The armistice commission arrives within the month," Halloran went on, returning to the window. "There will be historians attached to it. Men who read records the way magpies read a landscape — looking only for what shines. A clean registry serves the peace. A registry full of inflated numbers and irregular entries invites questions, and questions invite delay, and delay costs lives that are living now, Vane, not lives that are hypothetical or past." He turned. His face, in the slanted light, was not unkind. That was somehow the worst discovery of the morning. "I am asking you to do your duty as you have always done it. Accurately. Completely. As instructed."

*As instructed.* The third clause, the load-bearing one.

"Yes, sir," Elias heard himself say.

His own voice sounded distant to him, official, the voice of a man signing for a delivery. He gathered the file. It weighed nothing. It weighed more than anything he had ever carried down those pale-worn stairs.

At the door, Halloran spoke once more, not unkindly, not kindly at all: "Your career has been exemplary, Vane. I expect it to continue."

The threat wore the clothes of a compliment so well that a lesser man might have worn them home and slept in them. Elias climbed down the teak stairs with the file under his arm and felt, with each step, a hairline crack travel up through him — not loud, not dramatic, just the sound ice makes before dawn on a frozen river, the sound that means the surface has begun to doubt itself.

* * *

He certified the registry at four o'clock that afternoon, because delaying it would only draw attention, and attention was the one commodity the registry did not stock.

He did it properly. That was the obscenity of it — that he did it *properly*. He verified the seal impressions against the company die-book. He confirmed the pagination. He wrote out the certification in his own careful hand, the small copperplate script that three superiors had praised across six years: *Examined and found consistent with the authoritative consolidated record. Certified this day by Elias J. Vane, Archivist of Record, Meridian Trading Company.* He signed it. He pressed the blotter down over his name and lifted it, and for a moment his signature hung there on the blotting paper in reverse, ghostly, backwards, like something written in a mirror world where the opposite of everything here was true.

Then he filed it. That was the discipline of his trade, and it held him upright through the worst hour: file, label, shelve, log. The falsified register slid into its place among the coastal parish records between Tanama Point and Little Harbour, indistinguishable from honest paper to any eye but his, and even his eye only because he knew what had been removed — the way you know a face in a crowd is missing only after you have counted everyone twice.

He remembered the original. That was his curse, or his training; they had become the same thing. He remembered sitting with it two years ago in this same room with the shutters half-drawn against the heat, the register open on his knees, its margins crowded with the curate's annotations. *Twins, the Ocampo girls, God give the mother rest.* *Fever year — see loose sheet.* *Baptized at sea, father a diver, name given as Marisol.* Forty years of a village rendered in ink and care. He remembered the population figure because figures stayed with him the way tunes stay with singers: three hundred and eleven souls at last full count. He had written the number into his reconciliation summary himself, in this room, with this pen.

The amended register recorded ninety-four.

Two hundred and seventeen people, erased by an administrative correction. Not killed — the register made no claim about what had happened to them, that was the elegance of the method — merely corrected into a state of having been miscounted all along. Ghosts inverted: instead of people who died, people who had never fully existed. History did not even need to bury them. It needed only to stop writing their names down.

Elias sat at his desk until the counting rooms emptied and the lamps were lit, and he told himself he was staying late out of diligence. He was a man who lied well on paper and badly to himself, and he knew within a quarter hour that he was lying, and continued anyway, because the alternative to the lie was to stand up and walk somewhere he could not yet imagine walking.

By nine o'clock the building was silent except for the night watchman making his rounds on the far side, boots ringing on stone with the regularity of a slow clock. The registry was dark but for the single lamp Elias had trimmed and lit at his desk, its flame standing very straight in the windless air, throwing his shadow huge and wavering across the shelf-tops like a second, larger archivist haunting his smaller self.

He unlocked the deep drawer where he kept his personal correspondence files — innocuous, mixed in with old audit memoranda — and took out the folder he had prepared without admitting to himself that he was preparing it. Inside: forty sheets of good rag paper, a fresh nib, his traveling inkwell. And beneath these, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp the way one wraps anything precious in this country, because damp was the official story now and irony demanded the precaution —

— his notes. The ones that had *also suffered damage*, according to the director; meaning, the ones Halloran believed no longer existed, or had not thought to ask about, or — Elias allowed himself no comfort on this point — had deliberately left in place, a small test of loyalty tucked into a drawer like a lure beneath a float. He considered this possibility honestly, turning it in his mind the way he turned a doubtful signature to the light. If it was a test, copying the original would end him: dismissed, deported, perhaps worse in a port where witnesses had recently developed a statistical tendency toward drowning. If it was not a test, then the only other copy of the true Kessel Bay register outside his memory was the memory itself, and memory, he had come to understand in the past weeks, was a currency in this town that certain men were actively debasing.

He thought of Marguerite Thornbury.

She had come to his office ten days ago with her questions dressed as small talk, and he had watched her lie to him — skillfully, beautifully, the way a diver enters water, so clean you hardly mark the moment she leaves the air — and he had found himself lying back, and then lying *beside* her, two strangers swimming parallel through a conversation made entirely of misdirection, and the astonishing thing, the thing he had turned over every night since, was that he had trusted her more in that hour of mutual deceit than he trusted any truthful colleague in this building. Two people lying well enough to trust each other. He had gone home that evening and looked at his reflection in the washbasin water and seen a man at the beginning of something whose name he did not yet know.

She had asked, in her oblique way, about Kessel Bay. About records. About whether paper burned in the registry or only in the quarters. And he had answered in his oblique way, and neither of them had said anything at all, and both of them had heard everything.

If she was right — if the child, if the whispers from the divers' quarter, if the missing parish file and the amended numbers and the delegation of historians already booking passage meant what he now believed they meant — then the register in his memory was not merely an archive. It was testimony. Three hundred and eleven names, and every name a person, and every person entitled to have existed, which was, he suddenly understood, the whole of the matter, the entire ethics of his profession compressed to a single sentence: *people are entitled to have existed.*

The lamp hissed. He dipped the nib.

He began with the cover notation, the curate's title line, *Register of Baptisms, Marriages, and Burials, Parish of Kessel Bay, 1871–1909*, and then he worked backward through the ledger as he remembered it, page by page, entry by entry, in a fair hand that he deliberately altered — slightly, expertly, the way a forger alters — so that the copy would read as a working transcription rather than a duplicate, one archivist's study notes, deniable, personal, the kind of thing a man might keep for professional pride. He wrote the names of the dead and the names of the not-dead-either-merely-erased: the Ocampo twins, Marisol the diver's daughter, old Ferro the boatwright whose burial the curate had annotated *weighed anchor at last*. He wrote the number three hundred and eleven at the foot of the final page, and beside it, unable to stop himself, unable to prevent his hand from doing the one honest thing left available to it, he added in the margin:

*Original count per source document prior to amendment. Witnessed and transcribed from memory and prior notes by E.J.V., who certifies nothing but remembers everything.*

He looked at the sentence for a long time. It was insubordinate. It was, in the language of his profession, uncertifiable. It was the first sentence he had written in six years that was entirely, nakedly his own.

He did not strike it.

Outside, the watchman's boots passed along the corridor and faded. The harbor breathed somewhere in the darkness, hauling at its moorings. Elias wrote on into the small hours, page after page, his wrist aching, the lamp burning low, resurrecting a village in ink — and it was near dawn, his eyes gritty, the last pages nearly done, when he heard the footsteps again.

They stopped outside the registry door.

They did not belong to the watchman; they were lighter, quicker, and they paused — a hesitation of exactly the length a person takes when listening to determine whether a lamp is burning behind a locked door. Elias sat absolutely still, his pen raised above the final line, the wet ink shining, and in the silence he heard the handle turn once, softly, finding the lock, and release.

The footsteps retreated down the corridor, unhurried, and Elias Vane sat alone in the yellow circle of lamplight with three hundred and eleven souls drying on the paper before him, understanding that whatever this was — duty, treason, conscience, love, or merely the first crack widening — it was no longer possible to pretend it was diligence.

Someone knew he was here. And come morning, he would need to decide whether that someone was a danger to be feared, or the only ally he had — and whether the woman with the careful lies and the laundry-line secrets had sent those footsteps, or whether something far colder had just taken its first measure of him.
