## 9. The Historians Arrive

They came on a Thursday, in the grey hour before the market opened, when the harbor was still the color of unpolished tin and the gulls had not yet found their tempers.

Marguerite saw the ship first from the drying loft, where she had gone to shake out sheets that smelled of salt and someone else's wealth. The vessel sat low in the water and rode high on its own consequence—white hull, brass fittings, no cargo cranes, nothing aboard but itself. A passenger ship, or something pretending to be one. It wore no company colors. It wore no colors at all, which was its own kind of flag.

By the time she carried the basket down the hill, the pier was already filling with men who did not belong to it. She counted eleven of them coming down the gangway, and she counted because that was what this port had taught her: count everything, trust nothing, assume every number will be used against you later. Eleven men in pale linen and dark coats, none of them dressed for work, all of them carrying cases—leather boxes with brass corners, the kind that held instruments. Some held instruments for measuring land. She would learn these held instruments for measuring everything else.

The harbormaster met them with his cap in his hands, which he never did for company men, and certainly not for the company men who actually ran this coast. So whoever stood at the head of the delegation outranked the company, or intended to be seen as though he did.

Marguerite set her basket down by a bollard and became furniture. It was a skill her father had named once, half-laughing, when she was small: *the pearl diver's gift, little bird—we are invisible below and invisible above.* He had meant it as a joke about how the wealthy looked through divers as though they were fish with ledgers. She had not understood until recently that invisibility could be turned into an instrument of her own.

She watched the head historian—a tall man with a beard trimmed like a hedge and spectacles that caught the early light so his eyes were always hidden—walk the length of the pier without once looking at the water. That was the first thing she noticed, and it stayed with her like a splinter: *he did not look at the sea.* This was a port. The sea was the whole of it—the reason for the town, the trade, the indentures, the drowned fathers, the pearls still sitting in their oysters under forty feet of cold green light. And this man walked past all of it as if the water were wallpaper.

He stopped instead at the customs house, took out a small book bound in green leather, and began writing. One of his assistants unrolled a surveyor's tape along the quay and another photographed the warehouses with a camera on a wooden tripod, the shutter making a sound like a knuckle cracking.

*They are measuring us,* Marguerite thought. Not the coastline. Not the tonnage. *Us.*

She had seen appraisers work before. When her mother's hut was assessed for the poll levy, a man had walked its perimeter with a stick and a notebook, tapping walls, pricing rot. He had never once asked her mother a question. The house was worth what he said it was worth, and what it was worth determined what they owed, and what they owed was simply true thereafter. Truth arrived by instrument and left by receipt.

Now she watched eleven men do the same to history.

Aurelia was with the widow Sefa two doors down, sleeping in a borrowed cradle among the smell of bread, and for once Marguerite was grateful for the arrangement, because her arms felt wrong without weight in them and she needed both hands to grip the edge of her fear.

Because here was the thing she had been circling for days now, since Tomas Reyes first spoke Kessel Bay aloud in the dark of the drying loft, since Elias Vane told her, in his careful archivist's voice, that registries could be certified false by the same hands that made them true: *history is not what happened. History is what gets written down, by whom, and when.*

And these men wrote for a living.

* * *

By midmorning the town had rearranged itself around the visitors the way water rearranges around a dropped stone. The Meridian Trading Company's factor, Mr. Calloway—a man whose smile Marguerite trusted less than his frowns—had thrown open the customs house's upper rooms and installed the historians there with tea, lamps, and a view of the harbor that cost nothing and conveyed everything.

Word moved through the port the way word always moved: through laundry lines, through the fish stalls, through the ears of children paid in copper to listen at doors. By noon everyone knew. *Historians. Official ones. Come to write down what happened, so it can be finished properly.* There was even a proclamation, posted on the customs house door in three languages and nailed with unnecessary force:

*BY COMMISSION OF THE ARMISTICE DELEGATION: All persons possessing documents, correspondence, records, or testimony pertaining to the events of the coastal districts during the late hostilities are requested to submit such materials to the Historical Commission, Room 4, Customs House, for evaluation and preservation. Submission is voluntary. Preservation serves peace.*

Marguerite read it four times.

*Voluntary.* She touched the word with her finger as though checking whether it was wet. In this port, voluntary meant the same thing it had always meant: you may volunteer, or you may explain to the proper authorities why you did not. And *preservation serves peace*—she turned that phrase over too, feeling its edges. It was beautiful language. It was language built like the pier pilings: solid, load-bearing, and sunk deep enough that no one would ever see what it rested on.

Room 4. She knew Room 4. Every clerk, diver, and dockhand knew Room 4, because Room 4 had been the company's records room back when the company bothered pretending its papers belonged to anyone else's eyes. Now it had new brass numerals and a queue outside it by afternoon—mostly frightened people surrendering letters from sons at the front, receipts, photographs, anything that might look like loyalty to the losing side. They came out lighter and looking relieved, and Marguerite wanted to grab each of them by the shoulders and say: *you have just handed your memory to the men who will decide whether it ever existed.*

But she said nothing. She bought her rice. She nodded to the constable. She walked home the long way, past the customs house, and made herself study the building as calmly as a woman studying weather.

Three entrances. The rear loading door where the clerks hauled ledgers. The side stair to the upper rooms, guarded now by a soldier who spent most of his attention on the harbor girls. The front steps, broad and public, where the queue formed. Windows on the seaward face, high, barred—no. Not barred. Painted shut, maybe. She would need to know. She filed it away in the ledger room of her mind, next to the layout of the company archive, next to the tide tables, next to the name *Kessel Bay*, which lived there now permanently, like an entry written in ink that would not wash.

That night she fed Aurelia mashed banana and watched the baby grip her own toes with the fierce concentration of someone learning that her body belonged to her. Six months old, nearly. She had begun to make sounds with intent—not words yet, but arguments. *Ba. Ba-ba. Da.* She argued with her spoon. She argued with sleep. She argued, silently and devastatingly, with a note that had instructed Marguerite to forget her face.

"Nobody," Marguerite told her, wiping her chin, "is going to forget your face."

Outside, the lights burned late in the customs house upper rooms. She could see them from her window, three windows' worth of lamplight, and she imagined the historians inside with their green books and their cameras and their instruments, drafting the world as it would henceforth be remembered. Somewhere in those rooms, someone was deciding how many people had lived in Kessel Bay. Someone was choosing between *incident* and *engagement*. Someone was weighing whether a village deserved a paragraph or a footnote or silence, and the scales they used had been calibrated in a capital city she would never see, by men who thought distance was the same thing as objectivity.

* * *

She went to find Elias the next morning, taking the route through the company yard that let her pass as errand-bound, a bundle of washing over her arm that contained, beneath the shirts, nothing more dangerous than shirts. But the yard was full of unusual motion, and motion meant information moving faster than usual, so she slowed and listened with her whole skin.

Clerks were hauling crates from the registry annex—actual registry files, bound in the company's dull brown board, stacked onto a handcart bound for the customs house. She counted nine crates before a supervisor waved her along, and she memorized what she'd seen the way she memorized everything: nine crates, brown bindings, registry series, carted toward Room 4 at seven-forty in the morning.

They weren't waiting for people to bring documents in. They were collecting them. Voluntarily, presumably, the way the tide collects a sandcastle.

Elias was in the archive, alone among his shelves, and when she entered he looked up with an expression she was beginning to know well—relief crossed immediately with caution, a man glad and afraid in the same heartbeat, which she suspected was exactly what her own face did whenever he appeared.

"You've seen them," he said. It wasn't a question.

"I've seen the crates." She set the washing down. "Nine. Registry series."

"Eleven, as of yesterday evening." He rubbed his eyes; he had the look of a man who had not slept so much as negotiated briefly with sleep and lost. "Coastal district records. Shipping manifests. Labor rolls." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on a table. "Labor rolls, Marguerite."

She understood at once, and the understanding went through her like cold water through cloth. The labor rolls listed the indentured. The labor rolls recorded who had been contracted out of which villages—and Kessel Bay, before it burned, had supplied divers. If the rolls survived intact, they proved families had existed. If the rolls were *adjusted* first—

"Then the commission won't be lying," she said slowly. "They'll just be reading what they're given."

"Now you understand my profession." He said it without humor. He crossed to the window and looked out at the harbor, where the historians' ship rode at anchor, white and smug. "I trained six years to believe that an archive is a temple. That paper keeps faith when people can't. And in three weeks I have watched—" He stopped himself. Started again, quieter. "There's a phrase we use. *Provenance.* The chain of custody of a document. Who held it, who marked it, who kept it safe. A document's provenance is its honor." He turned to look at her, and behind the spectacles his eyes were tired and burning at once. "These men arriving on that ship don't want provenance. They want a clean chain. A short one. Document appears, document says what the treaty needs it to say, document is preserved. History with the middle cut out."

"The middle being us."

"The middle being everyone in this town who remembers otherwise."

She came to stand beside him at the window. Below, the delegation's assistants were photographing the harbor again, the tripod planted on the quay like a small patient animal. She watched the shutter crack and thought of Aurelia's face, unphotographed, unwritten, existing nowhere on any official paper in the world except in the memory of a handful of people who could be disappeared as easily as a file in a brown crate.

"Elias," she said. "How long does the commission stay?"

"Through the signing. Perhaps a week after, to compile." He hesitated, and she watched him decide to give her the rest. "The treaty text arrives within the fortnight. Once it's signed and sealed, the record closes. Whatever the commission certifies becomes the account. There's no appeal. There's no second volume. There's just the book."

"The book," Marguerite repeated.

"One book. Written once." His voice dropped. "After that, everything else is rumor. Everything else is *a woman claims*. Do you understand? Right now, today, while the ink is wet and the commission is still hungry for material—right now, testimony can still get in. It can be contested. It can be entered as objection, as appendix, as dissent. Ugly channels exist. But they close." He tapped the windowsill with one finger, once, like a judge's gavel. "When the treaty signs, they close forever."

Marguerite stood very still.

She had spent her life believing that history happened elsewhere—to admirals, to ministers, to people whose names got printed. She had believed the port was scenery, that divers were background, that events passed overhead like weather belonging to other skies. She had built her whole survival on that belief, the way a barnacle builds on a hull: attached, low, unnoticed.

But the historians had come *here*. To her port, her pier, her water. They had brought their instruments and their green books and their appetite, and they were measuring the only place she had ever lived, preparing to write the sentence in which her world would appear—or not—for the rest of time. History wasn't happening to other people anymore. History had landed at the pier with eleven men and a camera, and it was walking up the hill, and it was taking names.

And it was fast. That was the part that turned her blood to harbor water. The commission worked in days. Testimony took longer. Trust took longer. Getting Tomas Reyes to speak at all had taken weeks of patience and the weight of a child's abandoned face. The divers who had seen the crates go into the shallows off Kessel Bay spoke slowly, in whispers, in fragments—they were not men who could be hurried, and there was no time to be unhurried.

"They'll finish before we've barely begun," she said.

"Yes."

"Then we start tonight."

Elias looked at her then fully, and whatever calculation he had been performing since the day she walked into his archive asking innocent questions, she watched it resolve into something simpler and far more frightening: agreement.

"Not the loft," he said. "Not yet. Too soon after the crate removals—they'll have watchers on known gathering places. Small first. One witness. One statement, witnessed properly, dated, signed. We build a spine of paper, and we build it faster than they can bind theirs."

"One witness," Marguerite said. "Tomas."

"Tomas." He exhaled. "And Marguerite—" He caught her arm, gently, above the elbow, and for a moment neither of them mentioned it. "Whatever you carry into this, carry it lightly. Papers can be found. Papers can be read. From tomorrow onward, assume every conversation you have is happening in front of Room 4."

She walked home through the late-morning heat with the washing bundle against her hip, past the customs house where the queue had reformed, past the proclamation flapping on its nails. At the corner she stopped and looked back once, up at the three bright windows, at the silhouettes moving behind the glass—bent, industrious, harmless-looking, like men doing sums.

They were doing sums. Sums of villages. Sums of the dead. And the total would be published, bound, taught, believed, and the sea would keep the rest, and the sea tells nothing unless someone dives.

Marguerite shifted the bundle higher and climbed the hill toward home, toward Aurelia, toward the narrow window with its view of the commission's lamplight burning all night like a fever. Let them write, she thought. Let them measure and photograph and certify. Memory was slower than ink but it had deeper roots, and she knew now—knew with the certainty of a woman standing at the starting line of a race she had not chosen—that the only question left was whose version reached the page first.

Behind her, the shutter cracked again on the quay. Another picture of the town, taken for the record.

She did not look back a second time.
