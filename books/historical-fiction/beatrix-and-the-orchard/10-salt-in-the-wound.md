## 10. Salt in the Wound

Tomas Reyes had a way of knowing weather before the sky did. The old divers said it was in his blood—his mother had read the monsoon in the taste of well water—but Tomas knew the truth was simpler and crueler: when you have survived one burning, every wind that arrives afterward smells faintly of smoke.

He was mending nets behind his brother-in-law's drying shed when Marguerite Thornbury came down the shingle path, and he felt the change in the air before he saw her. A stillness. The gulls lifting all at once from the breakwater as though someone had whispered a warning only they could hear. Then her footsteps on the stones, careful the way a person is careful when approaching a dog they suspect has been beaten.

She stopped at the edge of the net-strewn ground and waited. That was good. He liked that she waited. Most company people—and she wore her indenture like a uniform whether she meant to or not, in the straightness of her back and the ledger-neatness of her speech—most of them did not wait.

"You found me," Tomas said, not looking up from the net.

"Old Sela told me where you'd be." She came closer, picking her way between the cork floats and the coils of rope, and crouched down so that they were nearly level, so that she was speaking to him and not down at him. "She also told me you don't talk about Kessel Bay."

"Sela talks too much for a woman who's lived this long."

"She said something else. She said if anyone could make the company listen, it wouldn't be her—it would be paper. Paper with names on it. Sworn."

Tomas pulled the needle through the mesh. His hands were steady. He made a point of his hands being steady; it was the one part of him he still commanded absolutely. In the two years since the bay had burned, his hands had never once shaken, and he took a fierce and private pride in that, the way other men took pride in their children or their gardens.

"And what would I be swearing to, exactly?" he asked. "That soldiers came in company boats with company lanterns? That they burned forty-one houses? That when the people ran to the water—the water, señorita, the place where every child in that village learned to swim before they learned to pray—the boats were waiting in the shallows?" He tied off a knot. Pulled it tight. "I could swear to that. Anyone left alive could swear to that. There are eleven of us left, by my last count. Nine now, maybe. People drift."

"Eight," Marguerite said quietly. "Sela makes eight."

The number sat between them on the shingle like a stone neither wanted to touch.

Tomas set down the netting needle at last and looked at her properly. She was younger than he remembered from the market—twenty-two, twenty-three—and there was salt-spray in her hair and a sunburn beginning across her nose that spoke of someone who had been diving recently, paying down her debt pearl by pearl the way penitents pay down sin. He had watched her at the fish stalls sometimes without meaning to. There was a look on her face he recognized because he had worn it himself once: the look of a person who has decided the world is a machine that grinds forward regardless, and who has arranged themselves accordingly, out of the gears' way.

Something had changed that arrangement. Something small and loud and wrapped against the cold, if Sela's gossip was to be believed.

"Why do you care?" he asked her. "You're not from Kessel Bay. You're not from anywhere the company hasn't built. Your history gets carried out on their ships same as everyone's. Why stick your hand into their ledgers?"

Marguerite was quiet for a long moment. Beyond them the tide was turning, the water sliding back off the flats with a sound like a long exhalation, exposing the pale ribbed sand, the stranded jellyfish, the small crabs righting themselves after the sea's rough handling. Somewhere out past the headland a ship's horn sounded, low and mournful, and Tomas's chest tightened around an old ache—the sound always brought it, the sound of departures, of things being carried away to elsewhere where they would become stories instead of wounds.

"A baby was left at my door," she said finally. "Three weeks old, maybe. With a note telling me to forget its face."

Tomas went very still.

"The seal on the note was half-melted," she went on, her voice low and even, the voice of someone reciting facts to keep from screaming. "Company wax. And the baby—" She stopped. Started again. "There's a family resemblance, Sela says. To people from Kessel Bay."

The net fell from Tomas's hands.

He stood up—not quickly, nothing about him was quick anymore, but with a deliberateness that Marguerite, to her credit, did not flinch from—and walked to the waterline, and stood looking out at the channel where the light lay on the water in long silver bars. The wind came off the sea carrying iodine and rot and the far-off tar smell of the company wharves, and Tomas Reyes breathed it in and understood, with the terrible clarity that visits a man perhaps twice in a life, that the thing he had spent two years burying was about to be dug up whether he consented or not. And that he had a choice only in the manner of it: dragged up, or climbed out of willingly.

"There was a family in Kessel Bay," he said, without turning around. "The Callistas. Fishers, pearl-culture, some trade in mother-of-pearl buttons. Prosperous enough to matter and not prosperous enough to be protected. Doña Rosa Callista was seven months pregnant in the autumn of the fire." He heard Marguerite's breath catch behind him and pressed on, because if he stopped now he might never start again, and he had promised himself—he had promised Doña Rosa's ghost, or memory, or whatever it is that remains of a woman when her house falls on her—that if the moment ever came he would not flinch. "Her husband Emilio had been petitioning the company over the button contracts. Petitioning loudly. There was talk of him going to the colonial office, of papers, of accounts. Emilio kept accounts like a priest keeps scripture." A bitter half-laugh escaped him. "Honest man's mistake. He thought numbers would protect him. Numbers, records, receipts. He thought the company respected paper."

"And the baby?"

"The baby was born early. Two weeks before the raid, in the house of the midwife, which is why—" His voice failed him on the first attempt, and he tried again, and the second time the words came out flat as slate. "Which is why the child wasn't home that night. The midwife's cottage stood apart from the village, up the creek. When the burning started, the midwife ran with the infant into the mangroves. She got as far as the next fishing settlement before her heart gave out, or her fear did—I never learned which. Someone else must have carried the child onward. Someone who decided the safest thing to do with a witness too young to testify was to leave it where no one would think to connect it to anything."

He turned then and looked at Marguerite Thornbury, and what he saw in her face made his throat close. It was not pity. It was recognition—the expression of a woman who had already done the arithmetic, who had held the child against the note, against the melted seal, against everything the port knew and refused to say aloud, and who had arrived at the sum before ever walking down this shingle.

"Its face," Tomas said softly. "Doña Rosa's brow. Emilio's chin. I'd know it, if you put the child in front of me. God help me, I'd know it, and I haven't let myself know anything in two years."

* * *

They walked up above the drying sheds, to the rocks below the ruined signal tower, where the wind scrubbed their words apart almost before they were spoken and no listener could crouch close enough to catch them. Tomas chose the place; it was habit now, this selection of wind-scoured venues, this instinct for places where eavesdropping required effort. He had learned the hard grammar of survival in Kessel Bay's aftermath: never say a name indoors. Never meet the same man twice in the same street. Never write anything down.

And here was this indentured girl proposing exactly that. Names. Dates. Sworn statements. Paper.

"You understand what you're asking," he said. They had settled among the rocks, facing each other, the sea booming hollow under the ledge. "Not whether the truth deserves telling. Whether we survive its telling. Those are different questions, and only one of them has a comfortable answer."

"I'm asking you to tell me what happened. What happens after is my risk to carry."

"Is it?" He shook his head slowly. "Then let me educate you, since the company taught you ledgers but not this arithmetic. In this port, a witness has the lifespan of milk in summer. You've seen how it goes. Old Ferro, who used to dive the eastern shoals—he talked, once, to a shipping magistrate about cargo that never appeared in manifests. Three weeks later he drowned in four feet of water inside his own front door, a man who'd swum since before he could walk. They called it drink. Nobody asked why a drunkard drowns upright with both boots dry." He ticked the examples off on his fingers, unhurried, each one a stone laid on a grave. "Marisol the customs clerk, who questioned a consignment of 'agricultural tools' that smelled of gun oil. Fever, they said. Fast fever. And little Anselm at the coal weigh-station, who counted barges going out lighter than they came in. He didn't even drown. He simply stopped existing—room emptied, name struck from the rolls, his landlady paid a month's rent and told to forget the face."

Marguerite flinched at that phrase. *Forget the face.* Tomas noted the flinch and did not soften.

"You see? I keep the list," he said. "Not on paper—in here." He touched his temple. "Paper can be burned, and they burn paper very well in this town. It's practically a civic art. But memory—" he tapped his skull again, harder, as though knocking at a door— "memory has to be killed out of you one witness at a time, and each killing costs them something. Risk. Money. Attention. So far they've considered us cheap enough to spend on. The question is whether we make ourselves expensive."

"I intend to," Marguerite said. "Very expensive."

The wind gusted, flattening the coarse grass between the rocks, and for a moment the whole hillside hissed like the sea itself had opinions. Tomas studied her. Underneath the sunburn and the salt-stiff hair there was a stubborn architecture to this girl's face, something load-bearing, something that had already decided things he was still arguing about.

"Ask me, then," he said.

She took out a notebook—a real one, bound in oilcloth, the kind clerks use—and a pencil stub worn to the length of a finger. But she did not open it. Instead she folded her hands and looked at him with an attention so complete it was nearly a physical weight, and said, "Tell me about the night of the fire. Start wherever you need to start."

So he started where he needed to start, which was three days earlier, with the arrival of two strangers in gentleman's clothes who registered at the company guesthouse under names that fooled no one, and with the way the harbor pilot—who owed Tomas a gambling debt—had mentioned, casual as spit, that two company steam launches had been coaled and provisioned though none were listed to sail.

"On the night itself, I was on the water," Tomas said. "That's my whole testimony and my whole damnation. I was three miles north, setting lines, when I saw the glow. You grow up learning to read fires. A kitchen fire is orange and lazy. A lamp knocked over is yellow, climbing. This was white at the base. White fire means accelerant. Means somebody brought the heat with them, in tins." His hands had begun, at last, very slightly, to shake—and he watched them shake with a kind of distant astonishment, as though observing a betrayal by an old friend. He pressed them flat against his knees. "I rowed back fast as the current allowed. By the time I came round the point, the village was fully alight, all of it at once, which no accidental fire does. Accidental fire spreads like gossip, corner to corner, roof to roof. This had been lit in six places simultaneously. I remember thinking that with a strange clarity—counting the seats of fire, six, seven—because my mind, you understand, was refusing the obvious conclusion and hiding in arithmetic."

He told her about the boats in the shallows, black hulls against the burning water, and the men with lanterns, and how the lanterns were the worst detail, worse than the rifles, because lanterns meant method—meant men moving house to house with light, checking their work, thorough as tax collectors. He told her about Doña Rosa's door standing open with its lintel scorched, about the church bell half-melted in the tower rubble, about the smell, which he refused to describe and Marguerite, to her lasting credit, did not press him to.

"And then," he said, and here his voice dropped into a register she had not yet heard from him, flat and cold as ballast stone, "then came the second burning. Not houses. Paper."

Marguerite's pencil, hovering over the page, went motionless.

"They came back at dawn. Same boats, different men—or the same men in different clothes, I couldn't swear which and I won't pretend otherwise. They went through the ashes with rakes and tongs, and they gathered. Not valuables—you'd expect looters, but these weren't looters, they left the silver spoons in the ruin of the Recta house. They gathered paper. Deeds. Contracts. Ledgers. Emilio Callista's famous accounts. The parish register—baptisms, marriages, deaths, forty years of a community written down. Everything that proved Kessel Bay existed and owned things and had signed things and could therefore sue or be sued, inherit or be taxed, be *legally* anything at all. Into canvas sacks. Onto the launch. Gone by seven o'clock, and the official story by noon: squatters' fire, spread from a cooking stove, tragic, regrettable, forty-one souls unaccounted for—unaccounted, señora, because they had accounted for the accounts first."

He watched her write, the pencil stub racing, and felt something loosen in his chest that frightened him more than any lantern had. Hope, he suspected. Or its cousin. He had spent two years teaching himself that hope was a luxury item sold only to people with exit visas.

"Do you understand what they did?" he asked her. "Anyone can kill people. Killing leaves bodies, graves, mothers, songs. Mothers sing, always; it's the one thing they can't confiscate. But if you destroy the record—if you unwrite the births and the deeds and the marriages—then in fifty years Kessel Bay is a story fishermen tell, and in a hundred years it's a confusion in an old map, a smudge, a place-name misspelled. They aren't just burning a village. They're burning the proof that it deserved to exist. And now"—he nodded at the notebook, at her ink-stained fingers— "now there are negotiations coming, and historians on a ship somewhere writing the official version, and every day that passes, the lie sets like concrete. They will write that Kessel Bay never properly existed. Mark me. That's the sentence being drafted right now, in first-class cabins, over brandy."

"Then we write faster."

"We die faster, you mean." He laughed, short and harsh, a gull's laugh. "Yes. All right. Yes. I'll give my statement. I'll sign it. I'll swear it before whatever notary you can find brave enough or bribable enough. But hear the terms, Marguerite Thornbury, and take them seriously, because I mean every word." He leaned toward her, and the wind chose that moment to drop, so that his words arrived whole and unmistakable. "I am a dead man the moment I put my name to paper. Understand that. Not possibly. Not at risk. *Is*. The only question is whether the death buys something—whether the paper outlives me, whether it lands somewhere they can't rake it into a sack and dump it in the channel. If you cannot promise me that the record will survive the witness, then don't ask me to speak, because I have done my dying already once, and I won't repeat it for a document that ends up lining a company drawer."

Marguerite set down the pencil. For a moment she looked out at the water, at the light breaking and re-forming on the chop, and Tomas saw her go somewhere inward—to a doorstep, perhaps, to a bundle that cried in the night—and when she turned back her eyes were the eyes of someone who had already wagered everything and was merely waiting for the table to catch up.

"I can't promise," she said. "Anyone who promises you safety is selling something. What I can promise is multiplication. Every statement gets copied. Every copy lives in a different house. We build the truth the way the sea builds an island—grain by grain, in enough places that no single sack can hold it all." She paused. "And there's more. Sela says the divers have seen things in the shallows off Kessel Bay. Things that went over the side of a launch one night. Crates."

The word landed in Tomas like a hook finding flesh.

Crates. Canvas sacks, he had seen—but sacks sink slow, and paper floats a while, a horrible snowfall of ash and documents on morning water. But crates, weighted crates, crates that never reached the company warehouse—

"Your divers," he said slowly, "have seen the sacks' big brothers. At the bottom."

"At the bottom, off the point, in twelve feet of water, where the company assumed no one would ever look because no one would ever dare ask what Kessel Bay's paper was worth."

Tomas Reyes sat among the wind-flattened grass with his shaking hands pressed to his knees and felt, rising in him with the tide below the rocks, the two oldest opposing forces in the human chest: terror, cold and specific, painting for him in perfect detail the four feet of water in old Ferro's doorway—and beneath the terror, older and deeper, the thing the company had never managed to burn out of the people of that coast, the thing that was in the salt itself.

"Twelve feet," he said. "Our divers work thirty with baskets and breath-holds. Twelve feet is nothing. Twelve feet is Sunday." He looked at her, and this time he smiled, and the smile was a terrible thing, thin as a blade and bright as one. "Get me your list of witnesses, girl. And find out which of the divers saw those crates, and where, and on what tide. Because if Emilio Callista's accounts are lying in the shallows where they threw them—"

He stopped. Out beyond the headland, another ship's horn sounded, and this time, for the first time in two years, the sound of departure did not feel like the whole story.

"—then the evidence of the massacre is sitting in plain view," he finished, "under water nobody owns, waiting for someone mad enough to go down and get it. And I think, Miss Thornbury, judging by the fact that you came to me at all, that you may know exactly who that someone is."
