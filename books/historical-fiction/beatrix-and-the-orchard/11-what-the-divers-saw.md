## 11. What the Divers Saw

The tide was going out when I came down to the drying sheds, and the whole harbor smelled of salt and rope and the particular sweetness of fish left too long in the sun. I had Aurelia strapped against my chest in a sling of old sailcloth, her breath warm against my collarbone, and I had told myself all the way down the hill that this was an ordinary errand—that a woman might walk to the water with a baby on her back for no reason at all, that no one would look twice.

No one did. In this port, everyone looked twice and said nothing. That was its own kind of courtesy.

Tomas Reyes was waiting by the third shed, mending a net he had no intention of finishing. He'd been mending it for three days, I suspected, the way a man mends a net when what he is really doing is waiting to see who comes looking for him. His hands were brown and cracked as old leather, and they never stopped moving, even when his eyes went still.

"You brought the little one," he said, not looking up.

"She doesn't like being left."

"Smart child." He tied off a knot, examined it, untied it. "The others are here?"

"In the loft. Old Sefa, the Ferro brothers, Marisol's boy. Seven, counting you and me."

"Eight." He nodded toward the shadow between the sheds, where an ancient woman sat wrapped in a blanket despite the heat, her face folded into itself like a walnut. "Mother Anouk insisted. She says she's been silent sixty years and she's done with it."

I knew Mother Anouk by reputation only—a diver from before the company, from before the war with the northern fleets, a woman who had supposedly held her breath long enough to bring up three pearls in a single dive and then never dived again. Her hands were curled around a clay cup she wasn't drinking from. When our eyes met, hers were pale as skimmed milk and just as opaque, and yet I felt seen through, all the way to the bone.

* * *

We gathered in the drying loft because it was the only room in the port where paper hung openly—sheets of split fish gut stretched on frames, membranes dried thin enough to write on, and above them the racks of pearl sacks that swayed in any draft like slow hanged men. The light came through gaps in the slatted walls in long dusty blades. It was a room built for patience: things hung here until they were ready, until the sea was out of them and only the shape remained.

That, Tomas said, was why we should meet here. "Paper remembers what it's for," he told me once, and I had written the phrase down inside my sleeve where no clerk would think to search a washerwoman.

Aurelia slept in a laundry basket lined with sacking. Sefa, who was seventy if he was a day and whose chest was a map of old dive-scars, lowered himself onto a crate with the groan of a ship taking swell. The Ferro brothers flanked him, twins so alike that even their silences matched. Marisol's boy—Dio, sixteen, already diving deeper than his father ever had—sat apart by the window with the restless energy of youth that has not yet learned what the water takes.

"I'll say it plain," Tomas began, "because plain is all I've got." He looked around the room, counting us the way he'd count divers before a descent. "You know what happened at Kessel Bay. You saw the smoke. Some of you—" his eyes touched Sefa, touched his brother twins, "—saw more than smoke. And you've kept it down like a stone swallowed whole, six years now, because keeping it down was how we stayed alive. I'm asking you to bring it up."

Silence. Outside, a gull screamed and something answered it.

Sefa spoke first, as perhaps we all knew he would. His voice was gravel rolled smooth by decades of current.

"You want to know what's under the water at Kessel Bay." It wasn't a question. He turned his scarred palms upward, studying them as though the answer might be written there in the language of old wounds. "Everyone asks about the village. The village is ash and story. But the bay—" He shook his head slowly. "The bay keeps its own ledger."

"We dove it," said one of the Ferro brothers—I never learned which, and after a while it stopped mattering; their testimony came out of both mouths like one voice with two echoes. "After the burning. Not right after—we weren't fools. Three weeks. Long enough for the soldiers to be gone, short enough that..." He trailed off, and his brother finished:

"Short enough that what they sank was still findable."

My heart was beating hard against my ribs, and I felt Aurelia stir against me, as though she could hear it, as though the quickening pulse passed through the sailcloth into her small sleeping body.

"What did you find?" I asked. My voice sounded very small in that high dusty room.

Sefa looked at me for a long time. Then at Mother Anouk. Then back.

"Crates," he said.

* * *

He told it slowly, the way old divers tell everything—with pauses for breath even on dry land, as if speech itself were a descent and he needed to ration his air.

"The soldiers burned the houses first. That was the show, the part meant to be seen from the ridge road. Burning is loud and bright and makes men feel like gods for an afternoon. But burning isn't thorough, whatever they tell you. Paper flies. Paper survives. A page can blow clear of a fire and land in a ditch with half its words still legible, and words are dangerous things when they're legible." He tapped his temple. "So after the fire, there was a second work. Quieter work. They went through what was left—the registry office, the mission house, the trading post—and they took every document. Every deed, every baptism record, every bill of sale, every letter. Everything with names on it."

"Why?" Dio asked from the window, and his voice cracked on the word, boyish and furious.

"Because a dead village is a tragedy," Sefa said. "But a village that never existed is a clerical matter." He let that sit in the room like a stone dropped into still water, and I watched the ripples move across every face. "You can mourn a massacre. Who do you mourn when there's no record anyone lived there? No parish rolls, no property lines, no contracts? The historians will come—they always come, after—and they will open the files and find nothing, and nothing is very easy to write about. Nothing takes half a page."

"They sank them," I said. Not asking. Seeing it now, the whole shape of it, the way you see the bottom of a lagoon when the light comes round right.

"They sank them." Sefa nodded. "Crates. We counted eleven from above—the shallows off the point are clear enough, ten fathoms, you can see pale shapes on a bright day if you know to look. Pine crates, iron-banded, weighted with chain and ballast stone. Too deep to burn cheaply, too heavy to haul inland, and water"—he smiled without any humor in it—"water has always been the company's favorite filing system. Where I come from, when a man wants a secret drowned, he doesn't dig. He dives."

"How far out?" I asked. And hated myself a little for how quickly the question came, how hungry it must have sounded.

Tomas caught it anyway. Of course he did. He'd been watching me the way a man watches weather.

"Not far," he said carefully. "Half a mile past the point, maybe less. Ten, twelve fathoms. Shallow water, Marguerite. Shallow enough that a good diver—" and here he paused, and the pause had my name written all over it, "—could reach it. Could reach it and come back."

* * *

The Ferro brothers took up the thread together, the way they seemed to do everything, one beginning and the other completing, sentences braided like rope.

"We went down three times that first day. The crates are stacked—not scattered, stacked—in a hollow between two coral heads, like someone intended to come back for them and never did, or like someone wanted them findable by the right person and no one else. The iron bands are rusting through. The pine is soft. Give it another few seasons and the sea will have them entirely."

"But the chains will last," Sefa said. "Chains mark the spot forever."

"What was in them?" I asked.

The brothers looked at each other. For the first time, their unity broke—one of them looked away, out the slats toward the harbor, and the other stared down at his own hands.

"Kessel Bay was a registry town," the one who remained said quietly. "You understand? Not a fishing village, not a pearl camp. A registry town. The company kept its coastal records there—deeds, indentures, births, deaths, the census rolls, the tax schedules. Eleven years of the coast's memory, all in one place because it was convenient, because Kessel Bay was central and defensible and full of clerks who owed their positions to the company." He swallowed. "When we pried one crate open on the bottom—it was already splitting, the sea had started its work—we got the lid up maybe a hand's width. Enough to reach in. Enough to feel."

"Feel?" I whispered.

"Paper doesn't rot fast in cold deep water. Not right away. It goes soft, it goes gray, but the ink—" His voice failed him briefly, and his twin finished for him from the window, having crossed the room without either of us marking it, so that suddenly both faces were close to mine, identical and grave.

"The ink holds. We brought up four sheets, tucked in the lining of a weight belt where no soldier thinks to look, because who searches a diver's ballast? Four sheets. Deeds, mostly. Land grants with signatures. One was a baptism roll—forty-one children of Kessel Bay, born across nine years, each name written in a fair clerk's hand." He stopped. In the laundry basket, Aurelia made a small sound in her sleep, a sigh, the sound of someone surfacing gently from somewhere deep, and every head in the loft turned toward it, and every face in the loft changed.

Because forty-one children. Because a note pinned to a blanket saying *forget this face*. Because a wax seal half-melted and a baby who was not merely abandoned but *erased*, filed away in flesh instead of paper, and here was the proof that the company drowned its records rather than burn them because drowning was deniable, because the sea keeps no receipts—

"We brought the sheets to no one," the first brother said. "We read them on the beach, the four of us who dove that day, and then we burned them ourselves. On the sand, with driftwood."

"Why?" The word tore out of me. "Why would you burn—"

"Because keeping them was death," Sefa answered, mild as tide. "And showing them was death, and we weren't ready. Six years ago nobody was ready. There was no armistice coming, no historians sailing in, no table where a piece of paper might finally mean something. There was only the company and the water and us." He spread his ruined hands. "But we remembered what was written. That's the thing about divers, girl—we hold our breath and we remember. Forty-one children's names. Two of the deeds bore the signature of a magistrate named Corvane. The baptism roll was countersigned by a company factor whose initials were H.M. We remember."

* * *

Mother Anouk had not spoken since we began. Now she set down her untouched cup, and the small sound of clay on wood silenced the room more completely than shouting would have.

"I dove Kessel Bay before any of you were born," she said. Her voice was dry as the membranes hanging overhead. "Before the company. Before the registries. We dove for pearls and we dove for food and we dove because the water was ours, and when I was young the bay had no point and no crates and no names written anywhere except in mouths." She rose, unsteady as a new foal, and stood among us, tiny and immense. "I am telling you this so you understand what they stole. Not paper. Paper is only the skin of a thing. They stole the fact that these people existed. My people traded with Kessel Bay when it was a market town with three hundred souls and a well that tasted of iron. I drank from that well. And now the official history will say there was a skirmish, perhaps, or a quarantine, or nothing at all—nothing, most likely, because nothing is cheapest—and the well will fill with sand, and no one will drink there again, and no one will know to grieve."

Her milk-pale eyes found mine, and though I don't believe she could truly see me, she looked at me as though I were the only thing in focus in the whole world.

"You're the one keeping the child," she said.

The room went absolutely still. Even the hanging sacks seemed to stop their sway.

"Yes," I said. There was no point denying it in front of eight people who had clearly decided the truth among themselves weeks ago.

"Then you understand," she said, "that what lies under that water is not evidence." She said the word *evidence* with contempt, flicking it away like a fly. "Evidence is for courts, and courts belong to the victors. What lies under that water is a birthright. It is the child's inheritance lying in eleven crates on the sea floor, and every day the sea takes more of it, and every day the men who sank it sleep a little sounder." She leaned closer, and her breath was salt and age. "Bring it up. Bring it up while there is still something to bring."

* * *

They talked after that—details, depths, currents, the timing of tides, which days the patrol boat ran its lazy circuit and which days it didn't—and I sat among them holding my sleeping daughter and listening with half my mind while the other half stood already at the edge of the water, already breathing deep, already counting fathoms.

Ten. Twelve. I had gone deeper for pearls. I had gone deeper for less than a handful of rice. Every diver in that room had.

It would be simple, physically. That was the obscene part. The hardest dives of my life had been for objects worth pennies; this one, this impossible, world-altering dive, would be easy as breath. Half a mile past the point. Find the chains. Follow them down into the green silence where eleven crates of a murdered town lay stacked like cordwood, holding everything the victors wanted drowned—names, deeds, signatures, the entire proof that Kessel Bay had been real, that its people had been real, that my daughter's face belonged to someone, to some family, to some line that existed on paper before the paper was chained to the dark.

Hope rose in me the way breath rises at the end of a long descent—uncontrollably, painfully, the body insisting on life regardless of what the mind has decided. I hadn't permitted myself hope since the night I found the bundle on my step. Hope was expensive. Hope got people disappeared. And yet there it was, breaking the surface of me, gasping.

Tomas crouched beside my crate. Up close I could see the white threading through his beard and the way his hands, for once, had gone still.

"You're going to dive it yourself," he said. Not a question either.

"Tomas—"

"Don't." He held up one cracked palm. "Don't waste your breath telling me it's foolish, because it is, and you'll do it anyway, and we both know I can't stop you. Just hear one thing first." He glanced around the loft—at Sefa, at the brothers, at Mother Anouk standing sentinel by the wall. "Those crates have been down there six years because no one dared. Tomorrow or the day after, a diver goes down alone, before dawn, on a slack tide, and comes up with whatever the sea will give her. If she's seen—" his voice dropped to almost nothing, "—if she's seen, Marguerite, it won't be soldiers who come for her. It will be quieter than that. So don't be seen."

Outside, the light was failing. The blades of sun through the slats had gone copper and thin, and the hanging membranes glowed like rows of small dim lanterns, like pages waiting for words.

Aurelia woke in her basket and looked up at me with those dark, unfathomable eyes—the face worth forgetting, the face no one was supposed to remember—and I lifted her, and she gripped my finger with her whole fist, fierce as an anchor.

Tomorrow, I thought. Before dawn. Slack tide.

The sea had kept their secret six years. It was time somebody asked it to give them back.
