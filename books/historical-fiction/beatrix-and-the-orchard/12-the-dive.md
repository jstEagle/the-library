## 12. The Dive

The sea before Kessel Bay was the color of a bruise healing—purple at the deep places, green-gold where the morning light came slanting through, and everywhere that terrible, patient blue that had taken my father and given nothing back but the shape of my own grief.

I had not meant to dive it myself.

That was the lie I had been telling since before dawn, since I'd borrowed Sefina's boat with a promise of pearls I had no intention of gathering, since I'd wrapped the lead weight belt around my waist with hands that would not stop their small tremor. I had told myself I would only point. Only mark the place on a slate and let the men go down—the divers who owed me favors, the old ones whose lungs had learned to forgive the sea its debts. But the men had looked at the water off Kessel Bay the way sailors look at a lee shore in a rising wind, and Tomas had said what we both already knew: *No one will dive there but you, girl. They've seen strange boats in the shallows all week. Anyone they catch asking questions ends up in the ledger as drowned.*

So it would be me. It was fitting, perhaps. My father had taught me to hold my breath until the world went soft at its edges, until sound became the ocean's own slow heartbeat and my blood sang with the need for air. He had taught me that fear was a thing you swallowed like salt water—unpleasant, unavoidable, survivable if you didn't fight it. What he could not have taught me, because he never lived to see it, was how to be afraid of something worse than drowning.

*The boat,* I told myself. *Watch the boat.* I had anchored it in the lee of the black rocks at the bay's northern jaw, where the water ran clear over a floor of broken coral. From there the burned village was a smudge of pale walls behind me, and the wreck site—if the divers' stories were true—lay forty fathoms out, where the shelf dropped away into deeper blue.

I stripped to my shift. The morning wind came off the water and found every inch of me at once, and I stood shivering in it longer than I should have, letting my body understand what it was about to do. Somewhere behind me a kingfisher dropped from the rocks like a thrown stone. Somewhere behind me, miles behind me now, Aurelia was asleep against Sefina's shoulder, smelling of milk and laundry soap, her small fist curled around a button she had decided was hers.

She was why I was here. She was why I did everything now.

I put the slate and grease pencil into the oilskin bag, checked the knife at my belt out of habit rather than need, spat three times into the sea—my father's ritual, an apology to the water for what you were about to take from it—and slid over the gunwale.

The cold closed over me like a door.

---

Down, then. Down the way my father had taught me, one long pull at a time, equalizing my ears against the pressure that wanted to fold them inward, letting my weight belt do its honest work. Ten fathoms the light turned silver. Twenty it turned to rumor. At thirty I passed through a cold stratum that raised the hair on my arms even through the numbness, and below that the world became what it always becomes on a deep dive: a cathedral with no god in it, vast and hushed and utterly indifferent to the small warm creature moving through it.

My heartbeat slowed. This was the gift the sea gave its divers, the one mercy in all its taking: the deeper you went, the quieter everything became, including yourself. Up there on the surface there were ledgers and armistices and historians measuring the town like appraisers at an estate sale. Down here there was only the pulse in my ears and the pale columns of my own breath rising above me, silver balloons ascending toward a sky I could no longer see.

I found the crates before I found the shelf edge.

They had not sunk neatly. Whatever barge or lighter had carried them out here had dumped its cargo carelessly—or hurriedly—and the crates lay scattered across the sand slope in a fan, some upright, some split open on the coral like seedpods, spilling paper. Even after all these months the paper was there: sheets of it drifting in the current like the ghosts of moths, pale and soft-edged, dissolving slowly into the water that had become their grave. A registry page turned end over end beneath me, its ink long since bloomed into gray clouds. I watched it settle and thought: *this is what they intended.* Not fire this time. Water. Water keeps no witnesses, they believed. Water is where things go to be forgotten.

My air was burning low. I chose the largest crate—one that had wedged itself between two coral heads, protected from the worst of the current—and worked at its lid with my knife. The wood had gone soft. The nails came away like teeth from old gums. Inside, bundled and bound with tarred cord that had kept the water mostly at bay, lay documents. Dozens of them. Land grants. Shipping manifests. A marriage register with Kessel Bay written across its spine in faded clerk's hand.

And near the bottom, sealed in wax inside an oilskin sleeve that someone—God knew who, God knew why—had bothered to wrap twice, a single dispatch.

I pulled it free. The oilskin had cracked along one seam and the paper within was sodden, translucent, the ink feathered almost beyond reading—but not quite. Not entirely. I held it up through the green murk, turning it toward the distant light, and read what I could:

*...by direction of the Hon. Marcus Whitcombe, Resident Commissioner... action at Kessel Bay concluded to specification... records of prior tenure destroyed per instruction...*

Whitcombe. The name meant nothing and everything—a name attached to a title, a title attached to a signature, a signature attached to an order that had turned a village into smoke and a population into a number in somebody's arithmetic. Here it was. Here, forty fathoms down, wrapped in oilskin by some hand I would never identify—some clerk, maybe, some soldier whose conscience had outweighed his obedience just once—was the thread that connected the company's clean white ledgers to the ash on the hillside.

The exhilaration hit me like surfacing too fast.

It came up through me in a great bright wave, and I actually laughed into my regulator of cupped hands, a burst of bubbles streaming silver past my face, and for one suspended moment I was sixteen again diving for stray pearls with my father watching from the boat, invincible, weightless, certain that the sea loved me. *I have it,* I thought. *We have it. Tomas was right, the divers were right—it's down here, it survived, they couldn't drown it—*

My chest was screaming. I had stayed far too long.

I stuffed the dispatch and two bundles of the best-preserved papers into the oilskin bag, cinched it shut, kicked for the surface—and that was when I saw the anchor chain.

Not mine. Mine lay off to the north, a pale line in the murk. This one hung from somewhere above and aft of me, iron links descending into the blue with a slack, patient curve, and at its foot, half-buried in the sand where it had dragged, lay a grapnel whose flukes had scored four fresh furrows through the crate field.

Someone had anchored here recently. Someone had come down and looked at what the sea was keeping.

Or someone was here still.

I hung in the water, weightless, my lungs two fists of fire, and listened with everything I had—not with my ears, which heard only the sea's indifferent rush, but with my skin, with the diver's animal sense for another body in the water. The current pushed gently against my left side. Above me the surface rippled with light, and through it, distorted into wavering ribbons, came the sound I had been dreading without knowing it.

An engine. Idling. Close.

---

I came up slow, the way my father taught me—never fast, never gasping, because a diver who breaks the surface in panic announces herself to everyone and everything within a mile. But every fathom of that ascent was agony, my body demanding air I refused it, and by the time my head broke through I was seeing sparks at the edges of my vision.

There were two boats.

Mine rode at its anchor by the black rocks, untouched, faithful, fifty yards north. Between me and it, blocking the channel like a hand across a doorway, sat a company launch—the flat-bottomed kind the clerks used for inspecting the pearl stations, flying no flag because it needed none. A man stood in its bow shading his eyes against the sun, looking directly at the place where I had surfaced.

For one long moment neither of us moved. The sea rocked us both. Gulls wheeled overhead, crying their thin cries, indifferent to everything.

Then I did the only thing there was to do. I became what I appeared to be.

I lifted my free hand in a fisherman's wave—easy, unhurried, the gesture of a woman gathering pearls on a morning like any other—and began to swim, side-stroke, steady, toward the black rocks and my own little boat. The bag I let trail behind me on its cord, low in the water, hidden against my hip by the angle of my body and the glitter of the surface. My legs screamed. My arms felt full of wet sand. I smiled at the man in the bow, and the smile cost more than the swim.

"Lost your grounds?" he called. His voice carried flat across the water. Young. A clerk's voice, not a fixer's—though I reminded myself that Tomas had been young once too, and that the men who burned Kessel Bay had surely started out sounding reasonable.

"My buoy line parted," I called back, and blessed my father again, for teaching me to keep my voice level in cold water. "Diving stray beds off the point."

He looked past me, toward the south, toward the crate field lying quiet under its fathoms, then back at me—at my face, my bare shoulders, the small wake trailing from my hip. "This is a closed station," he said. "Company waters. You shouldn't be here."

"I've dived this bay since I was a child," I said, which was true, and which was also the most dangerous sentence I had spoken in weeks. "Nobody ever told me the sea needed papers."

Something moved in his expression—not amusement, exactly. Assessment. He was deciding what I was. Behind him, at the launch's tiller, a second man had risen from the shade of the canopy and was watching me with the stillness of a heron over shallow water, and it was the second man my body understood first, before my mind caught up: the way he stood, feet apart, balanced to the boat's motion; the way his hand rested on the rail not casually but precisely, the way a man rests his hand on a thing he expects to use.

Fixer, then. Or worse.

"Name?" the young one called.

"Marguerite Thornbury," I said, and let the current take my voice out to them honestly, because a lie discovered here would drown me quicker than the sea ever could. "Meridian indenture, station seven. You're welcome to write it in your book."

I saw the flicker then—the small hesitation of a clerk confronted with paperwork. Indentures were sacred. Indentures were the spine of everything the company touched; a woman legally bound to station seven had a right to be in these waters the way a key has a right to its lock, and whatever suspicion had gathered in the young man's face now had to argue with procedure. Procedure won, as it always does, but slowly, grudgingly, and while it argued I swam.

The launch's engine turned over. It began to swing, ponderous, coming about—not toward me, I told myself, it's simply leaving, they simply came to look—but I counted strokes anyway, measured the widening gap between us in heartbeats, and when my feet found the rocks' submerged skirt I did not look back until I had hauled myself into my own boat and shipped the oars.

They were heading north. Toward the port. Toward the harbor master's office, where a clerk named Ovidio kept the rolls, where anyone with a company chit could ask a single question—*who dives station seven?*—and receive a single answer, complete with my contract number, my quarters, my debts, my whole reducible self reduced to a line in a ledger.

They had seen my face. That was the thought that arrived last and loudest, rowing hard for home with the morning wind in my teeth and the oilskin bag heavy under the pearl sacks in the bilge. They had seen my face, and I had given them my name to attach it to, and somewhere in the port's hierarchy of clerks and fixers and men who stood very still at tillers, someone would soon be connecting the two.

I thought of Aurelia's button-fist. I thought of the note that had come pinned to her blanket five months ago, its instruction simple as a knife: *forget this face.*

Row, Marguerite. Row.

Behind me Kessel Bay closed over its secrets, and ahead the port rose out of the heat haze, white and orderly and full of people who would want to know what a pearl diver had been doing in closed waters with a bag that did not clink like pearls.

By the time I passed the harbor light, I understood two things with perfect clarity. The first was that we had our dispatch—that Whitcombe's name now existed in ink, in duplicate, wrapped in oilskin beneath my floorboards, and that no amount of ledgers could unwrite it.

The second was that the forgetting had begun to reach for me.
