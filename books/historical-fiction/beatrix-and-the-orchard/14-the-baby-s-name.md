## 14. The Baby's Name

The monsoon had come early that year, and the port smelled of it—wet rope and brine and the sweet rot of fruit left too long in the market stalls. Rain came down in gray curtains that erased the harbor by degrees, first the masts, then the cranes, then the far breakwater, until the world shrank to what could be touched.

Marguerite sat on the floor of her room with the child propped against her knees, watching the water sheet off the eaves. The laundry was done. The ledgers she copied for extra credit against her debt were stacked and squared. There was nothing left to do but the thing she had been avoiding for six weeks, the thing that had been circling her like a gull circles a boat: the baby needed a name.

Not a secret name. Not a whisper-name, used only in the dark, only when no one else could hear. The child was four months old now, fat-thighed and loud-lunged, with eyes the color of tea held up to lamplight, and she had spent her whole life unnamed—or worse, named *no one's*, which is a kind of erasure that begins before a person can defend against it. Marguerite knew about erasure. She had watched her father go into the water a man with a name and come out of it an entry in a company ledger: *Thornbury, J. — diver — lost — no recovery required.* Four words and a dash. That was what the world did to people who let it do their accounting.

She would not do this child's accounting in someone else's book.

"You," Marguerite said, and the baby grabbed at her thumb with the fierce, unreasoning grip of the very young, "are going to be a problem for somebody someday. I can feel it."

The rain answered. The baby laughed at nothing, or at everything, the way infants laugh—as though existence itself were a joke told well.

Marguerite had been turning names over in her mind the way she turned shells over on the tideline, looking for the pearl inside them. Ordinary names first, safe names, the names of laundresses and fishwives, names that would let a girl pass through a port unnoticed. Then bolder ones, borrowed from saints and steamships. None of them fit. A name chosen for safety was just another note instructing her to forget a face.

It came to her on the third night of rain, of all times, when the lamp had burned low and the child would not sleep and Marguerite, desperate, had begun reciting the only long thing she knew by heart—the cargo manifests she memorized in the ledger room, tonnage and silk and pearl-shell, the litany of everything that left this place and never looked back. She got through rubber and copra and mother-of-pearl before her voice gave out, and in the silence the baby stared up at her with those tea-colored eyes, utterly awake, utterly attentive, as if waiting for the part that mattered.

*Gold,* Marguerite had been about to say. *Aurelia.* The word had been sitting in the manifests all along—*aurelian bar, specie, sealed*—and she had copied it a hundred times without once hearing it. But hearing was different from reading. Said aloud to a small damp head in a dark room, the word stopped being freight and became music: *Aur-el-ia*. Golden. The color of lamplight. The color of a pearl's warm heart, the ones divers called fire-stones because they glowed when you held them to the sun.

"Aurelia," she said, testing it.

The baby kicked. It may have been wind. It may have been fate. Marguerite chose to believe it was assent.

"Aurelia," she said again, firmer, and felt something turn over in her chest like a boat righting itself. "Aurelia Thornbury." She hesitated over it—giving the child her own name, the name chained to her contract, the name printed on a debt—but then she thought: no. Let the name mean something other than what the company made it mean. Let it be hers first. "Aurelia Thornbury. There. Now you exist somewhere besides my arms."

She said it aloud a third time, formally, the way witnesses said things, because she had learned in these past weeks that saying a thing aloud in front of another person made it more real than paper ever could. Paper burned. Paper drowned. Paper could be rewritten by men who had never seen Kessel Bay. But a name spoken between two living souls—that was testimony of the oldest kind.

The rain kept falling. Marguerite held her daughter—her daughter; the word still startled her, still arrived in her chest like a fish striking a line—and let herself, for the first time since the bundle appeared on her step, imagine a future.

* * *

It went like this:

In the future, the indenture was paid. She counted it sometimes, that arithmetic, the way other women counted beads on a rosary: eleven months and some odd days if she kept diving, eight months if the pearl prices held, six if Elias managed—no, she did not finish that thought, not yet, not even in imagination. Six months, perhaps, with help she was almost ready to ask for. And then the paper would be handed back to her, or more likely burned in the yard behind the company house with the other expired contracts, and she would walk out of the gate a free woman with a free child, and the guards at the gate would have no more power over her than the tide.

In the future there was a small house. Not in the port—never in the port, where every street was a company street and every window a company window—but up the coast a little, where Tomas said the fishing was good and the company's writ ran thin. A house with a door that latched. Two rooms and a porch. Aurelia growing brown in the sun, learning the names of fish before the names of ledgers.

In the future, Marguerite dove when she wished and not otherwise. She would miss the water if she never went back under; that was the strange truth of it, the inheritance her father had left her that no contract could claim. The sea had taken him and yet it had also given her everything she knew about holding her breath, about patience, about how the deepest things keep their own records whether anyone surfaces to read them or not. In the future she would dive for beauty and not for debt, and she would teach Aurelia to hold her breath, and Aurelia would surface with her hair streaming and her eyes bright and shout something joyful across the water, and there would be no one to tell her to stop.

In the future, the truth about Kessel Bay was known. She could not make this part detailed—it resisted detail, the way dreams resist description—but it was load-bearing, this piece, holding up all the rest. She could not build a future on a foundation of silence. She knew now what she had not known when she found the bundle: that the child at her breast was not merely abandoned but *edited*, cut from the record by men with wax seals and signing hands. And Marguerite had learned enough about editing to know it worked both ways. If a face could be struck from history, it could be written back in.

She looked down at Aurelia, finally asleep, her small fist still knotted in Marguerite's sleeve, and thought: *I will give you a documented life if it takes the rest of mine.* Not just a name whispered in a wet room, but a name entered somewhere permanent, witnessed, defensible. Someday you will be asked who you are, little one, and you will be able to say it—to a schoolmaster, a magistrate, a priest, whoever asks—and the saying will hold.

That was the future. It was thin as eggshell, and she knew it. Outside, the monsoon hammered the port flat; inside, a woman with eleven months of debt and a stolen infant and a waterlogged dispatch hidden in a false bottom of her trunk was dreaming of porches. But she had been a diver too long to be embarrassed by hope. Hope was what you breathed before the descent. Without it, you panicked at depth, and panic was death. You took your breath, you filled yourself with it, and then you went down into the dark and did the work.

* * *

On the fifth day of rain the sky cleared without warning, the way it does in that latitude—all at once, like a curtain snatched back—and the light that came through was so clean and gold that Marguerite laughed out loud, alone in her room, and lifted the baby to the window.

"Look," she said. "Look what was here the whole time."

The harbor gleamed. Water stood in every rut and gutter, mirroring masts and clouds, so that the whole town seemed to float doubled upon itself. And everywhere, everywhere, gold: gold on the wet rooftops, gold in the puddles, gold in the tea-colored face of the child in her arms.

"Aurelia," Marguerite told her, pointing at all of it. "That's yours. All of that. Don't let anybody's ledger tell you different."

They went out, because after five days indoors they both needed the world. The market was reopening, stalls shaking off their tarps, vendors shouting prices at the returning light, and Marguerite walked through it all with Aurelia riding her hip in the sling, openly, in daylight, for the first time. It was a decision she had made in the night: no more hiding the child among laundry, no more flinching when neighbors passed. Secrecy was a debt too, and debts grew interest. Better to be seen, to establish the simple visible fact of a woman and her baby, before anyone thought to ask when the baby had arrived or from where. Grief made good cover. Everyone in the port knew Marguerite Thornbury had lost her father to the sea; a woman who had lost so much taking in an orphan would surprise no one. She rehearsed the story as she walked: found at my door, no note worth mentioning, poor mite. Half of it was even true.

Old Mrs. Quezon at the vegetable stall pressed a hand to her mouth. "Ay, Marguerite. Whose is this one?"

"Mine now," Marguerite said, and heard how the words sounded—like ownership, like assignment—and corrected herself. "I've named her Aurelia."

Mrs. Quezon's eyes went soft and sly at once, the way old women's eyes do when they decide not to ask. "Golden," she said, in her own tongue, and then, touching the baby's cheek with one flour-dusted finger: "Good name. Strong name. My grandmother was Aurelia. She buried two husbands and outlived three governors." She looked at Marguerite over the baby's head. "The name will know what to do."

Word traveled the way it always did in the port, faster than ships. By evening the fruit-sellers knew, and the net-menders, and the boy who delivered ice. *Marguerite Thornbury has a daughter. Named her Aurelia.* People nodded at her in the street with a new curiosity, and some of the nods were warm, and some were merely counting, and she accepted both, because belonging in a port town has always been a bargain with watchers.

And then, near dusk, walking home along the seawall with the light going amber and the child asleep against her shoulder, she saw the two men.

They were not company men—not in dress, anyway—and they were not soldiers. They wore the neat, heat-wilted suits of clerical travelers, and they stood at the rail of the seawall consulting a notebook, and one of them gestured with his pen toward the harbor while the other wrote. Historians, she thought. The delegation had been in town ten days now, measuring and interviewing, polite as appraisers. There was nothing strange about two of them admiring a view.

Except that they were not looking at the view. They were looking at her.

Not rudely. Not long. One glance, unhurried, the glance of a man confirming something already suspected, and then back to the notebook. The pen moved.

Marguerite did not quicken her step. She had been a diver too long for that, too; sudden movement at depth draws things up from below. She walked at her usual pace, past them, nodding good evening the way any woman would, and they nodded back with perfect courtesy, and the pen kept moving, and only when she had turned the corner by the chandlery did she allow her heart its panic, one wild beat after another, all the way home.

She barred the door. She fed Aurelia by lamplight with steady hands and did not think about the notebook, and then, having fed her, she thought about nothing else.

*A woman with a baby*—that was all they had seen, all they could know. Unless. Unless the note at her door had not been the only copy of its instructions. Unless someone had told them to watch for exactly this: a woman, a child of the right age, tea-colored eyes. *Forget this face*, the note had said, and Marguerite had understood it once as an instruction to her. Now, watching the lamplight move on her daughter's sleeping face, she understood it might also have been a report—a description filed elsewhere, awaiting confirmation.

She put Aurelia down in the laundry basket, as always. She checked the false bottom of the trunk, as always. The dispatch was there, damp-warped and precious, the name she must never say aloud sleeping in its folds.

Then she sat down at her table, took a fresh sheet of paper, and did the bravest ordinary thing she had ever done: in her best ledger-hand, the hand the company had trained into her, she wrote at the top of the page, *Aurelia Thornbury, born at sea, no date given*—for even invented histories deserve ceremony—and beneath it began setting down everything she knew, so that if the men with the notebook came in the night, the record would already exist, in duplicate, in a hiding place only she and one other soul alive could name.

She was three lines in when she heard footsteps stop outside her door, and a knock, soft and familiar, and Elias Vane's voice, low and wrong somehow, saying her name through the wood.

"Marguerite. Open the door. They've taken Tomas."
