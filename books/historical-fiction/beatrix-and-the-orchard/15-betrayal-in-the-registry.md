## 15. Betrayal in the Registry

Tomas Reyes had learned to measure men by the way they held their hands.

His father, before Kessel Bay burned the first time, had held his hands open, palms up, as if everything he owned might be taken from him at any moment and he had already agreed to it. The company men who came after the fire held their hands behind their backs, knuckles white against their own spines, as though their bodies knew what their mouths denied. And Silas Merrick—Silas, who had sat across from him in the drying loft three nights running and sworn on his mother's grave in Cebu that he would testify, that his testimony would be salt in the ledger of the world—Silas had begun holding his hands closed. Fists loose but closed, thumbs tucked inside like a man warming coins he did not want counted.

Tomas noticed this on a Tuesday, mending nets on the quay because mending nets was what he did now, what he had done since the bay gave back its dead and kept its documents. The morning was the color of unpolished pewter, and the harbor breathed its usual breath of tar and brine and diesel-thin smoke, and Silas came down the gangway of the *Corvina* with his fists curled and his eyes moving the way a man's eyes move when he is counting exits.

They had all been counting exits lately. It should not have surprised him. But surprise is a luxury of people whose villages burn only once.

"You're up early," Tomas said, not looking up from the net. The twine bit his fingertips; he welcomed the small honest pain of it. "The *Corvina* doesn't sail till the tide turns."

"Couldn't sleep." Silas's voice had a new texture to it, a smoothness, like wood that had been planed too many times. He crouched beside Tomas on the quay and watched the water with the expression of a man watching something he had recently learned to fear. "You ever think about how deep it is out there? Past where you dive. Past where anyone dives. All that dark, just going down."

"Every day," Tomas said. "That's why I stay near the top."

Silas laughed—a short sound with no floor to it—and stood, and walked away toward the fish market with his hands still closed, and Tomas sat very still among his nets and felt the first cold thread of certainty move through him the way cold water moves through a wetsuit seam: slowly at first, then all at once.

* *

He went to Marguerite that evening, because she was the center of the thing now whether any of them had agreed to it or not. The circle had formed the way driftwood forms against a piling—not by decision but by current—and Marguerite Thornbury, indentured pearl diver, keeper of an infant no one was supposed to see, had become the piling around which all of them collected.

Her room above the laundry smelled of camphor and milk and candle wax. Aurelia slept in a repurposed fish crate lined with flour-sack cloth, her small face slack with the absolute trust of the very young, and Tomas looked at that face and understood, in the marrow-deep way he understood tides, exactly what it meant to be evidence. He had been evidence himself for eleven years. He simply hadn't known anyone was keeping the record.

"Silas," he said, without sitting down. Marguerite had learned to read him; she set down the shirt she was folding and waited. "Something's changed in him. He's gone smooth. He talks about the depth of the water now. Men don't talk about the depth of the water unless someone's reminded them of it."

"Someone threatened him?"

"Worse." Tomas turned his cap in his hands. His father's cap, though his father had been dead so long the wool had forgotten his shape. "Someone *paid* him. Threats make men harder or they break them. Payment makes them careful. He walks like a man carrying something breakable that isn't his."

Marguerite was quiet for a long moment. In the crate, Aurelia sighed and turned her face to the wall, one small fist escaping the blanket—open, palm up, the way babies hold their hands, offering everything to a world that had offered her nothing.

"We have statements from six divers," Marguerite said finally. "Four clerks. Two survivors. If Silas has told the fixers how many of us there are, what we have, where we meet—"

"He knows about the drying loft."

"He knows about the drying loft."

The lamp guttered and steadied. Outside, the port made its nighttime sounds—the slap of hulls, the far-off argument of gulls over the fish waste, a constable's boots on cobbles, unhurried, official. Tomas thought of the drying loft above the chandlery: the tarpaper roof, the lines strung with nets, the table where they had laid out their statements like bodies for identification. Candle stubs. Sworn words. A community assembled out of paper and grievance and the stubborn animal need to have happened, to have been witnessed, to matter in the ledger even if the ledger's owners wanted them erased.

"We could move," he said. "Tonight."

"And go where? They know the port. We'd be moving from a room we've warmed to a room we haven't." She rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand and for a moment looked young, and tired, and afraid—then the moment passed through her like weather and she was Marguerite again, flint under ash. "No. We watch him instead. You're closest to him."

"I'm closest to everyone. That's my use, isn't it? I survived, so I talk to everyone, and everyone tells me things because I look like a man who has already lost everything and therefore cannot lose more." He heard the bitterness in his own voice and did not apologize for it. Some bitternesses are earned the way scars are earned.

"That's your use," she said gently. "It's also your grace. Most men can't tell the difference anymore. I think you can."

* *

Three nights later, one of the divers did not come.

Rosa Lim had been coming to the loft since the beginning—Rosa with her forearms corded like hawser rope, Rosa who could hold her breath four minutes and had once, off Kessel Bay, held it longer than that, longer than anyone should, coming up through the burning surface scum of the bay with her brother's body in her arms because no one else would go into the water that night. Rosa did not miss meetings. Rosa had said once, in her flat island Spanish, *I missed enough already. I will not miss one more thing.*

By ten o'clock Marguerite sent word through the laundry girl. By midnight seven of them stood in the drying loft among the hanging nets, which stirred faintly in the draft from the broken vent, so that the whole room seemed to breathe, seemed to listen.

"The *Sable Ann* went out this morning," said old Chao, who crewed on anything that sailed and heard everything the sea said. "Rosa signed aboard. Regular run, two days, pearl grounds past the point. She'll be back tomorrow evening."

"She didn't say goodbye to me," said her cousin, a boy of sixteen named Felipe who had begun attending the meetings silently, sitting by the door. "She always says goodbye to me. She says a diver who leaves without goodbye is asking the sea to keep the difference."

Superstition. The port ran on superstition the way engines ran on oil. But superstitions are just observations that grief has refined, and Tomas felt the boy's words settle over the room like fog.

"It's the storm season building," Chao offered. "Sea's restless. Could delay them at the outer anchorage."

"Could," said Marguerite.

But she didn't believe it, and none of them believed it, and the nets breathed around them in the dark like a congregation murmuring.

Tomas walked home along the waterfront the long way, past the customs house where the historians' delegation had established itself behind windows that now glowed green late into the night—green-shaded lamps, the color of offices, the color of decisions made about other people's lives by men in comfortable chairs. He had seen the fixers too. Not historians themselves, these; historians wrote. Fixers *arranged*. Smooth men in good linen who appeared at the fish market and the taverns and the jetty asking questions shaped like small talk. *Busy season for the boats. Your cousin's girl still diving? Heard there was trouble out Kessel way once—what do you remember, friend? Just for color, just for the record.*

For color. As if blood were pigment.

A figure detached itself from the shadow of the customs house steps and fell into step beside him, and Tomas's hand found the belaying pin he carried in his coat the way other men carried rosaries, and then he saw it was only Elias Vane—the archivist, the company man with the crack running through him like ice in spring, who had chosen, God help them all, to stand on their side of the line.

"You heard," Elias said. It wasn't a question. He walked with his collar up against the harbor damp, and in the gaslight his face had the gray precision of the paper he spent his days among. "About Rosa."

"Everyone's heard about Rosa. That's rather the point of making someone disappear."

"There's something else." Elias glanced back along the quay—empty, gulls, the slow pulse of a buoy bell—and lowered his voice. "An inquiry was filed today in the registry. Routine, buried in shipping manifests, the kind of entry nobody reads twice. A salvage claim. Unclaimed property recovered from coastal waters, section nine." He paused. "Section nine is Kessel Bay."

Tomas stopped walking. The buoy bell tolled somewhere out in the dark, patient as a priest.

"Salvage claim on what?"

"On crates." Elias's jaw worked. "Six crates, manifest says, recovered by persons unnamed, contents listed as *obsolete correspondence, pending destruction.* Filed under the seal of the historical commission. Which means—" he exhaled through his teeth— "someone is converting stolen documents into legal garbage. Once that claim is certified, those papers can be burned lawfully. Publicly. With the historians signing off. They're not just hiding what happened at Kessel Bay anymore, Reyes. They're building a machine to destroy the proof, and the machine is made of paperwork, and every gear in it will be stamped and filed and perfectly legitimate."

The harbor air filled Tomas's lungs and tasted of rust. He thought of the crates on the seafloor, the ones the divers had spoken of in the loft by candlelight—confiscated records dumped in the shallows, weighted and forgotten, the bay doing the victors' shredding for them. He thought of Rosa, four minutes of breath, rising through black water with her dead brother in her arms, saying *I will not miss one more thing.*

"You think she found something down there," he said. "On the *Sable Ann*'s run. The boat goes past the point. She dives those grounds. She knows where the crates lie better than anyone." He turned the thought over and felt its terrible weight. "And she brought something up. And somebody paid Silas to find out who in our circle was close to the water."

Elias said nothing, which was its own kind of confirmation. An archivist learns early that silence is also a document.

* *

The next evening the *Sable Ann* came back without her.

Tomas stood on the quay with the others as the crew came down the gangway, and he read their faces the way he read hands, and their faces were wrong—too careful, too smooth, planed like Silas's voice. The bosun said she'd gone down off the point in rough water. Said she came up tangled in her own line. Said there was nothing anyone could do. Said it all in a flat recitation, the words delivered like cargo, and when Marguerite asked to see the dive log the bosun's eyes slid sideways before he remembered to hold them still, and that slide—that single involuntary flicker toward the customs house—told Tomas more than any confession would have.

Felipe wept on the cobbles, quietly, the way boys weep when they have already been taught that loud grief gets noticed by the wrong people.

They buried what the boat brought back—or said they buried; the canvas bundle weighed less than a woman should weigh, and Tomas, who had carried his brother's body up through burning water, knew what drowned women weighed, and said nothing, because some mercies are lies and some lies are load-bearing.

That night the circle met in the loft, and the nets hung still—no wind, no breath, the whole room holding itself quiet as a held breath—and Marguerite stood at the head of the table with the lamp turned low and spoke the arithmetic aloud, because someone had to and because numbers, unlike people, cannot be disappeared so easily.

"One witness sold. One witness gone. Nineteen days until the armistice is signed, and after that, whatever is not on the record never happened." Her voice did not shake. Tomas admired her for it and worried for her because of it; steel that does not show its flexing is steel nearest the snapping. "So we finish faster, and we trust less, and we assume everything we've said in this room is already in the commission's files. From tonight, nothing written here stays here overnight. Statements travel separately. Names go into code. And no one—" her eyes moved around the table, catching each face in the lamplight, gentle and merciless at once— "no one speaks to Silas Merrick again. Not to warn him. Not to confront him. Not even to look at him longer than courtesy requires. If he's been turned, confrontation only tells his handlers what we know. If he hasn't—if he's only frightened—confrontation might push him the rest of the way. We starve the channel. That's all we can do."

"And Rosa?" Felipe's voice cracked on it. "What do we do about Rosa?"

Marguerite was silent a moment. "We finish what she dove for," she said. "It's the only eulogy they leave us room to give."

Afterward, walking home alone through streets slick with the first spit of the building storm season, Tomas became aware of footsteps matching his—not behind, but parallel, across the street, in the deeper shadow past the shuttered chandlery. He did not run. Running marked you. He walked, unhurried, a man going home from cards, and let the footsteps follow, and counted doorways between himself and the laundry, and thought: *they know where I sleep, and they are telling me so.*

At his own door he paused, and turned, and looked directly into the dark across the street—the old defiance of his father, palms open, everything already given away—and said, to the shadows, in a voice worn smooth by eleven years of speaking to the drowned:

"Count us while you can. The number changes soon. It grows."

The shadows did not answer. But in the morning, when Marguerite sent word by the laundry girl, the message contained only five words, and Tomas read them standing in his doorway with the gulls screaming over the fish waste and the storm light gathering in the west like a debt coming due:

*Elias has the original registry.*
