## 16. The Armistice Clock

The clock in the registry office had stopped at eleven minutes past four on some afternoon years before Elias Vane's arrival in the port, and no one had ever wound it again. It hung above the filing cabinets like a small dead moon, its hands frozen in an attitude of perpetual hesitation. Elias had looked at it every working day for six years and had long ago stopped seeing it. Now he could not look away from it, because the clock on his own desk—the brass one, the one that ran—had just told him something that rearranged the architecture of his chest.

Nineteen days.

He read the telegram a third time, as though the words might reorganize themselves into something kinder on repetition. *ARMISTICE DELEGATION ARRIVES ON THE NINETEENTH INSTANT. SIGNING CEREMONY SCHEDULED FOR THE TWENTY-SIXTH. ALL REGISTRIES CERTIFIED AND SEALED BY THE TWENTY-FIFTH. NOTHING UNRECORDED AFTER THAT DATE SHALL BE ADMITTED TO THE PROCEEDINGS.*

*Nothing unrecorded after that date shall be admitted.* He knew what the sentence meant. Every archivist knew. But knowing it as a clause and knowing it as a countdown were two different countries, and he had just crossed the border between them without a passport.

Outside the registry windows, the port went about its morning with the cheerful amnesia of the living. Stevedores shouted over crates of copra. A trawler came in low against the tide, its hold stinking of silver fish. Somewhere beyond the breakwater, beyond the reef, beyond the drowned crates in the shallows off Kessel Bay, history was being loaded onto ships like cargo, addressed to elsewhere, insured against loss.

Elias folded the telegram along its creases and put it in his breast pocket, where it lay against his heart like a small cold coin.

* * *

Superintendent Halloway received him at ten, in the corner office with the ceiling fan that turned with the slow dignity of something being paid to do nothing. Halloway was signing papers when Elias came in, and he did not stop signing, because Halloway believed that a man who paused for subordinates taught them to expect pauses.

"You've seen the schedule," Halloway said. Not a question.

"Twenty-six days from now. The twenty-fifth is the certification deadline."

"The twentieth-fifth." Halloway signed, blotted, turned a page. "Which means the next three weeks are housekeeping, Mr. Vane. Everything tidy. Everything closed. I want the coastal registries bound, indexed, and sealed by the eighteenth so there's room for error. Errors," he added, with the mild emphasis of a man placing a single stone on a scale, "are not a thing this office can afford at present."

"I understand, sir."

"Do you?" Halloway set down his pen and looked up, and for a moment his eyes were not bureaucratic at all but something older and flatter, the eyes of a man who had watched harbors burn in other colonies under other flags and had learned precisely what memory was worth at market. "Then let me say it plainly, since you're a careful man and careful men prefer plainness. When the delegates sign, they will be signing a version of these last six years. There will be exactly one version available to them. Whatever is in the record when the ink dries—that will be the truth. Not because it resembles the truth, Mr. Vane, but because it will be the only thing anyone can point to and call such. History is not what happened. History is what we can prove happened. You of all people should appreciate the distinction. You keep the proof."

"Yes, sir," Elias said, and heard his own voice come out level, which surprised him, because underneath the levelness something was screaming in a register only dogs and guilty men could hear.

"That's all."

Elias walked back down the corridor with his spine straight and his hands cold. Behind the doors he passed, clerks scratched their pens in the old rhythm, recording births and deaths and cargo manifests and debts, each of them believing, insofar as they thought about it at all, that the ledger was a servant of fact. None of them knew—or none of them admitted—that a ledger could also be an assassin, that to omit was to kill, and that the killing had already begun, quietly, in bound volumes with gilt lettering, months before any soldier fired the last shot of anything.

Nineteen days. Nineteen days in which a village that had existed for a hundred and forty years would either have existed or would not, depending on which side of a certification seal the truth fell.

* * *

He did not go back to the registry. His feet made the decision for him, carrying him out of the administrative quarter, down the hill through the spice-smell of the market, past the fish-cleaning tables where gulls wheeled and screamed like creditors, to the laundry rows behind the divers' quarter where wet cloth hung in pale banners against the wind.

She was there. Of course she was there. Marguerite Thornbury stood with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and her dark hair pinned carelessly, feeding sheets through a mangle with the steady economy of someone whose body had learned that wasted motion was a form of theft. Aurelia sat in a woven basket in the shade of the eaves, gnawing on a wooden ring, her black eyes tracking the fluttering linen overhead with the solemn fascination of an emperor reviewing troops.

Marguerite saw him and did not smile. She never smiled first anymore; that was part of the treaty between them, unwritten and unsigned. She turned the mangle's crank, fed another sheet, and waited.

"You've heard from the historians' delegation?" she said. Not a question either. Word traveled fast in a port; word was the fastest thing in the port, faster than ships, faster than rumor of storms.

"The telegram came this morning." He kept his voice low, though the nearest living soul besides the baby was forty yards away, beating a carpet with methodical violence. "Signing on the twenty-sixth. Certification deadline the twenty-fifth."

"And what does certification mean? In your language."

"In my language," Elias said, "it means the record closes. Anything entered before the seal is admissible to the proceedings. Anything after—" He made a small gesture, the gesture clerks use for discarding drafts. "Anything after might as well never have occurred. No inquiry will hear it. No commission. No court that may someday convene. If Kessel Bay is not in the record by the twenty-fifth, then in fifty years a scholar will open the official archive and find a coastline with no village on it, and he will believe his own eyes, because archives do not lie—they only decide."

The mangle stopped. Marguerite's hands rested on the crank, and Elias watched her knuckles whiten, the tendons rising under brown skin like cables taking a load.

"Nineteen days," she said.

"Nineteen days."

"And Tomas is still—" She stopped herself, glanced toward the basket. The baby had dropped her ring and was studying her own fist with profound suspicion. "Tomas is still willing. The divers are willing. What we don't have, Elias—and correct me if I'm wrong—is paper that anyone outside this port would believe. Testimony is breath. Breath disperses. We need documents."

"We need more than documents." Elias moved closer, close enough that the smell of wet linen and lye soap surrounded him, close enough that speaking softly became possible. "We need placement. Do you understand what I'm telling you? It isn't enough for the truth to exist. On the twenty-sixth, the truth must be *in the room*. In the annex. Beside the treaty text, bound into the proceedings, so that no future hand can remove it without leaving a tear in the record visible to everyone who comes after. A document in a drawer is a corpse. A document in the proceedings is a witness."

"Then we put it in the room."

He almost laughed—not from humor but from the vertigo of hearing the impossible said simply, the way she said everything, as though the world were a mangle and difficulties were just sheets to be fed through. "The annex is compiled by the historians' secretariat. Access is by credential. I have credentials, but they open the wrong doors. My section is coastal registries, not proceedings. And Halloway has begun asking why I requested the original Kessel Bay folio twice."

"What did you tell him?"

"That I was verifying pagination."

"Was he persuaded?"

"He was patient," Elias said. "With Halloway, patience is worse than suspicion. Suspicion means he thinks you've done something. Patience means he's decided what you've done and is waiting for the paperwork to catch up with his certainty."

Marguerite unclipped the finished sheet from the mangle, shook it once, hung it. Her movements were unhurried, but Elias had spent enough evenings in the drying loft now, among candle stubs and sworn statements written in five hands on whatever paper the circle could buy, to know that her stillness was not calm. It was ballast.

"My father used to say," she remarked, to the linen rather than to him, "that the sea keeps time better than clocks. He said a diver learns to count her own heartbeats, because the moment you stop counting is the moment the bottom takes you. Nineteen days is—" she calculated without appearing to calculate— "roughly a million and a half heartbeats. I've held my breath longer than I thought I could. So have you."

"Aurelia hasn't," Elias said. "Aurelia doesn't get a vote on how long she holds hers."

Something moved across Marguerite's face then, brief as a shadow of a bird: the fear she carried always now, folded small and hidden among the laundry of her composure. The child in the basket kicked one bare foot, entirely unaware that she was evidence, that her face was a document, that nineteen days determined whether she would grow up as a foundling with no history or as the last living exhibit of a village that powerful men had voted out of existence.

"No," Marguerite agreed quietly. "So we count faster."

* * *

That evening Elias sat in his rooms above the chandlery with the shutters open to the harbor sounds and the certified-false registry open on his desk beneath the lamp. He had copied it once already, line by line, in a hand indistinguishable from his own—which was the point; it *was* his own—but tonight he was not copying. Tonight he was doing arithmetic.

On the left side of the page, the assets: the original registry, smuggled photograph of it taken before the forgery was certified; Tomas Reyes' sworn statement, witnessed by two divers; the manifest discrepancies he had documented himself, showing company steamers at Kessel Bay on dates the official log placed them two hundred miles south; and, sunk somewhere in the shallows or possibly already recovered—he did not know which, and the not-knowing scraped at him like barnacle—the dispatch crate that named names above Halloway's name.

On the right side, the liabilities: nineteen days. Halloway's patience thinning. The historians arriving within the week with their measuring instruments and their preconceptions. And himself—a man with keys to some rooms and not others, a man whose treason, if discovered, would be recorded meticulously, because the company never failed to record the crimes committed against it.

He dipped the pen and began, not the copy, but a list. A schedule. If the clock was going to beat them, it would have to beat a plan.

Day one through four: finish transcribing the registry duplicate and hide it outside his rooms, because his rooms were the first place a search would go, and he thought now of pearl sacks and laundry lofts and the underside of the drying-shed floor where Marguerite's neighbors stored salt. Day five: approach the delegation's junior secretariat before the seniors arrived, because juniors were ambitious and ambitious men could be reached through their ambition. Day seven: the dispatch, if it could be found—if Marguerite's divers had not been anticipated. Day ten: rehearse the procedure for entering a dissenting annex, because the rules of procedure were the only weapon in this entire harbor that could not be confiscated, and he intended to know that weapon down to its serial number.

He wrote until the lamp guttered, refilled it, wrote on. Outside, the tide turned with a long sigh against the pilings, and somewhere in the dark a ship's bell counted eight bells into the water, and the water took them and kept them, the way the sea kept everything—sunk crates, drowned divers, drowned fathers, the whole sunken archive of the world.

*Nothing unrecorded shall be admitted.*

He understood at last, sitting in his small hot room with ink drying on his fingers, that the sentence was not a threat aimed at Kessel Bay alone. It was aimed at everyone the armistice intended to erase: the drowned, the burned, the indentured, the infant in the basket. The victors had built a door and were going to close it, and on the far side of the door they were going to arrange the furniture of the past to their liking, and everyone who had ever lived in that past would live henceforth in the arrangement.

Unless, in nineteen days, someone got the true furniture into the room.

Elias blew out the lamp and stood at the window a while, watching the harbor lights tremble on the black water. Down the hill, in the divers' quarter, a light still burned in the drying loft—he could see it from here, a small stubborn gold point among the shadows, and he knew without looking who was awake inside it, feeding pages through her hands the way she fed sheets through the mangle, turning testimony into record one sheet at a time.

Nineteen days. A million and a half heartbeats.

In the morning, he would need to see her before the historians' ship was even a smudge on the horizon—because overnight, reviewing his schedule, he had found the flaw in it, the gap wide enough to lose everything through: the dispatch. Everything turned on the dispatch naming the raid's author, and the dispatch lay at the bottom of the sea, and the only person who knew exactly where was a woman who had gone diving three days ago—

—and who, if the fixers had been watching the reef the way Elias would have watched it, had been seen.
