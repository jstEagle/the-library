## 17. Interrogation by Courtesy

The invitation arrived on cream paper, edged in gilt, delivered by a boy whose shoes cost more than Marguerite had earned in three years of diving.

*Mr. Havelock Ashcombe, Historian Attached to the Armistice Delegation, requests the pleasure of Mrs. Marguerite Thornbury's company for tea, Thursday at four o'clock, at the Meridian Company House.*

*Mrs.* She was no one's wife. The title had been invented for her by someone who assumed a woman with a child must have a husband somewhere, dead or deserting, and found it tidier to say *Mrs.* than to leave a blank on the form. The port was full of such small inventions. She had learned not to correct them.

She read the card four times, then set it beneath the lamp and studied it as though it might confess something—the watermark, the weight of the stock, the faint impression where a seal had been pressed and removed. Paper told you things if you let it. Her father had taught her that about water; she had taught herself the rest.

*A certain infant.* That was what the boy who'd brought the card had mumbled when she asked what it concerned, repeating his master's phrase with the reverence of a parrot quoting scripture. *A certain infant.*

There was only one infant in this town that historians would trouble themselves over, and she was asleep in the laundry basket at that very moment, her fists curled against her cheeks like two small arguments she intended to win.

Marguerite put the card away and began to think about tea.

* * *

The Meridian Company House stood at the top of the hill like a wedding cake left out in the rain—white icing walls gone grey at the seams, verandas stacked one upon another, louvred shutters that never quite closed against the salt wind. Marguerite had walked past it a thousand times without once being invited in. Divers entered through the side gate, past the scales, into the smell of brine and money. The front steps were for men in linen suits who shook hands and signed things.

She climbed those steps at four o'clock precisely, wearing the only dress she owned that had never touched seawater—a dove-grey cotton, mended so finely at the shoulder that only she knew the seam was there. She had braided her hair and pinned it severely. She had left Aurelia with Old Sefa, the midwife whose hands were too arthritic for pearls but whose discretion was legendary, and she had spent the walk rehearsing.

Not answers. Answers were dangerous; answers invited follow-up questions. She had rehearsed *posture*. The way a tired workingwoman sits—honestly, heavily, with nothing to hide because hiding takes energy she doesn't have. She had rehearsed her hands, which wanted to wring themselves, and taught them instead to rest folded in her lap like sleeping birds.

The houseboy led her through corridors that smelled of floor wax and old ambition, into a room where the afternoon light came slanting through jalousies and lay across the floor in pale stripes, like something flayed.

Two people waited.

The first was Mr. Havelock Ashcombe himself, and he was exactly what she had expected: sixtyish, silver-whiskered, dressed in the uniform of empire pressed to a knife's crease. He rose when she entered—he was punctilious about that sort of thing—and gestured her toward a rattan chair angled beside a low table already laid with a silver service. His smile was the smile of a man photographed often. It lived separately from his eyes, which moved over her the way an assayer's thumb moves over a pearl, testing for flaws.

The second person sat further back, half in shadow near the window, taking no part in the courtesies. A woman—young, perhaps thirty, with ink-stained fingers and spectacles that caught the light and gave back nothing. A leather folio rested on her knees, closed. She did not rise. She did not smile. She looked at Marguerite the way Marguerite imagined a portrait looks back: steadily, without appetite, memorizing.

"Mrs. Thornbury," Ashcombe said warmly. "Thank you for coming. May I introduce Miss Adela Crane, of the Historical Commission. She assists me."

"Sir. Miss Crane." Marguerite lowered herself into the offered chair and arranged her hands.

"You'll take tea?" He was already pouring, and she watched his wrist—steady, practiced, a man who had poured tea in a dozen colonial ports while deciding the fates recorded in none of them. Milk? Sugar? She took both, though she drank neither sweet nor white, because a guest accepts what is offered; deference was the costume of the day.

"I must say," Ashcombe began, settling across from her, "what a remarkable town this is. Remarkable. I've been sent all over—the Commission has offices in six ports now—and I find the smaller places are always the ones with the richest textures. Don't you find?"

"Find what, sir?"

"Texture." He waved his cup vaguely toward the window, beyond which the harbour glittered with its usual criminal beauty—masts and cargo nets and the brown sails of the pearling luggers. "Life here. The industry. The people."

"It's home, sir," Marguerite said. "I don't look at it much. I look down, mostly."

"Down?"

"The sea floor. I dive for pearls." She said it simply, without apology, and watched him adjust—she could nearly hear the small gears turning as he re-sorted her from *person of interest* into *native informant*, a category he filed comfortably between furniture and weather.

"Ah! Yes. The famous beds." He leaned forward, pleased. "You know, I've been reading everything I can find on the trade. Fascinating stuff. The Commission believes history isn't only treaties and generals, Mrs. Thornbury—it's the lives of ordinary people. The oyster beds. The divers. The—" he paused delicately, "—the families."

And there it was. The word dropped into the conversation like a stone into still water, and Marguerite watched the ripples spread outward and made herself not look at them.

"Families," she agreed. "Mine's mostly drowned."

It was a good move and she knew it. Grief, offered plainly, stopped questions better than any lie; men like Ashcombe found sorrow embarrassing, and retreated from it the way they retreated from open graves. He murmured something condolent—her father, she supplied, lost to a squall off Kessel Bay nine years ago—and for a moment the room belonged to her.

Then he set down his cup.

"Now. To my purpose." He produced from his breast pocket a folded paper, opened it with ceremony, and consulted it as though it contained matters of state rather than what she suspected was a list of three questions. "You may be aware that the Commission is compiling a complete record of this region during the late hostilities. Everything. Trade figures, population registers, shipping manifests, parish books—everything. We want the account to be *thorough*, Mrs. Thornbury. Thoroughness is a kind of justice, we believe. What happened should be written down correctly, so that no one need ever wonder."

"Yes, sir."

"In the course of our work we've encountered certain... gaps. Records that are incomplete. Registers with pages missing. And occasionally—" he smiled again, that separate smile, "—we encounter living gaps, if you will. People whose names appear in documents but whom no one seems able to produce for interview. You understand how troubling that is for a historian. A name without a face. It's like a coin with the sovereign rubbed off."

He let that image hang. Somewhere below them, in the harbour, a ship's bell rang the half hour.

"A few weeks ago," he continued, "there was some discussion among the townspeople—I hear everything eventually, it's the curse of my profession—about an infant. Found abandoned, I'm told. Left at a doorstep. There was talk of a note." He consulted his paper again, though Marguerite would have wagered her freed right hand he knew every word of whatever was written there. "The constable's blotter mentions it. Curiously, the entry was struck through the following day, in different ink, and initialled by someone above the constable's station. Struck entries are like struck register pages, Mrs. Thornbury—they make a historian positively itch."

He looked up at her with frank, avuncular curiosity.

"So I wondered—towns being small, and women talking to each other as women do—whether *you* had heard anything about this child. A certain infant, as it were."

The silence lasted perhaps two seconds. Marguerite used all of them.

Behind Ashcombe, Miss Crane turned a page of nothing; the folio stayed shut, but the gesture was a clock ticking, and Marguerite understood suddenly that the young woman was timing her. Not the answer—the *pause* before it. They measured hesitation the way she measured breath underwater.

She gave them a pause now, but the right kind: the pause of a workingwoman assembling memory, not concealing it.

"There was a baby," she said slowly. "At the fish market. Two months back, maybe. Some woman left it on a crate and vanished into the crowd. Half the wharf saw it before anyone picked it up. There was shouting." She frowned slightly, honestly. "I wasn't close. I'd just come off the boats and my ears were still ringing with water. Why? Did no one claim it?"

Ashcombe's pen—which she hadn't even seen him take up—paused above the page.

"You weren't close," he repeated pleasantly.

"No, sir. I went home. My feet were cut from the shells." She extended one hand, palm up, callused in the distinctive crescents of the diving knife and rope-burned at the finger joints. Let them look. Let them see a life written in the skin, a whole alibi inscribed in scar tissue. "I sleep like the dead after a dive. If the whole town burned around me, I'd learn of it at breakfast."

"And the child at your door," Miss Crane said.

Her voice was the first sound she had made since the introduction—low, uninflected, precise—and Marguerite felt it land between her ribs like a diver's needle finding the exact seam of an oyster. Not *the child at a door*. *Your door.*

She turned, unhurried, and met the girl's gaze for the first time. Behind the spectacles, the eyes were watchful and—this unsettled her more than hostility would have—not unkind.

"I haven't got a child at my door, miss," Marguerite said.

"No," Miss Crane agreed softly. And then, worse: "I didn't say yours."

The room tilted, very slightly, the way a lugger tilts when a swell passes under the keel. Marguerite kept her face still through sheer muscle memory—years of holding expression steady while her lungs screamed, years of surfacing calm because panic on the surface meant the boatmen marked you as green and paid you accordingly.

"I beg your pardon?"

Miss Crane regarded her a moment longer, then looked down at her folio, and the moment passed—or seemed to. Ashcombe cleared his throat and resumed command of the room as though it had never left him.

"Miss Crane is thorough," he said, with the fond exasperation of a man for a favourite hound. "Thoroughness is her entire character. Now, Mrs. Thornbury—let me ask you plainly, because I respect plainness in a working woman. You live alone. Is that correct? No husband, no family in residence. A woman alone in a room above a drying loft, near the harbour. A woman of—forgive me—modest means." He ticked these facts off gently, kindly, each one a stone placed on a scale. "If an infant had been left anywhere on your street, you'd likely have heard of it. Women notice babies. It's nature." He smiled. "So I'll ask you directly, and please believe me when I say this is not an accusation but an inquiry, and your candour will be noted in our files either way: have you seen this child? Have you heard where it went?"

*Your candour will be noted.*

Marguerite lifted her teacup, sipped—too sweet, scalding, perfect—and set it down with a small china click that sounded, in the quiet, like a lock turning.

"Sir," she said, "may I speak freely?"

"Please."

"You've come a long way to write down what happened here, and I'm told that's important work, so I won't waste your time pretending to knowledge I haven't got. But I'll tell you what I do know, because it might help your book." She leaned forward a fraction, letting the lamplight catch the salt-bleached streaks in her dark hair, letting her voice drop into the register she used in the market when she wanted men to lean in and listen. "I know that in this town, children go missing all the time. Diver's children. Fever takes them, or the sea does, or they're given away to families up-coast when a widow can't feed them, and nobody writes any of it down because paper costs money and grief is free. If somebody's baby was left on a step somewhere, I promise you it's the tenth such child this year and nobody struck *those* entries from any blotter." She paused. "Begging your pardon—but why does *this* baby matter to the Commission? Babies are born and abandoned every day in every port from here to Singapore. What makes this one history?"

It was a calculated strike, and she watched it land. Ashcombe's whiskers twitched. For a heartbeat the avuncular warmth flickered and she saw underneath it something colder and quicker—a man recalculating, discovering that the native informant had asked a question with teeth.

"A remarkable question," he said at last.

"I dive, sir. We're taught to ask where things sink and why."

Miss Crane, in her corner, had begun—finally—to write. The nib scratched softly, and Marguerite could not tell whether the words going down were evidence or exoneration. That, she thought, was the trick of people like this girl: you would never know until it was too late whether you had been witnessed or archived.

* * *

Tea ended the way tides end—imperceptibly, and then all at once. Ashcombe rose, shook her hand with both of his (his palms were dry and cool as ledger paper), and thanked her for her time and her plain speaking. At the door he paused.

"One last thing, and forgive an old man's fussiness." He drew a card from his pocket and pressed it into her hand. "If you *do* recall anything—anything at all—about that child, or about strangers passing through your quarter in recent months, you'll send word. Immediately. There are people attached to this delegation, Mrs. Thornbury, who are less patient than I am. Less courteous." His smile returned, warm as a lamp. "I should hate to see courtesy mistaken for softness. In myself, I mean. In the Commission generally."

"I understand perfectly, sir," said Marguerite, who understood nothing of the kind and everything besides.

The houseboy showed her out. On the front steps the evening had come up gold and bruised-purple, and the harbour below was lighting itself lamp by lamp. She descended with her spine straight and her heart going like a hammer in a wet hull, and she did not allow herself to breathe fully until she reached the fish market and the noise of ordinary life swallowed her.

Old Sefa's rooms smelled of camphor and boiled milk. Aurelia was awake, sitting upright in a nest of blankets, and at the sight of her she emitted the imperious squeak that had become, over these weeks, the most familiar music in Marguerite's life. She gathered the child up and held her close enough to feel the small frantic engine of her heartbeat, and Sefa, watching from her chair, said nothing until Marguerite's breathing steadied.

"They asked about her," Sefa said. Not a question. Nothing in this town surprised the old woman anymore.

"They asked beautifully." Marguerite sat, keeping Aurelia against her chest where the trembling couldn't be seen. "That's the frightening part. The cruel ones shout, and shouting tells you where they are. These ones pour tea."

Sefa nodded slowly. "And the young one? The one who watches?"

Marguerite thought of Miss Crane's pen scratching in the lamplit corner, of the eyes behind the spectacles—watchful, tireless, unreadable. Of *I didn't say yours*, delivered so gently it might have been kindness, and might have been a hook set quietly in deep water, waiting to feel for the pull.

"She frightens me most," Marguerite admitted. "The old man wants a story for his book. The girl wants the truth. And only one of us knows which of those is more dangerous."

Aurelia seized her finger with her whole fist and held on, fierce as an oyster to its rock, and outside the window the delegation's lanterns moved along the hill road, two lights descending together toward the harbour, toward the Company House, toward the ledgers where the struck-out pages waited in their drawers like bodies waiting to be named.

Marguerite kissed the downy crown of the small head and made herself a silent vow, the kind that needs no witness and no paper: they would have to take this child over her drowned father's grave and through every lie she had left in her.

She was still holding the vow in her mouth when she saw, pinned under Sefa's door, a slip of paper that had not been there an hour ago—cream-coloured, expensive, unsigned, bearing a single line in a clerk's careful hand:

*They searched your loft this morning while you were on the hill. Your ledgers were read. Burn nothing. — E.V.*
