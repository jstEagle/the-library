## 19. The Testimony Circle

*Marguerite*

The drying loft above the pearl house smelled of salt and hemp and the long slow sweetness of coconut oil rubbed into weathered beams. By day it was a place of work—nets hung from the rafters like the molting wings of enormous patient birds, oyster shells stacked in crates to bleach pale as bone. But by night, this particular night, it became something else. Something that had no name yet, because no one had dared give it one.

Marguerite arrived after dark with Aurelia strapped against her chest beneath her coat, the child's breath warm and damp against her collarbone like a small secret animal. She had walked the long way, doubling back through the fish market where the ice was being chipped and the gulls screamed their endless litigation, watching behind her until the lamplight of the main street gave way to the loom of the harbor and the black shoulder of the hill.

Inside, seven candles burned in jars of sand. Seven. She had asked for six.

Tomas Reyes sat on an upturned crate near the center of the circle, his hands resting on his knees, palms down, as if pressing something flat. He had dressed for it—she saw this immediately and it moved her in a way she hadn't anticipated. His good shirt, the white one with the shell buttons, laundered so many times it had gone soft as paper. A man dressing to be remembered in.

"You came," he said. Not surprise. Confirmation, the way a man confirms the tide has turned.

"I said I would."

"Saying is cheap. The water's full of people who said."

She unbuttoned her coat enough for Aurelia to breathe freely and took her place on a crate beside old Serafina Mabini, the diver, whose hands were folded around a tin cup of nothing—she only held it, wouldn't drink, Marguerite noticed; her throat had been ruined by thirty years of pressure and brine, and swallowing was a labor she rationed like coin.

The others assembled slowly, in ones and twos, each arrival marked by a soft knock at the trapdoor—a pattern, two and three, that Elias had devised. Wen Delai, the junior clerk from the registry annex, came still wearing his company armband rolled up in his fist like a killed snake. Marisol Ibañez, whose brother had crewed a Kessel Bay transport boat and never come home. The old customs weigher, Anselm, who moved now like a man carrying furniture inside his body. Two others Marguerite knew only by face: a young woman from the laundry lines, and a quiet man who repaired the company's survey instruments and had not spoken a full sentence in anyone's hearing for years.

Elias came last. He stood at the edge of the candlelight a moment before entering it, and Marguerite watched him take them in—the way a man takes in a room he may be about to betray or save, and hasn't yet decided which. Then he stepped forward, set his leather satchel on the floorboards, and began laying out paper.

"Before we begin," he said, "you should understand what this is."

Silence. The harbor breathed below them, halyards chiming against masts.

"This is not a petition. Petitions can be refused. This is not a complaint; complaints are filed and lost. This—" he touched the papers, "—is testimony. Sworn, witnessed, dated. Once these statements exist, signed and sealed, they become facts. Facts are very difficult things. Companies have sunk ships to keep facts underwater." He looked around the circle. "I want every one of you to understand that you are building something heavier than water tonight."

Serafina made a sound in her ruined throat that might have been laughter.

"Heavy," she rasped. "Good. Everything worth keeping is heavy. Pearls sink. Corpses sink. Lies float. You know why? Lies are full of air."

Marguerite felt Aurelia stir against her breast and pressed her palm flat over the small warm weight of her.

They began with Tomas, because Tomas had insisted, and because what he carried was the largest thing in the room.

* * *

*Tomas*

He had rehearsed it against sleep for eleven nights, lying in his cot above the cooperage, saying the words into the dark until they stopped tasting like blood and started tasting like words. That was the trick nobody told you about testimony: the telling changed the thing told. Say it plainly enough times and it became history instead of wound. Or so he hoped. He would find out tonight.

Elias wrote the heading first. *Statement of Tomás Reyes, fisherman, resident of Kessel Bay prior to the third of October, nineteen hundred and nine.* Then the oath, read aloud in Elias's careful clerk's voice, and Tomas put his hand flat on the table they'd dragged out from under the nets, and swore by his father, by the water, by whatever was left of Kessel Bay, to tell it true.

"The bay faced east," he began, and stopped, because the smell came first—always the smell, hot resin and burnt coconut husk—and he had to let it pass through him like weather. Serafina's hand found his forearm without either of them arranging it. Old divers' habit. Hands that knew how to hold on.

"The bay faced east and the village faced the water, because we were water people before we were anything else. My father kept a boat and my mother kept the accounts for the copra buyers, and on the third of October there were forty-one houses standing along the shore road, and I could name every family in every house, and mostly I still can." He heard his own voice go flat and steady, the voice of a man reading a shipping manifest, and understood dimly that this was his mind protecting him, and let it.

"They came at dawn from the south, flying the company pennant but not company men—I knew some of those faces from the port, contract men, and others I didn't know at all, men in uniforms with no insignia, which itself is a kind of uniform if you think about it. They said it was quarantine. They said there was sickness. We believed them for almost an hour, which was long enough."

Wen Delai's pen had stopped. Elias reached over and touched his wrist and the pen started again.

"They put the men on the beach and the families in the church—which wasn't a proper church, it was the copra shed with a cross nailed to it, but it was ours—and then the burning started at the north end and moved south, house by house, orderly, the way a man counts. I want that in the record. It was orderly. Someone had drawn a list. Houses don't burn themselves in alphabetical order of the families that lived in them."

He told them about the beach. He told it all, in the plain voice, while the candles guttered and were trimmed by Marisol with a steadiness Tomas would remember afterward longer than anything else he himself had said. And when he reached the second burning—the records office, three days later, when the same men returned for paper rather than flesh—he found his voice cracking at last, not at the memory of flame but at the arithmetic.

"Forty-one houses. Eighty-three souls that I can name. And then they burned the registries, the birth rolls, the land titles, the parish book. Do you understand what that means?" He looked at them each in turn. "It means that legally, officially, according to every document the world keeps—those people never existed. You cannot massacre a population that isn't on any list. That's what the second fire was for. The first fire killed us. The second one unmade us."

In the silence that followed, Marguerite shifted Aurelia in her sling, and the child made one small sound—not a cry, a sigh, the sound of a sleeper turning—and Tomas thought: *there. There is the whole argument.* He pointed at the baby with his chin.

"That's a Kessel Bay face," he said quietly. "You write that down too. Write that the evidence has eyes."

Elias wrote it down.

* * *

*Serafina*

Her turn came after midnight, and she was glad of it. Night hours belonged to divers; the day belonged to everyone else.

She did not sit while she spoke. Standing was easier on the chest. She held her cup of untouched tea and looked at the candle flames, which behaved underwater the way nothing behaved anywhere else—leaning always, obedient to currents no eye could feel—and she began with the sea itself, because the land story was Tomas's to tell and hers lived below the surface.

"Thirty-one years I dove this coast," she said, in her gravel whisper. "I know the bottom between here and Kessel Bay like you know your mother's kitchen. So when the company boats came back in November of that year and began working the shallows off the bay—working them wrong, mind you, dragging not diving, hauling grapnels through water too shallow for honest pearling—I went to look. Because a diver looks. That's the whole trade. Looking."

She described the crates. Nine of them, maybe more, sunk in a line in six fathoms off the old jetty, weighted with chain, the wood already greying, stenciled with the Meridian mark. She described opening one—her hands made the motion in the air, patient, precise, thirty-one years of muscle memory—and finding paper inside, oil-wrapped, bundled, tied with government cord.

"Dispatches. Orders. Pay ledgers for men who were never on any company roster. I can't read, most of it, but I know a signature column when I see one. And I know what it means when you sink signatures in six fathoms of water." She set the cup down at last, deliberately, like a woman concluding an argument. "Paper sinks faster than bodies. Less struggle in it. But it doesn't dissolve, not wrapped like that. The sea keeps what men try to drown. I've always liked that about her."

Anselm the weigher spoke next—haltingly, about tonnage, about cargo manifests he had been ordered to falsify, weights that existed nowhere but in his own head, and how he had kept them there for four years like stones swallowed one at a time. Wen Delai talked about the registry annex, the gaps in the coastal rolls, the page numbers that skipped like a heartbeat with a murmur. The laundry girl, Ilanga, spoke barely above a whisper about the uniforms that came to the washhouse, collars that needed cleaning after certain nights, blood that was not the kind you could bill for. Even the silent instrument-man spoke—four sentences, total, about compasses recalibrated to read two degrees wrong, and what that does to every chart ever drawn afterward—and the room understood that he had chosen those four sentences across years the way other men choose weapons.

Elias transcribed until his hand cramped. Marisol took over. Marguerite witnessed each signature, and Serafina watched the girl do it—the young one, the indentured one, with the hidden child—and thought: *she witnesses like a diver descends. Carefully. Watching the whole way down.*

Near dawn, when the statements lay signed and stacked in three neat columns, Serafina spoke once more, not for the record.

"You know what we are now," she said to the circle, to the candles, to the grey light beginning at the shutters. "We're a wreck site. All of us holding our breath together."

* * *

*Marguerite*

She walked home as the sky went the color of unpolished silver, Aurelia awake now and solemn against her chest, watching the world upside down from her sling like a small disapproving inspector. The port was stirring. Fish carts. Steam from the bread ovens. A company launch cutting the harbor in half with its wake, going somewhere official.

Marguerite counted the night's harvest the way she counted pearls: seven statements, plus Tomas's, plus the pages of Elias's own deposition—his confession of the certified-false registry, signed with his own hand, which had cost her something to watch him sign, though she could not have said precisely what. Fourteen names. Fourteen people who, before last night, had each believed privately that they alone remembered correctly.

That was the thing she turned over on the walk home, the thing that frightened her more than the fixers and the soldiers and the wax seals: not that they could be silenced, but that they had existed at all—this community, this fragile raft lashed together out of clerks and divers and widows. Communities like that had happened before. She had read about them in the books that came through the company lending case, in the histories that were always written afterward, by the survivors, in exile, in the past tense.

Fragile rafts, she thought, holding fourteen people above deep water.

And rafts, she knew better than anyone on this coast, were visible from ships.

She reached her door and stopped. Across the lane, at the corner by the chandlery, a man stood smoking a cigarette he never seemed to draw on, in the posture of someone waiting for a launch that had no schedule. He tipped his hat to her—courteous, unhurried—and did not look away.

Marguerite went inside, barred the door, and stood in the grey light with her daughter breathing against her heart, doing the cold arithmetic she had learned in the ledger room: *one witness gone missing buys silence; two buy terror; but fourteen—*

Fourteen bought exactly what had been bought in Kessel Bay. Attention.

The cigarette man was still there when she next looked out through the shutter crack. And behind him, at the end of the lane, a second figure had joined the morning: a woman in a historian's grey traveling coat, consulting a notebook, walking toward her door.
