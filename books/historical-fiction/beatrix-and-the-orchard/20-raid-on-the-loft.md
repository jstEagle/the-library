## 20. Raid on the Loft

The candles had burned down to their stubs by the time the last statement was signed, and the drying loft above the pearl-house smelled of tallow and ink and the particular sweetness of shell that had lived in salt water for a generation. Marguerite stood at the long table with a blotter in her hands, pressing each page flat as though calmness could be pressed into paper, as though the words might settle into the fibers and refuse ever to be lifted out again.

Eleven statements. Eleven names signed in eleven different hands—some confident, some crabbed, one drawn as a thumbprint because its owner could not write but could swear. Tomas sat at the far end with his cap on his knee, watching the door the way old divers watch the surface: without hurry, without hope, only habit.

"It's good paper," said Old Sefa, running her thumb along an edge. "Better than the company uses."

"They'll know what that means," Marguerite said. "That we could afford to be careful."

Outside, the port was settling into its evening noises—the groan of winches being stilled, the cry of the last fish-sellers, somewhere a gramophone playing a song about a city none of them would ever see. Above all of it, faint as thread under cloth, came the sound of boots. Many boots. In step.

Marguerite's hands went still on the blotter.

Tomas was already standing. He had been standing, she realized, for some minutes; he had heard them before any of them, his ears still tuned from years underwater where sound traveled wrong and you learned to read its intentions through the body rather than the mind.

"Back way," he said quietly. "Take the papers. Take the child."

"How long do we have?"

"A minute. Perhaps less." He put his cap on. It was, she thought absurdly, the most ordinary gesture in the world, a man putting on his cap before going out. "They will want a name they can hang the night on. Mine is the oldest and the loudest. I will be at the door when they come, so they don't have to invent anything."

"Tomas—"

"Marguerite." He smiled at her, and it was the smile he wore coming up from deep water, when the light went strange and the air tasted like forgiveness. "I told you witnesses have short lives here. I did not tell you they are small prices. Go."

She gathered the statements into the oilcloth with hands that shook and did not fumble, which felt like a mercy granted by some part of her that had already left the room. Sefa took the south stair with two of the younger divers. The loft emptied itself the way a lung empties—all at once, silently, leaving only the ache behind.

Marguerite went down the back stair with the bundle against her chest and Aurelia strapped there beneath her shawl, the baby mercifully asleep, one fist curled against Marguerite's collarbone. Behind her, through the floorboards, she heard the front door go—not broken, not yet, but opened with the confidence of men who knew it would be theirs regardless.

A voice, official and almost apologetic: *"By authority of the Provisional Commission—"*

Then Tomas, loud enough to carry through the whole building: "You've come a long way for old men and wet paper. Come in, then. Mind the shells."

* * *

She did not run. Running drew eyes. She walked, fast and ordinary, into the lanes behind the pearl-house where the laundry lines crossed overhead like rigging and the smell of fish-gut and lye covered everything equally, making everyone anonymous. The oilcloth bundle rode inside a basket of wet washing. Her heart beat so hard she could feel it in the baby's back, a second pulse knocking gently against her own.

Behind her, the shouting started.

She made herself keep walking. Past the cooper's yard. Past the weighing shed where she had spent four years of Sundays hauling sacks for pennies off her indenture. Past the archway where Elias had once waited for her in the rain with a stolen hour and a borrowed umbrella, looking like a man who had never in his life done anything stolen or borrowed and was astonished to find he liked it.

*He is not here now.* She did not let herself think further than that. Thinking further was a luxury with teeth.

The lanes narrowed toward the drying beach, where the pearl sacks came to sweat and soften in the sun before shipment. At this hour it was deserted, the racks empty, the sand printed with the day's cart-tracks and gull-stamps. She crouched behind the last rack, breath sawing, and allowed herself one look back.

Smoke. Not much—a lamp knocked over, perhaps, or a brazier upended in the scuffle. And figures at the pearl-house door, four of them, five, marching out a sixth man between them with his hands bound at the wrist in front of him so that he looked, horribly, like a man walking to prayer.

Tomas did not struggle. That was the worst of it. He walked between the soldiers the way he walked into surf, accepting the water's weight as a thing that had always been coming for him. At the mouth of the lane he turned his head—not toward the soldiers, not toward the sea, but up the hill, toward the ridge where Kessel Bay had stood before it burned the first time, and then again. As if checking the weather. As if telling it: *not yet.*

Then the lane swallowed him, and Marguerite's legs nearly failed her, and Aurelia stirred against her chest with a small sound like a question, and Marguerite answered it by standing up and walking north along the waterline with a basket of laundry and a face like any other tired woman's face.

* * *

The pearl sacks were where she had left them, three days ago, buried above the high-tide line under a cairn of coral rubble that looked like nothing more than every other pile of coral rubble on that coast. She dug with her hands. The sand came away cold and damp and smelling of iron, and the first sack emerged fat and grey and gleaming faintly, mother-of-pearl buttons destined for shirts she would never own, sewn by hands she would never meet.

She cut the sack open with her thumbnail and pushed the oilcloth bundle down among the shells, deep in the center where a knife probing from outside would find only shell after shell after shell, the sea's cold currency, worthless to anyone who wasn't counting.

It had been her father's trick. *Nothing hides like value,* he used to say, pouring pearls into the ballast of his dive-boat. *Everyone searches for what things are worth. No one searches inside them.* He had hidden rice that way during the blockade year, and letters during the strike, and once—she had not remembered this until her fingers were in the sack, until the memory surfaced the way drowned things surface, bloated and unbidden—once a man, a fugitive, for three days, breathing through a reed while company boats circled.

Her father had hidden people too. And the sea had taken him anyway, or been made to take him, and she had spent eleven years believing that history happened elsewhere, to other people, in cities with real names.

She filled the sack, tied it, and slung it over her shoulder like any diver's wife carrying any heavy load home. Forty pounds of shell. Twelve pages of testimony. One sleeping child. The arithmetic of everything she had left.

* * *

Sefa's sister kept a room behind the chandlery, up a ladder-stair that smelled of rope tar, and it was there, in the blue hour, that Marguerite allowed herself to stop moving. The old woman asked no questions. She boiled water, fed Aurelia bread soaked in milk from a tin she clearly could not afford to spare, and set a cup of something bitter in front of Marguerite with the wordless competence of women who have survived four changes of flag and intend to survive this one.

They say it was the Reyes man," Sefa's sister said, stirring the fire. "Taken at dusk. Six soldiers. An officer who apologized to him—that's what Rahel heard, she sells near the quay. Apologized. Like a man closing a shop early."

"Yes."

"They will ask him questions."

"I know what they will ask him."

"And he is an old man," the woman said gently, which was not comfort, because it was true. Old men's bodies hold secrets the way dry rope holds water: briefly, and at a cost.

Marguerite looked into her cup. The firelight moved in it. She thought of Tomas's voice going deliberately loud in the loft, offering himself up like a coin tossed to buy the others time, and how he had looked up at the ridge before the lane took him—not goodbye, no. *Instructions.* Hold the line. Remember the bay. Not yet.

"He gave us the minute he promised," she said. "We owe him more than grief. We owe him paper."

"The papers are safe?"

"In shell."

The old woman nodded slowly, and something moved across her face that Marguerite had seen before on divers' faces at the moment they decided to trust someone with their life-line: fear, calculation, and underneath both, a kind of stubborn tenderness. "Rahel can carry word to the clerk Ovidio—he owes me for a net mended twice. And the young man from the archive house, if he still comes to the chandlery asking after you."

"He comes?"

"Every evening since the raid began tonight. He bought rope he did not need and asked if anyone had seen a woman with a basket." The old woman's eyes were shrewd and not unkind. "I told him nothing, because I had seen nothing. Now I have seen something, and I am still deciding whether to tell him."

"Tell him this," Marguerite said. "Tell him the dispatch is out of the water and out of my rooms, and that tomorrow at dawn I will be at the northern beach with the first tide, and that if he wants to find what he has been looking for, he should bring whatever he copied from the original registry by lamplight, because two halves of a lie make one truth, and we are nearly out of nights."

* * *

Dawn on the northern beach was grey and wide and empty, the tide going out over flats that gleamed like hammered tin. Marguerite stood with the pearl sack at her feet and Aurelia awake against her hip, gumming a shell fragment with the serene idiocy of infancy, utterly innocent of the fact that she was evidence, contraband, a face worth forgetting, a name not yet given.

Elias came down the dune path at half-light, hatless, coat wrong for the weather, carrying a leather folder held against his ribs the way she carried the baby. He looked as though he had not slept. He looked, she thought, exactly as frightened as she was, and she loved the fright on him because it meant he understood—really understood—for the first time, what side of the ledger he had stepped onto.

"You're alive," he said, and his voice broke slightly on *alive*, and he crossed the wet sand fast and put his free hand on her shoulder as though confirming she was solid.

"For now. They have Tomas."

"I know. I signed the intake log myself—it was that or explain why I wouldn't. His name's in the record as a rioter, which means procedure protects him for maybe six days before someone remembers procedure doesn't apply to riots." He opened the folder with shaking hands. "Copies. Everything I could reconstruct. And this—" A single sheet, older, foxed at the edges. "The original registry page for Kessel Bay. The one I was ordered to certify falsely. I reported it destroyed in the audit. It isn't. It's here. I am, God help me, a thief now."

The tide sucked and whispered around their ankles. Far out, a diving bell bobbed at its mooring, patient as a head above water.

Marguerite took the page. Her hands were steady now; it seemed all the trembling had been spent, saved up and paid out in one night, and what remained was something harder and quieter. She looked down at the names—whole families, dates, causes of death entered in a clerk's fair hand—and then at Aurelia, who had inherited those names without knowing it, who was chewing her shell and watching the gulls as though gulls were the most astonishing invention in the world.

"They wanted her face forgotten," Marguerite said. "So we'll put it in writing. All of it. Every name on this page and every voice from the loft, and Tomas's deposition the moment we can get it out of him—and the dispatch naming the man who gave the order. Before the treaty signs."

"Nineteen days," Elias said. "Less, now. They're talking about moving the date up."

Above the tideline, the gulls rose all at once off the dune, wheeling, screaming, and Marguerite turned—and saw, far down the shining flats, a single figure walking their way, unhurried, wearing the grey coat of a commission historian, and beside the figure, small and unmistakable even at this distance, the glint of a soldier's bayonet catching the first sun.
