## 21. Prison Visit

The harbor prison had been a customs house once, back when the port still bothered to pretend that everything passing through it was legitimate. You could see the bones of its former life in the architecture: the broad windows now bricked to slits, the weighing floor partitioned into cells, the great brass scale that had weighed cargo standing rusted in the courtyard like an idol whose god had died. Elias Vane passed it every time he came, and every time he thought the same thing—that the Meridian Trading Company had not corrupted this place so much as inherited it, the way one inherits a rot in the timber that was there before the first beam was raised.

He carried a leather folio under his arm and an expression of profound boredom on his face, and both were props. The folio held genuine inventory schedules—barrels of tar, coils of rope, tinned meat requisitioned for the garrison—which made his pretext respectable. The boredom was harder work. He had practiced it in the mirror of his lodging room until his own face became a stranger to him, which he suspected was the point of mirrors all along.

"Archivist," said the sergeant at the gate, reading his papers upside down with the deliberate slowness of a man who wanted Elias to know that his authority here extended exactly as far as the sergeant's patience. "Company property again?"

"Company property," Elias agreed. "The commandant wrote requesting a full accounting of stores. There are irregularities in the last quarter's figures."

There were no irregularities. Elias had invented them at his desk two nights ago, inventing them with the same care he gave to real audits, because a lie built badly was the only kind that got people hanged. The sergeant grunted, stamped something, and waved him through into a courtyard where the light fell white and hard as dropped china.

They kept the political prisoners—the word *political* being itself a fiction, since none of these men had politics, only memories—in the old bond wing, where indentured laborers had once been locked for attempting to run out their contracts early. Elias knew the irony was not accidental. Nothing in this port was accidental; everything was layered like sediment, each era pressing down on the last until the bottom strata forgot they had ever been sky.

Tomas Reyes was in the third cell. Elias had requested, through proper channels, permission to conduct an inventory interview with *the detainee Reyes, fisherman, concerning company equipment seized at the time of arrest*. The request had taken six days to travel up through channels and come back down approved, which told Elias that someone in the apparatus had considered it harmless. That someone had been wrong, but harmlessness was a kind of camouflage too, and Elias wore his like everyone else wore theirs.

The cell door opened with a sound like a cough. Inside: a plank bed, a bucket, a high slit of window admitting a blade of noon light that lay across the floor and moved, over the hours, like the hand of a clock counting toward nothing. Tomas sat against the wall beneath the slit, in the one corner the light never reached, and Elias understood immediately that this positioning was deliberate. A man who had spent his life reading water knew where the shadows pooled.

"Mister Archivist." Tomas's voice was rougher than Elias remembered, sanded down by whatever they did to prisoners between visits, but his eyes were the same—dark, patient, holding their depth the way the outer reef held its depth, concealing coral and wrecks both. "Come about your ropes and barrels."

"And your nets," Elias said. "Seized equipment must be valued before charges can be assessed."

"Charges." Tomas smiled without any humor in it, a movement of the mouth like a crack in mud. "Sit, then. The floor is honest, at least. It holds you up and asks nothing."

Elias lowered himself cross-legged opposite the fisherman, opened the folio on his knees as a screen, and set a blank sheet of paper on top of it—theatrically, a pen already dipped. Then he looked up and met the other man's eyes, and let the performance fall away from his face like a mask loosened at the jaw.

"They're watching?" Tomas asked quietly.

"The door. Two guards who rotate every half hour, and they talk in the corridor, which covers perhaps forty seconds at a stretch. The window slit is too high for sightlines. It will have to be enough."

"It will be enough." Tomas folded his hands. Up close, in the shadow, Elias could see what the arrest had cost him: a hand wrapped clumsily, two fingers splinted with slats that might have been broken off a fish crate; the way he favored his left side when he breathed. They had been neither gentle nor thorough. Men who tortured thoroughly wanted information. These men wanted only discouragement, and they had failed even at that, or succeeded beyond their own understanding—for Tomas looked less discouraged than any free man Elias had seen in the port.

"You understand why I can't write anything down," Elias said.

"I understand better than you do." Tomas's gaze went to the blank paper, and something ancient and weary moved behind his eyes. "Paper is a strange thing, Mister Archivist. In Kessel Bay we thought paper would save us. Titles, deeds, the registry of our village, births and deaths written in a fair hand by the company clerk who came twice a year. We thought: it is written, therefore it is true, therefore it cannot be undone. Then men came with lanterns and kerosene, and I watched the registry burn for the second time—" He stopped. Drew breath. Continued, flatter. "I have told Marguerite some of this. She will have told you. So you know that paper burns faster than houses. Houses take an hour. Paper takes a minute."

"Yes."

"And so you've come for the thing that doesn't burn." Tomas nodded slowly, and Elias saw the fisherman arrive at the truth of the visit before Elias could frame it—not from suspicion but from the same clear-sightedness that read currents. "You want me to give it to memory. Yours."

"Mine," Elias said. "And then to paper, yes—but paper that goes where paper cannot be burned. Into the annex. Into the record that Adela Crane is building inside the proceedings themselves. Once it is entered as a document of the armistice, destroying it becomes a public act, witnessed by every delegation. Even victors hesitate before burning things in front of the world."

"Do they." It was not quite a question. "You believe that."

"I have to believe it. Belief is part of the mechanism. If enough people act as though the record will hold, the record becomes harder to break. Institutions run on performed faith. I worked in one for nine years—I should know."

Something flickered across Tomas's face—not quite approval, but the neighbor of it. He shifted against the wall, wincing at his ribs, and settled into the posture of a man preparing to carry something heavy.

"Then listen," he said. "And don't write. Writing makes your eyes go down, and eyes-down men miss the door opening."

Elias put the pen down. It felt like setting down a weapon he'd been carrying wrong.

* * *

For the next hour, while the blade of light crawled across the floor and the guards' voices rose and faded in the corridor on their rotations, Tomas Reyes spoke his deposition into the dim air between them.

He did not tell it as testimony at first—he told it as story, because story was how a fisherman kept anything alive, and Elias did not interrupt to correct him. Later, in the dark of his lodging room, he would reassemble the narrative into numbered paragraphs, sworn form, the language of affidavits. But here, now, it arrived the way the man had lived it: in weather and names.

He told of the morning the raid came—how the smoke had been visible from the fishing grounds, ten miles out, and how the boats had raced home through a falling tide to find Kessel Bay already going orange at the seams. He told of soldiers who were not soldiers of any flag he could name, wearing uniforms assembled rather than issued, and officers who spoke the company's trade-pidgin fluently, which no foreign regulars ever managed. He told of the registry house, and the second fire—the one that consumed not homes but records—and how the officer in charge had stood in the street checking titles off a list as they fed the flames, like a man confirming a delivery.

"He read them aloud as he burned them," Tomas said, and his voice cracked along an old seam. "'Grant of commonage, 1874.' 'Burial ground deed.' 'Registry of baptisms.' He read my mother's name out of a book and put the page in the fire, and he was careful with the fire, Mister Archivist—careful, the way you people are careful with your ledgers. He didn't want the wind taking embers onto the wrong roof. Only the right ones."

Elias listened and did not move. Somewhere in his chest, the archivist's old calm—the professional detachment that had carried him through twenty years of other people's transactions rendered into columns—was coming apart quietly, rivet by rivet, and he let it, because to stay sealed while this man spoke would have been a second burning.

Tomas told of the boats that went out after the raid, company boats, and what the divers had found in the weeks following: crates in the shallows off the point, sunk with ballast stones, their contents soaked but legible—dispatches, requisitions, correspondence bearing signatures. He named the signatures. One of them Elias recognized, and hearing it spoken aloud in a prison cell by a man with broken fingers, he felt the name change weight entirely, become something dense and radioactive, no longer a line in a file but a live thing.

"The dispatch Marguerite recovered," Elias said carefully, when the corridor was quiet. "It bears that signature?"

"It bears it. But one dispatch is a thread. Threads get explained away—forged, misfiled, the ink questioned by three hired experts before the tea goes cold." Tomas leaned forward, and the shadow slid across his face. "What I hold doesn't sit in one crate. What I hold is the pattern. Dates. Sequences. Who signed and who counter-signed. I was on the water; I saw the ships; I can account for days that the official record says nothing happened on. Days, Mister Archivist. Whole days erased, and I remember every hour of them, because I was there and I am still here, and they cannot burn me fast enough to catch up."

The guards' voices rose in the corridor. Both men fell silent, Elias arranging his face into administrative tedium, Tomas staring at the wall with the vacant patience of a man bored of inventory questions. The voices passed. The door did not open. The light on the floor had traveled a hand-span.

"There's more," Tomas said softly. "The part I saved for last, because it is the part that costs the most to say."

"Say it."

"The officer who read the registry into the fire. I have seen him since. Here. In this port. Walking free, in a better uniform, with better men around him." Tomas's eyes fixed on Elias with terrible steadiness. "He is advising the historians. He carries their satchels. And every day he walks past me in this cage or one like it, the official history gets one day cleaner and one name safer. That is what you are racing, Mister Archivist. Not ignorance. Ignorance is easy to fix. You are racing a man who knows exactly what happened and has made a profession of its disappearance."

Elias sat with that. Outside the slit window, a gull cried once, twice—a sound so ordinary it seemed obscene, like laughter at a funeral.

"I'll need details," he said finally. "Dates. Ship descriptions. Tide states if you have them—tides are verifiable, there are tables. Anything that lets a neutral party check your account against the physical world. Testimony that can't be tested is testimony that can be dismissed."

"Tide tables." For the first time, something like warmth crossed the fisherman's ruined face—a grim, salt-crusted amusement. "You're learning. Yes. Ask me about the tides. On the seventeenth of the month, low water at dawn, and the raiders' launch drew too much to cross the bar until mid-morning, which means the orders that reached them must have been issued the evening prior, which means—" he ticked it off on his splinted fingers, and Elias watched a fisherman construct an alibi for the truth with the same rigor the company applied to concealing it, "—which means whoever signed those orders signed them in daylight hours, at a desk, with witnesses nearby, and lied about it afterward. Tides don't testify, Mister Archivist. But they keep records anyway."

* * *

Near the end of the permitted hour, when the folio was full of nothing and Elias's mind was full nearly to overflowing, Tomas reached out and gripped the younger man's wrist. His hand, despite the splints, was strong the way driftwood is strong—weathered into hardness rather than born to it.

"One thing more. Not for the annex. For you."

Elias stilled.

"They will offer you something. Soon." Tomas's voice dropped below the reach of the corridor. "A promotion, a passage, a clean desk in a clean country. They offered me passage too, once—passage out of Kessel Bay the week before the raid, on company business, a survey posting, very flattering. I declined because the water was good that season and I am a fool about water." He almost smiled. "They do not offer things to men they intend to destroy. They offer things to men they intend to *use*, and the using comes after the giving, always. When the offer arrives—and it will, because they have noticed you now, I saw it in the sergeant's face when he read your name—remember that the price was decided before you were shown the goods."

"How do you know they've noticed me?"

"Because you are sitting in my cell," Tomas said simply, "and no one let you in by accident. This place has one purpose: to show visitors what happens to the inconvenient. You are being taught a lesson, Mister Vane. The wise student asks what the lesson is *for*."

The bolt rattled. Time, that indifferent clerk, had closed its ledger on the hour. Elias gathered his empty folio, his blank paper, his unused pen, and stood. For a moment the two men faced each other in the failing shadow, the archivist with his arms full of nothing, the prisoner with his hands full of everything.

"Is there anything I can bring you?" Elias asked. It was the conventional question and he meant it unconventionally.

Tomas considered. "Salt," he said at last. "They feed us without it, and a body without salt forgets the sea, and I refuse to forget the sea while I'm in here." His grip tightened once on Elias's wrist, transmitting something that had no words—or all of them. "Tell Marguerite: the deposition is safe. Tell her the vault is not in the company's keeping. Tell her—" he paused, searching, and found it, "—tell her memory is the one archive they can't audit. Yet."

Then the door was open and the light was brutal and Elias walked out across the courtyard past the rusted scale, past the guards who watched him with the flat assessment of men cataloguing him for later, past the gate and the sergeant and out into the port's noise and glare.

He did not go back to his office. He walked to the seawall instead and stood there a long time, the folio under his arm, looking out at the water that covered Kessel Bay's drowned crates and held its records in green silence. Then he took out his notebook—the small private one, the one that lived against his ribs—and began to write, standing up, in a hand that shook only slightly, everything Tomas Reyes had poured into him in the dark.

Memory was the one archive they couldn't audit.

But Elias knew archives, and he knew that unrecorded memory died with its keeper, and Tomas Reyes was sitting in a cell where keepers had short lifespans. So Elias wrote, and wrote, filling page after page with dates and tides and names and fires, transcribing a man into paper as fast as his aching hand allowed—because the deposition had to exist in two places now, in the vault of Tomas's mind and in the hidden leaves of a modest notebook, each backing the other up like double-entry bookkeeping, that most sacred and most damned of disciplines.

When he finished, the sun was lowering and the harbor was gold, and Elias realized he was smiling, thinly, without joy. Courage without comfort, he thought. That was the whole of it. No reassurance that the annex would hold, no promise that the words would outlive the match and the kerosene. Only the doing of the thing because it was the thing that remained to do.

He buttoned the notebook against his chest and turned toward the drying loft, where Marguerite would be waiting, where the candle would already be lit—

—and stopped.

Three men stood at the far end of the seawall, unhurried, dressed in the plain good clothes of clerks or fixers, and they were not looking at the water. They were looking at him. And the tallest of them held, dangling from one finger with the studied casualness of a man displaying a purchased thing, a sheet of paper that Elias recognized even at thirty paces by its heading and its seal:

His own request, in his own hand, for permission to interview the detainee Reyes.

Approved, stamped, filed—and copied. Of course it had been copied. Everything passed through channels, and channels kept duplicates, and somewhere in the great sedimentary machine of the company, a duplicate had surfaced in the wrong hands, or precisely the right ones.

The tall man tipped his hat, an courteous, terrible little gesture, and turned away, and his companions turned with him, and the three of them strolled off into the evening crowd as if they had merely been admiring the view—as if the message required no words at all.

Elias stood very still on the seawall, the notebook hard against his ribs, and understood that Tomas had been right about everything, including the timing.

The offer was coming. Or the alternative to it.

Both were already on their way.
