## 22. The Historian Who Hesitates

Adela Crane had been trained, from the first day of her appointment, to believe that a fact was a thing you could hold in your hand—a card in a catalogue, a signature at the foot of a page, weightless and obedient. For eleven years she had arranged such facts into narratives that pleased the Crown and the Company in equal measure, and she had told herself, in the small hours of the night when honesty crept in like damp through plaster, that this was what history was: an act of careful arrangement.

She no longer believed it. She could name the evening the belief left her, the way one names the night a fever breaks.

It had been the third day of the delegation's residence in the port, and she had been compiling the chapter on Kessel Bay—the burned village, the regrettable incident, four paragraphs long and scrubbed of every name. The official registry certified that the village had held ninety-one souls, all of whom, the document implied with the elegant cowardice of passive voice, had been regrettably displaced. She had copied the figure onto her card, and her pen had hesitated above the paper for the length of one breath, because she had seen, that same morning, in the harbourmaster's older ledgers—the ones nobody thought to hide because nobody remembered they existed—a tax roll from three years prior listing two hundred and six inhabitants of Kessel Bay, each name written in a clerk's patient hand.

Ninety-one. Two hundred and six. Both numbers could not be facts. One of them was a wound dressed up as arithmetic, and Adela knew, with the sick certainty of a woman who had balanced other people's lies for a decade, which one it was.

She had written *ninety-one* anyway. Her hand had not shaken. That was the part that frightened her most—not the lie, but the steadiness of her hand performing it.

Now, on the sixth morning of her stay, she stood at the window of the delegation house and watched the port assemble itself out of mist. Fishing boats went out in a loose procession, their lamps still burning yellow in the half-light. Beyond them the water lay the colour of pewter, and beyond the water the hills stood in ranks, green and indifferent. Somewhere down there, in the tangle of streets below the company warehouses, lived whatever remained of those two hundred and six—or those who had loved them, or those who had seen. The official record did not require her to find them. That, precisely, was what the official record was *for*.

"You've been at the window since before the gulls woke," said Hargreaves from behind her, not looking up from his cards. He was the delegation's senior historian, a man whose conscience had been amputated so cleanly and so long ago that he spoke of his work the way a gardener speaks of pruning. "The Kessel Bay material is due to me by Thursday."

"It will be," Adela said.

"Four paragraphs. Keep to the certified figures." He tapped his pen against the registry. "There's no appetite for expansion on this point. The armistice requires a clean narrative, and clean narratives are short."

*Clean*, she thought, was a word for things that had been washed. She said only, "Of course," and turned back to the window, and watched a woman in grey walk along the sea wall below, carrying a bundle against her chest the way porters carry contraband—close, protected, angled inward.

Adela had noticed the woman before. She noticed everyone now; suspicion had become a kind of eyesight. The woman was young, sharp-faced even at this distance, with the sun-darkened skin of someone who worked where the air was made of salt. She walked as if being followed were a habit she'd learned to live with.

On the fifth morning, the woman had stopped across the street from the delegation house and looked up—at the windows, at the flag, at the whole apparatus of official memory housed in borrowed colonial grandeur—and her expression had not been fear. It had been assessment. Adela, watching from behind a curtain, had felt assessed the way a door assesses whether it is locked.

Hargreaves's fixers had mentioned a diver's daughter. An indentured woman who had been asking questions in the fish market. Questions about Kessel Bay. They intended, Hargreaves had said over dinner with the relish of men discussing vermin, to have a word with her once the signing was done. Afterward, there would be nothing to have a word about.

Adela had said nothing. She had eaten her soup and hated herself with a thoroughness that surprised her, because she had thought her capacity for self-hatred had been exhausted years ago, and yet here it was, renewable, like everything else in the colonies.

* * *

The reading room of the delegation house was open to residents of standing by special arrangement, which meant it was open to no one, which meant that when the clerk brought Adela the message on the fourth afternoon, folded twice and smelling faintly of brine, she read it twice before her senses caught up with her sense of danger.

*Madam—I have materials pertaining to the coastal registry which may interest a person of accuracy rather than convenience. I make no request and offer no threat. If you are curious, walk the drying lofts above the soap works at dusk on Friday. If you are not, burn this, and I will never trouble you again.*

No signature. A pressed flower of some kind between the folds—no, not a flower. A pearl, small and grey as a raindrop, sewn into the paper itself like a seal.

Adela sat very still for a long time. Around her the reading room ticked and settled, the way old houses do, and outside a cart went by crying oysters, oysters, fresh oysters, and the ordinary world went about its business while hers rearranged itself.

She should burn it. That was the correct action; she knew the correct actions the way a nun knows her offices. Hargreaves would burn it without finishing the second sentence. The fixers would burn it and then go looking for whoever wrote it. Burning it was safety, career, the smooth continuation of a life in which history was a thing you arranged.

But two hundred and six.

She had begun, in recent nights, a private ledger. Not of facts—for facts she had plenty—but of doubts, each one entered with a date and a citation, as though doubt could be made respectable by good referencing. *Entry 14: harbourmaster's roll vs. certified registry. Discrepancy: 115 souls.* *Entry 15: requisition of kerosene, quantities inconsistent with a single structure fire.* *Entry 16: the word "displaced" used forty-one times in draft chapters; the word "killed" used zero.*

Her private ledger had grown fat. And a fat ledger, she understood now, was not a record. It was a summons.

Friday came down over the port like a curtain drawn slowly, the light going amber, then rust, then bruise-coloured above the rooftops. Adela told her colleagues she wished to sketch the harbour at dusk—an innocuous vanity they permitted her with indulgent smiles, as though a woman's pencil were harmless by nature. She took the back lanes, past the chandleries with their smell of pitch and hemp, up the stair-streets where laundry hung across the gaps between buildings like signal flags of a private nation, and came at last to the soap works and the drying lofts above them, where timber frames stood in rows and lines of stretched cloth breathed in the evening wind like the sails of a becalmed fleet.

She almost turned back twice. She catalogued, as she climbed, every rational argument for retreat, and found—as one always finds—that reason had quietly absented itself from the proceedings and left the body in charge.

"You came."

The voice came from among the hanging sheets, and then the woman in grey stepped out from behind them, holding a lamp turned low. Up close she was younger than Adela had guessed and older too, in the way of people who have been responsible for themselves since childhood. Her hands were scarred at the fingertips in the manner of divers. Her eyes did Adela the discourtesy of neither flattering nor fearing her.

"You're the historian," the woman said. It was not a question.

"I compile the official record of the settlement negotiations. My name is Adela Crane."

"I know your name. Names are cheap here; we collect yours the way you collect ours." She tilted the lamp, studying Adela's face with the frankness of someone appraising a pearl for flaw. "I'm Marguerite Thornbury. You'll find my name in the company indenture rolls, if your people ever bother to look below stairs. I dive for stray pearls. I mend nets. And I keep records of my own."

"Records of what?"

"Kessel Bay."

The word dropped between them like a stone into still water, and Adela felt the ripples move outward through her chest. "The registry has been certified," she said, and heard, to her horror, how much she sounded like Hargreaves. Like a filing cabinet with a pulse.

"Yes," Marguerite said softly. "That's rather the point, isn't it? Someone certified a lie. Someone with a steady hand." She let that sit precisely long enough to draw blood, then gestured toward a bench among the drying frames. "Sit, Miss Crane. Or don't. But either way, look at this first."

She drew from beneath a bolt of canvas a packet wrapped in oilcloth, unwrapped it with the tenderness of a woman unveiling a sleeping child, and laid its contents on the bench in the lamplight: a waterlogged dispatch, dried stiff as autumn leaf, its ink faded but legible. Orders. Adela read three lines and stopped breathing.

"This authorizes—" she began.

"It authorizes everything," Marguerite said. "It names who gave the order for Kessel Bay, and it was signed above the company, which is why it sank instead of filed. Your certified registry says ninety-one souls. The harbourmaster's roll says two hundred and six. This paper explains why those numbers disagree."

"How do you have this?"

"The sea gave it back. The storm season is generous to those who ask politely." Marguerite crouched, bringing her eyes level with Adela's, and the lamplight moved in them like something alive. "Now I'm going to say the dangerous part, and you can decide afterward whether to run home and report me. Here it is: I think you already know the registry is false. I've watched you copy figures. I've watched your face. You have the look of a woman keeping a private ledger, and I know that look, because I invented it."

Adela rose. It was reflex, not decision—the body fleeing what the mind could not yet hold. She walked three steps among the drying frames, her heart going like a hammer on wet wood, and stopped, because ahead of her hung a sheet of washed linen, translucent in the last light, and beyond it the harbour lay open below, the ships at anchor, the treaty ship flying its pennant, the whole apparatus of victory riding at rest on water that had swallowed a village's papers and given them back to a pearl diver.

Two hundred and six names. She had never learned a single one of them. That, she realized, standing in the breathing dark among strangers' washing, was the true shape of her complicity—not the lies she wrote, but the names she had never asked for.

"Why me?" she said, without turning. "Why bring this to the enemy?"

Silence, then the soft sound of Marguerite also rising. "Because you hesitated."

"That's not a reason."

"It's the only reason there is. Everyone else in that house writes without hesitating. I've watched them through windows for a week. Hargreaves copies figures the way a machine stamps tin. But you—three days ago, at the window of the east room, you stopped writing for the length of a full breath, and you looked out at the water, and your mouth did something complicated. Grief does that to a mouth. So does shame." A pause. "I know both expressions intimately. I see them in my own mirror."

Adela turned. "If I take this to them, you'll hang. You understand that. Whatever protections you imagine, they'll find you, they'll take the child—" She caught herself too late.

Marguerite went very still. The lamp trembled once in her hand, and then steadied, and Adela watched a woman choose, deliberately, to show her fear and master it in the same breath, as though daring Adela to witness both.

"Yes," Marguerite said quietly. "They would take Aurelia. Which is why I'm not giving you anything, Miss Crane. I'm asking you to come and see. There's a difference, and it matters. A courier can be bought. A witness must be convinced." She lifted the dispatch, rewrapped it, and held it a moment before returning it beneath the canvas. "Come to the drying loft tomorrow night. Divers will give sworn statements about what they saw off Kessel Bay. Clerks who kept the requisition books. A survivor who carried children out of the smoke. No one will touch you. No one will press you. And if at dawn you want to walk back to that house and report everything, I won't stop you, and I'll spend whatever days I have left getting the child somewhere safe."

"And why would you risk all that on a stranger?"

Marguerite smiled, and it was not a comfortable smile; it was the smile of someone placing her entire life on one card and sliding it across a table. "Because history is currently being written by people who never hesitated. And I have decided—perhaps foolishly—that hesitation is the beginning of conscience, and conscience is the only thing left worth recruiting." She raised the lamp a little, so its light fell fully on Adela's face, and her voice gentled into something that was almost kindness. "You wrote *ninety-one* with a steady hand. I know. But your hand will never be steady again after tomorrow night, whichever way you choose. The question is only whether the shaking teaches you something."

Adela stood among the swaying sheets a moment longer, the harbour lights blurring below, the dispatch hidden ten feet away like a heartbeat under floorboards. Every instinct trained into her over eleven years said *walk away, burn the message, keep your hand steady*. And underneath all of it, older than her training, a girl's voice she had not heard since university—a girl who had gone into history because she believed it was a way of loving the dead—whispered: *two hundred and six*.

"Dusk tomorrow," Adela heard herself say. "I'll come."

Marguerite nodded once, doused the lamp, and the dark folded around them both like a page turning.

* * *

Adela walked home by the longest route, past the shuttered market and the sleeping customs house, and let herself into the delegation house through the garden door. In the corridor, lamplit and warm, she passed Hargreaves's study. The door stood open. He was at his desk with the certified registry spread before him, initialling pages with small precise ticks, and he glanced up with the bland contentment of a man whose world contained no discrepancies.

"Enjoy your sketching?" he asked.

"Immensely," Adela said. "The light on the harbour is remarkable this time of year."

"It is that." He returned to his ticking.

In her own room, she lit one candle, unlocked her writing case, and took out the private ledger. Beneath Entry 16 she wrote, in her smallest and most careful hand:

*Entry 17: Tonight I was offered the truth and did not refuse it. Tomorrow I will hear testimony the official record forbids. If I am discovered, this entry will hang me. If I am not, it may save me—which is to say, it may save the parts of myself I have not yet sold.*

She considered the entry, added a final line—*Discrepancy: 115 souls. Begin finding their names.*—closed the book, and blew out the candle.

Outside, far off in the hills above the drowned village, a dog began to bark, and did not stop for a long time.
