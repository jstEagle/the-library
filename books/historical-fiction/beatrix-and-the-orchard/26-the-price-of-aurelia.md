## 26. The Price of Aurelia

The first sign was the chalk mark.

Marguerite had passed the corner of Lantern Row a thousand times without looking at its walls, but she had learned, in these weeks of flight, to read a town the way her father had read water—the small disturbances, the wrongness in a current that meant something moved beneath. The chalk mark was a fishhook drawn at knee height, a fisherman's child's scrawl, except no fisherman's child had passed this way since the soldiers emptied the quarter. And beneath it, half rubbed away by rain, a second mark: a circle with a dot at its center.

An eye, looking down. A face, seen from above. A cradle's view.

She did not go home. She detoured through the fish market with her basket empty and her heart beating in her wrists, and by the time she reached the drying loft's back stair she had composed her face into the dull mask of a woman with nothing to hide, which was, she had discovered, the most difficult expression in the world to wear for more than an hour at a stretch.

Aurelia was asleep on the pallet beneath the sorting table, one fist curled against her cheek, her breath making the small sound Marguerite thought of as the sound of a bellows in a very small forge—her father's forge, where he had mended his own knives, and she wondered why her mind kept returning to him now, why grief kept surfacing like a diver who would not stay down.

Tomas sat mending net near the window, though his hands never truly worked; the net was an excuse, a way of keeping his eyes occupied while they roamed the street below.

"You saw it," he said, before she said anything.

"You saw it too."

"I saw it yesterday." He set down the shuttle. "Two men asking after wet nurses at the harbor office. Men don't ask after wet nurses. They ask after pearls, after cargo, after debts. A man who asks after a wet nurse is asking after a baby he already knows exists."

The light through the window had the flat pewter color of the season's first storms, and it fell across Aurelia's face and made her look, for a moment, as though she were underwater—as though she were already something the sea had taken and given back. Marguerite knelt and touched the child's dark hair, and the touch steadied her the way touching a hull steadies a swimmer in open water: not because it will save you, but because it tells you where the edge is.

"They know the face," Tomas said quietly. "That's what the marks mean. Not *where*—not yet. But *what*. Someone described her to them. Someone who saw her close."

Marguerite thought of the tea, the polite historian with her gentle questions and her notebook like a hymnal. She thought of the laundress who had smiled at Aurelia in the market. She thought of every pair of eyes that had rested on the child even once, kindly or carelessly, and understood that kindness and carelessness weighed exactly the same on the scale the fixers used.

"Then we move tonight," she said.

"There is nowhere left to move. Every safe house I know has gone quiet—no answer at the door, shutters closed at hours when someone should be awake." He looked at her with the terrible patience of a man who had buried a village. "Marguerite. There is one door left."

"No."

"You haven't heard whose door."

"I know what it costs to stand behind any door in this town right now. I won't add another debt to it."

Tomas folded the netting over his knees, slowly, as if folding away the conversation itself. "Then let me say the name anyway, and you can refuse it properly."

* * *

Sister Immaculata ran the foundling house on Vetch Street, a narrow building squeezed between a chandler's storehouse and a ruin, run by an order so small and so poor that the company had never bothered to notice it. She had buried more abandoned infants than the port had buried sailors, and she kept her registry in two hands—a false one for inspectors, a true one sewn into the binding of a psalter, because she had learned long ago that God required honesty and governments required only paperwork.

Marguerite had carried laundry to her once, twice, in another life, when her indenture still felt like a sentence rather than a vocation. She remembered a woman with hands like driftwood and a voice that could quiet a room of screaming children with one syllable.

"She owes you nothing," Tomas said. "That is precisely why it can work. Fixers trace debts. Kindness they cannot ledger."

The cost revealed itself within the hour, because Sister Immaculata was nobody's fool.

She opened the door herself, looked at Marguerite, then past her shoulder to the bundle there—Marguerite had taken to carrying Aurelia wrapped against her chest now, hidden under a shawl, because baskets could be opened and arms were assumed to belong to mothers—and her face did something complicated and final.

"So," she said. "It's come to me."

"Sister—"

"I know what's at your breast under that wool, girl. I have held four thousand babies. Do you think I can't hear one breathing?" She stepped back and let them in, and the entryway smelled of tallow and carbolic soap and the particular warm-sour sweetness of too many infants in too little space. "Sit. No—stand. I haven't decided how long you'll be sitting anywhere."

Marguerite stood. The foundling house was quieter than it should have been; she counted six children where there should have been twenty, and saw the sister follow her counting.

"The company took my older ones," Immaculata said flatly. "Indentured them. Eight years old, nine—old enough to sign, or old enough for a clerk to hold their hand on the X. What remained, I sent inland to my cousin's convent, and I kept only the ones too young to be worth paper." Her eyes went to the shawl. "Until now."

"I wouldn't ask if—"

"Yes, you would. That's rather the point of you, I think." She said it without malice, the way a surgeon says *this will hurt*. "I've heard things, Marguerite Thornbury. My girls hear everything—the harbor, the offices, the beds of clerks. I know there's a paper moving through this town that says a village full of people did not exist. I know you're the woman carrying it. And I know what happens to houses that shelter such women."

"Then you understand why I came to you last, and only because there was no second-to-last."

For the first time the sister almost smiled. It was not a comforting sight.

"What do you want from me?"

"To keep her three days. Maybe five. Until the search moves off the quarter. I'll take her out myself, west, to the mission road—there are orchard families there who owe Tomas, who hate the company enough to—"

"And then?"

Marguerite stopped.

"And then," Immaculata repeated, "you come back for her? Or you go back for your paper? Because you cannot do both, child, and every hour you spend deciding, the men with the chalk spend finding."

* * *

She stayed the night in the foundling house because Immaculata refused to let her leave until the streets emptied of patrols, and in the small hours Marguerite sat on a bench between two sleeping cribs and watched Aurelia sleep in one of them, in a stranger's nightdress, among strangers' blankets, and felt the future split before her like a channel dividing around a shoal.

One channel led west with the child. Orchard families, clean air, a name that belonged to no registry. Aurelia would grow up whole, unmarked, ignorant of Kessel Bay and of the mother who had carried the truth instead of her. Safe—and orphaned by choice, which was the only orphaning worse than the accidental kind, because it came with the knowledge that someone had weighed her against a principle and found her lighter.

The other channel led back into the port with the annex, the dispatch, the sworn statements—into the race, the risk, the possibility of a recorded world. And if she lost that race, she might lose Aurelia anyway, and lose her in front of witnesses, in front of history, turning her daughter into a lesson rather than a life.

Her father surfaced again, as he had all week—not drowned this time but diving, in memory, showing her how to equalize the pressure, how to let the ear crack open to the depth. *You go down or you come up,* he had said. *Staying in the middle is how divers die.*

"He isn't mine by blood," she said to the dark, testing the word *daughter* the way one tests thin ice. "I found her. Anyone could argue—"

But the argument collapsed the moment she made it, because blood had nothing to do with it. She had named her. She had fed her through the fever in the storm month. She knew that the crease above her left brow deepened when she dreamed, and that she woke angry at the world and forgave it within a minute, and that her laugh had two notes, one high and one lower, like a boat knocking twice against a pier. These were facts. She had collected them the way Elias collected documents. They constituted, together, a record. She was that record's only archive.

And archives, she thought with a bitter turn, existed to be raided.

Near dawn, Immaculata came and stood beside her, and for a long time neither spoke, because the children slept and silence was the sister's native tongue.

"I had a daughter once," the old woman said at last. "Before the veil. In another country, in another weather. She died of the flux at two years old, and I entered the order not out of grief but out of arithmetic—I calculated that if suffering was going to be the world's trade, I would rather work in the warehouse than the marketplace." She smoothed the blanket at the crib's edge with those driftwood hands. "Every child I've taken since, I have loved at the cost of knowing I will lose them—to adoption, to indenture, to the sea, to time. People think that's holy. It isn't. It's just expensive."

"Then tell me what to do."

"I wouldn't dream of it." She straightened. "But I'll tell you the price, since you came here to pay it, whatever you pretend. If you leave her with me, I may keep her a month. I may keep her a year. If the fixers come—and they will come, because a baby that matters to you matters to them—I will lie, and they will search my registry, and when they find nothing they may burn what they find nothing in. This house ends the day she leaves it. Twenty children scattered. Forty years of my work, spent on one child."

"That's not a price anyone should accept."

"No," Immaculata agreed. "It's obscene. That's how you know it's love and not charity—charity comes at a discount." She turned toward the door, then paused. "There's one thing more. The cost isn't only mine. If you send her west and stay here, you must decide, now, whether you're her mother or her courier. Couriers deliver packages. Mothers come back. If you cannot promise to come back—if the record might take you somewhere the orchards aren't—then leaving her is not saving her. It's only abandoning her in advance, dressed up as strategy."

The words went in like a hook under the skin, and Marguerite understood they were meant to.

* * *

Elias came at first light, gray and unshaven, with news that made the decision for her by making both channels worse.

"The signing's been moved up again," he said in the yard behind the foundling house, where the clotheslines stood empty and the wind off the harbor tasted of iron. "Not nineteen days. Word inside says less than a week now—the delegates want it done before the storm season closes the roads and gives the dissenters time to organize. Which means the annex must be complete and placed within days, not weeks." He hesitated. "And there's more. A fixer named Corr came to the registry yesterday asking about requisitions of infant clothing over the past three months. He's not searching houses yet. He's searching supply lines. Diapers, milk, wet nurses. He knows she's being kept close, whoever 'she' is. It's arithmetic now, Marguerite. He's subtracting."

Through the window behind him she could see the crib, the small rising chest, the fist curled against the cheek.

"Elias," she said. "If I carry her west, I'm out of this. Days in the hills, days back, maybe more in the storm. I'd miss the end of it. Everything we've built—Tomas's testimony, Adela Crane, your vault plan—it goes forward without me, and maybe it succeeds, and maybe she grows up safe in an orchard never knowing, and maybe that's the best ending available in this rotten ledger of a world."

"Or?"

"Or I stay. And finish it. And every day I stay, the net tightens around wherever she is, and the price gets paid by whoever shelters her, and someday—maybe soon, maybe in ten years—someone traces a chalk mark on a wall to a foundling house that no longer stands, and a girl learns that her mother traded twenty children's homes for a paragraph in an annex." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it, hated the audience, hated the honesty. "Tell me the truth the way you file it, Elias. Coldly. Which weighs more?"

He was quiet for a long moment. Wind moved the empty clotheslines like rigging on a ghost ship.

"You're asking the wrong archivist," he finally said. "I burned my promotion letter. My judgment about weighing things ended that day." Then, gentler: "But I'll tell you what I know about records. A document that is hidden protects no one. A document that is destroyed protects no one. Only a document *entered*—witnessed, countersigned, made part of the body of the law—only that one has power. And you are her record, Marguerite. If you scatter yourself into the hills, she loses the only witness who can testify to her own existence with authority." He met her eyes. "I'm not telling you to abandon her. I'm telling you that hiding her is not the same as securing her, and neither is the same as keeping her."

"And if the fixers take her?"

"Then we do what we have always done. We build the case that demands her back."

It was not comfort. It was not even advice. It was, she realized, a filing system—a way of arranging unbearable things into drawers that could be opened one at a time—and she took it, because drowning people do not inspect the rope.

* * *

She made her decision at noon, in the yard, with the wind rising and the first fat drops of the storm season spotting the cobbles like ink on blotting paper.

She would not send Aurelia west.

Instead she gave her to Immaculata for three days only—three days, sworn on the psalter with the true registry sewn in its spine—and took Tomas with her to arrange something no fixer had yet imagined: not hiding the child further, but moving her in plain sight, into the one place in the port where an infant's presence raised no question at all.

Because the historians' delegation had arrived with families. Wives, nursemaids, a colony of legitimate children trailing the great men of the peace like gulls after a trawler. And delegations hired local staff.

"Aurelia doesn't disappear," she told Tomas as the rain began in earnest, drumming on the chandlery roof beside them, on the whole listening town. "She gets *employed*. A nursemaid's charge, one of dozens. You can't chalk-mark a child inside the enemy's own nursery."

Tomas stared at her, and then began, slowly, terribly, to laugh—the laugh of a man watching the sea give back something impossible.

"It might work," he said. "God help us, it might actually—" He stopped.

Down the lane, unhurried despite the rain, walked a broad man in an oilcloth coat who did not glance at the foundling house at all—which was itself the glance—who paused at the corner of Lantern Row, took out a stub of chalk, and drew a new mark low on the wall.

Then he turned and looked directly at the window where Aurelia's crib stood, and smiled, and wrote something beneath the eye-and-cradle that Marguerite was too far away to read—but that Immaculata, arriving at the glass behind her, read aloud in a voice gone to ash:

"*Found.*"
