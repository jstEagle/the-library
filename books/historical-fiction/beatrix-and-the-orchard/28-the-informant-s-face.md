## 28. The Informant's Face

I had imagined this meeting a hundred ways, and in none of them did I bring bread.

But my mother's ghost stood at my shoulder that morning—she had been standing there for years now, ever since Kessel Bay's second fire—and she said, in the voice I could no longer separate from my own conscience: *You do not feed a man to make him confess. You feed him so that when he speaks, you remember he is a man.* So I bought the loaf from the Chinese baker on Lantern Row, still warm, and carried it through the fish-market stink of the lower town with my hands cupped around it like something rescued from water.

The betrayer was called Ferren Dalca. Everyone in the circle had known him for eleven years. He had mended nets beside us, drunk arrack with us at the Salt Cellar, taught my nephew to whistle through his cupped hands. When the divers began to vanish and the historians' fixers began to smile too broadly at our meetings, we searched for a stranger among us and found none, because the stranger had been one of us all along.

He was waiting in the back room of the chandlery where they had agreed to meet—a room that smelled of hemp rope and tar, lit by one high window cut with salt haze. He sat with his elbows on his knees and his head down, and when I came in he flinched at the door's sound like a man who has been listening for boots.

"You came alone," he said. Not a question. A hope.

"Tomas Reyes," I said, though he knew me. "We met first at your sister's wedding. You gave me too much rum and I gave your brother-in-law a black eye dancing."

Something moved in his face—not quite a smile, more the memory of where a smile used to live. "You danced badly."

"I danced honestly. There's a difference."

I set the bread on the barrel between us. He looked at it a long time, and then at me, and I watched him understand what it was—not charity, not bait, but a sentence spoken without words: *I know what you are, and I am feeding you anyway.*

"They told me you'd come to hurt me," he said. "The fixers. They said the others would cut my throat and weigh me down with the pearl sacks." He laughed, a dry terrible sound. "They described it in detail. They're good at details. That's their whole trade, isn't it? Details. The right detail in the wrong ledger."

"Is that how you justified it?" I asked. Not cruelly—or I tried not to be cruel. "Details?"

His shoulders dropped. For a while there was only the creak of the building settling, the far cry of gulls, the sea doing its endless indifferent arithmetic against the pilings below.

"My daughter is sick," he said finally. "Lung-rot, from the smoke the year of the fires—we all breathed it, but she was small, and small lungs keep what large lungs forgive. The company doctor would not see her. The company doctor sees company families." His hands opened and closed on nothing. "And then a man from the delegation found me at the pier and said: *your daughter will have a bed in the sanatorium by Thursday.* And I said yes. That's the whole confession, Tomas. One word. Yes. It took less breath than a prayer."

I sat down across from him. My knees were unsteady—I had crossed half the port since dawn, and fear does its own labor on the body—but I made myself sit like a man who has all the time in the world, because mercy, I was learning, is mostly posture. Mercy is deciding, again and again, not to be what they expect.

"I don't forgive you," I said. "Not today. Maybe not ever. Forgiveness is a harvest and I haven't finished planting."

"I know."

"But I need you to understand why I'm here instead of the ones who'd cut your throat." I turned the loaf toward him. "Eat. Then listen. Because there are two kinds of betrayal, Ferren. There's the kind that sells a friend for a bed in a sanatorium—which is what you did, and God knows most men would do it, which is exactly why they choose men like you, men with daughters, men with debts. And then there's the kind that lets a whole bay full of dead people stay dead forever because the living were too proud or too frightened to use every tool that came to hand." I leaned forward. "You are a tool that came to hand. I intend to use you."

He stared at me. "You want me to betray them back."

"I want you to *inform*," I said. "It's what you're good at now. It's what they trained you for, whether they meant to or not. You know which of them drinks before noon. You know which clerk keeps the real cipher book under the false one. You know the schedules of the launches, the names of the fixers' local contacts, the shape of their suspicions. Eleven years of friendship bought you entry into their rooms. Spend it."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I walk out of here with my bread, and within a week you'll be worth nothing to them, because Marguerite Thornbury will have learned everything you know by other roads—slower roads, bloodier roads—and they will have learned that you talked to me today. This meeting is already a fact, Ferren. Facts don't care whose side they land on. The only choice left to you is which ledger this morning gets entered in."

That was cruel. I heard it as it left me, heard my father's hardness in it, the diver's flat arithmetic of depth and air and how much pressure a man can stand. But cruelty aimed at rescue is a different animal than cruelty aimed at profit, and I think he heard the difference, because he reached for the bread.

* * *

He ate slowly, like a man remembering food, and while he ate he talked, and what he told us turned my stomach and steadied my resolve in equal measure.

The final draft of the signature pages—the armistice instrument itself, the paper that would end the war and bury Kessel Bay under its ink—was being held not in the delegation offices, as everyone assumed, but aboard the steam launch *Vigilant*, moored at the company wharf. The chief negotiator trusted no land-based safe; the port was full of sympathetic ears, and paper, he believed, was safest on water. Three copies existed. Two traveled with adjutants who never left the ship. The third—the master copy, the one that would actually receive signatures—was kept in a locked dispatch case chained beneath the negotiator's berth, and the negotiator slept aboard every night until the signing.

"The signing," Ferren said, licking crumbs from his thumb, ashamed even of that small pleasure. "It's been moved up. Again. The delegates are frightened of the storm season—if the typhoon closes the port, the treaty dies in committee for another season, and seasons are money. So they've compressed the schedule. Nineteen days became—" he hesitated, hating himself for the number, hating me for needing it— "become nine. Perhaps fewer. They sign within the fortnight, Tomas. After that, amnesty. Everything sealed. Everything forgiven. Everyone forgotten."

Nine days. I thought of the drying loft, of the candle stubs and sworn statements, of Elias Vane moving through the archive at night like a man carrying water in his hands. Nine days to build an annex solid enough that even victors could not refuse to look at it.

"There's more," Ferren said, and now his voice dropped to almost nothing. "They know about Adela Crane."

The name went through me like cold water through cloth.

"She's been seen with the salvage documents. She compared drafts. Someone reported her hesitation—not as loyalty, but as *infection*. If she tries to enter anything at the table, they have a procedure ready. They'll declare her compromised, remove her from the proceedings, replace her with a man named Ostrer who smiles too easily. She gets one chance, Tomas. One motion, one moment, and if she fumbles it, the annex dies with her career and nobody after her will dare pick it up for fifty years."

I closed my eyes. Behind them I saw the bay as it had been—the boats going out gray in the dawn, my father's laugh, the smoke rising the second time, the paper burning, always the paper burning, because these people understood something my people had learned too late: that a village can be rebuilt from ash, but a record cannot be rebuilt from nothing.

When I opened my eyes, Ferren was watching me with his daughter's illness hanging off him like wet rope.

"What happens to me," he asked, "after?"

"After?" I stood, and my joints protested, and I let them. "After, you go home to your daughter. You sit with her in whatever bed they've given her, and you tell her stories, and you grow old, and someday—maybe—she asks you what you did during the bad season, and you decide then how much truth a child's heart can carry. That's what happens to you. That's the mercy. Not because you've earned it, but because killing you makes us like them, and being like them is the one defeat that can't be written around."

He wept then. Quietly, the way divers cry—without noise, because noise costs air—and I stood in the tar-smelling dark and let him, and did not comfort him, and did not leave.

At the door I paused. "One more thing. The *Vigilant*. Who provisions her?"

He wiped his face with both palms. "Local contractors. Water, food, lamp oil. Twice daily. Why?"

"Because," I said, "a ship that eats from the shore can be poisoned from the shore—and I don't mean her food, Ferren. I mean her *paperwork*. Every provision manifest, every chit, every invoice goes through the harbor office. And every piece of paper that goes through the harbor office passes a desk where a certain archivist once worked, and habits die hard in that building." I smiled, and it felt strange on my face, rusty as an old lock. "Tell your handlers you're still useful. Tell them the divers suspect nothing. And then get me the provisioning schedule for the *Vigilant*, down to the hour, and you will have paid for your daughter's lungs ten times over."

"And if they catch me passing it?"

"Then you tell them the truth—that a grieving man fed you bread and asked questions, and you gave up nothing but a grocery list. Which will even be true." I opened the door onto the noise and light of the chandlery floor, the ordinary world going about its ordinary commerce. "Mercy isn't weakness, Ferren. It's strategy. The cruelest thing we can do to those men is beat them with better methods."

* * *

I walked home along the seawall as the afternoon thickened toward storm-light, the sky bruising green over the harbor, the air tasting of iron and coming rain. Nine days. The number walked beside me like a creditor.

At the corner of Rope Walk, Marguerite was waiting—she had a gift for appearing where decisions needed making, as though the port itself bent conversations toward her—and I saw at once that she already knew something had broken open, because hope does that to a face; it rearranges it.

"Well?" she said.

"The master copy sleeps on the water," I told her. "Aboard the *Vigilant*, chained under the negotiator's berth. And the signing has been pulled forward—nine days, maybe less, before the storm locks the harbor."

Her jaw set. I watched her do the arithmetic faster than I had—watched the numbers become a plan behind her eyes, some new geometry of manifests and tides and one hesitant historian's single chance.

"Then we stop preparing to speak," she said quietly, "and start preparing to be impossible to silence. Get word to Elias tonight. And, Tomas—" she touched my sleeve, brief as a bird's landing, "—you gave a traitor bread today. Don't let anyone, including yourself, call it anything but what it was. It was the first shot of the last battle, and it was fired with a loaf."

She turned toward the loft, and above the harbor the first fat drops of the storm began to fall on the wharf boards, drumming out a rhythm like a clock running down—and somewhere out on the blackening water, the *Vigilant* rode at anchor with the fate of Kessel Bay locked beneath a sleeping man's berth, nine days from becoming either the end of the story or the reason we had told it at all.
