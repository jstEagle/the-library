## 29. Nineteen Days Become Three

The news came with the tide, as most terrible things in this port did.

Tomas arrived at the drying loft before dawn, still thin from his weeks in hiding, his coat smelling of bilge and the cheap tobacco the fishermen rolled when they couldn't afford anything better. He didn't knock. He never knocked anymore; knocking was a courtesy for people who had time. He stood in the doorway with the gray light behind him and said, "They've moved it. The signing. Sixteen days sooner."

Marguerite had Aurelia against her shoulder, the child half-asleep, her small fist knotted in Marguerite's collar the way she always did when she sensed trouble before she could understand it. The loft smelled of candle tallow and dried ink and the salt that lived permanently in the beams overhead, and for one long moment Marguerite did not move at all. She felt the words land in her body the way a swell lands on a hull—no drama, just weight, arriving from a direction you couldn't see.

"Three days," she said.

"Nineteen became three." Tomas came inside and lowered himself onto the bench beside the long table where the annex had been growing for weeks, page by page, statement by sworn statement, like coral building itself out of nothing but persistence. "A courier came to the harbor office last night. The delegates are being recalled home early—there's talk of famine in the northern provinces, of a chancellor who wants his signature while he's still popular enough to give it. Elias confirmed it an hour ago. The table is being set."

Aurelia stirred and made a small sound, and Marguerite pressed her lips to the child's hair without thinking about it, the way she breathed without thinking about it. Three days. She had spent eleven months believing she understood what urgency meant. She had dived against deadlines, hidden against deadlines, lied against deadlines. But those had been urgencies of survival, which have a rhythm to them, a tide-like patience. This was something else. This was history closing its hand.

"Then we work," she said, and was surprised at how steady her voice came out, as if it belonged to someone calmer than she was.

* * *

By seven o'clock the loft was full.

They came in ones and twos, in the furtive way they had all learned, none arriving at the same hour or by the same street: old Sefa the diver, whose hands shook now unless she was holding a pen, and then they were stone-steady; Pell, the junior clerk who had smuggled ledger pages out under the waistband of his trousers for two months and had developed a cough he used as cover whenever anyone walked too close; Marta from the laundry rows, who could fold a lie into clean linen better than anyone Marguerite had ever known; two fishermen from the outer shallows who had seen the crates go into the water off Kessel Bay and had kept their mouths shut for a year because keeping your mouth shut was how you stayed alive here.

And in the corner, hooded against recognition even indoors, Adela Crane—the historian who hesitated—copying fair drafts in a hand so precise it looked typeset.

Marguerite stood at the head of the table and looked at them, and thought: *this is what an archive looks like when it is alive.* Not the marble rooms of the company, not the ledgers bound in calfskin behind brass grilles. This. A drying loft that smelled of fish and tallow, a borrowed table scarred with brine stains, twelve people who should by every law of the world be strangers to each other, holding paper as if it were kindling and kindling as if it were paper.

"The treaty secretariat publishes the final text on the morning of the signing," she told them. "Anything entered after the signatures is commentary. Anything not entered before them—" she made herself say it plainly— "never happened. So we have three days to finish a document that took the victors three years to falsify."

Nobody laughed. Nobody despaired either, which Marguerite counted as its own kind of miracle. Sefa unscrewed her ink bottle. Pell sharpened a pencil with a knife that had clearly done harder work. The fishermen pulled stools up to the table like men sitting down to mend nets.

"We divide the work," Marguerite said. "The registry comparison goes to Adela—her access to the official drafts is the only reason we know what they've changed. Tomas's deposition gets copied in full, every word, even the parts that name names above the company. Especially those. The divers' statements about the crates need countersignatures—we use the pearl-sack seals, they're recognized as trade marks, no one can claim we forged them. And the dispatch." She touched the oilcloth packet where it sat beneath the table's center board, where it had lived for weeks, where it would live until the last possible hour. "The dispatch goes in last. It names the author of the raid. It is the spine of the whole body. If everything else is seized, the dispatch must survive."

"And you?" asked Marta, looking up from her stack of linen-pressed pages. "What will you be doing, while the rest of us ruin our eyes?"

Marguerite looked down the table at them all—at the diver and the clerk, the laundress and the fishermen, the traitor-turned-ally and the historian who had crossed a line she could never cross back over.

"I'll be writing the part no one else can write," she said. "The introduction. The account of the child. They wanted a face forgotten. We're going to put it first."

* * *

The days stopped behaving like days.

They thinned and stretched at once, the way time does underwater, when the light above you shivers and your lungs begin their slow argument with your will. Marguerite worked eighteen hours at a stretch and slept in fragments on the cot in the corner, Aurelia tucked against her spine, waking not from noise but from silence—the sudden absence of pens scratching—and lying there a moment in the dark, listening to make sure the sound had returned.

On the second day Elias came at dusk, and she saw at once that something had been done to him.

He tried to hide it the way he hid everything, in posture and precision—coat brushed, cuffs squared—but his hands were wrong. There was a scrape across two knuckles, scabbed dark, and when he reached for the teapot his wrist trembled once before he mastered it.

"They searched my offices," he said, without preamble, because they had passed the stage of preambles somewhere around the storm. "Officially an inventory audit. Unofficially, three men who didn't know what an inventory is. They took nothing. That's what worries me. They took nothing because they already know what's missing, and they're no longer looking for documents. They're looking for people."

"Names?"

"Not yet. But the fixers were seen at the harbor master's again last night, and Pell says the passenger manifests are being checked twice now, once by the company and once by men nobody employs officially." He turned his cup slowly on its saucer, aligning it with the table edge, and Marguerite watched him do it and understood that this was how he prayed—with order, with small alignments, with the geometry of objects when the geometry of the world failed. "Marguerite. If this goes wrong—"

"It won't go wrong quietly," she said. "That's all I can promise. But listen to me." She reached across and stilled his cup with two fingers, and he looked at her hand instead of her face, and then at her face, and whatever he saw there seemed to steady him more than any argument could have. "For eleven months I believed history was something that happened to other people, in other places, written by other hands. Then a child was left on my step with an instruction to forget her, and I learned that history is exactly as big or as small as what you refuse to let go of. We are not going to lose. We may not win—but we are not going to lose, because losing would mean letting them define what happened, and I have decided"—she heard her own voice drop, go quiet, go certain— "I have decided that I am done being defined."

Elias covered her hand with his scraped one. Neither of them said anything else for a while. Above them the loft beams ticked with the night's cooling, and below, in the town, the lamps of the company offices burned late—burned, Marguerite knew, over their own final copies, their own clean versions, their beautiful lies getting their last polish.

"Three days ago I thought we needed nineteen," Elias said finally. "Now I think nineteen might have killed us. There's a kind of courage that only works fast."

"There's a kind that only works tired," Marguerite said. "You stop being afraid of falling when you're already falling."

* * *

They worked through the second night entire.

Somewhere past the middle of it, when the candles had burned down to pools and the exhaustion had gone through its stages—irritation, gallows humor, silence—and come out the other side into something almost like clarity, Sefa began to sing.

It was barely singing. An old diving song, half under her breath, the kind the women sang on the boats to keep rhythm with their breath-holds, a song about the bottom of the sea being patient and the top of the sea being a liar. One by one the others picked it up—not loudly, not as performance, but the way exhausted people do things: because it was easier together than alone. Even Adela, who could not have known the words, hummed the line when it came round, and the fishermen kept the beat with their palms flat on the table, softly, softly, and Marguerite sat with Aurelia awake against her chest, watching the child's eyes move from face to face in the candlelight, and thought: *remember this. Whatever else happens, some part of you was here for this.*

When the song ended, no one spoke for a moment. Then Sefa dipped her pen and signed her name to the last divers' statement with a flourish that belonged to a much younger woman, and pushed the page down the table, and the work resumed—but changed. Lighter, somehow, though no slower. As if the singing had wrung something out of the room that had been making everything heavier.

It was near four in the morning, gray seeping under the shutters, when Pell laid the final copied page beside the others and stepped back.

Marguerite stood at the end of the table and looked at what they had made.

It did not look like much. Paper, perhaps two hundred sheets, sewn at the margin with linen thread because wire could be claimed as tampering. The registry comparisons in Adela's flawless script. The depositions, each signed and sealed. The divers' accounts of the crates going into the water. The introduction in her own hand, the account of a note and a wax seal half-melted and a face someone had ordered forgotten, and a name given instead: *Aurelia*. And at the center, wrapped separately in oilcloth, ready for the last hour, the dispatch that named the raid's author.

Two hundred sheets. Against three years of falsification, against the full weight of the Meridian Trading Company and the chancelleries behind it, against armed men and polite historians and the enormous gravity of the official record.

"Marta," Marguerite said, and her voice cracked on the first syllable and she let it, "how many copies?"

"Three. One for the vault, one for the signing table, one that doesn't exist officially—which means it's the only one guaranteed to survive."

"And the third?"

Marta smiled for the first time in two days. "Sewn into pearl sacks. Already aboard a boat that fishes the outer shallows until we send for it."

Marguerite nodded and found, to her surprise, that her eyes were wet. Not from grief—or not only. There is a particular tears that comes only from finishing something you began in terror, and she let it come, standing in the gray light among the tallow smoke and the salt-stained beams, surrounded by divers and clerks and liars-for-good and one converted historian, all of them swaying slightly on their feet, all of them filthy with ink and candle soot, all of them awake.

"Tomas," she said. "How long since the signing was moved?"

"Sixty hours."

"Then we have less than three days left. Sleep in shifts. Eat what Marta brings. Touch nothing in the town that isn't necessary." She gathered the master copy into its sacking with hands that ached in every joint and held it a moment against her chest, against Aurelia, so that the child's heartbeat rested on the other side of the paper like a second seal. "Day after tomorrow, this goes beside the treaty. Everything ends or begins at that table."

* * *

She was carrying the bundle down the loft stairs at dawn, Aurelia on her hip and the annex heavy against her ribs, when she saw the harbor.

The delegation ships had come in overnight—three of them now where yesterday there had been one, riding at anchor in the flat gold light, their ensigns limp, their decks crowded with clerks and cipher-men and, on the quarterdeck of the nearest, a knot of figures in the black coats of the victors' historians, measuring the port with their eyes the way appraisers measure a house before the sale.

And on the quay below, unloading crates under guard, worked a line of men whom nobody employed officially.

Pell materialized at her elbow, breathing hard from the run up from the lower town. He didn't point. He had learned better. He simply said, very quietly:

"The vault. Someone broke the third lock on the company vault last night. Elias is being held for questioning—and the annex was supposed to go in tonight."

Marguerite stood on the stairs with everything she had built against her heart and her daughter's breath warm against her neck, and looked at the black coats on the quarterdeck, and felt the exhaustion in her body turn over, like a diver turning at the bottom of a dive, and begin the long kick upward toward air and light.

"Then it goes in tomorrow," she said, "by another road. Wake Adela. And find me a laundry cart."
