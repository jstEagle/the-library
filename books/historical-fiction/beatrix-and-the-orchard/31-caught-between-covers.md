## 31. Caught Between Covers

The knock came at the hour when the lamps along the harbor begin to lose their argument with the dawn—that thin grey time when even the gulls have not yet decided on the day. Marguerite was awake because Aurelia was awake, the child fretting against her shoulder with the particular restlessness that meant teeth, or weather, or some animal knowledge older than either.

She heard boots in the lane first. Not one pair—several, moving with the unhurried coordination of men who had been told where to go and did not need to hurry. Then fists on Elias's door across the courtyard, and his voice, muffled and terribly courteous, saying something she could not make out and understood anyway.

Aurelia stiffened against her chest. Even a baby not yet two years old knows the sound of authority arriving before it is wanted.

"Shh," Marguerite breathed into the down of her hair. "Shh, little pearl."

Through the shutter slats she watched them bring him out. Two soldiers flanking, a third carrying an armload of his papers—not roughly, which frightened her more than roughness would have. They were taking the documents carefully, the way you carry evidence, the way you carry a case already decided. Elias did not struggle. He turned once at the gate and looked directly at her window, and though he could not possibly have seen her through the slats in that light—or so she told herself afterward, lying awake—his gaze found it and rested there for the length of one long breath.

Then he inclined his head. Almost imperceptibly. A man tipping his hat to a lady at a garden party.

Go on, the gesture said. Finish it.

They put him in a cart and took him toward the registry, and the lane emptied, and the gulls finally made up their minds about the morning, and Marguerite stood in the dark of her room with her heart going like a diver's pulse at depth—slow, enormous, using its air carefully.

* * *

The last sworn statement lay beneath the floorboard under the washstand, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp the way everything precious in this port was wrapped: pearls, letters, identities. She had written it out herself three times until her hand could reproduce the words without her mind stumbling on them. It was Tomas's deposition, memorized in a prison visitation room and carried out in his voice inside her skull, and it named names—the author of the Kessel Bay orders, the company men who certified the false registry, the date the village burned and the later date it burned again, this time on paper. Fourteen witnesses' marks at the bottom. Some could not write their names; they had pressed inked thumbs to the page, and the whorls of their prints were still faintly visible, little storms of identity, irreplaceable as fingerprints on glass.

Elias was supposed to carry it. That had been the plan, refined over six weeks of candle stubs and whispered arithmetic: the archivist, with his legitimate credentials, would walk the statement into Adela Crane's rooms at the negotiation house as an inter-office delivery, unremarkable as a ledger. Now the archivist sat in a cart with his papers confiscated, and the plan lay on the floor of Marguerite's room wrapped in oilcloth, waiting to see what she was made of.

She dressed Aurelia first—clean dress, the blue one, because if this day was going to look at her child it would look at a child cared for—and then she took out the statement and considered it as she had never allowed herself to consider it before. Not as a weapon. As a thing made by hands. Divers' hands, clerks' hands, a survivor's hands remembering in the dark of a cell so that paper could hold what flesh might not.

"They took your godfather's friend," she told Aurelia, buttoning the small buttons. "So now it's mine to carry. That's how it works. That's how it has always worked. Someone carries."

The negotiation house stood on the ridge above the port in a building that had been a governor's residence back when governors still bothered to visit, all white columns and louvered shutters and a drive swept twice daily by men whose names appeared nowhere. Sentries at the gate. A clerk's desk just inside, where visitors were recorded in a book with three columns: name, business, host. Marguerite had rehearsed the crossing of that threshold a hundred ways in the night, and every version failed on the same reef—she was an indentured pearl diver with no business and no host, and the book would say so, and the book would be read by the wrong people before the hour was out.

But there were other doors. There were always other doors in a port town; the sea taught you that. Water finds the seam.

She went instead at midmorning to the hotel where the delegation lodged, carrying a covered basket—bread, small sweet bananas, a jar of preserved lime—and wearing her best and only good dress, the dove-grey one with the collar, laundered to stiffness. The lobby fan turned overhead with the languid industry of things that do not know they are decorative. A concierge looked up from behind a brass bell.

"I've brought refreshment for Madame Crane," Marguerite said, in the accent she had learned from watching company wives, softening nothing but smoothing everything. "From the harbour market. She asked particularly for the lime preserve—the ones bottled at the mission." This was true. Adela Crane had remarked on them once, at the end of a tea that had been an interrogation, and Marguerite had filed the remark away the way she filed everything now: as material.

The concierge consulted a board of room cards, hesitated over the propriety of a woman with a basket, and was rescued by the arrival of the delegation's second secretary hurrying past, who said, "Madame Crane isn't lodging here anymore, she moved to rooms above the print office on Calle Merced—something about being nearer the proofs," and was gone up the stairs before Marguerite could arrange her face.

The print office. Of course. The historians had taken rooms above the place where the treaty itself would be set in type, because proximity to power was a kind of power, and these people wasted nothing, not even a staircase.

* * *

Calle Merced smelled of hot lead and rags boiled for paper pulp. The print shop occupied the ground floor with its clamor and its apprentices, and a wooden stair at the side climbed to two doors under the eaves. Marguerite climbed with Aurelia on her hip, the basket on her arm, the oilcloth packet flat against her ribs beneath the grey dress, safety-pinned where a shawl would sit. The child's weight was familiar enough that she could forget it, mostly, until a stair creaked and small hands gripped her collar and reminded her that she was not one person ascending but two, and that everything she was about to do, she was doing in front of a witness who could not yet testify.

She knocked at the door with the printed card: A. CRANE, HISTORICAL COMMISSION.

"It's open," came the voice she remembered, dry as old vellum, "and if you're the boy with the proofs, leave them and mind the wet type."

Marguerite opened the door. Adela Crane sat at a table buried to the elbows in paper—a woman of fifty-odd with spectacles pushed up into greying hair, sleeves rolled, ink on three fingers, looking less like the architect of official memory than like a laundress drowning in her own wash. The room held two camp beds, one neatly made and one not, a stove, and perhaps four thousand documents in progressions of order that only she could perceive.

Adela looked up. The recognition moved across her face like weather—one moment cloudless professional blankness, the next the specific shadow of their tea, of the questions asked too gently, of the lies answered so beautifully that neither woman had believed them.

"You," Adela said.

"Me," Marguerite agreed. "I brought the lime preserve after all. It seemed a shame to waste them."

For a moment neither moved. Below them the press thundered through the floorboards like a heartbeat in the belly of some great patient animal. Then Adela removed her spectacles, set them down with exaggerated care, and said, "Shut the door. The boy comes up at noon and he asks no questions but I'd rather not test him."

Marguerite shut the door and did not sit, though Adela gestured. She stood with Aurelia shifting on her hip and said, quietly, in her own voice now, the port voice, the diver's voice: "You hesitated. At the tea. You asked me about the infant twice, and the second time you weren't asking as a fixer. You were asking as someone who wanted the answer to be innocent and knew it wasn't going to be. I've thought about that for weeks. I don't have anyone else to think about."

"That's very flattering," Adela said. "It's also very dangerous, and you should know I report conversations like this one within the day. It's my function. I compile. I don't editorialize."

"Then report this." Marguerite reached under the shawl-pin and drew out the oilcloth packet, and laid it on the table among the drafts, and unwrapped it with the deliberateness of a woman laying out a pearl of great price. The pages were warm from her body. "Fourteen witnesses. The Kessel Bay orders, transcribed from originals recovered from the water off Blackrock Shoal. The name of the officer who signed them. The certification of the false registry, dated and initialed, with the true registry described page by page by the clerk who kept it. And a deposition from Tomas Reyes, taken under oath in the presence of two witnesses, memorized in prison and carried out in his voice because they take paper from a man but they cannot yet take what a man remembers."

Adela did not touch it. Her hands stayed flat on the table on either side, framing it without enclosing it, the way you'd frame something hot.

"Do you know what I'm compiling?" she said. "Do you understand the document I am making? It will be bound in calfskin. It will have an index. In thirty years schoolchildren will learn from it that this coast settled its differences peaceably, that regrettable excesses occurred on both sides, and that the armistice of 1911 closed the matter with dignity. That is my commission. That is what I am *for*."

"And yet," Marguerite said, "you moved lodgings to be near the proofs."

The silence stretched. Downstairs the press took a fresh sheet with a sound like a caught breath.

"I moved lodgings," Adela said at last, slowly, as if confessing to something far more scandalous than geography, "because I kept finding discrepancies. Small ones. A village listed as *depopulated* rather than destroyed. A registry entry dated three days after the fire that describes the ledgers as being in excellent condition. I am a historian, Mrs.—Thornbury, is it? I am a historian, and I found myself writing a chronicle in which the paper survived the flames that killed the people who wrote it, and somewhere around the fourth draft I began to be ashamed of my own sentences." She took off her spectacles entirely and rubbed the bridge of her nose. "Ashamed of my own *sentences*. Do you know what that is like? To distrust the very instrument of your trade?"

"I know what it's like to distrust your hands," Marguerite said. "I've used them for the company since I was fourteen. Every pearl I brought up paid down a debt that was calculated to grow faster than I could dive. I used to tell myself history was something that happened to other people—people in capitals, people with titles. Then someone left a baby at my door with a note telling me to forget her face." She shifted Aurelia forward, deliberately, into the lamplight from the window. "Look at her."

Adela looked. It was not a glance; it was a long, unwilling, professional looking, the look of a woman who had spent months cataloguing the dead of Kessel Bay from manifests and knew exactly which family surnames carried those cheekbones, that set of the eyes. Her lips parted. Whatever she said next, she said to the child and not to Marguerite, and it was barely a whisper:

"You're evidence."

"She's a person," Marguerite said. "That's what makes her evidence. That's the whole point everyone keeps missing. Your annexes describe casualties in aggregate. Here is one of them, and she is cutting two new teeth, and she likes the blue dress best, and if your finished record enters without dissent, she will grow up a citizen of a country whose founding document says her village was depopulated by weather." She leaned forward and laid her hand flat on the sworn statement, on the fourteen thumbprints like little storms. "There is a procedure. I've learned it from Elias—learned more about procedure in six weeks than in nine years of indenture. A dissenting document may be entered at or before the signing. Entered, indexed, bound beside the treaty forever. Not stopping anything—I know better than to ask for miracles. Just standing there. On the record. Saying: this happened, and here are the names, and here is the face."

"Elias Vane." Adela's eyes sharpened. "He was detained this morning. I heard it at breakfast. His rooms sealed, his credentials suspended pending review of irregularities in the coastal registry."

"Yes."

"Then this document—" Adela touched the edge of the packet at last, one finger, as if testing water temperature. "This document has no custodian. No certified presenter. A dissenting annex doesn't simply walk itself onto the table, Mrs. Thornbury. It requires sponsorship from within the commission. It requires a historian of record willing to attach her name."

The press downstairs began a new run, rhythmic, inexorable. Marguerite became aware that her heart had gone very quiet, the way it did at eighteen fathoms when the air in her lungs mattered more than fear.

"That's why I came to you," she said, "instead of to the other one. The one who interviews people over tea and smiles at the end like a man closing a ledger. You hesitated. I'm betting a child's whole history on a hesitation."

Adela Crane rose, walked to the window, and stood looking down at Calle Merced for a long time. When she turned around, she had put her spectacles back on, and behind them her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely clear.

"The signing convenes in—" she consulted a card pinned above the table, and her voice changed as she read it, flattening with alarm— "they've posted the revised schedule. Not nineteen days. Three. The delegates accelerated. Everything to be entered must be submitted to the secretariat by tomorrow evening for inclusion in the final folio." She looked at the packet, then at Aurelia, then at Marguerite, and something in her face settled, like sediment finding the bottom of a jar. "Tomorrow evening. Which means tonight I compare every claim in this deposition against the salvaged originals I have been hiding in my second camp bed like a woman hiding love letters. If your paper contradicts itself even once—if one thumbprint rings false—they will use it to discredit everything, and Elias rots, and you hang, figuratively or otherwise, and I go down in the footnotes as the historian who broke her commission for a laundry basket."

"And if it holds?"

"Then tomorrow I stand up at the secretariat," Adela said, "in front of men who sign countries like cheques, and I enter a dissenting annex into the permanent record, and I attach my name." She picked up the top page, tilted it to the light, and studied the storm-whorl of the first thumbprint. "Bring me the originals you recovered. All of them. Tonight, after the shop closes—use the alley stair, the boy finishes at seven."

"There's one more thing," Marguerite said, and found her throat tight around it. "Elias. He took the fall for the registry irregularities. He'll be held through the signing unless someone with standing demands otherwise. A sponsor of record could—"

"A sponsor of record," Adela said drily, "could request commission access to detained personnel for the purpose of verifying documentary provenance. It's in the procedures. I told you—I don't editorialize. But I do read the rules." She was already clearing space at her table, sweeping drafts into ordered stacks with the economy of a woman who had waited her whole careful life for a reason to do precisely this. "Go home. Feed the child. Sleep if you can manage it, which you can't. Come back at seven by the alley with everything you have, and Mrs. Thornbury—"

Marguerite paused at the door, Aurelia's cheek pressed to her collarbone, the afternoon heat pressing in from the sea.

"—when this is done," Adela said, not looking up from the first page, "someone should write down how it felt to carry this. The official record will note the dates and the signatures. It takes a different kind of historian to record what a mother's arms weigh."

Marguerite went down the wooden stair into the lead-and-lime smell of the street, and the press thundered above and below her, printing the future one sheet at a time, and she walked home fast with the empty basket swinging, counting hours: seven until the alley, twenty-four until the secretariat closed, three days until the table where countries would be signed away—and somewhere in the town's holding cells, a man who had tipped his head to her window as if to say *finish it*.

She intended to. God help everyone, she intended to.
