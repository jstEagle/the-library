## 32. The Signing Table

The hall had been dressed for posterity, and Adela Crane felt the dressing like a costume fitted by strangers.

Someone had hung bunting in the colors of the Protectorate and the Republic both, knotted together at the center of the ceiling with a bow that no sailor on earth could have tied. The long table at the front had been covered in green baize—green, the color of ledgers, of counting houses, of money changing hands under the pretense of law—and set with inkwells of cut glass that caught the morning light through the tall windows and threw little fractured rainbows across the documents, as though the paper itself were trying to become something brighter than it was. Fans turned slowly overhead. Beyond the shutters, half-open against the heat, she could hear the port: gulls, hammers, the groan of a cargo derrick, the ordinary music of a town going about its business while its fate was signed into a book.

Adela stood at the edge of the delegation's cluster, her folio held against her chest like a breastplate. She had dressed with particular care—grey silk, high collar, the small silver pen that her father had given her when she'd passed her examinations, twenty years ago, in a city of fog and libraries that now felt like a previous life lived by a different woman. The pen was warm from her hand. Everything else in the room was cool, arranged, airless.

*This is how history is made,* she thought, watching the delegates take their seats. *Not in blood. In furniture.*

The Meridian Trading Company's representative arrived first—Undersecretary Hollis, a man built entirely out of waistcoats, whose smile operated independently of his eyes. Then the Protectorate's commissioners, then the Republic's, each of them preceded by aides carrying document cases with brass corners, each of them shaking hands in the prescribed order, left hand clasping right forearm, the grip of men who had learned diplomacy from manuals. The Kessel Bay clause was item fourteen. It would be reached before luncheon. Adela knew the running order because she had written it; the historians' office had been tasked with sequencing the proceedings so that nothing awkward followed anything sensitive. Item fourteen would be read aloud in the sanitized language they had all agreed upon—"the regrettable irregularities of the coastal campaign"—and initialed without discussion, and the room would move on to fishing rights.

She had spent nine days drafting the annex in secret. Nine nights comparing the certified registry—the false one, the one Elias Vane had stamped with his own trembling integrity—with the salvaged originals the storm had coughed up onto the mudflats, crusted in salt and silt and undeniable. Nine days of cross-referencing testimony she was not officially permitted to possess: sworn statements from divers and dock clerks, a deposition recited from prison memory, a waterlogged dispatch naming names that appeared nowhere else in the official record. And one marginal note, added in a firm feminine hand, documenting the birth and rescue of an infant whose existence the annex made legally necessary to acknowledge.

*Annex D,* they were calling it in her private inventory. A letter chosen precisely because it sounded administrative. Because the most radical document in the building should wear the dullest name available.

"Dr. Crane." Hollis materialized at her elbow, smelling of bay rum and confidence. "You've the fair copies? His Excellency wants the presentation texts on the table before the Republic's delegation sits."

"They're being brought up now," Adela said. Her voice did what she had trained it to do for twenty years: nothing. "All materials are accounted for."

"All materials." He smiled at the phrase, pleased by it, the way a man is pleased by a well-kept garden he has never once knelt in. "Good. There's been talk of—irregularities, you understand. Loose papers. Rumors of unauthorized compilations." His eyes performed a leisurely circuit of the hall, touching each clerk, each soldier at the doors, each window. "If anything of that nature surfaces today, it would embarrass everyone. Most especially the historians."

"I can't imagine anything surfacing," Adela said, "that wasn't already true."

Hollis laughed—a short bark, genuinely amused—and moved away to greet the Republic's chief commissioner. Adela let out her breath slowly. Somewhere in that sentence, buried under the flat professional surface, she had confessed everything, and he had heard nothing. That, she thought, was the whole tragedy of empire in miniature: it could not imagine that truth might be spoken directly into its ear.

The delegates sat. The reading began.

* * *

For two hours, the armistice proceeded exactly as designed.

A secretary with a beautiful tenor voice read the clauses in French first, then English, then the trade-pidgin that served as the port's common tongue, and after each reading the relevant commissioners initialed the margin in colored inks—black for the Protectorate, blue for the Company, violet for the Republic, a liturgy of pigments that Adela found herself cataloguing despite herself. Twenty years of training did not switch off. She noted the hesitation of the Republic's delegate at clause seven, the way the Protectorate's commissioner blotted his signature twice at clause eleven, the moment when the Company's counsel leaned over to whisper something that made the Republic's second-in-command go pale and initial immediately thereafter. All of it went into the part of her mind she had come to think of as the true archive—not the official record, but the record behind the record, the one made of hesitations and sweat and ink pressed too hard.

Then item fourteen.

"The parties acknowledge," the tenor voice read, smooth as poured cream, "certain irregularities arising during the coastal operations of the preceding year, including the unfortunate incident at the settlement designated in company records as Kessel Bay, which irregularities the parties agree to address through the amnesty provisions enumerated in clause fifteen—"

"Point of procedure."

The voice came from the back of the hall, and every head turned, and Adela's heart slammed once against her ribs and then went very quiet, the way it does at the top of a dive.

Elias Vane stood between the double doors, holding a leather portfolio against his chest in exactly the posture she herself had worn an hour ago. He looked terrible—she could see it even across the distance, the sleeplessness printed on his face, the collar of his coat dark at the edges—and he looked magnificent, the way a man looks when he has finally stopped pretending. Behind him, admitted under some pretext she would learn later, stood Marguerite Thornbury with a bundle in her arms, and old Tomas Reyes with his cap crushed in his fists, and a scattering of others: divers, clerks, faces from the drying loft, standing along the wall like a congregation that had walked out of one church and into another.

"On what grounds?" the chairman asked, in the tone of a man asking who has let a dog into the drawing room.

"Grounds of completeness," Elias said. His voice carried. Whatever fear he owned, he had set it down somewhere outside the door. "Article three of the procedural code governing treaty instruments requires that all material evidence bearing upon any clause be either incorporated or formally entered as a dissenting annex prior to signature. Clause fourteen references the Kessel Bay operations. This—" he lifted the portfolio, "—is material evidence. Sworn testimony. Original documents. Certified discrepancies between the company registry and recovered originals. I am the custodian of record for this archive, and I am duty-bound to enter it."

"You are," said the Company's counsel, rising with the unhurried menace of deep water, "a dismissed archivist. Your dismissal was executed yesterday afternoon. You have no custody of anything."

"My dismissal," Elias said, "was executed by men named in this portfolio. Which is rather the point."

The room made a sound—a collective intake, the sound of forty powerful people discovering simultaneously that they were interested. Adela watched Hollis's face perform a small civil war: outrage wrestling with the sudden, unwelcome arithmetic of witnesses standing in plain view, of a portfolio that might already have copies, of a room full of journalists' gallery and neutral observers where a scandal could not be quietly drowned.

"Chairman." Adela heard her own voice and marveled at its steadiness. She was already walking forward, folio open, moving toward the table as if summoned. "As senior historian of the commission, I am obligated to report that I have independently verified portions of the material Mr. Vane references. Cross-referenced against salvaged originals recovered after the storm of the ninth. My verification memorandum is appended." She laid her folio beside the green baize, beside the treaty text itself, close enough that the two documents touched. "I cannot certify clause fourteen as historically accurate in its current language. I can only certify it as incomplete."

"Incomplete." The Protectorate's commissioner said the word as if tasting spoiled milk. "Doctor, the language was agreed—"

"Language can be agreed," Adela said. "Facts cannot be un-agreed retroactively. What the gentlemen may vote upon—what procedure permits—is whether this record enters the proceedings as a dissenting annex, preserved alongside the treaty for all future consultation, or whether it is refused entry. Refusal, I am bound to inform the chair, must itself be minuted, with reasons stated on the record, and my dissent from that refusal recorded." She paused, and let them see her look at each of them in turn, unhurried, appraising, the way she had once appraised manuscripts. "Gentlemen. I have spent my career studying how nations explain themselves afterward. I promise you: the minutes of this hour will outlive every man in this room. Decide which sentence you wish to be quoted in."

Silence. A fan creaked overhead. Outside, absurdly, a fish-seller cried her wares, the ordinary world insisting on itself.

It was the Republic's chief commissioner who broke it—an old man with a diplomat's face and, Adela thought, possibly a survivor's memory. He turned the annex pages slowly, reading the way she had taught students to read: checking sources, weighing weight. When he reached the dispatch—the waterlogged one, the one with the raid's author named in fading iron-gall ink—he looked up, and his eyes found the Company's counsel, and something passed between the two men that had nothing to do with words.

"The chair notes," he said at last, "that the procedural code is, regrettably, unambiguous." A dry pause. "Regrettably."

"Objection entered," snapped the Company's counsel.

"Noted. Minuted. Overruled—by a margin of this chair's casting authority, exercised under protest from the Company's delegation, whose objection is recorded in full." The old commissioner set down the annex almost tenderly. "The instrument shall proceed to signature with Annex D appended. Let the record show the annex carries no force to alter terms. It alters nothing." He glanced down at the pages again—at the list of names, the burned village, the child's documented origin. "It preserves."

* * *

They signed anyway.

That was the thing Adela understood, standing at the corner of the table with her silver pen unused in her hand while the commissioners bent over their columns: the annex changed nothing about the signing, and that was precisely its victory and precisely its wound. Clause fifteen passed. Amnesty, blanket and gleaming. The men named in the waterlogged dispatch would walk out of this hall protected forever, their crimes converted into history's footnotes, their pensions intact. She watched one of them—a broad, comfortable man with a florid face, sitting among the Company's observers—watch the annex being bound into the record with an expression of mild distaste, the expression of a householder observing weather. He would never stand trial. He would grow old. He would perhaps, in some drawing room, refer to "the unpleasantness on the coast," and be believed.

And yet.

And yet the annex existed. Bound. Numbered. Entered. Every name in it—Tomas Reyes, the diver Sefina Oduya, the clerk Pellham, the drowned and the burned and the disappeared—every name was now a fact that officials would have to lie around rather than simply erase. Future historians, Adela thought, her own kind, hungry and skeptical and patient, would find it. Would pull the thread. Would ask why a registry was certified twice in two different hands, why a village appeared in shipping manifests and vanished from population rolls, why an infant's origin was documented in a treaty annex of all places. The truth would not win. But it would survive, and surviving, it would wait. She had learned enough history to know that waiting was not nothing. Waiting was sometimes everything.

The last signature dried. The chairman rose. Somewhere a band began to play, badly, in the courtyard.

In the confusion of adjournment—delegates clustering, aides gathering papers, the journalists' gallery emptying upward like startled birds—Adela crossed the hall to where Marguerite Thornbury stood holding the child. Up close the woman looked wrung out, salt-white with exhaustion, and utterly unbroken, a combination Adela had seen only in survivors of shipwrecks and sieges.

"He'll be released within the day," Adela said quietly. "Dismissal stands—he insisted, actually, when I offered to argue it. Said he'd rather be free of them than vindicated by them." She allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "He also said to tell you the vault cost him nothing he wasn't glad to lose."

Marguerite's arms tightened around the bundle. The baby—Aurelia, the annex said, *named in the margins, documented*—slept through the ruin and salvage of nations, one small fist curled against her cheek.

"It's done," Marguerite said. Not triumphant. Weighing it, the way one weighs ballast. "It's in the book."

"It's in the book," Adela agreed. "Forever. Which means, Mrs.—Miss Thornbury, that from this morning onward, your daughter's history is a matter of public record, and no one—not a company, not a delegation, not a fixer with a ledger—can make her face forgotten again." She looked down at the sleeping child, and felt the strange double grief of her profession: joy for what was saved, mourning for everything the saving had come too late to stop. "It tastes like compromise," she said, surprising herself by saying it aloud. "I want you to know I know that. Amnesty is a wound dressed as a bandage. But the annex—"

"The annex is the scar," Marguerite finished softly. "Scars remember."

Outside, the band stumbled into a patriotic march, and the delegates emerged blinking into the sunlight to shake hands for the photographers, and history, having been made, went looking for its lunch.

Adela gathered her folio, and her silver pen, and the twenty years of complicity she intended to spend the rest of her life paying down—and did not yet know that by autumn her name would be struck from the commission rolls, nor that she would come, hat in hand, to a drying loft above the harbor, asking to be taught how the other half of the record was kept.
