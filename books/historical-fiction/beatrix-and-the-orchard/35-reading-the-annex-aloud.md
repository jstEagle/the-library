## 35. Reading the Annex Aloud

*Marguerite*

The drying loft smelled of salt and bamboo and the ghost of fish that had hung there for thirty years before it ever hung a secret. Marguerite had asked Old Sefa to clear the racks three days early—no small thing, with a catch this good coming in—and now the long poles stood empty overhead like the ribs of some patient whale, strings swaying where nets once dried. Candlelight climbed those ribs and pooled on the floorboards, where forty-odd people sat shoulder to shoulder on crates, on folded sails, on the bare wood itself, as if the loft were a chapel and the tide below its slow organ.

She had not expected so many. Divers came down from the headland with their children asleep against their collars. Clerks from the registry arrived in twos and threes, still in their company shirts, buttons undone at the throat like men loosening a collar they had worn too long. The women who mended sails brought bread. Even the harbor pilot came, a man who had never in twenty years said more than *port* or *starboard*, and he stood at the back with his cap in his hands.

Word had gone out quietly, the way everything moved here: paper travels by hand, truth by whisper. *Tonight. The loft. It will be read aloud.*

Aurelia slept in a laundry basket lined with Marguerite's good shawl, tucked behind the partition where the candlelight couldn't find her face. Marguerite checked her twice in ten minutes and then made herself stop checking, because what she felt was not fear exactly but its opposite—a fullness, a pressure behind the sternum, the sensation of having carried something heavy up many stairs and finally reaching the top.

Elias stood by the table at the front of the room. On the table lay the annex: eleven pages of close-written foolscap, blotted and corrected, smelling faintly of the lamp oil under which most of it had been born, wrapped now in oilcloth because the roof leaked when it wanted to. Beside it, squared neatly, sat the certified-false registry of Kessel Bay, the one his own hand had stamped, and beside that the salvaged dispatches, water-stained to the color of weak tea, their ink legible as scripture.

He looked, Marguerite thought, like a man about to defuse something. Which he was.

"You understand what this is," Elias said. His voice carried in the low room without effort; years of reading inventory aloud to skeptical auditors had trained it. "And you understand what it is not. The armistice is signed. The amnesty is signed. No one in this loft will see a tribunal, a trial, an apology with seals on it. What we have—" he touched the pages—"is a dissenting annex. Entered into the record. Bound into the treaty text itself, where every government that signed will have to shelve it. Where any historian who comes after will have to open it."

Silence. Somewhere below, water slapped a piling in a rhythm like a patient heart.

"It does not give anything back," he said. "It only refuses to let the taking be called weather."

Tomas Reyes rose from his crate near the front. He had been out of the company prison eleven days and he still moved like a man listening for a door. When he spoke, his voice was roughened, but steady.

"I gave my testimony twice," he said. "Once to this woman"—he nodded at Marguerite—"with a candle burning down, and once inside me, in a cell, where no one could take the paper. I have waited nine years to hear Kessel Bay said out loud in this port without anyone lowering their voice." He sat down again. That was all. It was enough. Several people shifted toward the table the way iron filings answer a magnet.

Marguerite took the first page. Her hands did not shake, which surprised her; she had expected them to.

"Annex to the Armistice Record," she read. "Entered over objection. Compiled from sworn statements of the undersigned, being survivors, witnesses, and officers of record of the events at Kessel Bay and adjacent waters, in the year 1902."

Then the names. She had insisted the names come first, before any of it—before the dates, the tonnages, the orders. Names were the part the official record had left blank. *Absent. Uninhabited at time of survey,* the historians had written of a village of four hundred souls.

"Reyes, Tomas, fisherman. Reyes, Alba, his wife. Reyes, Pilar, aged four. Cruz, Mateo. Cruz, Inés. Cruz, the child Benedicto, aged six months—"

A sound came from the third row. A woman stood up slowly, one hand pressed flat over her mouth, the other gripping her son's sleeve as if the floor might tilt. It was Alba Reyes. Marguerite had met her only twice; since the prison release, Tomas's wife had kept to their house at the edge of the palisade, and Marguerite had understood why. But she was here tonight, and when her own name came off Marguerite's tongue—*Reyes, Alba*—she made a small sound, half gasp, half laugh, the sound of someone hearing themselves pronounced alive.

"They wrote it down," Alba said, not to anyone, to the room, to the ribs, to the dark. "They put us in a book. We are in a book."

Her son held her arm and she wept standing up, and nobody told her it was all right, because it was not all right, and everyone in the loft knew the difference between comfort and witness.

Marguerite read on.

* * *

*Elias*

He had read the annex perhaps two hundred times—in draft, in correction, in fair copy, in the version with Adela Crane's penciled objections in the margins, in the final version with those objections answered or conceded. He knew every clause the way he knew the registry's catalog system, which is to say he could have recited it blind, in the dark, underwater.

None of that prepared him for hearing it in this room.

In the archive, words were weightless. They were entries, cross-references, provenance chains. Here, each name landed on the floorboards with a sound. He watched it happen: *Cruz, Mateo* — a lean old diver Elias recognized from the harbor lifted one hand, palm out, the gesture of a man acknowledging his own name at a muster. *Okonkwo, Joseph, clerk, Meridian Trading Company, Port Office* — two young clerks looked at each other, and one of them nodded, and Elias understood that until this moment neither had been certain the other had signed. Signatures gathered in candlelight have a way of becoming real only when spoken.

His duty was the dates and figures, so when Marguerite reached the section on tonnage—the confiscated cargo, the burned registries, the dispatch of the third gunboat—he took the page from her and read it himself, flat and exact, the auditor's cadence, because numbers spoken plainly carry their own accusation.

"Item: order dated 14 March 1902, countersigned, directing removal of all parish and shipping registers from Kessel Bay prior to the action of 19 March. Item: manifest of the *Cormorant's Wake*, listing eleven crates designated *obsolete paper, for deepwater disposal*. Item: sworn statement of divers present at coordinates appended, confirming disposal by sinking in the shallows east of the point. Item: recovered dispatch naming the authorizing officer—"

He stopped. Not because he wished to spare them. Because the name belonged to a man who was, at that hour, dining somewhere in a capital with amnesty folded in his breast pocket like a napkin, and the loft needed to hear the fact of him without illusion.

"—naming the authorizing officer," Elias finished, and read the name, and the name sat in the room like a stone dropped on a table. No cheering. No cursing, except one voice low in the back that said something in the old dialect and spat, gently, toward the sea.

That was the strange discipline of the night, Elias thought. Nobody performed outrage. They had done their outrage in cellars and sleepless nights for years. Tonight was for something rarer: accuracy. Every statement was followed by silence long enough for it to be *kept*, the way you hold a note to tune it.

Halfway through the fifth page, Aurelia woke.

He heard her before anyone else—the particular fretful creak of her, not yet crying—and he saw Marguerite stiffen mid-sentence. He crossed to the partition, lifted the basket, and carried it to the table, and set it down where the candlelight fell across the child's face, and the room went very quiet.

There it was. The face worth forgetting. The face the note had ordered erased, looking out at forty people from a laundry basket, blinking at the candles, entirely unimpressed by history.

"The annex records her also," Elias said. "Foundling, recovered at Kessel Bay lineage, entered as *Aurelia*, of the household of Marguerite Thornbury. Her testimony consists of her existence. It is the only testimony in this document that cannot be cross-examined."

Someone laughed—a wet, broken laugh—and then others, and the laughter and the weeping braided together the way they do at funerals that are also homecomings. Alba Reyes reached out and touched the basket's rim, just the rim, lightly, the way you touch a reliquary.

Marguerite picked up the next page. Her voice had changed, Elias noticed. Steadier and softer at once. As if reading the names had tuned her.

* * *

*Tomas*

Nine years he had carried the bay inside him, whole and burning, and he had learned that a memory with no witness grows teeth. It eats the carrier from inside. His mother had taught him the old funerary custom—you speak the dead person's name once at the grave, once at the year's end, and once whenever you eat something they loved, and by the third speaking they are not lost, only elsewhere. The company had understood this too, in its cold bureaucratic way. That was why they burned names first and houses second. A village whose names are unwritten can be declared uninhabited. He had watched clerks do it with straight backs and clean cuffs.

Now a woman with diver's hands and a scar on her forearm stood in a fish-drying loft and said *Kessel Bay* out loud, and said *Pilar, aged four*, and his daughter existed again—not as the weight he carried, but as a girl who had lived, who had a birthday in the wet season, who was afraid of eels and loved them anyway.

He did not remember leaving his seat. He was standing at the table, and Marguerite paused, and the whole loft waited to see what a man who had been taken by soldiers and come back would do.

"Say the children's names again," he said. "Slowly. My mother said a name should be spoken three times to make it travel safe."

So Marguerite read the children's names again, slowly, and the loft repeated them after her, call and response, a liturgy improvised out of grief and the simple mechanics of the human throat. *Benedicto.* *Benedicto.* *Pilar.* *Pilar.* The candles guttered and steadied. Outside, the tide turned without ceremony, the way tides do, carrying on with the oldest record-keeping there is.

When it was done, Tomas sat back down beside his wife, and she took his hand, and he found that for the first time in nine years the burning bay weighed nothing at all. Not gone—it would never be gone—but carried *together*, distributed across forty chests, which is, he thought, the only storage method fire has never defeated.

* * *

*Adela*

She had come without announcement, in a plain coat, escorted by no one, which for a woman of the Historical Commission was a kind of confession. She stood at the back near the door—if the fixers came, she intended to be between them and the child, though she did not say so, even to herself, in quite those words.

She had written history for nineteen years and had never once heard it read to the people it was *about*. That was the revelation, and it arrived with physical force, like cold water. In the commission's halls, documents were sources. Here, they were the dead, resurrected in candle smoke, and the living answering them by name. Her professors would have called this methodologically improper. Her professors, she reflected, had never buried a village.

When Marguerite reached the section Adela herself had drafted—the procedural language of entry, *over objection, annexed to the original text and forming part thereof*—the historian heard her own sentences in this room and found them transformed. Words she had written as a legal maneuver read, here, as a promise. She thought of her colleague Pellweather, who had signed the objection with a flourish and gone to the reception afterward, and she felt the old institutional loyalty loosen another notch, painlessly, like a knot working free in warm water.

Near the end, the reading faltered—not from error but from exhaustion; Marguerite's voice had gone to sandpaper—and Adela heard herself say, from the back, "I'll take the last page."

The room turned. Forty faces, and among them faces that had every reason to hate what she represented. Marguerite looked at her for a long moment and then simply handed the page back through the crowd, hand to hand, until it reached her.

"*Recorded at the port of Meridian,*" Adela read, "*this annex to stand with the treaty in perpetuity, that the armistice may end a war but shall not be permitted to end the account. Signed by the undersigned, who ask only to be believed.*"

She lowered the page. Her eyes were wet and she let them be.

"They will be believed," she said. "Not soon. Not loudly. But I have spent my life watching how archives behave, and I will tell you what I know: paper outlives amnesty. Paper outlives the men who signed it. Someone always opens the box."

The candles had burned down to stubs by then. People rose slowly, reluctantly, as if leaving a church before the last blessing. Bread was passed around. Alba Reyes embraced Marguerite and then, unexpectedly, embraced Adela too, and Adela, who had not been embraced in connection with her work in nineteen years, stood rigid and then did not.

* * *

*Marguerite*

Last of all, when the loft had emptied to a handful of close friends and the racks overhead dripped their first light condensation of dawn, Marguerite took up the pen she had used for the marginalia and opened the annex to its final leaf. There was space there—an archivist's accident, a blank quarter-page Elias had refused to trim.

She wrote quickly, while the courage was warm:

*Aurelia, of Kessel Bay, daughter of the Cruz family line, raised in the household of M. Thornbury. Her face was ordered forgotten. Let this page remember it instead.*

Below it, she pressed Aurelia's tiny inked heel into the margin, and the print dried brown as old blood, brown as new earth.

Elias watched over her shoulder, and then he went still, his attention snagged on something past the doorway—down the ladder, along the dark quay. Voices. Boots. Not many. Two men, maybe three, walking with the unhurried confidence of people who are paid to be somewhere.

Lamplight swung at the foot of the stairs.

"They followed someone here," Elias said quietly.
