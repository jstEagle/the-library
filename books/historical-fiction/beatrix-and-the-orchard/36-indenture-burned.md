## 36. Indenture Burned

The news came to the drying loft the way most news came to the port—salt-stained, half-shouted, and carried on someone else's errand.

Marguerite was folding pearl sacks when Little Con ran up the ladder two rungs at a time, breathless, his shirt unbuttoned crooked as though he'd dressed while running, which he likely had.

"The registry," he gasped. "The registry's open. They're burning papers in the yard—company men—anyone can walk in and claim a debt or deny one, there's no clerk at the desk, there's *no one*, Marguerite. The amnesty's come through and nobody knows what counts anymore."

She set down the sack she was holding. Her hands, she noticed distantly, did not shake. They had learned too much steadiness this season for shaking; they had held waterlogged dispatches and a living child and the last sworn statement of a man in chains.

"Burning," she said.

"Or throwing them in the harbor. I couldn't tell from the fence line. There's smoke either way."

* * *

The registry hall had always been the coldest building in the port—not by design but by neglect, its stone walls sweating through even the hottest months, its windows fogged with the breath of men who spent their lives reducing other lives to columns of ink. Marguerite had walked past it for eleven years without entering except under summons: renewal days, deduction days, the gray anniversaries when her father's debt became her father's debt became hers, transferred across ledgers like a disease passed in blood.

Today the doors stood open to anyone.

She went in with Aurelia on her hip because there was no one left to leave the child with whom she trusted, and because—she understood this only later, standing amid the wreckage of arithmetic—it felt right that her daughter should see it. Should see what a ledger looked like when it stopped being a god and became only paper.

Chaos did not describe it precisely. Chaos implied energy. What filled the hall was closer to a fever breaking, the whole institution shivering and sweating out eleven years of debts at once. Clerks sat behind their cages with their collars undone, drinking tea they'd clearly forgotten they'd poured, watching porters haul armloads of contract files into the courtyard where a company man—no, not company anymore, she could tell by the absence of insignia, just a hired man doing hired work—fed them into a brazier meant for rope-caulking.

A woman Marguerite recognized vaguely from the divers' quarter was weeping at the counter, not in grief but in the ugly, disbelieving way of someone handed back something they'd stopped expecting. In front of her lay an indenture contract, stamped VOID in violet ink so fresh it smeared when she touched it.

"Three years left," the woman kept saying. "I had three years left. My husband signed for me when I was sixteen and I've been paying his death-debt ever since and now you're telling me—" She laughed, wet and wild. "Now you're telling me it doesn't count? That none of it counted?"

Nobody answered her. There was no answer. The amnesty, drafted in a room on the other side of the world by men who had never smelled the registry hall's cold stone, voided all labor contracts executed under wartime requisition powers—which was nearly all of them—and it did not apologize, because amnesties are not built to apologize. It simply declared, with the same serene violence that had enslaved them, that the enslavement had been illegitimate all along.

Marguerite found the T–through–Z cabinet herself. No one stopped her. The cage doors hung open like shot birds.

Thornbury. Thornbury, M., diver-class, indentured to Meridian Trading Company, transfer of debt from R. Thornbury (deceased, drowned, 1899), balance outstanding—

She pulled the file. It was thinner than she expected. Eleven years of her life, reduced to nine pages, and four of those were duplicate receipts.

She stood there in the cold hall with her daughter's fist curled in her collar and read every line. The deductions for lamp oil. The deductions for the dive suit's maintenance, charged to her at rates she'd never agreed to. The deduction, dated the month after her father died, marked *compassion advance*—fourteen dollars advanced against her future labor, presumably to buy her mourning clothes, though she had no memory of receiving fourteen dollars or of wearing anything but canvas since.

Aurelia reached for the page and Marguerite let her. Small fingers closed over the word *outstanding*.

"Sign the release at any open counter," said a bored voice. A young clerk, nineteen perhaps, gesturing vaguely with his pen. "You're free. Everyone's free. Next."

*Free.* The word went through her like a harpoon through water—all momentum, no direction.

* * *

She did not sign immediately. This surprised her. She had imagined this moment, in the way one imagines things one believes will never happen: signing her name, walking out, the doors closing behind her on everything owed. She had imagined it would feel like surfacing—the burst of air, the light, the whole sky suddenly belonging to her lungs.

Instead she stood at the counter with the pen uncapped and could not remember what she wanted.

That was the truth of it, the truth she would carry for weeks before she could say it aloud. The indenture had been a wall, and walls, however hateful, give shape. For eleven years every choice had been pre-made for her: where she lived, when she dived, what her body was worth per hour in the cold green dark. Now the wall was gone and the horizon went on forever in every direction and she discovered, standing in the registry hall with violet ink drying on her thumb, that a horizon with no edges is indistinguishable from falling.

"What name do I write?" she asked, absurdly.

The clerk looked up at last, seeing her properly for the first time. "Your own."

"I know my own name." A laugh escaped her, small and strange. "I mean—I haven't signed anything that wasn't witnessed by a company officer in eleven years. I don't know if my signature still belongs to anyone."

"It's yours," the clerk said, and something in his voice softened—he was nineteen and bored and hungover from the port's celebration the night before, but he was also, briefly, the first person in eleven years to say those words to her and mean them legally. "It's yours now. Whatever you write is yours."

She wrote *Marguerite Thornbury*. Her hand did not shake until the last letter, and then it shook enough that the *y* sprouted a small tail, like a bird lifting off the page.

The clerk stamped it. VOID, in violet, over nine pages and eleven years.

Outside, the smoke from the brazier rose straight into a windless sky, and the smell was only paper burning, which smells like nothing, like a bonfire of nothing, like all that weight turning out to have been made of air.

* * *

For three days afterward she woke at five as always, because the tide and the company owned five o'clock, and lay in the dark of the drying loft listening for the whistle that no longer governed her, and did not know what her body was for if not the whistle.

Aurelia slept against her side, breathing in the small bellows-rhythm of childhood, and Marguerite stared at the rafters where the pearl sacks hung empty and thought: *I could leave.* Thought: *the boat to the capital leaves Thursdays.* Thought: *I could go anywhere,* and felt the thought dissolve under her like silt, because anywhere was a country she had no map for. She knew the reef off Kessel Bay depth by depth. She knew which streets flooded in typhoon season and which clerks took bribes and how long a body stays warm in harbor water. She had spent eleven years becoming an expert in ten square miles, and freedom had just informed her, politely, that the world was larger.

On the third morning Tomas found her sitting on the loft steps with Aurelia, watching the light come up over a harbor full of ships that no longer needed her permission to sail.

"You look like a woman whose house has burned down," he said, settling beside her with the care of a man whose ribs still remembered the prison cell.

"It has. Sort of."

"Ah." He nodded as though this were the wisest thing he'd heard all week. "The burning-down that saves your life. I know it." He was quiet a moment. Below them the town was waking wrong—no factory whistle, no chain-gang of indentured stevedores double-timing to the docks, just people moving at whatever pace their bodies chose, which made the whole port look drunken, unhurried, newborn. "My mother used to say freed slaves make the worst sailors."

Marguerite turned to look at him.

"Because," Tomas said, "a slave learns to read the water for the master's benefit—where the pearls are, where the storms come from, all of it in service. And the day the master's gone, the poor man looks at the same water and sees nothing. Not because the knowledge left him. Because he doesn't yet believe the knowledge is *his*."

"And your mother?"

"A freedwoman. Terrible sailor. Magnificent cook. She gave it twenty years before she believed the kitchen belonged to her too." He shrugged, wincing faintly. "Give yourself the twenty years, Marguerite. Or don't. But stop standing at the counter waiting for someone to tell you what your hands are for."

"They're for diving."

"Then dive. Or don't. That's the whole miracle of it." He pulled a battered pipe from his pocket, considered it, put it away again—prison had taught him to distrust small pleasures, they could be revoked. "Elias is looking for you, by the way. He's been at the annex tables since dawn. He says there's a problem with chapter seven and he says it in the tone of a man who thinks problems with chapters are fires to be put out before lunch."

Despite everything—a heaviness three days deep, a vertigo that hadn't lifted—a smile found her face. "He would."

"He would," Tomas agreed, rising, groaning, descending the steps into the strange new slow morning.

* * *

She found Elias surrounded by paper in the loft's back corner, where the light came best through the eastern window. He had the annex spread out in stacks with river stones on top to hold them, and he was frowning at chapter seven—the testimony of Kessel Bay—as though the frown could improve it.

"There you are." He didn't look up. "We have a problem. Reyes's statement references the registry file numbers of six families, but if the registries were burned this week, the numbers may point at nothing. A historian could dismiss the whole testimony as unverifiable."

"Let them burn," Marguerite said.

Elias looked up then. Eleven years of loving ledgers lived in his face, and she watched something move through it—alarm, protest, and then, slowly, the beginning of understanding.

"The files," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Let them burn. Every number in that annex points at a person, not a page. My mother's name isn't in a cabinet anymore, Elias. Neither is mine. Neither is—" she touched Aurelia's head, the fine dark hair, the face the company had once ordered forgotten— "hers. We spent all spring terrified that history would be written without us. Well. Now the ledgers are ash and the only place any of it exists is here." She put her hand flat on the stacks, on the sworn statements, on the names of divers and clerks and survivors written out in candlelight by people who had risked their necks to be counted. "Here, and in us. Maybe that was the point all along."

Elias was silent for a long moment. Then he did something she had never seen him do to a document: he set down his pen, squared the pages, and left chapter seven exactly as it was.

"The numbers stay," he said quietly. "But you're right—they're not evidence anymore. They're memorials." He looked up at her, and the archivist in him and the man in him arrived, for once, at the same conclusion simultaneously. "You've signed the release, then."

"This morning. Three days ago." She laughed at his expression. "I'm free, Elias. Technically. Utterly. And I haven't the faintest idea what to do about it."

Something crossed his face—hope, carefully leashed, the way he leashed everything—and he rose from the table, and for a moment she thought he might take her hand, might say the thing that had been circling them both since the night he brought her the false registry like a man carrying his own heart in evidence bags.

But the door banged open instead, and Little Con came up the ladder again at his impossible speed, white-faced this time, gripping a folded newspaper printed on the press that had come ashore with the historians' delegation.

"You'd both better see this," he said. "Adela Crane's published. The *annex*. All of it. Under her own name, with sources. And there's a list, Marguerite—a list of witnesses, sworn and named, everyone who testified." His eyes went to hers, frightened and bright. "Your name is first."
