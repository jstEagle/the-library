## 37. A History for Aurelia

The annex came to her in an envelope with no return address, delivered on a Tuesday when the harbor light lay flat as hammered tin. Marguerite knew the hand on the outside before she broke the seal—Elias wrote his capitals like a man who had been trained never to let his letters lean, and then had taught himself, deliberately, to let them lean anyway.

Inside were forty pages of foolscap, printed from the official plates by some clerk whose conscience or carelessness Elias had purchased, and folded among them a single sheet in Adela Crane's small, upright script: *One hundred copies struck for circulation under the annex provision. This one is yours. I am told the author of a dissenting record is entitled to annotate it.*

Marguerite sat down on the step where the bundle had appeared six years before and read every word she already knew by heart.

It was strange to see it set in type. For two years the truth of Kessel Bay had lived in ink that smudged, in pencil that faded, in the memorized deposition of a man who had carried it behind prison walls the way other men carry prayer. Now it was roman and italic, justified at both margins, paginated in a government office by someone who had never smelled the smoke. *Annex C to the Protocol of Armistice: Testimony Concerning Events at Kessel Bay, Being a Dissenting Record.* The words looked smaller than they had sounded in the drying loft, read aloud by lamplight while the storm leaned against the shutters. But they were real. They existed in the way ledgers existed—inviolable, indexed, filed against forgetting.

Aurelia was at the water's edge below the house, hunting the tide line for sea glass with the ferocious concentration she brought to everything. Six years old now, quick-boned and dark-eyed, with a face that old divers still stopped mid-sentence to look at—because faces remember even when mouths are shut, and there were men on this coast who had known her mother's mother's family and could not see that brow, that chin, without their throats tightening.

Marguerite carried the annex inside and set it on the table beneath the window, where the afternoon came through clean.

She had promised herself this act for a long time. Since the night in the loft, really, since the candles and the sworn statements and the sound of Tomas Reyes saying the names of his dead into the room so they would exist somewhere besides inside him. She had promised herself that when the paper finally came home, she would not keep it pristine. A pristine document was a dead one. It sat in archives and gathered the dust of officialdom, and only people with credentials touched it. What she intended required a pen and a margin and the particular courage of a woman writing where she had not been invited to write.

She ground her ink herself. She had learned that from her father, who had kept his dive logs in a hand mixed of charcoal and squid ink because good ink cost what pearls cost, and he had said once, laughing, salt-crusted, half-drowned: *Anything you want to last, girl, make it yourself. Then it has your hands in it and can't pretend otherwise.*

She began at the beginning, which was page one, line one, where the typesetters had rendered the dry formula: *The undersigned witnesses, being of sound mind and free testimony, do submit the following account contrary to the findings of the Commission.*

In the margin beside it, in her own careful hand, she wrote:

*My name is Marguerite Thornbury, daughter of Emile Thornbury, pearl diver, drowned off the Meridian grounds the eleventh of October, 1904, when his boat was run down by a company tender that never stopped and was never named in any ledger. I was indentured to the Company at fifteen years old for a debt of nine pounds, four shillings, which arithmetic my father would have found funny if he had lived to hear it. Everything in this annex passed through my hands first as rumor, then as fear, then as paper. I write these notes so that whoever reads this record knows it did not arrive in this world as print. It arrived as people, and most of those people paid for every line.*

The house was quiet except for the floorboards ticking in the heat and, far below, the thin bright voice of her daughter negotiating with a wave.

She turned the page. The registry of Kessel Bay lay before her in facsimile—the true registry, the one Elias had copied by lamplight while his loyalty cracked like ice in spring, the one he had smuggled through the audit trails he forged with such terrible, beautiful competence that she sometimes thought he had been born for nothing else. Beside the entry for the village headman, the official record noted: *residence vacated, date uncertain.*

*Not vacated,* she wrote in the margin. *Murdered. His name was Serafín Alcalde-Bay. He kept bees. He gave my daughter—the child I will speak of further on—a spoonful of honey the one time she passed through his hands, though she was too young to hold a spoon and he was already a hunted man. He died in the first burning, aged fifty-one, and his wife Juana died beside him, and their sons' names are on page nineteen of this annex because Tomas Reyes would not let us leave them off, though the Commission wanted brevity and called them "dependents." They were not dependents. They were a family. There is no abbreviation for that.*

Her pen moved steadily. Outside, the light began its long gold slide toward evening, and the smell of the low-tide flats rose up through the window gap—that smell of brine and weed and something older, the smell of her whole life, which had once smelled to her like a cage and now smelled like something closer to ground, to root, to home.

She came to page eleven, where her own testimony stood in the third person, transcribed by Adela Crane with a precision that sometimes frightened her: *The witness Thornbury deposed that an infant was left at her residence on the night of March second, bearing a note bearing the partial seal of the Meridian Trading Company and the instruction "forget this face."*

There it was, the whole catastrophe of her life, in eleven lines of someone else's prose.

She dipped the pen again. Her hand did not shake. She noticed that it did not shake, and understood that this, too, was something her father had given her—not steadiness exactly, but the diver's knowledge that panic spends air, and air is the only wealth that matters down there.

*I have written around this child for forty pages,* she wrote, *because the annex demanded testimony and not tenderness. But margins are where the truth goes that does not fit in evidence, so let it live here. Her name is Aurelia. She was four months old when she was left on my step, wrapped in a wool shawl that smelled of woodsmoke, screaming with the whole force of her small lungs as though she knew already what the world was prepared to do with her. The note said to forget her face. I have looked at that face every day for six years and I can report that it improves upon acquaintance.*

She smiled at that, alone at the table, and let herself smile fully, because no one was watching and because Aurelia had inherited her laugh from somewhere and Marguerite liked to think it might have traveled all the way down from a beekeeper's wife in a burned village, some joke told generations back, surviving fire and water and the Commission of Inquiry itself.

*Aurelia is the last living blood of the Alcalde-Bay line of Kessel Bay, being the granddaughter of Serafín and Juana through their elder son Miguel and his wife Rosa, who fled the first burning with their daughter and were lost—how lost, we never learned, and that not-knowing is a wound in this record I regret. The child survived. She was carried out of the hills by persons unknown, who left her with me because I was poor and unimportant and therefore, in the arithmetic of the Company, invisible. Their arithmetic was wrong. It is always wrong. The invisible see everything, and some of us write it down.*

*I did not forget her face. Neither did old Marisol the diver, who recognized the set of her jaw across a market crowd and went white as bleached coral. Neither did Tomas Reyes, who wept over her cradle and then swore his oath with her asleep against his shoulder, which is why I believe his oath more than I believe most scripture. And neither, in the end, did the victors themselves forget her—though they tried, oh, how they tried, sending their fixers through the port like men searching for a coin dropped in dark water. You cannot drown a face. It comes up with the tide.*

Below, the light was going amber. Marguerite heard boots on the path and knew the gait—Elias, carrying fish wrapped in newspaper because after everything he still could not enter a house without bringing evidence of industry. She watched from the window as he reached the tide line and crouched down to Aurelia's level, and she saw her daughter hold up her day's harvest of sea glass for his inspection with the gravity of a magistrate presenting exhibits, and saw Elias examine each fragment as though the verdict mattered, because to Aurelia it did, and because Elias Vane had learned, somewhere between the vault and the signing table, that some records are kept in gestures.

They came up the hill together. The door opened on cold sea air and the smell of mackerel.

"You've got it," Elias said, reading the table in one glance—the annex open, the ink pot, the margins already black with her hand.

"Adela sent it. One of a hundred."

"A hundred." He set the fish down slowly. "I'd hoped for ten."

"There will be a hundred readers, then, and one of them will be her." Marguerite touched the page. "When she's old enough. When she asks—and she's begun asking, Elias, the questions are coming faster now, small ones, testing ones—I wanted there to be more than the official version for her to find. I wanted her to find me in the margins, waiting."

Elias came to stand behind her chair. He read in silence the way he read everything, thoroughly, his eyes moving line to line with the archivist's discipline, and Marguerite felt the particular vulnerability of being read by a man whose profession was detecting error. But he said nothing about error. When he finished the first pages he put his hand on her shoulder, and his thumb pressed once, deliberately, the way he underlined things he meant never to lose.

"The Commission's version says 'vacated,'" he said quietly. "Yours says murdered. Both are in the same bound volume now, in the archive, side by side forever. Do you understand what that means? Every clerk who ever consults Annex C will trip over your handwriting. Every historian. Every lawyer. Every schoolchild doing an exercise. The lie will never again appear in this world without the truth attached to it, in your hand, refusing to be separated."

"That was the idea."

"It's a better idea than mine ever was." He said it simply, without the old shame, because the shame had been spent long ago and what remained was something cleaner. "I spent years believing the archive was neutral. Boxes don't lie, I told myself; paper doesn't take sides. But paper takes whatever side the person filing it takes. You taught me that. Or Tomas did. Or—" he glanced toward the door, where Aurelia had come in and was washing sea glass in the basin, splashing with tremendous purpose, "—or she did, at four months old, before she could say a word."

Marguerite turned back to the annex. Page twenty-six held the statement of the diver Benicio Farrow regarding the crates sunk in the shallows, and page twenty-nine her own account of the dive—the dispatch she pulled from the drowned box, the shadow of the boat above her, the long gray minute of ascending knowing she had been seen. In the margin she wrote:

*The water off Kessel Bay holds what the land was made to give up. My father taught me that the sea keeps honest accounts: it takes without malice and returns without favor, and everything a man drops in it becomes the sea's eventually, and the sea does not destroy its holdings, it merely waits. The Company thought drowning documents would end them. The Company had never asked a diver. We read the bottom the way clerks read ledgers, and we brought up their arithmetic piece by piece until it convicted them—almost. Not entirely. See page thirty-three for what amnesty spared them. See my margins throughout for what mercy I could not bring myself to feel.*

* * *

That night, after supper, after the fish bones were buried in the garden where things grew surprisingly well in the rich dark soil of old habitation, Marguerite sat with the lamp and the annex while Aurelia slept in the next room. Elias was at the table across from her, mending a net he refused to admit he did not know how to mend, working at it anyway with the stubbornness of a man retraining his hands for a new country.

She reached the final page. The annex closed with the procedural note—entered into the record over objection, by a vote of the commission, seven to five, at the signing table, too late to stop anything and early enough to stop everything that mattered afterward. Below it, Adela Crane had signed as custodian. Below Adela's signature, space remained, blank and official-looking, where the printer's frame had run short.

Marguerite looked at that blankness for a long time.

Then she wrote, in her largest and clearest hand, as though inscribing a title deed:

*For Aurelia Alcalde-Bay Thornbury, born of Kessel Bay, found at my door on the second of March, 1911. Your face was ordered forgotten. It was not. It is remembered here by name, by line, and by love, and this page is proof, filed where proofs are kept. When you read this, you will learn that history is not something that happens to other people. It happened to you. You survived it. You are its continuation. Read the margins. Your mother lives in them.*

She signed it. She dated it. Then she blew on the ink, and the lamp flame bent and righted itself, and outside the wind came down off the hills smelling of rain and turned earth, and somewhere below in the sleeping town a dog barked once at the harbor and fell silent.

Elias looked up from his ruined net. "What will you do with it now?"

"There's a copy going to the archive with the rest of the set—Adela arranged the binding. Mine stays here." Marguerite closed the annex and rested both hands on its cover, feeling the faint ridges of type through the paper like the texture of a shell. "But there's one thing left undone. One thing the paper can't carry."

"The land," Elias said.

"The land." She looked toward the window, toward the dark bulk of the hills above the port, where a certain hillside waited, terraced and abandoned, its soil remembering orchards that had been cut down the same season the village burned. "Tomas says the Alcalde-Bays kept trees there for four generations. Pomegranate, fig, three kinds of pear. He knows where the rootstocks ran. He says the roots may have survived, suckering wild all these years, waiting for someone with the sense to graft them back."

"And Aurelia?"

"Asleep." Marguerite listened to the small sound of the child's breathing through the wall, steady as a tide table. "Which is why we'll tell her in the morning, and take her with us, and let her put her hands in the dirt of her grandmother's hill. Paper is for the world, Elias. But a tree—" she touched the annex once more, lightly, farewell, "—a tree you can't burn twice."

Elias set down the net. Through the window, far up the black shoulder of the hill, something pale caught the moonlight—a blossom gone feral on an old rootstock, blooming untended all these years for no one, waiting to be counted.
