## 1. The Summons

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which Felix would remember afterward because Tuesdays were the days Herr Brandt counted the till and swore at the tax schedule, and the swearing had just reached its customary crescendo when the boy at the door—actually a boy, for once, a genuine errand-boy with ink-stained cuffs and a wheezing cough—thrust the envelope into her hands and vanished down the lane as though pursued by something worse than winter.

Felix stood in the doorway of Brandt & Son, Clockmakers, and turned the envelope over twice before she opened it. Nobody wrote to apprentices. Nobody wrote to *Felix* at all; Felix Halloran existed on paper only as a signature on indenture papers five years old, a name borrowed from a dead boy who had never lived long enough to owe anyone money. Letters came to Herr Brandt. Letters came addressed in careful script to *the master of the house*, and it was Brandt's thick fingers that broke the seals.

This one was different. This one bore the seal of the Imperial Observatory, pressed deep into wine-dark wax, and it was addressed, in a hand so fine it looked printed, to *Herr F. Halloran, journeyman apprentice*.

She read it three times in the doorway, letting the cold in behind her, until Brandt's voice cracked across the workshop like a snapped mainspring.

"Shut the door, boy, or you can heat the place with your own wages."

She shut the door. Her hands were not entirely steady, and she folded the letter small and pushed it into her waistcoat pocket, where it lay against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

*The Observatory of the Imperial University has been directed to engage a competent hand for the restoration of its regulator clock, which has lately failed...*

Directed. Not requested—directed. She knew what that phrasing meant even if she was only seventeen and officially possessed of six years' trade experience rather than five; somewhere above the observatory, some minister or magistrate or man whose title required a carriage had decided that the great clock would be fixed, by whom, at what price, and by when. Such decisions rolled downhill in Vienna like barrels. It happened that this one had landed at her feet.

"Halloran." Brandt didn't look up from his ledger. His pen scratched. "If that's what I think it is, bring it here."

"It's nothing, Meister." She kept her voice low and flat, pitched always in the narrow band between insolence and servility that a working boy was permitted. "An advertisement, maybe. Someone wants a cuckoo clock."

Brandt raised his head. He had gray eyes set in a face like a walnut carved by someone in a hurry, and those eyes had missed nothing in the Währing workshop for thirty years, including—she had long suspected—the things they were kindest for not seeing. He held out one hand. She gave him the letter.

He read it slowly. His lips moved over certain phrases: *regulator clock*, *master mechanism*, *compensation offered commensurate with skill*. When he reached the bottom, where the sum was named, his eyebrows climbed toward his bald crown and stayed there.

"Well," he said.

"Well," Felix agreed.

"This is the observatory. The big clock. The one they say—" he waved the letter vaguely upward, in the direction of the hill above the city where the tower stood against the sky like a needle sewn heaven to earth, "—keeps better time than the Emperor's own watchmaker. They want my shop."

"They want *you*, Meister."

"They want whoever I send." Brandt set the letter down on the ledger and flattened it with both palms, as though it might otherwise escape. "That means you."

The fire ticked in the grate. From the back room came the sound of Marta the seamstress—Ivo's sister, lodger, mender of everything in the house except its silences—folding linen, each snap of the cloth sharp as a card being dealt. Felix became very aware of her own breathing and made herself slow it.

"Say what you're thinking," Brandt said. "You've got the face for it. You always have the face for it."

"I'm thinking," Felix said carefully, "that a commission like that puts a name in front of a lot of people. Names get remembered. Remembered names get asked about."

It was as close as they ever came, the two of them, to speaking of the thing beneath the floorboards of their lives: that Felix Halloran did not exist, that Beatrix Halloran had been buried under debts and shame and a brother's grave marker in Pressburg, that every day of the last five years was a small forgery committed in plain sight. Brandt had hired her without questions—or rather, she sometimes suspected, *with* questions, all of them answered correctly inside his own head and then locked away forever. He had never once said the word. Neither had she. Between them lay the silence the way a riverbed lies between two banks: wide, necessary, and supporting everything.

"Names get asked about," Brandt repeated now, slowly, tasting the fear in it. "Yes. And money gets paid out. Look at the figure again."

She looked. It was more than she earned in a year. It was more than Brandt paid her in three, though he didn't know she knew the arithmetic of her own worth so precisely—and if he did know, he had never insulted her by pretending otherwise aloud.

"Half up front," Brandt murmured, already calculating, his pen tapping the desk. "Materials billed separately. And the prestige—Halloran, do you understand what it does to a workshop when the observatory says your work is good enough for the stars? Every nobleman with a mantelpiece comes knocking. We could take two more apprentices. Real ones."

"I'm real," Felix said, and heard the edge slip into her voice before she could blunt it.

"You know what I meant." He didn't apologize. Brandt never apologized; apology, he maintained, was a luxury item and he dealt only in necessities. But he looked at her a moment longer than he needed to, and in that look was the whole strange architecture of their arrangement—his knowledge, unspoken; her secret, unguarded; the pact neither had ever put into words because words could be overheard and silence could not.

"The work itself," he said, turning back to business, which was his way of walking around a subject rather than through it. "A regulator of that class doesn't fail for no reason. Those clocks are pampered. Oiled by men in slippers. If it's stopped, someone let it stop, or something broke that shouldn't break." He shrugged. "Worn pallets. A cracked arbor. Cold, maybe—these nights are cold enough to thicken oil to glue. Probably nothing."

"Probably nothing," Felix echoed.

"But you'll go up there with your eyes open. You'll look at everything before you touch anything, the way I taught you. And if it's beyond you—if the movement needs me—you send word and I come myself, and we eat the cost together. No heroics. Clockmakers don't get to be heroes; we get to be right or wrong, that's all."

"No heroics," Felix agreed.

He slid the letter across the desk to her. "Keep it. Your name's on it." Something crossed his face then, quick and quickly gone—something that might have been pride, or might have been dread, and from Brandt's face it was impossible to tell the difference, possibly even for Brandt.

"Start tomorrow," he said. "Dawn. You'll want the whole day and you won't have half of it. Take the good loupe, and if you lose the good loupe I'll take it out of your—" He stopped, frowned at his ledger. "Take the good loupe."

* * *

That night the workshop settled into its noises the way an old ship settles into harbor: the settling of joists, the mutter of coals, Ivo upstairs dragging his feet deliberately on the boards—he was deaf, stone deaf since a fever at nineteen, but he had convinced himself and half the household that he could hear through his feet, and no one had ever had the heart to correct him. Felix lay in the narrow loft above the bench room with the letter open on her chest, reading it yet again by the light of a candle stub she was technically stealing from the shop and technically repaying in work.

*Herr F. Halloran.* Five years, and the lie had grown a callus. There were mornings she woke and reached for Beatrix the way one reaches for a limb that isn't there anymore—a phantom itch behind the eyes, in the set of the shoulders, in the way she used to laugh with her whole throat instead of keeping her laughs small and low where they wouldn't carry. Then the day started and Felix took over, the way he always did: competent, quiet, invisible. Invisibility was a craft too. She had served her apprenticeship at it alongside the clockmaking, and in some ways she had proven the finer student.

Her father's face surfaced, as it did on bad nights: the way he had looked in the last week in Pressburg, when the creditors came with their inventories and their polite, devastating voices. *Your daughter should marry well,* the chief creditor had suggested, looking at Beatrix the way men look at goods at auction. Her father had said nothing. That was the thing she could not forgive, even now, even knowing him ruined and desperate and dead within the year—that he had said nothing, and so she had said everything by leaving. Taken her dead brother's coat, his name, his unwritten future, and walked west along the Danube road until her shoes split and her voice settled into its new register and Felix was real enough to fool even her.

And here was the trap of it, laid out in fine stationery with an imperial seal: to rise in the trade was to be seen. To be seen was to be known. Somewhere in Pressburg there might still be a creditor with a long memory and a short temper, a cousin, a neighbor, anyone who had known a girl named Beatrix Halloran with Felix's same crooked smile. For five years she had survived by mattering to no one outside these walls. Tomorrow she would stand in the observatory among professors and astronomers and men who wrote letters on paper this heavy, and she would be *Herr Halloran of Brandt & Son*, a name in a book, a name in mouths.

Cautious hope. Hopeful caution. She turned the phrases over like two sides of a coin and could not tell which was the face and which the reverse.

From below came three soft knocks—two, pause, one—Ivo's goodnight, tapped on the stair rail where the whole house could feel it through the wood. Felix knocked the answer against the loft floor with her knuckle: two, pause, one. *Goodnight. Sleep. Still here.*

She blew out the candle and lay in the dark listening to the shop's clocks. Twenty-three of them ticking out of phase, a slow tide of sound, each one keeping its own faithful time while hers ran differently, faster, counting down to dawn.

* * *

Morning came gray and hard-frozen. Brandt met her at the door before first light with a canvas tool roll and an expression he had presumably intended to be neutral.

"The escapement," he said, without greeting. "When you get there, look at the escapement first."

"Why the escapement?"

"Because it's the heart. Everything else in a clock can limp along wrong for years—the strike, the hands, the calendar work—but when the escapement goes, the whole thing stops lying about it." He pushed the roll into her arms. "And because if someone wanted to make a good clock fail quietly, that's where they'd start."

Felix frowned. "You said last night it was probably nothing."

"I said that to make you sleep." Brandt's mouth did something that on another man would have been a smile. "Go. Be back by dark or I'll assume the astronomers ate you. And Halloran—" he caught her sleeve as she turned, and his grip was brief and hard, "—do the work well and speak little. Craftsmen they'll forgive for anything. Characters they'll examine."

The streets were ruts of ice and breath-fog as she climbed out of the Währing lanes toward the hill, the tool roll heavy on her shoulder, the letter in her breast pocket, and the whole sleeping city below her smoking gently into the dawn. The observatory tower resolved out of the mist by degrees—first the dark mass of the building, then the dome, then the slender column of the clock tower rising from it, and at the top, pale in the early light, the great dial with its single hand frozen at eleven minutes past four.

Stopped. Dead stopped, at some hour nobody had bothered to record, which for a clock of that reputation was less a malfunction than an insult.

Felix stood at the foot of the hill a moment longer, stamping warmth into her feet, and told herself the flutter under her ribs was only the climb, only the cold, only the ordinary fear of any young craftsman facing the biggest job of his short life.

Then she saw, cutting up the drive ahead of her, a black carriage with no markings at all, and a man descending from it who moved like a person arriving somewhere he owned rather than visited—and the morning tilted, very slightly, the way a shelf tilts before the plates begin to slide.

She adjusted her cap, shifted the tool roll, and went up toward the doors, telling her feet to walk at an apprentice's pace.

They found the child before she found the clock.
