## 2. Which Silences Keep People Safe

The night Felix Halloran stopped being Felix, it was raining in the manner of Vienna in autumn—not falling so much as hanging, a grey suspension that soaked through wool and into bone. She remembered that afterward with strange precision: not her mother's face, not the bailiffs' hands on the furniture, but the rain, and the smell of the Danube canal, and the way her brother's coat smelled of camphor because it had spent five years folded in a chest with the winter things of a boy who had died before he could wear it out.

She had been seventeen. The shop on Wollzeile—her father's silk shop, three generations of Halloran silks, bolts of Chinese crimson and Florentine gold stacked like the spines of holy books—had been dying for two years by then, though her father had fed it lies the way one feeds a sick animal, spoonful by spoonful, until there was nothing left in the house to feed it but the house itself.

* * *

It began, as these things do, with a supper that tasted of nothing.

Her father sat at the head of the table with his hands flat on either side of his plate, the posture of a man holding himself down against a wind only he could feel. Her mother had made dumplings from the last of the flour and pretended they were a treat. Her younger sister Greta, eleven, chattered about a puppet show until their father said, very quietly, "Enough," and the word fell into the room like a coin dropped down a well.

"The creditors come Thursday," he said then. There was no way to say such a thing gently, so he did not try. "Perhaps sooner. They have been patient longer than they should have been."

Felix's mother's hand went to her throat, to the garnet necklace that had been her own mother's, and Felix watched her fingers close around it and understood, in the same instant, what her mother understood: that the necklace was already counted among the losses, already weighed and written in some ledger they would never be permitted to see.

"Thursday," her mother repeated.

"There is a cousin in Pest," her father went on. "And an aunt in Linz who owes me more than money. We will scatter. It is not—" He stopped. He looked at his dumplings. "It is not the life I wanted for you."

Greta, mercifully, did not understand. She asked if they would take the cat. Their father said yes, yes, of course the cat, and Felix excused herself and went up to the room she shared with Greta and stood at the window watching the rain hang in the gaslight of the street below, and she thought: *I will not scatter. I am not a seed. I am not going to be planted in some aunt's spare bed in Linz, to be grateful and useful and slowly erased.*

That was the night she thought of Felix.

Not herself—her brother. Anton Felix Halloran, born second and dead first, at nine years old, of the fever that had swept the city the winter she was six. She had almost no memory of him. A boy who let her hold the reins once when they visited the Prater. A boy whose portrait hung in the stairwell, painted at seven, with a solemn face and a collar too large, so that he looked less like a child than like a small disappointed clerk. A name on a stone in St. Marx, weathered soft already.

But a name was a thing that outlived its owner. A name was a coat in a chest, still holding its shape.

She went down to the chest while the house slept and took the coat, and the trousers, and the boots that were stiff with disuse, and she stood in her chemise in the cold holding her dead brother's clothes against her body, and she felt—she had never confessed this to anyone, never even fully to herself—the first calm she had felt in two years. Fear was still there; fear would always be there. But under the fear, for the first time since her father had begun to fail, there was a floor. A plan has a floor. Desperation does not.

* * *

Her mother found her at dawn. Not packing—packing would come later, would be done in fury and tears over the following days—but standing at the little mirror with her hair cut. She had done it herself with sewing scissors, badly, the uneven ends sticking up around her ears like the feathers of a startled bird. In the glass, behind her own reflection, she saw her mother in the doorway, and she watched her mother's face move through three expressions in as many seconds: horror, understanding, grief.

"Anton," her mother said. Not *what have you done*. Not *you cannot*. Just the name, spoken like a question, like a prayer, like a wound being probed.

"It fits," Felix said. Her voice sounded wrong in her own ears already, or perhaps right, which was worse. "Mother, listen. Listen to me. If we scatter, we are four beggars asking favors. But if I go to Vienna and work—if I work as a boy works, with a boy's wages and a boy's freedom—I can send money. Real money. And when Father settles with the creditors, there will be one fewer mouth, and no one will ask after a dead boy."

"No one hires a girl in a trade—"

"I know," Felix said. "That is rather the point."

Her mother came into the room then and took her by the shoulders, and Felix braced for shaking, for slapping, for the full orchestra of maternal catastrophe. Instead her mother looked at her for a long time—at the cropped hair, at the boy's coat hanging open over the chemise—and something in her face settled, the way water settles when the stirring stops.

"You have his chin," she said finally. "Your brother. You never saw it, but you have his chin exactly." And then, quieter, in the voice Felix would carry for five years like a coal banked in ash: "Do not write. Letters can be read. Do not tell Greta where you are. Children cannot keep a secret they don't know they're keeping. And Beatrix—" the use of her true name, there, in that room, was the last time anyone would say it aloud for five years— "*Beatrix.* If it goes wrong, you run. You don't explain, you don't defend yourself, you don't wait to be believed. Girls like us are not believed. Promise me."

"I promise."

"And when it is safe—when it is truly safe—you come back and be my daughter again."

Felix promised that too. It was the promise of the two that neither of them believed.

* * *

Leaving was easier than it should have been, because ruin makes leaving respectable. Half of Vienna's ruined families were scattering that season; another young man taking the morning coach with a carpetbag was not worth a second glance, especially a young man with his collar up against the rain and his fare counted out in small coins. She rode north with her cheek against the cold glass, watching the autumn country unspool, and she did not cry until the coach crossed the Danube canal and the city walls rose up ahead of her, grey and enormous, swallowing the road. Then she cried the way one cries when a tooth comes out—all at once, shockingly, with relief underneath the pain—and then she wiped her face with her brother's sleeve and was finished with it.

Vienna took her the way cities take the desperate: indifferently, and then, gradually, on its own terms.

The first month she slept in a doss-house in the Leopoldstadt and learned the grammar of being a boy. It was not, she discovered, mostly about the clothes. It was about the walk—shorter steps, weight forward, the impatience boys carried in their knees. It was about the eyes: boys looked at everything and apologized to nothing. It was about silence. A girl who kept quiet was strange; a boy who kept quiet was merely busy. She learned to spit, badly, because a bad spit got laughs where no spit at all got looks. She learned to answer to *boy*, to *you there*, to a dozen names shouted across courtyards, and to answer to Felix as though it had always been hers, because hesitation was the one garment she could not afford to put on.

Herr Brandt's workshop found her in the sixth week, or she found it—a narrow frontage on a lane off the Schulerstraße, the window crowded with clocks, every one of them ticking, so that standing before the glass was like pressing your ear to the ribs of some enormous sleeping animal. The notice in the window asked for an apprentice, steady hands, no wages for the first year. She had gone in expecting to be turned away—too small, too young, too something—and Herr Brandt had looked at her for a long moment across the bench, a long moment in which her heart stopped entirely, and had said:

"Can you hold still?"

"Yes, sir."

"Steadier than that?" He nodded at her hands, which she now realized were trembling. "A clock forgives nothing. Every lie you tell it, it keeps. Every truth, too. That's why I trust them more than people." He held out his hand. "Show me."

She showed him. Whatever she showed him—some steadiness deeper than the flesh, some stubbornness in the tendons—he took her on. Four guilders a week after the trial year, a cot in the loft above the workshop, and a set of rules recited without inflection, like scripture: *Up at five. Church on Sunday, visible. No debts. No drink. No questions about where you came from.*

That last rule she took to like water. No questions about where she came from. She could have kissed him.

* * *

What she did not know—could not know then, learned only later, learned in fragments and glances over years—was how nearly she had failed at the door.

Brandt told her none of it. But Marta, years later, over mending, told her pieces, and Ivo, in his silent way, confirmed them with sketches in the dust, and so Felix came eventually to know the scene the way one knows a story of one's own life told by strangers: how Brandt had watched the boy cross his threshold and seen, immediately, everything. The jaw. The way the boy moved a needle—no boy moved a needle that way; boys stabbed, girls sewed, and this one sewed. The name *Halloran* given in a voice pitched a half-tone wrong, defended too quickly when Brandt observed that Halloran was a rare name in this quarter.

He had stood at his workbench that evening with the boy's application paper in his hand and thought: *This is a runaway girl in a dead boy's coat, and if I turn her out she goes to the docks, or worse, or she starves cleverly and is caught anyway, because cleverness starves slower than hunger but it does starve.*

And he had thought of his brother.

Lukas Brandt, who had been a clerk—not a treasury clerk, thank God, but a customs clerk, which in the Empire was near enough—who had found a discrepancy in a manifest and been the sort of man who reported discrepancies, and who had drowned in the Danube in April 1838 with his ledgers missing and his boots on, and whose death had been ruled accidental by men who had not troubled to look closely, because looking closely at certain things was itself a kind of trouble. Brandt had looked closely. It had bought him nothing but nightmares and a permanent understanding of how the world actually worked, which was: the Empire was a clock, and some wheels were greased, and the greased wheels were never, ever the ones that broke.

So he had looked at the paper in his hand, at the name *Felix Halloran* in a girl's careful hand pretending to be a boy's, and he had put the paper in the drawer with the others, and he had said nothing.

He said nothing for five years. He corrected the boy's grip on files and taught him the escapement and the fusee and the difference between a repair and a resurrection. He docked wages for sloppiness and paid bonuses for excellence and never once, in five years, asked a question he did not already know the answer to. When the boy grew—when the disguise became less a costume than a fact, when the apprenticeship papers were drawn up and signed *Felix Halloran* and witnessed by the guild, making the fiction load-bearing, structural, part of the building—Brandt signed beneath it without a flicker.

Once, only once, he came close. It was her third year. She had cut her thumb on a mainspring and he had bandaged it, gruffly, professionally, and as he tied off the linen he said, without looking up:

"In this workshop, a person is what they do. Nothing else. You understand me?"

"Yes, sir."

"What they do," he repeated, tying the knot hard enough to sting. "Nothing else matters here. Nothing else exists here." And he had gone back to his bench, and she had stood there with her bandaged thumb and her pounding heart, understanding that she had just been offered something enormous in the smallest possible wrapping: not absolution, not confession, but sanctuary. A border drawn around the truth and declared foreign territory. *Beyond this line, you are whatever you say you are. Within it, you are my apprentice, and my apprentice is good with an escapement, and that is all anyone in this room knows.*

She had loved him for it before she understood it, and understood it long before she could have said so.

* * *

Five years. Five years of Sundays visible in church, of letters home that were never sent because letters could be read, of sending money instead through channels her mother had devised—a pawnbroker, a laundress, a chain of women who moved coins the way smugglers move brandy. Five years of Greta growing up in her mind as a fixed image, eleven forever, because imagination cannot update what information refuses to feed it. Five years of being Felix so completely that some mornings, waking in the loft with the clocks ticking below, she had to lie still for a moment and find herself, the way one finds north with a compass, and half the time the needle swung to Felix and stayed there, and Beatrix Halloran was a name in a drawer, a portrait on a stairwell, a girl on a coach crying into a dead boy's sleeve.

She did not regret it. That was the strange thing she could never say aloud, because regret was the expected food and she refused to eat it. Regret would mean the trade-off had been real—that she had lost a self and gained only safety. But she had not gained only safety. She had gained hands. She had gained the deep pleasure of mechanism, of problems that yielded to patience, of a world where cause and effect were married faithfully and betrayal was impossible: a wheel did not pretend to be another size; a spring did not flatter you and then fail. She had gained Brandt's gruff, oblique, unwavering loyalty. She had gained Marta's gossip and Ivo's silent jokes. She had gained, though she would not have used the word, a family—the kind that is built rather than born, joint by joint, like a clock.

But some nights the needle swung the other way. Some nights she lay awake and heard her mother saying *come back and be my daughter again*, and knew that the door marked *again* had quietly closed, the way doors do, without any sound at all, sometime in the third year, maybe the fourth, and that if she walked through her family's door tomorrow she would bring a stranger in with her wearing her body. Some nights she thought of Greta at fifteen, sixteen, seventeen—a woman now, surely, a stranger too—and grieved not for the sister she'd lost but for the sister she'd never finished having.

Those nights she went down to the workshop, though it was forbidden, though Brandt would have docked her a day's wage, and she lit one candle and opened the case of the regulator on the bench and listened to it breathe. Tick. Tick. The escapement letting time out one tooth at a time, the way grief must be let out, the way memory must be let out—one measured instant, never all at once, or the spring snaps and takes the mechanism with it.

*Which silences keep people safe?* she would think, watching the pallets catch and release, catch and release. Her mother's silence, keeping Greta ignorant and therefore innocent. Brandt's silence, keeping her employed and therefore alive. Her own silence, keeping her whole—or keeping her hidden, which was not the same thing, though she had spent five years pretending it was.

Catch and release. Catch and release. Even the clock could not hold its tongue forever. Every tick was a secret escaping.

* * *

On the morning the letter came from the Imperial Observatory, Felix had been five years a boy and had almost forgotten to count.

She was at the bench fitting a balance staff no thicker than an eyelash when the postman's knock came, and Marta brought the letter in on her saucer of coffee like a dish served at table. Heavy paper. An actual seal. The workshop went quiet around it the way a room goes quiet around a bishop.

"Read it aloud," Marta commanded, delighted. "Anything with a seal is theater, and theater wants a voice."

Felix broke the seal. The handwriting was precise, bureaucratic, faintly impatient.

*To the workshop of Herr A. Brandt, clockmaker: The master regulator of the Imperial Observatory has failed and the failure resists the efforts of the court mechanicians. It is recommended by ——— that your apprentice, F. Halloran, whose repair of the St. Ulrich turret clock gave satisfaction, attend the Observatory at his earliest convenience. The instrument is of national importance. Discretion and competence are both required. The fee will reflect the former.*

"*His* earliest convenience," Marta purred. "Listen to that. Our Felix is becoming a personage."

"Discretion and competence," Brandt repeated, reading over her shoulder, and Felix felt rather than saw him go still. "Both required." He took the letter from her hands and read it twice, and his face did what his face always did, which was nothing, but his thumb pressed hard enough on the paper to dent it.

"The observatory," he said at last. "Imperial appointments. Imperial clerks. Imperial—everything." He handed the letter back. "You'll go, of course. Refusing would be noticed, and being noticed refusing is worse than being noticed going." He looked at her then, straight on, and for a moment the border wavered—the old border, the sacred line—and she saw in his eyes something she had seen only once before, the day he'd bandaged her thumb: the knowledge, held carefully, at arm's length, like something hot.

"Take the tools for a full dismantling," he said. "And Felix—" the name, her stolen name, in his mouth always slightly heavier than other names— "if anything there asks you who you are, you are what you do. Nothing else. Say it back."

"I'm what I do," she said. "Nothing else."

He nodded and returned to his bench, and the workshop resumed its breathing, fifty clocks ticking out their measured secrets, and Felix stood holding the letter with its imperial seal and felt the two futures pull at her like tides: the commission that would make her name, secure her mastership, make Felix Halloran a craftsman of record in the capital of the Empire—

—and visibility, which was the one thing her whole life had been built to avoid.

She packed the tools anyway. Steady hands. She was what she did.

She left at dawn, while the lamps were still burning pale in the fog, and she did not yet know—as she stepped out into the grey street, as the workshop door closed behind her with its familiar click like a pallet releasing—that before the sun was fully up she would find, on the observatory steps, a child, and a note, and a sabotage dressed up to look like an accident.

She only knew that she was walking toward the light, and that for the first time in five years, being seen felt less like a danger than like a summons.
