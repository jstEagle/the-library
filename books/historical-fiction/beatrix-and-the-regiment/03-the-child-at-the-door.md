## 3. The Child at the Door

The fog on the Glacis had not yet lifted when Felix came up the observatory hill, and in that grey half-light the great dome looked like the skull of some sleeping animal, patient and enormous, indifferent to the small figure trudging toward it with a bag of files over one shoulder. Her breath made little ghosts that vanished as soon as they were born. She counted her footsteps the way she always did when she was afraid—left, right, left—and told herself, for the hundredth time since Brandt had pressed the commission into her hands two days before, that this was a gift. That visibility was the whole point of a career. That no one in Vienna had reason to look twice at a lean apprentice with ink-stained fingers and a voice she kept low by habit, not by effort.

She did not quite believe it. She had not believed anything comforting in five years.

The observatory's master clock was famous in the way certain things are famous without being loved: every clockmaker in the city knew of it, and perhaps four of them had ever been permitted to touch it. Built forty years ago by an Italian whose name had been worn off its own nameplate by centuries of hands—no, decades, Felix corrected herself; she was always borrowing centuries from older things to make her trade feel nobler—it regulated the observation chronometers, drove the dome's shutter mechanism through a train of rods and gears, and struck the hours with a sound so deep that, on still nights, it was said to reach the Stephansdom. It was failing. That was all Herr Brandt knew: losing time, then gaining it, then stopping altogether for six minutes on Tuesday while the astronomers stood beneath it like mourners.

*You will go,* Brandt had said, in a tone that was not quite pride and not quite dread. *You have the lightest hands in the workshop, and the directorate wants the work done quietly. No apprentices gawking. No journeymen gossiping.* A pause, and then, softer, almost to himself: *Be back before dark.*

He never said *be careful*. He had stopped saying it years ago, because they both understood what the words would mean if he said them aloud.

The steps of the observatory rose out of the fog in three long flights, and Felix took them slowly, letting her hand trail along the cold stone balustrade, feeling the grit of lichen under her palm. The city behind her was waking—she could hear the milk carts, a dog somewhere, the far iron clatter of the first tram of horses on the Ring—but here on the hill there was only wind and the creak of the dome above, turning or settling or breathing. She reached the top landing.

The girl was asleep against the door.

Felix stopped so abruptly that her heel caught the edge of the top step and her whole body rocked backward, arms flailing once for balance, the tool bag swinging heavy at her side. For a heartbeat she thought—a mad, bright instant—that the child was a bundle of rags left by some servant, or a coat fallen from a shoulder. Then the bundle sighed, turned its face toward the stone, and Felix saw the fine bones of a cheek, the dark lash-smudge of closed eyes, a smut of dirt on a chin that was unmistakably, irrevocably alive.

A girl. Six years old, perhaps seven—small for either. Dressed well, or dressed as a child is dressed by someone who once dressed her better: a wool coat brushed and re-brushed until the nap shone, though the hem was soaked dark with dew; boots too good for the street; a bonnet tied with a bow that someone's fingers had made with care. Her hair had been braided. Not hastily. The braid was even, deliberate, the work of hands that loved what they were doing, and that detail went into Felix like a splinter: *someone braided her hair this morning, or yesterday, and then walked away.*

There was no one else on the landing. The fog held the whole hilltop to itself.

Felix stood very still. In the workshop she had learned to read a room in a glance—the angle of a dropped tool, the shadow of a crack in a brass plate—and now she read the scene at the door with the same involuntary precision. The child lay curled against the left side of the doorframe, tucked into the recess where the stone curved, out of the wind. Someone had chosen that spot. Someone had known that the recess would shelter her. And pinned to the child's coat, just below the collarbone, was a folded square of paper.

Felix knelt. Slowly, the way one kneels near a strange dog, presenting no sudden shape. Up close the girl smelled of soap and cold and something faintly sweet, a child's smell, and her breathing was deep and even, the terrible trust of real exhaustion. Felix un-pinned the note. The paper was good stock—rag paper, cream, the kind that cost more than a journeyman's dinner—and the hand across it was strong, upright, unhurried:

**FORGET THIS FACE.**

Nothing else. No signature, no instruction, no coin sewn into the hem for whoever found her. Just those three words in black ink, and beneath them nothing, and the nothing was louder than any threat Felix had ever heard spoken aloud.

Her fingers wanted to shake. She held them still, the way she held a file steady, and folded the note along its original creases and put it inside her shirt, against her chest, where it lay like a coal.

*Forget this face.*

But she was already memorizing it. That was her curse, if it was a curse—the same faculty that let her rebuild a broken gear-train in her head from three seconds' looking now assembled the girl's face into permanent inventory: the small scar at the eyebrow, healed white; the chapped lower lip; the way the child's thumb was hooked into her own collar even in sleep, holding on. Felix could forget nothing she had truly seen. She had proved it five years ago, on the worst night of her life, and every night since.

She should fetch the porter. She should knock, raise the alarm, give the child over to the astronomers and their housekeeper and wash her hands of whatever grief had been set down on these steps like a parcel. That was the sensible sequence. That was what a careful person did, a person who intended to remain invisible in Vienna for another fifty years.

Instead Felix sat down on the cold stone beside the girl, arranged her own coat over the small wet hem, and waited for her to wake, and hated herself for the tenderness already taking root, because tenderness was a debt and debts were the thing her family had taught her most about.

* * *

The girl woke when the sun finally broke the fog, a blade of gold sliding across the landing, and opened her eyes directly onto Felix's face with no startle at all—as if she had known who would be sitting there, as if she had been promised.

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The child studied her the way Felix studied mechanisms: thoroughly, without embarrassment, cataloguing. Felix let herself be examined. She knew what the child saw—not a woman, not yet a man, something between, in a workman's coat with sawdust in the seams and a face that was handsome in a blunt, unfinished way, all planes and watchfulness.

"Are you the clockman?" the girl said at last. Her German was Vienna's but finer, rounded at the edges, a drawing-room accent shrunk down to a child's size.

"I'm the clockmaker's apprentice," Felix said. "My master sent me."

The girl absorbed this. Then, with the terrible logic of the very young: "Papa said a clockman would come. He said wait for the clockman." She sat up, and Felix saw how stiffly she moved, how her joints complained, how long she must have lain on the stone. "I waited. I didn't cry." She said it like a report delivered to a superior. Like something rehearsed. "Am I allowed to come inside now?"

"Where is your papa?" Felix asked gently.

The change was instantaneous and total: the child's face shut like a door, like a case closed over its works, and behind her eyes something happened that Felix recognized because she had done it herself at eleven years old, standing in a gutted counting-house while creditors hammered at the shutters. The child went somewhere else. Somewhere interior and locked.

"Papa said I'm not to say," she said. Flat. Recited. And then, quieter, with a crack running through the recitation: "Papa said you'd know what to do with the clock."

Felix's skin went cold in the morning sun.

"The clock," she repeated.

"He said—" The girl frowned, working at memory the way one works at a knot, careful not to pull wrong. "He said it wasn't broken. He said somebody *made* it stop. And that when it was fixed, everyone would see. He made me say words after him, over and over, but he said those were only for later, only for when—" She stopped. Her jaw set, small and stubborn and frightened. "I'm not to say."

The words *over and over* hung in the air. Felix thought of the even braid. Of the good paper. Of *forget this face*, written in an unhurried hand. None of it fit together, and all of it fit together too well: this was not abandonment. This was placement. Someone had put this child here the way one sets a spring under tension—in a place where, sooner or later, only a clockmaker would stand.

"Come inside," Felix said. "It's cold."

* * *

The porter, when she finally got him to answer—old Ferenc, half-deaf, suspicious of everything including sunrise—grumbled that he'd found no child when he locked the west door at nine, that no child had come past him, that children did not simply grow on the observatory steps like mushrooms. But he had keys enough and questions few, which was why the astronomers kept him, and he let them in through the instrument hall with a shrug that surrendered the entire problem to higher wages than his.

They crossed the hall together, the girl's hand swallowed in Felix's, her small boots ringing on the flagstones. Around them the observatory woke in its slow institutional way: an assistant hurrying past with charts under his arm, glancing once, incuriously; the smell of lamp oil and cold stone and, beneath it, the particular metallic sweetness of old machinery. The girl's head turned and turned, drinking in the orrery, the star charts, the long brass telescopes resting in their cradles like tame cannons. Children are not supposed to be quiet. This child was silent as an escaped bird, watching everything, touching nothing.

"Have you a name?" Felix asked.

The pause was measured. "Lena," the girl said. And then, after a beat too long to be natural: "Just Lena."

*Just Lena.* As if the rest had been amputated. Felix filed the hesitation away with the rest and asked no more, because the corridor ahead opened onto the clock chamber, and everything else in her went suddenly, entirely quiet.

The master clock stood in its own vaulted room at the heart of the building, and it was magnificent even in illness: nine feet of walnut case glazed down its length, the movement visible within like a bronze heart suspended in amber, pendulum swinging—or meant to swing—from a wall bracket built like a cathedral buttress. The astronomers kept the chamber swept and warm. It smelled of dry wood and machine oil and dust burnt hot.

And it had been murdered.

Not failed. Felix had repaired failed clocks since she was nine years old; she knew failure the way a physician knows fever, its textures and excuses. A clock fails by degrees—dirt in the pivots, wear in the escapement teeth, a mainspring setting, oil gone to varnish. Failure has a texture of neglect. What she was looking at had a texture of intent.

Someone had been inside the movement with tools and knowledge.

She saw it in layers, each worse than the last. First the small things, forgivable: the crutch pin bent a degree off true, the suspension spring kinked—vandalism of a crude sort, the kind a drunkard's elbow might do. But then the count wheel: two teeth filed, subtly, expertly, so that they meshed but would skip under load, introducing errors no one could diagnose without stripping the train entirely. And then the worst of it, the thing that stopped her breath: the maintaining power spring, the device that keeps a clock alive during winding, had been *altered*—its stopwork displaced, adjusted so that at some unpredictable future winding the great barrel would be allowed to run free, the entire going train whipping to destruction in a shriek of stripped brass and shattered teeth. Not sabotage to stop the clock. Sabotage to destroy it, later, in the hands of whoever was blamed for winding it wrong.

Whoever had done this had understood the mechanism completely. Had understood that a casual repair would chase symptoms forever. Had understood that a good clockmaker would eventually find the truth—and had therefore arranged for the clock to destroy itself, and possibly its repairer, before that truth could be spoken.

Felix stood before the open case door for a long time. Behind her Lena watched, and Ferenc watched, and neither of them spoke, because the silence coming off Felix's back had weight to it.

"Herr Apprentice?" Ferenc ventured at last. "Bad, is it?"

"Not bad," Felix heard herself say, in the flat calm she used for delivering hard estimates to clients. "Deliberate."

She stepped closer, lamp raised, and began the examination properly, because craft was the one place fear had never been able to follow her. Hands steady now. Mind narrowing to a point. The bent crutch pin—she straightened it, tested it. The filed teeth—she marked them with chalk for replacement. She worked outward from the vandalism toward the center of the mechanism, and the center, always, was the escapement: the heart of hearts, where the clock's stored fury was released one restrained pulse at a time, tick, and tick, the whole art of the machine compressed into that small controlled surrender.

The escapement assembly had been removed and reseated. Recently. There were scratches in the dust film on the plates that were not in the dust on anything else—fresh work, done within days. Whoever altered the maintaining power had also taken out the escapement bridge, done something beneath it, and put it all back with a craftsman's neatness.

Felix fetched her smallest screwdrivers and her loudest heartbeat and began to disassemble what had been so carefully reassembled.

Under the bridge, where the recoil escapement lived among its jeweled bearings, she found that the depth of the lockings had been tampered with—but that was not all. Her eye, trained by five years of Brandt's merciless inspections to notice what did not belong, snagged on a discrepancy of shadow. One of the bridge pillars was thicker than its brothers. Thicker, and newer, the brass of it brighter under the grime. It unscrewed too easily, having never borne a load.

It was hollow. And packed inside it, rolled tight as a wick, was paper.

Felix did not touch it at first. She looked at it the way one looks at a snake in a garden: absolutely still, computing. Paper hidden inside a sabotaged clock, in the most important clock in the Austrian Empire, sealed with a precision that matched the precision of the sabotage itself. The same hands. The same night, perhaps. Someone had hidden something here and then rigged the clock to die—to burn its own heart out, scatter its wheels across the chamber floor, turn the hiding place into wreckage no sane searcher would comb. Destroying the message by destroying the messenger. If not for a failing rate that had drawn attention early—too early for the saboteur's schedule—the clock would have torn itself apart weeks from now, unremarked, and whatever was rolled inside that pillar would have died unnamed in the scrap bin.

*He said somebody made it stop,* Lena had said. *When it was fixed, everyone would see.*

Slowly, with two fingers, Felix drew the roll of papers out of the pillar. They were dense, closely written pages folded to the width of a finger, and around the middle of the packet a strip of paper had been pasted like a seal, blank—no crest, no name. Deliberately anonymous, and deliberately preserved. Whatever these pages were, they had been meant to survive. Everything else—the clock, the reputation of whoever was blamed, possibly the body of the apprentice sent to fix it—had been meant not to.

"Felix Halloran," she said to herself, very quietly, using the false name like a handrail, "what have you put your hands into?"

Behind her, Lena had gone to stand close to the great pendulum, small face tilted up at the slow arc of the bob, and she was whispering something under her breath—counting, maybe, or the words her father had drilled into her, *over and over*, the ones that were only for later. Felix stuffed the papers inside her shirt beside the note, felt the two of them lying together against her sternum like coals, and understood, with the full clarity that comes one instant too late to be any use, that the child on the steps and the sabotage in the clock were one story, that the note's three words were not advice but warning, and that the men who had written both wanted not only this face forgotten but this clock, and everything sleeping inside it, destroyed.

From the corridor came the sound of boots—several pairs, unhurried, confident, the tread of men expected nowhere and answerable to no one.

"Apprentice," Ferenc called from the doorway, his voice gone strange. "Gentlemen here asking after the clock. Well-dressed gentlemen."

Felix closed the empty pillar, seated the bridge, and turned to meet them with a craftsman's bland and careful face, while against her heart the stolen evidence lay warm as a second pulse.
