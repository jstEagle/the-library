## 4. What the Clock Keeps

The observatory's master clock stood in its tower chamber like a patient awaiting surgery, and Felix Halloran circled it the way a surgeon circles: slowly, respectfully, reading the body before touching it.

She had been at it since dawn—or rather, since after dawn, after the child on the steps had been fed bread from her own satchel and installed, weeping without sound now, in the caretaker's empty cottage at the lane's end. The girl—Lena, she said her name was, in a voice like something rescued from deep water—had fallen asleep within minutes of being left with a blanket and an instruction to stay. Felix did not trust the instruction to hold past noon. She worked with one ear cocked toward the stairwell, which was a poor way to work on an escapement.

But the escapement was where the wrongness lived. She had known it since yesterday, when she'd first opened the case and found the mechanism starved. A clock this age, this quality, did not simply fail; it was maintained by men who understood that neglect was a form of vandalism. Someone had let it starve deliberately—and then, when it still refused to die, someone had done more.

Felix set down her lamp, wedged the lens into her eye, and put her fingers into the heart of the machine.

* * *

Herr Brandt had taught her hands before he taught her anything else. On her first day in the workshop five years ago—a hollow-eyed boy of sixteen who flinched at loud voices and could not meet anyone's gaze—Brandt had placed a disassembled anchor escapement before her and said nothing at all for two hours. He had watched her reassemble it. Then he had dismantled it again while she watched, and left it there, and gone back to his bench.

She understood only years later what the lesson was: that a mechanism tells you the truth about itself if you refuse to look away. That every flaw has a history. That nothing breaks without cause, and no cause is ever only mechanical.

So now she looked. And she did not look away.

The escape wheel was sound. The pallets were sound, though fouled with old, thickened oil—the starvation she'd expected. The suspension spring was new, absurdly so, bright against the patina of everything around it, and it had been fitted by someone with competence but not care; the holes punched for its pins were a half-tenth too large, the kind of error a man makes working hurriedly, or frightenedly, by bad light.

And behind the spring, where the crutch should have swung free against open air, something resisted.

Felix held her breath. She had spent five years learning never to force a mechanism—to coax, to ease, to ask rather than demand—but her hands wanted to force now, because whatever sat behind that plate was not clockwork. It had no business inside a movement. Metal that was not brass. Weight where lightness belonged.

She eased the crutch aside. She loosened the retaining screws with fingers that would not quite stay steady, and felt the odd, breath-held intimacy of touching something hidden—something that had been hidden by hands, by a person, by intent.

The false plate came away in her palm.

Behind it lay a cavity that no drawing of this movement had any right to contain. It had been cut into the frame's spare mass, cleanly, expertly—by a clockmaker, or under a clockmaker's eye—and into it had been fitted a shallow box of tin, soldered shut along its seams, wrapped once in oilcloth gone stiff with age. The box was smaller than her hand. It weighed almost nothing.

It weighed everything. She knew that immediately, the way one knows a room has been cried in.

* * *

She sat back on her heels and did not touch it again for a long moment.

Outside the tower window Vienna was going about its morning: carts on the street below, a milk-seller calling, gulls quarreling over the river. Ordinary sounds. Sounds from a world in which tin boxes did not hide inside clocks, in which abandoned children did not appear on steps with notes sewn into their coats, in which a young woman wearing her dead brother's name could climb a tower, fix a clock, collect her fee, and remain invisible and safe.

The note from the child's coat was folded in her breast pocket. *Forget this face.* The hand had been hard, controlled, educated—the hand of a man accustomed to being obeyed. She had turned the phrase over all morning and it gave up nothing except dread. Forget this face implied a face worth remembering. It implied that remembering was dangerous. It implied that somewhere there were people who checked.

And now this. Two mysteries in one tower, arriving within an hour of each other. Felix did not believe in coincidence. Brandt said coincidences were what ignorant men called convergences—two rivers meeting in ground that had always been destined to hold their junction. She had come here to repair a clock. The child had been left at the clock's door. Something hidden in the clock's heart.

Her skin crawled with the shape of a thought she refused yet to finish.

* * *

She took up her smallest knife and worked it along the solder seam.

The tin gave with a crackle like stepped-on ice. Inside, wrapped in a second layer of oilcloth, lay papers—not many, perhaps twenty leaves, folded tight, pressed flat as pressed flowers. Their edges had foxed. Whatever they were, they had waited in the dark a long time, keeping company with the ticking of the great clock above them, counting out hours for no one.

Unfolding them on her cloth, Felix saw first not words but pattern: columns of figures, lines of dense script, and between them passages in a ciphered hand—clusters of numbers and letters arranged in groups of four, meaningless to her eye, deliberate beyond any doubt. At the foot of the first page, in ordinary ink, an ordinary signature:

*A. Voss.*

Below the signature, a title that stopped her breath: *Schriftführer, K.K. Finanz-Guardium* — clerk, Imperial Treasury.

She knew the name before she knew why she knew it. Then she did know why, and wished she didn't. Anton Voss. Three weeks ago the newspapers had given him two lines: a treasury clerk found dead in the Donaukanal, drowned, an accident of the winter ice, condolences to his family. Two lines because clerks drown every winter. But the workshop apprentices had talked about it anyway, the way apprentices talk—there had been gossip that Voss hadn't drowned where his body was found, that he'd been seen alive near the Prater a full day after the date his death was assigned, that his widow had been denied a look at certain documents. Gossip thinned as it traveled, and by the time it reached a workshop in the Schulerstraße it was mostly silence with a name attached.

Anton Voss, who signed these papers. Anton Voss, whose coded hand filled them. Anton Voss, dead in the canal while his papers slept inside the observatory clock.

Felix became aware, very suddenly, of how alone she was. Three flights below, the caretaker dozed in his chair. Above her, nothing but dome and sky. The tower door was unlocked; anyone might walk in; anyone might find her sitting among the scattered secrets of a murdered man.

She gathered the pages quickly, squared them, wrapped them, and then—her hands moving before her mind gave permission—slid them inside her shirt, flat against her ribs, where they lay cold as coins until her warmth claimed them.

* * *

She meant to go straight back to the workshop. She meant to put the papers in Brandt's hands and wash her own of them. That was the correct procedure; that was what an apprentice did with anything beyond his understanding—he carried it upstairs, so to speak, to the man at the master bench.

Instead she found herself reading one more page before she left the tower. Just one. She told herself it was diligence: an apprentice should understand what he repairs, and clearly the sabotage of the escapement was connected to this hiding place, and understanding the connection might matter to the repair. The reasoning had the smoothness of all self-serving reasoning, and she let it carry her anyway.

The page she chose was less ciphered than the others—half plain text, as though Voss had begun writing openly and lost patience with caution. The handwriting slanted downhill, hurried, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper.

*...they do not fear discovery, that is the terrible thing. Albrecht signs and the estates vanish into other names and the money returns dressed as fees, and the men who should audit are paid in the same coin. I have copied the true ledgers. The originals are where no auditor looks, where I have kept what matters most to me since my wife died—in the thing that counts, because it does not lie. If anything happens to me, whoever finds this must know: the child is innocent of all of it, and Kessler is a servant, not a master. Follow the signatures to the source.*

The canal, Felix thought. The thing that counts does not lie.

Her father had kept ledgers too. Ledgers of a failing wool concern, honest down to their final ruinous line, and the honesty had saved no one—the creditors had descended anyway, and the house had emptied, and her father had sat in the stripped parlor like a man listening to a clock that had already stopped. She had fled that house with her brother's name in her mouth and forty gulden sewn into her coat, and everything since had been built on the principle that invisibility was safety, that the seen were the seized, that a boy who fixed clocks in silence could outlast any storm.

And here was a man who had done the opposite. Who had counted, recorded, witnessed—who had trusted a machine to keep his truth because men would not—and who had ended in the canal while his daughter ended on the steps with an order to be forgotten.

*Follow the signatures to the source.* As though he had known someone would find them. As though he had chosen the hiding place not merely for safety but for address: a master clock in a public observatory is repaired by clockmakers, and only clockmakers would find what a clockmaker hid. Voss had written a letter and mailed it into the future, and the future had turned out to be a girl of twenty-one wearing a dead boy's clothes, standing in a tower with stolen evidence against her ribs and a motherless child asleep a lane away.

No one asked her to carry it. No one would ask. That was precisely the horror of it—the request had been made to nobody, addressed to whoever, and the whole world would absolve her if she walked away. Fold the papers back. Resolder the tin. Return it to the dark behind the escapement, declare the clock mended, take her fee, forget the face, keep her nameless little life.

She could do it. She had practiced disappearance for five years. She was very good at it.

* * *

The child was awake when Felix came to the cottage.

Not crying—that seemed to be beyond her, or beneath her, some exhausted country past tears—but sitting upright on the pallet with the blanket around her shoulders, watching the door with eyes that had the awful patience of a child who has learned that watching is all watching gets you. On the table beside her, Felix's remaining bread lay untouched. Beside the bread, arranged in a careful row, three buttons the girl had evidently picked from the hem of her own coat.

"I fixed your button," Lena said, pointing at Felix's sleeve, where a thread had been hanging that morning. "It's tucked in. So it doesn't unravel."

Something in Felix's chest turned over and would not turn back.

She knelt so their faces were level. Up close the girl smelled of coal smoke and old wool, and there was a bruise fading yellow along her jaw that Felix made herself look at, because looking away was a luxury of the uninvolved, and she suspected she was involved whether she consented or not.

"Lena. Your papa—did he bring you to the observatory?"

A small shake of the head. "A man brought me. Papa's friend." Her mouth worked. "Papa said if the clock stops, the man will know what to do. Papa loves the clock. He said—" The girl's brow furrowed with the effort of exact recall, of a six-year-old trying to preserve a sentence the way one preserves a pressed flower, intact and flat and meaningful forever. "He said the clock keeps what he gives it better than God keeps prayers."

The words fell into the room and lay there.

Felix felt the papers shift against her ribs—not physically; they were only paper—but she felt them, the way one feels a letter one has not yet opened lying in one's pocket through a whole church service. *The thing that counts, because it does not lie.* A father's faith, lodged in a machine. A dead man's testimony, ticking overhead in the dark all these weeks while the city walked beneath it.

"Did Papa give the clock something?" Felix asked gently. "Do you know what?"

Lena considered the question with grave seriousness. Then she reached out and, with two fingers, touched the place over Felix's heart—the way children do, artlessly, meaning nothing precise by it—and said, "Secrets. He gave it our secrets. He said they'd be safe there until it was time."

Until it was time.

Outside, the afternoon had begun to dim toward evening, and somewhere across the rooftops a church bell rang the hour, and Felix thought of the great broken clock above them all, silent these many weeks, holding its breath. She thought of Herr Brandt downstairs in the workshop, who suspected what she was and had said nothing for five years, whose silences had kept her alive. She thought of the hard educated hand on the note in her pocket—*forget this face*—and of how easy forgetting was, how practiced, how cheap.

She had come to this tower to mend a mechanism. She rose now from her knees beside the child with the weight of something else entirely settled on her shoulders: twenty leaves of a murdered man's certainty, a cipher she could not read, a name—*Albrecht*—that meant nothing to her and would mean everything to dangerous men, and a single sentence drilled into a small girl as though into soft metal, to be struck later, to ring.

Someone had sabotaged this clock to make sure the secrets stayed buried. Someone had left this child to make sure the last witness forgot. Both errands had failed by exactly one apprentice's stubborn habit of refusing to look away.

Felix took Lena's cold hands in hers and stood there a moment longer, saying nothing, deciding everything.

Then she gathered the girl onto her hip—she was lighter than she should have been, light as the tin box, light as everything in this affair except what it weighed—and carried her out into the darkening lane, toward the workshop, toward Brandt, carrying against her ribs the truth of a dead man and against her collarbone the breathing evidence of what that truth had cost.

She did not yet know that by morning she would have to decide whether to show it to the one man whose silences had always kept her safe—or defy him.
