## 5. Herr Brandt's Warning

Tobias Brandt had learned long ago that a workshop tells you everything, if you listen to it the way you listen to a movement. The rasp of a file worn past its temper meant an apprentice was hurrying. A lathe running quiet meant Ivo was at the bench and content. And silence from Felix—silence where there should have been the small industrious music of her tools—meant something was wrong.

He heard that silence on Thursday evening, coming up from the cellar with a crate of brass stock in his arms, and he set the crate down more carefully than he needed to, the way a man sets down a thing when his hands have begun to know something before his mind consents to it.

The workshop at number nine in the Schulerdgasse was his whole kingdom: forty years of it, sawdust in the grain of the floorboards, the smell of oil and rosin and cold metal, four benches under lamps whose shades he cleaned himself every Sunday so no one else would have to be blamed for their dimness. His father had kept the shop before him, and his brother Josef had been meant to keep it after, and there was a chair by the south window—still by the south window, forty years on, he had never once moved it—that had been Josef's chair.

Felix sat at the middle bench, not working. That alone was wrong. Worse: before the boy lay a packet of papers, folded small, and beside it the escapement plate from the observatory clock, its screws laid out in neat rows the way Felix always laid things out, orderly as prayer beads.

Brandt knew the papers were the trouble before he crossed the room. He knew it the way he knew weather in his knee, the way he had known, five years ago, on a raw October night, that the thin creature shivering on his doorstep with a dead boy's name in his mouth and fear coming off him like steam was going to be the ruin or the redemption of his shop, and quite possibly both.

"Felix." He set his hand flat on the papers. "What is this?"

The boy looked up. That was the other thing about Felix Halloran—Brandt had known within a month what the boy was, had watched the careful performance of a journeyman's swagger sit badly on those narrow shoulders, had seen him duck his head to hide the length of his lashes, bind and stoop and roughen his voice and do it all so competently that Brandt sometimes wondered whether he had imagined the whole thing. But he had not imagined it. He had said nothing for five years. There were silences, in his experience, that were the only honest form of love available to certain men.

"The observatory clock," Felix said. "It was sabotaged. Not broken—sabotaged. And this was hidden inside the escapement."

"Hidden inside—" Brandt's hand pressed flatter against the papers, as though he could press them out of existence. "Show me."

Felix showed him. The compartment was beautiful; that was what made it terrible. A false plate, cut and fitted so finely that Brandt himself, who had taught the boy everything the boy knew, might have serviced the clock a hundred times and never found it. Someone had built that hiding place knowing exactly who would one day open the movement and exactly what they would find—or not find.

"And the papers?"

"Coded. Mostly coded. But this page—" Felix's voice dropped, and there it was again, that habit of looking toward the door before speaking, which Brandt had never trained into him because it could not be trained, only inherited. "This page is plain. It names a clerk. Anton Voss, Imperial Treasury. It says he feared for his life. It says if the clock stopped, someone should look inside it. Herr Brandt, Voss is the clerk who died three weeks ago. Drowned in the Danube, they say. Fell in drunk, they say."

Brandt did not answer at once. He turned instead to the topmost paper, where Felix had folded it back to show the seal—and the floor of the world tilted, gently, horribly, the way it had tilted once before.

The impression in the wax was a double eagle within a cartouche, and beneath the eagle, small enough that a careless eye would take it for ornament, a set of scales. The seal of the Office of Sequestrated Estates. The Treasury's own hand.

He knew it because Josef had once spread a paper bearing that same seal on this very bench—Josef, who could never leave a locked box alone, who had taken a commission from a magistrate friend to copy a set of treasury schedules, seized estates, transferred holdings, names of buyers and names of fronts—and had come home white-faced and laughing, saying, *Tobias, do you know what they're doing? Do you know what they've done with half of Bohemia?* And Tobias had said what he had spent his whole life saying: *Burn it. It isn't ours. Burn it.*

Josef had promised. Josef had even tried, probably. But he was dead within the year—thrown from his horse on a road he rode every week, a road he could have ridden blind, and the horse had come home unhurt, which was the detail that had lived in Tobias like a splinter ever since, working its way deeper each winter. Horses did not stumble on that road. He had ridden it himself afterward, once, slowly, looking for the stone or the rut that had killed his brother, and found smooth gravel all the way to Tulln.

That had been thirty-one years ago. He was twenty-three then, and furious, and he had gone to the magistrate's office to ask questions, and a very polite man had received him with coffee and told him how sorry everyone was, and how accidents were God's will, and how fine a craftsman his father was, and how important it was that the workshops of Vienna remain—what had been the phrase?—*undisturbed.* He had understood the word *undisturbed* perfectly. He had gone home and burned what remained of Josef's papers himself, every sheet, including the letter Josef had written their mother listing what he'd found, and he had told his parents it was grief, only grief, that made him do it, and his mother had looked at him with eyes that said she knew better and said nothing either, and so their family had become a family of silences stacked like coins, and the coinage had held its value: no one else in the Brandt family had ever died strangely again.

Now he stood with the same seal under his thumb and his one vulnerable person across the bench from him, and he felt the old fear come up through the floorboards of the shop like damp.

"Burn them," he said.

"Herr Brandt—"

"Burn them now. Tonight. Not in the stove here—in the brazier in the yard, and stir the ash." He began screwing the escapement plate's cover back on with quick, hard turns. "You will tell the observatory the clock is repaired. You will repair it properly, mind, because it will be inspected, but you will tell them nothing of this compartment except that it was empty, if they ask, and they will not ask, because whoever built it does not want it asked about."

"They killed a man," Felix said quietly. "They left a child on the steps of the observatory in the frost."

The words went through Brandt like a nail driven home. He had been afraid of this since the moment he touched the papers—not merely the danger but the shape of the danger, which was precisely shaped to catch a person like Felix, a person who had spent five years being invisible and therefore knew the exact price of invisibility paid by others.

"There was a child?" he said carefully.

Felix hesitated. Five years, and the boy still hesitated before lying to him, which Brandt chose to find touching rather than alarming. "I couldn't leave her there. She's—I've placed her somewhere safe."

*Somewhere safe.* In God's own Vienna, in November, with the treasury's hounds already sniffing along the Ringstrasse after a drowned man's effects. Brandt closed his eyes briefly. When he opened them, Felix was watching him with that level, unflinching look that came and went—the look that surfaced through the apprentice's mask like a fish rising, and dove again.

"You don't understand what this seal is," Brandt said. "So I will tell you, once, and then we will not speak of it again." He pulled Josef's chair around—he had to make himself touch it, some Thursdays more than others—and sat, because this story was owed a seated man's gravity. "My brother worked for Magistrate Renner, copying documents. Among them, schedules from the Office of Sequestrated Estates. Estates taken from families for debt or disloyalty, sold or leased, the proceeds supposed to pass through the public accounts." He tapped the wax seal without looking at it. "They do not pass through the public accounts. They pass through private hands, and the hands are high up, higher than magistrates, higher than the men who sign your guild papers. Josef found out. Within the year he was dead, and the manner of his death convinced me—and should convince you—that these are not men who send soldiers in the night, which would at least be honest. They arrange the world so that you die of ordinary causes. A horse stumbles. A river takes you. A lamp falls. And afterward everyone shakes their heads and says what a pity, and your family buries you, and the accounts balance."

Felix had gone very still. "And you burned what he found."

"I burned what he found, and I kept my head down, and I raised this shop on that silence, and I took in a runaway because I recognized another kind of silence when I saw one." He let that land. He watched the boy absorb it—watched the flicker of something like relief, that the knowledge was finally spoken between them, followed immediately by the wariness underneath. "I am not telling you this to reproach you. I am telling you so that you understand I am not guessing. I know what these people do. I have paid the tuition. Burn the papers, Felix. The child cannot testify, the clerk is beyond helping, and you are one apprentice with clever hands and no name worth hanging. This is not cowardice. This is arithmetic."

For a moment he believed it would work. Forty years of authority stood behind his voice; the workshop itself seemed to side with him, the lamps steady, the tools in their ranks.

But Felix picked up the topmost paper and held it, not reading, weighing. And said, in the voice of the girl inside the boy, which Brandt had perhaps never been meant to hear: "Herr Brandt. If your brother had hidden evidence in a clock—if he had died making sure it would survive him—and someone found it and burned it, what would his ghost get from you? Arithmetic?"

"That is not—"

"You said yourself they don't kill loudly. Then they won't kill me loudly either. Which means I have time. Time to copy it, time to understand it, time to put it somewhere safer than my own pocket." Felix's hands were steady on the papers, and Brandt thought, with a strange sinking pride, *I taught those hands nothing but steadiness, and now they use it against me.* "I'm not asking you to help me. I'm asking you not to make me do it behind your back. You've kept my secret five years. Let me keep Voss's."

"The difference," Brandt said, and his voice cracked on it, which infuriated him, "is that no one hunts you for your secret. Yours costs you nothing but wearing a binder and answering to the wrong name. His cost him everything, and now it has landed on my bench, in my shop, among my people." He stood. The chair scraped. He heard how loud his own voice had gotten and forced it lower. "Marta sews at that window. Ivo sleeps upstairs. You brought a child into this. Do you imagine your arithmetic covers them?"

The question hit; he saw it hit. Felix looked down at the papers for a long moment, and Brandt permitted himself the hope—a foolish hope, he would think later, the hope of a man who had spent three decades mistaking silence for safety—that obedience might win.

"I'll burn them," Felix said at last, and Brandt's chest loosened— "if you tell me, honestly, that burning them makes Lena safe. Say it and mean it, and I'll feed them to the brazier myself."

*Lena.* So the child had a name already. Brandt stood there in his own workshop, in the lamplight, with his brother's chair behind him and his brother's ghost in front of him, and found that he could not say it. Could not stand in the house Josef had grown up in and pronounce the sentence *burning it makes you safe,* because he had said something like it once, thirty-one years ago—*burn it, it isn't ours, burn it and you're safe*—and the road to Tulln had been smooth gravel all the way.

"Think of it however you need to," he said stiffly. "Lock those in my office. The iron cabinet. Not your room, not the loft. My office."

He walked to the door of the shop then, needing the motion, and stood a moment in the cold of the entryway with his hand on the latch, listening to the street. A cart passing. The lamp-man far off, calling the hour. Ordinary sounds, the sounds of a city that arranged its murders to look like weather.

Behind him, he heard Felix gathering the papers with the same care she gave to a finished movement, and he understood two things with perfect clarity, and hated them equally. First, that the boy would obey him tonight and unlock that cabinet tomorrow, or the day after, because Felix Halloran was incapable of letting a mechanism—any mechanism, whether of brass or of empire—keep its secret unexamined. And second, that when it happened, Tobias Brandt would not report it, would not stop it, would stand aside as he had always stood aside—and that this time, standing aside would not be silence. This time it would be consent.

He turned back. Felix was blowing out the middle lamp, the papers gone into a leather satchel, the face above them composed and grave and entirely too young.

"Go home to your bed," Brandt said roughly. "And, Felix—this cabinet has one key. It stays with me."

Felix nodded. But at the foot of the stairs the boy paused, hand on the rail, and looked back with that unguarded face showing through one last time.

"Herr Brandt. Your brother. What was his first name?"

The question undid him more than any argument had. Nobody asked after Josef anymore; nobody living remembered to.

"Josef," Brandt said. "His name was Josef."

"Then perhaps," Felix said softly, "this time the papers won't have to burn."

And went up the stairs, leaving Tobias Brandt alone in the lamplight with the sound of his brother's name still in the room, and the sealed papers locked in the iron cabinet three steps away, ticking in his mind like something counting down.

* * *

He did not sleep. Past midnight he sat in his office with the cabinet key in his fist and the lamp turned low, doing sums he already knew the answers to. If he burned the papers himself while the household slept, the matter ended—but the child remained, wherever Felix had hidden her, and the men who had left her on those steps would not stop looking until they found her, and Felix would not give her up, and so the matter did not end; it only began without witnesses. If he went to the magistrate Renner's successor—a nonentity, a placeholder—he would be received politely, given coffee, and sent home *undisturbed.*

If he did nothing, the shop would burn. He had smelled that smoke once before, thirty-one years ago; he had simply been too young, then, to recognize the smell as smoke and not as rain.

Somewhere below, a floorboard creaked—Felix, or Marta seeing to the stove, or Ivo returning late from the Fugger's rooms where he sometimes played cards in silence and won. Brandt listened, and realized he was cataloguing the sounds of his household the way a man catalogues the contents of a house before the assessors come.

He turned the key over in his hand. On its bow, worn nearly smooth, was the mark his father had punched into every tool in this shop: a small anchor, for hope that holds.

"Damn you, Josef," he said quietly, to the empty office, to the smooth gravel road, to the chair by the south window. "You might have burned them when I asked."

The floorboard creaked again. And then, fainter, from the direction of the loft stairs—a sound that did not belong to any member of his household at all: a child's cough, quickly smothered, quickly hushed.

Brandt closed his hand around the key until the teeth bit his palm, and understood that the decision he had been refusing all night had just been taken out of his hands and installed, breathing, one floor above his head.
