## 6. A Name for the Girl

The girl had not stopped watching her.

Felix felt the weight of that gaze all through the morning, through the disassembly of the sabotaged striking train, through the careful cataloguing of bent teeth and sheared wires, through the long arithmetic of what had been done to the clock and what it would cost to undo. The child sat on the observatory's cold steps where Felix had deposited her with a blanket and a heel of bread, and she watched, unblinking, the way a bird watches a cat—not in terror exactly, but in that terrible fixed attention of something too young to know it ought to run.

*Forget this face.*

The note was still folded in Felix's waistcoat pocket. She had checked for it perhaps forty times since dawn, pressing her fingers against the paper as if to confirm it hadn't dissolved into her own flesh. Each time, the same thought arrived and was dismissed and arrived again: a face could be forgotten. A face was the easiest thing in the world to lose. But the girl herself—the small living weight of her, the chapped hands, the way she ate the bread in tiny deliberate bites like someone who had learned that food might be taken back—could not be set down on a step and unwitnessed.

By noon Felix had made her decision three times and unmade it twice. The third making held.

She climbed down from the clock's scaffold, wiped her hands on a rag that did little good, and crouched before the child at a distance she hoped was unthreatening. This required some care. In the workshop she was Felix, Herr Brandt's boy, all elbows and soot; but she remembered being six, and she remembered that grown men crouching made the world go dark.

"I'm going to touch your shoulder," she said slowly, in German, then again in the halting Czech she kept from her mother. "Like this. All right?"

The girl didn't answer. But when Felix's hand settled on the thin shoulder through the blanket, she didn't flinch either. That was somehow worse than screaming. Children screamed when they believed someone might come.

"What do they call you?" Felix asked. "Your name?"

Nothing. The dark eyes moved over Felix's face—over the smudged jaw, the cropped hair beneath the cap, searching perhaps for whatever it was people searched for—and found nothing to answer.

"All right," Felix said softly. "Not yet, then."

She looked up at the observatory doors, at the brass fittings she would have to leave until tomorrow, at the master clock hanging open like a patient mid-surgery. She thought about walking out the gate alone, reporting an abandoned child to the district office, letting the machinery of the city do what machinery did. The image lasted less than a breath. She had seen what the city's machinery produced: her father, ruined and then ignored; her brother, dead and then forgotten. Institutions were where things went to disappear.

No. Whatever this was, it would not be handed to the people who wrote notes like *forget this face*.

"Can you walk?" she asked, and held out her hand.

After a moment, the small fingers closed around hers.

* * *

The journey from the observatory to the workshop took the better part of an hour, and every minute of it Felix spent composing and discarding stories. A cousin's child. A foundling from the Karlskirche steps. A hire for kitchen work. None of them survived contact with her own imagination; all of them collapsed under the single obvious question—*why is Herr Brandt's apprentice carrying a girl through the Wieden district at dusk?*

In the end she abandoned narrative altogether and relied on the oldest trick available to the invisible: she made herself boring. She walked the way apprentices walked, slouched and hurried, and let the child trail half a step behind like a sister too young to matter, and no one gave them more than a glance. Vienna was full of children trailing adults. Vienna was full of griefs nobody examined. That, at least, the city offered freely.

Herr Brandt's workshop occupied the ground floor of a narrow house on a narrow lane, its windows fogged with lamplight by the time they arrived. Through the glass Felix could see the old man at his bench, hunched over a regulator movement, his spectacles pushed up into his white hair. Further back, Ivo sat at the engraving table, graver in hand, working by the light of two candles because he claimed candle-shadow showed line better than any lamp. Marta's sewing corner was dark; she would be upstairs in her room, or in the kitchen negotiating with the cook downstairs about the shared stove.

Felix stood outside with the child's hand in hers and understood that she was about to commit, in some small and irreversible way, to a course she could not name. Bringing the girl here was not a plan. It was a refusal of every plan that involved leaving her somewhere else.

"You'll be quiet," she told the girl, kneeling to her level. "Quieter than quiet. Can you do that?"

The girl looked at her. Then, deliberately, she pressed her lips together into a thin pale line—the first thing resembling an answer she had given anyone all day.

"Good," Felix said, and her chest did something complicated and painful. "That's very good."

* * *

Ivo saw them first, because Ivo saw everything first. He was deaf, had been since scarlet fever at nine, and he inhabited a world of such ferocious attention that a new person entering a room registered on him the way a tremor registers on a seismograph. He looked up from his plate, took in the child, took in Felix's expression, and set down his graver with the unhurried precision of a man clearing his desk for bad news.

He signed something—quick, fluid, questioning.

"Not here," Felix murmured, though she knew he read lips only when he chose to. "Loft. After Herr Brandt sleeps."

Ivo considered her for a long moment. His face was difficult to read at the best of times, all planes and stillness, but his eyes had a habit of holding a question until it was answered or withdrawn. He held it now.

*I found her,* Felix signed, clumsy and slow compared to his fluent hands. They had built their shorthand over five years of shared late hours—Ivo correcting her grammar with a flick of his fingers, her narrating the workshop aloud partly for practice and partly because silence, she had learned, could be its own kind of loneliness. *At the observatory. Someone left her. Someone bad.*

Something crossed Ivo's face at *someone bad*—not surprise, she realized later, but recognition. He glanced toward Brandt's bench, then back, and finally nodded once. Then he did something he almost never did unprompted: he reached across his table, took a scrap of copper sheet, and with three strokes of chalk drew a small bed, a closed door, and a hand laid flat over a mouth.

*Quiet,* the drawing said. *Hidden. Understood.*

"Thank you," Felix whispered, and meant it more than she could say.

Getting the girl upstairs was the simplest part. The loft above the workshop was reached by a ladder-stair behind the storeroom, a space originally built for storing cases and crates, half-claimed years ago by Marta for finished gowns awaiting delivery and by Felix for whatever she couldn't bear to keep in the dormitory room she shared with the journeymen. Marta kept a cot there for late nights. It was to that cot that Felix led the child, one finger pressed to her own lips, past the landing where Herr Brandt's voice rose faintly in argument with the housekeeper below.

The loft smelled of lavender and machine oil, an impossible combination that had become, over the years, simply *the smell of safety*. Felix lit no lamp. She sat the girl on Marta's cot, wrapped the blanket around her again, and dug into the crate beneath the eaves where she kept her few luxuries—a paper of sugared almonds gone stale, a novel with the covers torn off, a tin soldier that had belonged to no one in this household.

The tin soldier she pressed into the girl's hands. The girl held it the way soldiers hold orders.

"I have to go down," Felix said. "Someone will bring you food. You won't be alone long. I promise—" and here her voice caught on the strangeness of the word, because promises were things Felix Halloran had sworn off giving, "—I promise."

The girl's mouth opened. For one wild moment Felix thought she would speak, would produce a name or a plea or the words of whatever note had been written about her. Instead the child simply nodded, once, with the same terrible composure she had shown all day, and turned the tin soldier over in her hands.

* * *

Marta came up twenty minutes later with a bowl of soup balanced on a candlestick, and if she had questions—and Marta always had questions—she swallowed every one of them at the sight of the child.

Felix had met Marta Weiss four years ago, when the seamstress had arrived to deliver embroidered casings for a client's clock cabinet and had simply never quite finished leaving. She was fifty, sharp-faced, widowed twice over, with hands that could take thirty stitches to the inch and a tongue that could take a man apart in half that. She paid for her corner of the workshop in labor and gossip and an unshakable conviction that Herr Brandt's establishment was the last decent room in the Habsburg Empire. She had never once asked Felix why a boy apprentice slept with his boots on and flinched when grabbed. Felix had never once asked why a woman with two dead husbands and a thriving trade chose to live above a clock shop. Some arrangements were founded on the mutual agreement not to look too closely.

Marta set down the bowl, knelt, and studied the girl with the frank professional assessment she usually reserved for fabric.

"Underfed," she pronounced. "Scared out of her wits and pretending otherwise. Hair hasn't been properly brushed in weeks." A pause. "Whose is she?"

"I don't know," Felix said. Which was true. And then, because lying entirely to Marta was both impossible and ungrateful: "There was a note. I can't show it to you. I shouldn't even say that much."

Marta's eyebrows climbed toward her kerchief. Felix braced for the interrogation, the demands, the practical objections—*the police, the magistracy, the scandal, Herr Brandt's license*—all the sensible artillery that any reasonable woman would deploy.

Instead Marta sat down heavily on the edge of the cot, took the comb from her pocket, and began, without asking permission, to gently work it through the girl's tangled hair.

"Sensible of you," she said. "Some notes are best kept in pockets." She clicked her tongue at a knot, softened her grip. "Sit down, boy, before you fall down. And tell me what she eats, because whatever it is, it isn't enough."

"I don't know that either."

"Then we'll start with soup and find out the rest." Marta lifted a strand of the girl's hair to the candlelight—dark brown, with a glint of something warmer at the ends—and her hands went still for just a moment. Only a moment. "Pretty child. Somebody's pride once, whoever she belongs to."

"She doesn't belong to anybody now," Felix said, harsher than intended. "Whoever left her wanted her *gone*, Marta. Not placed. Gone. There's a difference and I felt it on those steps."

Marta was quiet for a while after that, combing. The girl ate the soup in small sips, watching them both over the rim of the bowl, and gradually—so gradually it could hardly be marked—the rigid line of her shoulders lowered by a degree.

"There's a christening gown in my trunk," Marta said at last, conversationally, as though discussing hem lengths. "From my second husband's family. No one to wear it, obviously." She smoothed the girl's hair flat with one palm, an oddly tender gesture from a woman who threatened the milk-boy weekly. "It'll want airing. And taking in. She looks to be a six-year-old wearing a three-year-old's hunger."

"Marta." Felix's throat was tight. "You don't have to—"

"Hush. I'm deciding things. It helps me think." The seamstress stood, brushed off her skirts, and pointed the comb at Felix like a verdict. "She sleeps here tonight and every night until matters clarify. I'll speak to Ivo—he'll hear nothing anyway unless he reads our lips, which he will, the scoundrel, so he may as well be included. You—" the comb swung to indicate the door, the stairs, the workshop below, "—will tell me tomorrow what you're not telling me tonight, or enough of it that I can keep us all from hanging. And none of us mentions her to Herr Brandt until we know how he'll take it. Agreed?"

Felix stared at her. Five years she had lived among these people, guarding her secret with sleeplessness and locked knees and a hundred small daily lies, convinced that concealment was a solitary craft, a one-woman performance sustained by sheer vigilance. And here was Marta, in the space of ten minutes, organizing a conspiracy as naturally as she organized a pattern layout—as though hiding a child were simply another form of work, requiring skill, materials, and a division of labor.

"Why?" Felix asked. It came out younger than she wanted. "Why would you risk it?"

Marta paused at the ladder-stair, candle in hand, and her sharp face went soft in a way Felix had witnessed perhaps twice in four years.

"Because somebody hid me once," she said. "Vienna, 1815. Everyone was hiding everyone, and mostly badly. A convent took me in and asked nothing, which the Church never does, so I've owed a debt thirty years unpaid." She lifted the candle. "This isn't charity, boy. It's bookkeeping. Now get some sleep, both of you. Ivo and I will mind the stair."

The light descended. Below, faintly, Felix heard Marta's voice in low conference, and Ivo's answering silence, and the particular quality of that silence—which was somehow more eloquent than speech—telling her that he was already in.

* * *

Felix did not sleep. She sat on the floor beside the cot with her back against the crate of secrets, watching the rise and fall of the child's breathing, listening to the workshop settle into its nighttime language of ticks and cooling metal. Down in the street a watchman called the hour. Up here, time was kept differently—in the girl's slow exhale, in the lavender-and-oil dark, in the folded note pressing against Felix's ribs like a second heart.

*Forget this face.*

She had failed already, then, because she knew she would remember this face until she died: the composure, the bread eaten in rationed bites, the way the child had pressed her lips together when asked for quiet, obedient to a stranger because obedience was clearly all she had ever been paid in.

Somewhere past midnight the girl whimpered in her sleep—a small sound, quickly suppressed, the whimpers of a child who had learned even unconsciousness was unsafe—and Felix reached out and rested two fingers lightly on her ankle through the blanket. The whimpering stopped. The breathing evened. And Felix sat in the dark thinking about clocks, because she thought about everything in terms of clocks these days.

A mechanism, Herr Brandt liked to say, was honest precisely because it could not lie about what it was. Every wheel declared its function. Every escapement admitted what it counted. People were the opposite; people were nothing but concealed trains and masked dials, and the whole art of survival was knowing which faces to show through the aperture.

But a hidden compartment changed the nature of the whole machine. She had known that since yesterday, standing before the sabotaged master clock. Something had been concealed inside it, and someone had wanted the concealment destroyed, and now she was harboring a child whose existence was connected to that destruction by threads she couldn't yet see. Bringing the girl here wasn't kindness, or not only kindness. It was also—she admitted this to herself in the honesty of the small hours—curriculum. Sooner or later the men who wrote notes would come asking about clocks, and when they did, Felix intended to know more than they did.

The girl turned in her sleep. The tin soldier slipped from her fingers, and Felix retrieved it and tucked it back into the small fist, which closed around it reflexively, trustingly, even in dreams.

"We'll call you something," Felix whispered. "Until you can tell me yourself. Everyone needs a name. Mine—" she stopped. Swallowed the sentence whole. "Everyone needs a name."

Below her, through the floorboards, she heard the front bell of the workshop give its single soft ring—the night bell, used only by clients who wished not to be seen. She heard Herr Brandt's tread crossing the shop, the muffled exchange of voices too low to parse, one of them the old man's, the other unfamiliar. Polite. Measured. The voice of a man accustomed to being answered.

Then footsteps retreating, the bolt sliding home, and a long pause before Brandt's tread resumed—slower now, heavier, pausing once near the foot of the loft stair as though considering whether to climb.

It did not climb. It withdrew to his rooms, and the house creaked into stillness.

But Felix sat unmoving in the dark with the child's warm ankle under her fingers, and thought: *someone has just asked about clocks.*
