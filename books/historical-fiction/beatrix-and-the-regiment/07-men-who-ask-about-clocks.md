## 7. Men Who Ask About Clocks

The knock came at half past nine on a Thursday morning, which was itself a kind of signature. Thieves knocked at night. Debtors knocked softly and repeatedly. The tax assessor knocked twice and entered without waiting. But this knock—three measured raps, unhurried, perfectly spaced, the knock of a man who had never in his life needed anything from anyone at an inconvenient hour—that knock was new.

Felix was at the bench with a barrel arbor pinched in the lathe, and she felt the sound travel through the wood of the bench and up through her wrists before her ears had properly sorted it. Ivo looked up from his gravers, and his eyes went to the door, then to her, and he tilted his head in that small question of his. *Do you want it answered?*

She set down the graver she wasn't using, wiped her fingers on the leather apron out of habit rather than need, and thought: *Lena is asleep above us.* Six days now the girl had slept in the loft behind the stored clock cases, wrapped in Marta's spare shawl, waking at odd hours to cry silently in a way that was worse than any loud grief Felix had ever heard. Six days of carrying her down to the water closet after dark, of teaching her to be quiet as a pendulum in a case, of telling Brandt nothing because Brandt did not want to know—and Brandt's not-knowing was a wall they had all agreed to build together without discussing the bricks.

Three raps. She crossed the workshop and opened the door.

The man on the step was perhaps forty, dressed in the grey of a good coat cut by a good tailor, neither military nor municipal in any way she could name and therefore all the more unsettling for it. He wore no badge. He carried nothing but a walking stick with a plain horn handle and gloves that had been worn enough to fit him and no more than him. His face was the sort that would pass through a room without being recalled afterward—medium height, medium coloring, eyes a shade of brown that might have been warm in another arrangement of features. He smiled, and the smile arrived precisely when a smile was called for, like a striking train releasing exactly on the hour.

"Good morning," he said. "Herr Brandt's establishment?"

"Herr Brandt's workshop," Felix said, giving the correction its full weight. Boys who corrected grown men were either insolent or well-trained; she had chosen well-trained years ago, and it served.

"And you are?"

"The apprentice. Halloran."

"Halloran." He said the name once, as if setting it on a shelf. "I am Inspector Kessler." He produced a card, and she took it, and the card told her nothing except that the paper was expensive and the engraving minimal—a man who could afford flourish and had chosen restraint, or a man who wanted no flourish traced back to him. "I wonder if I might impose upon Herr Brandt for a few minutes' conversation."

"Herr Brandt is at the University church this morning. The tower clock. They have a striking fault they won't trust to anyone else." This was true, which was a mercy; Felix had learned early that a lie built around a true frame stood longer than one built free-standing. "I'm finishing a commission for him. If you'd care to leave word—"

"I'd rather wait, if it's all the same." And he stepped past her into the workshop with the ease of a man who had already decided the invitation existed whether she extended it or not.

* * *

He walked the room the way Felix walked a strange movement—slowly, reading it, letting his eye catch on each irregularity without pausing long enough to alarm it. She watched him look at the bench where she'd been working, at the parts laid out in their order on the baize, at the wall of old case clocks with their faces like a jury of patient relatives. He looked at everything and touched nothing, and somehow the looking felt worse than touching would have been.

"A clean shop," he said. "Orderly. One could learn a great deal from a place like this."

"One learns mostly patience," Felix said, returning to her bench because standing idle beside him felt like standing beside a fire that hadn't decided yet whether to burn you. "And how to lose the same screw twice."

He laughed at that—the right laugh, in the right measure—and something in Felix curled tight and cold. She knew men like this the way a mouse knows the shape of an owl's shadow: not from experience of being caught, but from the inherited certainty of what the shadow means. Her father had been visited by such men in the last month of the family's solvency. They had also smiled correctly. They had also known her father's name before they were introduced, and used it just often enough.

"So. Halloran." Kessler settled himself on the stool by the door, uninvited, unhurried. "You're the apprentice who was sent up to the observatory."

Her hands were holding a pair of pliers. She made them hold the pliers. "I've been out there some days this fortnight. Their master clock stopped. It's a famous piece—anyone who can should see it kept alive."

"Famous," he agreed pleasantly. "I'm told it has not been running since the spring."

"It ran until eleven days ago. Then it stopped."

"Yes. Eleven days ago." He turned his hat over in his gloved hands, once, twice. "Curious timing, clocks stopping. Almost as curious as the things people leave near them when they do."

Felix lifted the arbor, fitted it into a plate, and did it slowly, because slow work could not be rushed and rushing would show. Her heart was going hard against her ribs, and she let that too become part of the labor—*you are a boy at a bench; boys' hearts race under the eyes of inspectors; let it mean nothing.*

"You'll forgive me," Kessler went on, conversational as a man remarking on weather, "but I've been asking after the observatory's affairs. A clerk of the Imperial Treasury, one Anton Voss, was found dead last month near the Danube canal. Unfortunate business. Drowning, they say, though the gentleman could not swim, which the police of this city find unremarkable and I find merely convenient." He paused, giving her space to be shocked, and she gave him none. "Before his death, Herr Voss had occasion to visit the observatory frequently. An interest in astronomy, one supposes. Or an interest in the clock. And now the clock stops, and a young apprentice is summoned, and—" he spread his gloved hands, palms up, an open page inviting her to read it "—one begins to wonder whether Vienna's horologists have taken up detective work."

"I couldn't say, sir. I repair movements. I don't follow the news."

"No," he agreed mildly. "Apprentices rarely do. Tell me—when you were sent for, who gave you the commission?"

"The observatory's director wrote to Herr Brandt. I don't know the man's name beyond that. There was a letter; I saw it briefly."

"And the letter is...?"

"In Herr Brandt's papers. He keeps everything. He still has apprenticeship contracts from the reign of the late Emperor." Let the man go digging through Brandt's hoarded decades if he liked; he would find every scrap of paper in that office except the one that mattered, because the one that mattered was sewn inside Marta's spare petticoat upstairs, wrapped in oilcloth, sleeping six feet above a sleeping child.

Kessler regarded her for a moment. Then he rose, strolled to the great case clock against the wall, and opened its trunk door with two fingers, peering into the movement with the expression of a connoisseur at a gallery.

"A Viennese grande sonnerie," he said. "Strikes the quarters on eight bells, hours on a gong. My grandfather had one. Do you know what I remember about it, Halloran? Not the time it kept—it lost four minutes a week and my grandmother swore at it in Hungarian—but the sound. When it struck at night, the whole house had to acknowledge it. You couldn't pretend you hadn't heard. That is the thing about a striking clock." He closed the case door gently. "It makes things public. A clock that strikes cannot keep a secret."

"I suppose that's why confessions are taken in churches," Felix said. "Bells everywhere."

His eyes came back to hers, and for the first time she saw the civility thin, just at the edge, the way ice thins first at the rim of the hole. "Quite so."

* * *

They spoke for another quarter of an hour, though afterward Felix could not have reconstructed most of it. He asked about Brandt's health, the state of trade, whether the workshop took commissions from government offices, whether many strangers came and went. She answered in the flat currency of a working day—yes, no, some, few—and each answer came out smooth and reasonable and shaped, and she hated it. Hated the ease. Somewhere in her fifth year here she had discovered that she could lie the way other apprentices filed brass: steadily, cleanly, without burrs. It was the legacy, she supposed, of having lied her way out of her own name. Every day of her life she answered to Felix when she was Beatrix; every greeting, every ledger line, every "the boy will fetch it" was a small forgery committed in public, and after five years the forging hand does not tremble.

But there is a difference, she thought, watching Kessler settle his hat, between lying to protect your own skin and lying while a child sleeps overhead whose father floated in the canal. The first is survival. The second makes survival feel like complicity.

"One last matter," Kessler said, at the door, and she understood that everything before had been prelude, the tuning before the piece. "Herr Voss's effects. His widow is destitute—there is a child, I understand—and certain personal items of his have gone astray. Papers, mostly. Nothing of value to anyone but the family." He smiled his correct smile. "If such items passed through a workshop like this—people hide things in the strangest places, you know; we once found a confession sewn into a saddle—I would take it as a kindness, and as no trouble at all, if word reached me. Discreetly. Before others less gentle come asking the same questions."

"There are others asking?" Felix let her eyes widen just enough. Curiosity was safer than fear; fear confirmed that there was something to fear.

"There are always others asking," Kessler said. "That is my whole profession. The only variable is how gently they ask." He put on his hat. "Good morning, Halloran. Give Herr Brandt my regards, and tell him—" the pause was small, deliberate, a jeweler's pause "—tell him we met."

He left. The door shut with the softness of expensive manners, and Felix stood with the pliers in her hand and listened to his footsteps diminish down the lane, and only when they were gone entirely did she discover that her knees had opinions about the whole conversation.

* * *

Ivo was beside her before she'd set the tool down. He tapped the bench twice—*what happened?*—and she gave him the shape of it in the workshop's shorthand they'd built over years: she pointed at the door, drew a circle in the air (a man, a stranger), mimed turning pages (papers), then pressed her palm flat over her heart and looked upward, toward the loft.

Ivo's jaw tightened. He took her chalk from the tray and wrote on the slate they kept for customers' estimates: **HE WILL COME BACK.**

"I know," Felix said aloud, because saying it made it real and real things could be planned against. "He'll come back, and next time he won't ask nicely, and he'll come when Brandt isn't at a church tower to be conveniently elsewhere." She rubbed her thumb across the card he'd given her, feeling the blind stamp in its corner—a seal she didn't recognize, some office or patron, pressed deep enough to read with a fingertip. "Ivo, he asked about Voss's effects. Papers. Which means someone knows papers exist. Which means whoever killed Voss didn't find what he was looking for, and hasn't yet, and that is the only reason we are all still breathing."

Ivo took back the chalk. Wrote, erased, wrote again: **THE GIRL HEARD NOTHING?**

"She sleeps like—no. No, she didn't hear. But she's six years old, Ivo, and she looks at me sometimes with those eyes her father gave her, and I think—" Felix stopped. Steadied herself against the bench. "I think he left her at that door on purpose. Not thrown away. *Placed.* At the one door in Vienna where a broken clock gets taken in."

Ivo studied her a long moment. Then he wrote, slowly, in his engraver's even hand:

**THEN SHE IS NOT WHAT THEY LOST. SHE IS THE MESSAGE. AND SOMEONE MEANT HER FOR YOU.**

Above them, faint through the floorboards, came a small sound—a footstep, a settling, the creak of the old ladder rung. Then silence, obedient and terrible.

Felix looked up at the ceiling, at the place she knew the shawl-covered head would be if the boards were glass, and felt the full weight of what Kessler's visit had changed. Until this morning, hiding Lena had been an act of mercy. Now it was evidence. Every day the girl stayed was a day the lie grew larger, and larger lies require better craftsmen, and somewhere in this city a man with a correct smile and a blind-stamped card was already measuring them for the job.

She picked up her pliers. She bent to the arbor, to the small honest work of making a thing turn true.

And beneath the floorboards, in the pocket of the coat she'd hung by the door, Kessler's card lay face-down against the wood, and Felix could not stop seeing, printed in her memory across its blank reverse side, the words he had not quite said:

*We once found a confession sewn into a saddle.*

Which meant, she realized with a cold that started in her fingertips and moved inward, that the inspector had not been warning her away from the papers at all. He had been telling her—courteously, deniably, in the language of a man too careful to speak plainly—exactly where to look for them.

The question that remained, and would keep her awake long after midnight, was this: did Inspector Kessler want the papers found?

Or did he simply want to know who else had them?
