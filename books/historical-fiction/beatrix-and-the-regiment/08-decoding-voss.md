## 8. Decoding Voss

Ivo Stein counted the world in lines.

Words had never come to him easily—not because his mind was slow, but because the world had arrived at his ears as nothing at all, a door closed since birth, and so he had learned early that everything worth knowing announced itself in shape and shadow and the angle of one thing against another. A face was a sentence. A hand's tremor was a paragraph. The grain of boxwood under a graver told him more honestly what it thought than any man's mouth ever had.

So when Felix laid the coded papers out on the workbench on a Tuesday evening, after the journeymen had gone home and Marta had drawn the shutters, Ivo did not see an impossible puzzle. He saw a pattern waiting to admit him.

The papers were seven in number, folded small enough to hide inside the escapement housing of Vienna's observatory clock, which meant they had been folded by someone who understood exactly how small a truth must become to survive. They were written in columns of figures—dates, sums, estate names rendered as numbers—and threaded through the columns ran a second script, tiny characters that looked at first like shorthand but were not. Ivo had seen shorthand. Shorthand flowed. These characters sat upright, each one constructed, each one deliberate.

He picked up his loupe, screwed it into the socket of his eye, and bent over the first page while the others watched him: Felix with her arms crossed so tightly she might have been holding herself together, Marta with her sewing abandoned in her lap, Herr Brandt at the far end of the bench pretending to examine a fusee that needed no examination, his face the color of tallow.

Ivo tuned them out. He tuned everyone out; it was his single advantage.

The characters were thirty in number. He was sure of it by the third pass—twenty-nine, then a thirtieth appearing only once, near the bottom of page two, shy as a mouse. Thirty characters. Not letters, then, not directly. Something mapped onto something else.

He reached for a scrap of copper plate and began to write them out with a scribe's pencil, reproducing each character exactly—the little hook here, the doubled bar there, the one that looked like a window with its shutter half-closed. Reproduction was the discipline he trusted most. An engraver who could not copy faithfully had no right to invent at all. And somewhere in the copying, as his hand learned each character's construction the way his hands learned letterforms for trade signs and banknotes plates, the logic underneath began to surface.

They were built on a grid. Every character fit inside an invisible square, and the strokes within the square were positional—a stroke upper left meant one thing, a crossbar high or low meant another. It was not writing at all. It was *engraving*. Someone had invented a script the way Ivo would have invented it, thinking in coordinates, in placements, in the geometry of a plate.

A clerk's script. A man who spent his days ruling ledgers and ruling columns and hiding small truths inside respectable grids.

Ivo looked up.

*Numbers,* he wrote on his slate, and beneath it: *It is numbers wearing clothes.*

Felix came around the bench and stood at his shoulder. She smelled of lamp oil and brass filings and, faintly, of the lavender water Marta made her use so that no one at the observatory would notice anything about the young man Halloran at all. She read his slate, and he felt rather than saw the change in her breathing.

*You can read it?*

*Not yet,* he wrote. *But I know its bones. Give me the night.*

---

He gave the workshop his night and most of the next day, working in the corners of hours, when a customer had gone and the apprentices were out on deliveries. The key, once he found it, was almost insulting in its elegance: the first column of ordinary figures on each page—the dates—was not data but instruction. Each date, reduced by a fixed offset, named a grid position. The dates told you where to begin reading the dressed-up characters, and in what order, and the offset itself was derivable from the number of characters on the page. Self-locating. A cipher that carried its own map, so that even if the pages were separated, each fragment could find its way home.

*He was clever,* Ivo wrote, and underlined it twice.

*Clever men are rarely murdered by accident,* Brandt said from the end of the bench, and then went back to his fusee, and the room was quiet except for the ticking of forty clocks, all of them disagreeing slightly about the hour, the way clocks do.

Decoding was slow work even with the key in hand. The characters resolved into numerals, the numerals into pairs, the pairs into names and sums through a substitution table that repeated often enough to learn by ear—or by eye, in Ivo's case, the way a musician learns intervals. By Wednesday midnight, alone in the loft above the workshop with a shaded candle and Lena asleep in Marta's old traveling basket behind the partition, Ivo had the first full page open before him like a dissected specimen.

He read it three times, because the first two times he assumed he had made an error.

*Esterházygasse parcel — seized 1843, assessed 4,100 fl. — sold at auction to N.N. — 4,100 fl. — resold same week, 11,600 fl. — difference to A. via W.* 

And below, in the clerk's plain hand where the cipher briefly fell away as though the writer could not bear to disguise this part: *I have checked the court records twice. The auctions are theater. We buy our own confiscations with their own money and call it the Empire's profit.*

*A.* Ivo set down his pencil. His hands were very steady—they were always steady; that was the first thing any master had ever said about them—but something behind his ribs was not steady at all.

He knew the name the initial stood for. Everyone in Vienna knew it, the way everyone knows the name of the man who owns the weather. Director Albrecht of the Imperial Treasury. A name spoken in workshops with the particular flatness reserved for powerful men—one did not curse such names aloud, one simply let them lie in the sentence like a stone in a shoe.

He worked until four in the morning. Page two confirmed page one with different estates, different years, the same resale arithmetic, the same quiet desperate marginalia. Page three introduced a second abbreviation, *via K.*, attached to payments that did not launder estates but *silence*—fees, the ledger called them, fees to a man whose business was inquiries. Inspector Kessler, who had sat in this very workshop three days ago with clean gloves and cleaner questions, asking about a clerk's effects.

Page four was the worst. Page four was not arithmetic at all. Page four was a list of names—witnesses, the marginal note said, *men who signed affidavits in the Steyr matter*—and beside each name a date, and beside each date, in the smallest possible cipher characters, a word that recurred with terrible regularity.

Ivo decoded the recurring word six times before he let himself understand it.

*Settled.*

Six names. Six dates. Six times the word *settled*, in the tidy hand of a man who ruled beautiful columns and hid truth in grids because it was the only craft he had, and who had then hidden the originals inside the largest, most public, most permanent machine he could think of—a clock in an observatory, struck by the heavens themselves, wound and maintained and never, ever discarded.

*If anything happens to me,* the final margin read, decoded now in pencil on Ivo's slate, *the mechanism will speak for me. It cannot be intimidated. It keeps better time than the Treasury keeps faith.*

Ivo blew out the candle and sat in the dark listening to the clocks tick, all those small brave hearts insisting on the hour regardless of what the hour contained.

* * *

In the morning he called a council of four.

It could not be called anything grander; the workshop was too small and the danger too large for grandeur. Marta locked the street door and left the sign reading *Back at One*. Brandt drew the shades himself, which he had never done in five years of Ivo's tenancy—the shades stayed open in a workshop, light was wages—and the gesture told Ivo more about the man's fear than his face did, because Brandt's face had been trained in the old school, the imperial school, the school that said a craftsman's expression was also a tool and was kept sharp and neutral accordingly.

Felix sat across the bench from Ivo with the child's absence like a presence in the room—Lena was upstairs with the baker's wife, an arrangement costing Marta in pastries and gratitude—and her eyes moved from the slate to Ivo's hands and back, waiting.

Ivo had prepared. Preparation was how a deaf man survived: he anticipated every exchange, rehearsed his answers, laid out his sentences like tools on leather. But this morning he found the preparation useless, because the thing he had to say was not a transaction. It was a confession of knowledge, and knowledge, once displayed, could not be re-holstered.

*The pages are ledgers,* he wrote, having cleaned the slate twice in the night to make room for this. *Seized estates. Auctioned to ghosts and resold at profit. The profit moves through a W.—a banker, I think—to A.*

Brandt closed his eyes.

"Say the whole name," Felix said, though she said it quietly, as if the room might have informers between the shade and the door. "Say it."

Ivo took the slate back, wiped it, and wrote in his careful engraver's letters, each one placed on its invisible grid:

*Director Albrecht.*

The clocks ticked. Somewhere in the case behind him a striking train gathered itself and released, and the small bell rang eleven, though it was barely half past ten, because that clock had always run fast and no one had ever been able to reason with it.

"And Kessler?" Felix asked. "Where does Kessler stand in it?"

Ivo turned to the third page's transcription, which he had copied out fair during the night, both because it was evidence and because copying calmed him, the way prayer is said to calm other men. He laid it flat and turned it to face her.

*Fees to K. for inquiries and settlements.*

"Settlements," Felix repeated. Her voice had gone thin. "Settled how?"

Ivo hesitated. Then he showed her the fourth page.

He watched her read it—he was good at watching people read; faces narrate—and he watched the moment she understood the recurring word, because her lips formed it silently and then stopped forming anything at all. Her hand went to the edge of the bench the way a hand goes to a banister on a ship.

"Six men," she said.

*Seven, if you count Voss,* Ivo wrote. *The seventh settlement is still wet.*

Marta made a sound low in her throat and put her needlework aside, and Ivo saw her look at the ceiling, toward the loft, toward the basket, and Ivo understood in that moment something he should have understood days ago—that the child asleep above them was not an orphan to be pitied but a loose thread in a fabric that had already been cut six times. The note had said *forget this face*. Men who wrote such notes were men who checked later whether the face had been forgotten.

Brandt had not spoken. He stood at the end of the bench with one hand flat on the wood, and Ivo, who read hands the way other men read sermons, saw the knuckles whiten slowly, joint by joint, like a lock being thrown.

"My brother touched a treasury file," Brandt said at last, to no one and to everyone. "One file. A shipping manifest, mislabeled, that showed double duties collected on the Danube line. He intended to return it. He told me he intended to return it." The hand lifted from the bench. "That was in the spring of 1838. There was a verdict of accidental drowning in the Danube canal in the autumn. I was asked no questions, because I had none to ask, and I have kept my shop clean and my mouth shut for nine years on the strength of that verdict." He looked at the papers on the bench the way a man looks at a snake he has only just realized is in the room. "Now there are seven of them."

"The difference," Felix said, and her voice had come back to her with an edge on it that Ivo had heard only once before, the night she had climbed into the loft with a sleeping child inside her coat, "is that Voss did not drown quietly. He built a machine that testifies. He put the proof where it would be found by the one trade in Vienna that opens everything, reads everything, and forgets nothing." She looked at Ivo when she said it, and Ivo felt the words land on him like a commission.

*I can finish the decode,* he wrote. *Two nights. Three at most. The last page uses a variation—I need time with it.*

"Then take the time," Felix said. "Take whatever—"

"No."

Brandt said it flatly, without raising his voice, and the word dropped into the room like a graver slipping.

"Burn them," he said. "Tonight. All seven pages, and the slate, and your transcriptions, and the pencil shavings swept and carried out separately. I have buried one brother on the strength of paper exactly like this paper. I will not bury an apprentice. I will not bury—" and here his voice did something Ivo had never heard it do, caught on a splinter halfway down his throat, "—I will not bury anyone else. Deliver the clock repaired and empty. That is the whole of your commission, girl, and it was ever thus: we fix what is broken, we keep what is private, and we survive."

The silence afterward had weight and texture. Ivo sat inside it, deaf to its sound but fluent in its shape, and he watched Felix's face do the thing it did when she was deciding—nothing at all, a perfect stillness, a coin held balanced on edge.

It was Marta who broke it, rising, gathering her sewing, her voice pitched low and fierce. But Ivo did not watch her; he watched the window shade, because just then, through the gap where the shade met the frame, he saw something in the street that made the back of his neck go cold.

A man in a fine gray coat, standing across the lane with the unhurried patience of a portrait subject, angled precisely toward the workshop's darkened front. Not walking. Standing. Looking at the closed sign, at the drawn shades, at the shop that had never once in nine years drawn its shades before noon.

As Ivo watched, the man raised one gloved hand and consulted, of all things, a pocket watch—and then, deliberately, lifted his eyes back to the workshop and held them there a long moment, as though timing something.

Then he turned and walked away toward the Graben, unhurried, his coat cutting a clean gray line through the morning crowd.

Ivo tapped the bench twice, the way he signaled the workshop when words failed, and when they all turned to him he pointed at the shade, at the gap, at the empty street beyond, and wrote four words on his slate in letters pressed deep enough to score the surface:

*We are being timed.*
