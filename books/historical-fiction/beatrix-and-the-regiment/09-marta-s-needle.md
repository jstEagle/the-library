## 9. Marta's Needle

The needle knew before Marta did.

She had been working by the loft's small window since the grey of morning, hemming a christening gown for Frau Lindqvist's granddaughter, and the child—Lena, they had taken to calling her nothing at all, because naming her felt like a promise none of them were ready to keep—sat on the floor below with Ivo's discarded brass shavings, arranging them into patterns no one had taught her. Spirals. Circles within circles. A child's game, Marta told herself, and went on stitching, and then the needle stopped in her fingers the way a clock stops, mid-beat, everything inside her going suddenly and terribly quiet.

Because she had seen that pattern before.

Not in brass. In silk. In white silk shot through with silver thread, on the bodice of a mourning gown ordered in haste three winters past by a woman whose husband had died so suddenly that his collar had still been at the laundress when the news came to the house on Wollzeile.

Marta set down the gown. She set it down very carefully, the way one sets down a thing that might go off.

* * *

She had dressed perhaps four hundred women for their griefs in thirty years at the needle, and she could have told you, if you'd had the patience and the coin, that grief dressed differently according to what killed you. Consumption wanted soft greys and loose sleeves, something a woman could breathe in through the long dying. A fall from a horse wanted black, severe and immediate, because there was nothing to be done slowly. But a suicide—and Anton Voss's death had been ruled a suicide within a week, though Marta had never once believed it, having dressed enough widows of suicides to know the particular flinch they carried, the shame that sat on them like ill-fitting lace—well. A suicide's widow wanted black so heavy it swallowed her whole, and wanted it yesterday.

Frau Voss had come to the shop on a Tuesday with her sister and had not wept once in the fitting. That was the first thing Marta remembered now, sitting very still while the child below her arranged brass into spirals. The not-weeping. Some widows wept so much the pins wouldn't hold; the shoulders heaved and shifted and you pinned and re-pinned until your fingers ached. Frau Voss had stood on the stool like a statue being fitted for a cathedral niche, arms out, eyes fixed on the middle distance, and had said only: *Make it plain. He hated ornament.*

He hated ornament. A strange thing for a widow to say of a dead man, and stranger still when you considered that Anton Voss had been, by all accounts, a treasury clerk—a man of ledgers and seals, a man whose entire life was ornament of a kind, flourishes and wax and ribboned documents. But Marta had not been paid to consider. She had been paid to make it plain, and plain it was: bombazine, high collar, no jet, no crepe trim, nothing to catch light or eye. A gown designed to be forgotten in.

And in the left breast of that gown, where a woman might carry a locket, Frau Voss had asked for an inner pocket. Deep. Buttoned. Marta had sewn it without comment because a seamstress who comments on pockets does not stay a seamstress long, and because Vienna was full of women carrying things in hidden places—letters, keys, coins, other women's sins—and it was not Marta's business what weight a woman chose to bear against her heart.

But she remembered the pocket. And she remembered, God help her, she remembered the second fitting, when Frau Voss had brought her daughter.

* * *

The girl had been three then, perhaps four. Small enough to sit on the fitting stool and swing her legs while her mother stood rigid in bombazine. Marta had given her a spool of blue thread to play with—the same trick she used on all the children, because children in a fitting room were either screaming or silent, and silence frightened her more—and the little girl had wound the blue thread around her own fingers until the tips went white, and had looked up at Marta with eyes too old for her face, and had said:

*Mama doesn't cry but Papa taught me a song for when she does.*

And Frau Voss had said, sharp as a snapped thread: *Elisabeth.* Just the name. Just the name in that voice, and the child had gone quiet and unwound the thread finger by finger, and Marta had bent over her pin cushion and thought: there is a whole tragedy in this room and my part in it is the hem.

Elisabeth. The child on the observatory steps had no name that anyone would give her. Felix called her *the little one* in the daytime and *kleine Maus* when she thought no one heard; Brandt called her nothing at all and pretended not to see her, which from Brandt was its own kind of sheltering; Ivo could not call her anything aloud and so had carved her a wooden bird with a bell inside its belly, which the girl carried everywhere and slept beside like a talisman. But Marta, looking down now through the floorboards' gap at the spiral of brass shavings, at the small serious hands arranging them, at the face bent over the work—Marta thought: *Elisabeth*, and the word fit the child like a sleeve cut to measure.

Three years older. Thinner. Darker under the eyes, the way any child goes dark under the eyes after a winter of hiding. But the same deep-set gravity between the brows. The same habit—Marta saw it now, saw it as clearly as she had ever seen anything through thirty years of reading faces across a fitting stool—the same habit of winding whatever she held around her fingers until the tips went white.

* * *

Marta did not go down immediately. Thirty years at the needle teaches you that haste wrinkles everything, and some seams, once you start them, cannot be picked out clean. Instead she did what she always did when a garment troubled her: she took it apart in her mind, piece by piece, on the flat of her memory, and examined each seam by lamplight.

The note. The note that had come folded around the child's blanket on the observatory steps, the one Felix kept folded in her waistcoat pocket like a coal she couldn't drop and couldn't hold. Marta had seen it twice—once the dawn it arrived, when Felix's hands shook too badly to refold it, and once last week, when Felix had spread it on the workbench among the gear blanks and stared at it as if staring might change its language.

*Forget this face.*

Five words. Marta was no handwriting expert, but she had spent three decades reading hands—not penmanship, but the hands themselves, the customers' hands, what rings they wore and how they worried their wedding bands, whether they handled fabric like owners or like thieves. And she had also, in those decades, addressed several hundred invoices, receipts, and letters to the great houses of Vienna, and received replies, and filed them, and one file in particular she had kept separate from the others, in the drawer beneath her pincushion drawer, because the correspondence had pleased her: a commission from the office of the Imperial Treasury itself, two years ago, for table linens embroidered with the imperial cipher, ordered personally by the Director of Seized Estates, Herr—

Herr Albrecht.

Marta rose from her chair. Her knees cracked the way they always cracked now, and she ignored them, and she went to the drawer beneath the pincushion drawer, and she lifted out the folder of cream-laid paper tied with the ribbon she had saved because the commission had been the largest single order of her career, and she untied it with fingers that were not quite steady, and she laid the top letter on the table beside the christening gown.

*Forget this face.*

She read it the way she read a pattern: overall first, then detail. The *F* with its long crossbar. The peculiar forward lean of the whole line, like a man hurrying. The ink pressed hard at the beginnings of strokes and dragged thin at the ends, the hand of someone who wrote quickly because he was accustomed to being obeyed before he finished writing.

Then she looked at the letter from two years ago. *Regarding the delivery of the ciphered linens to the Hofburg apartments...*

The same lean. The same long-crossbarred *F* in *Regarding*. The same hard pressure at the stroke's beginning, dragging thin. She laid one paper over the other, held both to the window's grey light, and the words of the two documents lay atop each other like double-printed type, ghosting through.

It was not proof. Marta understood that. Any advocate worth his fee would say that five common words in two hurried hands prove nothing, that half the directors in Vienna leaned forward when they wrote. But proof was for courtrooms, and certainty was for needlewomen who had spent their lives knowing a garment's origin by its seams. This note and this letter came from the same wardrobe. From the same hand. And that hand belonged to the man who ran the seized estates—the man whose ledgers, if Ivo's sleepless nights with the cipher were telling the truth, recorded the quiet swallowing of every property the Treasury took, the estates of the disgraced and the dead rendered into the private purse of the man who administered them.

Anton Voss had known. Voss had been the clerk who touched the papers; Voss had copied what he copied and hidden it where he hid it—in a clock, of all the mad places, in a clock, because Voss had known something about clocks that the rest of Vienna did not yet know, something that had made him carry his daughter through the November dark to the foot of the observatory and leave her on the steps like an offering—

No. Not like an offering. Like a message. Marta's breath caught on the thought and held it.

Only a clockmaker would look there.

That was the meaning of the steps. Not abandonment—delivery. Voss had known his clock, or his friend's clock, or the master clock he tended in whatever capacity a treasury clerk tends a clock, was due for repair. He had known that when it broke, a clockmaker would come. He had put his daughter where the right hands would find her, and the note in the wrong man's handwriting—Albrecht's own hand, or a copyist's trained to it—was meant to frighten away the curious, to make whoever found the child think herself marked, watched, disposable. *Forget this face.* A warning dressed as an instruction.

Except it hadn't worked. Except Felix Halloran, that careful, frightened, stubborn boy-that-was-not-a-boy, had looked at a six-year-old on cold stone and forgotten to be afraid. Or rather, had remembered something bigger than fear.

* * *

Below her, the child had finished her spiral and was now lining the shavings up in rows, precise as teeth in a wheel. Marta watched her a moment longer, and then she went down the stairs, because thirty years at the needle had also taught her this: a fitting begins when you pick up the pins, not when you decide to.

The girl looked up. Those grave dark eyes, the ones that never spoke, that had not spoken a single word since the steps, and Marta knelt down on the floorboards despite her knees, down into the brass smell and the lamplight, and said, quietly, in the voice she used for frightened brides:

"You had a song, didn't you. Your papa taught you a song."

The child went still. Not frozen—still, the way a mechanism goes still, all its parts present and waiting. Her fingers found the wooden bird and wound nothing around them, only held.

"Marta knows," she said. "Marta remembers the blue thread. You needn't say a word. But I want you to hear it from me first, so it comes from a friend: we know who you are. We know whose you are. And nobody—" her voice caught on the old iron of it, the fury she had carried since the fitting room three winters past, since the widow who did not weep and the inner pocket buttoned against the heart—"*nobody* is going to forget this face. Do you hear me? That note lied. Faces are the one thing Vienna cannot make me forget."

The child looked at her for a long moment. Then she did the last thing Marta expected: she reached out and touched the christening gown draped over Marta's arm, the white silk, and traced the hem with one finger, the way she had traced the blue thread once, and she looked up with an expression Marta had seen only once before in her life—on the face of a standing woman in bombazine who could not weep and so had asked for a pocket instead.

Marta gathered the child onto her lap, knees cracking, heart hammering, and rocked her the way she had rocked no one in thirty years.

* * *

She found Felix at the bench that evening, squinting through the loupe at a pallet spring, and she laid the two papers side by side on the workbench without preamble, without asking leave, the way she did everything.

"Read them together," she said. "Then send for Ivo. And then, child, we are going to talk about what a widow carries in a buttoned pocket against her heart—because I sewed that pocket, and I have wondered for three years what filled it, and tonight I am wondering whether it wasn't papers. Whether our Anton Voss didn't give his wife the same testament he gave the clock." She tapped Albrecht's letter with one blunt finger. "And whether she is still alive to tell us where."

Felix pulled off the loupe slowly. In the lamplight her face did what it always did—went first to fear, then to arithmetic—but underneath both, for the first time since the steps, Marta saw something else take root. Hope, maybe. Or its twin, purpose.

"Frau Voss," Felix said quietly, testing the name. "If she lives—if she's in Vienna—Kessler will have thought of her."

"Then we had best think of her faster," said Marta, and reached for her thimble.
