## 10. The Clock Stops

Kessler came back on the fourth day of October, when the fog off the Danube had thickened enough to swallow the observatory's dome whole, and Felix knew before he opened his mouth that he had not come about the clock.

He arrived without his earlier escort, alone but for a driver who remained with the carriage at the base of the hill, and he stood in the doorway of the instrument room for a long moment before speaking, letting his gaze travel over her workbench, her tools laid out in their precise rows, the great clock's open case with its pendulum hanging stilled. He was dressed as before in good grey wool, unremarkable and expensive, the clothing of a man who wished to be forgotten five seconds after leaving a room. His gloves were kid leather. He did not remove them.

"Herr Halloran." He said the name as though testing its weight on a scale. "You've made progress."

"Some." Felix kept her hands moving as she spoke—there was comfort in that, in having something to do besides meet his eyes. She was fitting a new pallet to the anchor escapement, filing it down by increments, and she let the rasp of steel against steel fill the silence he left. "The going train is sound again. The striking train will take longer."

"Yes. The striking train." He crossed the room without hurry and stood before the clock's case, close enough that his breath might have fogged the brass if he'd exhaled fully. "I confess I'm here with disappointing news. The Treasury has reviewed the repair estimate. The cost is judged excessive. The damage is judged—" he turned slightly, regarding her over his shoulder, and his expression was mild, almost apologetic, "—irreversible."

Felix set down her file. Slowly, so that it would look like deliberation rather than dread.

"Whoever judged it," she said, "doesn't understand what they're looking at."

"Enlighten me."

"The frame is cracked, yes. Two wheels are beyond salvage. But the escapement is intact, the barrel is sound, and the movement itself is a Reiner work—there are perhaps four men in Vienna who could restore it, and I am one of them. To declare it dead would be to lie, and I don't lie about mechanisms." She heard her own voice harden and did not soften it. "The clock can run again. It wants only time."

"Time," Kessler repeated. He said the word the way another man might say *money*, or *blood*. "Herr Halloran, I have been instructed to obtain a certificate of condemnation. A document stating that the master clock cannot be repaired and must be removed. You would be compensated handsomely for your time. There would be no reflection on your skill—the opposite, in fact. A man asked to perform miracles who honestly reports that no miracle is possible is a man whose judgment can be trusted."

*And a man whose report you need,* Felix thought, *because whatever was hidden in this clock, you want it gone with the clock—melted, broken, scattered into scrap where no curious apprentice will ever find it.* She thought of Voss's coded papers folded now inside her shirt, warm against her skin, and she made her face into the face of a craftsman mildly insulted.

"I'll write my assessment when the assessment is true," she said. "Not before. If the Treasury wishes to hire another opinion, they're welcome to. But I'll ask them to wait until I've finished the striking train, because a half-repaired clock condemns easier than a whole one examined honestly."

Something flickered behind Kessler's eyes—not anger, nothing so legible. Calculation, perhaps. Or recognition: the small surprise of a man who had expected an apprentice to fold and found instead a wall.

"Sentiment," he said at last. "Craftsmen are sentimental about their work. It's understandable. But sentiment, Herr Halloran, is a luxury of men who are not watched." He drew out a card, wrote something on its back with a pencil produced from his breast pocket, and set it on her bench beside the vise. "My address. When you reconsider—and I believe you will—send word within the week. After that, the matter leaves my hands, and other hands are less patient than mine."

He left the way he had come, unhurried, his boots quiet on the stone stairs, and Felix stood listening until the carriage wheels had ground away down through the fog. Then she picked up the card, read the address—*Wallnergasse, 9*—and fed it to the lamp flame, watching the paper curl and blacken and go out.

Then she went back to work.

* * *

The days after that belonged to Kessler; the nights belonged to her.

By daylight she performed her stall with the discipline of an actress who has learned to love her role. She worked slowly where she could work fast. She ordered parts from suppliers who were slow to fill orders, and let her letters sit two days before posting them. She measured the cracked frame three separate times with calipers and a straightedge, muttering to herself like a man wrestling with an impossible problem, while the observatory's astronomer—a myopic, gentle soul named Professor Lindt who cared for nothing under the stars or above them but the stars themselves—nodded sympathetically and begged her to save the old machine if any art could do it.

"The Treasury men were here Tuesday," Lindt confided, wringing his hands over his star charts. "Two of them, very civil, asking whether the clock held anything of value. Documents, records, that sort of thing. I told them it holds time, Herr Halloran, and they laughed in a way I didn't care for."

"What did you tell them exactly?"

"That I keep the sky and they may keep the ledgers." He smiled vaguely. "Was that wrong?"

"It was perfect," Felix said, and meant it, and added a lock to her mental list of things to be grateful for.

But the nights—God, the nights. She had moved her lodgings in all but name; she slept three hours in the loft above Brandt's workshop and worked the rest at the observatory by lamplight, the great doors barred behind her, the city below her window burning its thousand small fires and none of them hers to trust. She had dismantled the striking train down to its last pin and rebuilt it twice, because the mechanism was not merely broken—it had been *wounded*, deliberately, by someone who understood clocks well enough to make the wound look like wear. A file mark across the count wheel's teeth, disguised under grime. A locking pin driven half out of true. And in the plate that carried the strike release, drilled clean through the metal where no drill should ever go, a hole that destroyed the lever which governed the compartment's opening.

That hole had told her everything. Sabotage meant the saboteur knew what the clock contained—or feared what it might contain—and wanted it sealed forever inside a dead machine bound for the smelter. Kessler's certificate of condemnation was not bureaucracy. It was a coffin lid.

So she rebuilt the lever from raw brass, cutting it with gravers by lamplight, annealing and re-cutting when her first attempt snapped, working until her eyes burned and her fingertips were silvered with filings that no washing seemed to remove entirely. She had taken Voss's papers from their hiding place in the escapement compartment and copied nothing, trusting nothing to paper she didn't hold, and each night she sewed them back into place before dawn and left the clock looking wounded, plausible, condemned-in-progress.

On the ninth night she fell asleep at the bench with a graver still in her hand and dreamed of the girl in the workshop loft—Lena, though Marta had only begun calling her that last week, and the name had stuck the way names stick to children, softly and all at once. In the dream the girl was winding a small clock and saying, over and over, in her father's drilled cadence: *when the bell counts twelve, remember the twelfth tooth.* Felix woke with the words dissolving even as she reached for them, and sat in the cold dark of the observatory, heart hammering, knowing only that the dream had come from somewhere real.

She lit the lamp and went back to the count wheel.

* * *

Brandt met her at the door of the workshop on the tenth evening, and she saw at once that something had happened. He was a mountain of a man grown stooped, with forearms like dock rope and a face weathered past guessing his age, and in five years she had learned to read him the way she read dials: by small deflections. Tonight every needle pointed to storm.

"Inside," he said.

Marta was in the kitchen with Lena asleep against her shoulder, the child's thumb in her mouth, her other hand curled in the seamstress's collar the way she'd taken to doing in sleep—as if afraid the warmth would leave without warning. Ivo stood by the window with the shutters closed, which he never did unless told to. He was deaf, but he felt the street through the floorboards better than anyone in Vienna, and his face was grim.

"Kessler's men came by the shop today," Brandt said quietly, once Felix had hung up her coat. "Not him himself. Two clerks with a list. They asked how many apprentices I keep, their ages, where they lodge. They asked how long 'the boy' has been with me." His eyes rested on her, steady and terrible. "They wrote down that you live here, Felix."

"Then we knew they would."

"Knowing and hearing are different animals." He lowered himself onto the bench by the stove, and for a moment he looked older than she had ever seen him, older than the night he'd found a starving fifteen-year-old on his step with her brother's papers and a lie in her mouth, and had looked at her once, long, and said *you'll want the second shift tomorrow, boy, we start early.* "When I was young there was a man in Prague who kept lists too. My brother Tomas touched treasury business there—just touched it, one ledger, one favor—and the lists found him. They found him in the river with his pockets full of stones and his papers gone, and the magistrates wrote *accident* because writing otherwise was bad for their health." He rubbed his face with both hands. "I burned those men's names out of my memory, girl. I swore no one under my roof would ever be on one."

"I'm sorry, Herr Brandt."

"Don't be sorry. Be quick." He looked up, and beneath the fear there was iron, the same iron that had kept her secret five years without being asked. "How long until the clock runs?"

"Six nights. Seven if the weather turns and the brass won't hold its temperature."

"Then six nights. Finish it, hide your papers where they'll never look, and get yourself and that child somewhere that isn't my shop before Kessler's list grows a longer tail." He paused, and his voice roughened. "And Felix—when it's done, whatever happens—know that keeping you was the best work I ever did. Better than any clock."

Marta was crying silently, Lena still asleep against her, and Ivo crossed the room and put his hand flat on Felix's shoulder, squeezing once, then twice—a rhythm, she realized, matching the beat of an escapement. *Tick. Tock. Keep going.*

She stayed only long enough to eat, to touch Lena's hair and feel the child stir and settle, and then she went back up the dark hill to the observatory through fog that lay over the city like a shroud someone had forgotten to lift.

* * *

The fifth night nearly broke her.

The replacement lever refused to seat. Its geometry was right—she checked it four times against the surviving original's shadow-mark on the plate—and yet it jammed at the third position, binding as though the brass itself had decided against her. She filed, tested, filed again, and at two in the morning, freezing, half-blind, her hands shaking with exhaustion and something worse than exhaustion, she drove the lever too hard and snapped it at the neck.

The sound it made was very small. Her scream was smaller. She pressed her fist against her mouth and sat down on the cold floor among the wheel trays and the lamp smoke and shook.

It was the waiting that did it, she thought. Not the work—the work she could bear, the work was holy, the work was the one language in which she had never lied. It was the arithmetic underneath the work: six nights minus one. Five minus the night already bleeding away. Kessler's deadline ticking somewhere in Wallnergasse while she sat amid her failures. The image of the clock hauled out of the tower on a dray, carted to a foundry, the plates glowing orange, the papers inside them curling into ash before any magistrate in Vienna could read a single line. And Lena—Lena with her father's cadence sleeping in her bones, Lena whom Marta had named and fed and loved in eleven days, growing up in a city ruled by the men who had emptied her father's pockets with stones.

Felix got up. She swept the broken lever into the scrap tray, and then she knelt and took the tray itself and poured it out onto the bench, sorting through a winter's accumulation of offcuts with fingers that would not stop trembling, hunting for a blank of the right thickness and temper.

She found one near the bottom, cut long ago from stock Brandt had given her, stamped faintly on one edge with the workshop's mark: a small ox, absurdly patient, standing inside a gear. She almost laughed. She set it in the vise and began again.

Dawn found the lever seated. Not perfect—she could hear a whisper of friction in the third position that a better craftsman would have chased out—but seated, turning, alive. She stood in the grey light with her forehead resting against the clock's case, her hand flat on the wood, and she felt the mechanism breathing under her palm the way one feels a sleeper breathe, and she whispered to it, because she was past caring how mad it sounded:

"Hold together. Six more nights. Then you'll speak for a man who can't."

* * *

On the sixth day, Kessler returned without warning, and this time he did not come alone.

He brought two men in Treasury livery and a clerk carrying a leather folio, and he climbed the tower stairs at a pace that suggested the courtesy of his earlier visits had been spent down to its last coin. Felix heard them on the stairs and had just enough time to slide the compartment's cover plate home and sweep a cloth over the exposed works before they entered.

"Herr Halloran." Kessler's gloves were new, or freshly cleaned. "The week is ended. I've come for your assessment—one way or the other. These gentlemen will witness it."

Professor Lindt hovered at the door, wringing his hands, and Felix gave him a small shake of the head: *stay there.* Then she turned and faced the three men across the width of the instrument room, with the great clock standing behind her like a witness of its own.

"My assessment isn't finished."

"It is now." Kessler nodded to the clerk, who opened the folio and uncapped a pen. "You will certify the clock unrepairable. You will state that the movement cannot be safely restored and recommend removal and disposal. You will sign, and you will be paid, and you will walk away from this hill a wealthier man."

"And if I don't?"

Kessler smiled. It transformed his face the way frost transforms a window—same glass, same shape, nothing living behind it. "Then the assessment will be obtained elsewhere. And the elsewhere will note, I regret to say, that the young apprentice Halloran obstructed an imperial commission, mishandled Treasury property, and was seen removing documents from the premises." He glanced at the clerk. "We have a statement to that effect already drafted. All it lacks is a signature. Yours, ideally—but a statement is such a flexible instrument."

The fog pressed against the windows. Somewhere below, a church bell rang the quarter hour, and the sound came up the hill thin and delayed, as if the city itself were hesitating.

Felix thought of Brandt's voice: *be quick.* Of Ivo's hand beating tick-tock against her shoulder. Of Marta sewing by candlelight, and of Lena asleep with her fist in a seamstress's collar, holding on.

Five nights, maybe four, before the striking train was finished and the compartment mechanism timed and true. Four nights between her and a forged statement, an arrest, the river with its pockets full of stones.

She looked at Kessler, and she made her voice bored, and she thanked God and five years of practice that lying had never once required effort from her tongue.

"Gentlemen," she said, "you may witness whatever you like. But no honest clockmaker certifies a movement he hasn't heard strike. Give me until the equinox demonstration—the professor's own public trial of the clock, before the Academy, before witnesses you can't buy. If the clock doesn't run, I'll sign your paper myself and thank you for the lesson. If it does run—" she stepped aside, gesturing at the great silent case, at the pendulum hanging like a held breath, "—then your certificate dies, and Director Albrecht will have to explain to the Academy why the Treasury tried to bury a Reiner masterpiece."

Silence. The clerk's pen hung dripping over the page.

Kessler studied her for a long moment, and Felix watched him do the arithmetic she wanted him to do: the equinox was public, the Academy was public, Albrecht's name spoken aloud in this drafty room was a risk he hadn't expected her to dare. Something moved behind his eyes—that same flicker as before, calculation shading into something almost like respect, or almost like fear.

"The equinox," he said finally. "Nine days. If the clock fails to strike, Herr Halloran, you sign. And if you attempt to leave this city before then—" he tucked the folio under his arm, "—we will find you. We found Voss's daughter, after all. It took us eleven days, but we found her."

The words went through Felix like a bolt through a wheel's arbor. Her face did not change. Later, in the ringing privacy of her own skull, she would permit herself to be terrified. Now she simply inclined her head, craftsman to inspector, apprentice to empire.

"Nine days," she agreed. "The clock will be ready."

They descended the stairs. The fog took them. And Felix Halloran stood alone in the instrument room with her heart slamming against her ribs, counting the hours aloud in a whisper, because saying them made them solid, made them a mechanism, made them something she could build against:

Nine days. One lever seated, four to fit, the compartment train to time to the second, the plates to hide deeper than Kessler's imagination—and somewhere across the frozen city, in a loft above a workshop on a lane that smelled of solder and lamp oil, a six-year-old girl who was supposed to have been found eleven days ago was sleeping safe because strangers had refused to forget her face.

Nine days.

She lit the lamp, rolled up her sleeves, and went back to work.
