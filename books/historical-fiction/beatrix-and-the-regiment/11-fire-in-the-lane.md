## 11. Fire in the Lane

Elias Brandt had not smelled smoke since Vienna burned in his twenty-third year, and yet the smell woke him before the bells did—that particular sourness of pitch and old wood, threading under the door of his room above the workshop like a debt collector who knew he was home.

He was on the stairs before his boots were fully on. In the glass of the stairwell window, an orange light flickered from the direction of the Graben lane, two streets over, where the bookbinder Pemsel kept his shop. Brandt's hands, which could find a worn pivot in the dark without looking, fumbled twice at the bolt.

The lane was already filling with nightshirts and coats thrown over nightgowns, buckets passing hand to hand along a chain that formed itself the way such chains always did in this city—quickly, wordlessly, out of long practice. The fire had taken the upper floor of Pemsel's shop and was eating toward the roof beam with an appetite that seemed almost deliberate, as though it had studied the building first.

*It started at the back,* Brandt thought, standing at the mouth of the lane with his arms crossed against the cold, watching. *Back doors don't catch fire on their own at three in the morning. Not in November. Not with no stove lit.*

He did not join the bucket line. Fifty years in Vienna had taught him which men were expected to labor and which were expected to watch, and more importantly, it had taught him to watch. He watched a man at the far end of the lane who was not carrying water, not shouting encouragement, not doing anything at all except standing beneath the sign of the coffeehouse opposite, his collar up, his face turned toward the burning shop with the mild attention of a man reading an account that concerned him only as arithmetic.

Brandt memorized him: tall, gray-beaver hat, one glove missing.

The roof of Pemsel's shop came down at half past three with a sound like the sky clearing its throat. Sparks rose in a column and scattered over the lane like furious fireflies, and the crowd gasped and stepped back as one animal. Somewhere a woman was weeping—not for the building, Brandt guessed, but for the books inside it, forty years of other people's words going up in a single hour.

Pemsel himself stood beside Brandt, soot-striped, his hands hanging empty at his sides.

"Nothing saved," the bookbinder said. His voice was strangely calm. "The back door was barred from outside. My apprentice broke a window getting my daughter out, or I'd have lost her too. Barred from *outside*, Herr Brandt. Who bars a door from the outside and then lights a fire, unless they mean the fire to be read?"

Brandt said nothing. He was thinking about the clock on his workbench—the observatory clock, half-dismembered, its escapement open like a chest awaiting surgery, and inside it the papers he had ordered Felix to burn and Felix had not burned. He was nearly certain Felix had not burned them. He had raised boys for thirty years; he knew what obedience looked like, and he had never once seen it on that face.

"You should go home," Pemsel said quietly, not looking at him. "Shops like mine burn when someone wants the whole street to read the message. Shops like yours burn when they want only you to."

Brandt went home.

* * *

The workshop at dawn looked exactly as it always did: benches swept, tools racked, the great disassembled movement of the observatory clock laid out on felt under the south window where the light was honestest. Felix was already there—of course she was already there, she was always already there, she slept like a cat with one ear open—and she took one look at him and set down her pliers.

"You've heard," she said.

"The whole street has heard." He hung his coat on its peg, an action performed with more care than it required because his hands wanted something orderly to do. "Sit down, boy."

Felix sat. That was another thing about the boy; whatever else Felix Halloran was or wasn't, the boy knew when Brandt's voice meant business. Across the workshop, Ivo appeared in the doorway of the finishing room with a question in his eyebrows, and Marta's tread could be heard overhead, pausing—listening—then deliberately continuing, floorboards announcing her retreat to the kitchen. The household arranging itself around a wound, the way households did.

"Thirty-one years ago," Brandt said, "my brother Josef took a commission. A private commission, well paid, from a man in the treasury administration. Repair of a strongbox. Nothing but a strongbox." He turned a screwdriver on the bench a quarter-turn, aligning it with nothing. "Josef was the better craftsman. Faster than me, braver with metal, laughed too loud. He did the repair in four days and was dead in eleven. Thrown from a horse on the road to Brno, though Josef never rode anywhere if a cart could carry him, and the letter in his saddlebag was gone, and the man who paid him could not be found because the name given had been a name written on water."

Felix's face had gone very still. Stillness was the boy's native language.

"I have spent thirty-one years being careful," Brandt said. "Careful is not a vice in a clockmaker. Every wheel must mesh, every tooth must be counted, nothing moves before its time. Careful built this shop. Careful fed us all." He met Felix's eyes. "But careful has a price, and last night I saw the bill come due two streets over, addressed to whoever cared to read it. Pemsel is not part of this. Pemsel's daughter is not part of this. Next time it will not necessarily be a stranger's shop."

"Master—" Felix began.

"Hear me out. I am an old man who has rehearsed this speech for three decades and I will not give it twice." He drew a breath, and the breath cost him something; he felt it leave like heat from an opened case. "There is a magistrate named Ehrlich. Tobias Ehrlich, Landstraße district. When we were young, he and Josef were friends—drinking friends, chess friends, the kind of friends who argue about music until dawn. Josef trusted him absolutely, and Josef was not a trusting man, which made the trust worth more than most men's oaths. After Josef died, Ehrlich came to the funeral and stood at the graveside with his jaw set like a man biting down on something, and he said to me: *If ever you hold anything your brother died of, bring it to me and no one else.*"

"And you never went," Felix said softly.

"I never went. Because bringing it meant holding it up to the light, and light shows the holder as well as the held. Because I had a shop full of apprentices and a name I didn't want spoken in rooms I'd never enter. Because thirty-one years is a long time to keep a promise by breaking it." He straightened. "I am done breaking it. Here is what we do, and we do it today, because fires do not wait politely while one deliberates."

* * *

They walked separately to Landstraße, Brandt first, Felix ten minutes behind with a delivery box under one arm containing, ostensibly, a repaired mantel clock for a fictitious customer. It was the sort of subterfuge Brandt had despised on principle for thirty years and now found himself executing with the grim competence of a man discovering talents he never wanted. Gray-beaver hat might have watchers; gray-beaver hat had stood too calmly under that coffeehouse sign for there to be no watchers.

Ehrlich's house was narrow and plain, squeezed between two grander facades like a craftsman's conscience among courtiers. Brandt knocked, gave his name to a housekeeper, and prepared to wait an hour in the cold—but the door opened quickly, and Tobias Ehrlich stood there in shirtsleeves, gray-haired, broad-shouldered, with eyes that had not softened since a graveside thirty-one years ago.

"Elias Brandt," the magistrate said. "I have been expecting this visit for thirty-one years. Come in before God and the neighbors see you standing there like a penitent."

The sitting room smelled of coffee and law books. On the wall hung a cheap print of the Danube at Krems, and beneath it, on a small table, a chessboard mid-game—both players evidently Ehrlich, both losing. He poured coffee without asking and sat, and waited, in the manner of a man who had learned that witnesses spoke fastest into silence.

Brandt set his cup down. "You remember what you said to me at Josef's grave."

"I remember everything about that day except the sermon."

"He worked on a strongbox. Eleven days later he was dead. And now—" Brandt's voice caught on the edge of a thing he had carried so long it had grown into his flesh, and he had to cut it free. "Now there are papers, Herr Magistrate. Treasury papers. Ledgers, coded, bearing the seal of a clerk named Anton Voss, who was murdered last month in a way the newspapers were not permitted to describe accurately. They were hidden in a clock I have been commissioned to repair. The observatory clock."

Ehrlich set his own cup down with a click, precise as a detent. "The equinox demonstration," he said. "Which Director Albrecht's office has been pressing the observatory to schedule publicly. With dignitaries. With the press permitted." He said it flatly, and then, watching Brandt's face, his composure cracked just enough to show the man beneath—a man who had spent decades in the machinery of a city he could not fix, turning cases over, finding Albrecht's fingerprints under every one, and lacking only the one thing courts required: proof that would survive an appeal, a patron, a bribe, a knife. "You understand what you have just handed me?"

"A reason for my brother's grave to have meant something."

"I mean," Ehrlich said, leaning forward, "that Anton Voss came to me eight months ago. Came here, to this room, sat where you're sitting, and told me he had found irregularities in the estate seizures large enough to hang a director, and asked me what a magistrate could protect and what he couldn't. I told him honestly: I could protect a witness in custody, badly. I could not protect a witness in his own home. He chose his home. He chose wrong." Ehrlich's hands flattened on the arms of his chair. "I have dined on that answer every night since October, Herr Brandt. So yes—I remember the graveside. I have been waiting for you to remember it too."

There was a knock—two soft raps—and Felix entered at Ehrlich's nod, setting down the fictitious delivery box with a craftsman's care. Brandt watched the magistrate look at the boy, and past the boy, in that quick professional way; watched him notice the fine wrists, the careful voice, the things that were carefully not noticed. Ehrlich noticed everything and filed it, and said only:

"So you're the apprentice who opened the clock. Sit down, young Halloran. You are either the bravest or the most unfortunate person in Vienna, and I intend to find out which over the next month."

"What happens next?" Felix asked.

"What happens next," said Ehrlich, "is the difficult part. I cannot simply take possession of the papers. Papers can be stolen; magistrates can be reassigned; clerks can be bribed. Albrecht's reach extends into the Polizeidirektion—which is why Inspector Kessler visits your shop wearing civility like a coat, and why Pemsel's door was barred from outside. No." He rose and walked to the window, hands clasped behind him. "The papers must surface in a place where stealing them becomes its own confession. Publicly. Before witnesses who cannot all be bought. The equinox demonstration is in nineteen days. If the clock itself delivers the evidence—if the mechanism opens before a hundred pairs of eyes—then Albrecht must seize the plates in front of those same eyes, and seizure becomes scandal, and scandal, handled correctly, becomes trial."

"That was the boy's design already," Brandt said, and heard the pride in his own voice arrive before he could decide whether he approved of it. "I fought it. I lost."

"Then you have both outlived your caution, and I congratulate you." Ehrlich turned back. "But hear the cost plainly, because I deal plainly: between tonight and the equinox, you are all of you in danger, and I cannot guard a workshop any better than I guarded Voss. What I can do is this—" he ticked it off, a lawyer's fingers, "—I will put in writing, sealed and witnessed, that these materials were delivered to me and copies lodged with a second magistrate, so that killing you accomplishes nothing but multiplying the copies. I will request, quietly, that the equinox demonstration have official attendance—myself included—so that the crowd contains friends. And I will tell you the thing Voss never had: you will not sit in your shop waiting to be found. When the moment comes, you will not be alone."

Brandt looked at this man—gray, unbought, dining nightly on an old failure—and felt thirty-one years of careful rearrange themselves into something harder. Fear was still present; fear would be present at his funeral. But fear had stopped being master sometime between the bucket line and this cup of coffee, and what remained was simply work: teeth to count, wheels to mesh, nothing moving before its time, and everything moving exactly then.

Walking home through the waking city, Felix a half-step behind with the empty delivery box, Brandt spoke without turning his head.

"When we return, you will show me the compartment you built into the striking train. All of it. And you will show me where the girl sleeps, and how she is fed, and every lie this household is currently living—because I am apparently its head, and a head should know what the hands are doing."

There was a pause behind him, the particular quality of silence belonging to a young person recalculating rapidly.

"You knew," Felix said. "About Lena. About the loft."

"I suspected the girl the night you brought her in. I confirmed it Tuesday, when Marta sewed a christening gown for a child whose existence this shop does not acknowledge." He let that sit a few steps. "I said nothing, for the same reason I said nothing five years ago when a ruined merchant's daughter walked in wearing her dead brother's coat and calling herself Felix. Some silences protect people. I have simply finally learned to tell which ones."

The workshop door, when they reached it, stood ajar.

Brandt stopped on the threshold with his hand still on the iron ring, every sense snapping taut. Inside, in the gray morning light, the workshop lay undisturbed—benches swept, tools racked, the clock parts sleeping under their felt—except for one thing. On the center of his own workbench, precisely placed where he could not fail to see it, lay a single object: a burnt page, its edges charred lace, salvaged from some fire, delivered in the night. He crossed the room and picked it up with a craftsman's fingertips. Most of the writing was gone. But at the bottom, surviving the flame with the obstinacy of ink, was a signature he recognized from the treasury commissions of his youth, and beneath the signature, in a different hand, a single line:

*The next clock that opens will be yours.*

Behind him, Felix read it over his shoulder and went white. From the kitchen above came the small, ordinary sound of Marta singing to Lena over breakfast, and somewhere across the roofs of Vienna, nineteen days from the equinox, Director Albrecht's city went about its morning as though nothing had been promised to anyone.

"Nineteen days," Brandt said quietly, laying the burnt page on the bench like a challenge accepted. "Then we had best get to work."
