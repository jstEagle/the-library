## 12. The Child Speaks

Lena knew the loft by its sounds before she knew it by its light.

In the first weeks she did not speak—not because she had forgotten how, but because words were heavy things, and Papa had taught her that some words must be carried carefully, like eggs wrapped in wool, and only set down in the right place at the right time. *Not yet, Liebling. Not yet.* He had said it so often that the phrase had worn a groove inside her, the way water wears a groove in stone, and now the groove was all that was left of him: a channel where his voice used to run.

So she kept her mouth shut and her eyes open, and she learned the loft.

She learned that the roof sloped low on the north side, where Marta hung her drying lavender in bundles that filled the whole attic with a gray-purple smell, and that if she pressed her cheek against the warm chimney bricks on an afternoon when the workshop fires were lit below, she could feel the building breathing. She learned that Ivo came up the ladder at odd hours—his steps made no sound she could hear coming, which frightened her at first until Marta explained, drawing on the dusty boards with a fingertip, that Ivo's ears were closed doors, and that he spoke with his hands the way other people spoke with their mouths. His hands were beautiful. They made letters in the air like little birds taking flight.

She learned that Felix—that was the apprentice's name, though sometimes Lena caught the others watching him with a look she could not read, a look like people watching a candle in a draft—slept hardly at all. She would wake in the night to see lamplight leaking up through the floorboards from the workroom below, and hear the small patient sounds of files and pliers, and once, very late, a voice speaking low and urgent to Herr Brandt, and the old man answering even lower, and though she could not make out the words she understood the shape of them: two people carrying something heavy between them, something that could not be set down anywhere safely.

She understood that shape. She had been carrying one herself since the stairs of the observatory.

* * *

The dreams came most nights. In them she was back in the yellow room in Leopoldstadt, the one with the water stain shaped like a leaping fish above the stove, and Papa was kneeling in front of her with his hands on her shoulders, and his face was doing the thing it did when he was trying to be calm and failing, the corners of his mouth pulled down like wet paper. *Again,* he would say. And she would say the words. All of them. In order. The numbers first, then the names, then the strange little verse about the miller and the three sacks that made no sense but which she had never once gotten wrong, not once, not even the night the knocking came at the door and Mama went white and pushed her out the back way into the frozen lane.

*Say it again, Liebling. One more time. If anything happens to me—if you are ever alone, ever, and someone kind finds you—find the clock. The clock will remember what you say.*

She had thought he meant their clock, the black one in the parlor with the pendulum shaped like a brass pinecone. It was only later, cold and hungry on the observatory steps with the note pinned to her coat and the great dome rising against the gray dawn sky, that she understood. Papa had taken her there twice in the last month. Twice, on errands that made no sense, walking past the master clock while he touched its case the way other men touched the shoulders of old friends. He had been showing her where to go. He had known, the way she was only beginning to understand that grown men could know such things, that the knock would come.

And so she had sat down on the steps and waited for a clockmaker.

The one who came was young, with ink-stained fingers and tired eyes, and when he saw her his face did something complicated—fear and pity fighting behind it like weather. He read the note. His jaw went tight. *Forget this face,* the note said, in handwriting that even Lena recognized, because she had seen it on the documents Papa burned in the stove on the last bad night, elegant and slanted and cold.

She had not forgotten his face either, the man whose hand that was. He had come to dinner once. He had laughed too loudly and eaten nothing.

* * *

It was the miller verse that broke her silence, in the end.

She had been in the loft for nineteen days—she counted by the knots Marta tied in a length of red thread each evening, hanging them from the rafter nail like a rosary of days—and she had begun, cautiously, to belong to things. To the lavender smell. To the game Ivo played with her where he drew animals on slate and she named them and he mimed their movements until she laughed into her sleeve. To the plate of plum cake that appeared at her elbow every afternoon at four o'clock precisely, because the household below ran on clockwork and she had become, without anyone announcing it, one of the wheels in the train.

On the nineteenth day, Felix came up the ladder with the lamp and the box.

She knew the box. She had seen it hidden under floorboards, seen it passed between hands, seen the fear it made in everyone who touched it. Papers inside—Papa's papers; she knew his cramped clerk's hand even upside down, even folded, even through terror. Felix sat down across from her, cross-legged on the rough boards, and set the box between them like a offering or a dare.

"Lena," Felix said—softly, the way no one else said it; everyone else whispered it like contraband—"I need to show you something. And I need you to tell me if you know it."

There were pages. Pages covered in figures that were not quite figures, letters that were not quite letters, arranged in grids like a garden of symbols. Felix turned to the last page, the one that had resisted everything—the seamstress's patience, the engraver's eye, the clockmaker's logic—and laid it flat where the lamplight fell full upon it.

And Lena looked down at the grid of signs, and her heart began to hammer, because she knew them. Not their meaning—she could not read, not really, not yet—but their *shapes*. She knew their shapes the way she knew the tune of the bell at St. Ulrich's, without knowing why, without wanting to. Papa had made her trace them. Papa had made her say them aloud, over and over, in order, syllable by syllable, until they were stitched into her like the initials on her christening gown.

*Again, Liebling.*

Her throat closed. Her eyes stung. The lamp flame stood perfectly still between them, and Felix waited—did not reach for her, did not press, only waited, with the patience of someone accustomed to mechanisms that must not be forced—and that waiting, more than anything, more than plum cake or lavender or warm bricks, undid her.

"The miller," Lena said.

Her voice came out rusty, small, almost nothing—a hinge unopened for nineteen days and longer, for weeks, for all the weeks since the knocking at the door. Felix went very still.

"The miller had three sacks." The words came haltingly at first, and then, because Papa had drilled them into her a hundred times, a thousand times, because they lived in her body now deeper than fear, they began to flow. "The first sack held grain. The second sack held stones. The third sack—" she squeezed her eyes shut, seeing Papa's face, seeing his mouth shape the word along with her, always mouthing it with her, always—"the third sack held the names."

Felix's breath left him slowly, like air from a valve.

"Say the names," Felix whispered. "Can you say the names?"

And Lena said them. She said them in order, exactly as she had been taught, the foreign syllables her father had pressed into her memory like coins into wax—*Ebersdorf, Wranitzky, the estate at Klosterneuburg, Ebersdorf again, Albrecht*—and as she spoke, Felix pulled the final page close and followed her words with one stained fingertip, and Lena watched the finger stop and tap, stop and tap, matching her voice sign by sign, and she saw the moment the last lock turned. Saw it happen in Felix's face: the narrowing concentration, then the widening, then something that looked almost like grief, arriving after the understanding, the way thunder arrives after lightning.

"It's a key," Felix said. "You're the key. Your father hid the key in you, because—" His voice caught. He did not finish the sentence, but Lena finished it for him, silently, in the place where she kept all the things she could not say: *because he knew they would kill him, and he knew they would not think to search a child, and he knew that someday someone would open the clock, and his daughter would be there to speak.*

She was six years old and she was a vault, and her father had built her brick by brick over supper, between spoonfuls of soup, disguised as a game.

* * *

Afterward, there was cocoa, real cocoa, which Marta produced from some hoarded treasury of her own and which nobody drank in this city except on name-days. Lena held the cup with both hands and felt the warmth soak into her palms, and around her the loft filled with people.

Marta sat close on one side, her needle-work abandoned in her lap, her strong seamstress's fingers kneading Lena's shoulder in slow rhythm. Ivo perched on the trunk by the eaves, and his hands would not keep still—they moved and moved, quick questions flickering through the air, and Marta translated in her low voice: *Is she certain? Is she certain of the order?* And Felix, still cross-legged on the floor with the papers spread around like fallen leaves, answered aloud even though Ivo's back was turned, reading lips across the lamplight, nodding, tapping the page. "Certain. The verse gives the sequence—grain, stones, names—it's a substitution chain, that's why we couldn't crack the last page, it doesn't stand alone, it needs the spoken key." A shaky laugh. "He hid the cipher's heart in a child's nursery rhyme. No torturer thinks to ask a six-year-old to recite."

But Marta's hands had stopped moving on Lena's shoulder.

"No," Marta said quietly. "No, he thought of exactly that." And her voice, usually so practical, so full of pins and prices and the honest business of cloth, had gone thin. "I dressed his widow, God rest her, three weeks ago it must be. Black bombazine, ready-made, sleeves let out because grief had swollen her hands. She talked. The grieving talk, you understand, it pours out of them. She said Anton had changed in the last months of his life. That he'd taught Klara—this child, my dove, he taught *you*—songs and games every evening. That he said—" Marta paused, and Lena watched her decide whether to finish, and finish anyway. "He said the safest bank in Vienna is a child's memory. Because no one audits it."

The lamp hissed softly. Below them, muffled through the floorboards, the workshop clocks ticked in their uneven chorus—Brandt's old regulators, none of them agreeing, all of them going on regardless.

Lena drank her cocoa. It was very sweet. Papa had made it sweet too, on the nights he taught her the miller verse, and the taste opened something in her chest that hurt and warmed at the same time, like swallowing a coal wrapped in honey.

"I can say it again," she offered. "If it helps. I can say it as many times as you want. Papa said I should say it to whoever opens the clock."

Everyone in the loft looked at her then. Four faces—Ivo's pale and sharp, Marta's softening and hardening at once, and Felix's, Felix's worst of all, because on it she saw the same thing she had seen on the observatory steps: the complicated weather, the fear and the pity, except now there was a third thing too, something fierce and new, something that had not been there among the gulls and the gray sky of that dawn.

"Lena," Felix said, and his voice was not steady anymore, "you don't have to say it again. Not ever, if you don't want. It's written down now. It's safe now. You've carried it long enough."

But Lena shook her head, because Felix was wrong, and it was important—more important than plum cake, more important than belonging—that he understand.

"Papa said the clock remembers," she said. "But clocks only remember if somebody winds them." She said it patiently, the way one explains things to adults who have lost track of simple truths. "That's what he said. *Somebody has to wind them, Liebling. Somebody has to remember aloud.*"

Nobody spoke. Marta put her apron corner to her eyes. Ivo's hands, for once, hung silent at his sides.

It was Herr Brandt who broke the stillness—Herr Brandt, whom Lena heard only rarely, who climbed the ladder now slowly, one hand on the rail, an old man made older by three weeks of worry, holding in his other hand a letter. Lena noticed the letter because it was fine paper, cream-colored, with a seal of dark green wax, and because Felix's face, turning toward it, emptied of everything warm.

"Courier just left this at the door," Brandt said. His voice sounded like a clock that needed cleaning. "No mistake about the addressee. It asks for the apprentice Halloran. By name." He held it out. "Sealed with the Treasury's arms, boy. It's from Director Albrecht himself. He requests your presence. Tomorrow. At his office."

Lena looked at Felix, and Felix looked at the letter, and above the guttering lamp the red thread of knotted days swayed gently on its nail—one knot for every day Lena had been silent, and the thread, she saw, had a great deal of length left on the spool.
