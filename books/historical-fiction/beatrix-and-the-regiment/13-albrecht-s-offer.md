## 13. Albrecht's Offer

The summons came on a Tuesday, delivered by a boy in livery too fine for the neighborhood, who looked at Brandt's shingle with the expression of a man reading an insult in a language he didn't speak. The card inside was heavy cream stock, embossed with the Treasury's double-eagle, and it said only: *Herr Felix Halloran will attend Director Albrecht at his residence in the Himmelpfortgasse, Thursday, at four o'clock. On a matter concerning the observatory clock.*

No request. No *if it please him*. Just the flat grammar of power, the kind that did not ask because it had never needed to.

Felix read the card three times in the workshop's yellow lamplight while Marta pretended to mend a seam and Ivo pretended to clean his gravers, both of them watching her the way one watches a fuse burn toward a keg.

"He knows your name," Marta said at last.

"Everyone knows my name now. Kessler made certain of that."

"No." Marta set down her needle. "Kessler asked about you like a man checking a ledger entry—*the apprentice, the apprentice*. This one wrote your name down himself, and had it engraved, and paid for cream stock. There is a difference between being noticed and being *summoned*, Felix."

She was right, of course. Felix folded the card along its crease and felt the weight of it in her pocket all through supper, all through the night, lying against her ribs like a second heartbeat. It was not fear she felt, or not only fear. It was the older thing underneath, the thing she had carried since she was seventeen and running: the strange, shameful vertigo of being *wanted* by the world she had spent five years hiding from.

Thursday came gray and cold, the gutters running with the week's first honest rain. She dressed in her best—and her best was a coat brushed until the nap stood straight, boots blacked, collar turned so the worn side didn't show—and took the long way through the city so she could compose her face. By the time she reached the Himmelpfortgasse she had rehearsed eleven sentences and trusted none of them.

Albrecht's residence was not a palace, which somehow made it worse. Palaces confessed; this building merely *implied*. A plain façade, immaculate stonework, windows hung with damask that cost more per length than Brandt earned in a year. A servant took her hat without meeting her eyes and left her in an anteroom furnished like a scholar's study—a globe, a case of books bound uniformly in green, a single landscape painting of an estate in Lower Austria. She recognized the estate from Voss's ledgers. Seized in '43. Now, apparently, it hung on walls instead of feeding families.

*He keeps his trophies where visitors can admire them,* she thought. *Or where he can.*

"Herr Halloran."

Albrecht was smaller than she had imagined. That was the first ambush—that the voice she'd built up in her head, the voice behind Kessler's careful questions and Voss's coded pages, came out of a compact, silver-haired man with a scholar's stoop and kind creases around his eyes. He wore no insignia, no chain of office. Just good wool and good watch and the unhurried ease of someone who had never once been made to wait.

He offered his hand. His grip was dry, precise, brief—the handshake of a man who measured things.

"You'll take coffee," he said, not as a question, and poured it himself, which surprised her more than anything else in the room. He gestured her to a chair beside the fire rather than across a desk. No barrier between them. He wanted her to see there was no barrier. That was the second ambush.

"I've read your file," Albrecht began pleasantly. "Such as it is. Which is to say, I've read what isn't in it. Herr Halloran, apprentice to Christian Brandt, clockmaker, entered his service five years ago this autumn. Before that—" he lifted his cup, blew gently across it— "before that, the record is admirably thin. Apprentices drift about in those years. Papers go unfiled. Guild registrations lapse. It happens more often than people think, particularly in hard winters." He smiled, and the creases around his eyes deepened into something almost paternal. "I have always found it a mercy. Don't you? That the empire is so large, and its memory so imperfect, that a person might simply... begin again?"

The coffee in Felix's cup had gone still. So had she.

"I don't know what Your Excellency means," she said.

"Of course you do." He said it without sharpness, almost gently, the way a physician says *this will hurt*. "But let's not begin with lies between us. Lies are for Kessler. Kessler collects them; they're useful to him. You and I are going to speak a different language entirely—the language of what is *true*." He set down his cup. "Beatrix."

The name landed in the room like a dropped glass. Not *Fräulein*. Not *Halloran*. Her name, her true name, spoken aloud in a warm study over the smell of good coffee, by a stranger who had gone digging into the one grave she had made of her old life and pulled the bone out of it.

She kept her face still. Five years of practice bought her that much. But somewhere beneath her ribs, the seventeen-year-old who had cut her hair by candlelight and buried Beatrix Halloran in a pauper's grave of silence felt the earth shift.

"How," she said, and was proud that her voice stayed level, "do you know that name."

"The same way I know most things. Someone told me. People tell me things; it is my profession, more honestly than the Treasury is." He rose, went to the green-bound bookcase, and drew out a folder—not thick, but thick enough. He did not open it. He simply held it, letting her look at the wax seal, the Treasury stamp, the way his thumb rested on the string tie. "A girl born in Währing in 1824. Father ruined in the grain failures; house sold; family scattered. A brother, Felix, dead of fever at fourteen. And then—curious thing—an apprentice named Felix Halloran appears in Vienna with the brother's papers and no history anyone can verify." He shrugged, an apology for the empire's carelessness. "Nobody looked very hard. Nobody ever looks very hard at clockmakers' boys. Invisible trades attract invisible people. I know, because I have spent thirty years making myself visible enough that no one thinks to wonder what I am hiding."

*And what are you hiding?* Felix thought. *Ledgers. Estates. A clerk with his skull broken at the foot of the observatory steps.* She drank her coffee because her hands needed occupation.

"If you've brought me here to threaten me," she said, "you should know threats travel badly. Kessler has already tried the ordinary kind. I'm still repairing the clock."

"Oh, I imagine you are. You strike me as a young man who finishes what he starts—forgive me, a young *person*. I never get that wrong." He settled back into his chair and studied her with frank, unhurried interest, the way she herself studied a movement that refused to run. "And no, I haven't brought you here to threaten you. Threats are crude instruments, and worse, they leave marks. I brought you here because I intend to make you an offer, and I find offers go better when both parties understand exactly what is on the table."

He laid the folder on the small table between them, but did not push it forward.

"The observatory clock," he said, "is a fine instrument. Historic. Beloved. It has also, unfortunately, been failing for months, and despite every effort of every craftsman sent to it—including, I am told, yourself—it cannot be saved. When it is finally declared beyond repair, it will be dismantled and replaced with a modern regulator from Munich. A shame. An inevitability. This is the story everyone will tell, and it requires nothing from you except your signature on a report saying so. One line. *Unrepairable.* You write it, the clock comes down quietly, and life continues for everyone." He paused. "For the fee of two hundred gulden. Paid discreetly. Enough to buy Herr Brandt out of his debts twice over—yes, I know about those too; I told you, people tell me things."

"And if I don't sign?" Felix asked.

"Then you keep repairing it, and we keep letting you, and eventually the Munich men come anyway. You would lose nothing except time." He smiled again, and this time it did not reach the creases. "You see how reasonable I am? I am not even requiring the impossible. Only the empty."

*Delivered empty.* She heard the phrase in her head before he said it, and then he said it:

"Because here is the heart of it, Herr Halloran—or may I say Fräulein? Here is the heart of it, *Beatrix*. Something may have been concealed in that clock by a man who is now dead, and whatever it was, it is worthless. Paper. Old paper. It has no power unless it is found, and it will only be found if a young woman of no standing chooses to make a great deal of noise about it. If she does that—" he opened his hands, a gesture of genuine regret— "then everything must be examined, doesn't it? Everything becomes evidence. And evidence, examined thoroughly, cuts in every direction. It cuts the men who forged the seizures. It cuts the clerks who signed them. And it cuts"—he tapped the folder once, lightly—"a girl whose entire existence, whose freedom, whose very name before the law, rests on documents that were never properly filed. Do you understand? I am not threatening you with prison. Prison would be vulgar. I am merely observing that if the law wakes up and starts asking questions, it will ask them of *everyone*. And you, Beatrix, have spent five years making sure you could not survive being asked."

The fire ticked. Somewhere in the house a door closed softly. Felix sat with her hands wrapped around the cooling cup and understood, with a craftsman's clarity, exactly what she was looking at.

It was not a trap. Traps were crude; traps sprang. This was a *movement*—a wheel cut with perfect teeth, each one shaped to catch a different part of her. Two hundred gulden for Brandt. Silence for the workshop. Safety for Marta, for Ivo, for Lena sleeping in the loft above their heads. And threaded through all of it, hidden in the train like a false tooth in an escapement, the one bait that no threat could ever have reached: *papers making her apprenticeship legal under her own name.*

Because that was what he offered last, when the coffee cups were cleared away and he spoke in the tone of a man concluding business he considered already done.

"There is a version of this," Albrecht said, "where you walk out of here whole. Legitimate. Letters of registration filed in your true name, countersigned by my office—unimpeachable papers, Beatrix, the kind that close questions rather than open them. You would never again flinch when a guard asks for identification. Never again count the exits in a room. You could be yourself, openly, in daylight, for the rest of your life." He leaned forward, and his eyes were not cruel; they were patient, and that was far worse. "I am told—I am reliably told—that there is nothing a person wants more than to stop disappearing. All this, for one line written on one report. *Unrepairable.* The clock comes down. The paper inside it burns unread. And you become real."

* * *

She walked home through the rain without feeling it.

The city moved past her in grays and lantern-blurs, and inside her something moved too, vast and slow, the way ice moves on the Danube in late winter—apparently solid, groaning at its seams. She had expected to feel violated. She did not. That frightened her more than anything. What she felt, walking the wet cobbles with Albrecht's words lodged in her like splinters too deep to grip, was *understood*.

He had found the wound. Not the one she had guarded all these years—not the fear of discovery, the practiced lowering of the voice, the arithmetic of exits. He had gone past all of that to the older wound, the original one, the one she had never told anyone, barely told herself: that she was tired. That invisibility, which had saved her life, had also eaten it bite by bite, until there were days she could not remember the sound of her own laugh because Felix did not laugh, Felix was careful, and Beatrix had been buried so long she could no longer recall what being her had felt like from the inside.

*You could be yourself, openly, in daylight, for the rest of your life.*

Two hundred gulden. Legal papers. Her name. All of it priced against a dead man's testimony and a child's inheritance, and the obscene part—the part that made her stop at the corner of the lane and press her hand against the cold brick of the first house she passed—was that some part of her, a part she despised, whispered: *it's fair. It's even generous. Take it. Sign it. Burn the plates, deliver the clock empty, collect Beatrix Halloran back from the grave.*

By the time she reached the workshop her coat was soaked through and her mind had performed the negotiation a dozen times and arrived, every time, at the same terrible place: not *no*. Not yet. Just the trembling edge of *maybe*, and the knowledge that Albrecht had known it would be.

Marta was awake, waiting up with tea going cold beside her. She took one look at Felix's face and asked no questions, which was its own kind of question.

"Well?" she said finally.

Felix hung her coat to drip. In the loft above, faintly, she could hear Lena turning in her sleep, murmuring the little number-sounds her father had drilled into her, safe for another night because Felix had chosen to carry what no one had asked her to carry.

"He offered me my name back," Felix said.

Marta went very still.

"And God forgive me, Marta—" Felix's voice cracked, the first crack in five years of flawless joinery— "I sat in that warm room with that kind old monster's coffee in my hands, and I nearly said yes."

Above them, the loft floor creaked once, softly, and went quiet.
