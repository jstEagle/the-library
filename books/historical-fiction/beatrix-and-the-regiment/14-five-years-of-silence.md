## 14. Five Years of Silence

The coin sat in Felix's palm all the way home.

It was heavier than it should have been—gold always looked lighter in other men's hands—but she felt its weight through her coat lining as though it had burned a hole and settled somewhere against her ribs. Director Albrecht himself had pressed it there, had closed her fingers over it with his own soft hands, smiling the whole while like a priest distributing alms. *For your trouble, young man. And for your discretion. Deliver the clock repaired, and empty, and there will be ten more where this came from. Papers too. Legitimate papers. An apprenticeship made proper under your own name.*

*Your own name.* He could not have known how precisely the blade found its mark. He had said it casually, the way powerful men offer things that cost them nothing, and Felix had stood in that gilded office with her heart hammering against the false flatness of her chest and heard, beneath the director's velvet voice, the voice of her father on the last night in Pressburg: *A girl can inherit nothing, Betti. Not the shop, not the debts even—the debts they'll take anyway. A girl inherits only what a man bothers to leave her.* And then the creditors, and the auction notices pasted over the door of the house where she'd been born, and her mother's face going small and strange, and Felix running east with her dead brother's clothes in a satchel and his name in her mouth like a stolen loaf of bread.

She had said yes. God help her, she had said yes—or near enough. She had not said no. She had let Albrecht's gold sit in her hand, had bowed the bow of a workman who accepts honest wages, and had walked out of the Imperial Treasury with her ears ringing and the whole shape of her future suddenly, horribly legible before her: legal papers, a registered name, a workshop one day of her own, and Lena Voss handed over to an orphanage or worse, and Anton Voss's testimony melted down for scrap, and five years of hiding crowned at last with permission.

Permission. That was what he had sold her, and she had nearly bought it.

It was raining when she crossed into the lane behind the workshop—not rain so much as the fine grey drip of a Vienna November that seemed to come from the air itself rather than any cloud. The gutters ran loud. Somewhere a cart horse stamped and sighed. Felix stood a moment in the shadow opposite the workshop door, watching the light leak warm under the shutters, listening to Ivo's file whispering inside—she had learned to know each sound the workshop made at night, the way a mother knows a house of sleeping children—and she could not make her legs carry her across the lane.

Because if she went in, she would have to decide. And she knew, with the sick clarity that comes after a bargain has been struck in the soul if not yet on paper, which way her cowardice leaned.

*Ten years,* Albrecht had said. *Perhaps fewer, if you're ambitious. By thirty you could be a master in your own right, with a name no one questions.* A name no one questions. Five years she had worn Felix Halloran like a suit of armor, and she had told herself the armor was protection, but standing in the wet dark she understood at last that armor is also a prison you agree to live in, that every year the plates had fused a little closer to her skin, until the girl named Beatrix existed now mostly as an ache, a phantom limb, something that hurt in weather like this.

And Lena. Six years old, asleep upstairs in the loft with Marta's old shawl pulled to her chin, who had watched soldiers' lanterns slide across the ceiling two nights ago and had not made a single sound—had learned already, at six, the silence Felix had needed twenty years and a catastrophe to learn. *Forget this face,* the note had said. Albrecht's hand; Marta was certain of it now. Forget this face, as though a child were a debt to be written off, a ledger line struck through.

Felix's fingers closed around the coin until its edge bit.

Then she went in.

* * *

The workshop smelled of oil and cedar shavings and the onions Marta had fried for supper, and it was so familiar that it hurt, the way home hurts when you know you may be about to betray it. Herr Brandt sat at the bench nearest the stove, spectacles pushed up into his thin hair, mending the mainspring of a townhouse regulator with the unhurried precision of a man who had decided that if the world ended tomorrow he would still want this spring to be true when it went. Ivo was at his engraving table in the corner, bent low over the duplicate plates he was cutting—no, Felix thought, catching herself; he had stopped, and was looking at her. Ivo did not look at people often. When he did, it carried the weight of a summons.

Marta stood at the sink with her back to the door, and did not turn around.

"You've been to see him," Marta said. Not a question. Her shoulders were very straight under her shawl. "The director."

"How—" Felix began.

"Kessler's man followed you from the Treasury district. I saw him from the window when I went out for bread." Now she turned, and her face in the lamplight was the face Felix had seen perhaps three times in two years: stripped of its customary dry humor, aged by worry, furious. "You took the meeting."

"He summoned me. There wasn't—"

"There is always a choice, boy." The word *boy* landed strangely between them, an old habit Marta had never quite unlearned, though she had known the truth since the night Felix collapsed with fever and Marta had sat up with her and seen everything there was to see, and had said nothing then either—another silence kept out of love. Marta wiped her hands on her apron with terrible deliberation. "Tell me you didn't take his money."

Felix opened her coat. The coin dropped onto the bench and spun, wobbled, and lay still under the lamp, throwing off a small arrogant gleam.

Nobody moved. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"Herr Gott im Himmel," Brandt murmured. He set down his tweezers very carefully, as though sudden movement might detonate the thing on his bench.

"It isn't decided," Felix said, and heard how thin it sounded, how exactly like the lie it was. "He wants the clock delivered repaired and empty. In exchange—papers. Real ones. My name made legal. No more hiding. Ten years of my life given back to me, Marta, do you understand what that—"

"I understand perfectly what that costs." Marta crossed the room and picked up the coin, weighed it in her small strong seamstress's fingers the way she might weigh a bolt of inferior cloth, and set it down again with distaste. "It costs nothing. That's what should frighten you. It costs him nothing, and it buys him everything—the ledger gone, the girl gone, and the one person who knows both hidden forever behind a name he gives you like tossing a dog a bone." Her voice cracked on the last word and she steadied it. "He offered you yourself, Felix. He offered you your own self, and you stood in his office and counted the price of your soul in apprenticeship papers."

"You don't know what it's like," Felix said, and hated the whine in it, and kept going anyway because five years of silence will do that, will teach you that once you start speaking the truth it pours out ugly. "To be no one. To sign your name and feel it's forgery. To have a family that thinks you're dead, a brother's grave I couldn't stand at, a mother who—I don't even know if she's alive, Marta. He offered to end all of it. To make me *real.*"

The room was very quiet. Rain ticked at the shutters. Ivo had not moved from his corner, but his eyes had not left Felix, and their patience was unbearable.

"And Lena?" Marta said softly.

"She'd be provided for. He said—orphans are taken in, there are institutions—"

"Institutions." Marta said the word the way Brandt said *sabotage.* She came closer, and Felix saw that her hands were trembling now, though her voice was not. "Child. I dressed Anna Voss's body for burial. Did you know that? Two weeks ago, before we knew whose widow she was—just charity work, a poor woman dead of grief and want, and I sewed her into her shroud with my own hands. And I have been thinking ever since about what grief does. What wanting-to-forget does." She stopped an arm's length away. "You think your invisibility has protected you. Fine. It has. Five years, no one has looked twice at the quiet apprentice with the careful hands. But look at that child upstairs and tell me invisibility has protected *her.* Her father hid his testimony inside a clock because there was nowhere else in the whole empire safe enough. Her mother died of being forgotten. Someone wrote *forget this face* on a card and hung it around her neck like a price tag, and every day we keep her here, we are spending our safety on her—and you want to stop paying? You want to buy back your comfort with her future?"

"Marta—" Brandt started.

"No." Marta raised one finger without turning her head. "Let her hear it. Because I have held my tongue in this workshop for two years, out of respect, out of love, and love that only keeps silent is only half a thing." Her eyes were bright and wet and fixed on Felix's. "Your disguise saved your life. I will never say otherwise; God knows what would have become of you on the roads in '42, a girl alone with a ruined name. But it has run its course, child, and you know it has, because you didn't come home and put that coin in the tea tin like a woman who's decided. You came home and put it on the table like a woman hoping someone will tell her no. So I am telling you no."

Felix's throat worked. "If I refuse him—if I keep the plates, if we go on—he will find us. Kessler already knows where the workshop is. They burned out the bookbinder's shop next to the Färbergasse premises for less than what we're holding. What you're asking me—it isn't courage, Marta, it's arithmetic. My life against hers, and mine is the one I can—"

"—the one you can spend cheapest. Yes. I know the sum." Marta's voice gentled, which was somehow worse. "That is what five years of hiding teaches a person. That they are worth least. That disappearing is a kind of virtue." She picked up the coin again and pressed it back into Felix's hand, closing her fingers over it just as Albrecht had done, and the symmetry of it struck Felix like a blow. "But you were somebody before you were nobody, Beatrix Halloran, and that somebody is the only person in Vienna Anton Voss trusted enough to leave his daughter with. Think on that. Of all the doors in all the city, his desperation chose the door of a clockmaker's shop. As if the dead know things."

From the loft above, through the floorboards, came a small sound—a cough, a shifting, the creak of the cot. Lena, turning in her sleep. Everyone looked up. Everyone looked away again.

Brandt spoke at last, quietly, without rising from his bench. "My brother Josef took treasury papers to a magistrate in 1838," he said, to his hands. "I have never told you the whole of it, and I have used his death as a leash on you for a month, and I am tired of it." He removed his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, an operation that took far longer than clean lenses required. "Josef believed what you are deciding whether to believe: that some things must be kept by someone, or they are lost to everyone. He was not brave. He was frightened the entire time, his wife told me—he could not sleep, he ate nothing. He went to the magistrate anyway. And they killed him for it slowly, and called it fever, and I buried my anger with him and called that wisdom." He put the spectacles back on and looked up, and his eyes behind the glass were old and clear. "I was wrong, Felix. Silence kept me safe. It did not keep Josef safe, and it has not kept the empire safe, and it will not keep that child safe. There is a difference between hiding and being hidden, and I think you have lived long enough in the first to recognize the second when it is offered to you."

Felix stood in the middle of the workshop with the coin in her fist and the rain on the windows and three faces turned toward her—Ivo's steady, Brandt's grave, Marta's fierce and wet—and felt the full weight of the choice settle onto her at last, not as terror but as something almost like relief. The awful liberty of it. For five years every path had been chosen *for* her: her father's ruin, her brother's death, her flight, her disguise, the armor. Now the choosing was hers, and whatever it cost, and there was no one left to blame.

She thought of her mother's face. She thought of the grave in Pressburg she had never stood at, and understood suddenly that she could stand at it—could stand at anything—once she stopped needing the ground itself not to notice her.

She thought of Lena asking, two nights ago, in her small careful voice: *Are you the one who fixes things?*

"Yes," Felix said aloud, though no one had asked. Then, steadier: "No. We don't deliver it empty. We finish the clock, and we finish it *true*, and we put everything back inside it that belongs there, and we make them watch it open in front of the whole city." She set the coin down on Brandt's bench, deliberately, beside the delicate spring he was mending, and it looked smaller there, and cheaper, a little gold lie among true things. "Sell this. Buy wool for Lena, and candles—we'll need candles. And Marta—" Felix took a breath, and it went deep, deeper than breath had gone in years. "Marta, you said you sewed pages into a christening gown once, during the French wars, when a family smuggled their papers out of Milan. Teach me. If I'm going to be seen, I intend to be seen carrying everything."

Marta made a sound that was half laugh, half sob, and gripped Felix's hands hard enough to hurt.

Ivo rose from his engraving table without a word, crossed the room, and laid something on the bench beside the coin: a small brass plate, freshly cut, mirror-bright. Felix bent close. In Ivo's fine, certain lettering, along the bottom edge where only someone holding the plate to the light would ever read it, ran a single line:

**BEATRIX HALLORAN — SHE REMEMBERS.**

Not Felix. Not the apprentice. Not the borrowed name.

Her own.

She looked up, and Ivo inclined his head slightly, the closest thing to speech he offered, and Felix found that her eyes were burning and that she was, impossibly, smiling.

"Then we have work to do," she said. "Three days until I can get back inside the observatory without raising questions. Three nights to make copies they can't destroy and hide them where no raiding party will think to look." She swept the coin off the bench and pressed it into Marta's palm. "Wool for the child first. And Marta—buy red."

Marta raised an eyebrow. "Red?"

"If they're going to see her," Felix said, "let them see her properly."
