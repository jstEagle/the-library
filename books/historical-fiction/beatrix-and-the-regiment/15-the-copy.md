## 15. The Copy

Ivo had always believed that the eye was a more honest witness than the ear. Ears could be flattered, deceived by tone, lulled by a voice that knew how to lie in warm registers—he had watched it happen through the workshop window a hundred times, apprentices and customers alike nodding along to words that meant nothing. But the eye, if you trained it, saw what was truly there: the depth of a cut, the pressure of a burin, the difference between an original line and one made in imitation of it. He had spent twenty years teaching his eye to see everything.

Now he sat at his bench past midnight with a jeweler's loupe screwed into his orbit and Voss's copper plates laid out before him on soft grey cloth, and he taught his eye to see a dead man's hand.

There were four plates, each no larger than a prayer book's cover, each cut with rows of figures so fine that at arm's length they looked like weathering, like tarnish, like nothing at all. Only under magnification did the columns resolve—dates, estate names, sums moved from the seizure ledgers of the Imperial Treasury into accounts that did not officially exist, and beside them, initials. A.A. Director Albrecht's initials, repeated like a signature on a confession, forty-one times across four small sheets of metal.

Ivo had copied engravings before. Reproduction was half the trade; every workshop in Vienna kept its patterns, its ornament books, its transfer methods. But copying for beauty was a different labor than copying for survival. A flaw in a decorative scroll was a flaw. A flaw here—a digit transposed, a date smudged, an initial softened—was an opening wide enough to drive a carriage through. Kessler's lawyers would look for exactly that. Albrecht's men would look for exactly that. The copies had to be perfect, because imperfect copies were not evidence but opinion, and opinions could be dismissed by men whose dismissals carried the weight of the crown.

So Ivo worked the way he had not worked since his own apprenticeship: slowly, breath shallow, the loupe giving him a circle of light no wider than a coin. He used a burnished copper sheet cut from the same stock as the originals—Brandt had found it in the cellar, left over from a commission fifteen years dead—and he transferred the design first by dusting, then went over every line freehand, matching not just the shapes of the characters but their character. Voss had been a frightened man when he cut these plates. Frightened men pressed too hard on their curves and hurried their straights, and Voss's straights wavered almost imperceptibly, the way a voice wavers when the speaker is listening for footsteps. Ivo reproduced the wavering. It felt like forgery of a sacred kind, imitating another man's fear stroke by stroke, but it was also fidelity. The fear was part of the testimony. A copy that omitted it would be a copy of the metal only, not of the man.

He worked until his eyes burned, then stopped, because tired eyes make confident mistakes. That was the discipline Brandt had hammered into him two decades ago: *the work is smarter than your pride, boy; listen to it.* He wrapped the finished plate in cloth and set it beside the others, three done, one remaining, and blew out his candle, and lay on his pallet listening to the sounds of the sleeping workshop—the tick of the regulator clock Brandt refused to let stop even now, the settling of old timber, and above it all, faint and precious, the breathing of a child in the loft who did not know yet what she carried in her head.

* * *

Marta sewed in the mornings, when the light through the workshop's east windows was honest and white. She had claimed the corner bench nearest the glass decades ago, back when the workshop had employed six seamstresses instead of one woman who did piecework between odd jobs, and no one had ever displaced her, partly out of affection and partly out of awe. Marta's hands knew cloth the way Brandt's hands knew brass. When she ran fabric between her fingers she read it like a letter—who had spun it, how it had been dyed, what years of wear waited folded inside it.

The christening gown had come to her the way most things came to Marta: through grief and proximity. Widow Novak down the lane had lost her grandson before his christening day, and in her mourning had given Marta the unfinished gown rather than let it sit in a chest accusing her. It was beautiful work already—fine batiste, a yoke of whitework embroidery, roses and leaves stitched so densely they stood half off the fabric. Marta had finished it carefully, meaning to sell it, and then had not been able to, and it had hung on the wardrobe in her room like a small ghost waiting for a body.

Now it lay spread across her knees, and she was un-picking it.

"You'll ruin it," Felix said. She sat cross-legged on the floor nearby, ostensibly oiling a striking train but in truth watching, the way everyone in the workshop had begun watching Marta these days, with the particular hunger of people who needed to believe a plan could work.

"That is the point," Marta said. "A gown that has never been worn is a question. A gown that looks loved is an answer." She lifted the hem section into the light. "I dressed Voss's widow twice last spring, after the funeral and again at the month's mind. I know what her household wore, what her servants wore, what her child wore. This—" she touched the embroidery, "—is the kind of work a grandmother does. Every family has one garment like this, saved against occasion. If the day comes when someone searches us, and they find a baby's gown folded in my chest with camphor and the rest of my things, they will see an old woman's sentimentality. They will not think to weigh it." She weighed it herself in her hand, thoughtfully. "Unless it hangs wrong."

Felix set down her oilcloth. "Hang wrong?"

"Fabric carries what you put in it. Paper adds body. Four pages of thin ledger paper, properly split and distributed through the layers of the skirt panels, will add almost nothing to the drape—but *almost* nothing is still something, to hands that know cloth." She smiled without much humor. "Kessler will not search me himself. Men like him send men, and those men are looking for metal and documents, for satchels and false bottoms. No one has ever gone through a christening gown stitch by stitch, because no one has ever cared enough about a christening gown. Sentiment makes people look away. I have watched it happen my whole life. Grief embarrasses them, so they step around it." Her needle paused. "We are going to make them step around justice."

She worked with a seam-ripper finer than a needle's point, lifting stitches in the interior seams of the skirt where no eye would ever travel, creating channels between the lining and the face fabric. The ledger pages themselves had been chosen days ago—Ivo had identified which pages were duplicates of the copper plates and which existed only on paper, and it was the paper-only ones, irreplaceable, that would go into the gown. The plan had grown this way, organically, each person adding a limb to it until it walked on its own: the originals would stay hidden in plain sight within the master clock at the observatory, timed to reveal themselves publicly at the equinox demonstration; the copper copies would go to Magistrate Ehrlich through Brandt's old contact, delivered by means they had not yet settled; and the paper originals, the seed corn of the whole harvest, would ride in the seams of a dead child's unfinished gown, folded in camphor and lavender in the bottom of Marta's chest, one among many garments, indistinguishable from memory itself.

"Here." Marta took the first page, cut precisely into two narrow strips, and fed a strip into the channel with a bodkin, easing it flat with fingers that never trembled. "You see? It disappears."

Felix watched the strip vanish into the white fabric and felt something in her chest turn over. "It's like it was always part of the dress."

"Everything true is," Marta said. "That is what makes it dangerous."

* * *

They gathered on Sunday evening, when the workshop was closed and the shutters drawn and the single lamp turned low—not for secrecy alone, though there was that, but because Brandt insisted that plans made in daylight grew careless, and plans made by lamplight remembered they were conspiracies. He stood at the head of the long workbench with his arms folded, an old man shaped like his own doorframes, square and unyielding, and Ivo, watching from his seat near the lamp, thought that Brandt had changed in these weeks the way metal changes in tempering—same substance, different structure, harder where it had been brittle.

"Say it back to me," Brandt said. "All of it. If any part fails, every part fails."

Felix spoke first, standing with her sleeves rolled, ink on her fingers from drawing timing diagrams on scrap paper. "The equinox demonstration is confirmed for the twenty-third of September. The director of the observatory expects the master clock running and striking, with dignitaries present—Albrecht will attend; his office is patron to the observatory. I finish the clock by then regardless of what Kessler demands. The compartment in the striking train opens on a cam I have designed myself—it releases during the noon strike sequence, in front of everyone, and cannot be stopped once the winding is set. Voss's original plates are inside it now." She hesitated, then added, honestly, "If they take the clock apart before the demonstration, we lose the originals. That risk doesn't close."

"It doesn't close," Brandt agreed. "It narrows. Go on."

"The copies go to Ehrlich," Ivo said. His voice came out rougher than he intended; he spoke rarely in company and every sentence felt like an engraving, permanent once made. "Not by messenger. Not by post. By hand, by someone Kessler's people have no reason to watch." He looked at his own hands on the bench. "By me."

Silence, brief. Felix started to speak and Ivo raised one finger, a gesture he had learned from watching her do it, and she stopped.

"No one watches me," he said. "This is the whole truth of my life and for the first time it is useful. A deaf man carrying a package is invisible. I do not answer questions badly, because I do not answer questions. If they stop me, I show them my chalk slate and my apprentice papers and I am a nuisance, not a suspect. And—" here he permitted himself the smallest smile, "—if it goes wrong, I can swallow nothing, say nothing, betray nothing. My silence is not loyalty. It is architecture."

Brandt nodded slowly. "Ehrlich expects you Thursday week. He knows to write his questions; you know to read them." Then, to Marta: "And yours?"

Marta had brought the christening gown to the table, folded now, ordinary-looking, a white bundle like bread or linens. She laid one hand upon it.

"In my chest, under the winter woolens, with the camphor. If the workshop burns, it goes out with me in the morning basket—if I am taken, it stays where it is, and Lena—" she glanced toward the loft ladder, where a small shadow sometimes appeared and withdrew, listening to the parts of conversations she was meant to hear, "—Lena knows that Aunt Marta keeps her things safe, and nothing more. The paper pages are the root of the plant. Plates are copies; testimony is copies of copies. The paper is the only true thing besides the child herself, and so it hides in the most forgettable place in Vienna: an old woman's keepsakes."

"And the fourth hiding place," Brandt said quietly, "sits up that ladder and eats her supper without complaint."

No one answered that directly. There was nothing to answer. Lena was the key and the reason both—her drilled phrases had unlocked the cipher's final page, confirming what Ivo's eye had suspected, that the last column of figures was not bookkeeping but a confession, names and dates and one line rendered in the clerk's careful hand: *if I die, let the clock speak for me.* The girl had spoken those phrases the way children recite scripture, not knowing what they meant, and had gone pale and silent when Felix gently explained that Papa had put important words in her mouth, and that soon the words would help make bad men behave. She had considered this for a long moment, six years old and grave as a judge, and had asked whether the bad men would say sorry.

Nobody at the workbench had been able to answer that either.

"One more thing," Felix said. She drew a folded paper from her apron and flattened it on the bench: a diagram of the master clock's striking train, precise, annotated in her small upright hand. "The cam release. I want it checked by four eyes, not mine alone. If I have made an error in the escapement geometry, the compartment opens early or not at all, and everything we have done ends in that gearwork." She pushed the diagram toward Brandt, then toward Ivo, then around the table. "Check it the way Ivo checks a copy. Assume I was afraid when I drew it. Find the places my hand shook."

Ivo took the diagram and held it to the lamp, and his eye began its patient work, tracing lines, measuring intervals, reading the maker's hand beneath the drawing. Around him the others bent inward too, four heads converging over one sheet of paper like a compass needle finding north, and he understood, with the wordless certainty he usually reserved for metal, what they had become. Not a conspiracy, exactly. Conspiracies were made of fear. This was made of something else—an instrument, assembled from unlikely parts, each piece fitted to the next: Felix's hands, Marta's needle, Brandt's old roads and older stubbornness, Lena's innocent memory, and his own silence, load-bearing at last. An instrument built to do one thing, on one day, in front of witnesses who could not look away.

He found the error at half past eleven—a tooth profile on the release pawl, underspecified, the kind of thing a tired hand draws and a fresh eye catches—and marked it in red chalk without triumph. That was the nature of instruments. No single part got to be right by itself.

He was still holding the diagram when the knock came at the street door. Three slow blows, evenly spaced, deliberate—the knock of someone who was not afraid of waking anyone.

The lamp flame leaned. Above them, on the loft ladder, the small shadow appeared all at once, fully awake, and this time did not withdraw.
