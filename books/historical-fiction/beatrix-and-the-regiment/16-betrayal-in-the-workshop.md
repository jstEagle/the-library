## 16. Betrayal in the Workshop

The betrayal did not announce itself. It arrived the way rot arrives in timber—quietly, from within, wearing the familiar grain of home.

Felix should have seen it sooner. She knew that afterward, in the long nights that followed, when she replayed every hour of those last days in the workshop and searched for the moment she might have turned aside. But there was no single moment. There was only Tobias, twenty-six years old, broad-shouldered, good with a lathe and better with a joke, who had been with Brandt for three years and had never once been told what the rest of them carried.

That was her fault too. They had made a circle—herself, Marta, Ivo, old Brandt—and left Tobias standing outside it, polishing cases he was not permitted to ask about, watching Lena's meals carried up to the loft on trays he pretended not to see. A man excluded from a secret does not need to be bought. He needs only to be made curious enough to sell his way into it.

But she did not know this yet. On the morning it happened, Felix rose before the bells of St. Anne, dressed in the dark, and stood a moment at the loft ladder listening to Lena breathe. The child slept curled against the wall where the chimney's warmth collected, one hand fisted in the christening gown Marta had sewn flat and innocent-looking over its shelf of folded paper. Felix had learned to sleep in fragments now, surfacing at every creak of the house, every carriage in the lane. Five years of practiced invisibility, undone by six months of loving something loud.

She descended. Herr Brandt was already at his bench, spectacles low on his nose, working a hairspring under the lamp. He looked up when her boots touched the floorboards.

"You slept," he said. It was not quite a question.

"An hour. Maybe two."

"That's more than me." He set down the tweezers and rubbed his eyes with thumb and forefinger, and in that gesture he looked suddenly very old—the stoop deeper, the liver-spotted hands less steady than they had been before the fire in the lane next door, before the letters to Magistrate Ehrlich had begun their slow, dangerous journey. "The equinox is nine days off. The striking train?"

"Fitted. I want another week on the escapement before I trust it."

"The clock has kept time through wars, girl. It can keep time through nine days."

"It isn't the clock I don't trust."

He almost smiled at that. Almost. Then the smile went somewhere private, and he said, quietly, "Ehrlich sent word last night. He'll attend the demonstration. Officially, as an invited guest of the Observatory Society. Unofficially—" Brandt tapped the bench twice, a clockmaker's punctuation, *and there we are*.

"And Kessler?"

"Kessler is a man being pulled between two anchors. Sooner or later one rope snaps."

Felix poured herself the dregs of yesterday's coffee and drank it cold, standing. Through the workshop's front window the lane lay gray and ordinary: the knife-grinder's shutter opening, a cart of winter cabbage, the scarred black timbers of the burned shop two doors down, which no one had yet cleared away because no one wished to look at what it said. *We know where you work. We know what you love.*

Marta came down the inside stair from her room above the seamstress's shop she shared nothing with but a landlord, carrying bread and a pot of honey, already talking. "Lena wants to learn the wheel-cutting. She sat with Ivo half the evening drawing teeth in the margins of her slate, little saw-edged moons all over the place, and he let her, mind—he never lets anyone touch his margins—"

"Marta." Brandt's voice, gentle. "Not the wheel-cutting. Not yet. Not until after."

After. The word they all lived inside, the door at the end of nine days, behind which waited either safety or the gallows, depending on which men arrived first.

* * *

Tobias came back from the coffeehouse at noon.

Later, Felix would fix on this: the smallness of it. Treason conducted over a cup of melange, for a sum she later learned was thirty gulden—not even a year's wages, barely enough to matter, paid by a clerk who had never given his name and whose coat had been cut in the Treasury style. Thirty gulden and a promise of a position in Linz. For that, a man sold the only family some of them had.

But at noon she noticed nothing except that Tobias was cheerful, whistling something from *Der Verschwender* while he filed case hinges, and that his cheer had a bright, glassy edge to it, like new varnish over old wood.

"Tobias," she said, testing without knowing she tested, "you're in a mood."

"A man can't be in a mood?" He grinned at her over the file. "Spring is coming, Halloran. Even you must have noticed, buried down here in your brass bones."

It was not spring. It was the first week of March and snowing sideways. She laughed anyway, because it was easier, and went back to the escapement, and hated herself a little for how easily she laughed—how easily she lied, how easily she moved through the world as someone else. That was Kessler's gift to her, the one thing she'd taken from their two interviews: the knowledge of her own fluency.

At four o'clock, Tobias asked to leave early. His mother was unwell, he said; he'd be back at dawn. Brandt waved him off with his face still bent to the bench. Ivo, at his station by the north window, looked up from the plate he was engraving—a duplicate ledger page, lines of figures rendered in steel fine as frost—and watched the door swing shut. His eyes stayed on it a long moment.

Then he signed something, quick, toward Marta, and Marta frowned and shook her head slightly, and Felix caught only the tail of it: the sign for *door*, and then the sign Ivo used for *wrong weather coming*, which he had invented himself years ago because the sky meant nothing to him but light for working, and which he otherwise reserved for storms.

"What?" Felix said.

Ivo considered her. Then he took his slate and wrote in his careful engraver's hand: **HE COUNTED THE TRAYS.**

Felix set down her pliers.

Three weeks ago, Tobias had begun, casually, to remark on the loft. Nothing anyone could name afterward. He'd helped carry up firewood once—had stood in the doorway, looking at the bed, the stove, the shelf of children's things that could not possibly belong to anyone in a clockmaker's shop. He'd asked Marta, laughing, whether she was running an orphanage out of her sewing basket. He'd noted aloud, one evening, that the loft window latch was broken and ought to be mended, *before thieves notice.*

"He counted the trays," Felix repeated. The words came out flat, dead on arrival, the way the worst sentences do.

**HE KNOWS SHE'S UP THERE,** Ivo wrote. **OR HE SOON WILL.**

Brandt had gone very still at his bench. When he finally spoke, his voice was the one he used for delivering bad news about prices. "Options. We move the child tonight. We move the plates tonight. Or we do both and abandon the workshop."

"We can't move the plates tonight," Felix said. "They're sewn into the gown and engraved into steel and half of them are still loose on that bench because Ivo's hands are the only ones that can finish the duplicates and he needs three more days—"

"Then we have three days," Brandt said, "or rather, we have whatever Tobias chooses to give us, which may be considerably less."

"Or," said Marta, and her voice had the hard brightness it got when she was most frightened, "we're wrong. We accuse an honest journeyman of treachery on the strength of a glance at a door, and we tear apart everything Brandt built over forty years for nothing—"

"Ivo is rarely wrong about weather," Brandt said quietly.

The deaf man looked out at them all, patient, unsurprised. He had spent his whole life reading rooms through his skin, and he wrote one line more upon the slate, slowly, so that none of them could pretend they hadn't seen it:

**I SAW HIM LOOK AT ME LOOKING AT HIM. HE KNOWS I KNOW.**

* * *

They argued until dark, and decided nothing, because frightened people decide nothing—that was a lesson the night itself was preparing to teach them, though they did not know it yet. In the end they settled on the smallest possible motion: Lena would sleep downstairs that night, wrapped in blankets behind the finished cases in the storeroom, and Marta would sit with her, and the loft would stand empty and innocent, and in the morning they would decide more.

It was nearly eleven when Felix heard the carriages.

Not one carriage. Several. Wheels on frozen mud, the muffled iron rhythm of hooves, and beneath it all a sound she had never heard in that quiet lane before and would never stop hearing after: the soft, disciplined clatter of many men discharging from many vehicles at once, the sound of an operation, planned elsewhere, arriving here.

She was at the workbench with the striking train open before her, and her body understood before her mind did. Her hands were moving—closing the bench lid, sweeping the loose duplicate plates into the shavings drawer—before the first boot hit the step.

"Herr Brandt!" she called, low and sharp, up the stairs, and then, to the storeroom, "the back!"

There was no back door worth the name; there was only the coal hatch into the alley, which Marta had widened for herself years ago without asking anyone's leave, because Marta widens things. Felix heard the storeroom bolt scrape, heard the whisper of blankets, of a child gathered up mid-sleep, and then the particular silence of people making themselves into shadows.

The knocking, when it came, was civil. That was somehow the most obscene part of it. Three measured raps, and a voice pitched for politeness: "Herr Brandt. Imperial commission. Open the door, if you please."

Brandt crossed the shop at his own pace, unhurried, an old man answering his own door at his own hour, and Felix marveled even then at the craft of it—fear beaten into dignity, hammered thin as leaf gold. He unbarred the door.

They came in fast anyway, civility discarded at the threshold like a coat. Eight soldiers in gray, rifles, boots leaving wet prints across forty years of clean floor, and behind them Kessler in a greatcoat buttoned to the throat, hat dripping, his face composed into the expression Felix knew best of all his faces: regret performing diligence.

"Gentlemen." Brandt's voice did not shake. "You are damaging my floor."

"Forgive us, Herr Brandt." Kessler removed his hat. Water ran off its brim onto the boards, and he seemed genuinely sorry about it. "We seek a missing person. A child, approximately six years of age, believed to have been taken in from the street unlawfully. We have reason to search these premises tonight."

"On what warrant?"

Kessler produced the paper with a flourish of genuine bureaucratic tenderness, and Felix read the signature upside down from across the room: a magistrate's name, but not Ehrlich's. Albrecht's reach, then, extended even into the benches of the law—or at least into the hours of the night when honest magistrates slept.

"Search, then," Brandt said, and stepped aside, and added, in the tone of a man surrendering a point in an argument about hinges, "mind the regulator on the wall. It is older than your Emperor."

What followed lasted forty minutes and felt geological. Felix stood at her bench with her hands visible and empty, heart slamming so hard she feared it would sound against the wood like a dropped weight, while soldiers went through her life with rifle-butts and lanterns. They opened the tool drawers and found tools. They opened the scrap bins and found scrap. Two of them climbed to the loft, and Felix listened—she would always listen afterward, in dreams, to the sound of their boots crossing the boards above her head, directly over the spot where Lena's bed stood empty, where the cold hollow of her small sleeping body still held its shape in the straw tick.

One of them called down: "Bed here! Child-sized!"

"She lodges with the seamstress's niece on Sundays," Marta called up sweetly from the storeroom doorway, arms folded, chin high, a woman interrupted at her mending, a lamp lit behind her, and behind the lamp-rack, behind the finished tall cases with their weighted pendulums silent as held breath, a bundle of blankets that did not stir. "The child is at her aunt's tonight, gentlemen, and has been since supper. You may verify at number eleven, though you'll wake the whole house and learn nothing but that the aunt snores."

It was beautiful. It was flawless. Felix could have kissed her.

Kessler looked at Marta for a long moment, and then at Felix, and Felix gave him back exactly what the situation required: the mild, put-upon face of an apprentice dragged from his bed, hands ink-stained, shoulders slumped, a boy with nothing anywhere on him or near him.

"The plates, then," Kessler said, turning, and the casualness of it was the confession. He had not expected to find the child. Or he had expected the child and the plates both, and the child's absence had cost him nothing he cared about. "Anton Voss's papers. We understand documents of the late clerk may have passed through this establishment."

"I repair clocks," said Felix. "If papers pass through, they pass through other pockets than mine."

"Your master disagrees with you historically, I think." Kessler drifted along the bench, gloved finger hovering above the wood without touching, and stopped at the shavings drawer. Stopped. Rested his hand on its brass pull.

And Felix understood, in one vertical drop of ice down her spine, that this drawer was why they had come tonight—that Tobias had told them not merely of a hidden child but of a hidden drawer, or that Kessler simply knew drawers, knew workmen, knew where hands go when they want something out of sight—and that the loose duplicate plates were eight inches beneath that gloved hand, under brass turnings and steel dust, waiting.

"Mind the turnings," she heard herself say, pleasantly. "They bite."

Kessler smiled at her. He drew the drawer.

Brass shavings spilled silver over the lantern light. Steel dust. Broken pivots. The ordinary debris of honest work, and among it, if one knew what to look for, the faint wrongness of engraved lines where file-marks should be—but the light was bad, and Kessler's eye was a bureaucrat's eye, and he stirred the shavings once with one finger, and closed the drawer.

"Nothing," he said, to no one, to the record, to whoever watched him from above.

Nothing. The word landed in Felix like a bell-strike.

"Then we've disturbed your household for naught," Kessler said, replacing his hat. "Except—" and here he paused at the door, and the pause was surgical, "—that Herr Brandt will accompany us. Questions of documentation. The licensing of apprentices, the residency of persons on premises, irregularities of that nature. Merely administrative. He'll be returned within the day, I should think."

The shop went absolutely still. Even the soldiers stopped shifting.

"I am seventy-one years old," Brandt said, "and my bed is upstairs, and my shop is open at dawn regardless of who sleeps in it. If your administration requires me, it knows where I keep my clocks, and it will find me punctual."

"Herr Brandt." Kessler's voice gentled into something that was almost, horribly, kind. "It was not a request."

Two soldiers took his elbows—not roughly, that wasn't the style tonight—and Brandt went with them, upright, furious in the old quiet way, pausing only at the threshold to say, in the exact voice he used for closing the shop, "Halloran. The regulator. Mind the beat error; it drifts in cold."

*Yes, master.* The answer rose in her throat and died there, and the door closed, and the carriages rolled, and the lane swallowed them, and Felix stood in the wreckage-light of scattered lanterns and open drawers and forty years of a life turned over like a pocket, and discovered that she was shaking so violently she had to grip the bench to remain standing.

* * *

They put the shop half to rights in silence. There was nothing else to do with their hands, and their hands needed occupation desperately, because the alternative was thinking. Ivo righted the overturned stool and reset the fallen regulator and stood before it adjusting the pendulum with tiny, unnecessary touches, keeping time with the only heart in the room that still worked. Marta sat on the stairs with her face in her apron, and from behind the finished cases came the small, sleep-thickened voice of a child asking, twice, why everyone was so quiet, and being told, twice, that it was only the storm.

Felix stood at the window and watched the snow fill the carriage tracks, erasing them, as if the lane itself were complicit.

Guilt came first, because guilt is always first. *They took him because of me.* Because five years ago a frightened girl had walked in off the road with her brother's name in her mouth, and instead of turning her out—an act that would have been prudent, that any sensible man would have performed—old Brandt had handed her an apron and a bench and a life, and had said nothing, ever, to anyone, and tonight the silence had finally presented its bill. Seventy-one years old, marched into a wet night by soldiers, for her. For the secret he had chosen. For the child she had refused to forget.

Then fury came, and fury was worse, because fury had addresses. Tobias's lodging was over the saddlery, four doors up. She could see its lit window from where she stood. Thirty gulden. She could picture the transaction entire: the coffeehouse, the lowered voice, the clerk's Treasury-cut coat, Tobias telling himself it was nothing, information only, no harm done, a child and some papers, who could that hurt?

"Halloran." Marta's hand on her arm. Felix hadn't heard her cross the room. "Whatever you're planning, plan it with your eyes open."

"He sold us."

"Yes."

"He's four doors up. I could—"

"Do what? Beat the truth out of him? Wake the lane? Give them the riot they're hoping for, the excuse?" Marta's grip tightened. "Listen to me. They didn't find her. They didn't find the plates. And they took the one man in this city whose arrest makes *them* look like fools—because Brandt has friends, whatever he pretends, and Kessler knows it, and Kessler took him precisely because he's frightened and needs a hostage. Which means Brandt is alive, and means to stay alive, and will tell them nothing, because he's a stubborn old vulture. Do you hear me? He taught you everything. Teach yourself this: fury is fuel, not steering."

From the corner, Ivo struck the slate with his knuckle. Felix turned. He had wiped it clean and written one line, large, in his engraver's capitals:

**NINE DAYS BECAME ONE NIGHT. WE FINISH THE PLATES TONIGHT.**

And beneath it, smaller, after a moment's thought:

**AND SEND TO EHRLICH BEFORE DAWN.**

Felix looked at the slate a long time. Then past it, to the storeroom, where Lena's face had emerged from behind the tall case—pale, watchful, awake after all, taking in the ruined shop with the terrible adaptability of children—and Felix thought of her father hiding truth inside a clock, and of an old man walking out his own door between soldiers with his spine straight as a pendulum rod, and of the equinox demonstration now seven days distant, with Ehrlich invited and Albrecht forewarned and the master clock's striking train fitted and waiting, its compartment timed to open like a mouth before all Vienna.

They had come for the plates and missed them. They had come for the child and missed her. So they had taken the man instead, believing him the weakest beam in the structure.

They had misjudged which beam.

"Light the lamps," Felix said. Her voice came out steady, and she was grateful to it, because nothing else in her was. "All of them. Ivo, the duplicates—every page finished by morning. Marta, the boy at number eleven keeps a fast horse and owes you hems; he rides to Ehrlich tonight, tonight, before Kessler can build a story around an old man's absence. And I—"

She stopped. Down the lane, at the lit window above the saddlery, a shadow moved behind the curtain: a broad-shouldered silhouette, pacing. Unable to sleep either. Watching, perhaps, for the carriages to come back—for the second payment, or the first consequence.

"I'm going to learn," Felix said softly, "exactly what my master is being asked, and exactly what Tobias told them, and exactly what Kessler intends to trade him back for. Because Brandt walked out that door for us." She took her coat from its hook, and her cap, and pulled the brim low over her eyes, becoming in three small motions the boy nobody looked at twice. "And I intend to walk him back in."
