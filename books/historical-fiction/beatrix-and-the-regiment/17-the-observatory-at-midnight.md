## 17. The Observatory at Midnight

The observatory at midnight was a different country from the observatory by day.

By day it belonged to the astronomers—men in wool coats who moved through the great dome's shadow murmuring about declinations and transit times, their voices hushed not out of reverence for the instruments but because loudness seemed somehow unscientific. By day the master clock was a public thing, admired, consulted, its brass pendulum swinging its patient arc behind glass while students copied its corrections into notebooks.

At night it belonged to Felix.

She had the key now—the astronomer Ertl had pressed it into her hand ten days ago with the weary trust of a man who had stopped asking why his clock kept failing and started praying only that someone could make it stop. *You are the fourth clockmaker we've summoned,* he'd said. *You are the first who looked at the escapement as though it were a wound rather than a nuisance.*

She climbed the tower stairs with her tool roll under her arm and a satchel against her ribs that weighed nothing and everything: eleven engraved plates wrapped in oilcloth, each one thin enough to hide inside a coat lining, each one heavy enough to hang a director of the Imperial Treasury. Ivo had finished cutting the duplicates on Tuesday. The originals sat in her satchel now, riding against her heartbeat, going home to the place Anton Voss had chosen for them before he died.

The lamp at the top of the stairs burned low. She turned up the wick, and the light swelled across the stone floor, across the clock's open case, across the tools she had laid out in rows so precise they looked like a surgical theater awaiting the patient. She hung her coat on the peg by the window. Below, Vienna lay spread out in its winter dark—a scatter of lamps, a river of black glass, chimney smoke rising straight in the windless cold. Somewhere down there was the workshop, and Marta awake over her sewing, and Lena asleep in the loft with Ivo's spare blanket pulled to her chin. Somewhere down there, in a cell that smelled of wet straw, was Herr Brandt, who had told her once that a clockmaker's whole duty was to make sure the truth of the hours got told no matter who disliked hearing them struck.

*They will ask what you were doing there at midnight,* she imagined him saying, and then corrected herself: he would never say it that way. He would say, *Mind your hand positions. A slip at this stage costs you everything you've already paid for.* Practical to the bone. Loving in the only grammar he trusted.

Felix rolled up her sleeves and went to work.

* * *

The striking train was the heart of the sabotage—or rather, the heart of everything, if Voss had been the craftsman of secrets Felix believed him to be.

She had understood it three nights ago, sitting cross-legged on the workshop floor at two in the morning with the cipher pages spread around her like fallen leaves, when the shape of the mechanism finally assembled itself in her mind the way a melody assembles after you have heard it broken into pieces all day. The clock's strike train released a hammer onto a bell at the top of every hour. The release was governed by a locking plate—a wheel of notches, one notch per hour, twelve notches around, turning once through its full revolution between noon and midnight. It was the most public part of the clock's soul. Every hour it spoke. Every hour the whole city within earshot heard it speak.

And it had room inside it. Room that no one had ever needed, until someone did.

Voss had not hidden his plates in the case, where any repairman might find them. He had machined the locking plate itself with a second function—a spring-loaded inner sleeve, a compartment that would only open when the train ran through its complete cycle, when the twelfth hour struck and the wheel came home to noon. A hiding place that revealed itself exactly once a day, briefly, mechanically, and only if you knew to watch. She had found the traces of it the first week: hairline seams in the brass, a pin that served no purpose she could name, a countersunk screw whose slot had never known a screwdriver. Sabotage had nearly buried it. Whoever had wrecked the clock—Kessler's men, or Albrecht's; the distinction hardly mattered anymore—had torn at the escapement hoping to destroy whatever slept there, but they had been butchers, not clockmakers. They had broken what they could see and missed what they couldn't.

Now Felix had rebuilt all of it, and improved upon Voss's design in one respect that made her hands shake slightly even thinking about it: she had arranged the mechanism so the compartment would open during the demonstration itself.

Ertl had announced the equinox demonstration weeks ago—an occasion for patrons and university men, the restored master clock to be presented running true, striking the hour before an invited company. Albrecht's own office had confirmed the Director would attend. That detail had arrived like a gift wrapped in threat: *he will be standing beneath it.*

At noon on equinox day, the clock would strike twelve. The train would run its full revolution. And the locking plate would open—not for a breath, as Voss had built it, but for six full seconds, long enough for every eye in the room to see what rose out of the brass on a small lifting platform of her own devising. Six seconds. She had timed it against her pulse a hundred times. Six seconds was longer than a man needs to read a seal. Longer than a magistrate needs to recognize stolen paper. Shorter than a director of the treasury needs to reach the case and stop it—if he even dared, with a hundred witnesses watching him lunge at a clock.

She worked now with the lamp pulled close, fitting the final component: the lift platform, a shallow brass tray no larger than a playing card, mounted on a leaf spring that would carry the plates upward when the sleeve released. She had cut it from stock Ivo had given her without being asked, and she had polished it to a mirror finish, because if the truth of Anton Voss was going to rise out of a machine, it would rise cleanly, brightly, unmistakably. No smudged, half-legible confession. A presentation. Craftsmanship as testimony—every surface true, every fit exact, nothing about it deniable as accident.

Her fingers knew the work and did not tremble. It was only her mind that trembled, and minds, she reminded herself, could be set like escapements: regulated, damped, made to keep honest time.

* * *

She lost track of the hours the way she always did at the bench. The lamp burned down twice and she refilled it twice, and once she stopped to eat the bread and cold sausage Marta had packed, chewing mechanically while staring at the strike train's assembly drawing pinned to the case door. The stars wheeled past the observatory slit above her. Somewhere below, a watchman called the third hour, then the fourth, his voice thin and cracked with cold, and each call landed in her chest like a small hammer: *four o'clock and all is well.* She wondered who had decided the watchmen should announce that. She wondered whether any city had ever actually been well at four in the morning, or whether the words were simply the oldest lie in Europe, repeated nightly until it felt like comfort.

At some point she caught herself whispering to the clock.

"You'll speak tomorrow," she told it, pressing the last retaining screw home with a driver worn smooth from five years of Herr Brandt's grip before it ever touched hers. "You've spoken every hour since before either of us was born, and no one listened, because everyone already knows what a clock will say. But tomorrow—" she seated the screw, checked it, checked it again— "tomorrow you'll say something new."

The mechanism answered with silence, which was the correct answer. A finished clock, properly built, said nothing until the moment appointed. All its truth was held in tension. She understood this now in a way she hadn't five years ago, when she had come to Brandt's workshop with her brother's name in her mouth and her sisterhood folded away like a garment too dangerous to wear. She had thought concealment was a kind of death. Now she knew better: concealment was a mainspring. Everything hidden gathered force. The question—the whole moral question of her life—was what the release mechanism would be, and whether it would fire true.

Hers had almost failed. Five days ago, in Albrecht's warm study, with legal papers bearing her own true name spread on the desk before her like a banquet, she had stood at the very edge of accepting a lifetime of beautiful silence. *Beatrix Halloran, apprentice, papers in order.* She could still feel the pull of it, gravity-sure, the oldest want she owned. And she could still feel Marta's voice afterward, quiet as a needle going through cloth: *Invisibility saved you, child. It will not save her. You cannot hide a child inside a disguise forever. Sooner or later someone must stand in the open and say: this happened, I saw it, I remember.*

So: tomorrow. The open. Whatever it cost.

She wiped down the plates one final time—Voss's originals, his small fierce clerk's handwriting marching across the ruled lines, ledger columns proving that seized estates had flowed through false accounts into Director Albrecht's keeping, page after page initialed *A.V.* in a hand that would never initial anything again. Eleven plates. She laid them on the lift platform in the order Ivo had determined, first page uppermost, and secured them with the two brass clips designed to hold them steady against the hammer-fall shock of the striking train. Then she closed the sleeve, reset the spring, and rotated the train slowly by hand, watching the whole sequence perform: the wheel turning, the notches passing, the twelfth hour arriving, the sleeve releasing, the platform rising smooth as bread rising from an oven, the plates ascending into view—

—and holding. Steady. Bright under the lamp. Legible from three paces, which meant legible from thirty.

Felix sat back on her heels and let out a breath she felt she had been holding since the night she found the child on the observatory steps.

*Forget this face*, the note had said, in Albrecht's elegant, murderous hand. Well. Tomorrow the face would be remembered, along with everything else. Not by her alone—that was the point, the whole architecture of it. Memory hoarded in one skull could be silenced with one arrest. Memory distributed among strangers, witnessed by a hundred respectable eyes at once, became something else entirely: fact. History. The one thing Albrecht's money could not buy back.

* * *

She was packing her tools when she heard the footsteps on the stair—slow, uneven, a limp she knew better than her own heartbeat.

"Herr Brandt." She was on her feet before she finished saying it.

He filled the doorway of the stairwell, gray-faced, thinner than he had been nine days ago, his right wrist bound in a splinted bandage and his coat smelling of the cell they had finally released him from at dusk—released, Kessler had made clear, on conditions that amounted to nothing more than a leash of courtesy. Behind him, ducking under the lintel, came Ivo, carrying a lantern and a look of exhausted vigilance, and behind Ivo, wrapped in Marta's good shawl despite the hour and the climb, a small figure in a nightdress and boots.

Lena crossed the floor at a run and hit Felix at knee height, arms clamping around her legs.

"You didn't come home," Lena said, with the terrible precision of six-year-olds. "Marta said you'd be here all night. I made her bring me."

"I had to finish," Felix said. Her voice came out rougher than intended. She knelt, ignoring the protest in her stiff knees, and took the girl's face in both hands. Cold cheeks, sleep-warmed eyes, a smudge of ash on one ear from falling asleep against the workshop stove. "I finished."

"Is it fixed?"

"It's fixed." Felix glanced up at Brandt, who was looking at the open clock case with an expression she had never seen on him before—not the appraiser's squint, not the teacher's critical patience, but something older and rawer, the face of a man standing at his brother's grave. "It will run true," she said. "Better than true. It will say what it has to say."

Brandt limped closer. With his uninjured hand he touched the case, the way another man might touch a shoulder. "Nine days I sat in that cell," he said quietly, "telling myself they'd taken me because it was cheaper than taking you. Keeping myself sane with arithmetic. How many hours till equinox. How many turns of that wheel." His eyes came to hers. "Show me."

She showed him. She walked him through the whole mechanism with the lamp held high—the rebuilt escapement, the repaired train, the sleeve, the spring, the platform—and when she demonstrated the release by hand, the plates rising gleaming into the lamplight, Brandt stared at them for a long time without speaking. When he finally did, his voice was gravel.

"Josef would have loved this," he said. "He always said the best hiding place is inside the thing everyone trusts." He cleared his throat. "It's good work, apprentice." A pause, deliberate as a placed screw. "*Partner*."

The word went through her like warmth through frozen joints. She had no answer ready. She bowed her head briefly, which was the only acknowledgment either of them needed.

Ivo, who had been circling the case with his engraver's eye, tapped the glass and signed rapidly: *Albrecht knows the demonstration date?*

"He confirmed attendance himself," Felix said. "His office sent the reply Tuesday."

*Then he's coming to stop it,* Ivo signed, blunt as a chisel. *Or to stand close enough that stopping it looks like protection.*

"That's Magistrate Ehrlich's problem," Brandt said. "He'll be in that room. He has the duplicates' existence sworn in writing, lodged with a notary Monday. If Albrecht destroys the originals, the copies convict him anyway. If he lets the clock speak—" he shrugged, wincing at his splinted wrist— "then God help him, because the law finally will."

Lena tugged Felix's sleeve. "What does the clock say?" she asked. "Tomorrow?"

Felix considered how to answer. She thought of the note on the steps, of the word *forget* written in a beautiful hand, of this small girl drilled by a dying father with phrases she hadn't understood until a deaf man's eyes unlocked them. She thought of her own five years of forgetting, chosen and enforced, and how much of Beatrix Halloran still remained to be dug out like a mechanism rusted shut.

"The clock says *remember*," Felix said. "That's all it's ever said. We just weren't listening."

Lena considered this with enormous seriousness, then nodded once, satisfied, and leaned her head against Felix's shoulder.

They stayed a while, the four of them—or five, counting the clock—in the lamplit dome, and then Brandt said dawn was coming and clocks deserved rest before their wedding day, and they descended together, Ivo leading with the lantern, Lena's hand in Felix's, Brandt's uneven tread marking the rhythm of the stairs.

At the bottom, in the blue-gray cold before sunrise, Felix looked back once at the tower. At the top of it, behind stone and glass, the master clock ticked on through its final night of silence, every wheel loaded, every spring coiled, holding eleven pages of a dead man's courage in its brass heart, waiting for noon.

She squeezed Lena's hand and turned toward the lane, toward the workshop, toward Marta and the christening gown and the last few hours of hiding.

Behind them, faint in the freezing air, the clock began to strike five—and Felix found she was counting every stroke, the way she would count every hour between now and the moment the world finally heard what it had been telling them all along.
