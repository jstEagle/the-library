## 20. Her Own Name

The night before the tribunal, Beatrix did not sleep. She sat at the workbench with Lena asleep against her shoulder, the girl's breath warm and even through the coarse wool of Marta's borrowed shawl, and she disassembled in her mind—gear by gear, tooth by tooth—the thing she would have to do come morning.

It was not the plates. The plates were safe; Ivo had confirmed it twice, once with his hands and once with his eyes, tracing the duplicate engravings he had cut so finely that under lamplight even Brandt could not tell original from copy without the secret mark. It was not Albrecht's lawyers, though they would be many and silk-mouthed. It was not even Kessler, who had kept his bargain so far and sat somewhere in the city tonight wearing his fear like an ill-fitting coat.

It was the name.

Magistrate Ehrlich had come to the workshop after dark, when the shutters were closed and the fire banked low. He was a narrow man with ink-stained cuffs and the patient voice of someone who had spent thirty years listening to men lie. He laid out the procedure plainly, because he believed—rightly—that plainness was a kindness.

"The ledger must be authenticated," he said. "Voss is dead. His clerk is dead or fled. The cipher, the engraver's testimony—all of it supports the claim, but the law requires a living chain. Someone who can swear to where the documents were found, how they were preserved, who handled them. You, Fräulein—" he paused there, deliberately, having already been told "—you are that chain."

"Herr Halloran," Brandt corrected, too quickly.

Ehrlich did not look at him. "The tribunal will ask why a clockmaker's apprentice—a young man of no family, no standing—took custody of evidence implicating the Director of the Imperial Treasury. They will ask why he concealed it for months. And when the questions exhaust themselves, someone will dig into who Felix Halloran actually is." He folded his hands. "I would rather you tell them first. Truth offered is armor. Truth excavated is a wound."

Beatrix had looked down at her hands then, at the calluses five years of files and lathes had built over the softer hands she'd been born with, and she had thought: *I have been maintaining myself like a clock. Oiled, regulated, keeping another woman's time.*

"I'll testify as myself," she said.

Brandt had stood up so fast his stool scraped like something breaking. He said nothing—he was a man whose anger always arrived ahead of his words—but his face said everything: *they will take the apprenticeship from you, they will take the guild, they may take more.* And underneath all of it, plainly visible if one knew how to read him as she did: *I cannot lose another person.*

"Her name is Beatrix Halloran," Ehrlich said quietly, writing it down. "Daughter of Josef Halloran, cloth merchant, of Pest."

"Daughter of a ruined man," Beatrix said. "But yes. That's the name."

Now it was nearly four in the morning, and Lena stirred against her shoulder and murmured something in her sleep—a fragment of her father's litany, perhaps, the numbers drilled into her small head like coins sewn into a hem. *Seven, three, eleven.* Beatrix kissed the top of her head and thought about what it meant, that a six-year-old had carried the truth inside her body for months while grown men killed to keep it out of the world, and that tomorrow Beatrix would have to do the same thing: open herself up in public and trust that the mechanism held.

Marta came down the loft ladder just before dawn, hair unbound, carrying two cups of chicory. She set one beside Beatrix without comment and stood looking out the frosted window at the lane.

"You haven't slept."

"Neither have you."

"I slept enough for a woman my age. Which is to say, badly." Marta sat on the bench across from her, and for a long moment neither spoke. Then Marta said, "I dressed Voss's widow in black. Do you remember me telling you? I remember every stitch of that gown. I remember thinking, *this poor woman, and no one will ever tell her the whole truth of it.*" Her eyes went to Lena. "Tomorrow somebody tells her. Let it be done properly."

"And if doing it properly costs me everything?"

"Then you'll still have your hands," Marta said. "And we'll still have this shop. And she'll still have us." She reached over and took Lena's small foot, sticking out from the blanket, and squeezed it gently. "Family isn't the thing that requires you to disappear, girl. I learned that at forty. You're learning it at twenty-three. You're ahead of schedule."

* * *

The tribunal convened in the Landstraße assizes hall, a chamber designed to make individuals feel like insects—vaulted ceiling, imperial eagle in gilt, windows set high enough that light fell in cold columns rather than warmth. Benches filled with clerks and curiosity-seekers; word of the equinox demonstration had leaked despite every effort, and Vienna's appetite for scandal was bottomless. Albrecht's advocates occupied the front table like a row of well-fed crows, and beyond them, flanked by two guards but not manacled—the indignity of that restraint reserved, apparently, for lesser men—sat Director Albrecht himself.

He was smaller than Beatrix had imagined. She had spent months building him into a colossus, a force of nature like weather or debt, and here he was only a heavy-jawed man of fifty-odd in an excellent coat, watching the room with the flat patience of someone who had never once doubted the outcome of anything.

Until, perhaps, now. His eyes found hers across the chamber, and she understood with a jolt that he knew what she was about to do. Kessler had told her as much: *He reads every file. He has known what you are since the second week, and let you work anyway, because he assumed fear would finish what money started.*

She walked the length of the aisle with Lena's hand in hers. There had been argument about this—Ehrlich suggesting gently that a child might be better left with Marta in the gallery—but Lena had taken Beatrix's sleeve that morning and said, in her careful, hoarded voice, "Papa said the numbers go where the people can see them. I'm coming." No one had argued further.

Brandt waited at the witness barrier. He looked terrible—two weeks in custody had grayed him around the temples and taught him a new stillness, a wariness in how he held his shoulders—but when Beatrix and Lena reached him, he did something he had never done in five years: he put his hand briefly, heavily, on Beatrix's head, the way a father blesses a child, and said, loud enough for the front benches to hear, "Steady as a escapement, girl. Strike true."

The presiding magistrate called her.

*Felix Halloran,* the clerk began to write, and Beatrix stood, and felt the whole room lean toward her the way water leans toward a breach.

"My name," she said, and her voice surprised her—it came out level, pitched like a struck bell, carrying to the high cold windows, "is Beatrix Halloran. Daughter of Josef Halloran, cloth merchant of Pest. My brother was Felix Halloran. He died of fever in the winter of 1841, the same season my father's debts destroyed us. In the spring of 1842, I put on my dead brother's clothes, took his papers, and came to Vienna, and I have lived and worked as Felix ever since, because a woman alone with no fortune has exactly two choices in this empire, and I chose the one that let me keep my hands."

The chamber erupted—not loudly, not all at once, but in the way of a clockwork reaching some critical tension: rustles, indrawn breaths, a lawyer's chair scraping back, Albrecht's lead advocate rising with his mouth already open. The presiding magistrate hammered for order, and in the ringing pause that followed, Beatrix heard Lena's hand tighten in hers, and she looked down, and the girl looked up, and there was nothing in her face but absolute, uncomplicated trust, and Beatrix thought: *five years of silence, spent. Every coin of it. And it buys this.*

* * *

The examination took most of the day.

They tried, first, to break her on the disguise. Albrecht's advocate—his name was Dr. Fenner, and he had the smooth relentless quality of a man who had never been told no by anyone who mattered—walked her through it with surgical courtesy. When did she begin the deception. Who else knew. Had she defrauded the guild. Had she defrauded Herr Brandt specifically. Was she aware that perjury carried a sentence of—

"The apprentice indenture names Felix Halloran," Beatrix said. "Felix Halloran exists. He is documented, examined, and certified. I am the same person, performing the same labor, with the same hands. If the crown wishes to charge someone with fraud, it may charge the disguise—and I will answer for it gladly, once this tribunal has finished asking whether the Director of the Treasury arranged the murder of a clerk."

That landed. She watched it land—in the shift of Fenner's shoulders, in the way several clerks stopped writing to look at Albrecht, who himself did not move at all, which was its own kind of confession.

They moved to the evidence. Here Beatrix was precise, because precision was the one language she had mastered completely. She described the sabotage of the observatory clock—the filed escapement teeth, the loosened barrel—described in such detail that the tribunal's own expert witness, summoned from the Polytechnic Institute, was made to confirm each point with a nod. She described finding the compartment behind the striking train, the oilcloth packet, the coded pages signed *A.V.* She described the months of concealment without apology and without flourish: "I hid the evidence because the last person known to have touched it was dead in the Danube canal, and the note pinned to a child abandoned at the clock's door read *forget this face.* I concluded that forgetting was not, in this matter, a virtue."

Someone in the gallery laughed—one short, startled bark—and was silenced, but the laugh had done its work. Rooms are governed by laughter the way clocks are governed by weights.

Then Fenner produced his counterstroke, and it was the one Beatrix had been waiting for since midnight.

"The documents," he said, approaching the exhibit table with reverent contempt, "are paper. Paper can be forged. Your engraver produces copies for a living, does he not? We are asked to convict a director of the Imperial Treasury on the say-so of a woman who has admitted to five years of sustained impersonation, relying on documents that surfaced from a machine she alone controlled." He turned to the bench, arms spread. "Where is the chain of custody? Where is anything but the word of a confessed deceiver?"

Ehrlich rose. "The prosecution calls upon the witness to establish authenticity by means available to her."

And this was the hinge. This was the gear-tooth on which everything turned. Because the cipher's final page—the page Lena's drilled recitation had unlocked—contained not merely figures but a protocol, written in Voss's own cramped hand: instructions for verification. A challenge and response. Questions only Voss and his chosen custodian would know, cross-referenced against entries in the sealed registry of seized estates at the Hofkammer archive—entries whose true figures existed nowhere but in the originals Voss had hidden, and in the memory of a dead man's six-year-old daughter.

"The ledger's seventh leaf," Beatrix said, "records the disposition of the Wolkensdorf estate, seized from Baron von Ries in March of 1844. The official registry entry states a purchase price of forty-one thousand gulden paid to the estate's creditors. The true price—recorded here, in Voss's cipher, and confirmed in every particular by his daughter's testimony—is twenty-two thousand, with nineteen thousand diverted through the Meridian Credit Association under accounts designated by the letters K.A. The discrepancy is verifiable against the creditors' own receipts, which exist independently of any document I could have forged, because the creditors were paid in full and were never parties to the theft." She looked at Fenner. "Your client stole from the men he robbed twice, Doctor. Once their land, and once the difference. The baron's agents in Pressburg hold their receipts still. Send for them."

The tribunal sent for them. There was a recess of three hours, agonizing, spent in a stone corridor where Lena ate half a bread roll and fed the other half to pigeons with the solemnity of a girl performing a sacrament, while Brandt stood against the wall with his eyes closed and his lips moving in what might have been prayer or arithmetic.

When the court reassembled, a courier's satchel lay on the bench before the magistrates, and a Pressburg attorney with side-whiskers and an expression of vindictive joy testified for forty minutes about receipts, and figures, and a baron who had sworn for three years that he had been robbed and been called a liar for it.

Beatrix watched Albrecht during that testimony. He did not look at the attorney. He did not look at the documents. He looked at Kessler—who sat in the well of the court under guard, pale, having given his own testimony that morning in a voice like a man reading his own autopsy—and Albrecht's face performed, slowly, the entire collapse of a worldview: disbelief, calculation, the frantic search for leverage, and then, arriving last and settling deepest, the understanding that there was none. That the mechanism he had built had a compartment in it he had never inspected, and it had opened on schedule, in public, and it could not be closed again.

* * *

They recalled her once more, near evening, to authenticate the final page formally—her oath, her signature, entered into the record against the exhibit.

"And the record should show your name as?" the presiding magistrate asked. It was not hostility; it was formality, giving her, perhaps deliberately, one last door out.

Beatrix stood. Lena stood beside her, because she had refused the gallery all afternoon, and Beatrix's hand closed around the small fingers and held on.

"Beatrix Halloran," she said. "Born the eleventh of September, 1824, in Pest. Daughter of Josef and Anna Halloran. Sister of Felix." Her voice held. She had expected it to shake—she had rehearsed trembling for days, braced for it the way one braces for a blow—and instead it rang clear in the vaulted dark, steady as a train of wheels releasing their stored power all at once, exactly, beautifully, on time. "For five years I testified to a lie every day of my life simply by being seen. Today the record gets the truth, and the truth can cost whatever it likes. I have already survived losing everything once. I am told it makes the second occasion easier."

The clerk wrote. The pen scratched like a small animal in the silence, and then the presiding magistrate said, "The tribunal accepts the authentication. The exhibits stand as evidence." And then, off the record, in the margin of procedure where humanity occasionally survives: "The court notes the witness's service to this investigation and recommends—formally recommends—that any question of her apprenticeship be resolved with corresponding gratitude."

Gratitude. Beatrix walked out of the well past Albrecht's table, past the crows packing their papers in hurried silence, past Kessler meeting her eyes with something that was not quite apology and not quite warning, and she thought: *it will not be enough. Men like him have courts of their own.* But the thought was lighter than it had been for months, because it was no longer her burden alone; it was written down now, witnessed, sealed in the record, part of the machinery of the world.

In the corridor, dusk coming blue through the tall windows, Lena tugged her hand.

"That man," Lena said, pointing back toward the courtroom doors, where guards were escorting Albrecht out under closer custody than he'd entered. "He used to come to our house. Papa always poured the wine himself, and stood up when he did."

"Yes," Beatrix said. "I expect he did."

"Papa said the numbers go where the people can see them." Lena considered the closing doors, the sound of bolts, the whole vast apparatus of the state grinding slowly toward whatever justice it could be persuaded to carry. Then she looked up at Beatrix, and her chin lifted in a way that was pure Anton Voss, and something in Beatrix's chest turned over like a great wheel beginning to move. "You stood up," Lena said. "So I saw."

They came out onto the steps together into the cold, where Marta waited with the carriage, and Brandt, and Ivo, and where—apart from the others, hatless in the wind, with the look of a man who had run much of the way from the Hofburg—a junior clerk of the Magistrat was scanning the dispersing crowd, searching faces, a folded paper in his fist.

He found Ehrlich first, then Beatrix, and hurried over, breathless, holding the paper out.

"From the Magistrat," he said. "Priority seal. It concerns the prisoner Brandt's case—or—" he glanced at the paper again, and his throat worked, "—no. Forgive me. It concerns the observatory. There's been a fire report at midnight, and a break-in at the tower, and the master clock—" he swallowed— "the master clock is missing its striking train."
