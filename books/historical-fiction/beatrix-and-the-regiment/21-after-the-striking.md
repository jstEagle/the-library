## 21. After the Striking

The cell smelled of straw gone sour and the iron patience of a city that had held prisoners for four hundred years. Herr Gottlieb Brandt had spent six days in it, and he had learned what he suspected he would learn: that a man's fear does not leave him in darkness, but sits down beside him like an old friend who has been waiting for an invitation.

He had expected worse. He had expected Kessler to come at him with lantern-jawed questions and a clerk taking notes, or perhaps not to come at all—expected instead to rot here while Albrecht's money moved through the courts like water moving through gravel, finding every crack. But on the seventh morning, when the bolt drew back and the grey light of a Vienna October fell across the floor, it was not Kessler who stood in the doorway.

It was Magistrate Ehrlich.

He was a narrow man, Ehrlich, with spectacles that caught the light before his face did, and hands folded in front of him as though he were about to examine a witness rather than open a door. Two guards waited behind him in the corridor, but they did not enter.

"Herr Brandt," Ehrlich said. "You are free to go."

Brandt rose slowly. Six days of straw had put an ache into every joint he owned, and he found he was unwilling to show it. "Free," he repeated. "That is a word I have not heard used honestly in this city for some time."

"Then let me use it precisely." Ehrlich stepped aside, gesturing toward the corridor. "Director Albrecht was taken from his house last night. The plates from the observatory clock are in the custody of the court. Clerk Voss's ledgers are being read aloud in chambers as we speak—I am told the reading will take two days. And Inspector Kessler has given a full deposition naming every hand that took Albrecht's coin, including his own."

Brandt stood very still. Somewhere below, in the belly of the building, a door slammed, and the sound traveled up through the stone like a pulse.

"Kessler," Brandt said. "He turned?"

"He turned the way a man turns when the floor gives way beneath him—quickly, and without dignity. But he turned. His testimony is the keystone. Without it, Albrecht might have bought his way out by Christmas. With it—" Ehrlich permitted himself the smallest dry movement of his mouth, which was, Brandt supposed, the man's version of laughter. "With it, the trial will be short, and the verdict will be long remembered."

They walked. The corridor was cold and smelled of lamp oil, and Brandt's legs remembered their business only grudgingly. At the end of it, past a desk where a clerk looked up and then quickly down again, a door opened onto the street, and Vienna poured in—the rattle of cart wheels on cobbles, a woman crying fish prices, the smell of wet stone and roasting chestnuts, all of it so ordinary and so enormous that Brandt stopped on the threshold like a man stepping into a river.

"You understand," Ehrlich said quietly behind him, "what you have purchased with your silence these many years. You kept names. You kept your brother's letters. When the girl came to you with those papers, you said burn them—and then you gave her my name instead. That is not nothing, Herr Brandt. In another year, under another magistrate, it would have been enough to hang you alongside them."

"I know."

"Do you?" Ehrlich's voice remained level, but there was something beneath it, patient as sediment. "Your brother died because he touched treasury business. You spent thirty years teaching yourself that survival meant looking away. And yet when the moment came—the true moment—you handed a frightened apprentice the one honest magistrate you knew. Curious arithmetic."

Brandt looked out at the street. A boy was sweeping leaves against the gutter, and each stroke of the broom seemed to Brandt unbearably beautiful, unbearably ordinary, the whole visible world restored to him like sight after blindness.

"My brother believed the law would come," Brandt said. "It came too late for him. I decided I would not wait for it. I decided waiting was a kind of dying." He paused. "I was wrong about half of it. The law is slow. But it came. It came because a girl refused to stop winding a clock."

Ehrlich considered this. Then: "Go home, clockmaker. There is a child in your workshop who asks every hour whether you have returned, and an apprentice who has not slept since the equinox. I imagine they will want to see you standing upright."

* * *

The workshop in the Werdertorgasse had never looked so small, or so bright.

Brandt stood in the lane outside it for a full minute before going in—not hiding, he told himself, only gathering. Through the window he could see lamplight, and shadows crossing it: Marta's broad silhouette bent over her table; Ivo's stiller shape at the engraving bench; and moving between them, quick and slight, a figure whose walk he had known for five years without ever once allowing himself to know it.

Beatrix Halloran. Felix. The apprentice who was no apprentice at all, and who was, as of yesterday morning, the most talked-about woman in Vienna outside the opera.

He pushed the door open, and the bell above it gave its small worn note, and everything stopped.

Marta dropped her needle. Ivo set down his burin with exaggerated care, as though any sudden movement might shatter the moment. And Lena—the child, Voss's daughter, six years old and grave as a small judge—came off her stool by the fire and crossed the floor at a dead run, throwing her arms around his legs with such force that he staggered and had to catch the doorframe.

"You came back," she announced, into his coat. "She said you would. She said it every single day, even when she didn't believe it, and I could tell she didn't believe it because she did the thing with her mouth."

"Lena," Beatrix said, mortified, from across the room.

"It's true! You do the thing with your mouth!"

Brandt put his hand on the child's head. Her hair was finer than thread, the kind of hair a watchmaker's daughter would have, he thought absurdly, hair like the hairspring of some delicate movement, and his throat closed around whatever he had planned to say first.

He looked up. Beatrix stood by the workbench with her arms crossed—not defensively, he realized, but holding herself together, the way she held herself together always, five years of practice in the gesture. She was thinner than she had been a month ago. There was a bruise-yellow shadow under one eye that had not fully faded from the night of the raid. Her hands, he noticed, were steady.

"Herr Brandt," she said formally.

"Felix," he answered.

Something went out of her shoulders. Not all of it—five years does not leave a body in a day—but something, a plank lifted from a load.

"You look terrible," she said.

"I have been sleeping on government straw."

"They fed you?"

"Bread. A soup whose ingredients remain a state secret."

Marta was already moving, already muttering, already seizing a kettle and a cloth and Brandt's own chair by the stove as though the six days had been a personal affront to her kitchen. Ivo touched Brandt's shoulder once, briefly, in passing—a pressure that said more than the deaf man would ever say aloud—and returned to his bench, though Brandt saw that his hands did not immediately resume their work.

And Lena would not let go of his coat. So Brandt sat down in his chair with a six-year-old attached to his leg, and drank tea too hot, and let the workshop close around him like a hand.

It was late—past midnight, the lamps burned low, Marta asleep in her room above, Ivo gone home to his sister's—when Beatrix finally sat across from him at the bench where she had served her apprenticeship. Between them lay the works of a mantel clock in need of cleaning, laid out on chamois in the old discipline, wheel by wheel, because there was no better way for two clockmakers to talk than with their hands occupied.

"You knew," she said. It was not quite an accusation. "From the beginning. Or near it. The night I came with my brother's papers in my coat."

Brandt selected a pivot and examined it against the lamp. "I knew a girl running from something was sitting in my shop wearing her grief like a borrowed coat. I did not know the particulars."

"Why didn't you ask?"

"Because I had asked questions once before." He set the pivot down. "My brother asked them of the treasury, in writing, politely, with citations. They buried him in a pauper's plot and called it fever. After that I made a religion of not asking. It is not a good religion. But it kept the shop open and it kept me alive, and I told myself that was virtue." He looked at her. "Then a boy walked into my shop with steady hands and empty pockets and eyes like someone who had already survived the worst day of her life, and I hired him inside of ten minutes, and I told myself that was charity."

"And what was it?"

Brandt was quiet a moment. The mantel clock on the shelf ticked its small faithful count.

"Hope," he said. "Disguised, as most things in this shop are. I hoped that if I taught you the trade, the trade would keep you. That gears do not care what name you wear. That a clock is the one honest thing left—that it cannot be bribed, and it cannot lie, and if a person can make one well, that person has made something true in a lying world." He picked up the mainspring barrel, turned it in his fingers. "I did not know I was right until the equinox. Until the clock opened in front of all of them and spoke with Anton Voss's voice."

Beatrix's eyes had gone bright. She looked down at the bench and began, with great concentration, to sort screws.

"The magistrate says the trial begins in nine days," she said. "I have to testify. As myself. Under my own name, in front of the court, in front of—" She stopped. Started again. "Everyone will know. The Halloran name is still mud in certain drawing rooms. My father's creditors' grandchildren will be telling the story at dinner parties for a decade. And the guild—" She laughed, short and bitter. "A woman. A woman ran your bench for five years, and the guild masters will want someone's head, and yours is nearest."

"Yes," Brandt said.

"That's all? Yes?"

"What would you have me say? That I regret it? Sit there." He pointed at the stool she was on. "Every screw you ever fitted in this shop was fitted true. Every escapement you adjusted has kept time within a second a week since. If the guild wants my head for employing the finest apprentice I have trained in forty years, they may take it, and I will tell them at the door that they are fools." He set the barrel down with a click that ended the subject. "But that is not why you are sitting here at midnight sorting screws instead of sleeping."

She was silent for a while. The clock ticked. Outside, far off, a watchman called the hour—two o'clock, all's well—and neither of them believed it entirely, and both of them smiled at the same time, which surprised them both.

"When they arrested Albrecht," Beatrix said finally, "and Kessler confessed, and Ehrlich read us the charges—I thought I would feel finished. Done. Like a clock that has finally struck the hour it was built for." She shook her head. "Instead I feel wound tight. I feel like I'm waiting for the next thing to break. Five years of hiding, and now everyone can see me, and I don't—" Her voice thinned. "I don't entirely remember how to be seen."

"No," Brandt said. "You remember how. You simply haven't practiced."

He rose, joints protesting, and went to the tall cabinet at the back of the shop—the one that held the old papers, the deeds, the dead weight of the business. From the bottom drawer he took a document that crackled like autumn. He had drafted it six days ago in his cell, on the backs of two prison forms, in pencil, and had recopied it this afternoon in ink with a steadier hand than he had expected of himself.

He laid it on the bench between the sorted screws and the lamp.

Beatrix read it. He watched her read it—watched her lips move slightly over the words, watched her reach the middle of the page and go very still.

"*This instrument certifies,*" she read aloud, barely above a whisper, "*that Beatrix Anna Halloran has served five years at the bench of Gottlieb Brandt, clockmaker, in all respects equal to a completed apprenticeship, and is hereby admitted as partner in the said establishment, with full share of its profits, debts, and reputation.*" She looked up. "Partner."

"The word is 'partner.' I had the prison chaplain check my spelling."

"Herr Brandt—"

"Gottlieb," he said. "If you are to be a partner, you will use my Christian name, and it will be strange for a month, and then it will not."

"Gottlieb." She tried it like a key in a stiff lock. "Why? Why now? You could wait until after the trial. Until the guild settles. Until it's safe."

"Because I have spent my whole life waiting until things are safe, and it cost me my brother, and it nearly cost me you, and it very nearly cost that child her entire childhood in a loft." He tapped the paper. "Safe is a place clocks go when they stop, Felix. Everything else worth doing happens while the mechanism is still moving. Sign it or don't. But I wrote it in prison, and I mean it, and I am an old man who does not intend to die calling his partner 'the boy.'"

Beatrix Halloran looked at the document for a long moment. Then she took up the pen, signed her true name in a firm hand—her first signature as Beatrix in five years, and it wobbled only a little—and blew on the ink, and pushed it back across the bench.

"There," she said, unsteadily. "Now you're stuck with me."

"I have been stuck with you for five years," Brandt said. "This merely makes it legal."

* * *

They opened the shutters at dawn because Lena demanded it.

"Everyone hangs something," she declared, standing on a stool at the window with the authority of a person who had recently learned that cities celebrated things. "When Papa got his letter from the Director, Mama hung ribbons. Ribbons mean good news. We have good news. Where are our ribbons?"

"We have no ribbons," Marta said from the doorway, arms folded, but her eyes were shining, and Brandt saw that she had known this argument was coming and had already lost it.

So it became, instead, a working morning conducted in plain view of the lane—which was itself the celebration, Beatrix understood, though she said nothing. For five years the shutters had been half-closed when she worked at the front bench. This morning they stood wide open, and the October light came all the way in, and anyone passing on the Werdertorgasse could see plainly through the glass: the old master at his lathe, the seamstress mending at her table, the deaf engraver showing a small girl how to hold a burin, and the young journeyman—people would say journeyman, the story was already settling into that shape, and perhaps that shape would serve—who moved among them with her sleeves rolled and her face bare and her chin up.

People did stop. A fishwife slowed. Two students pointed. An elderly gentleman in a fur collar stood so long at the window that Ivo, deadpan, held up a card: THE CLOCK SHOP IS OPEN, YES, COME IN OR MOVE ALONG. The gentleman came in and bought a carriage clock he did not need, at a price he did not negotiate, and left bowing.

Toward noon, a boy arrived with a note from Magistrate Ehrlich's chambers. Beatrix read it standing in the open doorway, in full daylight, and Brandt watched her face as she did.

"The trial date is confirmed," she reported. "Nine days. Ehrlich writes that Kessler's deposition runs to forty pages, and that Albrecht's counsel is already talking about a plea, which means Albrecht's patronage has evaporated overnight—money, Ehrlich says, 'has discovered it never knew the Director personally.'" She lowered the page. "And there is a postscript. He says—" she hesitated, and read it aloud anyway, because they were done with reading things silently in this shop— "*Tell the clockmaker that justice in this empire moves at the speed of a glacier, but that glaciers, once moving, do not stop for anything. Tell him also that his brother's petition, filed in 1819, has been retrieved from the archives and entered into evidence. Posthumous recognition is being prepared.*"

The shop went quiet. Even the mantel clock seemed to hold its beat.

Lena, sensing weather she did not understand, took Brandt's sleeve. "Who is the brother?"

"A man named Lukas," Brandt said. His voice came out rough. "Who asked the law a question in 1819, and died waiting for the answer." He cleared his throat. "The answer is arriving twenty-eight years late. Which is punctual, for a glacier."

"Will they give it to him? Even though he's dead?"

"Yes."

"Good." She nodded once, severely, satisfied that the universe had been brought into order, and returned to Ivo's bench to ruin a piece of scrap copper with her first engraving.

Brandt stood in the open doorway a while longer, watching the lane. Somewhere a church bell rang the noon hour, and was answered a half-second late by every lesser bell in the district, and he found himself, as he had a thousand times, checking the sound against his inner count—and found, today, that he did not mind the imperfection. The bells were allowed their lag. Men were allowed theirs. Twenty-eight years, even.

Behind him, Beatrix was already at the bench, sleeves rolled, sorting the morning's repairs, and Marta was arguing cheerfully with Ivo about whether a christening gown should be altered for winter, and Lena's burin scratched its brave crooked line into the copper, and the light lay across all of it like a blessing no one had asked for.

"Partner," Beatrix said suddenly, without looking up, trying the word on again in the daylight. "Say it back to me. I want to hear it in the air."

"Partner," Brandt said.

She smiled—the real one, not the thing with her mouth—and reached for the next clock that needed saving.

It was then that the boy came back, breathless, with a second note, and this one bore no magistrate's seal but a plain wax stamp Brandt did not recognize, and the handwriting on the front was elegant, unhurried, and unfamiliar:

*For the clockmaker's new partner — from a friend who has not yet finished being useful.*

Brandt read it twice. Then he passed it to Beatrix, and watched the wariness come back into her eyes like frost forming on glass, and understood that the striking of the clock had been an ending—but that endings, in this city, in this trade, in this life, were only the point at which one train of wheels ceased and another, hidden deeper in the case, began to turn.
