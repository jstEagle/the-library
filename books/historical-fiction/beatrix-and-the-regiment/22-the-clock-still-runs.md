## 22. The Clock Still Runs

The equinox came to Vienna on a morning so clear that the mountains stood out behind the city like a promise kept. Beatrix Halloran felt it before she saw it—the quality of light through the workshop's tall windows, thin and gold and honest, the kind of light that permitted no secrets and forgave all of them anyway.

She was up before the others, as she always was now, though the habit had changed its shape. Five years she had risen in the dark as a woman pretending not to be one, moving through Herr Brandt's workshop like a rumor of herself, careful even in her own footsteps. Now she rose in the dark because the work was good and there was more of it than hands to do it, and when her bare feet found the cold floorboards they belonged there.

The workshop smelled as it always had—oil and brass, cedar shavings, the faint ghost of Marta's soup from the night before—but layered over it now was something new: paint. They had repainted the sign over the door in August. *Brandt & Halloran, Uhrmacher*, it read, the ampersand Ivo's own engraving, each curl of it precise as a balance spring. Herr Brandt had stood in the street beneath it for the better part of an hour the day it went up, hat in his hands, saying nothing, which for Brandt was a kind of oration.

Beatrix banked the stove, put the kettle on, and took down the ledger—not Voss's ledger; that one slept in a magistrate's evidence room now, under seal, its plates photographed and copied and argued over by men with better Latin than she would ever have—but the workshop's own ledger, the honest one, listing commissions and debts and the small economy of their lives. She ran her finger down the column of names. The Hofburg commission. The church of Saint Anne. A widow in the Landstraße district who wanted her late husband's regulator repaired and could pay only in preserves, which Beatrix had accepted without shame, because Brandt said a clockmaker who refused jam would soon refuse work.

From the loft overhead came a thump, then another, then the particular scrambling rhythm she had learned to know better than her own heartbeat. Lena came down the ladder the way she came down everything—at speed, with total faith that the ladder would continue to cooperate—and landed in the middle of the floor with her hair standing up like a startled hedge.

"Is it today?" the girl demanded, before good morning, before anything.

"It is today."

"You promised I could wind it."

"I promised you could help me wind it." Beatrix poured the tea. "There is a difference, and the difference is that a master clock is not wound, it is *attended*. It is a ceremony, not a choreography. You will learn the difference if it takes us all year, which at your current rate it will."

Lena accepted this with the grave nod of a six-year-old—seven now, though she had claimed seven since January on no authority whatsoever—and climbed onto the stool across the workbench, where her own small tools waited in their own small roll of leather. Marta had sewn the roll. Ivo had engraved her initials into the leather: *L.V.* The V was Voss's initial, and no one had ever made a secret of it, and Lena had been told, in the way of this household, exactly as much truth as she could carry and not one gram less.

She did not remember her father's face. She remembered his voice, and the phrases he had drilled into her, and sometimes at night she recited them in her sleep like a rosary—numbers and dates that had once been a key to a cipher and were now only a bedtime ritual, the way other children said their prayers. Beatrix had decided, after long thought and one terrible argument with herself, that the girl should keep them. They were the last thing her father had given her. It was not the child's burden to set down.

"Eat first," Beatrix said. "A clockmaker who winds on an empty stomach shakes, and a shaking hand strips teeth, and stripped teeth mean a dead train, and dead trains mean—" She paused, letting the lesson hang.

"—mean Herr Brandt is unhappy," Lena finished solemnly.

"*Mean we have failed our trust,*" Beatrix corrected, laughing. "But also yes. Herr Brandt is unhappy."

* * *

Herr Brandt came in at seven, precisely, because he had come in at seven precisely for forty years and saw no reason to let justice disrupt horology. He was thinner than he had been a year ago—the weeks in custody had taken flesh from him that never fully returned—but his hands were steady, and since his release he had touched Beatrix's shoulder exactly twice, both times without a word, and both times she had understood the entire speech.

He looked at the ledger, then at Lena, then at the kettle, in his usual order of priorities.

"The equinox demonstration," he said. "They are expecting you at noon. Ehrlich wrote again—I have it here." He produced the magistrate's letter from inside his coat, where Beatrix suspected he kept all letters, including ones that arrived by ordinary post. "He asks whether you will speak this year."

"Tell him yes."

"He asks whether you will speak *under your own name* this year. He wishes to print it in the program. There is a faction on the council—he does not say which—that thinks the whole affair should be retired quietly. A scandal best folded away, like winter clothes."

Beatrix turned her cup slowly on its saucer. A year ago, that sentence would have sent her hand to her collar, checking the buttons of a boy's coat. Now she only felt the old reflex rise and pass through her like weather.

"They may print it," she said. "Beatrix Halloran. Spelled correctly. If they spell it *Beatriz* I shall be annoyed for a month."

Brandt almost smiled. On his face it appeared as a slight resettling of the mustache. "I will convey the spelling," he said, "with emphasis."

Lena, who had been listening with the ferocious attention she gave to anything concerning names, tugged Beatrix's sleeve. "Say mine too."

"We shall see what fits in the program," Beatrix said. "You may yet crowd me off the page entirely."

* * *

Marta arrived at nine with fabric over one arm and news over the other, which was her custom: she brought both in equal measure and distributed both freely. The news was that the widow Voss—Anton Voss's sister-in-law, actually, a distinction that had mattered enormously during the inquest and not at all afterward—had written from Graz to say that the pension finally awarded her family had arrived, and that she had wept over the letter, and that she wanted Lena to have her father's watch, which had been held in evidence and released at last.

The watch sat in Marta's palm when she laid it out on the workbench: a plain hunter-case, worn at the hinges, stopped at eleven minutes past four. No one knew whether that was when he had died or merely when he had last wound it. Beatrix voted silently for the latter. Some things were kinder as repairs than as monuments.

"She should clean it herself," Beatrix said. "Under supervision. It's a simple lever escapement—it will teach her the pallet stones."

"It will teach her to cry into the movement," Marta said, but gently, and she was looking at Lena across the room, at the girl bent over her practice plate with a loupe nearly as large as her face, and Marta's voice did the thing it did when she spoke of Lena, going soft at the edges like cloth worn at the fold. "Well. Both, perhaps. Weeping is also a skill this house has had occasion to develop."

Marta had sewn the ledger pages into a christening gown a year ago—a gown that had hung in the shop like a saint's relic, venerated and slightly feared, until the trial ended and Magistrate Ehrlich himself had come to collect it, unpicking the seams at Beatrix's table with a delicacy that suggested he understood he was performing surgery on history. The gown had gone back to being a gown. Marta had since lent it, unaltered, to three different christenings in the neighborhood, and reported with satisfaction that it was now the luckiest garment in the Leopoldstadt.

"Some things should carry what they carry and then be done," Marta said, folding her fabric. "Cloth remembers enough without being asked to remember everything."

Ivo arrived last, as he always did, coming up the lane with his coat collar up against nothing in particular—the day was warm—and carrying a flat package wrapped in oilcloth. He set it on the bench, unwrapped it with the unhurried precision of a man for whom every gesture was drafting, and revealed a plaque: polished brass, eight inches square, engraved around the border with oak leaves, and in the center, in his small severe capitals:

**IN THIS HOUSE NOTHING WAS FORGOTTEN AND NO ONE WAS LOST.**

Beneath it, smaller: the date of the equinox demonstration, and beneath that, four initials—B.H., T.B., M.W., and L.V.—with space left, Beatrix noticed, at the bottom edge. Blank space. Room.

"For the wall by the door," Marta translated aloud, though Ivo needed no translating for any of them anymore; a year of living alongside him had taught them all to hear silence fluently. "For anyone who comes looking. And for whoever comes after."

Ivo tapped the blank space at the bottom and raised his eyebrows at Beatrix. The question needed no translation either.

"Room for more names," she said. "Yes. Leave it. Houses should grow."

* * *

They went up to the observatory at half past eleven, all four of them, walking through streets that had learned their names. This was the thing Beatrix still could not entirely accustom herself to: being known. The baker's boy called out to Lena. A woman at the corner curtsied, absurdly, to Brandt. At the foot of the hill a carter touched his cap to Marta and asked after her mother's hip, and Marta gave him a full medical report while walking, without breaking stride, the way generals give orders.

And everywhere, underneath everything, the clocks. Vienna ticked. From open windows and church towers and shop fronts, from the fob watches of clerks hurrying along the Graben, the city kept its ten thousand small agreements about time, and Beatrix thought—as she thought every year on this day, and could not stop thinking—that among all those ticking things, one clock had very nearly swallowed a murder, and one clerk's stubbornness had traveled through gears and cipher and a child's drilled memory to arrive, at last, at daylight.

The observatory steps were crowded. Dignitaries in the front rows—councilmen, two archdukes' functionaries, a row of professors who had opposed the restoration's funding until it became fashionable and then claimed they had proposed it. Behind them, the public: students, tradespeople, children on shoulders. And at the rail, in a position of honor he plainly distrusted, Magistrate Ehrlich, gray and exact, holding his program rolled like a baton.

The program, Beatrix saw with a start, bore her name. *Restoration by Brandt & Halloran. Demonstration address to be delivered by B. Halloran, partner.* Not apprentice. Not *her assistant*. Partner, spelled correctly, printed in ink that would not take it back.

Her hands, when she mounted the step before the great clock, did not shake. She had spent five years teaching them not to shake in far worse rooms than this.

"This clock," she began, and her voice carried in the stone drum of the tower better than she expected, "stopped on the ninth of March, 1847, and was reported broken. It was not broken. It was silenced. I want to tell you what the difference is, because it is the whole of why we are here."

She told them. Not all of it—there was too much, and much of it was not hers to tell—but enough. She spoke of Anton Voss, a treasury clerk who found numbers that did not agree and was too honest to look away. She spoke of a hiding place built into an escapement, and of papers that rode in the dark inside a machine for months, patient as seeds. She spoke of a note left on these steps with an abandoned child, ordering the finder to forget a face, and of a workshop full of people with private reasons for keeping quiet who chose, instead, to remember.

"I was one of those people," she said. "I had spent years being invisible on purpose. Invisibility is warm, in winter. But I learned in this tower that some silences do not keep people safe—they only keep people gone. Anton Voss is gone. His daughter is not. Everything this clock struck this past year, it struck for both of them."

Then she stepped aside, and Lena stepped forward—this had been rehearsed for two weeks with the gravity of a coronation—and the girl, on a stool brought for the purpose, inserted the winding key into the great barrel and turned it, one deliberate quarter-turn, her small hands white-knuckled and her chin set exactly the way, Beatrix now recognized, her father's portrait showed it set.

The master clock took the wind. Somewhere in its depths the escapement caught, released, caught—the heartbeat Beatrix had listened to through a stethoscope a hundred nights—and the striking train gathered itself and delivered the noon hour across the city, twelve strokes rolling out over the rooftops, and on the twelfth stroke, with a click engineered to last exactly one year and be heard forever, a small panel near the base swung open on its hinge.

Inside, on a velvet-lined shelf Ivo had cut and fitted himself, lay a single engraved plate, displayed under glass: not evidence now—evidence slept safely elsewhere—but a memorial. Voss's name. The date of his death. And Ivo's line, cut deep enough to outlast the brass:

*HE PUT THE TRUTH IN THE CLOCK SO THAT TIME ITSELF WOULD CARRY IT.*

The crowd was quiet. Then the quiet broke the way quiet does, into applause and weeping and a professor loudly claiming prior credit, and Ehrlich was shaking Brandt's hand, and Marta was crying freely into her good shawl, and Lena was still holding the winding key against her chest like a medal.

Beatrix stood back from it all, beside the opened compartment, and let the noise wash over her.

A year ago she had stood in this same spot with a forged letter of introduction in a boy's pocket and a dead man's name in her mouth, hoping only to be paid and unseen. Now her name was in a printed program, her signature was on a partnership deed, and the child everyone had ordered forgotten was winding the city's most public clock in front of two hundred witnesses. Justice had not arrived quickly. It had arrived the way this clock kept time—one escape tooth at a time, each release followed by a catch, each catch followed by another release, patience machined into a principle.

Nothing was forgotten. No one was lost. The plaque would go up by the door tonight.

* * *

They walked home in the late gold light, and Beatrix carried Lena part of the way on her shoulders, and Brandt pretended not to notice, and Marta collected gossip from three separate acquaintances, and Ivo walked with his hands in his pockets looking, for him, positively festive.

At the workshop door, Lena slid down and ran her finger along the fresh-mounted plaque, sounding out the words she already knew by heart, and then pointed at the blank space at the bottom.

"Who goes there?" she asked.

"Whoever earns it," Beatrix said. "That is how houses like this work. You leave room."

Lena considered this with her whole face. Then, "Tomorrow can I start on Papa's watch?"

"Tomorrow," Beatrix agreed. "We'll begin with the mainspring. And Lena—" the girl turned at the door, backlit by the low sun, seven years old and utterly certain of her welcome— "when we open it, whatever we find inside, we look at it together. That is the rule of this house. Nothing faced alone."

"I know," Lena said, with the mild scorn of the young for unnecessary instruction, and went in ahead of them, up the ladder at speed, trusting the rungs.

Beatrix stood a moment longer on the step, looking at the lane she had once slipped down before dawn wearing her brother's coat, and at the sign above her own head bearing her own name, and heard, faint and faithful from the hill, the observatory clock beginning its afternoon quarter-chime.

Then she went in, and closed the door gently behind her, and reached for the lamp—because there was work yet, there was always work, and tomorrow a little watch stopped at eleven minutes past four would begin, at last, to move.
