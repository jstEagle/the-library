## 1. The Weight of Lead

The shop smelled the way it had smelled all of Beatrix Dunmore's life: ink and oil, hot metal, the tallow-sweetness of lamp black, and beneath it all the cold mineral breath of lead. On winter mornings that smell was the whole world. She could have found her way through Dunmore & Son blindfolded — past the paper racks breathing damp from the river, past the stone sink where the rollers soaked, to the composing bench where the light came down through the north window in a long gray ribbon.

She stood in that ribbon now, composing stick in hand, setting the week's notices for the *Vellum Falls Advertiser*. Eleven lines on barge schedules. Fourteen on the church bazaar. A notice from the harbormaster about ice stakes on the pilings, which meant the bridge would be closing soon, which meant everything.

*Set it like you love it,* her father always said. *The reader will never know why it looks right. But they'll feel it.*

Her fingers knew the California job case the way other women's fingers knew psalms. Lowercase left, capitals right, figures above; e's under her first finger, thick as clover in any paragraph, and she fished them out without looking, without thinking, while some other part of her mind counted the days until the bridge closed and the capital across the water became not a city but a rumor. Nine days. Maybe eight. The ice was already talking to itself out there at night, groaning like an old man turning over in bed.

"Bea." Casper's voice, from the back room. "Bea, where's the good glue?"

"There is only one glue."

"The bad glue, then. Where's the glue that isn't mine?"

She smiled at the case. Her brother was twenty-two and had been in the town four minutes before he began trying to leave it. He came through the doorway now with his collar up and his hair ruined by wind, carrying the smell of the wharf with him — tar, fish scales, the cold iron tang off the bridge road. He'd been down watching the barges again. He was always down watching something: the barges, the soldiers drilling in the customs yard, the men who gathered at the corner of Rope Walk and spoke low and looked over their shoulders.

"Father wants to know if you've pulled the proofs," she said.

"I'm getting to it."

"He asked an hour ago."

"He asked through you because he doesn't want to ask me himself." Casper leaned against the doorframe, and for a moment the restlessness in him went still, and he just looked at the shop — at the two presses, the tall one and the little hand press their grandfather had carried upriver; at the cases and cabinets; at the framed specimen sheets of Aldous Dunmore's typefaces hanging along the stairwell like family portraits, which, in a way, they were. "It's a good shop," he said, quietly, as if apologizing for something.

"It's the best shop on this side of the water."

"That's not saying what you think it says." He pushed off the frame. "Proofs. Yes. Going."

* * *

Their father worked in the type-cutting room at the top of the stairs, a closet of a room that most printers would have used for storage. Aldous Dunmore used it for making letters. He sat bent over his graver with a blank of hardened type-metal clamped in the vise, cutting a lowercase g by lamplight, and he did not look up when Beatrix brought the tea, because looking up cost him the line, and the line was everything. She had watched him cut faces since she was small enough to sit on the floor and sort his rejected blanks into piles. He cut slowly, the way other men prayed. Punches, matrices, molds — he made them all himself, and printers three hundred miles away paid in advance for Dunmore types, and her father treated each order like a small act of penance for sins nobody had named.

"How's the g?" she asked.

"Cockeyed." He sat back, took the tea anyway, held it under his chin for the warmth. His hands were enormous for a man who did such small work, scarred across the knuckles from forty years of nipped fingers and slipped gravers. "I've been fighting this letter since Tuesday."

"You're fighting it because you keep cutting it like your old face. It wants to be its own letter."

Aldous looked at her then, over the rim of the cup, with the expression he wore whenever she said something about type that he didn't want to admit was true. "Your brother in?"

"Downstairs avoiding the proofs."

"Hm." He set the tea down and bent back to the vise. "The harbormaster was in yesterday while you were at the Halstows'. Buying stakes for the bridge road. Official business."

"I know. I set the notice."

"Then you know what nine days means." He didn't say anything else for a moment. The graver whispered against metal. "Once that bridge closes, whatever's brewing over there brews alone until thaw. And we're on this side of it."

"We've always been on this side of it."

"Yes," her father said. "That's what I keep telling myself."

* * *

The customer came at half past three, when the light had gone the color of dishwater and Beatrix was washing the forme after the bazaar notices. The bell over the door rang once, and a man came in stomping snow off boots that were too good for Vellum Falls — city boots, thin-soled, the kind a man wore when he rode carriages rather than mud. He was ordinary otherwise. Gray coat, gray beard trimmed close, eyes that moved around the shop the way a man counts exits.

"Printer?" he asked, though the presses were roaring behind Beatrix and the sign said DUNMORE & SON in letters her own hands had distributed across the front of the building.

"My father's upstairs. I can help you."

He considered her. She had the sense of being weighed — not as a girl, not even as a printer, but as something between a lockpick and a door. "You set type here? Yourself?"

"Everything that leaves this shop goes through my hands."

Something changed in his face at that, a small settling, like a man finding dry ground. From inside his coat he drew a folded packet wrapped in brown paper, and laid it on the counter between them, and did not let go of it right away.

"I need five hundred," he said. "Before the bridge closes."

Beatrix unwrapped one corner. Inside was a printed pamphlet, eight pages, sewn at the spine. She recognized the style of it — the cheap paper, the double column, the breathless title — *An Address to the Working Men of the River Towns* — and she had seen pamphlets like it before, smuggled over in barge crates and passed hand to hand and burned nervously in stoves when the wrong person knocked. But she had never seen one brought in like contraband to be *multiplied*.

She read the first page standing there. It was not wild writing. That was what unsettled her. It was measured, reasonable, almost gentle — wages, hours, the price of flour, the right of men to speak without a permit — and it ended with a single sentence that seemed to reach off the page and put its cold hand around her wrist: *When the bridge closes, remember that the river still runs.*

"The governor's office prints its proclamations here," she said. Carefully. Not a refusal yet. Just placing the fact on the counter next to the pamphlet, the way her father placed a proof beside copy.

"I know," the man said. "That's rather the point. Who suspects the governor's own printer?"

"And if I say no?"

"Then I'll ask at Millbrook, or Corvey's landing, and you'll never see me again." He straightened his cuffs. "But Corvey uses cast-off type and lets his boys set the pages, and every pamphlet out of that shop has a crooked e in the third line of the title, and the colonel's men know it, and they watch for it. You people are better than Corvey. That's exactly why I'm here."

Beatrix looked at the pamphlet. At the sentence about the river. Five hundred copies. She thought of her father's framed specimens going up the stairwell, each letter cut by hand, each one made to carry somebody else's words cleanly and without judgment. Type didn't choose sides. That had always seemed to her a kind of holiness.

Now it seemed like something else. A door that locked from either side.

"Leave it," she heard herself say. "I can't promise. My father decides what we print. But leave it, and come back tomorrow."

The man looked at her a long moment. Then he nodded once, released the packet, and was gone, and the bell rang behind him, and the cold air of the street came in and lay down across the counter like something that intended to stay.

* * *

They argued about it after supper, the three of them among the drying proofs, and it was the strangest argument Beatrix had ever heard, because no one raised their voice and everyone told the truth.

Casper wanted to print it. She had known he would before he opened his mouth; she had watched him try not to look at the packet all through dinner, the way a man tries not to look at a woman across a room. "Five hundred copies of the truth," he said, "before the bridge closes and we sit here all winter pretending nothing is happening. Father, they're not monsters over there. They're dockmen. They're our kind of people."

"They are a side," Aldous said. "And the governor is a side. And we are—" he stopped, and rubbed his scarred knuckles, and started again. "We are a print shop. Your grandfather carried that press upriver on his back through two floods, and it survived both because it belonged to no one. We print notices. Bazaars. Barge schedules. Weddings." He said *weddings* the way another man might say *hospitals*, and Beatrix understood suddenly that her father had been waiting his whole life for the world to stay small enough that those were the only words a printer ever needed.

"And if a side simply takes us?" Casper asked. "If they come in the spring and say, print for us or else? What will neutrality be worth then?"

"More than it's worth today," Aldous said. "That's the arithmetic of it. Every day you stay unspent, you're worth more."

He said no, in the end. He wrapped the pamphlet in its brown paper and put it in the locked drawer of the proof desk, the drawer where they kept the bail bonds and the deed, and he said, "We sleep on it. Nothing is decided tonight," which Beatrix had learned long ago was her father's way of deciding things very slowly indeed.

But she lay awake past midnight in the cold bedroom under the eaves, listening to the ice talk out on the river, and she thought about the man's gray eyes counting the exits, and about the crooked e in Corvey's third line, and about how the colonel's men could read a typeface the way she read a psalm — and how her own hands, tonight, had touched the packet that could hang them all.

Below her, faint through the floorboards, she heard the cellar door open and close. Then, much later, close again.

In the morning the pamphlet was still in the locked drawer. Beatrix checked twice.

But the brown paper had been refolded — carefully, expertly, along new creases — and inside, someone had turned the pamphlet over and pressed flat the back page, and there, faint as a watermark, was the ghost of fresh printer's ink, as though the folded sheet had been laid facedown on a forme of set type and leaned on, hard, by someone practicing.

By someone rehearsing.

She stood in the cold shop with the packet in her hands and did not need to look toward the stairs to know that her brother's bed, when she finally saw it, had not been slept in.
