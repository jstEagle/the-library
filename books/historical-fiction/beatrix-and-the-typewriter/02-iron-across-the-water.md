## 2. Iron Across the Water

The proclamation went up on the customs house door on the first morning the river began to think about ice.

Casper read it twice, once for the words and once for what was underneath them, because in Vellum Falls you learned early that official language was like a broadsheet printed on cheap stock — the truth bled through to the other side. *By order of His Excellency the Governor, the Great Bridge shall close to all traffic from the fifteenth of November until such time as the ice renders crossing unsafe, whereupon it shall remain closed until the thaw.* Signed, stamped, pasted at eye level beside the tariff notices and a faded bill for a circus that had come through four summers ago.

Until such time. Until the thaw. As though anyone needed a proclamation to tell them the bridge closed every winter. It had closed every winter of Casper's life, and every winter before that, back to when his grandfather had set type for steamboat bills with woodcut swans on them. The river froze; the iron span stood empty over the ice like a sentence with its verb missing; the town turned inward and waited for spring. Nobody had ever needed the Governor's seal to say so.

But there it was, three paragraphs of it, and beneath the proclamation, nailed into the same doorframe at a slightly crooked angle by someone in too much of a hurry to care about symmetry, a second notice: *All persons attempting to cross the frozen river on foot or by vehicle will be considered in violation of martial ordinance 44-B and subject to arrest.*

Martial ordinance. Casper stood in the gray light with his collar up and his breath smoking, reading that line a third time, and felt something turn over in his chest — not fear exactly, not yet, but the particular lightness he'd learned to recognize as the beginning of a secret. He'd felt it at fourteen, when he'd found his father's locked drawer open and seen the ledger inside with names in it that Aldous Dunmore never spoke aloud. He'd felt it last spring, when a stevedore named Hollis had pressed a folded paper into his hand at the loading dock and said, *You're the printer's boy. You understand these things,* and Casper had read the workers' committee's demands on the walk home and understood, with a clarity that frightened him, that he agreed with every word.

*You understand these things.* That was what people said when they meant: you have access. The printer's son had access the way the doctor's son had morphine, the way the banker's son had numbers. Paper, ink, presses, and a father whose neutrality made him useful to everyone and trusted — Casper suspected — by no one.

He looked upriver. The fog was lifting off the water in rags, and through it, on the far abutment, he could see them already: soldiers. Not the usual winter detail, two bored men in greatcoats who checked lanterns and waved sleighs through until the ice came. There were twenty of them at least, maybe thirty, unloading crates from a flatbed dray, and even at this distance Casper could see they weren't unpacking stove wood. The crates were long. Rifle-length. Two men carried a tripod-mounted thing between them that caught the light and held it, bright and deliberate, like a promise being made.

For weather, you posted one notice and went home to your supper. For weather, you didn't emplace a machine gun on the far abutment of your own bridge, aimed — Casper tilted his head, following the geometry of it down the barrel's implied line — aimed not out at the river but along it. Downriver. Toward the workers' quarters on the capital side, the tenement rows where the committees met in candlelit rooms above the cooperage, where Hollis lived, where the strike fund was kept in a biscuit tin under a loose floorboard that Casper knew about because Hollis had told him, laughing, drunk on one glass of holiday gin, trusting him absolutely.

The closure wasn't weather. The closure was a siege line drawn in iron, and the proclamation on the customs house door was the polite version of it.

Casper pulled his cap lower and walked home through streets that smelled of coal smoke and the river's cold mineral breath, and he did not look back at the bridge again, because a man who kept secrets learned quickly that looking twice was how you got remembered.

* * *

The shop smelled of lead and oil and the sweetish tang of the drying ink, and it was the only smell in the world that meant safety to him, which was its own kind of betrayal now, though he couldn't have said of whom.

His father was at the stone, sleeves rolled, cutting punches with the small deliberate movements of a man performing surgery on metal. Aldous Dunmore cut typefaces the way other men prayed — slowly, silently, entirely absorbed — and the family had long ago learned that the hour after breakfast belonged to the graver and was not to be interrupted. This morning he was working on a lowercase g with a double-story bowl that Beatrix had designed and he was refining, their silent collaboration, the closest thing to tenderness either of them could manage in daylight.

Beatrix stood at the case across the room, setting a job lot of funeral invitations, her hands moving in the composing stick's rhythm — pick, place, justify; pick, place, justify — with the unconscious fluency of someone who thought in type the way musicians thought in notes. She didn't look up when Casper came in, but she said, "You were gone early."

"Wanted to see the proclamation."

"Ah." A pause in the rhythm, half a second, then resumed. "Father says it's nothing. Ice closes the bridge."

"Father isn't always right."

That got her attention. She turned, a piece of type still held between finger and thumb like a coin about to be flipped. Her eyes went to their father first — an instinct Casper noted and filed away — and then back to him, and in them he saw the question she was too careful to ask aloud in front of Aldous: *what do you know?*

"Nothing," Casper said, before she could ask it. "It's just strange, is all. Ordinances about walking on the ice. We've walked on the ice my whole life."

"People drown on the ice," Aldous said without turning from the stone. "Every few years somebody does. The ordinance is theater, Casper. The Governor wants the towns on both banks to feel governed. Let him spend his soldiers' wages standing in the cold if it makes him feel secure." He blew a curl of brass shaving off the punch and examined the g against the light. "B. Your terminal is too heavy. It crowds the bowl."

"You're crowding the bowl," Beatrix said, coming to stand beside him, and father and daughter bent together over a letter smaller than a fingernail, arguing about the curve of a tail, and Casper watched them with the peculiar double vision that had been his constant companion since autumn: love and concealment occupying the same gaze, like ink printed so both images share a single plate.

He almost told her that night. He lay awake in the narrow bed under the eaves listening to the wind worry the sign outside — DUNMORE & SON, though the ampersand had always struck him as generous, since the son did little more than sweep and deliver — and he composed the confession the way he'd once composed school essays, carefully, with an appeal to justice in the closing paragraph. *I know things, Bee. I know where the committees meet. I know Hollis. They're going to need printing done this winter, real printing, and Father won't do it and someone has to—*

And then he imagined saying it, and heard how it would sound in this house, in this town, with the bridge closed and soldiers on the abutment, and he stopped. Not because he was afraid for himself. Because he was afraid *of* himself — of the thrill, which was real and shameful and delicious, of how much he wanted to be the one who knew, the one who moved, the one the story happened to. His sister set type like breathing; his father cut steel like prayer; and Casper swept floors and delivered parcels and had, until recently, been perfectly content to be furniture in his own family. The committee had looked at him and seen not furniture but access. He was not going to hand that feeling over to anyone else, not even Beatrix. Especially not Beatrix.

He slept badly and woke resolved to tell no one anything, which is the resolution every conspirator makes right before he begins.

* * *

Hollis found him at the loading dock on Thursday, which was fast becoming their arrangement — Thursdays, deliveries, a minute of talk disguised as a discussion of freight charges.

"They've closed it," Hollis said. He was a big man gone soft around the middle, with a face like a dropped loaf of bread and eyes that missed nothing. "The bridge."

"The proclamation says it's the ice."

"The proclamation," Hollis said, "says a great many things." He glanced down the dock, empty except for gulls picking at fish offal. "We had a man across. He came back overland, took him two days through the marshes to get around the headwaters. He says there are regiments moving on the capital side, Casper. Real ones. And the committees are asking—" He stopped, and Casper watched him decide, watched the calculation move behind the bread-dough face like a shadow behind a curtain. "The committees are asking whether certain facilities might be available. Come spring. Or before."

"Certain facilities," Casper repeated.

"A press, lad. A printer who can keep his mouth shut and his ink flowing." Hollis smiled, and it was meant to be disarming and mostly was. "Your father's neutral. Everyone knows it. Neutral is fine. Neutral is actually better, in some ways. Nobody suspects the neutral man."

"He'd refuse."

"Probably." Hollis shrugged, vast and easy. "Which is why I'm not asking him."

The dock seemed very quiet then. The river muttered against the pilings. Somewhere upriver the soldiers would be drilling or stamping their feet against the cold or cleaning the long crates' contents, and here was Hollis, warm as a stove, offering Casper Dunmore a door into the century.

"I'm not my father," Casper said.

"No," Hollis agreed pleasantly. "You're not."

They settled it the way such things are always settled — vaguely, deniably, with a handshake that lasted half a second too long and a price discussed only in the abstract, in units of *when things change*. Casper walked back to the shop carrying a list of legitimate orders he'd invented on the spot, and under the weight of the parcel, under the weight of the ordinary afternoon, he carried the other thing: the knowledge that he had just become, in however small a way, part of the machinery.

Guilt arrived on schedule that evening, punctual as the mail. It sat across from him at supper while Beatrix talked about kerning and their father hummed over his stew, and it whispered its questions. *What if they trace it? What if Hollis talks? What if your father loses everything because his son wanted to feel important?* And then, in a kinder voice, the guilt asked the question that was worse than all the others: *and would you stop?*

He wouldn't. He knew it the way he knew the weight of a galley of set type. The thrill and the guilt had shaken hands somewhere inside him and agreed to travel together, and Casper understood, dimly, that this was what it meant to be a man in a year like 1899: you carried both, and you told no one, and you waited for the ice.

* * *

On the last Sunday of November, the whole town turned out to watch the bridge officially close, because Vellum Falls would make a ceremony of anything in winter, and the Governor had sent an officer down from the capital to preside over the locking of the toll gate.

Casper stood in the crowd with his mother's old scarf wound to his ears and watched the officer — young, gloved, magnificent in a way that seemed almost apologetic — read the proclamation aloud in a voice pitched for parade grounds. Behind him, soldiers stood in ranks on the near abutment, and the machine gun Casper had seen being assembled sat on its tripod under a canvas tarp, present and unmentioned, like a corpse at a wedding.

"—until such time as the thaw permits safe passage," the officer concluded. "His Excellency wishes the good people of Vellum Falls a peaceful and prosperous winter."

A smattering of applause. Someone's dog barked. And then, from the back of the crowd, a voice — thin, cracked, an old man's voice — called out: "And if we need to cross? If there's sickness? If there's trade?"

The officer smiled with professional warmth. "The ice will bear careful feet by January, friend. Until then, His Excellency asks only patience."

Patience. Casper turned the word over as the crowd dispersed, as the soldiers shouldered arms and marched back to their billets in the converted granary, as the great iron bridge stood locked and empty over the darkening water with its new garrison and its new gun and its new meaning. Patience was what you asked of people you intended to keep in a place.

That night he passed his sister's door and saw lamplight burning under it, late, later than any funeral invitations required. He stood a moment in the cold corridor, hand raised to knock, the secret swelling in his throat like a word set upside down — legible to anyone who cared to look, meaningless unless you knew to read it backwards.

He didn't knock. He went to his room and lay in the dark and listened to the river, and somewhere below in the shop, faintly, he heard the sound he'd know anywhere, the sound of a composing stick clicking against a lead slug, quick and rhythmic, like a heart deciding something.

Beatrix was working. At midnight. On what?

He filed the question away with the rest of his collection, and slept, and dreamed of the bridge, and in the dream it was open.
