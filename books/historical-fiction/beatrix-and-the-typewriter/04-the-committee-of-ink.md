## 4. The Committee of Ink

Aldous Dunmore had kept his shop neutral through three administrations, two floods, and one fire that had taken the corner of the building and spared the presses as if by professional courtesy. Neutrality, he liked to tell apprentices who never stayed long enough to learn the lesson, was not a virtue. It was a craft. It required the same patience as setting type: every piece in its place, every edge aligned, no letter standing prouder than its neighbor. A man who printed only what he believed was a propagandist with a slow hand. A man who printed what he was paid to print, cleanly and without comment, was something rarer — a servant of the trade itself.

He had repeated this to himself so many times over forty years that it had worn smooth, like a quooin worked loose from use, and he had stopped noticing that it was a lie he'd built a life on top of.

The morning the governor's man came calling, Aldous was cutting a new face — a condensed gothic for auction bills, nothing political, nothing that would outlive the season. He heard boots on the stairs before he saw them, heavy ones, government-issue, the kind that announced themselves because they wanted to be announced.

Major Corvane filled the doorway the way wet paper fills a frame: soft-looking until you understood the weight behind it. He wore the governor's gray and carried his hat under one arm like a man who had been coached in courtesy and meant to use it before he forgot how.

"Master Dunmore." He glanced around the shop with the appreciation of a man reading a menu. "I've admired your work. The assize notices last spring. Very crisp."

"The assizes are crisp work," Aldous said, wiping his hands on his apron though they were already clean. A habit. His hands were never clean; that was the trade.

"I'll come to it, then, since you're a busy man." Corvane set a folded document on the composing stone, squared its edges with two fingers — a printer's gesture, Aldous noted, and resented it. "Proclamations. Weekly bulletins on the bridge closure and the security situation. Rationing schedules when they come. The governor wants consistency of appearance. One hand, one voice, town-wide."

"And you've chosen mine."

"Yours is the only press worth choosing this side of the water."

The truth sat between them, plain as a locked forme. There were two other shops in Vellum Falls — Hobbes's jobbing outfit, half-blind, and the mortuary's card press — but Corvane had walked past both to stand here. Which meant either the governor wanted quality, or he wanted the shop that the other side would also want. Control the crossroads, even if no one was crossing.

"There's a matter of payment," Corvane said, producing a ledger slip. The figure on it was generous. Generous enough that refusing it would look like a statement rather than a preference.

"And if I decline?"

Corvane smiled. He had good teeth and bad eyes — the eyes did all the arithmetic while the teeth did the talking. "Then I'll have to find another press, won't I. And I expect I would. Eventually. Though I confess I'd wonder why a printer of your reputation didn't want his town to read official news." He let that hang. "No one's asking you to write anything, Master Dunmore. Just to set it."

*Just to set it.* Forty years of telling himself exactly that, and here was the government handing the sentence back to him with a signature line beneath it.

"I take work as it comes," Aldous said. "Bring me your copy. I don't draft. I don't editorialize. And I print for whoever pays — that includes people whose politics I don't share, which is most people."

"Naturally," Corvane said, and Aldous understood that the man had expected precisely this answer, and that the answer itself had just been filed somewhere, in some column or other.

* * *

They came at night, the other side. Of course they did. The rebels came the way their pamphlets came: quietly, pasted up where lamplight didn't reach.

It was Casper who brought him — Aldous realized that only afterward, reconstructing the evening the way you'd reconstruct a collapsed page, seeing too late where each piece should have sat. Casper came down to the shop after supper saying he'd left his gloves, and twenty minutes later there were three knocks on the street door, spaced wrong for a customer, right for a signal.

The man who entered called himself Ferrand and looked like a schoolteacher gone slightly to seed — spectacles, ink-stained cuffs, the gentle stoop of a man used to leaning over desks. Only his hands gave him away. They were steady in a way that suggested they had done things requiring steadiness.

"Your son may have mentioned the committees," Ferrand said. "The Workers' Circle. We're printing nothing yet. We're asking about capacity."

"You're asking whether I'll print for you what I'm printing for him."

"I'm asking what a fair rate is. We pay better than governments. Governments pay in promises; we pay in coin, because we know we might not survive long enough to owe anyone anything."

Aldous looked at Casper, who stood by the type cabinets wearing an expression of elaborate unconcern that fooled no one in the room. *You brought him here,* Aldous thought. *You opened this door with my name on it.*

"The governor's man came this morning," Aldous said.

"We know. We know everything he does, roughly a day late." Ferrand took off his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, unhurried. "Here is my thinking, Master Dunmore. You cannot refuse him without becoming a symbol, and symbols get burned. You cannot refuse us without becoming a target of the committees, and committees have longer memories than majors. So you will print for both. That is not treachery — that is the oldest arrangement in wartime printing, older than any of the causes currently renting it. Amsterdam did it. Paris did it. The shop that prints for everyone prints for history, and history pays its debts slowly but pays."

"And if both sides eventually decide a man who prints for everyone is a man loyal to no one?"

Ferrand replaced his spectacles. "Then you'll want friends on both sides of whatever wall stands that year. Consider this the laying of foundations."

They talked terms. That was the obscenity of it, Aldous thought later — how quickly it became terms. Weight of stock, run counts, delivery hours, a surcharge for risk that Ferrand offered before Aldous could ask. The money went into a tin box under the floorboards of the stone sink, wrapped in oilcloth, and Aldous told himself the sentence he'd spent a lifetime perfecting:

*I am not printing their ideas. I am printing their words. The distinction matters.*

He said it to himself twice more before bed, and noticed, for the first time in forty years, that the sentence needed saying at all.

* * *

December came down the valley like a creditor. The first real snow fell on a Tuesday, and by Thursday the shop smelled of candle wax and cold lead, and Beatrix was working nights — she claimed the quiet helped her compose, and Aldous, remembering his own young nights at the stick, believed her, or chose to.

That was the season's strangeness: his children had become figures at a distance. Casper vanished on errands he no longer bothered to disguise as errands, coming home with river-mud on his boots when the riverbanks were frozen iron. Beatrix sat at her case by lamplight, hands moving with a fluency that made Aldous proud and uneasy in equal measure, because fluency like hers was not taught, it was *recognized* — as if the type had been waiting for someone who spoke it.

And Aldous printed.

He printed the governor's bulletins in the mornings: THE BRIDGE IS CLOSED FOR THE SAFETY OF ALL, in Corvane's condensed gothic, the very face Aldous had cut for auction bills now announcing curfews and confiscations. He set the lines with perfect justification, flush left and right, not a hair of space wasted, because his craft deserved better than its uses.

He printed for the committees in the dark hours, after Beatrix had gone up, working alone with shutters drawn: short sheets on cheap stock, unsigned, the kind of thing that traveled folded inside coat linings. He read none of them closely. That was the discipline. He handled each word the way he handled any customer's copy — as freight, not cargo. What a man carries is not what a man believes.

Except that he began, despite himself, to recognize phrases. A turn of sentence in the third pamphlet that echoed in the fifth. A particular way of ending paragraphs on a rising note, like a fist closing. Whoever wrote these wrote them fast and wrote them angry and wrote them well, and Aldous, who had spent a lifetime reading other men's hands through their type, felt the writer becoming familiar the way a voice becomes familiar through a wall.

One night in mid-December he paused over a half-set galley and caught himself finishing a sentence before his eye reached its end. Knowing what came next. Anticipating the argument.

He put the composing stick down and stood in the cold shop listening to the stove tick, and understood that something in him had shifted and could not be shifted back. He was no longer printing their words. Somewhere along the way, without signing anything, without deciding anything, he had begun to *agree* with them — and agreement, once admitted, converted the whole enterprise. The tin box under the sink stopped being payment and started being wages. The distinction he'd built his life on had curdled quietly in the winter air, the way milk curdles: not spoiled all at once, but changed past recovery, and only noticeable when you tried to pour it.

He thought of his wife, eleven years dead, who had once told him he was the most honest dishonest man in Vellum Falls — that he'd invented neutrality because it let him feel clean while touching everything. He had laughed then. He did not laugh now.

* * *

On the last Friday before the solstice, Major Corvane returned, earlier in the day this time, and without his courtesy.

"The bulletin," he said, laying a fresh manuscript on the stone, "will concern sedition. Specifically, anonymous pamphlets currently in circulation. The governor wishes the public to understand that possession is a chargeable offense, and that the source has been identified." He watched Aldous with those arithmetic eyes. "You'll print it Saturday morning."

"Source identified," Aldous repeated carefully. "Identified as whom?"

"That's above your rate of pay, Master Dunmore." Corvane straightened his hat. "Though I'll say this much, since I respect craftsmanship: the pamphlets are well written. Too well. Good writing leaves traces. Hand habits. A fondness for certain constructions. Type remembers what its setter tries to forget."

He left. Aldous stood a long time with the manuscript unopened on the stone, snow ticking against the dark windows, and felt the shop around him — his shop, forty years of his hands in every cabinet and chase — suddenly full of listeners.

Above, faintly, he could hear the floor creak where Beatrix worked late, the small rhythmic sounds of composition drifting down through the boards like something being whispered through a wall.

Aldous looked at the manuscript, and at the ceiling, and at the tin box's hiding place, and understood that the neutral ground he had tended so carefully had never existed at all — that he had merely been standing on someone else's territory and calling it his own.

The question was whose.
