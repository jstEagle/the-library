## 5. Winter of Pamphlets

The governor's broadside wanted twelve hundred copies, and Beatrix gave him fourteen hundred, because a lie travels better when it is generous.

She set it in Caslon, the plainest face in the shop — *THE BRIDGE IS CLOSED FOR YOUR PROTECTION* — and locked the forme at half past four in the afternoon, while the light still held. Official work was done in daylight, done properly, with the shutters open and the apprentice boy fetching coffee from the corner house, everything visible to anyone who cared to look through the window of Dunmore & Son, Printers. Let them see her ink-stained fingers. Let them see her yawn over the galley. A printer who looked bored was a printer who looked safe.

By candlelight she did other work entirely.

* * *

It had begun with a single sheet left inside the returned copy of a trade journal, folded to the size of a prayer card. No signature. Only a sentence set in Baskerville italic: *You read type the way priests read scripture. We have need of a priest.*

She should have burned it. She told herself she would burn it, and instead she read it four times by the banked stove after her father went upstairs, and then she read it a fifth time, and on the fifth reading she understood that she was not deciding whether to answer but only deciding how quickly.

The reply took her three hours. Not the writing — the writing came out of her whole, like a tooth. It was the setting that took time, because she hid the message twice over: once in the words themselves, innocent enough, an inquiry about paper stock from a fictional mill; and once again in the spacing, a hair more space between certain letter pairs, a kerning pattern that spelled out, for anyone who knew her hand, the true message beneath. *I will print for you. No name. No payment. The words must be mine to choose.*

She left the slip tucked into the same trade journal on the same shelf, and did not touch it again, and thought about nothing else for six days.

The answer came back through the coal merchant, who was not a coal merchant, delivered as a delivery: a sack of fine paper, unrequested, uncharged, with a manifest written in a clerk's careful hand. She read the manifest the way she now read everything — first for what it said, then for what it hid. There were orders in it. Real orders. Routes, times, names reduced to initials, all threaded through the ordinary language of tonnage and price per hundredweight.

They had accepted her terms. They had also, without meaning to or perhaps meaning to exactly, shown her how much they trusted her: enough to send her their secrets in plain sight, folded into a coal receipt.

Beatrix stood in the cold doorway holding the manifest and felt something open in her chest that she could not afterward close. It was not fear. She kept waiting for it to be fear. It was closer to the moment the press comes down on the forme and the impression is perfect and she knows, before lifting the sheet, exactly what it will say.

* * *

She worked nights now, after Aldous retired, with the shutters barred and two candles shaded behind tin so no glow escaped to the street. She printed the rebellion's voice in quarto pamphlets of eight pages, stitched with thread from her own sewing basket: *Letters From Across the Water*, unsigned, unattributed, each one a small cold fire.

The prose was not hers alone — drafts came smuggled in the paper sacks, written by committee men whose names she never learned — but the drafts arrived rough, bloated, full of the kind of rhetoric that makes a hungry man feel lectured rather than fed. So she cut them. She tightened every sentence until it would fit in a workingman's mouth. She chose the examples, chose the rhythm, chose which grievance led and which followed. By the third pamphlet the committee had stopped sending revisions. Whatever she sent back, they printed. Whatever she wrote, the town read.

And Vellum Falls read. She saw it happen in increments. A carter on Front Street reciting a line about the grain levy to his laughing friends. A copy of *Letters* number two left deliberately on the counter of the chandlery, squared up neat as a hymnal, so no one could pretend it had blown there. The barber's boy charging a pin a read. Governor's notices began disappearing from the market board overnight, and the governor's replacements appeared each morning already curling at the corners, as if even the paste knew better than to hold them.

Her name was in none of it. That was the intoxication of it — that her hands moved across the town invisibly, that hundreds of people carried sentences she had made and did not know whose mind they sat in. She had spent her life making other people's words permanent: aldermen's proclamations, funeral broadsides, a fat man's advertisement for a lost dog. Now her own words walked abroad wearing no face at all, and every stranger quoting them was quoting her without knowing it.

She caught herself, one night, smiling at the forme as though it were a mirror. She stopped smiling immediately. But she noted, somewhere in the ledger she kept behind her eyes, that she had smiled at all.

* * *

Casper found out on a Tuesday.

She heard him come down the stairs — he still slept in the shop like a boy some weeks, refusing the house, claiming the smell of ink helped him think — and she had just time to snuff one candle before he rounded the composing stone and stood in the light of the other, barefoot, hair wild, staring at the flatbed press where the forme was not the governor's business at all.

Page four of *Letters*, number seven, locked in its chase. Eight hundred printed sheets stacked and hidden under tarpaulin against the wall.

"Bea." His voice came out strange, half whisper, half laugh. "What is this."

"It's printing," she said. "You know what printing looks like."

"Don't." He crossed the room and picked up a sheet, and she watched him read it, watched his lips move over the lines about the bridge garrison requisitioning winter flour, watched his face go through recognition and disbelief and arrive somewhere hot. "This is yours. This is your hand — I've read these since November, I passed the second one to the dock boys myself, I—" He stopped. "You wrote *the river does not recognize the bridge's keepers*. I underlined that. I memorized that."

"You shouldn't have memorized it."

"Bea." He sat down on the tarps as if his knees had been taken away. "Do you know what I've been doing? Every week I've been carrying these to Halvorsen's yard, folding them into fish crates, lying awake wondering who in God's name was writing them, wishing I were brave enough to write anything half as true — and it was you. In this room. Twenty feet above my head."

"Casper—"

"Does Father know?"

"No," she said. "And he won't."

But she saw at once that she had misjudged him, that she was still treating him as the restless boy who chafed at the shop, when the thing she had just witnessed was not restlessness but conversion. He read the page again in the candlelight. Then he began, unasked, to collate the next stack — quick, sure folds, the hands of a printer's son — and when he spoke his voice had gone level and adult.

"The committee meets at Halvorsen's on Thursdays. I'm expected. I've been expected since December." He looked up. "Now I'll have something to tell them."

"No," she said, sharper than she intended. "No names, Casper. Not theirs, not mine, not yours. You don't understand what you're—"

"I understand exactly what I am." He stood. In the candlelight he looked taller than he was. "I've been a courier for two months and ashamed of being only that. And now I find out the author of the thing I've been risking my neck for is my sister, and you want me to go on being a boy who carries fish crates."

"I want you to stay alive."

"That's Father's sentence," he said, almost gently. "Yours used to be different."

That landed harder than any argument could have, because it was true. Her sentences used to be different. She thought of the forme downstairs, of the words she chose, of the power she had felt opening in her chest like warmth — and here was its price walking around barefoot in front of her, wanting in.

She bargained, because bargaining was what she knew. "You may distribute. You may speak for the pamphlets anywhere but this shop. But you set nothing, you print nothing, and Father hears it from neither of us — not because we're ashamed, but because his name is on the window, and whatever happens to us happens to those presses tenfold. Both sides want them, Casper. You know that. If he suspects what's under his own roof he'll burn the type himself to keep us safe, and then there's no voice at all. Not mine, not yours, not anyone's."

Casper considered it for a long moment, and she could see the negotiation happening in his face, the part of him that wanted to shout his new self from the dockyards warring with the part of him that was, after all, their father's son.

"Distribution and speech," he said at last. "And Thursdays at Halvorsen's, openly, as a member. Not a fish boy."

"Not a fish boy," she agreed.

He put out his hand and they shook on it, solemnly, absurdly, like children closing a treaty — and then he pulled her into a hug that cracked one of her ribs, and she hugged him back, and for a moment they were just the two of them in the dark of the shop they'd grown up in, conspirators now, siblings still.

"We make a good government," she said into his shoulder.

"One of us should probably be elected," he said, and laughed, and the laugh broke the solemnity, and they fell to work together, collating by candlelight, faster with four hands than two.

* * *

Aldous noticed the candles. Of course he noticed; he noticed everything about wax and lead and paper the way other men notice weather. He said nothing, but Beatrix came down one morning to find the good beeswax stubs replaced by a fresh box, and a note in his upright hand propped against the till: *Mind your fingers near the flywheel. — A.*

He thought she was doing private job work, she realized. Wedding invitations, maybe, or a rival alderman's circular — moonlighting that shamed him a little and pleased him more, proof his daughter had the family hunger. She let him believe it. The lie sat in her stomach like a coin swallowed sideways.

Other things accumulated too. The governor's secretary began arriving personally with the official work, a soft-spoken man named Pell who watched her hands while she explained kerning and asked questions that were not quite questions: whether the shop did anonymous commissions, whether her father had political feelings, whether she herself ever wrote. She gave him answers polished smooth as furniture. When he left, she found she had been holding the composing stick hard enough to bruise her palm.

And the pamphlets kept going out. Nine. Ten. Eleven, in February, the boldest yet — a point-by-point rebuttal of the governor's flour requisition, naming dates, naming amounts, naming the warehouse where the garrison's surplus sat while the east end queued for ration bread. She wrote that one almost entirely herself. Reading the committee's raw notes, she had felt the sentences assemble in her head the way type assembled under her fingers, block by block, and when she finished she sat back and read it through and thought, with a satisfaction that frightened her a little: *this will do damage.*

It did. Within a week the ration queue had become a demonstration, the demonstration a march to the warehouse gates, and the governor's men fired over the crowd's heads — over, not into, but the sound carried the length of Front Street and broke something in the town that had been straining all winter.

Vellum Falls began talking about the pamphlets the way sailors talk about weather. *Number eleven called it.* *Number eleven knew before the proclamation did.* Someone chalked on the warehouse wall, in letters a story high: WHO WRITES THE LETTERS?

Pell stood in the shop that Thursday with the official requisitions and did not ask the question aloud. He didn't need to. Beatrix set the governor's notices in Caslon and smiled her bored smile and answered his not-quite-questions with furniture-polish words, and all the while she could feel his attention moving over the shop like a hand over a forme, feeling for letters that didn't belong.

That night, collating number twelve with Casper, candles shaded, she paused with a sheet half-folded.

"Cas. If it goes wrong — if they trace the type, the paper, anything—"

"They won't."

"Everyone says that until they do." She smoothed the crease with her thumbnail. "Promise me something. If it ever comes to that door coming down, you let me be the one who isn't here. You and Father are known. You're the Dunmores, the printers, the shop. Nobody knows my name exists in any of it. That's the whole point of what I built."

Casper looked at her across the candle, and for a moment she watched him weigh the promise, and she understood with a cold clarity that he was already deciding how much of it he intended to keep.

"Nothing's coming through that door," he said finally. "Except spring."

Spring was three weeks out and the ice on the river was still a foot thick. But two mornings later, coming down before dawn to heat the press balls, Beatrix found the shop door already unlocked — unlocked from outside — and lying on the composing stone, square in the center, weighted at the corners with slugs of lead:

a single printed sheet, in a typeface she did not recognize, listing the contents of pamphlets one through eleven, dated, described — and beside each, in a clerk's meticulous hand, a partial measurement of the column width.

Whoever had written it was narrowing the field. Whoever had written it knew exactly what a compositor's measure could prove.

And at the bottom, in the same careful hand, four words: *WE KNOW IT'S LOCAL.*
