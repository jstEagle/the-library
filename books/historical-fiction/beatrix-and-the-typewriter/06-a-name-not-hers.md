## 6. A Name Not Hers

The soldiers came on a Tuesday, and Casper had known they would since Sunday.

He had known it the way a man knows a storm is coming — not from any single sign but from the whole pressure of the air. On Sunday he had stood in the shop doorway and watched a lieutenant of the governor's constabulary walk the length of Millwright Street twice, unhurried, pausing at the corner to look not at the shops but up at them, at the upper windows, as if reading a page held at arm's length. On Monday morning a boy had left a copy of the *Vellum Falls Gazette* wedged in the shop's door handle, folded to page three, where an item no longer than a man's thumb reported that authorities in the capital had "in hand certain evidence" regarding the authorship of seditious pamphlets circulated through the river towns over the winter.

And Casper had read that item four times, and then he had gone upstairs and eaten his supper without tasting it, and watched his sister's hands move over the mutton as though nothing in the world were heavier than a fork, and thought: *They traced the type.*

It was the type. Of course it was the type. He had told her — God, he had told her a dozen times — that the governor's men had samples, that every press leaves fingerprints in lead the way every man leaves them in snow, that the little hooked serifs on her lowercase *f*, cut years ago by their father for a commissioning job and never duplicated anywhere in the valley, were as distinctive as a scar across a face. She had listened. She had nodded. And then she had gone on printing, because Beatrix could no more stop than the river could, and because — this was the part that frightened him, the part he carried now like a stone behind his breastbone — she was good at it. Her pamphlets were everywhere. The dockworkers read them aloud on the quay. The committee in the capital quoted them. Somewhere out in the frozen dark of the occupied valley, men who would never meet her were repeating sentences she had set in metal at two in the morning by candlelight, and those sentences had teeth.

*The governor calls the bridge closure a matter of weather. Count the cannons on the far bank and tell me what weather they predict.*

That one. That was the one they'd hang him for, if hanging came to it. He'd read it on the wall of the customs house himself, pasted up with a reward beneath it, and he had felt the strangest doubling sensation, standing there in the cold: pride and terror in the same heartbeat, twins fighting for room.

* * *

They came at nine in the morning, when the frost was still furring the windows. Four of them, plus the lieutenant from Sunday, whose name was Voss — no relation, though he introduced himself with a thin smile as if the coincidence amused him — and they filled the print shop the way iron fills a mold, displacing everything soft.

Aldous came down from the cutting room with sawdust on his sleeves and went very still on the bottom stair. Beatrix was at the composing stone, and Casper watched her hands, which did not shake, which kept working a line of ten-point bodoni as if the room contained only furniture — and that, more than anything, convinced him that she knew exactly what this was.

"Lieutenant," Aldous said. "We're plain tradesmen. If you're wanting job work, the book's on the counter."

"We're not wanting job work." Voss moved along the cases of type with one gloved finger trailing, touching the brass labels, *caslon*, *baskerville*, *dunmore no. 3*, like a man greeting acquaintances. "We're wanting an author."

"Authors are a different trade entirely," Aldous said. "This is a print shop. We print what's brought to us. It's an old rule of the craft — we don't ask the text its opinions any more than the paper asks the ink."

"A charming rule." Voss drew from his coat a pamphlet, creased and thumbed soft with handling. Casper knew it before it cleared the man's pocket; he had set a copy of it under his mattress weeks ago, and he felt the mattress-weight of it even now. The lieutenant laid it flat on the composing stone, next to Beatrix's galley, and smoothed it open with two fingers. "*The governor calls the bridge closure a matter of weather.* Ringing any bells in this establishment?"

Beatrix looked at the pamphlet the way a woman looks at a stranger's baby — polite, blank, faintly bored. "I've seen it about. The quay, mostly."

"And your type, Miss Dunmore. This distinctive lowercase *f*. Your father cuts lovely faces. Very particular serifs." Voss lifted the pamphlet and held it beside the galley of type at her elbow, tilting them both toward the window light. "Particular enough to swear to in a courtroom, I'd say. Particular enough to hang from."

The silence that followed had weight and grain. Casper heard the stove ticking. He heard the river, far off, grinding its ice. He saw his father's face going the color of the sawdust on his sleeves, and he saw — with a clarity that would visit him afterward for the rest of his life, in dreams, in doorways, at the bottoms of stairs — his sister's hands finally go still on the composing stone, and the look that crossed her face. Not fear. Something worse than fear. A kind of arithmetic, fast and terrible, done in the space of a breath: *what will this cost, and who pays it.*

She opened her mouth.

"No," Casper said.

Everyone turned. He hadn't planned to speak; the word had simply come out of him, the way a cork comes out of a bottle, and once he was open he found there was no stopping what poured. He stepped forward off the wall where he'd been leaning and put himself between the lieutenant and the composing stone, between the lieutenant and her, and heard himself say, in a voice that sounded remarkably steady:

"I wrote it."

"Casper—" his father began.

"I wrote it." He said it again, firmer, tasting how easily the lie sat in his mouth, how naturally it fit the shape of his tongue. And it wasn't entirely a lie, and that was the mercy of it, the thing that let him hold his chin level. He had believed every word of it. He had wanted it printed. He had carried copies through the snow in his coat and slipped them into coat pockets at the market and under doors along Cannery Row. His sympathies were no secret in his own chest. Only the handwriting was hers. "I wrote it, and I had it printed here because my father prints what he's paid to print and asks no questions. Every word of it is mine. You can take me for it."

Voss studied him for a long moment. The lieutenant had a narrow face, patient, the face of a man who does crosswords and finishes them, and Casper understood with sinking certainty that the man did not believe a word of it — that he had perhaps already noticed the difference between a printer's son who talked revolution at taverns and whoever actually composed those clean, surgical lines — but that belief was not the currency being traded here. Someone had to be arrested. Someone had to be produced. The paperwork required a body, and here was a body volunteering.

"Well," Voss said at last, folding the pamphlet away into his coat with something almost like courtesy. "That simplifies matters."

* * *

They let him gather his coat. They did not let him do anything else. When he moved — casually, so casually, one hand drifting toward the cold stove where three days ago a matrix had been burned, where the evidence had already been melted down and poured out and scattered among a thousand ordinary sorts — Voss's hand closed on his arm above the elbow, gently, terribly, like a father correcting a child at church, and shook its head.

At the door Casper stopped and looked back once. He made himself look at all of it: his father white-knuckled on the newel post; the presses standing silent and enormous, the great Washington hand-press like a crouching animal, the cylinder press his grandfather had bought secondhand; the slanted winter light falling across the stone where their family had set words for sixty years. And last, longest, Beatrix — Beatrix standing straight as a column of type, her lips pressed white, her eyes fixed on him with an expression he had never seen on her face before and hoped never to see again. It was not gratitude. It was horror. She had looked at him the way a person looks at a bill come due.

"It'll be a fine or a short stay," he said lightly, because someone had to say something and it might as well be the condemned man. "The cells at the custom house can't hold me. I've seen the locks."

Nobody laughed. He hadn't expected them to. He laughed anyway, alone, all the way down the steps and into the street, because laughing was a thing you did with your whole body and he needed his body to believe it was still his.

* * *

The cell was cold but not cruelly so; the constabulary wanted no deaths before trial. They gave him bread and a blanket and, after two days, questions. He answered them with a novelist's invention — no, that wasn't quite fair, with a printer's invention, careful, plausible, consistent — claiming sole credit for six pamphlets he had never set, describing composition sessions in taverns, attributing to himself phrases he had first read aloud on the quay with his heart hammering. The lieutenant Voss came each evening and listened with that patient crossword face, and Casper understood that the man was letting him talk, hoarding details, waiting for the lie to contradict itself somewhere, and Casper worked each night to keep the architecture sound.

What he had not accounted for was the guard change on the fourth night, and the boy — barely older than himself — who leaned against the bars during the quiet hour and said, conversationally, because prisoners and guards will talk when the night is long enough:

"They're saying in the yard your sister's the one with the clever hands. That the *f* gives it away. That they only took you because somebody had to hang for the crowd."

Casper lay on the plank bed and looked at the ceiling, where the torchlight from the corridor made shifting shapes like letters sliding in a stick.

"They're saying wrong," he said.

"Probably," the guard agreed comfortably, and spat, and walked on.

Casper closed his eyes. In the dark he did the arithmetic she had done in the shop, the same terrible sums, and arrived at the same total: that whatever happened at the trial, whatever bargain his father struck or money changed hands or names were signed, there was a version of the coming weeks in which Casper Dunmore was remembered by history as the author of things he never wrote — while the true author went on writing, invisible, unclaimed, her name attached to nothing she made. He should have felt relieved. He had saved her; that had been the whole design. Instead he lay in the cold and felt the strange grief of a man watching his own name get used as a shield, and knowing that shields are meant to be broken.

On the fifth morning, they moved him from the holding cell to a proper chamber in the custom house, with a table and two chairs and a clerk, and Lieutenant Voss came in rubbing his hands as though the room were warm, and sat down across from him, and smiled the smile of a man who has finished his crossword and started another.

"Good news, Mr. Dunmore," he said. "Or bad news. Depends, I find, on who's asking. You see — overnight, we received a communication. Anonymous, of course. Concerning certain evidence in your case." He laid a single sheet of paper on the table, face down, and rested his fingertips upon it. "Evidence suggesting that the authorship of these pamphlets may be rather more complicated than your confession allows. Now." He turned the chair slightly, settling in. "Tell me about your sister."
