## 7. The Bargain at the Bridge

They came for Casper at dusk on a Tuesday, and by Wednesday morning the town knew his name the way it knew the names of hanged men.

Beatrix did not weep. She had expected the knock — had half-hoped for it, some traitorous part of her whispering that if they took Casper, they would stop looking for the press — and so when the governor's sergeant read the charge from a folded paper she had once set with her own hands, she only stood on the shop's threshold with ink to her wrists and watched them walk him down Cordwainer Street. He did not look back. That was worse. A man who looked back was asking to be remembered; Casper walked like someone already practicing for a cell, shoulders squared, jaw set, performing courage for an audience of one.

The charge was sedition. The evidence was type — a slanted *f*, a chipped asterisk, the telltale spacing of the Dunmore compositors' house style. Authorship claimed in full, in Casper's own looping hand, signed twice.

Alone in the shop, Aldous sat down heavily at his desk and did not rise for two hours. Beatrix let him sit. She climbed the stairs to the type room, lit no lamp, and stood among the cases in the dark, listening to the river grind its ice beneath the pilings.

She had done this. Not Casper — never Casper, who could not have composed a seditious pamphlet if you put a gun to his spine; he still set quotation marks backwards when he was hurried. Her hands had made every line of it. Her eye had chosen the em-dashes. And now her words were going to stand trial in her brother's body, because he loved her enough to confess to something he could not even have written.

Grief is a slow thing. Rage is not. By the time Aldous's chair scraped downstairs, Beatrix had already stopped grieving.

* * *

The proof lived in three places, and she had made sure of it.

One copy sewn into the ticking of her mattress. One copy pasted as endpapers inside a water-damaged folio of sermons that no one, in six years of inventory, had opened. And one copy folded small against her breastbone, wrapped in oilcloth, riding above her heart like a second pulse.

It had taken her four nights after the arrest to decide, and one night to act. The decoded correspondence — the orders threaded through the confiscated manuscript's kerning, the ones naming Major Corrane and Captain Ilse and two other officers of the governor's own garrison as buyers of rebel intelligence, sellers of troop movements, men who wore the government's blue while feeding the committees everything the government wished hidden — that proof was worth more than Casper. Worth more than the shop. It was, if she judged rightly, worth the careers of four officers and possibly the governorship itself, and both sides wanted it: the rebels because it would gut the garrison's command from within, the governor because he would give anything to make it unexist.

Which meant, Beatrix reasoned, standing before the mirror with her collar buttoned to the chin, that it belonged to neither. It belonged to whoever would trade for it. And there was exactly one thing in Vellum Falls she wanted bought.

She wrote two letters that night in a hand not her own — she used her father's, stiff and upright, because her own hand was known now, might be traced, and she would not hang a second Dunmore signature on anything. One letter went under the door of the Committee of Ink's safehouse on Tanner's Row. One went, by way of a laundress who owed the shop thirty years of good prices, to the office of the governor's provost.

Each letter said the same thing, addressed to no one:

*I hold the Corrane correspondence entire. I will trade it for the release of Casper Dunmore, unmarked and untried, on the last barge out. I will not sell it twice. Whoever comes second gets ash.*

She signed it with a single character: the chipped asterisk. Let them chase their ghost through the type cases. She was done being a ghost's instrument. If the revolution insisted on wearing her letters as its skin, then she would learn what it felt like to be the hand that moved.

* * *

The rebels came first, because rebels always do — hungry people move faster than frightened ones.

He arrived at closing time wearing a carter's coat over boots too fine for a carter, and Beatrix served him a spool of twine and a ream of foolscap across the counter while Aldous inventoried the back room, until the man said, quietly, "You set a pretty page, miss. We've admired your work."

"Have you." She cut the twine. "We stock three weights."

"You know why I'm here."

"I know you're paying for twine." She met his eyes. He was younger than she'd expected, with a schoolmaster's careful voice, and the exhaustion around it was real. "And I know that Major Corrane dines with Captain Ilse every Thursday, and that between the two of them they've moved your arms caches through the east checkpoint for five months without losing a crate. I know who else knows, and I know how many days it would take the provost to learn it if my hand slipped. Shall I itemize further, or shall we discuss my brother?"

Something shifted in the man's face — the particular stillness of a person recalculating. "You read the Hollis manuscript."

"I read everything that comes through this shop. That's rather the point of a print shop." She lowered her voice to match his. "Casper Dunmore walks out of the provost's jail and onto the rebel barge when the ice breaks. In exchange, the original and every copy I've made comes to your committee, complete, before any officer learns it exists."

"And if we simply take it? Take you, take the pages, search this place?"

Beatrix reached beneath the counter and laid the oilcloth packet on the wood between them, but kept two fingers on it. "Then you'll find the first three pages, and learn that pages four through nine are in the provost's keeping already, timed for delivery unless I send word otherwise. I'm a compositor, sir. I believe in redundancy." She slid it back into her bodice before he could move. "I learned the trick from the men who wrote the code. Never trust a secret to a single hiding place. Or a single side."

The schoolmaster studied her for a long moment, and Beatrix watched him do the arithmetic — the value of the proof against the insult of bargaining with a printer's daughter, the risk of her against the risk of the provost getting there first. She watched him arrive at the answer she had built for him.

"The committee will want assurances," he said.

"The committee can have my word. It seems to be worth more than it was a month ago."

He almost smiled. It was not a friendly smile. "You understand what you're making yourself, Miss Dunmore. A woman who sells to one side today can be bought by the other tomorrow. Neither of us will ever quite trust you again."

"Yes," Beatrix said. "That's the price. I've seen the ledger. My brother's life against my good name." She rang the bell on the counter, sharp as a closing argument. "Twine is fourpence. The rest we'll settle when Casper is on the water."

* * *

The provost's man came the next morning, and she gave him the same bargain with different arithmetic: the proof in exchange for Casper's release into her custody before the thaw, charges dissolved, the family's file closed. He was older, grayer, better at hiding his recalculation, but he arrived at the same sum. Both sides left convinced she would deal with them and betray the other. Both sides were correct.

Aldous found out on Friday.

She should have told him. She knew that even as she chose not to, because telling him meant watching his face do what faces do, and she could not yet afford the weight of it. Instead he found out the way fathers find things — by accident, by lamplight, by coming up the stairs at midnight to say something about the funeral arrangements he was grimly planning for his son's future and finding his daughter at the type case, sorting sorts with the oilcloth packet open beside her, copying out the Corrane correspondence a third time in case the first two burned.

"What is that." Not a question. Aldous's voice had gone flat, which she had learned long ago was his fury's true register.

"It's insurance."

"That is treason, Beatrix. That is treason in triplicate, on my counter, under my roof—"

"On your counter?" She heard her own voice climb and did nothing to stop it. "Whose counter did Casper sign his confession on, Papa? Whose type hanged him in advance? You keep saying *my shop*, *my roof*, as if walls ever protected anyone in this town. Walls just mark where the bodies fall."

Aldous crossed the room and took the packet, and she let him — let him read, by candle stub, the orders she had pulled letter by letter from another man's typography, watch his face go gray at Corrane's name, at Ilse's, at the sums. When he finished, his hands were shaking, and hers were not, and she understood in that moment that something had reversed itself between them forever.

"You decoded this," he said. "Months ago. Before the pamphlets. Before—" He stopped, because the arithmetic was arriving. Before the arrest. Before Casper. Which meant she had held this all along, and chosen her moment, and her moment had cost her brother eleven days in a cell.

"I waited," Beatrix said quietly, "until it was worth the most. That's what Papa does with paper stock. Buy cheap, spend dear."

"No." Aldous set the pages down as though they were hot. He looked at her the way he had once looked at a misregistered plate — grief and admiration tangled past separating. "No, that is not what I do. I stall, I hedge, I tell myself stories about neutral men, and my son is in a cell because of it. What you did—" His voice failed and rebuilt itself. "What you did was colder than anything either army has done to this family. You turned yourself into currency, Bea. Both sides will spend you, and neither will mourn you."

"Then they'll have learned what you taught me," she said. "Everything in this trade is lead. You melt it into whatever shape you need."

The silence afterward was long. Downstairs the presses ticked as the floor settled, the sound the shop made when it dreamed. Aldous picked up his candle.

"When they bring him home," he said at the door, not turning, "I'll be glad. God help me, I'll be glad, and proud, and I will never again entirely know whose daughter you are." A pause. "Set the eight o'clock forme before you sleep. The governor's proclamations won't wait on our consciences."

She worked until dawn. And when the forme was locked, when the type sat square and obedient in its iron frame, Beatrix pressed her palm flat against the cold metal and understood what she had become: the one thing Vellum Falls had never had — a person fluent in both armies' secrets, owned by neither, trusted by none, holding pages that could break the garrison or save it.

Outside, the ice on the river groaned — a long, deep sound, earlier in the season than any thaw in living memory.

It was breaking early. And somewhere downstream, a barge was loading.
