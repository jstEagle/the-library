## 8. Thaw

The ice broke on the fourth of February, a full month before its time, and it did not break politely.

Aldous Dunmore was at the press, cleaning rollers he had no orders to run, when the sound came up the street — a long groan from the river, deep as a struck bell, followed by a crack that scattered the crows from the churchyard elms. He set down his rag and went to the door with the unhurried steps of a man who already knew what he would see. The whole town was in the street. Down at the water, the gray sheet that had held Vellum Falls prisoner since November was heaving itself apart, plates of ice the size of barn roofs rearing up and shearing along one another with a sound like grinding teeth. The river had woken angry.

Behind it, far off across the flat white distance, the bridge stood empty in the haze, its far end lost in smoke that was not fog. Aldous had watched that smoke for three days now. It did not move like weather moves. It sat and thickened and occasionally spat a dull orange pulse, and each pulse meant something burning on the capital side — a depot, a barracks, a print office, God knew. The fighting had come to the water's edge. It would cross when the river let it.

He stood on his own doorstep in the cold and thought, with terrible clarity: *A month early. Nothing about this winter has gone as it should.*

* * *

The news of the barge came to him the way everything came now — sideways, in fragments, carried by people who would not give their names.

He was at Hessel's buying lamp oil because the shop burned it past midnight more nights than he cared to admit even to himself. Hessel leaned across the counter, wiped a spot on the wood that did not need wiping, and said, in the flat voice of a man reciting weather: "There's a barge coming up from the mills Thursday. Rebel flag, but they're honoring passage papers. One berth left after the cargo. One."

"One," Aldous repeated.

"One." Hessel set the oil can down between them, carefully, as if it were fragile or dangerous. "You didn't hear it from me. And you'd want to be at the dock by dawn, whoever's going. They won't wait on the ice being friendly. It's moving all night."

Aldous paid for the oil and walked home through streets that felt newly provisional, as if the thaw had loosened not only the river but the ground under every building in town. One berth. He turned the arithmetic over and over and it always came out wrong. Three of them. Casper, whose name was on a rebel muster roll by now — Aldous had known for weeks, had said nothing, had hated himself for the silence and been more silent still. Beatrix, whom both sides had learned to need, though neither understood yet how much. And himself, with his two presses and forty years of type, wanted by a governor who wished to strangle truth slowly and a committee that wished to publish it in haste.

One berth. His wife had been dead eleven years, and there were moments — this was one — when he wanted nothing so much as to lay the problem in her lap and let her solve it, the way she had solved everything, with that dry look over her sewing glasses. *Well, Aldous. Whose life is worth the most? Answer honestly, and then answer again, because the first answer will be a coward's.*

He laughed alone in the stairwell, and the laugh came out wrong, and he stopped it.

* * *

That evening he found Beatrix in the composing room, and the sight of her stopped him in the doorway the way a locked door stops a man who thought he held the key.

She was setting type by candlelight — his type, lead he had poured and matrices he had cut across thirty years — but she was working faster than he had ever taught, faster than was careful, her hands moving with a fluency that had passed beyond craft into something like speech. She didn't look up. She was talking to herself in the language of the trade, half-aloud: *em quad, thin space, hang the indent* — and the forme taking shape under her fingers was not any job he recognized. Small type. Dense. A pamphlet page, and not the governor's.

He should have been afraid. Some cold part of him registered that he ought to be. But what he actually felt, standing unseen in the dark of his own shop, was an old printer's awe — the same feeling he'd had at nineteen, the first time he pulled a proof so clean it seemed the words had always existed and he had merely uncovered them. His daughter had become that clean a proof. Whatever she was printing, whatever war she was feeding with his lead and his candles, it was good work. It was better than good work.

"Your ink is low in the right well," she said, without turning. "I've been meaning to tell you for a week."

"How long have you known I was there?"

"Since your third breath." She looked up then, and in the candlelight her face was one he had to work to find the girl in — the girl who used to sort quoins by color for the pleasure of it, who fell asleep against the tympan at eight years old with a picture alphabet open on her chest. She was there. But she had been built around now, the way a building keeps its foundations under new floors. "Sit down, Papa. You've been circling the stairs for an hour. You only do that when you've decided something and haven't told anyone."

He sat. The candle guttered and steadied.

"There's a barge Thursday," he said. "One berth."

Her hands went still over the type. Not startled — still, the stillness of someone closing a ledger to total the column. "I know," she said. "I set the notice myself. In the mill's type, not ours." A pause. "So. Who are you going to send?"

"That's rather the question, isn't it."

"Casper." She said it immediately, without hesitation, and it stung him in some place he hadn't known was exposed — that she had answered before he could, that his own daughter had done the choosing while he was still rehearsing it. "He's marked now. If the governor's men hold the town, they'll hang him for the pamphlets whether or not—" She stopped herself, but not quickly enough.

"Whether or not they were his," Aldous finished quietly.

Beatrix looked down at the forme. "Whether or not they were mine," she said.

There it was, laid out on the stone between them like a case of type spilled across the floor. He had known for months — since the arrest, since the bargain at the bridge he still could not think about without his hands going cold — that the pamphlets were hers. Her cadence. Her fury, pressed small. He had never accused her of it, because accusation implied he had ever had authority over it, and somewhere in that winter he had lost track of exactly when that had stopped being true.

"I'm not sending anyone," he said. "I'm asking. There's a difference, and I've been trying to remember all day whether I've ever earned it."

"You've earned it." She reached across and put her hand flat on the forme, covering her own words. "Ask me."

"The barge takes one. I'm old, Bea. My eyes are going; I mis-justify a line twice a week now. Casper is young and marked and believes things, which is a combination that gets men killed here and makes statesmen elsewhere. You are—" He stopped. He had prepared a sentence for her and found he could not deliver it. "You are the best printer in this shop. That is no longer the most accurate description of you, and we both know it."

"And you want to give me the berth." Her voice was very even. "Because you've decided I'm the future of the trade, or the family, or the truth, or whatever we're calling it this week. Papa. Listen to me. I don't want it."

"You don't mean—"

"I mean I'm not getting on that barge." She lifted her hand. The type gleamed wet-black in the candlelight. "And before you say it — neither is Casper, if I can help it, and here is why. He'll be needed here. When the fighting crosses, whichever side holds this town, they're going to want a press running by noon the next day. Both sides. And the man who runs it needs to be a man they've already decided to trust — a rebel with a martyr's arrest behind him. Casper survives here. Casper *works* here. But you—" For the first time her voice moved, cracked along a seam. "You have presses the governor wants and the committee wants and neither will forgive you for keeping. The moment the shooting starts, this shop becomes the most dangerous room in Vellum Falls, and the safest place for its owner is anywhere else. Take the berth, Papa. Please. I've worked it through four times. It's the only arrangement where nobody has to bury anybody."

Aldous sat with it. Outside, the river boomed and ground, tearing itself into pieces, and the sound filled the pause the way organ music fills a church.

"And you?" he said at last.

"What about me?"

"What happens to you, in this arrangement of yours? Which of us buries you?"

She smiled then, and it was the smile that undid him — not hard, not frightened, but *settled*, the smile of a person who has finished a long calculation and checked it twice. "Nobody," she said. "I stay. Somebody has to keep an account. Someone literate in the type, someone both sides underestimate because she's the daughter, the sister, the girl at the counter. I've spent a winter learning exactly how invisible that makes a person, and I intend to spend the spring making it useful." She took his hand. Her fingers smelled of ink and lamp oil, his own smell returned to him transformed. "You taught me that a printed thing outlives the hand that set it. Well. Somebody has to stay near the press."

* * *

He did not sleep that night. At two in the morning he went down to the shop, not to work but to be among his things, and found Casper awake in the back room, sitting on a type cabinet with his coat on, watching the window like a man on watch, which perhaps he was.

"Papa." Casper stood. "The barge. Beatrix told me."

"Then you know what she's decided."

"I know what she's *ordered*," Casper said, and for a moment he sounded twelve years old again, indignant at the unfairness of sisters. Then it faded, and something older came into his face. "She's right, isn't she. All of it. I ran it through and I can't fault a line of it. She thinks the way I used to imagine generals thought." He rubbed his face with both hands. "When did that happen? When did she get to be the one who decides?"

Aldous looked at his son — tall, marked, alive because of his daughter's cleverness and his own silence — and heard himself answer with the truest thing he knew.

"It didn't happen to her, Casper. It happened inside her, sometime this winter, and none of us were looking. That's the trick of children. You spend years believing you're the mold, and then one morning you notice the shape walking around isn't yours at all. It never was. We just held it long enough for it to set."

Casper was quiet a moment. "And the berth. You'll take it?"

"I'll take it." Saying it aloud made him feel every one of his fifty-eight years and lighter than he had felt in twenty of them, both at once. "Under protest. Under many protests, which I will lodge with the captain, the committee, and anyone else unfortunate enough to ask."

Casper laughed — a short, cracked thing, but real — and then crossed the room and embraced him, hard, the way boys embrace fathers when no one has taught them a gentler version, and Aldous held on and let the boy's shoulders shake and did not trust his own voice enough to use it.

* * *

Thursday came gray and raw, the river running brown and fast with its broken winter. Aldous rose before dawn, packed one case — two shirts, his wife's miniature, and a single font of twelve-point roman, his favorite, because a man does not cross into exile without his type — and stood a last time in the composing room, where Beatrix was already at the stone, dressing a new forme by lamplight as if it were any other working morning.

"Manifest says one passenger," she said, not looking up. "It'll read Aldous Dunmore, printer. It won't mention luggage." A glance at him, quick and bright and unreadable. "In case anyone official counts heads at the dock."

He understood then what she intended, and opened his mouth to argue, and found nothing — no argument she had not already composed, justified, and locked into the chase. His daughter had set his escape in type before he consented to it. He closed his mouth. Some proofs you simply pull.

They walked to the dock together through a town holding its breath, and the barge sat low in the water with its rebel rag at the mast and its single berth waiting, and upriver, faint but unmistakable, came the first sound he had been dreading since November — not the groan of ice this time, but gunfire, flat and distant, like a door slamming repeatedly in a house two streets away.

Beatrix squeezed his hand once at the gangplank, hard, and let go.

"Write it down," she said. "All of it. Everything you saw. Someday I'll print it under a name everyone will recognize."

Whose name, he almost asked — but the captain was shouting, the lines were cast off, and the current took the barge sideways into the brown flood before he could form the word. He stood at the stern rail watching his town shrink, his daughter already a dark figure on the dock turning away, walking upstream toward the smoke and the shop and the war, upright as a line of type, and he understood, with the finality of a forme locked tight, that history had chosen her long ago —
