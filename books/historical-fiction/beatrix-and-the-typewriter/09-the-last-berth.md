## 9. The Last Berth

The ice went out of the river on a Tuesday, with a sound like the world's largest door being forced. Beatrix heard it from the composing stone, her hands still black with the morning's ink, and she did not need to look up to know what it meant. The thaw had come early. Everything that had been held apart all winter was about to be let loose at once — the water, the armies, the truth.

The barge was called *Mercy of Coal*, which everyone in Vellum Falls agreed was either a joke or a prayer, and it would make one crossing before the fighting reached the bridge. One crossing. One berth.

Aldous had told them at supper the night before, his voice carefully level in the way she had learned, over these long winter months, to distrust. "One berth," he said. "I'll take it. The presses are wanted by both sides, and if they fall into the wrong hands—" He stopped himself, because they both knew there were no right hands anymore, only hands.

Casper had said nothing. He sat with his spoon standing upright in cold soup and looked at the wall where their mother's sampler used to hang before he sold it for gun money, and Beatrix watched him and understood that he had already decided to give the berth back. That was Casper. He would surrender anything except the chance to surrender something.

She had gone upstairs after supper and taken out the manifest.

It was a plain document, freight paper, the barge company's stock, and she had set its type herself two winters ago when the company still paid promptly and the river still belonged to no one in particular. She knew its every quirk: the way the clerk's hand looped his *f*s like fishhooks, the way the column for *NAME OF PASSENGER* ran narrow so that long names had to be compressed or abbreviated. She knew how the clerk blotted his entries, how the ink feathered leftward on damp nights.

She also knew, because she had printed the governor's own requisitions all winter, exactly what an authentic manifest looked like when it bore the seal of the Office of River Transport. And she knew, because she had spent four months becoming someone else entirely in type, how to make a document say one thing while meaning another.

One berth. But a berth, read narrowly, was a name. And a name, set well, could hold two men the way a line of type holds two words — snug against each other, kerned so tight no reader thinks to separate them.

*ALDOUS DUNMORE*, she wrote, in the clerk's looping hand, practicing first on scrap until the fishhook *f* came natural even though there was no *f* in it, only habit. Then, beneath, in smaller script, the notation the company used for servants and dependents: *and man-servant, traveling as accompanied*. The ink was Aldous's own iron-gall, aged in the bottle until it matched. She blotted it leftward. She breathed on the page to make the ink feather just slightly, just enough.

It was the finest forgery she had ever set, and she had set pamphlets that toppled a captain of dragoons, and she felt nothing at all while she made it. That frightened her more than the gunfire upriver would frighten her by morning. Her hands were steady. They had simply done this kind of work too long to tremble at it anymore.

* * *

The dock at dawn was gray and crowded with everything the town wanted gone: crates, coffins, two grand pianos nobody could explain, a cage of geese that screamed like the whole revolution in miniature. The *Mercy of Coal* sat low in the water, already straining at its lines, and the bargemen moved among the cargo with lanterns though dawn was coming up rust-colored behind the hills.

Beatrix walked between her father and her brother and carried the forged manifest folded inside her glove, and neither of them knew yet. She had decided they would learn on the dock, where it was too late to argue. This was not cruelty. It was typography: you locked the chase before the press can run, and once it is locked, argument is useless.

Aldous stopped at the gangplank. He was carrying only the sample case — the little mahogany box of specimen typefaces he had cut across thirty years, each letter a small monument to some year of his life — and he stood holding it against his chest like a breastplate and looked at the water.

"One berth," he said again, to her, not to Casper. His voice had the flatness of a man who has rehearsed a hard sentence so many times it has worn smooth.

"Yes," Beatrix said. "Read the manifest."

He took the paper from her glove. She watched his eyes travel down the column, watched him arrive at the notation, watched his lips move over *accompanied* without sound. Then he looked up, and for a moment she saw in his face every stage of her life at once — the girl at the composing stick, the stranger who had traded secrets at the bridge, the forger, the daughter — all of them passing over him like frames of a flip-book, too fast to hold.

"Beatrix." Her name in his mouth sounded like an error he had caught too late for correction. "This says—"

"I know what it says. I wrote it."

Casper had gone very still beside her. He was in the rebels' gray coat now, openly, and there was a rifle slung across his back that their father pretended not to see. "Take the berth," he said to Aldous, low and urgent. "Both of us. She's made it so. Take it and don't waste it arguing with her, because I know my sister and she will stand on this dock until spring before she lets us off this boat."

"That is precisely what frightens me," Aldous said. But his hand closed around the manifest, and crumpled it slightly, and then, deliberately, smoothed it flat again — and Beatrix knew, watching that small motion, that he had surrendered. Not to her. To the type itself, which does not argue.

"The presses," he said. "If they come for the presses—"

"They'll find them," she said. "That's rather the point. Let them fight over lead, Father. Lead doesn't choose sides. I do."

He made a sound that might have been a laugh in some earlier year. Then he did the thing he had never once done in her memory: he put his arms around her, sample case and all, and pressed his cheek to the top of her head, and she smelled ink and lamp oil and the cold iron of the shop, and she allowed herself, for exactly three heartbeats, to be his child.

Then Casper's turn. He held her by the shoulders at arm's length and grinned his old grin, the one from before the bridge closed, and said, "You know what you've written about me in those pamphlets? You made me braver than I am."

"You're welcome," she said. "I'll make you braver still, next edition."

"Don't." The grin fell away all at once. "Don't print me anymore, Bee. Print the truth. Just — print the truth, and let me be ordinary in it."

She promised. It was the only promise she ever made him, and later, holding the chronicle in her mind like a locked chase, she would keep it.

The bargeman shouted the five-minute warning. Gunfire had started upriver an hour ago — a soft, intermittent knocking, like someone setting type very far away — and now it thickened, and smoke rose past the bend in a column so straight it looked drawn with a rule.

Aldous boarded first. At the rail he turned and raised one hand, not waving — *casting*, she thought wildly, his fingers spread the way he spread them to catch stray sorts on the stone — and then Casper went up the plank backwards, facing her, walking toward the future the way you walk into deep water.

They cast off. The lines came in dripping. The *Mercy of Coal* swung its blunt bow into the current and the current took hold of it the way history takes hold of people — gently at first, then completely.

Beatrix stood on the dock with her empty hands and watched them shrink. Her father at the rail. Her brother beside him. Two men, one berth, one forged line of type carrying more weight than any she had ever set, and she thought: *this is what I am for.* Not the pamphlets, not the codes, not the cleverness. This. Putting the ones you love into a line that will hold them, locking the chase, and letting the press come down.

The barge cleared the point. The smoke from upriver leaned out over the water and began, very slowly, to cover it.

She turned and walked back toward the shop through streets that smelled of burnt powder and thawing mud, and behind her the gunfire kept its distant rhythm — knock, knock, knock — like a compositor working the night shift somewhere beyond the hills, setting the next page of a country.

The shop door was unlocked; she had left it so, meaning to be gone. Inside, everything waited: the stone, the sticks, the cases, her father's specimen drawer still half open where he'd taken his last measure of type. On the bed of the old Washington press lay the forme she had locked two days ago and never had courage to run — ten pages, quarto, the first installment of the true account, and beneath the final line, where a pseudonym should have gone, a blank space she had left deliberately, the size of a name.

Beatrix hung her coat on its peg. She lit the lamp. Upstream, faintly, through the walls, the knocking went on.

She reached for the case marked with the typeface she had cut herself, alone, in the small hours, letter by letter — and began to fill the blank space with her own name.
