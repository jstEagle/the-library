## 10. Set in Her Own Type

The new year came in on a wind that smelled of smoke and river ice, and Beatrix Dunmore spent its first quiet hours exactly as she had spent so many others: alone in the shop, her sleeves rolled to the elbow, her hands black to the second knuckle.

But this time the type was hers.

She had cut it herself — every letter, every serif — through the autumn weeks after the barge left, working by tallow-light at the bench her father had built, with his gravers and his gauges and the patience he had taught her without ever meaning to. He had always said a typeface was a voice cast in lead. *Choose your letters the way you'd choose your words,* he told her once, holding a piece of type to the lamp so she could see how light pooled in the counters. *A face can lie even when the text doesn't.* She had not understood him then. She understood him now. The face she had cut was narrow and unornamented, honest as a ledger line, with a slight irregularity in the lowercase *e* that no foundry would have tolerated and that she loved precisely because it could never be mistaken for anyone else's work. When she set a line of it, the page looked like her handwriting felt.

The chronicle ran forty pages. *A True Account of the Closed Bridge*, she called it — no flourish, no pseudonym, no borrowed authority. Below the title, where an anonymous pamphlet would have carried nothing and a loyalist one would have carried the governor's seal, she set three words in her own new type:

*By Beatrix Dunmore.*

Her hand shook when she pulled the first proof. Not from fear — she had passed beyond fear sometime in December, when the shelling upriver had become as ordinary as church bells — but from the strangeness of seeing her own name in ink. She had spent a year signing herself as nobody: the anonymous hand behind the pamphlets, the printer's daughter, the woman whose words were paid for in Casper's blood and redeemed with secrets she should never have owned. Casper had taken her authorship onto the scaffold's edge and come back changed, thinner, older, refusing to speak of those nine days in the guardhouse. Aldous had sailed away carrying her forgiveness like contraband. And all winter the town had read her sentences without knowing they were reading her.

Now they would know.

That was the danger, of course. The governor's men knew her name; so did the committee's. She had bought her survival by making herself useful to both sides and trustworthy to neither, and an open declaration — forty pages naming names, dating dates, setting down who had ordered the bridge closed and why, who had profited from the winter of shortages, which officers had sold coded correspondence to the very rebels they were shelling — was a confession of allegiance to truth alone, which both sides would treat as allegiance to the other.

She printed anyway. Two hundred copies on the old Washington press, then two hundred more, working until dawn, hiding the sheets in the paper loft beneath bales of rag stock while the ink cured. On the third morning of the year, the fighting reached Vellum Falls properly — the boom of artillery close enough to rattle the windows, the smell of burning from the granary district — and in the chaos of that morning, while soldiers of both sides marched past the shop without sparing it a glance, Beatrix went out into the streets and began leaving books behind her.

One on the step of the church, where the curate would find it and be too decent to burn it. One on the counter of the coffeehouse, weighted with a coin. A dozen bundled and left with Widow Harsch, who had lost two sons to the bridge closure and could be trusted to distribute faster than any postal service. Copies slipped into the reading room at the hotel. A copy laid deliberately, almost tenderly, on the governor's abandoned desk in the customs house, which she entered at noon behind a clerk who had forgotten to lock it — because whatever else happened, the man who had closed the bridge would read her account, and know it was true, and know whose hand had written it.

By evening the town had changed hands. There was no particular ceremony to it. The governor's green coats withdrew across the frozen fields eastward, and rebel columns came up the river road singing, and men who had spent a winter whispering in doorways suddenly embraced in the open street. In the square, someone tore down the proclamation board — the one that had announced the bridge closure as a matter of weather and prudence — and Beatrix stood in the crowd and watched it crack against the cobbles.

An officer with mud on his boots stopped her near the fountain. He was young, exhausted, carrying one of her own pamphlets from autumn folded in his breast pocket — she recognized the kerning before she recognized the title.

"Did you see these?" he asked, holding up a copy of the chronicle someone had already passed him. "They're saying a woman printed them. That she put her name right on the front."

"They're saying correctly," Beatrix said.

He looked at her a long moment — at her ink-stained fingers, at the smudge of black along her jaw where she had pushed back her hair with a dirty hand. Something moved across his face that might have been awe, or suspicion, or simply the exhaustion of a man discovering the war was stranger than his orders.

"Then you'd best come with me," he said. "There are people who will want to shake yours."

* * *

Her father read the chronicle for the first time in the spring of 1903, in a rented room above a bakery in a city whose name he still occasionally mispronounced, four years and one ocean away from Vellum Falls.

The copy had traveled further than he had. It arrived wrapped in brown paper, postmarked twice, forwarded through three addresses, with a note in an unfamiliar hand explaining only that a bookseller in the capital had mentioned keeping it behind the counter for a gentleman named Dunmore, should such a gentleman ever surface. Aldous sat down on the edge of the bed with the parcel still in his lap for a full minute before opening it, because he had learned, in exile, to dread packages — they usually meant money owed or money asked.

Inside was the book. He knew it at once by its weight, by the smell of its ink, by the particular narrow honesty of its letterforms — and then he turned to the title page and saw, set in letters no foundry in the world possessed, the name he had been afraid to hope he would see.

*By Beatrix Dunmore.*

His hands were not steady anymore; exile had taken that from him along with his presses, which stood somewhere now in a government depot, or perhaps melted, he preferred not to determine which. But they were still printer's hands, and they read the book the way they had taught his daughter to read type — feeling the impression, weighing the spacing, listening to the voice in the lead.

It was her. Not the anonymous pamphleteer's voice, urgent and masked. Not the careful, neutral voice she had used to survive the winter. This was something he had never heard from her: unhurried, exact, unsparing of anyone including herself. She wrote of the bridge closure in dates and figures. She wrote of the governor's officers selling code to the enemy, and of the committees' own cruelties, the hoarded flour, the neighbors denounced on thin evidence — she spared neither side, and she did it without a single flourish, in a face as plain and level as a level itself. And in the chapter about the arrests — his breath caught here, and he had to set the book down and look out the window at the unfamiliar street for a while — she wrote plainly of Casper's claim of authorship, of the nine days in the guardhouse, and of what she called *the debt I cannot repay, being the debt of another man's courage purchased with my silence.* Casper, alive, working the docks in the same city, had gone very quiet when Aldous showed him the passage that evening, and had finally said only, "She got it right," in a voice that suggested getting it right had cost her something.

But it was the last pages that undid him.

She had appended an account of the final barge — the manifest, the forged line, the single berth that had carried two men out of a burning town under one name — and she described her own choice there without sentiment, as a fact of arithmetic and love. Then, on the final leaf, she had set a short note to the reader, and Aldous read it four times and then aloud, once, to the empty room:

*This chronicle is set throughout in a typeface of my own cutting, the first I have made. I cut it in the winter after my family left, in a shop that belonged to my father and now belongs to me, though ownership in wartime is a rumor. I say this not from vanity, though I have earned some small vanity, but because the whole story of the closed bridge is a story about voices — who is permitted one, who must borrow one, who dies wearing someone else's. My father taught me that type is a voice cast in lead. For most of my life I spoke in other people's letters. These pages are set in mine.*

Aldous closed the book and held it against his chest the way he had once held his children, and sat in the bakery-smelling dusk until Casper came up the stairs with bread and cheap wine and found his father weeping without any shame over a forty-page book.

"She cut her own type," Aldous said. "Do you understand what that means? Do you understand what she—"

"I know," Casper said, and sat down beside him, and poured the wine.

There was a blank leaf at the back of the copy, and on it, in a hand Aldous did not recognize but could guess at — quick, sure, slanted the way her composing stick slanted — a single line of ink:

*Papa — the shop stands. So do I. Come home when you're ready; the press misses you, and so does your daughter, who signs her work now.*

Beneath it, unmistakable even in handwriting, even across an ocean and four years of war and peace: *Beatrix Dunmore.*

Aldous read it until the light failed. Then he began, quietly, to make plans.
