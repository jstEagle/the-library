## 1. The Summons

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which Felix Whitlock would later consider fitting, because Tuesdays were the days his master pretended to inventory the brass stock and mostly drank tea and complained about the government. The envelope bore the crest of the Royal Observatory, Greenwich, pressed into black wax with the kind of authority that made the shop's three crooked shelves of clock parts seem suddenly, shamefully dusty.

Marlow read it twice. Then he set it face-down on the bench, which was the first strange thing.

"Well?" Felix asked. He was fitting a mainspring for a widow in Deptford whose mantel clock had taken to striking thirteen times at midnight, a fault he privately found charming but had promised to cure.

"It's nothing," Marlow said. "A routine repair."

"Then why are you holding it like it's a bill from the tax man?"

His master was sixty-one years old and built like the furniture he refused to replace—solid once, now gone soft at the joints. He had taught Felix everything: how to listen to an escapement the way other men listened to horses, how to file a pivot so true you could balance a hair on it, how to stand in a room so quietly that people forgot you were there. That last one Felix had learned too well. It was not a skill so much as a condition. Twenty-three years old, and he could walk past his own reflection without startling it.

Marlow turned the letter over. Turned it back. "They want the master clock looked at," he said at last. "It's been losing time. Four seconds in a week."

Four seconds. Felix whistled low. At the Observatory, where the great regulator governed every railway timetable and naval chronometer in the country, four seconds was not a delay; it was a scandal. Ships navigated by that clock's time. Men had been known to lose their commissions over less.

"And they've asked for *me*?" Felix said. The word came out brighter than he intended. His first solo commission. Three years of apprenticeship spent grinding pinions and being sent out only for the humblest calls—a church clock here, a banker's repeater there—and now this. The Observatory itself.

"They've asked for the workshop," Marlow corrected. "And I'm sending you because my hands—" He flexed them, as if demonstrating some tremor. There was no tremor. There had never been a tremor. "My hands aren't what they were."

Felix looked at those hands, steady as ever on the bench, and said nothing. That, too, was a habit.

* * *

He packed his tools that night while Marlow sat by the stove pretending to read a catalogue of Swiss movements he'd owned since before Felix was born. The workshop occupied the ground floor of a narrow house off Cree Lane, and it was cramped in the way of places where a man has lived alone too long—every surface claimed, every drawer labeled in Marlow's small precise hand. Felix knew that hand better than any handwriting alive. He had spent two years of evenings copying it, stroke by stroke, until he could produce a passable Marlow signature between heartbeats. A game, Marlow had called it. Discipline for the fingers. Every apprentice needed a party trick.

The tool roll went into the satchel first: screwdrivers with handles worn to the shape of his grip, oilers, tweezers, the loupe that had been Marlow's own before it was his. Then the good files. Then, after a moment's thought, his spare spectacles, though his eyes were fine.

"You'll want to check the suspension before anything else," Marlow said, not looking up from his catalogue. "If it's losing time evenly, it's the pendulum's effective length. Temperature. But four seconds isn't even, I'd wager. It never is."

"I know how to diagnose a regulator, Master."

"I know you do." A pause long enough to hold a whole conversation. "That's rather the problem."

"The problem?"

Marlow closed the catalogue. In the stove light his face did something complicated—pride and dread wrestling for the same chair. "Greenwich is not Cree Lane, boy. Up there, every gear you touch belongs to the Empire. You'll be watched. Measured. There will be men who'd like nothing better than to find a workshop like ours wanting." He rose, crossed the small room, and put both hands on Felix's shoulders, which he almost never did, and which frightened Felix more than any scolding could have. "Do the work. Touch nothing you don't have to. Speak to no one longer than courtesy requires. And come home."

"Master, it's a clock."

"Yes," Marlow said softly. "It is. Isn't it."

* * *

The morning was October-grey, the river running the color of pewter, when Felix climbed the hill to the Observatory with his satchel and his heartbeat doing unscheduled work. Flamsteed House rose white against the sky, and beyond it the great dome of the telescope, and below it all London lay spread out like something spilled and glittering. He had seen it a hundred times from the far side of the river and never once from here. Being unnoticed meant, among other things, never being invited anywhere.

At the gate a porter checked him against a list, frowning. "Whitlock? The letter said Marlow."

"My master's indisposed. I'm his journeyman. The work will be done properly—you have my word and his."

The porter looked as though words were poor currency indeed, but a clerk was summoned, and the clerk was younger and more bored, and boredom admitted Felix where suspicion might not have. He was led through corridors that smelled of paper and lamp oil, past doors with brass plates bearing titles he didn't dare read slowly, until they reached the stair to the clock tower.

The master clock stood in its own chamber like a patient awaiting surgery: a regulator of serious reputation and current disgrace, its case tall and dark, its pendulum swinging with a rhythm just slightly, unforgivably wrong. Felix stood before it and felt the sensation he always felt—the world narrowing to brass and steel, everything else going soft at the edges—and then felt something new on top of it: terror. Because this time, if he failed, the failure would be official.

He opened the case. He put his ear close. And there it was, beneath the tick: a whisper of drag in the train, faint as a secret told behind a hand.

"There you are," he murmured.

"Talking to it already?"

Felix straightened so quickly he cracked his head on the case hood. A woman stood in the doorway—not a servant, not a clerk, judging by the ink stains on her fingers and the way she held a ledger against her chest like a shield. She had sharp grey eyes and the particular stillness of someone who catalogued things for a living, and she was, unmistakably, cataloguing him.

"I wasn't—I was listening," Felix said.

"To what?"

"The third wheel. It's binding somewhere. You can hear it if the room's quiet."

"It's never quiet here," she said, but she tilted her head, listening anyway, and for three full seconds the two of them stood together in silence like conspirators, waiting for a wheel to confess.

She didn't introduce herself. She asked instead, in an offhand way that fooled no one, whether he was the one who would be handling the Observatory's records access forms, since repairs required logging, and logging required signatures, and signatures went into the archive. Her eyes stayed on him the entire time, friendly as a lockpick.

"I only sign for my own work," Felix said carefully.

"Everyone signs something eventually," she replied, and left, her footsteps fading down the stone stair.

He stood a moment with his hand still resting on the open case, telling himself the odd feeling in his chest was only the climb, the altitude, the unaccustomed attention. Then he bent back to the clock, and the whisper of the dragging wheel filled his ears again, patient and small, like a truth waiting to be wound out one tooth at a time.

It would take him three days, he estimated. Perhaps four.

He would finish on Friday, pack his tools, and go home, and none of it—none of it—would be any of his business.

Behind him, unnoticed even by Felix, a courier's boots passed along the corridor above, carrying sealed letters down from the astronomers' wing, one of which was addressed in a dead man's hand.
