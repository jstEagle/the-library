## 2. A Stranger with a Letter

The thing about working in the archives of the Royal Observatory, Beatrix Calloway had decided long ago, was that it was rather like being a lighthouse keeper for other people's lives. Ships came in—letters, logs, manifests, receipts—and she stood at her lamp and steered them to their shelves, and no one ever thought to ask whether the keeper herself might once have been a ship.

It was a Tuesday in October when the ship came in wearing a boy's coat and a guilty expression.

She heard him before she saw him: the particular arrhythmic thud of someone running who did not want to be caught running. Couriers were meant to walk. The Observatory's corridors were hushed as chapels; even the astronomer's clerk, Mr. Pemberton, whose voice could carry across a parade ground, modulated it here into something resembling reverence. A courier who ran was a courier who hoped to be gone before anyone asked questions.

Beatrix set down her pen with great care, the way she did everything, and stepped out into the corridor.

He was young—fifteen perhaps, all elbows and anxiety—with a leather satchel clutched to his chest like a stolen loaf. He skidded to a halt so abruptly that his boots squeaked against the flagstones, which was how she knew he was new to this route. Old couriers knew the floors of the Observatory better than they knew their own mothers.

"Delivery," he said, as though announcing a diagnosis.

"For whom?"

"Doesn't say." He produced a sealed packet from the satchel. Cream paper, wax the deep red of arterial blood, addressed in a slanting hand to *The Astronomer Royal, by private hand only*.

"Then you'll forgive me," said Beatrix pleasantly, "for asking what it does say."

"Nothing else. That's all there is."

She held out her hand for the packet, because that was procedure—the archive received all correspondence, logged it, copied the addresses—and because her heart had begun doing something entirely unprocedural. It had started climbing.

Because she knew the hand on the front.

Not merely recognized it. Knew it. Knew the way the capital *A* leaned back like a man tipping his hat. Knew the little hook the pen made at the end of *Royal*, a flourish Edmund had invented at eleven years old to annoy their father and had never, apparently, grown out of, because Edmund never grew out of anything. Knew the way the ink sat slightly heavier on the downstrokes, because Edmund pressed too hard, always, as if the paper might otherwise forget him.

Three years ago the Admiralty had sent her mother a letter in a different hand entirely—a clerk's hand, gray and tidy—to inform her that her son had gone down with the *Meridian* off the coast of Portugal. There had been a storm. There had been a list of the lost. There had been, eventually, a stone in a Greenwich churchyard with an empty grave beneath it, because the sea keeps what it takes and gives back nothing for the grief.

Edmund Calloway was dead. Dead men did not address packets to the Astronomer Royal in handwriting that leaned back like a man tipping his hat.

"Who gave you this?" Beatrix asked. Her voice came out level. She was rather proud of that, later.

"A man at the docks."

"Which man?"

"Just a man. Paid me sixpence." The boy's eyes darted past her shoulder, calculating the distance to the door, freedom, breakfast.

"What did he look like?"

"Tall. Grey coat. Had—" The boy shrugged, the universal gesture of witnesses everywhere, surrendering detail before it could be demanded. "Look, miss, I just carry 'em. I don't read 'em and I don't remember 'em. Sixpence is sixpence."

"Where at the docks?"

"Near the Custom House. Miss—" He was edging backward now, satchel and all, like a crab who had heard about nets.

"Stay a moment—"

But he was gone, boots squeaking indignantly all the way down the stairs, and Beatrix stood alone in the corridor holding nothing at all except the certainty that her brother's hand existed somewhere in the world outside her memory.

* * *

Procedure, as has been established, was Beatrix's native language. She returned to the archive, sat down, and logged the delivery in her fair round script: *Correspondence received, 14th October, 1889. Sealed packet, addressee: Astronomer Royal. Courier: unknown, juvenile. Provenance:* —and here her pen hovered for a long moment before she wrote— *uncertain.*

Uncertain was not a word she used often. Uncertain was an open drawer in a room full of locked ones. But she found she could not write *the docks* without also writing everything else, and everything else would have required three pages and possibly a constable.

Mr. Pemberton appeared at the archive door precisely as he always did—at the moment least convenient—dusting biscuit crumbs from his waistcoat.

"Calloway. There's a packet come up from town, has there? For the Astronomer?"

"It's in the log."

"I'll take it up myself, then." He held out a pudgy, expectant hand. Pemberton believed that documents, like soup, should not be allowed to stand.

"Of course." She fetched it, watched him tuck it under his arm without a glance at the direction or the seal—Pemberton never glanced at seals; seals were beneath the dignity of a man who had opinions about biscuits—and listened to his footsteps fade toward the upper rooms.

And then she sat very still for approximately four minutes, which was, she felt, a reasonable amount of stillness given the circumstances, before admitting to herself that four minutes was not going to be enough.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about grief, Beatrix thought, climbing the narrow stair to the record room where the older ledgers lived: it does not stay buried. You put it in the ground with honors and a stone and your mother's good black gloves, and then one Tuesday afternoon a boy with squeaky boots walks through your corridor carrying your dead brother's handwriting in a leather satchel, and you discover that grief has simply been waiting. Patiently. Like a cat by a mousehole.

Edmund had joined the merchant service at seventeen because he wanted to see the world and could not afford to buy a ticket. He had written home constantly—terrible letters, rambling, funny, full of sketches of albatrosses that looked suspiciously like chickens. He had promised to bring her back a shell from every port. He had kept that promise eleven times, and the shells still lined her windowsill in Blackheath, and sometimes, in the worst months after the letter came, she had wanted to throw them all into the river and had instead spent an entire evening polishing them, which told her something about herself she was not sure she liked.

The storm off Portugal had taken the *Meridian* and twenty-two souls. The Admiralty's records were thorough. Beatrix knew, because in the first raw year she had requested them—every scrap: the manifest, the weather reports, the survivor lists (there were none), the inquiry notes (there were hardly any). She had read them until she could recite them. And she remembered, with the terrible precision of the bereaved, that some of those pages had bothered her. Small things. A manifest amended in two hands. A date corrected. Nothing she could have named then, when every page hurt equally. But she had been an archivist even then, and something in her had filed the discomfort away, and now the mousehole cat was awake.

The record room smelled of dust and lamp oil and the particular sweetness of old paper, which Beatrix maintained was the smell of time itself. She pulled the *Meridian* file again—the same file she'd requested eleven times, until poor Mr. Hobbs at the desk stopped asking why—and spread its contents across the reading table.

Storm report, 3rd June, 1886. Casualty list, dated 10th June. Letter to next of kin, 12th June. All orderly. All gray.

Except.

She laid the casualty list beside the storm report and looked at the dates the way she'd taught apprentices to look at watermarks: softly, letting the eye go unfocused until discrepancies surfaced like fish.

The *Meridian* had sailed from Lisbon on the 29th of May. The storm was the 3rd of June. The casualty list was drawn up the 10th—but the certificate of death recorded aboard, the one signed by the ship's master, bore the date of the *1st of June*. Two days before the storm.

Dead men did not write letters. And drowned sailors, apparently, did not die before the storm that killed them.

Unless they had never been drowning at all.

Beatrix sat back. The lamp hissed gently. Somewhere above her, the great time ball on the roof waited for one o'clock, as it always did, faithful beyond all deserving.

"That's twice," she said aloud to the empty room, because saying things aloud helped her think, and because the room had long ago stopped judging her. "That's twice, Edmund Calloway, and I am keeping count."

* * *

It was on her way back down—ledgers reshelved, hands washed, face arranged into its usual pleasant neutrality—that she saw the envelope.

It lay on the floor of the corridor outside the Astronomer's office, half-tucked against the wainscoting where a broom might have swept it and missed. Cream paper, matching the packet's. Blood-red wax, cracked along the fold where someone had opened it carelessly. No address remaining—the outer cover, discarded once the inner packet had been extracted.

Pemberton, she thought. Pemberton opened things in corridors, standing up, like a barbarian. Pemberton filed the inner papers and dropped the envelopes for the charwoman, and the charwoman had evidently not come yet.

Anyone honest would have picked it up and put it in the waste bin.

Beatrix picked it up and, after a pause measured in fractions of a second, slid it into her pocket instead. Her heart hammered in a manner most unbecoming an archivist. The envelope was light as a moth against her ribs.

In the privacy of the stairwell, she examined it. Nothing useful on the outside—no direction, no postmark, only the faint ghost of an impression where the inner packet's address had pressed through. She carried it to the archive, laid it flat, and did what archivists do: she took a soft pencil and rubbed the shadow of the pressure marks onto paper, coaxing up the ghost of the writing.

*...by private hand only...* — the same instruction, mirrored and faint.

And below it, smaller, angled into the corner where Edmund always tucked his postscripts, a single line:

*Tell B. the twelfth shell was never a cowrie.*

The pencil slipped from her fingers.

She read it four times. Then she folded the rubbing into small precise squares, put it inside her glove for want of anywhere safer, and sat down slowly upon her stool, laughing a little in a way that alarmed even her, because the alternative was weeping in a government archive, and Calloways did not weep in government archives.

The twelfth shell. There had been eleven shells on her windowsill. Edmund had always sworn the twelfth—from Valparaíso, the port he'd never reached on his last voyage—was his finest find, and had teased her for years that he'd send it when she least expected it. She had assumed, after the letter came, that the twelfth shell had died with him at the bottom of the sea.

It was a message. It was proof. It was a joke, an Edmund-shaped joke, arriving three years late by way of a careless clerk and a dropped envelope, and it meant—

It meant the dead do not write letters, but the living write them in code, and her brother was alive, and somebody in this building was receiving instructions in his hand, and she was going to find out who if it took the rest of her natural life.

The clock above the archive door ticked on, indifferent and exact. Beatrix straightened her collar, squared her shoulders, and resolved, with the quiet ferocity of a woman who had spent three years being patient, that patience was hereby concluded.

What she needed now was someone who understood handwriting—not a clerk's opinion, but a craftsman's. Someone who could look at ink and tell her not merely *whose* hand it was but *when*, and *how*, and whether a hand could lie.

She had heard, that very morning, that a clockmaker's apprentice was coming up from the town to mend the master clock.

Fate, Beatrix thought, pocketing the envelope more securely, does love an apprentice.
