## 3. Gears and Ghosts

The master clock of the Royal Observatory, Felix decided on his second morning, was not broken so much as heartbroken.

He sat inside its case with a lamp balanced on his knee, one arm buried to the elbow in the great going train, and listened to it argue with itself. The escapement ticked—then hesitated, the way a person does before saying something unkind. The pendulum swung with perfect dignity, pretending nothing was wrong, while behind it three years of accumulated neglect ground away in miniature: dirt packed into the pivots like grief packed into a widow's house, worn teeth, a suspension spring so tired it had begun to whisper.

"You're not ill," he told it quietly. "You're lonely. Nobody's touched you properly since old Hargreaves retired."

The clock, being a clock, did not answer. But when he eased the pallet stones free and began to dress them with fine emery, the hesitation shortened by half a second, which for a timekeeper of this consequence was practically an apology.

Felix liked talking to clocks. It was a habit Master Marlow had never cured him of—had, in fact, encouraged, on the theory that a boy chattering to himself drew no attention whatsoever, whereas a boy who seemed clever invited questions. *Be dull, Felix,* the old man always said, wiping his spectacles with that cloth he kept folded in his sleeve like a conjurer. *Dull men keep their fingers. Clever men lose them.*

Felix had been dull for nineteen years. He was very good at it.

He was so good at it that it took him another quarter hour to notice he was no longer alone.

She stood at the tower doorway with a ledger under her arm and a pencil tucked behind one ear, and she was watching him—not idly, not the way visitors watched the great clock as a wonder of the age, but the way he himself examined a movement: cataloguing. Her eyes went from his hands to the disassembled pallets laid out on flannel in strict order, to the lamp, to his face, then down to a small notebook she produced from her pocket. She wrote something. The pencil went back behind her ear like a soldier returning to post.

It was the archivist. Miss Calloway. He'd seen her twice from a distance, once carrying a stack of volumes taller than herself and refusing assistance with the expression of a woman who had been offered assistance before and found it wanting.

"May I help you?" he asked, keeping his voice at workshop pitch—flat, modest, forgettable.

"I'm watching you work," she said, without a trace of embarrassment. "Is the oil you're using whale-based or mineral?"

"Whale. Mineral leaves a film on the steel after a season."

"Hm." She wrote that down too. "And the pallets—you've dressed all four faces, but you've only replaced one stone."

"The other two are sound. Replacing sound parts teaches a clock bad habits."

Another note. Felix felt an odd sensation he could not immediately name, and then could: it was the discomfort of being *seen*. His whole training had been in the art of not occupying space. Here was a woman with a notebook treating him as though he occupied quite a lot of it.

"You write everything down," he said, to fill the silence.

"That's the job." She came further into the tower, her footsteps careful among the scattered tools. "Records fail the same way clocks do. Slowly, then suddenly. A clerk skips an entry here, transposes a date there, and thirty years later nobody can prove what happened to anything." She looked up at the great wheel turning above them both. "Or anyone."

There was a weight on the last word that Felix felt settle somewhere beneath his ribs.

* * *

She came again the next day, and the day after. By the fourth day Felix had stopped pretending surprise and started leaving a second stool by the case, which she took without acknowledgment, as though it had always been there.

Her name, she allowed, was Beatrix. She was responsible for the observatory's correspondence archives, meteorological logs, and—"a small private matter"—the records of the Board of Visitors. She spoke of paper the way Felix spoke of brass: with tenderness, precision, and a low simmering fury at whoever had last mishandled it.

"A junior clerk filed the 1886 storm reports under shipping," she said darkly, on the fifth afternoon, "under *shipping*, as though the weather were a commercial concern. I found them because I re-shelve by hand. Nobody re-shelves by hand anymore. They trust the system." She said *the system* the way Master Marlow said *the Admiralty*. "Systems are just mistakes wearing uniforms."

Felix laughed before he could stop himself—a real laugh, surprising them both. Beatrix looked at him with sudden interest, the way she might look at a movement that ran truer than its label promised.

"You don't laugh like a dull man," she said.

"I'm told I'm very dull."

"Mm." She opened her notebook, then closed it again without writing. "Mr. Whitlock, may I ask you something improper?"

"I'd be disappointed if you didn't, at this point."

"How would a person tell whether a written record had been altered? Not forged badly—I mean altered *well*. Same ink, roughly same period, same hand. A date changed. An entry inserted. What would survive?"

Felix kept his eyes on the escapement. His hands, fortunately for him, had been trained never to stop working while his mind did something dangerous. He fitted the suspension spring to its block, the tiny screw finding its thread by touch.

"Pressure," he said at last. "Even the best pen betrays itself in pressure. A man writing naturally lets the stroke breathe. A man copying—or composing slowly, thinking about each letter—the strokes go even. Too even. Like a heartbeat under anaesthetic." He turned the screw home. "And ink sits on top of old ink differently than on fresh paper, if you know where to look under the lamp. And the holes. Punch holes and file marks never lie. If pages were replaced in a bound volume, the piercings won't match."

Silence. When he glanced up, Beatrix was looking at him with an expression he couldn't read at all, and had gone rather still.

"That's a remarkable amount to know," she said carefully, "for a clockmaker."

"My master authenticates documents sometimes. Provenance work for collectors. He made me learn the mechanics of it—said forgery and horology were the same trade." He meant it as a joke. It came out sounding like a confession, and he heard it land.

"Did he," said Beatrix.

The clock ticked between them, honest as only machines are.

* * *

It was on the seventh evening, with the tower going amber and the light falling in long bars through the observation slits, that she finally told him.

Felix was rehanging the pendulum, and she was perched on her stool with her legs crossed at the ankle like a schoolgirl who had outgrown school but not curiosity, and she said, without preamble:

"I had a brother."

Felix set the pendulum down very gently. "Had?"

"Edmund. Merchant marine. Lost in the Atlantic storm of November '86—so says the record." She recited it flatly, the way one recites a thing learned by rote until the words wear smooth of meaning. "Ship *Cormorant*, reported foundered off the Azores. No wreckage recovered. No bodies. They issued the death certificate anyway; they usually do. My mother died believing it. My father follows her now at the pace grief allows."

"I'm sorry," Felix said, and meant it.

"So was I. For three years." She picked up a discarded gear tooth from the flannel, turned it over in her fingers. "Then last week a courier came through the courtyard with sealed instructions for the Astronomer Royal, and I saw the address, and Mr. Whitlock, I have read four thousand letters in my brother's hand. I wrote to him every fortnight for eleven years. I would know that handwriting if it were carved on the moon." Her thumb whitened on the gear tooth. "It was his."

The clock ticked. Somewhere below, the river worked at its moorings.

"The dead do not write letters," Beatrix said. "So either my brother lives—and everything the records say is false—or someone writes in a dead man's hand, which is worse, because it means someone wanted him dead *on paper*, and spent effort doing it." She finally looked up at him, and her eyes were bright and terrible and steady. "I've been through the logs. Quietly. There's an entry inserted around the twelfth of November '86—a weather confirmation, routine, except the pagination shifts by half a line and one punch hole is bored twice. Altered *well*. Exactly the way you described."

Felix's mouth was very dry. "Miss Calloway—"

"Beatrix."

"*Beatrix.* Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you're the first person in this building whose hands I've watched for seven days, and they never hesitate, and they never perform." She stood, smoothing her skirt with the brisk motion of a woman resuming armor. "Because you knew in an instant how a good alteration is done, which means you know how to catch one. And because—" for one moment the steadiness cracked, and underneath it was simply a tired young woman who had grieved alone for three years, "—because I am so very tired of carrying this by myself, and you look like a man who knows how to keep quiet things quiet."

Felix thought of the workshop. Of Master Marlow's spectacles-wiping pause, the cloth folded in his sleeve, the way the old man had pressed this commission into Felix's hands with unusual urgency—*go in my place, boy, I'm needed here, mind you say nothing of the shop's business.* Of the letter Beatrix had shown him yesterday, or half-shown him, angling it toward the lamplight: *Dear Miss Calloway, regarding final settlement of your brother's estate—* in a hand Felix could copy blindfolded, because he had, hundreds of times, since he was nine years old.

His master's hand. Wearing Edmund Calloway like a glove.

"I'm not brave," Felix said. "I want that understood up front. I've spent my whole life being unnoticeable on purpose. It's a trade, like any other."

"And?"

"And—" he looked at her, at the fierce careful grief of her, and felt invisibility curdle into cowardice somewhere in his chest, "—I think I may know whose handwriting taught me to spot a forgery. And if I'm right, then we're circling the same secret, Miss Calloway, from opposite ends of it."

She went very still. "Whose?"

Felix looked up at the great clock, its restored heart beating above them, telling the true time to a city that trusted it absolutely, and thought: *if a machine can be mended, why not a life.*

"Give me two days," he said. "Two days, and I'll show you the hand that wrote your brother's letter."

"It belongs to someone you love," Beatrix said. Not a question. She had catalogued him too well for that.

"Yes," said Felix. "That's the trouble."
