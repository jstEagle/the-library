## 4. The Hand I Know

Beatrix had rehearsed the approach eleven times on the walk from the archive room to the clock tower, and by the twelfth telling it almost sounded like an ordinary professional errand.

*Mr. Whitlock. A question of documentation. Nothing personal. Nothing desperate.*

The envelope sat in her apron pocket like a coal that refused to cool. She had carried it for three days now—carried it to bed, carried it to breakfast, carried it once into a committee meeting where she'd been asked for the 1886 tidal records and had instead recited, to the confusion of all present, the postal rates for foreign correspondence. The other archivists had begun giving her the look one gives a person who is visibly carrying a secret: polite, slightly widened eyes and a wide berth.

She climbed the tower stairs with her ledger clutched to her chest as a shield. The door at the top stood open, and from within came the small, industrious sounds of a man talking to machinery.

"No, you don't," Felix Whitlock was saying as she stepped onto the platform. "You don't get to sulk. I know exactly how many teeth you have, and I will count them if I must."

He was flat on his back inside the clock's skeleton, only his boots and the crown of his head visible, a jeweller's loupe screwed into one eye. A lantern hung from the great escape wheel above him like a small captive moon. He hadn't heard her. Of course he hadn't; the clock filled the world up here, its tick so vast it stopped being sound and became weather.

Beatrix waited in the doorway and permitted herself, briefly, the indulgence of watching him work. There was something restful about a person who was good at a thing and knew it and was too busy doing it to perform it. Felix Whitlock did not fill rooms. He was a narrow, careful young man with ink-stained cuffs and the posture of someone who had spent his life being told to stand behind things. But his hands—his hands were another matter entirely. They moved over the mechanism with a tenderness Beatrix had only otherwise seen in surgeons and in her mother, deadheading roses.

Then he saw her, sat up too fast, and cracked his head on the great wheel, which boomed like a funeral bell.

"Miss Calloway." He emerged rubbing his skull, ears pink. "I didn't—I was just—" He gestured vaguely upward, as though the clock might corroborate his alibi.

"I can see what you were just," Beatrix said. "How is she?"

"She?" He blinked. "Oh. Oh, the—the clock. She's improving. She's been running eight minutes slow since before I came, which for this establishment is rather like a priest turning up to church drunk."

It was the first joke she'd heard him make, and it surprised them both. He looked faintly alarmed at himself, as though the humor had escaped without authorization.

"Eight minutes," Beatrix said, settling onto the work stool he'd cleared for her—the clearing itself done with a flurry of apologetic sweeps of the arm. "The whole empire runs on this clock, doesn't it? Ships, trains, everyone setting their watches by the time ball."

"That's the idea." He picked up a rag and began wiping hands that were already clean. It was, she noted, a nervous habit. He had several. He arranged his tools when anxious, squared them against a leather roll with the edges perfectly parallel. He was squaring them now.

"Mr. Whitlock," she said. "May I ask you something outside the scope of escapements?"

"You may ask me anything," he said, and then, hearing himself, added hastily, "within reason. Within the bounds of reason."

She drew out the envelope.

* * *

She watched his face while she did it, because watching faces was half her trade. Archives taught you that documents lied most elegantly in their margins; people lied there too.

"This came to the observatory on Tuesday," she said. "Sealed instructions, courier-delivered, no return address. Routine enough—we get correspondence constantly, corrections, requests, the usual dust of administration. But I open and log everything that touches the record rooms, and when I opened this one—" She turned the letter toward him, keeping her fingers steady through an act of will. "Tell me what you see."

Felix took it the way he took everything, carefully, by the edges, as though it might startle.

"It's a delivery instruction," he said. "For a crate to be held at the observatory pier rather than the customs house. Dated the fourteenth. Hand is—" He stopped.

His thumb had moved across the paper, quite unconsciously, tracing the downstroke of the capital F. Beatrix saw the exact moment recognition arrived. It came not as surprise but as something worse: confirmation. His face did the terrible arithmetic of a man who has just realized the answer was always going to cost him.

"Whose hand, Mr. Whitlock?"

"It's—" He set the letter down on the bench with exaggerated care, the way one sets down something that might go off. "It's a copperplate hand. Commercial training. Common enough."

"Felix." She used his Christian name deliberately, and watched it land like a hand on his shoulder. "I have read my brother's handwriting every day of my life. I learned my letters copying it. He wrote me forty-one letters from four oceans and I have every one of them in a box under my bed, and this—" her voice did something she hated, wavered, and she pressed on, "this is either Edmund Calloway writing three years after he drowned, or it is the finest forgery in England. And I need to know which, because the first means my brother is alive, and the second means somebody wanted me to think so badly enough to risk this. Either way, I need a workshop that knows hands. Your workshop does authentication work. I checked."

"You checked," Felix repeated faintly.

"I check everything. It's my whole character." She gentled. "I'm not asking you to break any confidence. I'm asking whether Marlow & Sons could verify a hand. Yes or no."

And there Beatrix made her error, though she couldn't have known it: she gave him a moment to decide, and a man like Felix Whitlock, given a moment to decide, would choose to protect whatever he loved first—and at present that was a frightened, loyal, misdirected love for the man who had raised him, wearing the borrowed costume of discretion.

"Yes," he said. "We could look at it. Certainly. Bring it round, and Master—Mr. Marlow could examine it properly. He's very thorough. Very—" He squared a tool that was already square. "Very thorough."

Silence. The clock ticked its enormous patience over them both.

"That's your answer?" Beatrix said quietly.

"That's my answer."

"All right." She stood, retrieved the letter, and held it a moment before tucking it away. "One more thing, then, and then I'll leave you to your sulking machine. When you took the letter just now, before you said a single word—your thumb moved. Here." She touched the side of her own thumb, the pad of it. "Once, along the stroke. People do that when they recognize writing. Not when they recognize a *style*, mind—a style they'd study with their eyes, like a picture. A thumb-move is the body saying hello to something it knows intimately. Like touching a scar."

Felix had gone very still. Beneath his collar, his pulse was visible at the throat, quick as a trapped bird.

"I don't—"

"And," Beatrix continued, relentless now because if she stopped she would begin pleading, and she had sworn not to plead, "when I said my brother's name, you didn't ask who Edmund was. Everyone asks who Edmund was. You looked at the floor as though it owed you money. So here is what I think, Mr. Whitlock, and I'll say it plainly because plainness is all I have: I think you know whose hand this is, and it isn't only my brother's, and you are afraid of something far larger than a dead woman's grief."

The lantern hissed. Somewhere below, the observatory murmured on, indifferent.

"I never said you were a dead woman's—" He stopped. Started again, and his voice came out low and roughened. "If I knew something. Anything. What makes you certain I'd tell you true, even then? People lie, Miss Calloway. Kind people. Loyal people. Lying's not the vice you think it is; sometimes it's a debt you pay for other people's sins."

"Then pay a different debt," Beatrix said. "Because I have spent three years being lied to gently by an entire empire, Felix, and gentle lies are the cruelest kind—they let you go on loving the dead comfortably, in the dark, like a candle left burning in a window for a ship that sank the day it left harbor." Her voice cracked on the last of it and she let it. She was tired of being composed. "I am not asking you to betray anyone. I'm asking you to consider what your silence buys, and for whom, and whether the price isn't being paid out of my account."

He looked at her then—really looked, the first full unguarded look she'd had from him—and something in his face broke open like ice in spring. Guilt, she thought. Good. Guilt meant conscience, and conscience meant there was a door.

"I'll bring it to the workshop," he said at last, hoarse. "The letter. If you'll trust me with it."

"That's the trouble," Beatrix said. "I haven't any choice. You're the only thread I've found, and I intend to pull until something gives."

* * *

She left him there among the gears, and he listened to her footsteps descend the tower stair—steady, unhurried, the steps of a woman who had decided things—and Felix Whitlock stood alone in the vast ticking dark and admitted the truth to the only witness who'd keep it.

He knew the hand. He had known it at first touch, the way one knows a parent's step on the stairs. That capital F with its heavy foot and flourished crossbar; the way Marlow crossed his sevens twice, always twice, an old habit from ledger-keeping; the little backward lean to the whole line, like a man perpetually glancing over his shoulder. Felix had spent six years learning to produce it. He could write it in the dark. He had written it in the dark, more times than he cared to count—invoices, receipts, the master's correspondence when his hands were bad with rheumatism. Forging Marlow's hand was the second thing Felix had ever been taught, right after how to hold a file.

Which meant the letter in Beatrix's pocket might be her brother's writing.

Or it might be Felix's own—or something near it—written by the man who had fed him, clothed him, kept him invisible and safe.

"Oh no," Felix said aloud to the clock, and his voice sounded very small inside all that brass. "No, no, no."

He thought of her thumb-touch demonstration. He thought of her face when her voice had cracked—how she'd let it crack, refusing even the shelter of composure, spending her dignity openly in front of a stranger because the truth was worth more than pride. And he thought of Master Marlow at the workshop door three days ago, gripping Felix's sleeve with those ruined hands and saying, *Go alone, lad. Don't mention where you're from. Don't mention me,* with a fear in his face that Felix had been too green to read and could not now unsee.

Invisibility had been a fine coat while it fit. He had worn it for twenty-three years and mistaken it for a virtue. Now he understood it was simply a coat, and that he had been warm in it while a woman walked the cold asking questions no one would answer.

Felix picked up his loupe, set it down again, and looked up into the great wheel-work looming over him, patient and enormous, every tooth accounted for.

"I have to go home," he told the clock. "Early tonight. There are floorboards I've never looked under, and I find I've stopped trusting floors."

The clock ticked on, keeping its own counsel, and above Greenwich the afternoon light thinned toward evening as Felix Whitlock buttoned his coat, snuffed his lantern, and began the long descent toward the truth.
