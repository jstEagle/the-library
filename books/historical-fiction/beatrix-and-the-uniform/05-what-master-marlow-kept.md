## 5. What Master Marlow Kept

The workshop smelled of oil and old brass when Felix let himself in, and for the first time in nineteen years it smelled of something else too: opportunity.

Master Marlow had gone out. This was rare enough to be nearly miraculous — the old man guarded his evenings the way misers guard coins, and his absences could be counted, Felix suspected, on the fingers of one injured hand. But there had been a note on the bench, in that beautiful, terrible hand: *Back late. Do not open the strongbox. Do not touch the east wall.*

Felix read the note twice.

*Do not touch the east wall.*

In nineteen years of apprenticeship, Marlow had issued many instructions. *Do not breathe on the polished escapements.* *Do not hum while filing.* (Felix hummed while filing. Constantly. It was, he felt, what made the filing bearable.) But never once had he said *do not touch the east wall*, chiefly because no one in the history of horology had ever wanted to touch the east wall. It held a cupboard of dead mantel clocks awaiting parts nobody would ever order, a water stain shaped vaguely like Portugal, and forty years of dust.

Which meant, of course, that Felix touched the east wall within four minutes.

He told himself he was being methodical. He had spent three days at the Royal Observatory with Beatrix Calloway's letter folded into his memory like a splinter working toward the heart, and every gear he'd cleaned since had turned under his hands while some quieter part of him turned other questions. *The dead do not write letters,* she had said, and her voice had not shaken, which was somehow worse than if it had.

And this afternoon, checking the workshop ledger against an invoice — innocent work, honest work — he had found an entry that snagged him like a burr in cloth. Dated the eleventh of March, 1886. *One sea chest, stored. Fee waived.*

March, 1886. The month Edmund Calloway drowned.

Fee waived. Master Marlow waived fees for exactly two categories of person: family, and men he was afraid of.

* * *

The east wall's cupboard opened with the groan of hinges that hadn't moved since Gladstone was a young man. Inside: the dead mantels, the dust, Portugal. Felix lifted the largest of the clock cases aside and found nothing but plaster.

Then he knelt, because kneeling was where you looked when you were looking for what people hid, and saw that one floorboard sat a hair's breadth proud of its neighbors. A craftsman's error. Master Marlow did not make craftsmen's errors — not visible ones. Which meant this one was meant to look like an error to everyone except someone who knew his hands as well as Felix did.

The pry bar from the tool roll did the rest.

Beneath the board, wrapped in sacking and smelling of tar and cedar, lay a sea chest.

Felix's hands went cold around the pry bar. He knew the shape of sea chests; every boy who grew up near the docks did. But this one bore brass corners gone green, rope handles worn to silk, and stencilled across the lid in flaking white paint:

**E. CALLOWAY — S.S. *MERCY OF WHITBY***

Edmund Calloway. The name arrived in Felix's chest like a dropped weight. The brother of the archivist with the steady voice and the eyes that catalogued everything. The man whose handwriting Felix had spent three days failing not to recognize.

He got the chest up onto the bench, and his heart was hammering so loudly he half expected it to knock itself off the wood. The lock was a cheap thing, salt-corroded; a fine pick made short work of it, and he felt briefly ashamed of how good he was at that, at all of this, at opening things that were never meant for him.

Inside: a sailor's spare shirt, folded with military neatness. A tin cup. A bundle of letters tied with string — addressed to Miss B. Calloway, undelivered, and Felix did not open them, though his hands trembled wanting to. A navigator's sextant. And beneath everything, a small oilcloth packet that clinked.

Coins. Foreign coins, minted in ports Edmund Calloway should never have reached if he'd drowned in March of '86.

"Alive," Felix whispered to the empty workshop. "He's alive."

The word changed the shape of the whole world. It un-drowned a man. It explained the letter in Beatrix's hands, the courier, the sealed instructions still arriving from a ghost. And it meant —

It meant somebody had gone to enormous trouble to make a living man dead, and Master Marlow kept that somebody's sea chest under his floorboards.

*Fee waived.*

Felix sat down slowly on the stool, quite without meaning to, and looked around the workshop as if seeing it for the first time. The bench where he'd learned files before he'd learned letters. The shelf of failures Marlow refused to discard, labeled in his immaculate hand: *F.W., age 11, overconfident.* The stove where the old man warmed two cups every morning and drank from both, because he said conversation slowed the tea.

Nineteen years of that. Nineteen years of being raised by a man who taught him that a mechanism either told the truth or it didn't, and that a clockmaker who tolerated a lying clock wasn't a clockmaker at all.

"Ah," said a voice from the doorway. "You found it, then."

* * *

Master Marlow stood in the entrance with his hat dripping rain, and he did not look surprised, or angry, or even particularly sad. He looked tired, in the way Felix had only ever seen him look after the long death of his wife, twenty years ago now — a tiredness that had given up pretending to be anything else.

"You left a note saying not to touch the east wall," Felix said. His own voice sounded far away.

"I know. I've always found that telling you *not* to do a thing is the surest way to get it done by nightfall. I considered writing *please touch the east wall* and hoping you'd rebel against that instead." Marlow hung his hat on its peg with the unhurried precision of a man setting a escapement. "Did you open the letters?"

"No." Felix's jaw was tight. "They're hers. They're not mine to read."

Something flickered across the old man's face — pride, Felix thought, horribly, at exactly the wrong moment.

"Sit down, lad."

"I'd rather stand."

"Yes, I imagine you would." Marlow lowered himself onto the opposite stool anyway, joints cracking like a case settling, and studied the open chest between them. "How much have you worked out?"

"That Edmund Calloway is alive. That his death was arranged. That you stored his belongings and waived the fee, which means either he's family—" Felix's voice cracked and he hated it, "—or you were afraid. And you've never once in my life been afraid of anything except moths."

"Moths are a reasonable fear. They eat the very fabric of things." But the joke came out flat, an escapement with no spring behind it. Marlow rubbed his eyes. "Debts," he said simply. "That's where it starts, Felix. Not with villains in cloaks. With debts. After Margaret died I owed money to men who lend to grieving clockmakers at rates designed to keep them grieving. And a man came to me — pleasant fellow, soft voice, name of Corrigan — and said he could clear the books. All he wanted was a little paper work. Handwriting work."

"The death notice."

"The certificate. The notice for the Admiralty. A manifest entry or two." Marlow's hands lay open on his knees, palms up, the gesture of a man showing he holds no tools. "Edmund Calloway had seen cargo moving through the docks that wasn't on any bill of lading, and he'd been foolish enough to say so out loud. Corrigan's people couldn't just kill him — sailors ask questions. Better that he die officially. Storm, lost overboard, tragic. So they faked the drowning, paid off the right clerks, and put him on a boat to nowhere with orders never to come back. And I wrote the papers that killed him." He smiled, and it was the worst thing Felix had ever seen him do. "Beautifully, mind you. My best work. They said so."

"You taught me that hand," Felix said. The words came out of him like teeth being pulled. "You've been teaching me it for years. *Copy Marlow, boy, copy Marlow until you can't tell which hand is which.* I thought — I thought it was pride. Craft."

"It was craft." Marlow met his eyes. "And it was insurance. A man who can forge his master's hand is worth more to them than the master, eventually. I was training my replacement, lad. That's what apprenticeship has always been. I just hoped yours wouldn't come due for another twenty years."

The stove ticked. Rain worked at the windows. Felix became aware, distantly, that his hands had curled into fists on his knees and that he was looking at the man who had fed him, housed him, bandaged his cut fingers, mourned his mother with him — and feeling something he had no name for yet, because *betrayal* was too clean a word for it, too tidy, like saying a smashed watch was merely *stopped*.

"Why keep the chest?" he managed. "Why keep any of it?"

"Because Corrigan makes men disappear, and I wanted proof somewhere that Edmund Calloway had existed. Leverage runs both directions, if you're clever enough to hold it." Marlow's gaze drifted to the coins, the sextant, the tied letters. "And because once a year, a letter comes for him care of this shop, and once a year I send it on through channels I'm not proud of. He writes to his sister, you know. Every year. She's never received one."

Felix thought of Beatrix's face when she'd held that envelope. Three years of grief, and her brother had been writing all along, and the letters had died in a smuggler's warehouse or a cowardly clockmaker's drawer.

"He's not hiding abroad by choice, is he," Felix said quietly. "Corrigan's still watching him. One step wrong and the drowning becomes real."

"Now you understand why I've been careful." Marlow leaned forward. "Felix. Whatever you're carrying back to Greenwich tomorrow — and I can see you're carrying something, you've been carrying it since the girl showed you that letter — you must understand what testimony costs. Not to me. To him. Drag Corrigan into the light and the first thing his people do is finish the job they started in '86. You'd be trading a lie that keeps a man breathing for a truth that gets him buried."

The words landed where they were aimed. Felix felt them sink.

"And you?" he asked. "When the Admiralty comes asking — and they will, she won't stop, she's not built to stop — what will you say?"

Marlow was quiet a long moment. When he answered, he looked not at Felix but at the shelf of failures, at *F.W., age 11, overconfident*.

"I'll say whatever you tell them I said," he said. "I've been a liar so long I'd like to end as something else. But I'm an old man, and old men break easily, and Corrigan knows where I sleep. So think carefully about what you carry back to Greenwich, lad — because whatever you decide, decide it fast. There's a man coming tomorrow morning to collect a debt of paper. He asked for your hand specifically."

The rain filled the silence.

"Mine?" Felix said.

"Yours." Marlow finally looked up, and his eyes were wet. "Congratulations, boy. You've become valuable. God help us both."
