## 6. Uniforms

The uniform arrived folded in brown paper on a Tuesday, tucked inside a delivery of cataloguing supplies that Beatrix had absolutely not ordered.

She knew the hand that had written her name on the label—Felix Whitlock's, careful and slightly left-leaning, the letters of an apprentice who had learned penmanship from someone stricter than any schoolmaster. Beneath it, a note in the same script: *Yours to borrow. Return it unworn or don't return it at all. —F.W.*

Inside lay a junior clerk's coat, navy wool with brass buttons, and a peaked cap with the observatory's badge stitched at the front. It smelled faintly of clock oil and camphor, which was to say it smelled of Felix, though she would never have admitted to knowing what Felix smelled like. She held it up against herself in the archive's little looking glass—the one she used for checking whether her collar sat straight before meetings—and found that the coat hung off her shoulders like a sail before the wind catches it.

It would do.

It would have to do, because tomorrow morning the restricted log room would be open for exactly two hours while Mr. Pemberton, the astronomer's first clerk, attended the quarterly meeting across the courtyard, and because Beatrix Calloway had spent three weeks being polite about a dead brother who apparently could not be bothered to stay dead.

*The dead do not write letters.* She had said it to Felix in the tower, half-joking, and watched his face do something complicated and terrible. She thought of that face now as she pinned up her hair and stuffed it under the cap. Whatever he knew, he hadn't told her. Whatever he suspected, he'd gone home to his master's workshop carrying it like a stone in his pocket, and she was done waiting for stones to speak.

If the men who kept the records would not open them for a grieving sister, then a grieving sister would become someone else entirely.

* * *

The trouble with disguises, Beatrix discovered at seven o'clock the next morning, was not the wearing of them. It was the walking.

A man walked as if the ground owed him nothing. A man took stairs two at a time without checking whether his skirts permitted it. A man scratched himself, whistled badly, and stood in doorways occupying space with the serene confidence of someone who had never once been asked to move along.

Beatrix practiced in the shadow of the Flamsteed House colonnade, cap low, hands clasped behind her back the way she'd seen Pemberton do it—a pose she privately called *the administrator at rest*. She squared her shoulders. She lengthened her stride. She passed a groundskeeper, who touched his cap to her, and she returned the gesture so stiffly that she nearly dislodged her own hat.

"Morning," she said, in the lowest register she owned.

"Morning, sir."

*Sir.* The word went through her like a spark through dry tinder. Three years of being spoken over, of having her cataloguing corrected by men who could not alphabetize their own bootlaces, and all it took was one borrowed coat and a voice like a wet boot to earn a *sir*. She was furious about how easy it was, and more furious about how much she liked it.

The log room stood at the end of the eastern corridor, behind a door Pemberton locked with the ceremony other men reserved for crown jewels. This morning the door stood ajar, Pemberton's keys still dangling from it—he was nothing if not punctual about his own absence—and beyond it Beatrix could see the shelves: bound volumes of shipping manifests, weather telegraphs, port authority returns, and the registry of deaths reported at sea, 1884 through 1888.

Volume five. November 1886.

Her heart performed something unprofessional against her ribs. She slipped inside, eased the door to within an inch of its frame, and pulled the volume down into a shaft of grey window light.

Edmund Calloway. Third mate, *Meridian Star*, lost overboard in the storm of the fourteenth of November, 1886. Body not recovered. Witness statement appended.

Beatrix knew this page. She had requested it officially, twice, and been refused twice—once on grounds of jurisdiction, once because Pemberton had mislaid it "temporarily" for eleven months. Now here it was, sitting quietly on a shelf where anyone in a borrowed coat might read it.

And here, in the corner beneath the witness signature, was the thing they had not wanted her to see.

The date of registration.

She read it three times, the way she proofed a difficult manuscript index. The storm of the fourteenth of November. The death registered on the eleventh.

Three days before Edmund drowned.

Or rather—she felt the cold arrive in her hands first, then her throat—three days before Edmund was supposed to have drowned. You could not certify a death before the death occurred unless the death had been decided upon in advance, drafted like an invitation, filed like a bill of lading. Someone had known. Someone had written her brother into the sea before the sea had agreed to take him.

Which meant either the storm was a fiction, or Edmund had stepped out of it alive, and both possibilities made her dizzy.

There was a countersignature. She bent close. The registrar's mark she recognized—it matched the port office copies she'd memorized—but beside it, in the margin, a second notation in a small, precise, backward-leaning hand: *Papers verified per instruction. No further inquiry recommended.*

No further inquiry recommended. As though grief were a suggestion to be declined politely, like sugar in tea.

Beatrix copied the notation into her notebook, letter by letter, because her memory was good but her fury was better, and fury distorted things. The handwriting nagged at her—not Edmund's, not the courier's, but familiar in some peripheral way, the way a tune is familiar when you cannot name it. She had seen it somewhere. Perhaps in the archives. Perhaps somewhere else entirely.

She was still staring at it when she heard the door.

* * *

Later, Beatrix would reconstruct those four seconds with the precision of the very archivists who had spent three years stonewalling her. The door hinge creaking. The scrape of a key ring being gathered from its hook. Pemberton's voice, already mid-complaint—"Honestly, if Hargreaves thinks I'll re-index the telegraph room by Friday, he may come and do it himself"—and then the silence of a man noticing that his locked door was not locked, and that someone was standing at his shelf holding volume five upside down, which was the single cleverest thing Beatrix had ever accidentally done, because a man reading a book upside down is clearly a man who has just picked it up and knows nothing whatsoever about its contents.

"That will be all for now, thank you." She pitched her voice low and did not turn around.

Silence. Then: "I'm sorry?"

"The first clerk. Pemberton." She turned, cap brim dipped, notebook closed against her palm, and gave him the administrator-at-rest pose with her weight settled back on her heels. "He's wanted at the meeting. He asked me to check the '86 registry was shelved correctly. It wasn't. It's been done now."

Pemberton was a thin man with the complexion of uncooked pastry and eyes that missed nothing except, historically, everything that mattered. He looked at her. He looked at the coat. He looked at the volume in her hands, which she now realized she was holding at arm's length in a manner no actual clerk would hold anything, the way one holds a dead rat.

"You're new," he said.

"Transferred Tuesday." Tuesday was safe. Tuesday explained the coat, the unfamiliarity, the smell of camphor. "From Deptford."

"Deptford." He said it the way he said everything, as though tasting something faintly disappointing. "I don't recall a transfer order."

"I wouldn't expect you to," Beatrix said, and heard herself add, with a boldness that seemed to belong to the coat rather than to her, "paperwork does get mislaid around here. Eleven months, I hear, in one case."

Something flickered across Pemberton's face—too fast to name, gone before she could catalogue it. Suspicion? Recognition? Or simply the ordinary vanity of a bureaucrat accused of ordinary failings?

"Mislaid," he repeated.

"So I'm told." She set the volume back on the shelf with exaggerated care, aligning its spine with its neighbors, buying herself the two seconds she needed to step sideways toward the door. "Well. I'll leave you to your morning, sir."

"Wait." He did not move to block her, but he didn't step aside either, and the corridor behind him was long and empty and full of morning light in which she was suddenly, horribly visible. "Your name. For the transfer ledger."

Beatrix's mind, that great reliable index, presented her with nothing. Not a name. Not a syllable. Every name she had ever known had been swept clean from the premises.

"Ferrell," said a voice from down the corridor.

They both turned. Felix Whitlock stood by the stairwell with his tool bag over his shoulder, dressed in his work jacket, looking exactly like what he was—an apprentice come to service a clock—with the notable exception of his eyes, which were fixed on Beatrix with an expression of quiet, stricken alarm.

"Ferrell," Felix repeated, stepping closer. "New lad. Sent over yesterday, weren't you? I showed you where the stores were." He glanced at Pemberton with the apologetic shrug of one workingman vouching for another. "Bit keen, this one. Been at the ledgers since dawn. Thinks he'll learn the trade faster."

"Does he," said Pemberton.

"Don't we all," said Felix.

The three of them stood in the corridor in the particular silence of people each waiting for another to make a mistake. Somewhere below them the master clock ticked, steady as a pulse, counting out the seconds Beatrix had perhaps thirty of remaining.

"Well," Pemberton said at last, drawing the word out like taffy. "Ferrell. I shall look forward to seeing your transfer papers." And he smiled—a thin, pastry-colored smile—and walked past them both into the log room, and locked the door behind him with a click that sounded, to Beatrix, exactly like a trap closing.

She and Felix walked down the corridor side by side, neither speaking until the stairwell swallowed them, and even then only in whispers.

"Tuesday?" Felix breathed.

"It was the first day I thought of."

"Ferrell?"

"It was the first name I thought of."

"You're a terrible liar," he said, but there was no scorn in it. If anything there was something like awe, and underneath the awe, fear. "You know that, don't you? Your face does things. It tells whole stories."

"My face," Beatrix said, removing the cap and letting her hair fall loose in the stairwell gloom, "has just read a document that says my brother was declared dead three days before the storm that killed him. My face can tell whatever stories it likes."

Felix had gone very still. In the dim light she saw him swallow, saw the conflict move across him like weather.

"There's something else," she said. "The notation in the margin. *Papers verified per instruction.* I've seen that handwriting before, Felix. Small, precise, leaning backward. I can't place it, but I know it."

And she watched him understand—watched it land in him like a dropped weight—because whatever name lived in that handwriting, Felix Whitlock already knew whose it was.

"We can't talk here," he said quietly. "Meet me in the tower tonight, after the nine o'clock strike. There are things you need to see. Things I should have shown you days ago." He hesitated, and then, in a voice barely above the ticking of the building itself: "Bring the letter, Beatrix. The one in Edmund's hand. Bring everything. Because I think—I think the man who taught me my trade wrote your brother's death with his own pen, and I need you to help me prove I'm wrong."

Above them, muffled through floors of stone, the master clock struck the quarter hour, and the sound rolled out over the river like a warning.
