## 7. The Escapement

By the third week of October, Felix Whitlock had stopped being afraid of the master clock and started being afraid of everything else.

The distinction mattered. Fear of a mechanism was a respectable, manageable thing—a fear you could hold in your palm and turn over until you understood its shape. The great regulator in the tower of the Royal Observatory was the most honest adversary he had ever faced. It did not lie. When something inside it was wrong, it told him so in the only language it knew: three seconds slow, then five, then a shudder through the whole train like a man clearing his throat before an unwelcome confession.

He had found the culprit on a Tuesday: the escapement itself, its pallets worn to a polish that gleamed almost lovingly in his lamplight. Someone had been oiling it with the wrong compound for years—probably decades—smoothing the friction instead of correcting it, the way a bad physician treats symptoms and calls it cure. Felix had stripped the assembly, re-cut the pallets by hand over four evenings, and now, on this grey Thursday morning, he sat cross-legged among the gears with the new escapement cradled against his chest like a sleeping cat.

"Right," he said to it. "Let's find out what we're both made of."

He fitted it home. The click of the seating was the sweetest sound he'd produced in twenty-three years of living. He released the train, held his breath out of pure superstition, and watched.

Tick. Tick. Tick.

Steady as a heartbeat that had never once considered misbehaving. The pendulum swung with the long, patient authority of a judge. Somewhere below them, all across London, every clock that took its orders from Greenwich would feel the correction within days, though not one of their owners would ever know why the world seemed to have settled on its shelf.

Felix allowed himself exactly one minute of pride. He kept track of it on his pocket watch, because pride unmeasured had a way of expanding to fill available time.

Then he went back to worrying about everything else.

* * *

The proof arrived, as proof often does, while he was looking for something entirely different.

The clock's maintenance log—a fat ledger bound in cracked maroon leather—had lived in a niche behind the weight channel for longer than anyone could remember, and Felix had taken to consulting it each evening, entering his adjustments in a careful hand. That Thursday, reaching back for it, his knuckles struck something else wedged deeper in the niche. A second book, smaller, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with string gone stiff as wire.

He should have left it. Every instinct his master had ever instilled in him—the instinct to be unnoticed, unhindered, uninvolved—said *leave it*. But those instincts had been eroding for weeks, worn down like the escapement's pallets, and what remained of them was not strong enough to stop his fingers from working the knot loose.

It was a correspondence book. Copies of letters sent, kept in the tidy hand of some clerk long dead. And tucked between two pages, folded twice, a single loose sheet: a draft of a death notice, undated but unmistakable.

*Edmund Aloysius Calloway, mate, s.s. Persephone, lost with all hands in the gale of March the ninth.*

Felix knew the handwriting.

That was the trouble. He knew it the way he knew his own pulse. The particular forward lean of the capitals. The *y* that always trailed off into a small impatient hook. The way the writer pressed harder at the start of words than the end, so that each line began dark and faded toward evening.

Marlow's hand. His master's hand. The hand Felix himself had been drilled in for six years—not to write, never to write, Marlow had been very clear about that—but to *read*, to repair, to recognize, because a clockmaker who could not read a maker's marks was no clockmaker at all.

And beneath the draft, in a different pen, a different hand altogether, someone had written a date. Not the date of the gale. The date of the *notice*—and it preceded the storm by eleven days.

Eleven days. One does not announce a death eleven days before the storm that causes it, unless one has received instructions.

There was no signature beneath the instruction. There didn't need to be. The paper smelled faintly of tar and tobacco, and in the corner, where a seal might have gone, someone had pressed their thumb into wax and left it there—an old smuggler's signature, Felix realized, the kind of mark a man makes when he wants deniability more than credit. He had seen such thumbprints before, once, years ago, on a delivery docket his master had burned in the workshop grate while telling Felix to mind his own files.

His hands were steady, which surprised him. He copied the page—dates, draft, thumbprint—all in his own plain, forgettable hand, and put the original back exactly as he'd found it, and sat for a long while afterward listening to the clock tick, which now sounded less like a heartbeat and more like a countdown.

* * *

She came at dusk, as she had taken to doing, carrying two cups of tea in defiance of every regulation governing the clock room, and set them down on the stone ledge beside him without asking whether he wanted one. She had learned he always wanted one.

"You look," Beatrix said, "like a man who has swallowed the works of a watch."

"Only the mainspring." He wrapped his hands around the cup. "Beatrix. I need to tell you something, and I need you to let me finish before you say anything."

Her eyebrows rose, but she sat down on the ledge beside him, skirts gathered against the cold stone, and waited. Waiting was a thing she was very good at; it was, he suspected, most of what archivists actually did.

"I found something today. In the maintenance niche." He handed her his copy, watching her face rather than the paper, because her face would tell him things the paper couldn't. "A draft death notice. For Edmund. In my master's handwriting."

She went very still. He had learned that stillness about her—it wasn't emptiness, it was compression, a whole sea drawn back from the shore before the wave comes.

"And the date," she said quietly.

"Written under it. Eleven days before the storm."

"The storm." Her voice stayed level, which must have cost her enormously. "The storm that killed him. The storm they wrote to me about. My mother received the letter and took to her bed for a month, and it was *drafted* before—" She stopped. Drew breath. Started again in that archivist's tone, flat and precise, which he knew now was how she kept from breaking. "Eleven days. Someone knew he would need to be dead before there was any reason for it."

"There was a thumbprint in the wax. No name. But I've seen that kind of mark before—on dock papers, years ago. Papers my master burned."

"Your master." She turned to look at him fully. In the lamplight her eyes were the colour of deep water over sand, and just as difficult to see the bottom of. "Felix. What is he to you? Truly. Because I've watched you circle his name for three weeks the way the pendulum circles its arc, and I think you're trying to decide whether loving a man and suspecting him can live in the same house."

"They've been living in mine since I was nine years old," Felix said. "He took me off the streets. Taught me everything. Fed me when feeding me meant less for him." He laughed, without much humour in it. "And taught me to read his own handwriting well enough that I recognized a forgery of a drowned man's death notice on sight. So yes. Both things live in the house. They're not speaking to each other."

Beatrix was quiet a moment. Then, unexpectedly, she smiled—a small, tired, real smile. "My mother used to say that about grief and gratitude. That they share a bed and steal each other's blankets."

"She sounds like she'd have got along with Marlow."

"No one gets along with Marlow. You *apprentice* with Marlow." She looked down at the paper again. "Eleven days. It means Edmund was alive when they wrote it. It means whoever wrote it knew he'd have to die soon—which means either they were going to kill him, or help him disappear. And if he disappeared—" Her breath caught on the arithmetic. "Then the letter I intercepted. The handwriting. It isn't a forgery, is it? Not that one. That one is *his*."

Felix thought of the sea chest under the floorboards. Of Marlow's face when he'd caught Felix kneeling beside it—not guilty, exactly. Frightened. A frightened man guarding something he'd promised to protect.

"I don't know," Felix said, which was mostly true. "But I know someone who does, and I'm going to make him tell me. Even if it costs—" He stopped.

"Costs what?"

"Everything I have," Felix said simply. "He's all the family I've got."

The clock ticked between them, sixty times a minute, keeping faith.

"Not all," Beatrix said.

He looked at her. She was studying her tea with tremendous concentration, as though the leaves might arrange themselves into a tactful silence.

"What did you say?"

"I said not all. Family. You said he's all the family you've got, and I'm correcting the record, because correcting records is rather the whole of my profession." She still hadn't looked up. "You've got me. If you want me. Which is an absurd thing to say sitting on cold stone next to a national timepiece, and I've rehearsed it badly, and if you laugh I shall push you down the weight channel."

"I'm not laughing," Felix said. His voice had gone strange and light, as though someone had counterweighted his whole chest. "I'm trying to remember the last time anyone corrected a record on my behalf."

"Then allow me to note for the archive," she said, and finally looked up, and her eyes were shining and fierce and quite close, "that Felix Whitlock, apprentice clockmaker, was observed on the fourteenth of October in the company of Beatrix Calloway, who intends to remain in his company indefinitely, weather and Admiralties permitting."

"That's remarkably formal."

"I'm an archivist. We declare things properly or not at all." And then, because declarations properly made demand sealing, she kissed him—quickly, gently, tasting of tea and cold air, her cold hand finding his warm one—and pulled back with the expression of a woman who has filed something important and does not intend to lose the receipt.

Above them the great clock measured out seven o'clock, and the bells went rolling down over the river, and Felix thought: *this is what time is for.* Not schedules, not shipping, not the empire's punctuality. This. Two people in a dark tower agreeing to spend theirs together.

"We should go," Beatrix murmured against his shoulder. "I'm expected at the archives, and you—"

The lamplight caught the far wall of the clock room, and Felix stopped breathing.

Someone had chalked a mark on the doorframe. A single sign, small and neat: a circle with a bar through it. The same mark, he realized with a cold drop in his stomach, that had been chalked on the workshop door in Wapping two nights ago, which he had assumed was a cooper's tally and thought nothing of.

They weren't being careless anymore. Someone was counting the doors they walked through.

"Felix?" Beatrix followed his gaze. "What is it?"

"That mark," he said slowly. "I've seen it before. On my master's door."
