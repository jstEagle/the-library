## 8. Fire Watch

I had learned, in my years among the Observatory's archives, that disasters rarely announce themselves. They arrive disguised as errands. A clerk asking for a file. A courier with a sealed letter. A smell of smoke where no smoke ought to be.

That last one came on a Thursday, while I was mending the spine of an almanac from 1841 with paste I had made myself and was rather proud of.

The smell drifted in through the window of the records room—thin at first, the way a lie is thin when you first hear it spoken aloud. Woodsmoke. Coal smoke. And beneath both, something sharper. Pitch. Tar. The smell of a fire that has been *helped along*.

I set down my brush.

Felix had gone to the workshop that morning. He had gone against every instinct he possessed and against my explicit advice, which he had received twice over breakfast and once on the ferry, delivered with what I felt was admirable restraint. But Marlow's ledgers were there—the real ones, the ones Felix had found beneath the floorboards—and after the Admiralty summons arrived, naming Felix as a witness, we both understood there would not be another quiet day in which to copy them. He had taken the chest of papers under his arm like a man carrying an infant through a crowded market, and he had kissed me once, quickly, the way people kiss when they are pretending to be calm, and said:

"If I'm not back by four, come looking."

"And if you *are* back by four?"

"Then we'll have tea and I'll complain about the state of his floorboards."

It was now twenty minutes past three.

* * *

I ran.

I am not, by constitution or training, a running woman. My legs are archival legs, built for stairs and long sitting. But grief had taught me three years ago that the body will do remarkable things when the mind gives it permission, and so I ran down the hill from Greenwich Park with my hat coming loose and my skirts gathered in both fists like a woman fleeing a scandal rather than pursuing one.

The smoke told me where to look before my eyes did. It rose from the lane behind the clockmaker's row in a grey column, too orderly for an accident. Accidents are chaotic. This smoke rose the way a signature is written—with intent.

The workshop's front door stood open. Heat rolled out of it like breath from a furnace mouth, and inside, the place Felix had described to me a hundred times—his bench by the window, Marlow's stool, the wall of small drawers each labeled in a hand I now knew too well—was turning orange.

"FELIX!"

Nothing. Or rather, something: a cough, from deep within, from the back room where the floorboards had been.

A man across the lane was shouting for water. Two others stood at what I can only describe as a prudent distance, doing the thing Englishmen do at fires, which is to form an audience. I did not have time to despise them properly; I filed it away for later.

I pulled my shawl up over my mouth and nose and went in.

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened next, because I have since testified about it and testimony demands honesty even when memory offers something kinder.

I did not think. Thinking is my profession and I abandoned it entirely. I saw heat, I heard coughing, and I moved toward both, which is the opposite of every sensible instruction ever given to anyone. The smoke low near the floor was thinner—I knew this much, some fragment of a pamphlet on household safety—and so I went low, half-crawling through a room I had only ever heard described, past a workbench whose drawers were already glowing at their edges like coals in a grate.

The back room was worse. The floorboards Marlow had hidden things beneath had been pried up and thrown onto the fire itself; someone had wanted the hiding place destroyed as thoroughly as its contents. And in the corner, on his knees amid the wreckage, was Felix—my careful, methodical Felix, who catalogued his screws by size—who had a ledger clutched against his chest with one arm and was groping along the floor with the other hand as though the floor might still offer up more.

"Felix!" My voice came out as a croak. The shawl ate most of it.

He turned. Even through the smoke I could see his face—blackened, wet-eyed, wild in a way I had never seen him and hoped never to see again.

"Beatrix—" A cough doubled him over. "The second ledger—it was *here*—"

"The second ledger is ash, my love, and so are you if you don't get up."

"I can find it, I know where he kept—"

"*Felix.*"

I had never used that voice before. It was my mother's voice, the one she had used on Edmund when he climbed the church roof, and it worked on Felix exactly as it had worked on my brother: he stopped, blinked, and obeyed.

Getting him out was the hardest physical labor of my life, and I include the year I single-handedly reorganized the Admiralty's chart room. He was heavier than he looked—or perhaps fear adds weight—and the smoke was thicker now, pressing down like a hand. I got us both below it, his arm over my shoulders, the ledger still crushed between his ribs and mine, and we went through the orange doorway into air.

We made it perhaps ten feet before my legs resigned their commission and we collapsed together in the gutter of the lane, coughing, filthy, alive.

The audience of Englishmen applauded. I believe one of them said "bravo." If I live to be ninety I will not forgive him.

* * *

Later—much later, after the fire brigade had come and gone, after the workshop had been reduced to a blackened shell smelling of pitch and ruin—we sat on the step of the chandler's shop next door, wrapped in blankets a kind widow had fetched, and I finally looked at what Felix had carried out of the fire.

One ledger. Small, water-stained at the corners from years beneath the floorboards, its leather cover scorched along one edge. Felix held it in both hands the way one holds a wounded bird.

"It's enough," he said. His voice was ruined, gravel and smoke. "Beatrix, I checked. It's the shipping ledger. Every vessel, every date, every payment in and out. Marlow kept everything because he never believed he'd be caught, and now—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Now being thorough is going to hang him."

"Not hang," I said gently. "Testify. There's a difference, and tomorrow you're going to stand in front of the Admiralty and make it."

"He taught me everything." Felix's thumb moved over the ledger's cover, back and forth, a clockmaker's gesture, soothing a mechanism that could not be soothed. "How to hold a file. How to read a movement. How to write his hand so well that—" He stopped again, but I finished it for him, because we had been finishing each other's terrible sentences for weeks now.

"So well that you could forge a dead man's letters without blinking."

"Yes."

I put my hand over his, over the ledger, over the whole terrible weight of it. "And tomorrow you'll un-teach yourself all of it. Out loud. In a room full of men with epaulettes."

He laughed—a broken, smoky laugh, but a laugh. "You make it sound almost bearable."

"That is my function in this partnership. You mend clocks; I make catastrophes sound administrative."

* * *

It was full dark when the boy came.

He was one of the dockside children who ran messages for pennies, and he approached our step with the exaggerated casualness of youth delivering bad news, hands in pockets, eyes everywhere but on us.

"You the clockmaker?" he asked.

"I'm the archivist," I said. "Close enough."

He produced a letter. Not sealed—simply folded, once, and soft at the edges as though it had been carried a long way in a warm pocket. "Gentleman at the dock said give it to the one who coughs."

Felix took it. His hands were steady—he had trained them to be steady—but I felt him go still beside me, the particular stillness of a man hearing a escapement tick out of rhythm.

By the light of the chandler's lamp, we read it together.

The handwriting was Edmund Calloway's. I would have known it anywhere—in the archive, in my mother's keepsake box, in my dreams. The letters leaned the way they had always leaned. The crossings of the t's fell just so.

But I had spent months learning what forged handwriting looks like, and Felix had spent his whole life learning how forgery is done, and we read it the same way at the same moment, our eyes meeting over the page before either of us spoke.

"It's good," Felix said quietly. "Better than mine ever was. He's practiced."

*Edmund* had practiced. Which meant Edmund was alive, and writing, and writing in a hand he had needed to perfect—which meant someone had taught him, or he had taught himself, and either way it meant the dead man was hiding so carefully that even his own hand had become a disguise.

The letter said only this:

*I know what was burned tonight. I know what survived. Do not go to the Admiralty alone—they are not all clean. Trust the one who signs himself H.*

There was no signature. There didn't need to be. The handwriting was signature enough—a dead man reaching across three years of ocean and lies to touch the shoulder of the sister who had refused to stop reading.

Felix looked at me. I looked at Felix. Somewhere across the river, faint and faithful, the Observatory's great clock began to strike the hour—a sound I had heard ten thousand times and never once, until this night, heard as what it truly was.

A promise that whatever burned, whatever lied, whatever waited in the dark—the time would come anyway. And we would be there to meet it.

"Tomorrow," I said, folding the letter into my pocket beside the envelope I had stolen from a courier a lifetime ago, "we go to the Admiralty."

"Together," Felix said.

Together. It was, I reflected, the one word in the English language that had ever frightened me more than fire—and the only one that had ever been worth it.
