## 9. Testimony

The Admiralty building smelled of beeswax, pipe smoke, and the particular despair of men who had spent their whole lives in rooms where nothing was ever decided before four o'clock.

Felix stood in the corridor outside the hearing chamber with his hat in his hands, turning it slowly by the brim, and tried to remember a single useful thing about breathing. The salvaged ledger lay in an oilcloth bundle under his arm. It was warm from his body, or perhaps he only imagined so; he had been carrying it for three days as though it were a sleeping child, and somewhere along the way his arms had stopped believing it could ever be put down.

"You'll do," Beatrix said beside him.

She wore her good grey dress, the one with the collar that stood up like a small act of defiance, and she had pinned her hair with the severity of a woman who intended to be believed. She had also, Felix noticed, positioned herself between him and the door to the chamber, which meant she thought he might bolt.

"I might bolt," he admitted.

"You won't."

"How can you be certain?"

"Because you spent six weeks inside that clock tower making sure every gear told the truth," she said. "You're not going to stop now just because the gears have faces."

A clerk appeared — the same clerk, Felix realized with a small cold jolt, who had nearly caught her in the records room, though neither of them acknowledged this — and announced that Mr. Whitlock would be heard. His voice had the flat courtesy of a man announcing luncheon.

* * *

The chamber was smaller than Felix had expected, and colder, all dark paneling and high windows through which the November light fell like something rationed. Five men sat at a long table beneath the Admiralty crest. Four of them looked bored. The fifth — silver-haired, sharp-eyed, with the weathered look of a man who had once commanded something larger than a committee — did not look bored at all. He looked at Felix the way Felix himself looked at a clock that had stopped for no apparent reason: with the patient certainty that everything needed was already inside.

"State your name and trade."

"Felix Whitlock. Clockmaker's apprentice." He paused. "Journeyman, I suppose, as of last week. My master is no longer—" He stopped. Started again. "I repair clocks, sir."

"Mr. Whitlock." The silver-haired man — Rear Admiral Hargreaves, the placard said, though the placard spelled it wrong, which Felix noted out of pure professional habit and then despised himself for noting — folded his hands. "Three years ago, the merchant vessel *Cormorant* was lost off the Dutch coast with all hands. Among the dead was Edmund Calloway, second mate. Last month, this office received evidence suggesting Mr. Calloway's death was not merely misreported but actively fabricated, and that the fabrication was executed by a trained hand. You are here because you are said to know hands."

"Yes, sir."

"Do you understand what you're about to say will end certain careers? Possibly your own?"

Felix looked at the table. There was a scratch on its surface, a long looping gouge, and he found himself thinking that some apprentice had done it with a compass point and never owned up to it, and that he had spent twenty-two years being the sort of person who never owned up to anything louder than a whisper.

"My master taught me two things, sir," he said. "How to make a clock keep honest time, and how to make a lie keep honest-looking time. He always said the second paid better." He lifted his head. "I'd like to tell you which of them I'm choosing."

The bored men became slightly less bored.

* * *

He told them. It came out of him more steadily than he had feared, once begun — the way water finds the channel cut for it. He described the workshop on Carter Lane, the smell of brass filings and the kettle that never quite boiled. He described how Marlow Kept had made him copy letters at fourteen, pages of practice script until Felix's own hand disappeared entirely into his master's, until Felix could write *Edmund Calloway* better than Edmund Calloway probably wrote it himself.

"He called it training my eye," Felix said. "It was training my erasure. A man who can write any hand has no hand of his own left. I didn't understand that until Miss Calloway put a letter in front of me and asked if it was genuine, and I recognized the writing the way you recognize your own reflection in a window you didn't know was there."

"And the letter in question?"

"The death notice, sir. And eleven letters besides, dated across eighteen months after the *Cormorant* went down — letters written *as* Edmund Calloway, from ports he never saw, to creditors and family, keeping the fiction of a living man alive exactly as long as the ring needed debts deferred and insurance held in suspense." He set the oilcloth bundle on the table and unwrapped it. "This ledger survived the fire at our workshop. It records payments. Dated entries, initials, and in three places, my master's own true hand — not forged, sir. His own. Men are always most careless in their own handwriting, because they believe no one is looking for it there."

The committee examined the ledger with the reverence of men handling scripture they intended to use as a weapon. Felix watched them and felt something strange happening in his chest — a loosening. All his life he had practiced being unnoticed: the apprentice in the corner, the boy who swept up, the man whose name went on no work because the work went out under his master's mark. Invisibility had been his craft and his shelter both.

Now five strangers were looking directly at him, and the world had not ended. It felt, in fact, rather like the moment a stopped clock starts again — frightening, and then unmistakably alive.

"The smuggling ring," Hargreaves said. "Names."

Felix gave them. Denham at the docks. The customs man whose initials appeared every quarter-day. The shipping agent who had arranged the *Cormorant*'s cargo of untaxed brandy and silk, and who had decided, when the revenue cutter got too close, that a dead second mate was cheaper than a hanged one — but that a *slowly* dead one, dying by paperwork over months, was cheapest of all.

"And Kept himself. Did he profit?"

"No, sir." Felix's voice caught, and he let it. "He owed them. My master took me in when I was eleven years old and nobody's. Whatever else he is, he is the man who fed me. I've spent three days trying to find a version of the truth that doesn't include him, and I couldn't, and I think—" He steadied himself against the table. "I think he spent three years trying to find a version that didn't include a drowned boy, and couldn't either. That's why the letters kept coming. That's why he couldn't stop. Every letter was him trying to write his way out of what he'd done, and every letter dug the hole deeper."

The room was quiet. Somewhere above them, faintly, a bell marked the half hour — Greenwich time, carried across the river, keeping faith with everyone whether they deserved it or not.

Hargreaves consulted a paper. "Marlow Kept was taken this morning at a house in Rotherhithe. He has declined counsel. He has asked, in fact, to speak." A pause. "Would the witness care to hear what he says?"

Felix discovered his hands were shaking. Beatrix, seated behind him in the public row, leaned forward slightly — he could not see her face, but he could feel her attention land on his shoulders like a hand.

"Yes," he said. "Yes, sir."

* * *

They brought Marlow in through the side door, and Felix's heart broke in a new direction.

His master had always been a large presence in a small room — broad-shouldered, loud-kettled, filling space with complaints and shavings and the smell of tobacco. The man who walked in now seemed to have been boiled down to some essential thinner broth of himself. His coat hung wrong. His hands, those great precise hands, wouldn't stay still.

But when his eyes found Felix, they were steady.

"Sit down, lad," Marlow said, before anyone could instruct him. "You look like you're about to apologize. Don't you dare apologize. You're the best thing I ever made and I'll not have you apologizing for me."

"Mr. Kept—" began the chairman.

"Aye, aye, I'll answer your questions, but let me say my piece first, because I've had three days in a cell to rehearse it and I'll botch it if interrupted." He turned to the committee, and something in his posture shifted — the craftsman's straightness, the pride that had been beaten down but not out. "Everything the boy said is true. I wrote the notice. I wrote the letters. I taught him the trade and I used the trade, and I'd have gone on using it until they buried me, because they had me by the debts and by worse than debts." His voice roughened. "But there's one thing he doesn't know, and he should hear it from me while I've still got the courage of a condemned man, which is considerable, I'm told."

The room leaned in.

"Edmund Calloway isn't dead," Marlow said. "Never was."

Beatrix made a sound behind Felix — small, strangled, quickly mastered.

"They faked the drowning to get him out of England, because Edmund had found their ledgers — the real ones, not this pretty copy — and they couldn't decide between killing him and exiling him, and exile was cheaper and quieter. I forged the papers because they held my notes, and because—" He looked at Felix. "Because they told me if I refused, the apprentice would inherit my debts instead. You were seventeen, lad. What was I supposed to do?"

"You could have told me," Felix said. His voice came out very quiet. "Sometime in the last three years. Any of the thousand nights we sat ten feet apart."

"Aye." Marlow's eyes glistened. "I could have. That's the crime I'll serve hardest for, I expect. Not the forgery. The silence." He straightened. "He's in Antwerp. Working under the name Verhoeven at a chandlery on the Schelde — I've had letters, smuggled ones, the one honest correspondence of my life. He thinks his sister believes him dead. He stayed away to keep her safe from them." A bitter half-laugh. "And now the ring's broken, thanks to the pair of you, and the poor devil can come home to find his sister's been breaking into government offices on his behalf. Reckon they deserve each other."

The chairman rapped for order, but the damage — the wonderful damage — was done.

* * *

Afterward, in the corridor, the November light had turned gold against the windows, and Beatrix stood with both hands pressed flat over her mouth, and Felix stood beside her and did not touch her, because some moments must be allowed to finish assembling themselves.

"He's alive," she said finally, through her fingers. "All this time. All those tides I watched. He was *writing letters*, Felix. To a chandler in Antwerp."

"To my master," Felix said. "Who read every one, I'd wager, standing in the shop after I'd gone to bed." He shook his head slowly. "Two liars corresponding in the dark, and both of them doing it out of love. It's the most honest thing I've ever heard of."

She laughed — a wet, cracked, glorious laugh — and then she took his lapel in both fists and kissed him, right there under the Admiralty crest, in full view of a passing clerk who pretended magnificently not to notice.

When she pulled back, her eyes were bright and fierce. "Antwerp," she said. "How soon can we—"

"There's a packet boat Thursday," Felix said. "I checked this morning. Before the hearing. I checked before I knew any of it, because I'm a coward who plans escape routes." He smiled. "We can be on it by noon."

Behind them, the chamber doors opened and Marlow emerged between two officers, pausing at the sight of them. For a moment the old man simply looked — at Felix's hand in Beatrix's, at the ledger under Felix's arm, at the boy he had raised now standing fully visible in the light of a corridor window.

"Whitlock," he said. "Sign your work from now on. Your own hand."

Then they took him away, gently, and Felix stood holding the woman he loved and the ledger that had cost everything, and across the river the observatory bell began to strike the hour — one, two, three — each note falling on the water like a promise kept.

Thursday, he thought. Antwerp. A brother alive at the end of it.

For the first time in his life, Felix Whitlock could hardly wait to see what time would bring.
