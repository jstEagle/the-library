## 10. Keeping Time

The shop on Tanner's Row had been a tobacconist's, and before that, Beatrix suspected, something older still, because the floorboards sang when you walked them the way old ships do. It had one window, wide enough for two workbenches, and a door that stuck in damp weather unless you lifted the handle and thought kindly of it. Felix had repaired the door twice before conceding that some things preferred to be persuaded rather than fixed.

"Sign of our trade," he'd said, standing back to admire his own defeat. "Everything keeps its own time. Our job is only to listen."

Beatrix had hung the sign herself — **WHITLOCK & CALLOWAY, CLOCKWORK REPAIRED** — and then, when Felix wasn't looking, had added beneath it in her small archival hand: *& Records Restored.* Because there were more broken things in Greenwich than clocks, and word had begun to travel.

The morning the letter came was the first proper day of spring, or as proper as London allowed — pale sun, wind off the river carrying salt and coal-smoke in equal measure. Beatrix was at the counter cataloguing a consignment of pocket watches from an estate in Blackheath, each one wrapped in newspaper like a small dead bird, when she heard Felix go still at his bench.

She knew his silences now. There was the silence of concentration, which hummed. There was the silence of frustration, which clattered. And there was this silence — sudden, total, the whole workshop holding its breath.

"Felix?"

He was standing at the open door with an envelope in both hands, held the way one holds something that might be hot, or holy, or about to dissolve.

"Edmund's hand," he said.

Beatrix crossed the room without deciding to. She took the letter because her hands knew how — three years of grief had taught her hands everything except what to do with themselves — and turned it over. The address was hers, at the shop, written in that slanting, impatient script she would have known in the dark, in the bottom of a well, at the end of the world. The postmark read Antwerp.

"The Admiralty forwarded nothing?" she managed.

"Nothing yet." Felix's voice was careful, the way it went careful when he was frightened for her. "This came straight through. Ordinary post. Two pence."

*Two pence,* she thought absurdly. *My brother alive costs two pence.*

She did not open it at the counter. Some letters deserved better than a counter, and besides, her fingers had begun to tremble in a manner unbefitting an archivist. She carried it to the back room where they kept the tea things and the single window that looked down the row toward the river, and she sat, and Felix sat across from her without being asked, close enough that his knee touched hers, which was their way of holding hands in company and, lately, in private too.

She broke the seal.

*Dear Bee,*

*I have read the shipping reports. I know what you did — what you both did. Master Marlow's confession reached even Antwerp, which I am told has excellent newspapers and terrible coffee.*

*Forgive me the three years. Forgive me most of all the first week, when I could have found a way and chose not to, because they were watching you, Bee, and a dead man's sister is safer than a threatened one. I told myself this every day until I believed it, and then every day after that until I stopped believing it, and now I am simply sorry.*

*I am coming home. Not yet — there is a man in Rotterdam whose testimony must reach the Admiralty before mine can, and papers to be untangled, and I find I am no longer willing to travel under any name but my own. But soon. Save me a chair by your fire and tell Whitlock I owe him a debt no ledger will hold.*

*Your brother,*
*Edmund*

*P.S. — The watch I left behind. Is it running?*

Beatrix read it twice. On the second reading her vision blurred so thoroughly that Edmund's handwriting dissolved into its component strokes — and she laughed, wetly, helplessly, because even now, even weeping, part of her mind was annotating: *consistent slant, pressure heavy at the terminals, the same impatient crossbar on the t.* Three years of archives do not wash out of a person.

"He wants to know if his watch is running," she said aloud.

Felix reached into his waistcoat and produced it — Edmund's watch, salvaged from the sea chest beneath Marlow's floorboards, cleaned and oiled and ticking faithfully these many months on Felix's bench, waiting without either of them saying so for exactly this question.

"It hasn't stopped once," Felix said. "I checked it every morning. Seemed rude not to."

Beatrix set the letter down between them and pressed both hands flat over her heart as though keeping something from escaping. "He's coming home. Felix. My brother is coming *home*, and he signed his name —"

"I saw."

"— in his own hand, in full daylight, on an envelope that crossed the sea —"

"Bee." Felix covered one of her hands with his. His palm was warm and slightly oil-scented and it steadied her the way it always did, as though he were regulating her. "Breathe. You're spiralling faster than a fusee chain."

That startled a laugh out of her, which was, she suspected, his intention. She wiped her eyes with her sleeve — unarchival, undignified, entirely necessary — and looked at him across the letter, across the little shop they had built out of the wreckage of everyone else's secrets.

"Do you remember," she said, "when I first watched you in the tower? Climbing around inside that clock like a spider with a toolbag, pretending not to notice me noticing you?"

"I remember thinking the archivist was going to have me transported for touching her ledgers."

"I was deciding whether to trust you."

"And now?"

Beatrix considered the question seriously, because he had earned seriousness. Now there was a ring on a thin chain around her neck that she caught herself touching when she read letters. Now there was a second chair by the fire, already saved. Now there was this: a man who had chosen truth over the only father he'd known, and who still woke some mornings with the guilt sitting on his chest like a cat, and who let her talk him back to sleep.

"Now," she said, "I trust you with time itself. Which is more than I'd trust the Royal Observatory."

"That's treasonous."

"I'm an archivist. We keep records; we don't necessarily respect them."

* * *

They opened the shop together that afternoon — properly opened it, shutters back, door persuaded — and the customers came as customers do, in ones and twos and then in a small reliable tide. A retired naval man whose chronometer ran slow in cold weather ("They all do, sir; it's not sentiment, it's physics"). A widow with a carriage clock that had belonged to her late husband and had stopped, she insisted, on the night he died, and could Mr. Whitlock tell her whether clocks could grieve. Felix had said, gently, that clocks kept going precisely so that people didn't have to, and the widow had cried and paid double and come back the next week with biscuits.

Beatrix handled the books and the correspondence and the growing reputation of the back room, where damaged documents were flattened, de-acidified, and coaxed back toward legibility. Word of her skill had travelled from the Admiralty trial — there were men in that room who had watched her present evidence with a steady voice and steady hands and had thought, *there is a person who understands what paper remembers.* The first commission arrived by courier in April: a water-stained logbook from a merchant vessel, its ink bloomed into ghost-flowers. By June there were four such jobs waiting, and Felix had built her a special table by the good window with drawers shallow enough for maps.

It was, she reflected often, a strange kind of happiness — one assembled from small repairs, like everything else they made. Neither of them spoke much anymore about Marlow. He was in prison at Chatham, serving seven years, and wrote Felix a letter every month that said very little: *the light in my cell faces east; the warders' clock loses four seconds a day; mind the humidity in your shop or the veneer will lift.* Felix read each one twice and answered them all, and Beatrix never asked what he wrote back, and loved him, she thought, partly for whatever it was.

Once — only once — she had asked him whether he regretted it. The testimony. The ruin of the man who raised him.

Felix had been working on a striking train at the time, and he finished the tooth he was filing before answering, which was his way.

"When I was an apprentice," he said, "Master taught me that a clock doesn't lie. If it stops, it stops. If it runs fast, something's wrong inside it. You don't punish the clock. You find what's wrong and you mend it." He blew filings off the plate. "He taught me everything except how to apply it to himself. So I did it slowly. That's all. I mended it slowly."

"And us?"

"Us," Felix said, "was never broken. Only unwound."

* * *

On the last evening of summer, they closed early. This was unprecedented and Beatrix had marked the occasion by actually locking the door at half past five, to the visible alarm of a passing clerk who had never in his life seen a shopkeeper commit such an act.

They walked down to the river and stood on the old stairs where the tide lapped green against stone, and across the water the observatory sat white and certain on its hill, exactly where it had been through everything — through the forged certificates and the burning workshop and the long grey winter of testimony. The master clock Felix had repaired stood inside it still, keeping Greenwich time for every ship and every sailor and every ordinary soul who owned a watch and trusted it.

"You know what I think sometimes," Beatrix said. "In the tower, that first day. If you hadn't been the one sent to fix it. If they'd sent anyone else."

"They'd have sent someone competent."

"They sent you."

Felix smiled at the river. "And you intercepted a courier, broke into restricted archives in stolen livery, and nearly got transported. Between the two of us, Bee, history should be grateful it wasn't anyone else."

Across the water, faint with distance but unmistakable, the great bell began to strike six. They counted together — one, two, three, four, five, six — the notes falling on the darkening river the way they had fallen on the night everything changed, except that now the sound carried no threat in it, no secret, only the plain honest fact of another hour survived and another beginning.

When the last note faded, Beatrix felt a tug at her sleeve. Felix was holding out Edmund's watch.

"It's running," he said. "It's been running since March. I thought — well. I thought he'd want to hear it himself."

She took it. Through the case she could feel the small persistent heartbeat of it, patient as an archivist, faithful as a clockmaker, and somewhere beyond the sea her brother was walking toward that same tick, one port at a time, under his own name at last.

"Two pence," she said. "That's all it cost to tell me he's alive."

"Worth every farthing." Felix offered his arm, and she took it, and they turned up the hill toward home — past the lamplighter making his rounds, past the widow's window with its candle lit, past the small bright shop whose sign swung gently in the evening air, promising anyone who read it that broken things, given honesty and patience and a steady pair of hands, could be made to keep time again.

At the door, Beatrix paused. A second envelope lay on the mat, fresh postmark, foreign stamps — Rotterdam, this time, not Antwerp — and addressed not to her, nor to Felix, but in Edmund's unmistakable hand to:

*Messrs. Whitlock & Calloway, Clockmakers.*

She looked at Felix. Felix looked at the envelope.

"Well," he said, and his eyes were shining. "Shall we see what time brings?"
