## 1. The Summons

The letter came on a Tuesday, in the grey hour between the lamplighter's rounds and the baker's, when Marlow's Bend still belonged to the fog and the fog belonged to the river.

Tamsin Ishikawa heard the postman's boots before they saw him—everyone in Guilder Lane heard everything, the houses being pressed together like teeth—and set down the burnishing cloth and went to the door before old Mr. Quill could be woken by knocking. It was a habit learned early: answer things quietly, before they become noise, before they become questions.

The postman held out an envelope thick as a slice of bread, cream-colored, sealed in wax the color of dried blood. The address was written in a hand so fine it looked engraved.

*To the Master Clockmaker, premises of Quill & Sons, Guilder Lane.*

Not to Mr. Quill by name. To the *master*.

"Sign for it," said the postman, bored, already turning away.

Tamsin signed *A. Quill* in their careful, borrowed hand, and stood in the doorway watching him go until the fog folded him up, and then stood a while longer, holding the letter the way one holds a bird that has flown into a window—afraid that warmth or movement might break it.

Inside the shop, the clocks were doing what the clocks always did at half past six: eleven of them ticking in loose agreement, none of them quite right, all of them loud about it. Tamsin wound past the workbench with its scatter of pinions and mainspring hooks, past the shelf of customer pieces waiting like patients, up the narrow stair to the room where Mr. Quill slept behind a curtain he'd hung himself three winters ago when his legs began to fail him on the stairs.

"Ambrose," Tamsin said softly. "There's a letter."

The curtain moved. Old Quill's face appeared, gray as the morning, eyes sharp as always beneath it—the eyes of a man whose body had made its peace with dying long before the rest of him agreed to it. He saw the envelope and something crossed his face that Tamsin had never seen there before, not once in twelve years.

He was afraid of it.

"Well," he said. "Open it, then."

* * *

Tamsin read it aloud twice, because the first time their voice shook too badly to trust.

*Harrow Observatory, above Marlow's Bend, 14th October, 1893.*

*Sir,—It is known to us that your house maintains the finest clockwork in the county, and we write to engage you regarding our master timepiece, which has of late developed irregularities we are unable to correct by any means within our competence. The mechanism strikes falsely and keeps an hour we can neither confirm nor trust. You will understand that in a house given to the study of the heavens, this is no small embarrassment, nor small matter.*

*We request your attendance at the observatory at your earliest convenience. Remuneration will be generous and commensurate with the difficulty of the work. Kindly present this letter at the tollgate.*

*Yrs.,*
*E. Harrow*

Below, in a different hand—one older, shakier, written as though against resistance—a single line:

*Come quickly. Before the water comes.* — A.H.

Tamsin read the second line three times. *Before the water comes.* Every soul in the valley knew what that meant. The autumn rains were gathering in the hills the way debts gather in a ledger, and by November the river would take the bridge road, and whatever business waited on either bank would wait until spring or drown waiting.

"The Harrow Observatory," Mr. Quill said from his bed, and laughed—a dry sound, like a mainspring snapping in an empty case. "Old Aldous Harrow. I repaired his father's regulator forty years ago, back when my hands did what I told them. They say the old man hasn't left his chair in two years. They say he watches the sky through that great telescope the way other men watch a grave."

"He says come quickly."

"I heard." Quill pushed the curtain aside and sat up, and Tamsin moved without thinking to help him—hand under the elbow, weight accepted and returned, a choreography perfected over years. "And you'll go."

It was not a question. That was the trouble with Ambrose Quill; he had never once in twelve years asked Tamsin anything he wasn't prepared to answer himself.

"I'll go," Tamsin said. "You'll go. It's addressed to the master."

Quill looked at the letter. Then at Tamsin's hands—small, scarred at every knuckle, steady enough to balance a pallet stone on a needle point—and something in his face closed like a door.

"My boy," he began, using the word he used for convenience, the word the guild required, "if I could climb those stairs to that tower, do you think I'd send—" He stopped. Started again. "My hands shake now. You've seen it. A movement that delicate, a job that wants doing *quickly*, before the water—it wants young hands. It wants your hands."

"It wants *his* hands," Tamsin said. "That's what the letter says. That's what the guild seal on the tollgate book will want, when they check it. Master clockmaker, certified, entered in the rolls. Not—" They stopped themselves, but too late; the word was already in the room, filling it the way fog fills a lane.

*Half-blood.*

The silence stretched. Downstairs, eleven clocks ticked their uneven argument. Somewhere beyond the shop front a cart passed through standing water, wheels hissing.

"Tamsin." Quill's voice dropped low, the way it did when he spoke of things that must never be spoken of loudly. "Twelve years you've worked this bench. Twelve years, and I have watched you do work I could not do at thirty, work I never did at thirty. And the Guild of Horology would not put your name in their rolls if you built a clock that rang the sun down from heaven, because your mother was Japanese and your father was nobody at all, and because men who make rules are frightened of anyone the rules don't fit." His jaw worked. "I am not proud of the arrangement we've kept. But it has kept you fed, and sheltered, and out of the workhouse, and it has let your hands learn everything they know. And now—"

"And now the richest house in the valley is asking for me," Tamsin finished quietly. "*Us.* Asking for the master of Quill & Sons. And if the master goes, the master goes alone, because apprentices stay at the bench." They turned the letter over in their hands. The wax seal caught the lamplight. "Unless the apprentice doesn't sign the book at all."

Quill stared at them.

"They won't look closely," Tamsin said, and heard their own voice go flat and practical, the voice they used for hard measurements. "They're gentry. Gentry look at coats before faces. Your coat fits me well enough—I've been mending it for years so it would. And 'Ambrose Quill' is a name anyone will believe, because why would anyone lie their way *into* a clock repair?" They almost smiled. It didn't hold. "The tollgate keeper writes the name down. The name opens the gate. I do the work. I come home before the water takes the bridge, and no one in Marlow's Bend ever learns there was ever anyone at that tower except the master they summoned."

"No."

"You said yourself it wants my hands."

"*No.*" And then, softer, in the voice of a man losing an argument he'd been losing for weeks: "Tamsin. Listen to me. The Harrows are not a family one borrows a coat into. There are stories about that house. About the mother who died up there. About the will. Old Harrow summons a clockmaker and adds a line in his own hand—*come quickly, before the water comes*—and you think that's a repair? A repair gets done at leisure, invoiced, forgotten. That letter is a man reaching across a river for help with something he cannot say aloud. You walk into that house wearing a lie and carrying nothing else, and if it goes wrong—if they find you out, if the work goes ill—you'll be stranded on the far bank with the flood rising and no name at all to protect you. Not even a false one worth having."

Tamsin set the letter on the bedspread and smoothed it flat with both palms, the way they smoothed every difficult thing, brass and paper alike.

"Mr. Quill," they said. "In twelve years, how many jobs has this shop turned away?"

"None."

"How many have we taken that we shouldn't have?"

"One." His mouth twisted. "The Rennick carriage clock. I still dream about that escapement."

"And we survived it. Because you went, and you lied about the price, and you fixed what couldn't be fixed, and the guild gave you a medal for it that you keep in a drawer because you say medals are for men with nothing better to hang on walls." Tamsin picked the letter up again. "This is the first door that has ever opened toward me instead of past me. I know what's written on it isn't my name. But it's the only key I've ever been handed, sir, and I mean to use it before the water takes the lock."

For a long moment the old man said nothing. The fog outside the curtained window had begun, very faintly, to lighten—not dawn yet, only the promise that dawn was somewhere behind it, working its way downriver.

"If you go," he said finally, "you go as Ambrose Quill. Fully. No half-measures, no nervousness. A nervous liar is a caught liar. You speak when spoken to, you charge double what seems decent because gentry distrust cheap work, and you—" He raised a finger, and his hand trembled doing it, and he seemed to hate it more than he hated anything else in the world. "You come home. Before the bridge closes. Whatever state the clock is in, whatever state *they* are in, you come home. Promise me that."

"I promise," Tamsin said, and both of them knew it was a promise shaped like the ones customers made about payment—sincere at the moment of speaking, subject to the weather.

* * *

The coat was black wool, cut twenty years ago for a broader man, and Tamsin had spent three evenings making it fit—taking in the shoulders, letting down nothing, because height could not be altered and so must be disguised with posture. In the cracked looking glass at the back of the shop, the person who stood wearing it looked like exactly what the letter demanded: a master craftsman, respectable, unremarkable, a name and nothing more.

Tamsin practiced the face that went with it.

Not their own face—that was the difficulty. Their own face announced itself: the high cheekbones and dark, level eyes of their mother, the stubborn jaw of a father they had never met and never mourned, skin that in certain lights made shopkeepers follow them to the door and in others let them pass unremarked. Twelve years in Marlow's Bend had taught them every variation. Cap low, collar up, eyes down, and they were *the foreign boy at Quill's*. Head bare, chin up, meeting every eye, and they became something worse: a question people felt entitled to ask.

But under the coat, with the hat brim squared and the hands clasped behind the back the way Mr. Quil stood when pricing a job, they could be nobody. A function. A pair of hands attached to a signature.

"You'll want these." Mr. Quill laid the tools out on the green baize of the workbench with a ceremony he pretended was casual. The good loupe, the one he'd bought in London before Tamsin was born. The jeweler's screwdrivers rolled in flannel. A small brass oiler filled with the clock oil he guarded like blood. And last, wrapped separately in a scrap of chamois, a pallet file so fine its edge disappeared when you looked at it straight on.

"For the escapement," he said. "If it's striking wrong, it's the escapement or the count wheel, and a man who can't tell the difference has no business climbing anybody's tower." He paused, then added, gruffly, "Though I suspect whoever wrote that letter knows it too. *Strikes falsely and keeps an hour we can neither confirm nor trust.* That's not how a layman describes a fault. That's how a clockmaker describes it. Someone up in that observatory knows mechanisms."

"Then they'll know good work when they see it."

"Then they'll know *your* work," Quill corrected. "Which is the same thing, only better." He said it plainly, without performance, and Tamsin had to turn away and begin packing the tool roll so their face had somewhere to hide.

They left before full light, because the walk upriver took four hours and the summons said *quickly*. At the door, Quill caught their sleeve—the borrowed sleeve, the master's sleeve—and gripped it hard.

"Tamsin." Not Ambrose. Just the name, quiet, his own private word for them, the one true thing he'd give them to carry. "Whatever that old man wants—and mark me, he wants something, men who add lines to letters always want something—remember what you are. Not theirs. Not the guild's. Not mine, God forgive me for keeping you these years. You're the best clockmaker in this valley and the papers just haven't caught up. Go prove it."

Tamsin nodded, throat too tight for words, and went out into the fog.

* * *

The river road followed the water westward, and the water was already talking.

Not high—not yet—but restless, brown-green and muscular, running faster than its summer self, carrying leaves and fence rails and one entire hen coop downstream at a speed that made the morning feel less solid than it should. Farmers along the way were moving stock uphill without being told twice. At the second milestone Tamsin passed a woman stacking sandbags against her doorstep with the grim efficiency of someone who had done this dance before and knew the music would change soon enough.

*Before the water comes,* the old man had written. As if the water were a guest he expected. As if he were setting a place for it.

The tollgate stood where the river road met the foot of Harrow Hill, a stone arch straddling the bridge approach, and beside it the keeper's hut with its stovepipe smoking. The keeper was a broad, red-faced man reading a newspaper he held upside down, and he looked Tamsin up and down with the particular slowness gentry-trained servants reserved for strangers on foot.

"Business?"

"Summons to the observatory." Tamsin produced the letter. "Master clockmaker, Quill & Sons, Guilder Lane. Your book, if you please."

The keeper read the letter with his lips moving, which took some time, and then wrote in his ledger with enormous gravity, narrating as he went: "*One clockmaker, from town, for the Harrow place.* Name?"

"Ambrose Quill."

The pen scratched. The lie went into the book in official ink, and Tamsin watched it land there—*Ambrose Quill*, in a stranger's handwriting, becoming a fact—and felt something twist in their chest that was equal parts shame and exhilaration, a sensation they would spend the rest of this story learning to name.

"Bridge road's open today," the keeper said, handing the letter back. "Can't speak for tomorrow. River's come up a hand's width since Sunday. If I was going up that hill, I'd plan to stay till it's done and come down quick after."

"That's the intention."

"Mm." The keeper looked past Tamsin at the hill, where the observatory tower stood against the pale sky like a raised fist, its dome catching the first weak sunlight. "They don't get visitors up there. Years, sometimes. Odd household." He said it the way men say such things—offering the gossip and withdrawing it in the same breath, testing whether the stranger would bite.

Tamsin did not bite. "Thank you," they said, in Mr. Quill's flattest professional voice, and walked on.

The bridge itself was iron and timber, older than anyone living, sagging slightly at its center like a tired animal, and crossing it meant crossing the river with nothing but planks between. Halfway over, Tamsin stopped despite themselves and looked down. The water slid beneath, fast and brown, close enough to touch. A branch spun past in an eddy, snagged, tore free, and was gone downriver toward the sea and every road out of the valley.

On the far side, the road climbed. And climbed. Through wet pasture, through a stand of birches already stripped to bone, switchbacking up the shoulder of Harrow Hill until the town below shrank to a scatter of roofs and smoke, until the river showed itself whole for the first time—a great silver-brown serpent coiled through the valley, wider than Tamsin had ever seen it, and still rising.

The observatory resolved slowly out of the hillside: a long stone house built with its back to the valley, windows shuttered on the river side, all its attention turned inward and upward to the dome. A garden gone to seed. A stable with one horse. And everywhere that particular silence that belongs to houses where something is being waited for.

The door opened before Tamsin knocked.

A tall woman stood there, perhaps thirty, with her dark hair pinned so tightly it looked architectural, and eyes that went over Tamsin the way the tollgate keeper's had—coat first, face second, and then, unusually, face again, longer, as though filing something away.

"You're the clockmaker." Not a question.

"Ambrose Quill, madam. Of Quill & Sons. Summoned by letter regarding the master timepiece." The words came out exactly as rehearsed, and Tamsin marveled distantly at how steady they sounded, how completely the borrowed name fit the borrowed coat.

"I'm Verity Harrow." She stepped back to admit them, and as Tamsin crossed the threshold she spoke again, lightly, in the tone of a hostess discussing weather:

"You'll want to see the clock at once, I expect. It's in the tower room. Father is waiting there—he does so hate to be kept waiting by clocks." A pause, precisely measured. "Or by anyone else."

Somewhere overhead, muffled by stone floors, a clock struck the quarter hour.

It struck five ticks.

Tamsin counted them again without meaning to, the way they counted everything, and the number sat cold in their stomach all the way up the stairs.

Five. For fifteen minutes.

The clock was lying already, and they hadn't even begun.
