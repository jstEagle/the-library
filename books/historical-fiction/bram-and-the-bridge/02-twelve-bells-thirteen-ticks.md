## 2. Twelve Bells, Thirteen Ticks

The tower stairs wound counter-clockwise, which Tamsin thought was either an accident of masonry or a small cruelty designed for anyone carrying a toolbox. They climbed with the case strapped to their back like a coffin lid, one hand on the damp stone wall, counting landings the way they counted teeth on a wheel—by feel, by rhythm, by the certainty that the number would eventually arrive.

Ninety-one steps. The letter had said ninety-one steps to the clock room, and Tamsin had already learned that the Harrow Observatory did not write things down unless it meant them.

The air changed as they rose. At the bottom it had been river-damp and cold, the smell of drowned leaves and old rope. By the fortieth step it thinned into something drier, dustier, laced with lamp oil and the particular metallic sweetness of oiled brass that Tamsin knew better than their own mother's cooking. Somewhere above, through stone, came a sound they had been listening for since the tollgate: ticking.

But wrong.

They stopped on the landing at step sixty and held still, the way their master had taught them—*be quiet first, boy, and the machine will tell you its troubles*. Except the machine was not telling them troubles. It was telling them something stranger. A tick, tick, tick-tick pattern, regular as breathing, but with a hitch buried in it, a fractional hesitation every eighth beat, so faint that a lesser ear would have called the clock healthy and gone home.

Tamsin stood in the dark with their heart going faster than any pendulum in the building and thought: *someone has touched you.*

Then they told themselves they were being romantic. A clock of this age, this size, installed by some long-dead horologist who had probably never serviced it properly—the escapement could be worn, the suspension spring tired, the pallets gummy with forty years of impure oil. There were a hundred innocent explanations for a hesitation. They resumed climbing and made themselves recite the innocent explanations in order, like a rosary, all the way to the top.

The clock room occupied the whole upper chamber, octagonal, its walls pierced by tall arched windows that showed the sky going the color of pewter over the river. Rain streaked the glass. In the center stood the master clock itself, and Tamsin forgot, for a moment, everything else.

It was magnificent. A nine-foot regulator, weight-driven, with a deadbeat escapement visible behind a glazed door and a pendulum of rod and bob that swung with the unhurried authority of something that had outlived everyone who ever argued about time. The dial was white enamel, black numerals, hands of pierced brass. Someone had loved this clock once. You could tell from the wear patterns—the door handle polished bright by generations of thumbs, a shallow crescent worn into the floorboards where someone had knelt to wind it ten thousand times.

And someone had hated it too. Recently.

Tamsin set down the toolbox, unstrapped it, and let themselves look without touching. Master's rule number two: *look until you know what you'll find, then touch once.* The case was clean, too clean, the glass smudged in one place near the lower hinge—a hand print, small, wiped hastily. On the floor beneath the pendulum, a faint arc of scratches in the dust, fresher than the others, as though the bob had been swinging wider than its usual arc. And there—Tamsin crouched, squinting—a curl of bright metal shaving caught in the seam between two floorboards. Brass. Freshly cut. No clockmaker sweeps up completely; there is always a leavings, if you know where to kneel.

The clock struck eleven.

Or it tried to. Tamsin counted the way they counted everything, silently, lips still: *one, two, three*—the bell voice was deep and slightly cracked, a fine old bell with a flaw in it—*four, five, six, seven, eight, nine, ten, eleven.*

Twelve.

Tamsin went cold all over, not from the rain at the windows but from the inside out.

The clock had struck twelve at eleven o'clock. Not eleven strokes and one stray. Twelve clean, deliberate strokes, spaced evenly, the last dying into the room like a word someone had decided not to take back.

They waited, hardly breathing, half expecting the thing to correct itself, to ring again, to apologize. It didn't. It simply resumed ticking, that same hitched rhythm, *tick tick tick-tick*, as though nothing had happened, as though it had always been noon when it was eleven, and would go on insisting so.

"That's the trouble," said a voice behind them. "It lies."

Tamsin turned so fast they knocked the toolbox with their heel.

In the corner of the clock room, half-hidden by the curve of the wall, sat a wheelchair, and in the wheelchair sat an old man. He was very thin, wrapped in a dressing gown of dark wool despite the chill, with white hair combed carelessly and a face like a topographical map of some hard country. His eyes were the surprise: dark, quick, entirely awake, fixed on Tamsin with an attention that felt less like looking and more like weighing.

"I—" Tamsin began, and caught themselves, and produced instead the sentence they had rehearsed on the road: "Good morning, sir. I've come from Master Fenwick's shop, regarding the master clock. He sends his apologies—he's been taken ill—but I'm instructed to make the repair under his name and to his standard." Their voice only shook a little on the last part. The borrowed coat hung heavy on their shoulders, two sizes too large, smelling of another man's pipe smoke. The lie sat inside it like a second coat.

Mr. Harrow did not answer at once. His gaze traveled down Tamsin and up again, slowly, and Tamsin felt it catch on all the places they had worried at all night: the too-young hands, the slight accent they could never fully file off the vowels, the face beneath the hat brim that no amount of shadowing could make ordinary. Half of Marlow's Bend had stared at them across shop counters for twelve years, doing arithmetic with their eyes—*foreign father, local mother, neither one thing nor the other*—and Tamsin knew every variety of that stare. This was not quite any of them. This was closer to how they themselves looked at a movement with the dial off: reading what was underneath.

"Eleven o'clock," Mr. Harrow said. "It struck twelve. Yesterday it struck noon at twenty past. The day before, it lost nearly an hour, then gained it back overnight, as though it had repented." He folded his thin hands atop the blanket. "I have owned better clocks and worse clocks, but I have never owned a dishonest one before. Can you mend dishonesty, young man?"

*Young man.* Tamsin swallowed the small complicated relief and the smaller complicated guilt that rode with it, and gave the professional answer, because the professional answer was the only territory on which they had ever been allowed to stand.

"A clock doesn't lie by nature, sir. Something is making it lie. My work is to find the something."

"And if the something is a person?"

The question landed in the room like a dropped gear. Through the windows the rain thickened, hissing against the tower stones. Below them the whole house of Harrow seemed to hold its breath—the observatory was quiet in the particular way of houses with a dying man in them, every footstep softened, every door closed like a held tongue.

"I would still find it," Tamsin said quietly. "The clock keeps the record either way."

Something moved across the old man's face—not quite a smile, more the place a smile had been quarried out of. "So it does," he murmured. "So it does. Well then. Look at my liar. Look as long as you like. Dinner is at six; Mrs. Pell will find you." He turned the wheels of his chair with small precise movements and rolled toward the stair door, pausing at the threshold. "Master Fenwick's apprentice," he added, not turning around. "You wind it counterclockwise, and you don't force the fourth click. And you noticed the shaving on the floor before you touched anything." The chair went through the doorway. "Fenwick never notices anything. I wrote to him twice about his own shop sign hanging crooked."

His footsteps—his chair's slow wheels—faded down the ninety-one steps.

Tamsin stood alone with the clock and the rain and the ringing absence of twelve false strokes, and understood two things with perfect clarity. First: the old man knew exactly who he had hired, or at least exactly that he had not hired whom he thought. Second: they had just been given permission to do the only thing in the world they actually knew how to do.

They worked through the afternoon, and the work was a conversation, and for long stretches Tamsin forgot to be afraid.

The movement came apart under their hands in stages, each stage photographed by memory: dial off, hands wrapped in cloth and laid in order on a bench Mrs. Pell must have stocked that morning, because beside the toolbox sat a tray with bread, cheese, and a jug of water nobody had mentioned. The great plates lifted away. The barrel came free, heavy with cable, its mainspring humming with stored patience. Gear train laid out left to right, escape wheel centered, pallet fork cradled in a rag like a bird with a broken wing.

The escapement was the heart, and Tamsin examined the heart last, because hearts should be examined last.

Under the loupe, lit by a lamp carried up from the shop and burning whale oil that smelled of their entire childhood, the truth assembled itself tooth by tooth. The escape wheel was good steel, sixty teeth, and forty-two of those teeth were sound. But the remaining eighteen—every eighteenth tooth, in sequence, so regular it could not be accident—had been filed. Not broken, not chipped by age. *Filed*, with a fine edge, by someone patient, each reduced tooth letting the pallet drop a hair deeper before catching. That explained the hesitation every eighth beat. And the pallets themselves—one stone had been shifted in its setting, a fraction of a millimeter, enough to alter the lock and let the wheel occasionally advance an extra impulse. Over hours, the error compounded unpredictably: sometimes slow, sometimes fast, always deniable. No honest repairman would call it damage. Even a suspicious one might call it wear.

But Tamsin had grown up learning wear the way farmers learn weather. Wear follows physics. This followed intent.

They sat back on the worn kneeling-crescent—someone else's knees had made that hollow, decades of them—and looked at the disassembled heart spread across the bench, gleaming in lamplight, and felt the unease of the staircase sharpen into something colder and more interesting. Whoever had done this work was skilled. Not watchmaker-skilled, but close. The filing was confident, the reassembly invisible to a casual eye. It would have taken hours at this bench, in this room, with the door closed—and the clock room was not a room one entered casually. It sat at the top of ninety-one stairs in a private house.

Which meant the person who filed these teeth had a key. Or was trusted with the door open. Or both.

And the twelve-stroke strike at eleven—that was simpler, nastier. Tamsin turned to the striking train, found the count wheel, and there it was: the gathering pallet shimmed, the pins altered so the rack released one extra tooth per hour. Every hour, forever after, the clock would claim one stroke more than the hour it kept. Eleven would ring twelve. Midnight would ring thirteen. The bell would stand in the tower announcing a time that did not exist, and everyone below would set their days by the lie, because a town trusts its master clock the way a family trusts its mother.

Unless, said a small stubborn voice that sounded like their mother's, you are the one person listening closely enough to hear the extra tick.

Rain drove harder against the octagonal windows. The light failed early, the way October light fails, going from pewter to iron to nothing. Tamsin lit a second lamp and kept working, because working was safer than thinking, and thinking was dangerous in a specific direction: *why?* Why would anyone teach a clock to lie, in a house where—Mrs. Pell had said it herself on the stairs, kindly, terribly—*the master keeps his papers in order, poor soul, knows the end is coming and wants everything settled proper.* Papers. Wills. Hours. Dates. Times of signing, times of—

Tamsin put the thought down carefully, the way you set down a live spring, and picked up the pallet fork instead.

The repair itself would not be difficult. That was almost funny. Eighteen teeth to restore—re-cut and harden, a week's patient work, or replace the wheel entirely if the observatory kept spares, which a house like this would. Reset the pallet stone. Remove the shim. Three days' labor to make the clock honest again, and no tool on earth could make the people in this house honest, and Tamsin was beginning to suspect those two facts were entangled in some way that had nothing to do with gears.

Outside, far below, the river muttered. Between rain gusts Tamsin heard other sounds rising up the stairwell from the body of the house: a door closing somewhere, firmly; a woman's voice raised briefly and cut off, like a page torn out; the creak of the old man's chair moving along a corridor, unhurried, patient, a sound with intention in it.

And once—Tamsin froze, loupe still at their eye—once, footsteps on the tower stairs. Ascending. Slowly. Stopping, it seemed, two turns below the clock room door, where the narrow slit window let in the last grey light. Standing there. Long enough for Tamsin's heart to perform three full irregular cycles. Then descending again, fading into the bones of the house.

Not Mr. Harrow. The chair wheels had a sound; these were boots.

At half past five Tamsin began reassembling, because whatever else was true, a stopped master clock helped no one and reflected badly on a name that was not theirs. They fitted the train, hung the pendulum, set the hands to a pocket watch whose honesty they could vouch for, and left the escapement gutted deliberately—the pallets out, the escape wheel wrapped—in the one piece of insurance they possessed. A clock that cannot tick cannot lie. Whatever game was being played upstairs in this tower, tonight it would have to pause, and whoever needed the clock running would have to come and ask the new apprentice why.

Let them come. Let them knock on this door and see what sat at the bench: a borrowed coat, a borrowed name, and hands that knew every tooth of a movement by heart.

Tamsin washed the oil from their fingers in the basin, packed the tools, and took one last look around the octagon room—at the great silent dial, at the rain-blurred windows, at the bright curl of brass shaving they had left exactly where it lay, evidence waiting for whoever thought to look.

On the floor by the door, they noticed, someone had chalked a small mark. An X, knee-high, fresh, the chalk dust still crisp at its edges.

Below, dimly, through ninety-one steps of stone, the bell began to count six. Six strokes, even and cracked and true—for now.

Tamsin picked up the toolbox, and went down to meet the family.
