## 3. The House That Faces Away

The observatory was built like a man who had turned his back on the town and did not intend to apologize for it.

Tamsin understood this by the second day. The house stood on the highest knoll east of Marlow's Bend, a long grey body of stone with the great dome capping it like a bowed head, and every important window faced away from the river—away from the bridge, away from the town, away from the smoke of chimneys and the sound of the market bell. What windows looked toward Marlow's Bend were small and high, slit like the eyes of a man listening without wanting to be seen to listen. From the tower, Tamsin knew, you could see everything. From the rooms below, you saw only sky and your own reflection.

*They watch the heavens,* Tamsin thought, carrying a bucket of coal up the back stair the first morning, *and let the earth keep itself.*

It was not so different from Master Fenwick's shop in the town, where Tamsin had slept in the loft for six years and learned to move through another person's house like a draft under a door—invisible, useful, unremarked. But there the silence had been one language, and Tamsin had been fluent in it. Here the silence came in dialects.

There was Edmund's silence, which was made of courtesy.

There was Verity's, which was made of watching.

And there was Mrs. Pell's, which was not a silence at all but a room with the door left open.

---

Edmund Harrow came down to breakfast at half past eight precisely, and Tamsin learned his clockwork before they ever touched the great movement upstairs. He entered at the same minute each morning; he drew out his chair at the same angle; he unfolded his napkin with two motions, never three. He greeted Tamsin each time with the same sentence—*"I trust the work proceeds"*—in a tone that made clear the question required no true answer, only evidence of industry. He was perhaps thirty-four, tall, grey-eyed as river slate, with his father's long jaw and none of his father's warmth, if the old man's warmth could be believed—and Tamsin was no longer certain it could. The first evening, dying Mr. Harrow had watched them from his chair by the clock room door with eyes like a man reading an instrument. Edmund watched people the way a creditor watches accounts.

"You'll take tea in the workroom," he said on the third morning, not asking. "Mrs. Pell will see to it. I'd rather you didn't come down while my father is resting. The noise of the household disturbs him."

"The noise of me," Tamsin said, "is mostly ticking."

Something crossed Edmund's face then—not quite a smile, more the place where a smile would be kept if he ever chose to store one. "Quite," he said, and folded his napkin, and rose, and was gone into the morning room where the estate papers lived, closing the door behind him with a click as exact as a pallet dropping into place.

Tamsin sat a moment longer at the long table, alone with the cold toast and the silver that needed no polishing because it was polished hourly by someone else's hands, and wrote the rule in their head the way they wrote all rules: *In this house, Edmund's courtesy is a wall with a door in it. The door opens for him only.*

They thought of Master Fenwick then, unexpectedly—a smell of oil and burnt coffee, a voice saying *left-hand thread, girl, pay attention*—and pushed the thought down where it couldn't be seen from the outside.

---

Verity Harrow was harder to map, because Verity did not announce herself.

She appeared in places the way light appears through moving leaves. On the fourth day Tamsin looked up from the disassembled escapement—the escapement, that wrong-ticking heart of the place, laid out now on chamois cloth like a surgery—and found her standing at the clock room doorway, arms folded, saying nothing.

"Miss Harrow." Tamsin put down the tweezers.

"Don't stop on my account." She didn't come in. She stood exactly at the threshold, one hand on the frame, as though she'd learned long ago which rooms were hers to enter and which weren't, and had decided this one wasn't, and resented it. She was twenty-six or twenty-seven, dark-haired, with her mother's face according to Mrs. Pell—though Tamsin hadn't yet dared ask how Mrs. Pell would know—and a habit of looking at people slightly longer than politeness allowed, as if checking their seams.

"You found something," Verity said.

"Found?"

"In the clock. You've had it apart four days. My father's clocks are temperamental, not mysterious. Either you don't know what you're doing—" she tilted her head "—which the way you handled that recoil pallet yesterday suggests is a lie, or you found something."

Tamsin kept their hands still. This was a skill trained into them across twelve years and two countries' worth of being looked at: hands still, face smooth, give nothing. "A clock that strikes thirteen has something to say," they said carefully. "I'm listening."

Verity's eyes narrowed—not in suspicion, Tamsin realized afterward. In recognition. It was the look of someone who had spent her whole life in this house listening to things that would not say themselves plainly, and hearing a fellow listener speak her own dialect.

"Hm," she said, which in her mouth was evidently a whole conversation. Then: "You're younger than I expected. For a master repairer."

"I'm older than I look."

"That," said Verity, turning away, "is also a lie, and a worse one than the first." And she was gone down the stair, quick and quiet, leaving Tamsin with their heart tapping against their ribs like a balance wheel run fast.

That evening Tamsin added the rule: *Verity counts everything. Assume you have already been counted.*

---

Mrs. Pell broke all the rules, and did it with bread.

She was the housekeeper, sixty if a day, built low and broad like a mooring post, with forearms that had hauled kettles for forty years and a face that had decided, somewhere around 1850, what it thought about nearly everything and saw no reason to revise. She was also, Tamsin discovered within twenty-four hours, the only person in the house who spoke above a whisper.

"You're not eating," she said the second night, appearing in the workroom doorway with a tray Tamsin hadn't asked for. Stew, bread still steaming, a heel of cheese. "Mr. Edmund says take tea in the workroom. Tea isn't supper. Eat."

"I couldn't trouble—"

"Trouble's already happened, dear. You're in it. Eat."

Tamsin ate. Mrs. Pell stayed, ostensibly to set the tray straight, actually to talk, which she did in a low continuous stream that reminded Tamsin of water finding its way downhill—around obstacles, never arguing with them, arriving anyway. She talked about the rain coming, about Mr. Harrow's medicine costing what it cost, about the cook who'd left in spring and not been replaced because Mr. Edmund said the household must economize, *economize*, she said, *as if the man eats air*. She asked Tamsin three questions in ten minutes and answered most of them herself before Tamsin could decide whether to lie.

By the fifth night she was sitting properly, on the stool by the bench, hands folded over her apron, watching Tamsin work with frank interest.

"My late husband was a wheelwright," she said. "He said a man who works with his hands thinks with them too, and there's no trusting anyone who doesn't. No offense meant to the clergy."

"None taken," said Tamsin, who was neither clergy nor, strictly speaking, a man, and let both facts sit quietly in the room.

Mrs. Pell looked at them for a moment—a long, level, unhurried look, the kind that went in and checked the locks—and then nodded once, as if some question had been answered to her satisfaction, and began talking again about the rain.

It was on the sixth night, over the stew, that she said the thing Tamsin turned over for an hour afterward in the narrow bed they'd been given under the eaves of the servants' wing.

"Twenty-two years I've kept this house," Mrs. Pell said, gathering the tray. "Kept it through the mistress dying, God rest her, and the mistress before her being the boys' mother and no mistake, whatever anyone says. Kept it through two wills I know of and I'd wager my pension on a third in the drawer nobody's opened." She paused at the door. "Paper burns easy, dear. Brass don't. Remember that when you're working up here among all this brass."

Then she was gone, and Tamsin sat with the lamp and the open movement and the word *brass* ringing in their ears like a struck bell that refused to settle.

---

On the seventh day, Tamsin mapped the house itself, because a clockmaker trusts drawings more than impressions.

They did it the honest way, by carrying tools and coal and trays of parts through every corridor the household used, and the dishonest way, by the simple trick of being small, quiet, and presumed to have business everywhere a clock might hang. Every room held a clock. Eight-day movements in the hall and morning room, a carriage clock on Edmund's desk, French enamel on Verity's mantel, and in the old master's chamber a plain black case that ticked with the slow, heavy patience of a man counting out what remained.

The geography told its own story. The household lived in the east range: Edmund's rooms nearest the study, Verity's at the corner with windows on two sides—she liked to see things coming, Mrs. Pell said, and didn't explain—the old man's chamber at the foot of the tower stair, so that his nurse had only forty paces to carry him to the clock room, which he visited daily in his wheeled chair, saying nothing, watching everything. The west range was shuttered: the mistress's rooms, closed since Tamsin didn't know when, dust sheets pale behind the glass like ghosts told to stand still.

And the servants' wing, where Tamsin slept, connected to the main house by one door and one stair, and the stair passed directly beneath the clock room.

This last fact Tamsin noted with the particular attention of someone who had spent six years learning which floorboards in Master Fenwick's shop announced visitors, and had not yet decided whether they were the sort of person who would use such knowledge or merely possess it.

*Everyone in this house knows where everyone else is,* they wrote in the little notebook they kept for measurements, beneath a sketch of the escapement's damaged pallet. *The architecture makes sure of it. Which means either no one has anything to hide—* they looked at the filed tooth gleaming innocently among its brothers on the chamois cloth *—or everyone has gotten very good.*

---

The rain began properly on the eighth night.

It came as Mrs. Pell had promised it would, off the western hills, a soft grey curtain drawn across the valley, and by morning the river voice had changed. Tamsin heard it even up in the tower—a new undertone beneath the ticking, deeper and more insistent, the sound of a patient animal beginning to shift its weight.

At breakfast Edmund read the weather aloud from the *Gazette* as though the paper were personally responsible for it. "River expected to crest by the week's end. Bridge traffic suspended at the tollgate when the gauge passes nine feet." He set the paper down. "Which it will."

"How long?" Tamsin asked.

Edmund considered them across the marmalade. "In '81 it was five weeks. In '87, seven." A pause, calibrated. "You needn't look alarmed, Mr.—Fenwick. The house stands high. We've ridden out worse with fewer hands." His use of the borrowed name was flawless, unhurried, and somehow made Tamsin feel the lie of it pressed flat between them like a flower in a book. "Though I confess we're not accustomed to guests of such duration. I trust the arrangement causes no difficulty at home? Your own establishment being so suddenly deprived of its master?"

"I'm certain the shop survives without us," Tamsin said. "Clocks are patient."

"Yes," said Edmund, and for just a moment his eyes went upward, toward the tower, toward the movement that struck thirteen. "They are. That is precisely their value."

Verity, who had been buttering toast with the concentration of a surgeon, looked up at her brother and then at Tamsin, and Tamsin saw it happen: the flicker of arithmetic behind her eyes, one silent person measuring another against a third. Then she returned to her toast as if nothing had occurred, because in this house nothing ever occurred where it could be observed.

*Three silences,* Tamsin wrote that night, by candlelight, under the sloping eaves, rain loud on the slates. *One made of walls. One made of counting. One made of doors left open. And mine—*

They stopped. They listened. Beneath the rain, beneath the floor, faint and steady as a pulse carried through the bones of the building, the master clock ticked on in the tower above—wrong, still wrong, thirteen strokes at noon, running slow by four minutes a day, lying with every beat—and Tamsin understood that their own silence was not separate from the others. It was load-bearing. Remove it and something would come down.

They blew out the candle.

In the dark, sleep would not come, and after an hour Tamsin gave it up, dressed, took the lamp, and went up the servants' stair—past the turn where the stair ran directly beneath the clock room floor—to fetch the notebook they'd left on the workbench.

The lamp guttered as they climbed. And through the boards underfoot, faint but unmistakable, came a sound that did not belong to any hour Tamsin knew: the deliberate, careful *tick... tick... pause... tick* of a pendulum being adjusted by hand.

Someone was in the clock room, and it was not yet midnight, and the old master's chamber at the foot of the stair had been silent when Tamsin passed it.

Tamsin stood very still on the stair, lamp lowered, breath shallow, and listened to the house teach them its final rule:

*Whatever happens in this house, someone is always already there.*
