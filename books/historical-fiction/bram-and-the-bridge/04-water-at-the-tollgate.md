## 4. Water at the Tollgate

The rain began on a Tuesday, and by Friday the river had swallowed the tollgate.

Verity Harrow stood at the drawing-room window with her arms crossed, watching the brown water climb the stone piers of Marlow's Bridge. The gatehouse lamp still burned at its post, though the keeper had abandoned it two days past, and now the light swung in its bracket like something drowning. Below, the river carried whole trees sideways, their roots clawing at the air, and once—she was almost certain—a drowned sheep, white belly up, turning slowly as it went under the arches.

"Three days," Edmund said behind her, without looking up from his ledger. "That's what Pell's boy says. Three days of rain like this and they'll close it."

"They've already closed it." She nodded toward the far bank, where a knot of rivermen had gathered under oilskins, shouting across the water to no one who could hear them. "Look at them. They're not warning anyone. They're saying goodbye to the season."

Edmund turned a page. The scratch of his pen was the only other sound in the room besides the rain.

Verity had lived her whole life in a house that faced away from the river. Her father had built the observatory on the high ground above Marlow's Bend with its back to the water and its face to the sky, as if he could not bear to look down at the town that had made him rich, or perhaps could not bear for the town to look up at him. From the tower windows you could see everything—the bridge, the bend, the smoke of the mills—and from the ground floor you could see nothing but your own reflection in the glass. Verity had always thought the house was shaped exactly like her father's heart.

Now the river was rising to meet it anyway.

* * *

She went up to check on him after luncheon, taking the back stair because she did not want to pass through the clock room.

It was not that she disliked clocks. It was that she disliked what the clock room had become: a territory. For eleven days the stranger had been up there, morning to night, with the door half-open and the smell of oil and hot metal drifting down into the hall, and the whole house had rearranged itself around that smell the way iron filings arrange around a magnet. Mrs. Pell brought tea up twice a day and came down with reports—the escapement is worse than we thought, the mainspring wants replacing, there's a crack in the great wheel, ma'am, a crack you could put a shilling through. Edmund had begun taking his evening brandy in the corridor outside the clock room door, ostensibly reading, actually listening. Even Father, who had not left his chair by the east window in a month, had asked—had *asked*, in that thin voice that cost him something every time he used it—whether the young man had made progress today.

*Young man.* Verity pressed her lips together as she climbed.

There was something wrong about the stranger. She could not have said what, precisely, and this irritated her more than if she could. He was competent—that much was undeniable; she had stood in the doorway once, unannounced, and watched his hands move inside the movement with a certainty that made her own fingers itch. But competence was not the same as belonging. He wore a coat a size too large for him, a master clockmaker's coat with another man's initials inked inside the collar—she had seen them, *A.W.*, when he hung it on the peg. He never ate in the kitchen with Mrs. Pell, though she invited him. He answered questions correctly and completely and never once volunteered anything. And when Verity looked at him directly—which she made a habit of doing, because people who were hiding something always flinched eventually—he did not flinch. He simply became very still, like a hare that has decided the fox has not seen it yet.

Her father's door was open. He sat in his chair by the window with a blanket over his knees, and the light coming through the glass was the color of weak tea.

"You're standing in the draught," he said, without turning.

"I'm standing in the doorway."

"In my house, the doorway is where the draught lives." He waved her in with two fingers. His hands had grown thin since midsummer; she could see the architecture of them now, the tendons standing up like bridge cables. "Come and tell me what the river's doing."

"Drowning the tollgate. Edmund thinks the bridge closes within the week."

"Mm." He gazed out at the grey sheeting rain. "And our clockmaker?"

"Still tinkering."

"You disapprove of him."

"I don't know him. That's different from disapproval. I'd have to know someone before I could disapprove of them properly."

That earned her the ghost of a smile. Her father's smiles had become ghosts of themselves—all the shape remaining, the substance gone. "Your grandmother used to say that. Word for word."

"My grandmother also buried three husbands and outlived all of them comfortably, so perhaps her methods deserve respect."

He laughed, and then coughed, and Verity was beside him before she had decided to move, one hand on his shoulder, feeling the bones beneath the wool. When the coughing passed he patted her hand twice—an old gesture, dismissive and grateful at once—and she withdrew to the window seat and pretended, as they both preferred, that nothing had happened.

"The will's settled," he said presently, to the rain. "You know that."

"I know you say it's settled."

"It is settled." There was steel in it, sudden and surprising, the old merchant's voice from thirty years ago. "Whatever Edmund tells you, whatever he implies over his ledgers—there is a paper in Mr. Grieve's office with my signature and two witnesses, and it says what I intend it to say. You are not to let him wear you down with arithmetic, Verity. Arithmetic is how he loves, and it is not love at all."

"Father—"

"That's all." He closed his eyes, which was how he ended conversations now, and Verity sat a moment longer watching the rain crawl down the glass, wanting to ask the question that had been sitting in her chest for eleven days: *why me? why not him? why, after forty years of building everything for your son, would you turn at the end and give it to your daughter?*

But asking it felt like digging in a grave to see whether the person was really dead. So she rose, kissed the top of his head where the hair had gone fine as frost, and left him to his window.

In the corridor, she stopped. Below her, through the floor, she could hear it: the tick of the master clock, transmitted through the timbers of the house like a pulse through a wrist. Except that it wasn't right. She had noticed it days ago and told herself she was imagining it, but she was not an imaginative woman and she knew what she heard. Twelve strokes at noon, yes—but between the strokes, in the long silences, the mechanism ticked with a small irregular hitch, a stutter, like a heart with a murmur.

*Tink-tick. Tink-tick-tick.*

She stood listening until the sound made her teeth ache, and then she went down to find Mrs. Pell.

* * *

The kitchen was the warmest room in the observatory and the only honest one. Everything else in the house performed—the drawing rooms performed comfort, the study performed industry, her father's bedroom performed convalescence—but the kitchen only cooked, and steamed, and smelled of bread, and Mrs. Pell moved through it like a ship through weather, unbothered, sleeves rolled, forearms dusted with flour.

Verity sat at the scrubbed table and accepted tea she hadn't asked for.

"How is he?" Mrs. Pell asked, meaning the father, not the patient upstairs. In this house there were two invalids now, and everyone knew which one mattered.

"He coughed. He laughed first, which is either good or very bad." Verity wrapped her hands around the cup. "Mrs. Pell. How long has the clock been wrong?"

The older woman didn't pause in her kneading, but Verity saw her shoulders change—saw the thought move through her like a fish under ice. "Wrong how, miss?"

"Slow. Or uneven. There's a hitch in it. A tick too many, sometimes, between the hours. You'd hear it if you listened."

"And have you been listening?" Mrs. Pell said mildly, to the dough.

"Everyone in this house is listening. That's my point. Edmund stands outside that door every evening pretending to read. Father asks after the repair like it's a matter of state. You carry up reports twice a day. We are a household of spies arranged around a clock, and nobody will say aloud what we're all listening *for*."

Mrs. Pell set the dough down. She wiped her hands on her apron with great deliberation, and when she turned, her face was kind and careful, which was how Verity knew she was about to be managed.

"The master clock keeps the time for the whole town, miss. Railway schedules, the mill bells, the magistrate's court. If it runs true, everything under it runs true. If it doesn't—" She shrugged. "Well. It's worth repairing properly, isn't it? That's all any of us are waiting on."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the answer there is." Mrs. Pell came and refilled her cup, though it was half full, which was a way of ending a conversation while appearing to continue it. "Drink your tea. The rain's set in for the night, and there's nothing on this earth so miserable as a wet evening spent suspecting things."

But at the door, with her hand on the jamb, Verity paused. "Tobias was on the bridge this morning," she said. "I saw him at the tollgate with the rivermen. He looked ill."

Something crossed Mrs. Pell's face then—quick, gone in a breath, but Verity caught it the way you catch a card sharp's glance at his sleeve. Fear. Not for Tobias on the bridge. Something else.

"He's got a cold coming," Mrs. Pell said. "They all catch colds on that water. Good night, miss."

* * *

That night the rain turned from weather into event.

Verity woke past midnight to a sound she had never heard before: the river, audible from the tower bedrooms, a low continuous roar like a train that never arrives. She lit a candle and went to the window, and what she saw made her stand there a long time with the wax dripping over her knuckles.

The valley below had become a lake. The mill lane was gone. The tollgate was a lamp burning on an island. And the bridge—Marlow's Bridge, the only road out of the Bend, the road her grandfather had helped pay for, the road she had crossed a thousand times without ever thinking of it as a rope tied around her ankle—the bridge showed only its crowns, five stone humps rising out of black moving water, and even those would go under by morning if the rain held.

They were sealed in. All of them. Sealed into a house with a dying father, a brother who loved arithmetic, a stranger with another man's name in his collar, and a clock that ticked wrong.

She dressed properly and took the candle down through the dark house, meaning only to look—to stand outside the clock room door and satisfy herself that the sounds she'd heard were nothing, a trick of the wind in the tower, an old mechanism grumbling the way old mechanisms do. That was all. Suspicion was a habit, not a conclusion; her father had taught her that, ironically enough, in the counting-house summers of her girlhood. *Verify before you believe, Verity.* He had been pleased with himself over the rhyme.

The corridor outside the clock room was dark, but the door stood ajar, and light moved within—not lamplight, steadier than that. Candlelight. And voices, low.

Verity stopped. She pinched out her own candle and stood in the black, breathing through her mouth.

Two voices. One was the stranger's—she knew its odd careful music by now, the accent that couldn't be placed, educated and foreign at once. The other was Edmund's, and Edmund was speaking very quietly, in the voice he used for negotiations, the one that sounded like reason and was not.

"—a matter of some delicacy," Edmund was saying. "My father's affairs are not public matters. Whatever you find in that mechanism, you'll report to me before anyone else. That's not a request. It's the condition of your employment."

A silence. Long enough that Verity's heart began to hurt against her ribs.

Then the stranger said, softly, "And if what I find should concern the mechanism's *honesty*, sir? Rather than its health?"

Another silence, longer.

"Clocks don't lie," Edmund said at last, and there was something in his voice she had never heard there before—something almost like fear, quickly buried. "They're machines. They do what they're built to do. Good night."

Footsteps. Verity had three seconds, maybe four. She stepped backward into the recess of the linen cupboard door, pulling her dark dressing gown close, and Edmund passed within arm's reach of her without seeing, his candle held high, his face set hard as the stone of the bridge below.

She waited until his door clicked shut. Then she waited longer, counting the ticks of the faulty clock through the floor, twelve of them, twenty, thirty, until her eyes had adjusted and her hands had stopped shaking.

The clock room door was still ajar. The light was still burning.

And through the gap, faintly, she could hear the stranger working—not the confident sounds of repair, but something slower, more careful. The sound of someone taking a thing apart who already knows what they will find inside, and is afraid of it.

Verity stood in the dark corridor of her sealed house, with the river roaring below like a held breath finally released, and understood that the clock was not broken by accident.

Tomorrow, she decided, she would stop listening at doors.

Tomorrow, she would walk straight through one.
