## 5. The Dying Man's Request

The summons came on the third morning, not by letter but by a child — Mrs. Pell's youngest kitchen girl, breathless from the stairs, her cap askew.

"He wants you. The master. Up in his room. He said—" she swallowed, "—he said *the clockmaker*, and Mrs. Pell says that's you."

Tamsin set down the pallet file they had been dressing. Their hands were grey with brass dust, and they wiped them on the rag at their belt out of habit rather than need, because the dust did not come off so easily as that. It never did. It was in the creases of their knuckles, under the nails, ground into the whorls of their thumbs like a second skin — the mark of the trade they were permitted to practice only in another man's name.

"Did he say why?"

"No, miss." The girl hesitated. "Sir. He didn't say."

*Miss-sir.* The household had been arguing quietly with itself for three days over what to call them, and had settled, in the end, on both, delivered with the careful neutrality of people stepping around a hole in the floor. Tamsin had decided to be flattered. At least the hole was acknowledged.

They climbed. The observatory's central stair wound upward through the heart of the building like the screw of a great press, and every turn of it brought the sound of the house closer: the tick of corridor clocks, the murmur of Mrs. Pell below, and above everything, faint but constant, the master clock in its tower room, keeping — Tamsin now knew — one tick too many in every minute. Sixty-one where there should be sixty. A small lie, repeated without pause, all day, all night, patient as water.

They had not yet told anyone what they'd found in the escapement. They were not sure yet what they had found. Filed teeth could be age. A shifted pallet could be careless repair by some journeyman decades dead. But the filing was recent — the bright metal of it unoxidized, sharp-edged, deliberate — and Tamsin's fingers, which had never once lied to them about metal, kept returning to the same word. *Deliberate.*

Old Mr. Harrow's room occupied the chamber directly beneath the clock room, which struck Tamsin as either sentiment or strategy. A dying man who wanted to hear time pass above his head every hour of the day and night. Or a dying man who wanted to keep an ear on it.

The door stood open. Mrs. Pell had left a tray — broth, bread, a glass of something amber — untouched on the side table, and the smell of the sickroom was underneath everything else: camphor, coal smoke, and the sweetish scent of a body burning through its own last fuel.

Aurelius Harrow sat propped in a chair by the window, wrapped in a dressing gown of dark green wool, and he was watching the door when Tamsin came in. His eyes were the surprise. Everything else about him had gone to paper and ash — the hands spotted, the cheeks hollowed, the once-powerful frame folded down into the chair like a tent whose poles had been quietly removed — but the eyes were still working. Grey, level, and quick. They went over Tamsin's face the way a jeweler's loupe goes over a stone.

"You're young," he said. His voice was gravel poured slow.

"I'm competent," Tamsin said. "The youth is temporary."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. The ghost of a man who had smiled, once, and remembered the shape of it. "Sit down, clockmaker."

There was only one other chair, across the small iron stove, and Tamsin took it. Between them the fire ticked and settled. Above their heads, muffled by floorboards and plaster, the master clock ticked its dishonest sixty-one.

"You've been up there three days," Harrow said.

"Yes, sir."

"And?"

And. He wanted it plainly, then. No guild courtesies, no apprentice's bowing deference. Tamsin weighed the answer for the length of two ticks and gave it true.

"The escapement has been altered. Someone has filed the escape wheel teeth and reset the near-side pallet. The clock runs slow — eleven seconds in the hour, give or take, drifting worse in damp weather. It's been done recently, within the year, and it was done by someone who understood exactly what they were doing, because they did it in such a way that a casual inspection would read as wear."

The old man did not move. The fire popped. Somewhere below, a door closed — Edmund's tread, heavy and even, crossing the hall toward the study.

"Do you know," Harrow said at last, "what my son would say if he heard you say that?"

"No, sir."

"He'd say the clock is sixty years old and old mechanisms drift, and that a half-trained foreigner with grease on their cuffs doesn't know a worn pallet from a filed one." He said it without heat, the way a man reads out a list of items he has already counted. "He'd be wrong about the mechanism. He may or may not be wrong about you."

"I'm not foreign," Tamsin said. "I'm from Ashford. My mother was from Nagasaki. I was born four miles from your tollgate."

Harrow's eyebrows rose a fraction — the first genuine expression Tamsin had seen on him. "So the coat is borrowed too."

Tamsin's stomach dropped. They had been careful. Master Aldous Vane's coat, cut for a broader shoulder, sleeves let down; Vane's card in the breast pocket; Vane's name signed in the visitor's ledger in a fair imitation of a hand Tamsin had watched sign invoices for six years. Three days of careful silence, and this dying man had unwound it in an afternoon.

"Mr. Vane is ill," Tamsin said. Each word placed like a component into a tight fit. "The letter asked for him. If I had come as myself, the letter would have been answered by no one. Your clock would still be lying to you."

"Is it lying to me?"

"That depends, sir, on what you want it to say."

For a long moment the old man looked at them, and Tamsin had the distinct, vertiginous sense of being measured — not for skill, not for honesty, but for something harder to specify. Weight-bearing capacity. What load could be hung on this person before the beam failed.

"Mrs. Pell tells me you eat in the kitchen," Harrow said. "That you thanked her for the stew as though she'd given you back a limb. That you wind every clock in this house whether it's on your list or not, because, you told her, a stopped clock in a house is a small death." He coughed then, briefly, terribly, and waved off the concern before Tamsin had fully risen from the chair. "She also tells me you sleep four hours and work the rest, and that you have not once asked what the repair pays."

"It hasn't occurred to me that there would be pay."

"Because Vane will take it."

"Because I am not here as myself," Tamsin said. "Whatever the work earns belongs to the name that did it."

Harrow studied them. Then, slowly, with the care of a man dismantling something fragile, he reached beneath the lapel of his dressing gown and drew out a cord, and on the cord a key.

It was brass, old brass gone the color of honey held to lamplight, and it was unlike any key Tamsin had ever seen — not barrel-shaped, not skeleton-cut, but flat and rectangular, stamped along its shank with a row of tiny numerals too fine to read at a distance. It hung against his knuckles as he held it out, turning slightly, catching the grey window light.

"What comes after," Harrow said, "is going to be ugly. I want to tell you that plainly, because everyone in this house will tell you otherwise right up until it arrives. There is a will. There are two wills. There may be, by the time I'm done, a third. And the difference between them will come down to hours — to what hour I signed, and what hour I died, and whether any witness can swear to either." His hand trembled; the key shivered on its cord like a plucked string. "I built this observatory to keep honest time. Not for astronomy. Astronomy was the excuse. I built it because I grew up poor in a town where the mill owner set the factory clocks fast by twenty minutes and stole forty minutes' wages from every soul in the parish, and no one could prove it, because *he owned the clocks*. Time is power, clockmaker. Whoever keeps the time keeps everything else."

Above them, faintly, the master clock completed another false minute.

"I am dying on a schedule I did not choose," Harrow went on. "I know it the way you know weather coming off the river. Days, perhaps. A week if I'm stubborn, and I am stubborn. When I go, there will be men in this house — lawyers, magistrates, my own blood — and every one of them will want the record to say what suits him. The clock is the only witness in this building that cannot be shouted down. Provided," and here his voice roughened, "provided someone makes it stop lying first."

"You believe the tampering is connected to the will."

"I believe nothing. Belief is for church. I *know* my clock ran true for fifty-nine years and began running slow fourteen months ago, and I know what changed in this house fourteen months ago." He did not elaborate. Tamsin did not ask. Some doors you do not knock on until invited.

"Why me?" Tamsin said. "You have a son. You have lawyers. You could send to London for a firm of clockmakers with letters after their names."

"London would take a week to answer and a month to arrive, and every man it sent would be a stranger who could be bought, delayed, or drowned in the flood season. You are already inside my walls. You found the lie in three days when I am told the best men in the county missed it in fifty years of servicing." He leaned forward, and the effort cost him visibly — Tamsin saw the tendons stand in his neck, saw the breath catch and recover. "And because you signed another man's name to get through my gate and then told me the truth the moment I asked. That is the exact proportion of cunning and honesty I require. Don't look so surprised. I've run an estate for forty years. I know how rare that alloy is."

He held out the key.

Tamsin did not take it. Not yet. "What does it open?"

"A drawer in the base of the regulator case. Under the movement. You'll have noticed the case sits proud of the wall by more than its works require." A dry sound that might once have been a chuckle. "I designed that clock myself, thirty years ago, with help from a man in Geneva who thought he was designing an astronomical regulator and not a witness. There is a compartment. Inside it there is a register — brass discs, punched automatically whenever the rate deviates beyond tolerance. Every drift, every tampering, dated by the machine itself. It cannot be forged, because it was never written by a hand. It was written by the deviation."

Tamsin stared at him. The scale of it settled onto their shoulders slowly, like snow. A clock that testified. A dying man who had spent thirty years preparing for a betrayal he could not yet name, and who had chosen, out of everyone alive, an apprentice in a borrowed coat to finish the work.

"If I open that drawer," Tamsin said carefully, "and the register shows what you think it shows — then someone in this house committed a crime, and I will be the one holding the proof."

"Yes."

"And whoever it is will know I hold it."

"Yes."

"They might be standing behind me when I find out."

The old man's eyes glinted. "Almost certainly."

Tamsin looked at the key turning on its cord between his spotted fingers, and felt the full weight of it before it had even touched their palm — heavier than brass, heavier than the master clock, heavier than the tower itself. This was what trust looked like when a powerful man spent it: he did not give you comfort. He gave you danger and called it confidence.

"Mr. Harrow," Tamsin said. "Why should I? I owe you nothing. I came here under a false name to fix a mechanism, and fixing it honestly is the whole of my commission. Whatever your family does with wills and hours is—" they searched for the phrase the guild masters used, "—outside the movement."

"Outside the movement." He tasted the words. "Everything is inside the movement. That's the first thing they teach and the last thing apprentices learn. A clock stands in a room. The room stands in a house. The house stands in a town, and the town stands on the lies of whoever owns the clock. You cannot repair a mechanism without taking a position on the world it measures." He drew a breath that whistled faintly. "But if you want a reason nearer to hand: you want to be a clockmaker. Truly one, known as one, under your own name. Am I wrong?"

Tamsin said nothing. The silence admitted everything.

"When this is over — however it ends — I can make that true. Or my estate can. One word in the right ear and the guild's door opens for Tamsin Ishikawa instead of staying shut for Vane's shadow." He paused. "Yes, I know your name. Mrs. Pell knows your name. She has known since your second night, when you talked in your sleep in two languages. She hasn't told a soul, and neither shall I. But understand what I am offering. Not payment. *Standing.* The thing you crossed a county in a stolen coat to reach."

The fire had burned low. Outside the window, the river showed through the trees, brown and swollen, carrying whole branches downstream like straws. Somewhere far off, a work gang on the bridge was hammering — the sound arriving thin and late across the water, the sound of a road closing.

Tamsin put out their hand.

The key was warm from his skin. Lighter than it had any right to be. The fine-stamped numerals along the shank resolved as they turned it over: a serial, a date — 1861 — and a maker's mark they did not recognize, a circle with a single vertical stroke through it, like an eye with a closed lid.

"For what comes after," Harrow said. It was not a blessing. It was an instruction.

"How long," Tamsin asked, "has it been since you wound the register yourself?"

"Fourteen months." His gaze went up, through the floorboards, to the ticking overhead. "I stopped winding it the week it started running slow. I wanted to see who would climb up and notice." His mouth did that almost-smile again. "Three days. Vane's reputation travels faster than Vane, apparently."

Tamsin rose, the key closed in their fist, its teeth pressing a small cold alphabet into their palm. At the door they paused.

"Sir — if I open that drawer tonight, and the register names someone in this house — what do you want me to do with it?"

Aurelius Harrow looked at them for a long moment, and for the first time the strength went out of his eyes entirely, and Tamsin saw underneath: a tired man, alone at the top of a tower full of liars, betting his last weeks on a stranger.

"Keep it true," he said. "That's all. Keep the time true, and keep yourself safe doing it, and when the hour comes, let the machine speak. I've said my piece in the wills. The clock can say the rest."

Tamsin went out. The stair turned twice around the tower's throat, and with every step the ticking overhead grew louder, until, passing the clock room door, they stopped — key in hand — and listened to the sixty-first tick fall, soft and regular as a heartbeat, patient as a liar.

Below, faint through the floors, Edmund Harrow's study door opened and closed.

Tamsin climbed the last steps and let themselves into the clock room, and locked the door behind them, and knelt at the base of the regulator case with the honey-colored key held up to the grey afternoon light — and froze.

The panel beneath the movement was already ajar.

Someone had opened the drawer. Recently. The dust along its lip was broken by a clean crescent — the print of a fingertip, pressed there in haste, pointing inward like a compass needle.
