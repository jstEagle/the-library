## 6. Edmund's Ledger

The study of Harrow Observatory smelled of lamp oil and old paper and, beneath both, the river — a damp mineral breath that came up through the floorboards no matter how high the fire was stoked. Edmund Harrow had sat at his father's desk for eleven years without ever once being invited to sit there, and tonight, with the old man sleeping under laudanum two floors below, the chair received him like it had always known him.

He opened the ledger with the reverence other men reserved for scripture.

The accounts were worse than he'd feared. He turned pages by lamplight, his fountain pen hovering, making small marks in the margin — a dash here, a circled figure there — the way a surgeon might chalk a body before cutting. The observatory's income had been declining for a decade: subscriptions from the Astronomical Society lapsed, the meteorological contracts lost to the new station at Ashcombe, the farmland let go fallow because his father would not countenance tenants trampling "his" meadows. His father had spent thirty years building an instrument pointed at the sky while the ground beneath it quietly rotted.

And now he meant to leave all of it — the tower, the instruments, the debts, the name — to Verity.

Edmund set down the pen and pressed his fingertips against his closed eyes until he saw constellations.

It was not jealousy. He wanted that understood, if only by himself, in the privacy of his own skull. Jealousy was a small man's disease, and Edmund Harrow was not a small man. What he felt was closer to what a ship's officer feels watching a drunkard take the wheel: a cold, clarifying horror, and the immediate assumption of responsibility. Someone had to take the helm. The universe did not care whether that someone had been formally appointed.

Verity.

He said the name silently, tasting its absurdity. Verity, who was four-and-twenty and had never in her life balanced so much as a household book. Verity, who spent her days in the gallery with her star charts and her father's old telescopes, cataloguing things no one would ever buy. Verity, who had looked at him across the supper table last Tuesday — across the soup, across Mrs. Pell's good bread, across twenty years of sibling silence — and said, in that flat, level voice she'd inherited from their mother, *You know Father means it, Edmund.*

He did know. That was the intolerable part. Their father had never been more lucid than in these final months; the illness had burned away everything soft and left only the iron will, and the iron will had bent itself, deliberately, vindictively, toward this one act. Two rewrites of the will already. Edmund had seen the drafts — his father kept nothing from him except, apparently, sense — and the second draft named Verity outright, in language so plain it could not be misread: *to my daughter Verity Harrow, the whole of the estate, real and personal, together with the observatory and its instruments, she to hold the same entire and unmortgaged.*

*Entire.* As though Edmund were a leak in the hull to be caulked out of the inheritance entirely.

He opened the drawer on the left — his father's drawer, the one with the swollen lock that yielded to any firm hand — and took out the draft again. Third iteration, unexecuted, still awaiting witnesses. He read it through once more as though repetition might change the words into something else. It did not. The ink was his father's own hand, steady even now, each letter upright and unforgiving.

There was a clause near the end that Edmund had read perhaps forty times and which still made his jaw tighten:

*I make this disposition knowing my son Edmund considers it unjust. I do not consider it unjust. I consider it just. The estate requires a keeper, not an owner.*

*A keeper, not an owner.*

His father had a talent for the knife. He always had. Even dying, even dosed to the gills on tincture of opium, he could find the exact phrase that would keep Edmund awake at three in the morning, staring at the ceiling, composing rebuttals to a man who was not there to hear them.

Edmund returned the draft to the drawer and locked nothing, because locking would be an admission that something needed locking.

He poured himself a measure of the port from the decanter — his father's port, another thing he consumed without invitation — and carried it to the window. Below, the river had come up over the lower meadow, a vast pewter sheet stitched with rain. The bridge lamps made broken yellow lines across the water. Somewhere out there, the tollgate bell rang the half hour, thin and drowned-sounding.

Three weeks, the ferryman had said. Three weeks before the water dropped enough for the bridge to bear a carriage. Perhaps longer, if the rains held.

Edmund watched the flood and felt something in him settle, the way a mechanism settles when the last gear finds its tooth.

* * *

He had not planned to visit the clock room. That was what he told himself later, in the years afterward, whenever the memory surfaced: *I had not planned it.* The truth was less comfortable and more precise — he had been drifting toward the clock room for days the way water drifts toward a low place, finding the gradient without deciding to.

The master clock lived in a stone chamber at the base of the tower, a room built like a vault because timekeeping, his father always said, was a moral discipline and deserved architecture to match. The clock itself dominated the space: a great weight-driven regulator, nine feet of mahogany case and brass movement, its pendulum swinging behind glass with the patience of something that had outlived every argument ever conducted beneath it.

Edmund stood in the doorway with his candle and listened.

The tick was wrong.

He was not a clockmaker. He was a man of accounts and ledgers, of columns that added or did not add, and he knew almost nothing of escapements and pallets and trains. But he had grown up in this house, and the clock's voice had been the metronome of his childhood — the sound against which every supper, every scolding, every Christmas had been measured — and he knew its rhythm the way a man knows his own pulse. And the pulse had changed. There was a hesitation in it now, a fractional drag between the fourth tick and the fifth, as though the machine were thinking twice about something.

Or as though someone had taught it to think twice.

He crossed the room and set his candle on the workbench, beside the stranger's tools.

The clockmaker — the apprentice, rather, the young person his father had summoned from Marlow's Bend with that imperious note of his — slept upstairs in the blue room. Tamsin. A strange name, a foreign name, though the face was ordinary enough under the cap and the borrowed coat two sizes too large. Edmund had shaken the offered hand at the door and found it small and hard as a bird's claw, and had thought: *my father has sent for a child to fix the one thing in this house that still works.*

But the child had been up in the tower for six days now, and the clock still dragged.

Unless.

Unless the dragging was not a fault to be fixed but a condition to be maintained.

Edmund was not a superstitious man, but he was a thorough one, and thoroughness required him to consider the possibility that the timing of his father's death — the *recorded* timing, the legal timing, the timing that would appear in the parish register and before the magistrate and in the reading of whatever will happened to stand — might matter very much indeed. His father's solicitor had been explicit, in that dry way solicitors had, during the drafting of the second will: the document must be executed and witnessed while the testator possessed testamentary capacity, and capacity, at the end, was a matter of hours. Days, even. An execution at the wrong hour could be challenged. A death at the wrong hour could unravel everything signed before it.

And who recorded the hour? This machine. This great brass heart in its stone vault, ticking out the official truth of Harrow Observatory to the tollgate, the church, the river traffic, the world.

Edmund looked at the pendulum through the glass. It swung, and swung, indifferent to him, keeping whatever time it had been taught.

He thought of Verity's face at supper. *You know Father means it.*

He thought of the estate accounts, the red margins, the falling subscriptions. He thought of himself at fifty, landless, dependent on a sister's charity, reduced to being *a keeper's brother* in the house their father had built. He thought of the word *entire*, and how thoroughly it excluded him, and how legally, cleanly, irrevocably it would exclude him once witnessed.

The candle guttered. Rain worked at the high windows.

Here is what Edmund Harrow told himself, standing in that room, and here is what he believed with the whole force of a conscience that had been carefully educated for exactly this moment: *I am not stealing anything. I am correcting an error. My father is ill; his judgment is disordered; the will is a symptom of the illness, like the wasting and the tremor. To let it stand would be a dereliction of duty — duty to the estate, to the name, to the generations of Harrows who built this tower before Verity was ever imagined. If the law can be made to see the truth of the matter, then the law is not being cheated. It is being helped.*

Cold ambition dressed as duty fits well and wears long. Edmund buttoned it on and felt warm.

He examined the clock's face first — the great enamel dial with its black Roman numerals, the hands that drove the tower dials above through rods and gearing. Then, with fingers that did not shake (he noted this with satisfaction; his hands never shook), he opened the case's side panel, the one the apprentice left unlatched during the day's work.

The movement breathed out its smell of oil and brass and dust. Wheels within wheels, gleaming faintly in the candlelight, the escapement at the top like a small silver crown.

He did not touch it. Not yet. Not quite yet. He stood there a long while, listening to the drag in the tick, learning it, letting his eye memorize the geometry of pallets and teeth so that tomorrow night — or the night after — he would know instantly if anything had been moved.

Because that was the thought that arrived last, quiet and complete, the way his best thoughts always arrived: *the apprentice will find it.* Any competent repairer, opening this movement, would find whatever was done to it. Which meant whatever was done must look like wear. Like age. Like the honest decay of an honest machine. It must be slow, patient, deniable — filed just enough, shifted just enough, so that when the clock finally lied, it would lie plausibly, the way old clocks do.

And it must be done soon. Before the will was witnessed. Before the end.

Edmund closed the panel, took up his candle, and paused at the door for one last look at the pendulum, still swinging behind its glass, faithful to whatever it had been told.

"I'm sorry," he said aloud, to the machine, because the machine was the only witness available and some part of him — the part he kept in a locked drawer of his own — wanted the words spoken somewhere, anywhere, even here.

Then he climbed the stairs, past the blue room where the apprentice slept behind a door he'd noted was not locked, past Verity's darkened room, up to his own bed, where he lay awake until dawn composing the speech he would give someday, to someone, explaining why everything he had done had been necessary.

* * *

In the morning, Edmund was downstairs early, shaved and correct, and found Mrs. Pell laying the breakfast things with the brisk tenderness she extended to everyone in that house including, apparently, himself.

"The master woke in the night," she said, not looking up from the spoons. "Asked for Miss Verity. Asked after the clock, too."

"After the clock?"

"Wanted to know if it was keeping true time." She set down the last spoon and met his eyes, and for a moment her plain, flour-dusted face held something he couldn't read. "Mr. Ishikawa's been up there since six. Says the movement wants taking apart proper. Every wheel of it."

Edmund felt the words go through him like a blade finding the gap between ribs — *every wheel of it* — and he smiled, easily, the smile of a man with nothing in his pockets but lint.

"Well," he said, buttering his toast with a steady hand. "Let's hope the young person knows their trade."

Upstairs, faintly, through floors and stone, he could hear it: the tick, the drag, the tick — counting down toward something only the machine seemed to understand.
