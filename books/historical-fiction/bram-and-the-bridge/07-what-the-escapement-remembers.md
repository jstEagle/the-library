## 7. What the Escapement Remembers

The clock room sat at the top of the tower like a heart in a chest of stone, and Tamsin had learned its rhythm the way one learns a patient's breathing — by lying awake at night listening for what was wrong.

Three mornings after their arrival, with the rain writing its long gray sentence against the eastern windows, they carried the movement down from the great frame a piece at a time, laying each component on the baize cloth Mrs. Pell had provided with the solemnity of an offering. The anchor escapement came last, cradled in both hands. It weighed almost nothing. That was the thing about truth, Tamsin thought, setting it down: it was always lighter than you expected, small enough to hold, small enough to hide.

Master Aldous would have said *begin with the beat*. Every clock had a heartbeat, and every heartbeat told you where to listen.

Tamsin lit both lamps, pulled the chair to the bench, and picked up the loupe.

* * *

The first hour passed in silence but for rain and the far-off argument of gulls driven inland by the floods. Tamsin worked the way they had been taught — which was to say slowly, which was to say reverently — inspecting each tooth of each wheel under magnification, running a fingernail along the escape wheel's teeth the way a blind man reads.

It was on the fourth wheel that the fingernail caught.

A hitch so slight most eyes would have called it imagination. Tamsin went back. Caught again. They adjusted the lamp, angled the wheel into the light, and looked.

Filed.

Not broken — filed. One tooth in twelve, ground down by perhaps the width of a hair, so smooth and so deliberate that the work could only have been done with the wheel removed from the frame, done at leisure, done by someone who knew exactly what they were about. The finish on the file marks had been stoned away afterward. Polished. Someone had wanted this to look like wear, like age, like honest failure.

But clocks did not fail dishonestly or honestly in this manner; metal wore where it worked, and this tooth sat in the one position on the wheel that barely worked at all. Wear went where the labor went. This was elsewhere. This was *placed*.

Tamsin sat back. Their own heartbeat seemed suddenly loud, an intruder in the room.

They lifted the next wheel. Found nothing. The next — nothing. Then the escape wheel itself, the crown of the whole mechanism, the part that doled out time in equal measures like a miser counting coins, and there Tamsin stopped breathing altogether.

Two teeth adjacent, shortened. And the pallet stone of the anchor — the little ruby-hard lip that caught and released each tooth — had been shifted in its setting. Not much. A degree, perhaps less. Enough. With the pallet shifted and the teeth filed, the escapement's beat stretched: tick . . . drag . . . tick. Each cycle stealing a fraction of a second, and fractions compounding as fractions did, minute upon minute upon hour, until the clock fell behind not wildly, not obviously — a clock that lost time dramatically announced itself, invited correction — but gently. Respectably. By minutes a day. By an hour in a fortnight.

An hour.

The word landed in Tamsin's chest like a dropped weight.

Anyone who watched this clock believed it. Anyone who consulted it for any matter of record — a signing, a death, the legal hour at which anything occurred in this house — consulted a lie. And no one would know, because the lie moved slowly, politely, wearing the respectable face of ordinary mechanical decay.

Tamsin set down the loupe very carefully, because their hands wanted to shake and clockmakers' hands were not permitted to shake. That was the first law of the trade, taught before files, before theory: *your hands belong to the work*. So they made them still, and in making them still, made everything else still too, and looked at what they knew.

Someone had come into this room — more than once, for the stoning alone spoke of patience — and bent over this movement with a file and a whetstone and all the time in the world, and taught the master clock of Harrow Observatory to lie about time itself.

* * *

They should have gone downstairs at once. They understood that even then, sitting in the lamplit circle with the saboteur's work spread before them like a confession. Whatever else was true, this was no longer a repair. A failing clock was weather; this was intent. Intent meant a person, and a person meant a name, and every name in this house had already been spoken to them in the tones people used when arranging furniture: Edmund, coldly courteous. Verity, watchful as a cat at a mousehole. Old Mr. Harrow, dying by inches upstairs. Mrs. Pell, kind in the way that made Tamsin's throat ache with homesickness for kindnesses never received.

And old Mr. Harrow, who had watched them climb past his door that first day with those pale unblinking eyes, saying nothing about the extra tick. Had he heard it too? A man does not keep an observatory for forty years without knowing his own clock's voice better than his children's.

Instead of going down, Tamsin did what apprentices are trained to do and masters are paid for: they gathered evidence.

They took paper and pencil from the bench drawer and drew the escape wheel, marking the filed teeth by position — third and fourth from the locking notch. They measured the pallet displacement with a watchmaker's rule and recorded it to the sixty-fourth of an inch. They noted the direction of the shift, which mattered enormously, because direction told you the saboteur's intention: shifted so, the clock ran slow; shifted the other way, fast. Slow was chosen. Slow was deliberate among all possible deliberates.

Then they did the arithmetic, because arithmetic was the one language in which no one had ever been able to make them lie.

The clock had been reported failing roughly a month prior — Mrs. Pell had mentioned it, the letter had confirmed it. At the measured rate of loss, rewound daily to the frame's full lift, the mechanism would have accumulated its first full hour of error approximately eleven days ago.

Eleven days ago. What had happened eleven days ago in this house? Nothing that Tamsin knew of. But something had been *planned* for eleven days hence, or twenty, or thirty — something whose recorded hour mattered enough to file steel for.

Or — and here the dread arrived, cold and complete, settling into the bones the way floodwater finds a cellar — something had already happened once at a disputed hour, and the sabotage existed to make the dispute permanent. To make the machine itself swear to the wrong time.

Tamsin looked at the escapement lying innocently on the baize, and understood, with the particular clarity of their trade, that they were not looking at a mechanism at all anymore. They were looking at testimony. Tampered testimony. Perjury in brass and steel, committed by someone with access, skill or money enough to buy it, and patience.

Access. That was the word that closed the fist. The clock room stood at the top of forty-one steps behind a door that Mrs. Pell locked nightly and old Mr. Harrow — or whoever held his keys — kept otherwise. No stranger had climbed those stairs. Whoever had done this slept under this roof, ate at this table, passed the salt.

* * *

The light had gone from gray to pewter by the time Tamsin surfaced from the drawings. Down below, the house sounded its afternoon noises: a door, Mrs. Pell's voice raised in some kitchen negotiation, the dogs barking at the river. Tamsin reassembled what they dared — left the escapement itself apart, claiming to themselves that it needed cleaning, which was even true — and went down to wash the tower off their hands and decide what a person did with a thing like this.

They met Verity on the second landing.

She stood at the window that faced the bridge, arms crossed, and she did not move aside — not rudely, but in the manner of someone establishing that the landing belonged to her and visitors passed through by grace. Rain silvered her dark hair at the temples. Behind her, through the wet glass, the river ran brown and swollen, dragging whole trees toward the sea like a giant clearing its throat.

"You've had the movement apart all day," she said. Not a question.

"The beat was wrong. I needed to see why."

"And? Why is it?"

The truthful answer stood in Tamsin's pocket, folded into a page of pencil drawings. "Wear," they said. "Age. Some parts will need replacing."

Verity studied them the way she studied everything — as if reading a document for the forged signature. Her father's eyes, Mrs. Pell said; everyone said. "My father believes that clock keeps perfect time," she said at last. "He built half his life around it. Observations, records, the almanac correspondence." A pause, precise as a knife laid flat. "If it's failing, I'd want to know precisely how. Precision matters in this house."

"Then he'll have my report."

"Yes," said Verity. "I imagine he will."

She stepped aside then, and let them pass, and Tamsin descended the rest of the stairs feeling her attention on their back like a hand between the shoulder blades — steady, curious, and not remotely warm.

* * *

Mrs. Pell found them at dusk, in the narrow room they'd been given off the instrument gallery, with a tray of bread, cold chicken, and the small pickled things she seemed to believe kept strangers alive. She set it down, glanced at the drawings spread on the desk — Tamsin had forgotten to put them away, and cursed themselves silently — and paused.

"That's the escape wheel," she said.

Tamsin looked up. Mrs. Pell stood with her hands folded in her apron, her round face unreadable for once, firelight catching the gray at her temples.

"Thirty years in this house," she said. "I've carried the cleaning rags up those stairs since before you were born, miss—since before anyone now living was born, near enough. I know what the parts are called if not what they do." Her eyes went to the pencil marks, the two small crosses Tamsin had drawn beside the filed teeth. "Those crosses mean something's wrong where it shouldn't be."

"It means the teeth are damaged," Tamsin said carefully.

"Damaged." Mrs. Pell turned the word over like a coin she suspected. "You mean worn."

"I mean cut," Tamsin said. And then, because the lie sat in their mouth like a swallowed pin, because this woman had brought them stew on their first night and had not once looked at their face the way the town below looked at faces it couldn't sort: "Filed. On purpose. The pallet's been shifted too. Someone has made your master's clock run slow, Mrs. Pell — deliberately, skillfully, and by just enough that no one would ever catch it without taking the whole movement apart."

The fire popped. Somewhere below a door closed; the house settled.

Mrs. Pell sat down slowly on the edge of the narrow bed, as though her knees had submitted a request her dignity had denied until now. She looked at her hands in her apron.

"Eleven days," she said.

Tamsin went cold. "What?"

"How long since the error began, by your figuring?" She looked up, and her eyes were terribly clear. "I do sums too. Kitchen sums, but sums. Eleven days back was the Thursday the lawyer came. The Thursday Mr. Harrow sent for Mr. Crane and changed his will again." She rose, smoothed her apron with both hands, and became briskly, deliberately practical, the way women of her station became when the alternative was screaming. "You'll want hot water and clean linen for the parts. I'll bring them up after the family eats. And you'll say nothing of this to Mr. Edmund or Miss Verity — not yet. Promise me that."

"Why not?"

"Because in this house," Mrs. Pell said, pausing at the door, "the ones who talk first are the ones who get talked about. Learn that and you'll live long." Then she was gone, her footsteps fading down the gallery stairs, leaving Tamsin alone with the tray, the fire, and the drawings of a crime.

* * *

That night Tamsin did not sleep.

They lay in the narrow bed listening to the tower above them — for the clock's voice reached everywhere in this building, through stone and timber, the way a pulse reaches the wrist — and counted the corrupted beat. Tick. Drag. Tick. Each stolen fraction falling into the dark like a coin into a well.

Someone in this house had bent over steel with a file and taught time to perjure itself. Someone had done it for a reason rooted in wills and inheritances and the exact hour at which a dying man's pen had touched paper — or would touch it. And now Tamsin Ishikawa, who had climbed these stairs under a borrowed coat and a borrowed name, carrying skills the guild refused to certify and a face the town refused to read correctly, held the only proof of it in a folder of pencil drawings.

If they repaired the escapement, the clock would tell true time again — and whatever scheme depended on its slowness would collapse, and its author would know within a day who had undone it, and would begin calculating what to do about the foreign apprentice in the tower. If they said nothing and replaced the worn parts quietly, the lie would continue, ticking politely toward whatever hour it had been built to falsify.

And underneath both choices, cold as the river rising in the dark, lay the other question, the one they could not put down: *whose hands had held the file?*

Mrs. Pell's warning circled back. *The ones who talk first.* But Mrs. Pell had known about the lawyer's visit before Tamsin mentioned it. Had summed eleven days without being told the figures. Kitchen sums, she'd said. Perhaps. Or perhaps the household's silences were not walls between its members but a web, and Tamsin had just felt three separate threads pull tight.

Near midnight, footsteps on the tower stair. Slow, heavy, careful of the treads — Edmund's tread; Tamsin had begun to know them. They passed below the floor, going up, and did not come down again for the better part of an hour.

When they finally descended, the great clock's voice, high overhead, stumbled once — a tick arriving late, a hesitation like a held breath — and then resumed.

Tick. Drag. Tick.

Tamsin lay in the dark with their hands outside the blanket, palms up, the way they'd been taught to sleep so that the calluses would form evenly, and made themselves a clockmaker's promise, quiet and absolute:

*I will find out what hour you were made to lie about. And then I will decide whose side the truth serves.*

Above them, patient as sin, the clock went on counting toward something only the machine seemed to understand.
