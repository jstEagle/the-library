## 8. Mrs. Pell's Kitchen

The kitchen at Harrow Observatory sat in the tower's wide skirt like a warm heart in a cold chest, and Mrs. Pell governed it the way she governed everything else under that roof—quietly, thoroughly, and with a wooden spoon that had settled more disputes than any magistrate.

She had noticed the apprentice on the first evening. Of course she had. Forty years in other people's houses taught a woman to read faces the way sailors read weather, and this one was a weather all its own: the high flat cheekbones, the eyes dark as river water, the skin between Mr. Ishikawa's coloring and something else besides. The letter had said *my apprentice, Tamsin Ishikawa*, in the old clockmaker's cramped hand, and the person who arrived wearing his coat and his name did not entirely match either.

Mrs. Pell had said nothing. She had hung the coat by the fire to dry, set a bowl of stew before the child—for apprentice or no, anyone young enough to be sent alone up a mountain in October rain was a child to her—and watched them eat the way hungry people eat when they are afraid the food might be taken back.

Now, four days into the rain, she found them again at her door, drawn downstairs the way cold things drift toward heat.

"The master's sleeping," Tamsin said. Their English was careful, each word set down like a gear into its seat. "I thought—I could check the strike train again, but I've checked it twice, and Mrs. Pell, I thought perhaps—" They stopped. Asking for things was plainly not a skill this one had been permitted to practice.

"Perhaps you'd rather sit where it's warm than stand where it's cold," Mrs. Pell finished for them. "Yes. Sit. That stool by the oven. Mind the flour, it's fresh."

They sat. They always sat where they were told, precisely, as though stools were instruments and there might be an examination after. Mrs. Pell put bread on the board—still warm from the coals, split and buttered because butter made truth come easier—and a smaller bowl of stew than she would have given Edmund or Verity, who left food, and more than either of them knew, because a hungry apprentice ate slowly and stayed.

Outside, the rain kept up its argument with the windows. Down in Marlow's Bend the river was rising; she'd heard it in the tone of the tollgate bell that morning, lower and lazier than usual, the way bells went when their air was thick with water. Three days, maybe four, and the bridge would close. She didn't say so yet. There was a particular expression people got when you told them they were trapped, and she had decided she didn't want to watch this one wear it.

"You've been up in the clock room all week," she said instead, ladling herself a bowl and sitting opposite. "What do you find?"

Tamsin's hands stilled on the bread. "Wear," they said. "And something else."

"Something else." She nodded slowly, as if this confirmed a suspicion rather than opened a wound. "I thought as much."

"You thought—"

"That clock has been wrong since summer. Not broken-wrong. *Opinionated*-wrong." She tore her bread. "I've kept this house twenty-six years, Tamsin Ishikawa. I know the sound of every floorboard and every bell in it, and I know when a machine is lying, because machines in this house learned it from somebody."

The apprentice looked up sharply—a flash of something quick and bright under all that trained stillness. "You believe someone has tampered with it."

"I believe nothing yet. Believing is for churches." Mrs. Pell sipped her tea. "But I'll tell you what I *know*, if you've the stomach for house gossip, and seeing as you're eating my bread, you'll listen politely whether you have the stomach or not."

* * *

The kitchen was hers, and had been since before either of the Harrow children could walk. She had come up the mountain in the year of the great ice, a young widow with quick hands and no last name worth pronouncing, and she had never left—not through the mistress's illness, nor the mistress's death, nor the slow closing of the house like a hand making a fist.

"Marianne Harrow," Mrs. Pell said, and the name came out of her mouth soft, the way names do when they've been carried too long without speaking. "Their mother. Died eleven years ago this month, in the blue room upstairs, with the river fog so thick you couldn't see the bridge from the window."

Tamsin set down their spoon. "Edmund's mother."

"Verity's too, though you'd not know it from how that girl speaks of her. Or doesn't." Mrs. Pell looked into the middle distance, which for her meant looking at the past directly. "Marianne Ashdown she was, before. A merchant's daughter from downriver, pretty and sharp as a new chisel, and old Mr. Harrow courted her with clocks—did you know that? Gave her a little enameled carriage watch instead of flowers. Kept it on her dressing table till the day she died. Afterward it went missing, and nobody in this house ever said one word about where."

"Verity has it," Tamsin said quietly.

Mrs. Pell looked at them properly then, and smiled, and the smile changed her whole face the way sun changes a north wall. "There's hope for you yet, watching like that. Yes. Verity has it. Wears it under her bodice where Edmund can't see, which is the only place in this house anything true gets kept."

She rose, stirred the stew she wasn't eating, and came back to her chair. Some stories needed a body in motion to get out.

"Marianne was the only person in this household who ever stood up to Mr. Harrow, and he loved her the more for it, which tells you most of what you need to know about him. She wanted the estate divided—evenly, she said, but that wasn't quite the truth of what she wanted. What she wanted was for Verity to have the observatory itself. The clocks, the instruments, the star-charts, all of it. Because Verity was the one who loved it, even at six years old, even before her eyes were tall enough to see over the transit telescope. And Edmund was the one who loved *owning* things, and Marianne could tell the difference, being a merchant's daughter."

"And Mr. Harrow?"

"Mr. Harrow said yes to his wife the way he said yes to everything she said, which is to say immediately and then spent ten years finding reasons why he hadn't." Mrs. Pell sighed. "So. First will: everything to Edmund, because that was tradition and tradition is cheaper than thinking. Then Maranne sickens, and he writes a second will—Verity gets the observatory and its endowment, Edmund gets the land and the mills and the town houses. Fair as fair, everyone furious, which is usually the mark of fairness done properly."

"What happened to it?"

"It sat in the solicitor's office in town, and Mr. Harrow sat in his chair, and the years went by, and Edmund got married and buried a wife inside eighteen months and came back sour as vinegar, and Verity grew up into a woman who watches doors." Mrs. Pell turned her cup once on its saucer, a full slow circle. "And then this spring, Mr. Harrow calls for paper and ink and writes a *third*. And he doesn't send it to the solicitor. He keeps it in the house."

Tamsin had gone very still—the particular stillness of a person doing arithmetic quickly. "Where in the house?"

"If I knew that, child, I'd be more than a housekeeper." Which was not quite false and nowhere near true, and Mrs. Pell let the half-truth sit on the table between them like a third bowl nobody touched. "What I know is that he started having visitors sign things in front of witnesses. What I know is that Edmund stopped eating dinner in the same room as his father around midsummer. And what I know—" here she leaned forward, and lowered her voice though there was no one to hear, which is habit in a house with ears in its walls, "—is that the master clock began running wrong within a fortnight of that third paper being signed. You put those three facts in your clever hands and turn them over, since turning things over is evidently what you're for."

* * *

Tamsin ate the rest of the bread without appearing to notice they were doing it, eyes far away, fingers moving faintly against the table edge as though counting escape-wheel teeth. Mrs. Pell watched them with the patience of long practice. There was a thing she had not said, sitting behind her teeth like a cherry stone, and she was not going to say it tonight, and possibly not ever, and she hated herself a little for both halves of that sentence.

Instead she said: "Your master. Mr. Ishikawa. He took you on young?"

The apprentice surfaced. "Twelve years ago. I was—" A pause, the careful kind, where a person checks a word for splinters before using it. "He found me at the docks. In Kōbe, when his ship called there. My mother was Japanese. My father was English, a clerk for the trading company, and he—he did not stay. When my mother died, there was no one. Mr. Ishikawa had known her somehow. He brought me back on the ship as his ward, and apprenticed me when I turned fourteen."

"The guild wouldn't have you."

It was not a question, and Tamsin did not pretend otherwise. "The guild has rules about persons of mixed blood. He appealed twice. He is—" a small, fierce motion of the jaw, "—appealing still. But his hands shake now, so the work falls to me, and the letters go out under his name, and I wear his coat when I answer them. It is simpler."

"Simpler," Mrs. Pell repeated, tasting the word and finding it thin gruel. "Is that what we're calling it now."

"You don't mind." Not asking, exactly, but close. "That I'm—that I look—" The words ran out of gears. It occurred to Mrs. Pell, watching them struggle, that this child had probably never once been asked the question kindly, and so had no practice answering it.

"Tamsin Ishikawa." She said the whole name deliberately, both parts, and watched the apprentice flinch at *Ishikawa* the way people flinch at a blow that has landed many times. "I keep house for a man who is dying, in a house where the mother died unspoken-of and the daughter wears her mother's watch where nobody can see it and the son burns his letters. Do you imagine I have opinions to spare about whose grandmother was born where?" She pushed the bread board across the table. "Eat. You're skin and escapements."

For a moment the child's face did something complicated—something that began as disbelief and ended somewhere dangerously near tears, mastered, barely, by the discipline of someone used to mastering everything. Then Tamsin bowed their head slightly, the way their mother must have taught them before anyone taught them otherwise, and took another piece of bread, and Mrs. Pell looked hard at the fire until her own eyes behaved.

Twenty-six years in service taught you that kindness was expensive. It cost in bread, in coal, in the risk of caring about people who would leave or die or be taken from you by men with ledgers. But it was the only currency worth holding, and she had resolved long ago to spend it while she had hands to spend it with.

* * *

Later, when the stew was gone and the fire banked and Tamsin risen reluctantly from the warm stool, standing at the door with lamplight gilding the edge of that quiet, watchful face—

"Mrs. Pell." Hesitation. "Why are you telling me these things? The wills. The clock. If Mr. Edmund suspected you spoke of it—"

"Edmund suspects everybody of everything; it saves him the trouble of thinking." Mrs. Pell gathered bowls with brisk hands. "I'm telling you because you're the first person to come into this house in eleven years who looks at that clock like it's a witness and not furniture. And because—"

She stopped.

Because her son had come home from the town in June with a new coat and white knuckles and a story about cards that explained neither. Because Tobias couldn't meet her eye at chapel. Because two weeks later the master clock, which she wound and dusted and had known for a quarter century like a heartbeat, began to lie.

Because whatever her boy had done or been made to do was ticking away above their heads this very minute, forty feet of brass and steel counting out falsehoods over a dying man's bed, and Mrs. Pell was too old and too tired and too much a mother to carry it alone anymore—but not yet brave enough, God forgive her, to carry it aloud.

"—and because someone should," she finished. "Now go on. The master wakes at nine for his broth, and Verity prowls the east stair at midnight like a cat that's lost something, and you'll want to be neither here nor there when she does."

Tamsin went, footsteps climbing the spiral stair, fading into the tick and clank of the tower's sleepless heart.

Mrs. Pell stood alone in her warm kitchen with the rain writing its long letter down the black windows, and pressed her flour-dusted hand flat against her breastbone as though she could hold something in.

Three days, maybe four, and the bridge would close.

She looked up, toward the clock room, and whispered to no one—to the house, to the dead woman whose watch ticked invisibly against Verity Harrow's ribs, to her own absent boy—"Whatever's coming, come gentle."

Outside, the river answered, rising.
