## 9. The First Flood Warning

The rain had been falling for three days without conviction—thin, gray, the kind of rain that seemed less like weather than a rumor of weather—and on the fourth morning the river answered it.

Tamsin heard it first through the clock. That was the strange part, the part they would remember afterward: not the sound of the water itself but the way the great regulator in the tower room went suddenly wrong, its pendulum swinging a half-degree out of true as though the whole stone building had flinched. The observatory stood on its knoll above the bend, and the bend was where the Marlow gathered itself before committing to the long fall toward the lowlands, and that morning the gathering had become a roar. The floor hummed with it. The clock felt it in its suspension and complained the only way a clock can complain, by losing four seconds before noon.

Tamsin had learned to read those complaints the way Mrs. Pell read weather in her knees. They were up on the scaffold platform by seven, sleeves rolled to the elbow, cleaning the pivot jewels with a whisk of badger hair, when the shouting started from the far bank.

At first it was only voices, thin and torn apart by wind. Then a horn—a cattle horn or a foghorn, something animal and enormous—and then more voices, many more, all pitched in that particular register that needs no translation in any language: *come look, come now, come quickly.*

They went down the tower stairs two at a time, which Mrs. Pell would have scolded them for, out onto the gravel terrace where the Harrow house ended and the sky began. The rain had stopped an hour before. The clouds were still there, low and bruised, but beneath them the whole valley had changed its mind about what it was.

The river was out of its bed.

Not flooding—not yet—but standing where fields should be, brown and muscular, shouldering against the willows, carrying entire fences downstream like driftwood. And the bridge—

Tamsin's stomach dropped before their eyes finished understanding.

The bridge was gone. Not collapsed—the piers still stood, dark shoulders breaking the current—but the roadway between the second and third spans had been lifted clean away, planks and railing swallowed whole, leaving a gap of white water wide enough that a man could have dropped a house into it and watched it disappear. On the far bank, at the tollgate, a crowd of rivermen had gathered, small as toy figures, and one of them stood on the tollhouse roof with his hands cupped around his mouth.

"—cut off!" The wind delivered it in pieces. "—three weeks if it's a day!—get your people clear of the lower road!"

Edmund Harrow stood at the terrace rail with a spyglass he had not bothered to raise, as though the sight required no magnification, only endurance. He wore his riding coat though there would be no riding anywhere for a month. Verity was beside him, her hands folded very still, and Tamsin had been in the house long enough now—six days, nearly seven—to know that Verity's stillness was not calm. It was the stillness of a person counting.

"Three weeks," Edmund said. He did not make it a question. He made it an arithmetic problem, and Tamsin watched him solve it behind his eyes, watched the answer arrive, watched whatever the answer cost him get filed away in whatever ledger he kept instead of a face.

"Could be longer," Verity said. "Da said 'ninety-three would be a flood year. He marked it in the almanac."

"Our father marks everything in almanacs," Edmund said, and there was something under the words that Tamsin could not name yet—something that was not grief and was not quite fear, but lived next door to both.

Mrs. Pell came out with shawls over her arm, distributing them with the brisk authority of a woman who has decided that catastrophe is best met dressed warmly. She put one around Tamsin's shoulders without asking, and Tamsin let her, because refusing Mrs. Pell was like refusing gravity.

"Rivermen say the ferry chain's snapped at Wetherby," she said quietly. "That's the only other crossing this side of the gorge." She looked out at the broken bridge, and her mouth did something complicated. "My sister lives on the far bank. I'd not seen her since Michaelmas anyway." She said it the way people say such things—as if distance were a habit rather than a sentence.

On the far bank, the man on the tollhouse roof was still shouting, but the wind had turned and took his voice away in rags. A boy ran along the near towpath below the terrace, waving both arms, and Edmund finally lifted the spyglass, read something off the boy's face, and lowered it.

"Lower road's already underwater past the mill," he reported, flat as a bill of lading. "They're advising everyone to keep to high ground." He turned and looked—at the terrace, at the house, at the tower rising behind it with its dome full of instruments and its stairwell full of clocks—and Tamsin saw him perform a small, terrible calculation: *the high ground is us.*

"Very well," Edmund said. "We are an island."

* * *

An island. Tamsin carried the word back up the tower stairs like a stone in their shoe.

They thought about it while they worked. Work was the only language they trusted; their master, old Corvus Ishikawa, who had taken in a starving half-blood child off a ship at Nagasaki and taught them that gears do not lie even when men do—Corvus used to say that whenever Tamsin's thoughts got ahead of their hands, they should slow the hands until the thoughts caught up, or better, give the thoughts a job. So Tamsin gave the thoughts a job: they cleaned the escapement of the transit clock, oiled the great regulator's bearings, wound the subsidiary dials that told the hours to no one, since the tower had no public face and never had. The Harrow Observatory was a private instrument, built by a private obsession. Old Mr. Harrow had spent forty years and most of a fortune making time accurate in one small room in one tall building in one river town, and now he lay upstairs dying in it, and his clock ticked above him like a patient heart, and nobody outside this island would ever know what hour anything happened here until the water went down.

Until spring.

The thought arrived on its own schedule, uninvited, the way the worst ones do. Tamsin set down the oiler and made themselves finish the bearing first, because Corvus's rule cut both ways: if the thoughts outran the hands, you finished the work before you let yourself think. But the work ended, as all work ends, and then there was nothing between Tamsin and the thought at all.

*I am trapped in this house.*

Not merely stranded. Stranded meant inconvenience—meant cold suppers and letters that couldn't go. This was different. Six days ago they had walked across that bridge wearing their master's coat with their master's name sewn into the collar's shadow, carrying forged papers that named them *Aldous Finch, journeyman, licensed by the Guild,* and every step of the crossing had been a lie laid end to end like paving stones, and the plan—the whole architecture of the plan—had rested on one simple fact: the bridge went both ways.

If the repair went well, they crossed home and were nobody again. If the repair went badly—if they were discovered, exposed, asked one question too many about why a licensed journeyman had hands that looked like theirs, a face like theirs, a name they stumbled over—they could walk away. Disgrace was survivable at a distance. Discovery was survivable if there was somewhere to *be* after it.

There was nowhere to be. There was brown water where the road used to be, and three weeks minimum, and Verity's cool gray eyes already resting on them at supper with a weight that felt less like suspicion each day and more like recognition, which was worse.

Tamsin stood at the tower window and looked down at the gap in the bridge where the river ran through like a sentence with its middle missing.

Somewhere south, in Marlow's Bend proper, their master lay coughing in his rooms above the shop, believing his apprentice had gone on a simple errand and would return inside the week. If the guild came asking why Aldous Finch hadn't come back—if anyone came asking—there would be no answer to give until the ferry chains were mended and the spans rebuilt. Whatever happened in this house now would happen entirely inside these walls. Every silence, every discovery, every dangerous thing Tamsin had already begun to suspect about that escapement—all of it sealed in, like air in a jar.

And underneath the fear, shamefully, treacherously, something else. Something that sounded almost like relief.

Because the truth—the truth Tamsin had not said aloud even to themselves—was that the guild doors had been closed to them for twelve years regardless of skill, that the borrowed coat was not a disguise chosen for one errand but a costume worn for a lifetime, and that here, on this island cut off from every eye that might judge them, they had held in their own two hands the finest movement they had ever seen, and repaired it, and been *trusted.* A dying man had watched them work and said nothing and let them stay. In Marlow's Bend they were a secret. Here, for three weeks, they might almost be a person.

Then they remembered the extra tick. Thirteen strokes where the mechanism allowed twelve. The filed metal they had not yet found but had not stopped feeling, the way a tongue returns to a chipped tooth. Someone in this house had bent time itself to some purpose, and Tamsin was sealed inside with them, and the water was rising, and there would be no witnesses coming, no magistrate, no escape, no second chance—only the clock, and whoever touched it next.

* * *

Supper that night was a strange ceremony. Mrs. Pell had outdone herself—there was a ham, and pickled things, and a plum pudding she claimed was left over from Midsummer, which fooled no one and pleased everybody—and the table was laid for five, which was one more than the house contained, and everyone pretended not to notice the empty place at the head.

Old Mr. Harrow no longer came down. His meals went up on a tray. But the chair remained where it had stood for fifty years, and the family ate in its shadow the way sailors eat beneath the portrait of a drowned captain.

"The ferry at Wetherby won't run till the chain's replaced," Edmund said, carving with surgical attention. "That's a fortnight at least, if the smithy on the far side is flooded. Which it will be." He served Verity first, which surprised Tamsin—he was precise in his courtesies, but rarely generous. "We should inventory the cellar. If this runs long, we'll want to know our stores."

"I've done it," Mrs. Pell said. "Six weeks comfortable. Eight if we're careful with the flour."

"Eight weeks." Edmund set down the knife. For just a moment his composure showed a seam. Eight weeks was longer than three; eight weeks pushed past the boundaries of whatever timetable he had built in his head, and Tamsin watched him rebuild the timetable in real time, the little muscles around his eyes working. "Then we are provisioned. Good."

Verity said almost nothing through the meal. She ate neatly, listened completely, and twice Tamsin caught her looking at them—not at their face, which most people managed to avoid doing, but at their *hands*, at the burn scars and the fine steel calluses, with an expression Tamsin could not decode and did not enjoy trying to.

"You've been up the tower all day," she said at last, to Tamsin, directly. It was the first thing she had said to them all evening.

"There's maintenance owing," Tamsin said. "The humidity's hard on the movements. Wood swells, oils thicken."

"And the master clock? Does it keep good time?"

The question was perfectly ordinary. That was what made Tamsin's spine prickle. They had rehearsed ordinary answers all week.

"It keeps excellent time," Tamsin said, which was true, which was exactly the trouble. "For now."

Something passed across Verity's face—too quick to read, gone before it landed—and she returned to her plate, and Edmund glanced up from his ham with the mild expression of a man following a conversation he had already decided the ending of.

"For now," he repeated pleasantly, and raised his glass a fraction, as if toasting the phrase itself.

After supper Tamsin helped Mrs. Pell stack dishes, because it was the kind of thing that couldn't be written into any ledger of debts but somehow needed doing anyway. At the pantry door she stopped them with a hand light on their sleeve.

"You looked at the water today like a man reading his own sentence," she said. Not unkindly. "First flood?"

"First one I haven't been able to walk away from."

Mrs. Pell nodded slowly, as though this were the most reasonable answer in the world. "The river takes the road every few years. Always gives it back." She paused, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "It's what happens on dry land while it's gone, dearie, that folks don't always recover from."

She squeezed their sleeve once and was gone into the kitchen, and Tamsin climbed the tower stairs in the dark with a candle, past the sleeping transit clock, past the regulator with its honest pendulum, up to the room where the master clock stood against the wall like a witness under oath.

They opened the case. They lit a second candle and set it close. And they began, methodically, with the dread sitting warm and heavy in their chest like swallowed tea, to take the escapement apart.

The pallet stones were wrong. They had suspected it for days, suspected and refused to confirm it, and now there was no refusing. One of the locking faces had been filed—filed carefully, expertly, by someone who understood exactly how much metal to remove to make a clock lose time slowly, gracefully, deniably. Minutes a day. Hours a week. Enough to shift any recorded moment by a margin no casual observer would ever catch.

Someone in this house wanted the clock to lie.

Tamsin sat back on their heels in the candlelight and listened to the river roaring through the gap where the bridge had been, and understood, with the clarity of cold water, that they were locked in with the answer. Three weeks. Eight, if Mrs. Pell's arithmetic held. No road out, no letter sent, no witness summoned—nothing in or out but the water's permission.

And below them, faintly, through floors of stone, they heard a door close somewhere in the house that had no reason to be open at this hour, and footsteps beginning to climb.
