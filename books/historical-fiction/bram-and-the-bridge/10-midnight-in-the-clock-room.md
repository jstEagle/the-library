## 10. Midnight in the Clock Room

I did not mean to be awake.

That was the first lie I told myself that night, and I have since learned to count my lies the way I count ticks—carefully, one by one, because a clock that loses count is worse than a clock that stops. The truth was that I had lain in the narrow bed Mrs. Pell had made up for me with my clothes folded over the chair and my boots set beside them, toes pointing at the door, exactly as they pointed every night since the flood warnings began. A person who might need to run keeps their boots where their feet can find them in the dark. Twelve years of apprenticeship had taught me that much about myself: I was always half-packed, always half-ready to leave, even in rooms that had been kind to me.

The second lie was that I heard something. I didn't. What woke me was silence—the particular silence of a house where a clock has stopped striking. The Harrow master clock struck every quarter hour without fail, even falsified as it was, its voice carrying down through the tower's stone throat into every room like a heartbeat through a floor. When it went quiet, the whole house held its breath, and I felt the breath stop against my own chest.

I dressed without lighting the lamp. Down the back stair, past the kitchen where banked coals breathed orange under the grate, along the passage that smelled of tallow and cold stone. The tower door stood at the end of it, iron-banded oak, and I had taken to leaving it unlatched during the day so I could come and go with my tools—but at night I always latched it. Always. I remembered latching it that evening. I could feel the memory in my palm, the cold bite of the latch.

It stood open six inches.

* * *

The clock room sat at the top of the tower stair, a round chamber walled in pale brick, lit by day through four tall windows that faced the four quarters of the sky. At night, when the moon was up, the light came down through those windows in long grey blades and lay across the floor like fallen timber. The movement itself occupied the room's heart: the great going-train rising two stories through an open well in the ceiling, the pendulum hanging below it on its steel suspension spring, nine feet of lenticular brass bobbing slow and patient over a pit lined with felt to kill the draughts. My master used to say a tower clock was not a machine but a kept animal—you fed it weight, you cleaned its pivots, and in return it told you the truth whether you wanted it or not.

Someone was feeding it lies.

I stopped on the last step and put out the candle of my breath. In the moonlight, Edmund Harrow stood beside the pendulum pit with his coat off and his sleeves rolled, and he was working the rating nut with a small brass key.

Not a file this time. Not the escapement. He had learned—or someone had taught him—that the teeth I'd found filed were a wound too visible, the kind of damage a repairer would find and mend. This was subtler. The rating nut raised and lowered the bob by fractions, and fractions were all he needed. A turn here, a turn there, night after night, and the clock drifted slow by degrees no one would notice until the drift became doctrine. The house would simply grow accustomed to a clock that lagged, the way a family grows accustomed to a limp, and when the hour came that mattered—the hour written on a death certificate, the hour weighed against a will's witnessing—the record would be wrong by enough to matter and too little to prove.

He worked with his head bent, and the moonlight made a grey blade of his parting, and his hands—I hated myself for noticing, but I am a clockmaker's apprentice before I am anything else—his hands were good. Steady. The hands of a man who had watched work done and practiced it alone. There was no trembling in them, no fumbling of a first attempt. This was the fourth or fifth night of his education, and he had been an apt pupil.

I should have gone back down the stair. I knew it then as clearly as I know anything. Whatever happened next in this house, I wanted no part of standing in a dark room watching the heir commit a crime against time itself. But my boots were on the stairs behind me, and the stair was old, and the third step from the landing sang a small dry note under any weight—

—and Edmund turned his head.

I moved without deciding to move. That is the honest account, though honesty has cost me more than lying ever did. There was a recess in the wall behind the stair's newel post, a shallow arch built to hold a statue some ancestor never bought, and I folded myself into it the way a mainspring folds into its barrel, tight and silent, my shoulder against cold brick, my heart going fast and shallow the way a balance wheel goes when the oil is failing.

Edmund crossed the room. His shadow preceded him, long and thin, and fell across the archway's mouth.

For three full seconds—one tick, two, three, though the clock was ticking wrong and so even my counting lied—he stood close enough that I could smell the camphor on his coat and hear the small wet sound of his breathing. Then he stepped back. Whatever he saw or suspected, he chose not to see it. I understood that choice intimately; I had been making it my whole life. You look at a thing, and you decide what it is permitted to be, and then you look away.

"Rats," he said aloud, to no one, in a voice pitched to carry. "The place is full of them."

He returned to the pendulum. The key clicked against the nut—a sound I felt in my teeth, a quarter-turn, perhaps less—and then he stood watching the bob swing, timing it against his pocket watch, and I watched the watch's lid glint as he consulted it, once, twice, three times, a man checking his arithmetic against the very instrument he was corrupting. It occurred to me, pressed into the cold of that arch, that he was not merely tampering. He was calibrating himself. Learning how much a quarter-turn stole. Building, night by night, a private table of theft.

When he finished, he wiped the nut with his handkerchief—removing the bright scratch-marks of fresh brass, leaving only the older patina—and rolled down his sleeves, and took up his coat, and went. His footsteps descended the stair without hurry. On the third step from the landing, the wood sang its small dry note, and he paused, and I heard him breathe once, and then he went on down, slower than he had come.

I counted to two hundred before I unfolded myself from the arch. My left arm had gone numb where the brick had pressed it, and my fingers, when I tried to flex them, answered slowly, like a train of wheels run dry.

The pendulum swung in the moonlight, patient, obedient, lying.

I went to the pit and looked down at the rating nut. Under my thumb, faint but present, the metal still held warmth. Brass holds heat longer than it holds truth; my master said that too, or something like it, and I had laughed at the time. I did not laugh now. I took out my own tools—not to correct the nut, I told myself, only to measure—and I measured. One-eighth of a turn, maybe a hair less. By morning the clock would lose another eleven seconds. By the week's end, near two minutes. And somewhere in the papers locked in Edmund's study, a dying man's signature waited to fall outside the hour that gave it force.

I stood there with my dividers open in the moonlight, and I did not close the gap.

Understand me. I could have corrected it in ninety seconds. I could have turned the nut back, checked the beat, let the clock run true until morning, and let Edmund find his work undone and know that someone in this house was watching him. That was the argument I made to myself, standing in the cold: that correction was prudence, self-defense, the act of a repairer protecting his repair.

But beneath the argument, quiet as water finding a low place, ran another current. If I corrected the clock, the falsified hours already recorded would stand unexplained. If I corrected it, Edmund would only change his method. And if I corrected it—if I made the clock tell true time *now*, tonight, days before the death that was coming for us all the way winter comes—then I would be choosing. Choosing Verity. Choosing the will I had never read, on behalf of a family whose quarrels I understood the way I understood weather: from underneath, feeling only the pressure change.

And there was a third thing, which I did not let myself name, which I will name now because I am done being generous with myself. Mr. Harrow had put a brass key in my hand and asked me to make the clock keep true time *for what comes after*. For what comes after. Not for now. The dying man had wanted the falseness preserved, or had wanted something else entirely that I did not yet understand, and until I understood it, every turn of that nut was a word spoken in a conversation I had only partly heard.

So I closed my dividers. I wiped my thumbprint from the nut with my sleeve, the way Edmund had wiped his scratches, learning his tidiness the way he had learned his theft. And I climbed back down through the sleeping house with the clock losing time above me, second by stolen second, and I lay in my narrow bed with my boots pointing at the door, and I did not sleep.

* * *

It was near three when I heard the stair again.

I was sitting up in the dark before I knew I had decided to move, and this time I did not take the passage and the tower door. There was another way. Mrs. Pell had shown it to me my first week, laughing: a servants' stair, steep and windowless, running up inside the tower's outer wall to a hatch that opened behind the case of weights. She used it to carry coal up to the clock keeper of the old Mr. Harrow's youth, she said, back when the clock had a keeper who lived in the house instead of visiting from town. Nobody used it now. Nobody but me, sometimes, when I wanted to check the movement without crossing the household's morning geography of eyes.

I went up it now in absolute blackness, one hand on the wall, my boots in my other hand, stocking-footed on stone worn hollow by a century of climbing feet. The hatch opened without sound—I had oiled its hinges myself, in my first week, a small anonymous kindness to a mechanism nobody thanked—and I looked out through the gap between the weight-case and the wall.

The clock room lay silver and still. The pendulum swung. And on the far side of the chamber, at the top of the main stair, half-hidden by the newel post—by the very arch where I had hidden an hour before—stood Verity Harrow.

She was in her dressing gown, her hair unbound, and she was not moving. Not hiding, exactly. Hiding is active; it has a shape. She was simply *there*, the way furniture is there, the way a portrait is there, arranged by the accident of the stair's geometry into perfect concealment, and she was looking at the pendulum pit with an expression I could not read at that distance except to know it was not surprise.

She had seen him. Or she had come to see. Either way, she had known.

I watched her for a long time—long enough that my knees ached against the weight-case, long enough that the clock above me lost another minute to Edmund's careful arithmetic. She did not go to the pit. She did not touch anything. She stood in the moonlight and looked at the evidence of her brother's nightly work the way one looks at a wound one has decided not to treat, and then she turned, and her gaze swept the room—past the movement, past the windows, past the weight-case where I crouched behind three hundred pounds of cast iron—and lingered, just barely, just for the length of one swing of the pendulum, on the hatch.

Then she went down the stair, and the third step sang its note, softer than it had sung for Edmund, because she was lighter and knew the house better, and she was gone.

I stayed in the dark behind the weights until my legs shook. Three people, I thought. Three people now stood inside this secret like stones inside a wall, each bearing a share of its weight, none of us speaking to the others, the whole structure holding only because we each pretended the others were not there. Edmund, who built the lie. Verity, who watched it built. And myself, who could end it with a quarter-turn of a nut and a handful of words, and had chosen—had *chosen*, with open eyes and steady hands—to let the clock go on lying.

My master would have called it the unforgivable thing. A clockmaker's whole covenant, he said once, driving a taper into the stove on a morning like any other morning, is with time itself. Break that and nothing else you build will keep.

But my master had never been handed a brass key by a dying man. My master had never stood in a flood-bound house where kindness wore borrowed names and inheritance hung on a single recorded hour. My master had never been me, half of everywhere and whole of nowhere, trusted by a stranger precisely because I had no stake in the family's arithmetic—and discovering, in the dark at three in the morning, that having no stake was itself a kind of stake, that neutrality was a position, that silence was a turn of the nut.

Above me the clock struck the quarter, muffled and wrong, and I counted the strokes against my own pulse and found them short by exactly what I knew they would be.

Three people shared the secret now. But secrets, I thought, pulling the hatch shut behind me, are like clocks: they do not stay wound forever, and when they run down, they run down all at once, and everyone hears the stopping.

Two nights later, Verity found me in the kitchen doorway after supper, with flour on her sleeves and a look on her face that meant the pretending was finished.

"We should talk," she said, "about what you saw."
