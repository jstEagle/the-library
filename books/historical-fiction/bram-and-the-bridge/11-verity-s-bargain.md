## 11. Verity's Bargain

Verity had learned to read people the way her father read weather: by what they did with their hands.

Edmund's hands were always still—folded, clasped, resting on ledgers like a man posing for a portrait of himself as a responsible heir. Her father's hands, in the last months, had gone restless, plucking at the blanket, reaching for instruments that weren't there. Mrs. Pell's hands were never still at all, which was its own kind of honesty; a woman scrubbing a pot could not also be composing a lie.

And the clockmaker—the one who called himself Master Aldous Finch, though the letterhead said the observatory had written to *Finch & Apprentice*—the clockmaker's hands told Verity everything she needed to know.

They were too careful. Too deliberate. A master with forty years at the bench worked the way she imagined water moved through limestone: without thinking, without ceremony. But this person handled every tool like it was being witnessed. Every file lifted, every screw turned, was performed rather than done. And beneath the performance, when they thought no one watched—and Verity always watched—there was something else in those hands. Hunger. The particular ferocity of someone who had been told for years that they were not permitted to be good at the only thing they loved.

She had been watching from the tower stair for three days now, in the hour before dinner, when the light came slantwise through the east windows and caught the brass of the movement like fire caught and tamed. She told herself it was suspicion. She told herself it was duty: her father dying upstairs, Edmund circling the estate like a gull after offal, and into all of it a stranger with borrowed authority, alone with the one machine in this house that kept the time everything depended on.

But there was more to it than that, and Verity Harrow did not lie to herself, even when lying would have been kinder.

The clockmaker fascinated her.

It was not only the disguise—though the disguise was magnificent in its audacity. She had seen it on the second evening, at supper, when Mrs. Pell set down the mutton and their eyes met over the candle. Something passed across that face too quick to name and too slow to miss: the flicker of a person checking whether they'd been recognized, and finding, to their visible relief, that they hadn't. A master craftsman did not look relieved not to be known. A master craftsman expected to be known.

Then there were smaller things. The way "Finch" sat slightly wrong on their shoulders, like a coat cut for a broader man. The way they ate—economical, grateful, the manners of someone who had learned good table habits from a book or a benevolent employer rather than a nursery. The way they never once, in five days, used their master's first name, though colleagues who'd corresponded for years always did. And once—only once—she had heard them murmur over the escapement in a language that was not English, low and rhythmic, a sound like a lullaby sung to a machine, and the vowels had come from somewhere much farther away than any guild hall in London.

Verity had spent twenty-six years in a house that faced away from the road, learning to notice what other people declined to see. It was, she sometimes thought bitterly, the family trade. Edmund noticed weaknesses. Father noticed errors. Verity noticed people.

So she knew. And knowing, she discovered, was a currency—and she was about to spend it.

* * *

She chose the hour carefully. Not the morning, when Edmund took his ride along the drowned lower road as far as the water allowed, inspecting his inheritance like a buyer at auction. Not evening, when Mrs. Pell's kitchen filled the house with warmth and everyone performed family. She chose the dead middle of the afternoon, when the rain came down in gray sheets against the observatory glass and the whole household dissolved into its separate silences.

The clock room door stood half open. She knocked anyway—one knuckle, precise—and stepped inside without waiting.

Tamsin—Ishikawa, she thought, trying the name silently, tasting its foreignness—looked up from the bench, a loupe at one eye, a pair of tweezers poised over an open gear train. For a moment neither of them moved. Rain drummed the cupola above like a thousand small impatient fingers.

"Miss Harrow." The voice was pitched low, carefully neutral. Another performance. "I wasn't expecting—"

"No," Verity said. "You weren't."

She crossed the room and closed the door behind her. The latch clicked, a small definitive sound, and she saw the clockmaker's shoulders tighten by a fraction. Good. Let them feel the walls drawing in. She pulled the stool near the window around to face the bench, sat, and folded her hands in her lap—still, composed, Edmund-still, because she wanted this meeting conducted on her terms and her terms required the appearance of patience.

"I've been coming up here every afternoon," she said, "to sit with my father's clock. He built this room, you know. Before I was born. He used to bring me up here as a child and let me hold the winding key while he counted the turns. He said a clock was the only honest thing a man could own, because it couldn't flatter him." She paused. "I have been sitting here watching you work. And you are very good. Better than good. Whoever trained you wasted nothing."

"Thank you," the clockmaker said carefully.

"But you are not Aldous Finch."

The tweezers did not move. The rain filled the silence. Verity gave it three full seconds, then four, watching the face across from her perform composure and slowly, beautifully, fail at it—not collapsing, but fraying at the edges, the way wet paper frays.

"The letter your office sent," she continued, conversationally, "was written in Mr. Finch's hand. I know his hand; he corresponded with my father for eleven years. But the reply confirming the appointment—the one dated the ninth—was written in a different hand entirely. Firmer. Younger. Left-slanted where his slopes right." She tilted her head. "I assumed he'd dictated it. Masters do delegate. But then I considered: if Aldous Finch were ill enough to send an apprentice under his own name and seal, he would have written so plainly. He is a fussy, punctilious old man, and fussy punctilious men do not leave ambiguity in correspondence about money. Which means the deception was yours. You wrote to my father as your master. You packed his coat—" she nodded at the heavy wool garment hanging by the door, two sizes too generous in the shoulder— "and you climbed our hill and you lied your way into the most sensitive room in this house, at the exact moment when the timing of my father's death will decide everything he owns."

The clockmaker set down the tweezers. Very slowly. As though any faster motion might shatter something.

"You're mistaken," they said.

"I am many things, but I am not mistaken." Verity leaned forward. "Here is what happens next, if you prefer to keep insisting. I tell Edmund. Edmund tells the magistrate—if we can ever reach the magistrate—or failing that, Edmund simply puts you out into the rain and lets the river decide how far you swim. You lose your place, whatever name you're hiding, whatever future you were gambling on. And I lose nothing, because I will simply have reported what I saw." She sat back. "That is the shape of your situation. I want you to understand it completely before I offer you the way out of it."

Something shifted in the clockmaker's face then—not fear. Anger, maybe, banked hard and fast, the coals glowing through the grate. Their chin came up a degree.

"And what," they said quietly, "do you want?"

"Ah." Verity smiled, and knew it was not a kind smile. "There it is. The real voice. Not Finch's apprentice. *You.*"

* * *

For a long moment the only sound was the rain and, beneath it, the deep patient heartbeat of the great clock—tick, tick, tick—that steady pulse Verity had fallen asleep to her entire life and which now, she understood, might be the most dishonest thing in the house.

"I want you to help me prove a forgery," she said.

The clockmaker blinked. Whatever they had braced for, it was not that.

"My father rewrote his will in August. Twice, if Mrs. Pell's memory serves, and Mrs. Pell's memory is better than any solicitor's ledger. The final version names me. Everything—the observatory, the land, the endowments, the toll rights on the bridge. Me. Edmund knows it. He has known since August, and he has been waiting ever since, patient as a stone, for father to die and for the paper to turn to ash in his hands." She stood, pacing now, unable to help it; the words had been sealed in her too long. "Two weeks ago I found the witness page loose in the document case. Loose, do you understand? The ribbon seal had been re-tied, and tied badly—Edmund ties a beautiful knot, always has, sailing habit—but this knot was left-handed. My brother is right-handed. Someone else retied it, or someone made him use his wrong hand, or—" she exhaled— "or I am a madwoman seeing conspiracy in ribbon. That is the other possibility, and some nights it seems the likelier one."

"And you need me because—"

"Because I cannot prove it. Because a daughter challenging a will looks like exactly what it is—a daughter who wants the money. Because the witnesses are dead or distant, and the solicitor in Ashford keeps copies I can't reach while the bridge is closed, and because Edmund controls the household, the accounts, and soon, God help us, the narrative." She stopped pacing and turned to face them fully. "But you—you are a stranger. Neutral. Skilled with your hands and your eyes. You notice things mechanical. A re-tied knot, a pressed seal, ink laid over older ink—these are mechanical problems. And no one watches a clockmaker. You are furniture to them. Invisible. I intend to make that invisibility useful."

The clockmaker was silent for a long moment. When they spoke, their voice had lost its borrowed flatness entirely.

"If I refuse?"

"Then I tell Edmund what I know, and we discover together whether he protects you or destroys you. I genuinely don't know which. That's the worst part—he might protect you, actually, if he thinks a fraudulent clockmaker serves his purposes better than a real one." She let that settle. "But I don't think you'll refuse."

"No?"

"No. Because you didn't come all this way, wearing a dead man's name"—she saw them flinch at that, and noted it: *dead man*, or close enough— "to repair a clock. You came to prove something. To yourself, or to your guild, or to whoever told you that you weren't allowed. I've watched your hands for three days. They are the hands of someone who intends to matter." She softened her voice by a single degree. "I know what it is to be competent and dismissed. I have been the competent, dismissed daughter of Marlow's Bend for twenty-six years. So consider this, apprentice-who-is-not-an-apprentice: I am offering you the one thing no one in this valley has ever offered you. Partnership between equals. Blackmail today, perhaps. Alliance tomorrow. I'm flexible on the vocabulary if you're flexible on the loyalty."

The rain intensified, a sudden gust throwing it against the cupola glass in handfuls. Below them, faintly, the river could be heard—even through stone, even through rain—a low continuous roar like the breathing of something enormous asleep.

The clockmaker looked down at their hands. When they looked up again, something had changed in their face—the wariness hadn't gone, but it had acquired a companion, something almost like respect.

"You said the witness page was re-tied," they said. "Show me."

Verity felt something loosen in her chest that had been tight since August. "Now?"

"Now. While Edmund is out. If your brother altered that document, I want to see it before he decides to alter it further. Paper burns quickly, Miss Harrow. Especially in autumn."

* * *

They went down together through the house's hushed corridors, past the closed door behind which their father slept the thin sleep of the dying, past the schoolroom converted to a study where Edmund's ledgers lay in ranks like soldiers. In the library, Verity knelt at the iron strongbox behind her father's desk—her father's desk, she reminded herself, whatever the paper currently said—and drew out the document case.

The will lay inside, heavy cream paper, ribbon threaded through punched seals. She spread it on the reading table under the lamp and watched the clockmaker bend over it, and she watched their hands again—those careful, hungry hands—as they hovered above the witness page without touching, tracing the air just above the signatures the way a physician traces a fracture through skin.

"Lamp closer," they murmured.

She moved it. The light pooled yellow, and the clockmaker tilted the page until the ink caught at an angle, and went very still.

"What?" Verity whispered.

"Look at the date line. Under the last signature." Their finger indicated, not quite touching. "The ink is layered. See how it sits *on top* of the paper fibers here, glossy, but here—" the finger slid a quarter inch— "it has sunk in. Bitten. Two different writings, two different days. Someone wrote the attestation clause fresh, over a page that already existed. And the ribbon—" they lifted it delicately, turning the punched seal toward the lamp— "the punch is clean on this side, torn on the back. It was re-threaded. The original holes were widened with a needle to take a thicker ribbon. This isn't the same binding, Miss Harrow. This page has been dressed up to look untouched."

Verity stared at the page and felt the floor tilt gently beneath her certainty. She had been right. God help her, she had been right, and there was no longer any comfortable corner of the night in which she could hide from it.

"Can you prove it?" she asked. "Prove it so a magistrate would believe it?"

The clockmaker was quiet a moment. Outside, the river roared. Somewhere below, the front door opened and closed—Edmund, returning early, boots striking the hall flags—and both of them froze over the table like conspirators in a painting, which, Verity thought wildly, was precisely what they now were.

"I don't know yet," the clockmaker said at last, barely audible. "But I know someone who works with documents' cousins—machines that record. Machines that remember what was done to them and when." Their eyes came up to hers, and there was something in them she hadn't seen before: fear, yes, but underneath the fear, a kind of terrible resolve. "Give me until tomorrow night. And Miss Harrow—when I show you what I find in that clock, you may wish you had never asked me to stay."

"Try me," Verity said, sliding the will back into its case as Edmund's footsteps started up the stairs. "I was raised in this house. I stopped wishing for safety a long time ago."

The footsteps reached the landing. The clockmaker slipped out the library's far door into the corridor's shadow, and Verity remained alone with the lamp, the rain, and the knowledge—new, cold, and irreversible—that she had just bound her fortune to a stranger whose real name she did not know.

Above her, muffled through floors and stone, the master clock struck the half hour.

One tick too many.
