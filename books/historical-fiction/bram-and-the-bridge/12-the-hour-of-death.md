## 12. The Hour of Death

Tamsin did not sleep that night. They told themselves it was the movement—the pallet stones half-dressed in their mind's eye, the filed teeth they had measured twice and would measure again by lamplight—but the truth was that the brass key sat against their breastbone like a second heart, and every time they closed their eyes they felt its small weight turning.

The clock room held the dark differently than other rooms. In Mrs. Pell's kitchen the dark was a blanket; here it was a medium, thick and listening, and the master clock stood up through the middle of it like a drowned mast. Tamsin kept one lamp turned low on the workbench and sat with their back to the case, notebook open, recording what they had found in a shorthand of their own devising: *third wheel, teeth 4–7 dressed down; pallet shifted 2mm; loss ≈ 1 min/hr.*

One minute an hour. Twelve minutes a night. A full hour gone by the time a day had passed.

They had written the figures and then stared at them until the numerals stopped looking like numbers and started looking like a sentence.

Someone had wanted this clock to lie slowly. Not to stop—a stopped clock announces itself, invites a repairer, invites questions—but to drift, gently, the way a river drifts a boat toward a weir. Anyone consulting the observatory time would trust it, because observatory time is the time the town trusts. And anyone recording an hour against it—an hour of death, say, entered into a family Bible or a magistrate's ledger—would record not the true hour but the machine's hour, which could be made to say whatever its saboteur wished.

*For what comes after.* That was how old Mr. Harrow had put it, pressing the key into Tamsin's palm with fingers that had once set chronometers and now could scarcely hold a spoon. He had known. Of course he had known; he watched from his chair while his clock struck twelve bells and thirteen ticks, and he said nothing, because he was a man who built his proofs before he needed them.

Tamsin turned the key over in their mind the way they would turn a strange screw, feeling for its thread. It fit something. They did not yet know what. But it had been given into their keeping by a dying man who trusted no one else in his house—not his sons, not his daughter, not the woman who cooked his meals—and that trust lay across Tamsin's chest now heavier than the coat they had borrowed to walk through Marlow's Bend wearing another man's name.

Outside, the rain went on. It had been going on for days, patient as interest accruing, and beneath it, if you listened past the clock's tick, you could hear the river working at the world—gnawing the towpath, shouldering the bridge piers, rising an inch at a time toward the tollgate lamp that had burned red over the closed crossing since Tuesday.

Tamsin wrote one more line in the notebook, then blotted it:

*If he dies while the clock runs false, the hour will be false too.*

Then they sat very still, because somewhere below them a bell rang—Mrs. Pell's handbell, soft and urgent, once, twice—and then footsteps began climbing the tower stairs, and they were not Verity's, and they were not Edmund's.

They were uneven. Dragging. The footsteps of people carrying something together.

* * *

Mr. Harrow died at seventeen minutes past five by Tamsin's pocket watch, though no one in the house would ever agree on the hour, and that was precisely the point—or someone's point, planned months ago in a workshop Tamsin could not imagine, by hands Tamsin could not yet name.

They heard it first as absence. All night the sickroom sounds had filtered up through two floors—the rasp and catch of failing lungs, the clink of the medicine glass, Mrs. Pell's low voice reading scripture in a tone closer to argument than devotion—and then, in the grey hour when the rain thinned to mist, the sounds stopped, all at once, like a mechanism reaching the end of its train.

Tamsin was already dressed. They had been dressed for hours. They came out onto the landing as Mrs. Pell emerged from the stairwell below, her apron wringing itself in her fists without her permission, her face doing the terrible arithmetic that faces do when grief arrives mixed with something else.

"He's gone," she said. "You'll want to know the time."

It was not a question. Tamsin understood, in the space of a heartbeat, that Mrs. Pell knew more than she had ever said over bread and stew—that the whole household understood the clock above them was a loaded instrument, and that the moment of death had just become a document, and documents in this house were weapons.

"By my watch," Tamsin said carefully, "it wants twenty past five."

"And by the master?"

Tamsin hesitated. The hesitation lasted less than a breath, but Mrs. Pell saw it; her eyes, kind and tired, registered it and filed it away.

"The master runs a minute or two behind," Tamsin said. "I've told you. It's why I'm here."

"Aye." Mrs. Pell looked down at her wringing hands. "Well. There'll need to be a time written down. There always is, with this family. I'd best tell Miss Verity." She turned, paused on the top stair. "He asked for you, at the end. Not by your name," she added, and there was no cruelty in it, only the gentle blunt honesty that made her kitchen the only warm room in the tower. "By your trade. He said, *send the clockmaker.* I thought you'd want to know somebody remembered you kindly, whoever you are."

She went down. Tamsin stood alone on the landing while the house woke around death the way a body wakes around a wound—with hushed motion, doors opened a careful few inches, floorboards avoided where they creaked.

The clock room door stood open. Tamsin had left it shut. They were certain they had left it shut.

Inside, the lamp had been trimmed and refilled. Someone had sat here recently—recently meaning within the hour, while downstairs a man finished dying—and had consulted the great dial, and had perhaps touched nothing at all except to leave the faintest disturbance in the dust along the case base. Tamsin crouched. Two impressions in the grey film: boot heels, narrow, well-kept, angled toward the pendulum housing.

Edmund's boots. Tamsin had learned them on the stairs these many days—narrow-heeled, quiet, the boots of a man who moved through his father's house like an auditor.

So it had happened already, or was happening still. The clock stood at four hours and some odd minutes past midnight when the true time was past five; it ran slow by nearly the full stolen hour now, the sabotage compounded nightly by whatever adjustment Edmund made at his midnights, each visit winding the lie tighter. If a time of death were taken from this dial tomorrow—if a magistrate, or a lawyer, or merely the family itself wrote down what the observatory said—it would place Josiah Harrow's death inside four o'clock. And the final will, the one naming Verity, had been witnessed and signed at ten minutes past four on the morning of the third of October. Tamsin knew because Verity had shown them the paper by library lamplight, her finger steady on the date, her voice flat with the effort of flatness.

*A death before the signing,* she had explained, *and the older will stands. That's the law of it. Papa signed the new will after his last lucid hour; if he died before that hour, the signature means nothing and Edmund's paper rules.*

An hour. Everything hung on an hour, and Edmund owned the instrument that measured hours, and the town below—flooded off from any magistrate, any lawyer, any second opinion—would take the observatory's word for it because the observatory had never lied before.

Tamsin rose on unsteady legs and stood before the great case, and the guilt, when it came, came not as a wave but as a slow seep, cold water finding the seam of a boot.

Because they had known. They had known for days—had disassembled the escapement and held the filed teeth in their own hands and felt under their thumbnail the burr of metal cut deliberately, patiently, by someone who understood exactly what they were destroying—and they had said nothing. They had hidden on a stair at midnight and watched Edmund's white cuffs at the pendulum and said nothing. They had let Verity bind herself to them in the library and still, even then, holding the proof in their notebook in their own private shorthand, said nothing.

And now a man was dead upstairs, and the hour of his death was already being decided, and Tamsin Ishikawa—who had come to this town under a borrowed coat and a borrowed name, who had dreamed for twelve years of a day when their hands would be trusted to speak for them—was standing guard over the lie with both hands in their pockets.

*Say it now,* whispered some small clear voice that sounded, uncomfortably, like their mother's. *Go down. Tell them the clock is broken and how it is broken and who has been breaking it.*

But the voice answered itself before Tasmion could move. *Tell them what? That you found the sabotage days ago and kept silent? That you watched him tamper and hid like a thief? That you are not even the person the letter summoned?* Every truth in Tamsin's possession was poisoned at the root by the silence it had grown in. To speak now was not to save the hour of death; it was to confess, and confession in this house, among these people, with the flood sealing the roads and Edmund's lawyers waiting in the dry country beyond the water—

Tamsin pressed their palms flat against the clock case, feeling the tick come up through the bones of their hands like a pulse.

*I'll fix it,* they told the machine. *I'll fix it tonight, before anyone consults you. True time restored, and the hour of death recorded honest, and no one the wiser about anything else.* It was a plan. It was almost a plan. It was what a brave person would do while remaining safe, and Tamsin clung to it the way a drowning man clings to spar, not noticing yet that the spar is attached to the wreck.

Behind them, the door clicked.

Verity stood in the frame in her black walking dress, hair unpinned, a candle in her hand that she did not seem to know she was holding. Her face was terrible and composed, grief and war occupying it in equal measure, and her eyes went from Tamsin to the clock dial and back with a speed that missed nothing.

"It's after five," she said. "Your watch. His watch. Whatever you keep time by." She stepped inside and shut the door softly, as if the dead could sleep through anything so long as no one raised their voice. "And that thing says four. Say it, clockmaker. Say what I think you're thinking, so I don't have to be the only one awake in this house."

Tamsin took their hands out of their pockets. There was nowhere honest to put them.

"If the time is written from this dial," they said quietly, "he will be recorded as dying before the will was signed."

"Yes." Verity set the candle on the bench beside Tamsin's lamp, and the two flames leaned toward each other like conspirators. "And you can stop it. Can't you. You can make it right before anyone reads it."

"I can." The words came out smaller than Tamsin intended. "But Verity—when I open that escapement in daylight, when the correction is visible, someone will ask why the error ran for weeks and no one caught it. Your brother will ask. And he will look at me, and he knows what I am even if he doesn't know my name, and—" Tamsin stopped. Breathed. "Every road out of this room goes through the truth, and the truth burns everything it passes. Your brother. Mrs. Pell's boy, whoever he is. Me."

"Then we choose what burns." Verity's voice did not rise. That was the frightening thing about her; she had her father's gift for lowering her voice exactly when it mattered most. "You said silence keeps people safe. I've lived in this house twenty-nine years and I'm telling you it doesn't. Silence in this family is just noise we've agreed not to hear."

Below them, muffled by stone and floors, the household gathered around a bed to do what households do. Tamsin heard Mrs. Pell's voice leading a psalm, and Edmund's answering—level, correct, already the voice of the estate's master, already speaking of arrangements and telegrams and *the appropriate authorities when the water allows*—and under it all, steady as sin, the master clock ticking out its false hour, one tick too many, marking time for a dead man whose true hour had already slipped past unwritten.

Tamsin picked up the lamp.

"Not tonight," they said. "Tonight they'll write the time from the wall clock in the death room, or from Edmund's watch, and those run true enough. Tonight nothing hangs on me." It was a rationalization and they both knew it; the entry made tonight would be checked against the observatory tomorrow, corrected or confirmed, and by tomorrow it would be too late to unmake. "Tomorrow. Give me one day. Let me find what your father's key opens, and make sure of everything, so that when I speak there's no way back for any of us."

Verity studied them for a long moment. Rain ticked against the cupola glass overhead; the river muttered below.

"One day," she said. "My father gave you a key, didn't he. I watched his face when he did it—he looked the way he looked at his best mechanisms. Like he'd already seen the end of the spring." She lifted her candle and moved to the door, then paused with her hand on the latch. "Whatever it opens, clockmaker—open it fast. Because Edmund has gone downstairs to fetch the family Bible, and he is going to write the hour himself."

She was gone. Tamsin stood in the lamplight between the lying dial and the closed door, the brass key a coal against their chest, and understood with perfect, useless clarity that the one thing they had never been able to afford—silence—was the one thing they had just spent, and that the bill would come due before the flood crested.

From below, faintly, came the scratch of a pen.
