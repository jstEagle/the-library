## 13. Reading of the Paper

The rain had not stopped for six days when Mr. Quillon, the family solicitor, arrived at the observatory door with his coat steaming and his ledger wrapped in oilcloth like a wounded bird. He had come across on the ferry from Ashcombe because no carriage could manage the drowned road, and he stood dripping in the entrance hall with the expression of a man who had already rehearsed this scene and found it disagreeable.

Edmund Harrow watched him from the top of the stair and thought: *now.*

Below him, the house arranged itself. Mrs. Pell came from the kitchen with her hands still floured. Verity descended from her father's room, where she had sat the whole morning with a book she was not reading, and took up her post by the cold fireplace as though it were a rampart. The clockmaker—the apprentice, rather, the boy or girl or whatever it was under that borrowed coat—appeared at the edge of the hall holding a rag stained with clock oil, as though summoned by the smell of paper.

"Mr. Quillon," Edmund said, coming down. "You've made good time, considering."

"The river makes its own schedule, Mr. Harrow." Quillon's voice was dry as August. "As I suspect you know better than any of us."

Edmund smiled at that and led him to the study, and if his hand paused once on the banister—not from weakness but from the particular pleasure of a man touching the rail of a ship he is about to inherit—no one remarked upon it.

* * *

They read the will in the drawing room because the study window leaked. Quillon sat at his mother's writing desk, which Edmund had ordered moved there two days ago, along with the good chairs, along with everything that signaled authority. The fire was built high. Outside, the river spoke continuously against the terrace wall, a sound like slow applause.

His father's body lay above them, in the east room, packed in ice that Mrs. Pell had begged off the fish cart before the road closed entirely. He had died at dawn nine days past—or at what the observatory clock said was dawn—and since then the house had moved through its grief the way a mechanism moves through a broken gear: with grinding, with skipping, with a terrible regularity.

"There are two instruments," Quillon began, laying out papers with a notary's precision. "A will executed in 1887, witnessed by myself and Mrs. Pell. And a later instrument, dated the fourteenth of March last, also witnessed by myself and Mrs. Pell, which supersedes it—*provided* it was validly executed, and provided the testator possessed testamentary capacity at the time of signing, and provided—" he looked up over his spectacles— "the date of death does not fall outside the period of its validity."

"Say it plainly," Verity said.

Quillon did not look at her. "The later will leaves the estate and the observatory to Miss Verity Harrow entire, with a maintenance provision for the household staff. The earlier will divides the estate between the children, with the observatory and its endowment to Mr. Edmund Harrow." A pause. "The later instrument was signed in the presence of witnesses on the fourteenth of March. The deceased died—" he consulted a slip of paper, and Edmund saw the hesitation, the small professional distaste— "according to the observatory's master clock, at twenty minutes past five on the morning of the ninth of October."

"And?" Verity's voice was flat.

"And the fourteenth of March instrument contains a codicil of confirmation, added the second of June, requiring the testator to re-affirm the will within ninety days of any serious decline in health. Dr. Lassiter records the final decline as beginning the eleventh of July. The re-affirmation never occurred." Quillon folded his hands. "There is a further complication. The master clock has been running irregularly. Mrs. Pell informs me it struck twelve at what the village church called eleven. If the recorded hour of death is uncertain—if the time of death cannot be established to the standard the codicil requires—then the validity of the later instrument is compromised. The presumption falls to the earlier, duly executed will."

The silence afterward had texture. Edmund felt it settle over the room like dust.

He had prepared for argument. He had prepared for Verity to produce the doctor, for the clockmaker to be trotted out with some testimony about escapements, for his sister's cold fury or, worse, her tears. What he had not prepared for was the quality of the quiet itself—that it should feel less like defeat than like an audience waiting.

"That is absurd," Verity said at last. "Papa signed the will in March. I sat beside him while he did it. He knew precisely what he was doing; he made a joke about the garden going to me whether the roses liked it or not. The codicil is a technicality."

"Codicils are technicalities," Quillon agreed. "That is rather their function. The law is a machine, Miss Harrow. It runs on what can be proven. And what can be proven here is that a dying man's wishes were recorded on a clock that no longer keeps honest time."

Every head turned toward the clockmaker.

Tamsin—Ishikawa, Edmund corrected himself, though the name still sat strangely in his mouth, a foreign coin among familiar ones—stood near the door with the rag still in hand. Their face did what it always did, which was nothing. That blankness had irritated Edmund from the first week. Grief-stricken households were legible; servants were legible; this person was a locked case walking through his halls.

"You repaired the clock," Edmund said. It was not a question. "You've been at it a fortnight. Tell Mr. Quillon when my father died."

The clockmaker looked at him. There was something in the look he could not parse—not defiance, not fear. Something almost like pity, which was intolerable.

"I wasn't in the room," they said quietly. "I only know what the clock says. And the clock," a pause, delicate as a pallet dropping into place, "has been unwell for some time."

*Unwell.* The word went through Edmund like a splinter entering skin too deep to find.

"Then we are agreed," Quillon said briskly, already squaring the papers. "Pending probate—which the flood will delay, but delay only—the estate passes under the will of 1887. Mr. Edmund Harrow takes the observatory, the endowment, the lands north of the mill stream. Miss Harrow takes her portion and, I believe, the London house." He gathered his coat. "I'll want statements from the household regarding the clock's irregularities. Routine. Sign nothing else without me."

He left in the same rain he'd arrived in. Edmund walked him to the door himself, and stood watching the boat recede down the flooded lane until it became a gray smudge between gray water and gray sky.

When he turned back, Verity was gone from the drawing room. The clockmaker had vanished too. Only Mrs. Pell remained, clearing teacups that no one had touched, and she looked at him with an expression he had never seen on her round, kind face before—something measured and sorrowful, like a woman watching a child walk into deep water.

"You'll want supper," she said, and carried the cups away.

* * *

That evening Edmund sat alone in the study with the estate accounts spread before him and permitted himself, for one full minute, the feeling of having won.

It was supposed to taste like this. He had imagined it for years—in the boarding school dormitory where letters from home arrived thin and infrequent; in the countinghouse in Bristol where he'd spent eight profitable, airless years proving himself useful to men who would never call him anything but *young Harrow*; in the sickroom these last months, watching his father's mind go soft around the edges like fruit, hearing him murmur about roses and river levels and Verity, always Verity. The estate should have been his by every logic except sentiment. His father had built the observatory's fortune on shipping insurance and grain futures; Edmund understood those instruments the way the clockmaker understood gears. Verity understood roses. The old man had let affection overwrite arithmetic, and Edmund had merely corrected the error.

Corrected. The word was a comfort. He turned it over as he turned the ledger pages. He had not forged anything. He had not altered the will itself—he would not have known how, would not have dared. All he had done was see that the codicil's requirement went unmet, that the doctor's dates were accurately recorded, that the machinery of law ran as it was built to run. If the clock had happened to fail in the same season—if its recorded hours had drifted just far enough, just long enough, to make the death-hour disputable—then that was providence, or luck, or the ordinary treachery of mechanisms.

Except.

Except that he had been in the clock room on the fourth night after his father's decline began, with the lamp shuttered, easing the pendulum's rating nut a quarter-turn so the old regulator would lose another minute a day. Except that he had filed nothing himself—his hands were not steady enough for that work—but he had paid a boy in the village to do it, a soft-faced debt-ridden fool named Tobias Pell, whose mother cooked his meals. Except that he had stood in that dark room listening to the great pendulum breathe and heard, distinctly, a third breath in the room that stopped when his stopped.

Someone had been on the stairs. He had told himself it was a cat. The observatory kept no cat.

Edmund closed the ledger. The lamp guttered. Above him, through three floors of stone and timber, he could feel the clock working—it was a sensation he had developed only recently, the way a man with a bad tooth develops awareness of his own jaw. Every ten seconds the tick arrived, faint but insistent, transmitted down the tower's bones into the study ceiling. Ten seconds. Ten seconds. But sometimes eleven. Sometimes thirteen. The intervals had grown strange since the clockmaker began their repairs, as though the machine were hesitating over something, deciding something.

*It's a mechanism,* he told himself. *Mechanisms do not remember.*

But he had watched Tamsin Ishikawa disassemble that movement piece by piece over two weeks, laying each wheel on flannel like a surgeon laying out organs, and he had seen the moment—they had thought no one saw, but Edmund made a business of seeing—when the clockmaker held a small steel pallet up to the window light and went absolutely still, the way a person goes still when they recognize a face in a crowd that should not be there.

Filed teeth. He had paid Tobias enough to file teeth. Someone had found them.

And the clockmaker had said nothing. Had put the pallet back, or not, had continued cleaning wheels with those unnervingly patient hands, and had stood in the drawing room that afternoon and offered him *unwell*, a word with a knife hidden inside it, and a look he now understood had not been pity at all.

It had been patience. The patience of a mechanism that counts.

Edmund rose, took the lamp, and climbed. He told himself it was to check the roof leads above the clock room, which had been leaking; the lie was smooth and he barely bothered to notice it.

* * *

The clock room smelled of oil and cold brass. In the lamplight the movement hung open like a dissected thing, wheels exposed, pendulum hanging in its long patient arc. On the workbench lay the clockmaker's tools, aligned with a precision Edmund found vaguely accusatory. And beside them, a wooden box with a sliding lid, half open.

He should not have looked. He looked.

Inside the box: brass discs, perhaps forty of them, each the size of a sovereign, punched through with small holes in patterns. He lifted one to the light. Around its rim, stamped in tiny characters, ran a date. *9 June.* Another: *4 July.* Another: *14 September.* Each disc was different; each was dated; and though he could not read the pattern of holes, he understood with sudden, absolute certainty that they meant something—that they were not decorations but records, that the clock had been writing something down all along in a language of punctures, and that someone had taught it to write.

His father's key. The old man had pressed a brass key into the clockmaker's hand the week before he died—Edmund had seen it through the sickroom door, had assumed a sentimental gift, a trinket for the servant. His father, who missed nothing, who trusted machines more than sons.

*For what comes after,* the old man had reportedly said.

Edmund set the disc down with fingers that were not quite steady. Above him the escapement ticked—ten seconds, eleven, thirteen—and for the first time in his life he heard it correctly: not as a heartbeat but as testimony. Every interval a sentence. Every tampered hour a paragraph. Somewhere in that brass architecture was the fourth of October, and the fifth, and the nights he had climbed these stairs with his lamp shuttered, all of it punched into metal by a dead man's foresight.

He had won the estate that afternoon. He could feel the weight of it already settling on him like wet clothing—the lands, the endowment, the name spoken with deference in the county rooms. And underneath the weight, cold against the skin, the certainty that none of it would keep.

The clockmaker knew. Verity knew something too—he had caught her twice now watching him with that new, terrible attentiveness, cataloguing him. Even Mrs. Pell's bread had tasted different tonight, though he could not have said why.

Edmund stood a long while in the ticking dark, and then, very carefully, he slid the box lid shut and returned it exactly as he had found it, and went down to bed, where he did not sleep.

In the morning he would begin. There were papers to burn and a boy in the village to silence and a solicitor's ear to bend, and floods, thank God, that kept magistrates and witnesses alike on the far side of the water. He had weeks. Perhaps a month.

Above his head, the escapement ticked on—ten seconds, eleven, thirteen—as though counting down.
