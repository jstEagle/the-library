## 14. The Key in the Escapement

The house had gone quiet the way a body goes quiet—not asleep, but finished. Tamsin felt it through the tower's stones as they climbed the last spiral of stair, one lamp held high, the brass key warm in their pocket where it had lived for three days now, worn smooth against their thigh like a second coin of conscience.

Below them, the household slept. Below that, the river climbed.

They could hear it even here, even through forty feet of quarried granite—the Marlow swelling under the autumn rains, thick and muscular, pressing at the foundations like a patient animal. The lower road had gone under on Tuesday. By Wednesday morning the boathouse would follow. Mrs. Pell had moved the china up two flights without being asked, and Verity had stood at the east window for an hour saying nothing, watching the water take the gardens her father had planted the year she was born.

And upstairs, in the cold room where he had ruled his family by lamplight for sixty years, Aldous Harrow lay in his bed with the sound in his chest like a clock winding down. They said he would not see Friday. Tamsin, who knew something about mechanisms and about stopping, believed it.

So tonight—while Edmund kept to his study with his ledgers and his locked drawer, while Verity walked the corridors like a sentry who had forgotten which side she was guarding, while grief and greed circled each other down in the warm rooms—Tamsin came back to the clock.

Not to repair it. Not yet. They had promised themselves they would not touch the movement again until they understood what they were touching. For six days they had carried the knowledge of the filed teeth like a stone behind the breastbone: *someone inside wants time itself to lie*, and every hour they spent in this house fed the wrongness, let the great regulator above them tick out its slow, poisoned arithmetic—one lost second here, half a minute there, an accumulating falsehood dressed up as ordinary wear.

Tonight they meant to understand the *why* of it. And there was only one thing in the tower they had not yet examined.

They set the lamp on the workbench and took out the key.

It was heavier than it looked. Old Harrow had pressed it into their palm with fingers like winter twigs, and his grip had still been astonishing—that final reserve of strength some dying men keep for the one gesture that matters. *Make it keep true time,* he had said, *for what comes after.* Then his eyes had gone to the door, and his voice had dropped to almost nothing: *Everything in this house is watched. Everything.*

Tamsin had assumed he meant people. Edmund's careful footsteps. Verity's habit of appearing in doorways. Mrs. Pell's soft, sad knowing.

But Tamsin was an apprentice of horology, and their master had taught them the first rule of the craft before he'd taught them to hold a file: *if you cannot find where the power goes, you have not understood the machine.*

Where did the clock's power go?

Through the escapement, tick by tick, into the hands. Into the dial. Into the strike train that threw the hammers against the bells. That was what the manuals said. But this was not a manual clock. This was Aldous Harrow's clock—built to his own design thirty years ago by a man who had made his fortune measuring other men's hours, who trusted no living soul, who had rewritten his will twice—

Twice.

*Everything in this house is watched.*

Tamsin lifted the movement's rear plate away, laid it on felt with the reverence owed to anything older than oneself, and looked at the architecture of the going train from the inside. They had mapped it before, over four days of disassembly and drawing. The barrels. The wheels. The escape wheel with its three mutilated teeth, filed so subtly that only someone who had cut teeth themselves would ever find the wound.

But tonight, knowing the key, they saw what they had been trained not to see: the space that should have been empty and wasn't quite.

Behind the strike-work, where a decorative pier of the frame met the back plate, there was a small brass boss—a disc the size of a shilling, polished brighter than everything around it. Polished by use. Tamsin bent close. Around its edge ran a fine engraved ring of numbers, sixty of them, and inside the ring, a slot.

A keyway.

Their heart began doing something unprofessional. They wiped their palms on their apron, took the brass key, fitted it home—and felt the mechanism answer. A click, deep in the frame, soft as a knuckle cracking. The boss turned ninety degrees under the key and stopped, firm as a vault door refusing.

Nothing else happened. The clock ticked on overhead, steady, wrong.

Tamsin sat back on their heels and stared at it, and thought about their master's second rule: *when a machine does something useless, look again. Machines do not do useless things. Only their makers do.*

What would turn? What would move, if—?

They fetched the hand-crank from the tool chest, the one used for winding the strike barrel, and fitted it to the boss instead. It resisted, then gave, and somewhere inside the pillar of the frame something began to wind—tight, tight, a ratchet singing its small note—and then a shutter, a thin brass curtain Tamsin had taken for a dust guard, drew itself aside in the hollow of the pier.

Behind it: a stack of discs.

Brass discs, each the width of a crown coin, punched full of holes in patterns Tamsin did not yet understand but recognized in their bones—the way one recognizes writing in a language one cannot read. They were strung on a spindle like the plates of an adding machine, forty or fifty of them, each stamped along its rim with a date. Tamsin tilted the lamp.

The topmost disc read *14 October*. Yesterday.

Yesterday, when Edmund had come to this room after midnight and adjusted the pendulum bob while Tamsin hid behind the tool chest and Verity watched from the stair, and none of them had spoken, because speaking would have made it real—

The machine had seen it too.

Tamsin's hands were shaking. They made them stop, the way their master had drilled into them across seven years of early mornings: *your hands are not yours when they work; they are the work's.* Slowly, with two fingertips, they drew the topmost disc from the spindle and held it to the light.

Holes in rings. Outer ring: the day, punched in numerals. Second ring: the hour. Inner rings, finer: minutes, and within them, deviations—each tick that fell short of true recorded as a puncture, a little constellation of error. And near the center, smallest of all, a cluster of punches that formed, when Tamsin turned it against the lamp, unmistakably, deliberately, the shape of letters.

An initial.

*T.H.*

Tamsin nearly laughed aloud, and the laugh curdled before it left their throat. *Tobias Harrow Pell.* Mrs. Pell's son, who worked the observatory grounds and stoked the boiler, who had a quick shy smile and a limp he tried to hide, who was twenty-two years old and, according to Mrs. Pell's careful silences, in trouble of some kind in the village below. Tobias, whose initials were punched into a brass record of tampering dated yesterday at eleven minutes past midnight.

No. Wait. Tamsin turned the disc again. Not *T.H.* Two letters, but the second was different—a deeper punch, a different punch entirely, and beside it a third mark, smaller, that might have been a maker's seal. They spread the discs out on the felt, all fifty, dates running backward through September, August, July—and saw the pattern emerge like a photograph developing.

Every disc carried the initial of whoever had opened the case that day. Most read *A.H.*—the old man himself, checking his own machine, hour after honest hour. Some read *V.H.*—Verity, winding it, as she did each evening; her discs showed deviation of zero, perfect time, a woman who touched the clock and left it alone. A handful read *E.H.*, scattered through October, each one followed within days by a new drift in the rate, a fresh lie creeping into the hands.

And three discs—all recent, all since Tamsin's arrival—read *T.P.*

Tobias Pell. Filing the escape wheel teeth. Shifting the pallet. Three nights of quiet sabotage, each one written into brass by the very machine he was corrupting.

Tamsin sat very still in the lamplight and let the whole shape of it assemble itself in their mind, gear by gear, the way one assembles an understanding of a movement before ever touching a file.

Aldous Harrow had known. Of course he had known. He had built a register into his own clock years ago—had he suspected then? Had he always suspected everyone?—a witness that never slept, never flinched, never accepted a coin or feared a threat. He had known his clock was being falsified. He had known his death was coming, and that whoever controlled the recorded hour of his death would control which will stood and which will burned. So he had made the clock itself into a witness, and then he had put the key into the hand of a stranger—because a stranger owed nothing to anyone in this house, because a stranger could be bought by no one, because—

Because Tamsin had lied their way through the gate with a borrowed coat and their master's name, and perhaps the old man had seen straight through the lie and chosen them anyway. Or perhaps—worse, better—he had chosen them *because* of it. Because someone who had spent a lifetime concealing their name would understand exactly what a name was worth.

*Make it keep true time for what comes after.*

He hadn't asked for a repair. He had asked for a testimony.

Tamsin gathered the discs back onto the spindle, all except the three marked *T.P.* Those they held, one by one, feeling their small weight, their terrible completeness. Each hole was proof. Each proof pointed at a boy who had not, Tamsin would have bet their tools, done any of this freely. Edmund Harrow had the money, the motive, the cold patience—and Mrs. Pell's son had debts, and mothers, and a limp that kept him from the harder labor that might have paid them down. It was arithmetic. Anyone could do it. Mrs. Pell had probably already done it, lying awake in her room below, doing the sum no mother should have to do.

Take these three discs to a magistrate, and Tobias Pell went to the assizes. Keep them in a pocket, and Verity Harrow—watchful, sharp, kind-eyed Verity, who had confronted Tamsin in the corridor with the truth of their false name and offered alliance instead of exposure—lost everything her father had intended for her, undone by a falsified hour, cheated by a machine that had known the truth all along and been silenced by a boy with a file.

There was no setting on this register for mercy. The machine recorded what was done; it did not record why. Brass did not care about coercion. The holes were holes.

Tamsin thought of Mrs. Pell's kitchen, the bread torn and shared, the story of the dead mother told without flinching, the way the housekeeper had looked at Tamsin's face—at the Ishikawa jaw, the eyes that were not English eyes—and seen only a person worth feeding. *She treats me without flinching,* Tamsin had written once in the private corner of their notebook, *and I am lying to her about my name every single day.*

If the truth came out through the magistrate, it would come out through Tobias. And when it came out through Tobias, it would come back upon Mrs. Pell—her son, her blood, her kindness repaid in a courtroom. And Tamsin would be the instrument of it. The borrowed coat, the borrowed name, the borrowed trust, all of it ending in a boy in the dock and a mother in the gallery and Tamsin Ishikawa standing in the witness box holding three brass discs like coins for the ferryman.

And if the truth stayed hidden—then the clock would keep lying, and the liar upstairs would inherit, and the one clean thing in this whole crooked house, the machine that had faithfully written down every sin committed against it, would go on being forced to perjure itself forever.

Tamsin closed their hand around the discs until the edges bit.

Outside, the river spoke against the tower's foot. Above, the clock ticked its slow false count, patient as guilt. Somewhere below, in her narrow bed, Mrs. Pell was sleeping the sleep of a woman who already knew, or almost knew, or would know by morning—and Tamsin realized with a cold sliding feeling that they could not carry this alone anymore, and could not share it with anyone in this house without spending someone's life to buy someone else's justice.

The lamp guttered. Tamsin trimmed the wick, reassembled the register behind its brass curtain, and turned the key back to lock it—but paused, discs still in hand, unable to put them back. Not tonight. Tonight they would sleep with the evidence beneath their pillow like a thief, like a saint, like both.

As they rose to leave, the clock began the quarter-chime—and faltered. The hammers hesitated, caught on a tooth that had been filed a fraction too shallow, and delivered a struck, stumbling note, wrong the way a voice is wrong when it tries to sing through tears. In the silence after, Tamsin heard something else: a floorboard on the stair below, easing under a careful weight. Then another. Someone climbing toward the clock room, slowly, in the dark.

The steps were light. Deliberate. And they stopped—entirely stopped—one turn below the landing, as though whoever climbed them had heard the lamp trimmed, had seen the glow under the door, and was now standing in the black spiral, deciding.

Tamsin slid the discs into their shirt, against their skin, and blew out the lamp.
