## 15. A Debt in the Village Below

Mrs. Pell had baked bread in the same low kitchen for nineteen years, and she knew to the minute how long it took a man's courage to cool.

It took Tobias less time than most. He had come in through the scullery door at half past ten at night, mud to his knees from the flooded lane, and he had stood there dripping with his cap crushed in his fists like something he meant to drown, and he had said, "Mam," in the voice he'd used when he was seven and had broken her blue willow plate — the voice that came before confession, that asked to be caught.

She did not stop kneading. If she stopped, her hands would shake, and he would see it, and then neither of them would be able to speak at all.

"Sit down," she said. "There's stew."

"I don't want stew, Mam."

"Everyone wants stew. Sit down."

He sat. That was the thing about Tobias that broke her heart worst of all: he was obedient. Nineteen years old and six feet of him, shoulders like his father's father, hands that could fix any wheel or pump or gate within three miles of Marlow's Bend — and he sat down when his mother told him to, because underneath everything else he was still a boy who wanted very badly for someone to tell him what to do.

The trouble was that other people had learned this about him too.

* * *

Three weeks earlier, on a Sunday when the church bells had rung flat because the wind came upriver wrong, Mrs. Pell had noticed the change in him first as a change in his boots.

That was how you read a household, after thirty years below stairs: not by faces, which lied, but by small wear. Tobias's boots had always been scuffed at the toe from dragging, the way idle boys' boots are. Now they were polished. Now they were wet at the seams in a pattern that said he had been standing still on damp ground for long stretches — standing at cards under an awning behind the Anchor, where the rivermen played, was her guess, and her guess was right, though she would not learn how right until later.

She had watched him push food around his plate. She had watched him flinch when the post came. And on the Tuesday she had gone through his coat while he slept — a thing she was not proud of, and would do again without hesitating — and found the paper.

Fourteen pounds, eleven shillings, owed to a Mr. Cregg, of no fixed address but a great many fixed acquaintances. The figure meant nothing to her and everything at once. It was more than a year of her wages. It was, she understood with a coldness that surprised her, exactly the kind of sum that is never really about money. Sums like that were levers. Somewhere a hand was already resting on the long end of it.

She put the paper back exactly as it had been, fold to fold, and went downstairs, and baked bread, and thought.

Here is what Mrs. Pell knew that Tobias did not know she knew: she had worked for the Harrows for nineteen years, through the death of the first Mrs. Harrow and the long grey quiet after, and she had learned to read that house the way she read households — by its small wears, its scuffs and its polish. She knew that Mr. Harrow, who was proud as a steeple and twice as exposed to weather, had been rewriting his papers for a year. She knew that Edmund came and went through the front door like a man who owned it prematurely. And she knew — because the village knows everything, eventually, and sometimes before — that Mr. Cregg drank at the Anchor on Wednesdays and talked too much to rivermen who were paid to listen.

She did not yet connect the two facts. She was a woman who connected facts for a living; it took her only another fortnight, and by then it would be too late to matter.

* * *

The approach, when it came, was almost gentle.

Mr. Cregg himself did not come to the door. Instead it was a solicitor's clerk named Whistable, a small damp man with a collar too clean for his work, who called on a Thursday afternoon while Tobias was out and asked, politely, whether Mrs. Pell might have a word about her son's "difficulties."

"We have no difficulties in this house," said Mrs. Pell, holding the door at the width of her arm.

"Of course not." Whistable smiled like a crack in glaze. "Then I'll leave this. No reply expected."

The paper inside was simple. It offered to settle Tobias's debt in full — fourteen pounds, eleven shillings — upon completion of "a piece of skilled work requiring discretion." There was no name signed. There was a description, in a fair copying hand, of the interior mechanism of a regulator clock, and a note that the work required "a light file, a steady hand, and no questions," and that failure or disclosure would result in the debt being "renewed with interest, together with costs."

Mrs. Pell read it four times by the window. Then she folded it into quarters, put it in the tin where she kept the candles, and sat down to do arithmetic.

She was good at arithmetic. Every mother below stairs is. You add what a boy earns against what a boy owes; you subtract the law, which does not come to villages like Marlow's Bend unless someone is dead; you divide by the number of people who might help, which was one — herself — and you get the remainder, which is what is left of your sleep.

But this sum had a second column, and she made herself enter it honestly. In the second column she wrote, in her head, in the neat hand she used for the household accounts: *Verity.*

Because whoever wanted the clock slowed — and only one person in that house benefited from a clock that ran slow, though she did not yet know the shape of the trick, only its outline, cold against the light — that person wanted the old man's last paper made worthless. And the last paper, everyone whispered and nobody said aloud, named Verity. Verity, who at eleven years old had sat in this kitchen with skinned knees and asked Mrs. Pell why the bread rose, and listened to the answer, all of it, as if rising bread were worth knowing about. Verity, whose mother had died in the room above this kitchen with her hand in Mrs. Pell's hand because Mr. Harrow could not make his feet climb the stairs in time.

Her boy's freedom against that girl's inheritance.

She sat with the two columns for a long time. The fire ticked. Somewhere above, the observatory dome turned on its ring with the slow grinding sound she had stopped hearing years ago, the sound of the house thinking.

And here is the terrible honesty she permitted herself, alone, in the one hour of her life she would never describe to anyone: she did not decide instantly. She weighed it. She actually weighed it — her son against Verity, fourteen pounds against a fortune, the boy asleep upstairs against the girl across the yard — and for the space of several breaths the scale hung level, and she saw with perfect clarity that she was capable of letting it fall either way.

That was the moment she became old, she thought afterward. Not the grey hair. Not the knees. That balance, and the discovery of what lived underneath her, patient, doing sums.

Then she got up, took the paper out of the candle tin, and burned it in the grate.

* * *

Burning it settled nothing, of course. Men like Cregg do not require replies; they require only that you fail to prevent them.

She tried anyway, the way mothers try, sideways and with insufficient tools. She took extra mending. She sold her mother's silver thimble to the pawnshop in Lower Marlow and put the shillings in Tobias's coat pocket one at a time over five days, so it would look like luck accumulating. She spoke to the vicar, vaguely, about "young men and cards," and received a sermon aimed generally at the parish and specifically at no one. She even wrote to her sister in Reading for money, and got back a letter about thrift.

And every evening Tobias came home cleaner, quieter, and more frightened, and she knew the file was already in his hand before he ever told her so.

He told her on the night of the flood-lane, standing dripping in the scullery, refusing his stew.

"Mam, they want me to do something at the observatory."

"Does it have a clock in it?"

He stared at her. Then his face did what faces do when a boy discovers his mother has known everything for weeks — it went through surprise, betrayal, shame, and relief, all four, in about a second and a half, and landed on relief, because he was Tobias, and relief was always waiting for him at the bottom like the ground under a fall.

"You went through my coat."

"Yes."

"That's—" He sat down hard. "I'm glad," he said, in a small voice entirely wrong for his size. "God forgive me, Mam, I'm glad you know. They've got a paper says I owe them fourteen pound and they'll renew it and add costs if I don't do the work, and if I do it I'm done forever anyway, aren't I? Because it's not fixing something. It's breaking something so it looks fixed. It's the master clock, Mam. Up at the observatory. They want the teeth filed on the escape wheel — just two of them, just enough — and a pallet stone shifted, and they've drawn me pictures." His hands were shaking now, big capable hands that could true a wagon wheel by feel. "They know I've been up there delivering coal since spring. They know Pell, T., is chalked on the coals. Whoever it is has been watching me for months. Mam, who hates the Harrows enough to do this?"

Mrs. Pell looked at her son, and made her choice for the second time, and this time it did not hang level for even one breath.

"Nobody hates them," she said. "That's what should frighten you. People do worse things for wanting than for hating." She ladled stew into his bowl and set it in front of him, and sat down opposite, and folded her hands, and told him the truth in the plain terms she had saved for this exact table, this exact hour.

"You will not do the work. Whatever they hold over you, they hold less than what you'd owe afterward, because debts to men like that are never paid off, only renewed. You'll take whatever comes — the court, the beadle, me selling everything but the stove — and we'll stand it. Because there's a girl in that house, Tobias, who sat where you're sitting with her knees skinned, and if that clock lies, it lies her out of everything her mother ever was. I won't have it bought with you. Do you hear me? I won't have my son be the price of it."

He was crying, a little, silently, the way men of her family cried. "And if they send Cregg's men?"

"Then they'll find you here," said Mrs. Pell, "eating your stew, under your mother's eye, and I am a great deal more frightening than I look."

He laughed — one wet, broken laugh — and picked up his spoon, and for one evening she believed she had won.

* * *

What she had not accounted for — what her arithmetic had left out, because love always leaves it out — was the simplest term of all: fear does not need a reason to keep working after the debt is burned.

Cregg's men did not come with cudgels. They came with patience. Over the following week Tobias was followed home three times; a dead crow was nailed to the scullery door; and on the Saturday, Whistable called again with a new paper, identical to the first except for one added line, in the same fair hand:

*In consideration of the family's circumstances, the work may be performed by any party; however, the obligation attaches to the debtor until discharged, and shall descend.*

*Descend.* Mrs. Pell read the word and understood it at once, and hated herself for understanding it, because she had taught Tobias to read with the same primers that taught her, and she knew exactly what a word like that was for. Descend meant: if not you, then your mother's wages. If not your mother, then your mother's place, her character, her reference, the roof. Descend meant the debt had grown a root.

Tobias went up to the observatory on a Tuesday, carrying coal.

He came back having done it, or half of it — she never learned precisely what he touched and what he refused, only that his hands smelled of metal filings and vinegar for days, and that he washed them past the point of skin, and that at supper he ate nothing and looked at the middle distance where clocks must live.

"Mam," he said finally, when the fire was low. "There's someone up there now. A stranger. A clockmaker's apprentice, come to mend it." He turned his spoon over. "They'll find what I done. What then?"

What then. Mrs. Pell looked at the table, scarred by nineteen years of serving, at the candle, at her own hands which had held dying women and living boys and one burning paper, and she gave him the only honest answer left in the ledger.

"Then we hope," she said, "that whoever mends it minds more about the truth than about us. And if they don't—" Her voice did not quite hold. She made it. "If they don't, you'll stand up and say it was me who told you to refuse, and everything after that is mine to carry. That's the arithmetic, my love. Mothers keep the remainder."

Above them, faint through the floorboards, the tower clock struck ten — eleven strokes, though neither of them counted it, though both of them would remember, forever after, that they had not counted.

Down in the village below, in kitchens with their shutters open to the same rising river-smell, other mothers were also awake, also listening, also keeping sums. But only Mrs. Pell knew both columns entire: knew what had been filed, and who had filed it, and who had drawn the pictures, and what the lying hour would cost a girl with skinned knees.

She banked the fire. She lay down beside her sleeping son's wall, where the breathing came through the plaster, and did not sleep.

Two floors above the kitchen, in the tower, the stranger with the borrowed coat was at that very moment lowering her lamp into the opened heart of the clock — and Mrs. Pell, who could not have known it, felt it anyway, the way one feels weather turning: the whole house leaning toward a truth that would either save her boy or name him.
