## 16. Rising Water, Rising Stakes

The river came for the lower road in the night, and by morning it had taken it.

Verity stood at the east window of her father's study with a cup of tea going cold in her hand, watching the water move where the road had been. It did not look like water. It looked like something alive that had been waiting a long time for permission—a brown, muscular thing shouldering through the hedges, folding the tollgate flat, carrying off a fence rail as though it were a splinter it had tired of holding. The willows along the bank stood to their waists in it, their leaves already browning at the tips, and beyond them, on the far side, the village of Marlow's Bend might have been another country.

"Three days," Edmund said behind her. "The ferryman said three days before it crests, and then weeks before it falls enough to cross."

She had not heard him come in. She never did; he moved through this house like a man who owned it already, quiet and certain, his boots making no complaint on the boards. That was the thing about Edmund she had understood only lately: he was not loud the way their uncle had been loud, not greedy in any way you could point at. His greed was made of arithmetic. It was patient. It filed its teeth slowly.

"Then we're an island," Verity said.

"We're a tower with a house attached to it." He came to stand beside her, hands clasped behind his back, and regarded the flood with the same expression he brought to the estate ledgers—assessment without alarm. "The lower rooms will go under if it crests past the 'ninety mark. We should move what matters up today. Mother's things. Father's medicines."

*Father's medicines.* As though the medicines mattered more than the man. As though anything they carried upward would change what was happening in the bedroom down the hall, where their father lay breathing in a rhythm Verity had begun to count against without meaning to—shallow, shallow, long pause, shallow—and where Mrs. Pell sat most hours now like a lamp left burning.

"And the clock room?" she asked.

Edmund's reflection in the window glass did not move. "What about it?"

"The clock room is below the 'ninety mark."

"It's a clock, Verity. It can be rebuilt."

*It can be rebuilt.* She turned from the window so he could not see her face do whatever it did when he said things like that—something cold and quick, some flicker of the girl who had once thrown a slate at his head for drowning her doll in the horse trough. She was twenty-six years old and she still felt that slate in her hand whenever Edmund spoke in his ledger voice.

"I'll see to the moving," she said.

"See that you rest too." And he left, unhurried, closing the door behind him with a click so gentle it was almost an apology.

Verity stood alone in her father's study, in the room where the second will had been signed and the third one perhaps unmade, and listened to the river eating the road.

* * *

They moved the household upward through the morning, and it was like watching a ship take on ballast in reverse—everything the Harrows had gathered in sixty years being hauled stair by stair into the tower's lower floors while the water rose to meet it. Mrs. Pell directed the two stable boys with a calm that Verity suspected cost her more than anyone would ever know; her son Tobias worked among them, silent, gray-faced, moving furniture as though his arms belonged to someone else. He had not met Verity's eyes since the night she had seen him come out of the clock room with file dust on his cuff. She had said nothing. She was saving what she knew, the way one saves coins in a falling market, and every day the coin seemed worth less.

By noon the water stood in the yard. By two it had found the kitchen steps. Mrs. Pell cooked the last meal the lower kitchen would see for a season on a stove that would be underwater by dark, and she cooked it as though nothing in the world were ending—ham and dumplings and apples stewed with cloves, the smell of it climbing the tower stairs ahead of them like a dog that refused to be left.

Their father insisted on being carried up himself rather than wheeled, gripping the banister, stopping twice, saying *I have climbed this tower four thousand times and I will climb it a four thousand and first,* and Verity walked behind him with her hands half-raised and her heart in her throat, and hated him a little for the pride, and loved him entirely for it.

They settled him in the round chamber above the clock room—the old observation floor, with its slit windows facing all four quarters of the sky. The doctor had been fetched across the water by boat before the crossing closed, and had said, in the way doctors say such things, that it was a matter of days now, and that he should not be moved again, and that someone should stay near.

It was Tamsin who stayed near.

Verity watched it happen over the following hours with a suspicion she could no longer fully disguise even from herself. The apprentice—*the apprentice*, the young man with the careful hands and the borrowed coat that fit wrong at the shoulders, whose letter of introduction bore her master's name but whose eyes held none of Master Quill's sixty years—sat by the dying man's chair reading aloud from an almanac, or adjusting the pillows, or simply sitting, and old Mr. Harrow, who had spent his whole life distrusting everyone within arm's reach, let them. Let them touch his things. Let them hear him cough. Once, passing the door, Verity had heard her father say something low and quick, and Tamsin answer even lower, and the tone between them had been the tone of two people conducting business—not nurse and patient, not servant and master. *Business.*

Her father was doing something. Her father, who had rewritten his will twice, who had summoned a stranger clockmaker the very week his heart began to fail, who kept a brass key on a ribbon around his neck now that Verity had noticed and said nothing of—her father was doing something, and he had chosen this half-stranger to do it with, and Verity did not know whether to be furious or afraid.

Both, she decided. Both was honest.

* * *

The water reached the ground floor at dusk.

They heard it before they saw it—a new sound beneath the rain, soft and vast, the sound of a house being drunk. Mrs. Pell went down and came back pale, reporting six inches standing in the hall, rising, and after that no one went down again except Edmund, once, alone, carrying a leather satchel, and Verity marked that too.

The tower's middle floors filled with displaced life: beds dragged into the library, trunks stacked in the stairwell, the two stable boys bedded down by the telescope mount with their boots on. Rain hammered the dome overhead. Through the slit windows the world had become a single color—brown water to brown sky, no horizon anywhere, the village lights across the flood small and few and unreachable.

Verity found Tamsin on the landing outside the clock room, sitting on the top step with a candle, working at something small in their lap. A movement part, of course. Even now. Even with the river in the yard, the apprentice sat filing and fitting by candlelight as though order were a thing one could build against chaos, and Verity supposed—for the first time letting herself suppose it—that maybe it was. Maybe that was the whole of what a clockmaker believed: that somewhere under all the flood and fever there was a mechanism, and mechanisms could be understood, and what could be understood could be set right.

"Tamsin." She said the name deliberately. Not *you*. Not *apprentice*. The name itself, which she had pried out of Mrs. Pell a week ago and had never once used in Edmund's hearing.

Tamsin's hands went still. They looked up, and in the candlelight their face did the thing it always did—closed, watchful, a shutter drawn halfway. "Miss Harrow."

"You should call me Verity. If we're going to lie to each other, we needn't stand on ceremony while we do it."

Something moved behind the shutters. Not quite a smile. "That isn't lying."

"No," Verity agreed, and sat down on the step above them, so that they had to look up at her, which was petty, and she knew it was petty, and she did it anyway. "Lying is telling a magistrate your death hour was witnessed when it wasn't. Lying is a will signed by men who were paid to hold the pen. Lying is—" she stopped herself, because the next item on the list was *a letter of introduction written in a dead man's name*, and she wasn't ready to spend that coin yet either. "Lying is a great many things in this house just now."

The candle flame leaned in a draft from the stairwell. Below them, faintly, through two floors of stone and timber, the river went on drinking.

"My father is dying," Verity said. "You know that better than anyone; you sit with him more than I do, which I confess I don't understand and have decided to stop pretending I do. When he dies, everything in this family becomes a contest, and the clock—" she nodded toward the door at the bottom of the landing, the clock room door, behind which the great regulator ticked its slow, wrong-hearted tick— "the clock is going to decide who wins. You know that. Whatever my father asked you for, whatever that key around his neck opens, you know it."

Tamsin had gone very still. Stillness, Verity was learning, was this person's native language. Stillness and small precise movements of the hands.

"I repair clocks," Tamsin said quietly. "That's all I've done here."

"That's not all you've done here." Verity leaned forward. "I've watched you. You walk past the escapement case the way people walk past a grave they recognize. You know what's wrong with it. You've known since the first week—I saw your face when it struck twelve and counted thirteen. So I'm going to ask you plainly, because the water is in the kitchen and my brother is burning papers in the study grate and I have run out of time to be delicate."

Tamsin's breath caught—she heard it, a small hitch, the first unguarded sound they'd made. "Burning papers?"

"This evening. Alone. A satchel down and back, and the smell of smoke in the flue afterward, and ash in the grate that used to be documents. Draw your own conclusions." She let that sit. Then, softer, because it was true and because true things cut deeper than accusations: "He's destroying the proof, Tamsin. Whatever proof exists that the final will was signed when it was signed—that it was witnessed honestly, that the older paper is void—he is feeding it to the fire tonight, and by spring there will be nothing but his word against a dead man's clock, and the clock has been taught to lie."

The candle guttered. Somewhere above them the rain redoubled against the dome, a sound like applause, like a crowd that had come to watch something end.

"You think I can stop that," Tamsin said. It wasn't quite a question. Their voice had gone thin.

"I think you're the only person in this house who knows how the machine works. I think my father trusted you with something, and my father trusted no one, which means he must have known something about you that I don't—which, believe me, I intend to find out eventually, but not tonight. Tonight I'm asking you as one keeper of secrets to another." She held out her hand, palm up, empty. An absurd gesture. She made it anyway. "Help me. Before the water takes the clock room, before the fire takes the papers, before my father takes his last breath and leaves us all to Edmund's arithmetic. Help me make the clock tell the truth while there's still a truth left to tell."

For a long moment Tamsin looked at her open hand as though it were a mechanism they were trying to read—escapement, pallet, tooth—and Verity watched the war happen behind those closed eyes: fear against something else, something that had been coiled in this person since the day they arrived wearing a coat that didn't fit and a name that didn't belong to them.

"You don't know what you're asking," Tamsin said at last. Very low. "If I open that movement—if I show what's been done to it—it won't stop at Edmund. It won't stop at the will. There are other people in this house whose whole lives are caught in those gears, and I—" They stopped. Swallowed. "I haven't decided what I owe them yet."

*Tobias.* Verity thought it but did not say it. Mrs. Pell's boy, with the file dust on his cuff and the gambling debts that Edmund's steward had been so casually generous about settling last spring—generous, Verity realized now, the way a spider is generous. She had wondered why the housekeeper's kindness to the strange apprentice had such a desperate edge to it, such fierce, protective warmth. Now she wondered no longer.

"Then don't decide tonight," Verity said. "Decide tomorrow. But decide, Tamsin. Because I've lived in this house twenty-six years and I have learned exactly one thing about silence, and it's this: silence always keeps someone safe, and it is almost never the person paying for it."

She rose. At the top of the stairs she paused, and looked back down at the candlelit figure on the landing—this borrowed-name stranger, this half-person the guild wouldn't certify and the world wouldn't see, sitting in a flooding tower holding the truth of a rich man's death in two careful hands.

"He asked you to make it keep true time," Verity said. "*For what comes after.* Those were his words, weren't they? I heard him through the door."

Tamsin said nothing. But their hands, folded in their lap, tightened—one knuckle whitening, a small precise failure in the mechanism—and Verity knew, with the certainty she had once known throwing slates, that she had struck home.

She climbed toward her father's room. Behind her the candle burned on the landing, and below the landing the water climbed too, inch by patient inch, toward the clock room door, where the great regulator ticked its slow false tick in the dark, keeping, with perfect mechanical devotion, the wrong time.
