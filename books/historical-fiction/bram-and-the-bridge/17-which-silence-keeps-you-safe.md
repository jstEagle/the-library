## 17. Which Silence Keeps You Safe

The discs weighed nothing. That was the terrible thing about them.

Tamsin had carried heavier coins in their apron pocket without a thought—brass blanks for the lathe, halfpennies for Mrs. Pell's market list—and never once felt their weight settle into the bones. But these six small circles of punched brass, each no wider than a shilling, each stamped with a date and a set of initials in holes so fine they might have been made by an insect with a jeweler's eye, sat against their thigh like a stone from a riverbed that had learned to press back.

They had taken them from the register three nights ago. Three nights of carrying them up and down the tower stairs, through the flooded lower hall, past Edmund's closed door, past Verity's open one, past Mrs. Pell singing softly to herself over the wash-basin because singing was all she had left to hold onto. Three nights, and still the discs had not told Tamsin what to do.

The tower had become an island entire. The flood had taken the lower road on Tuesday, and by Thursday it had taken the kitchen garden, and by Friday morning it stood at the second step of the outside stair and licked at the boathouse roof like something tasting before it swallowed. The household lived now in the upper rooms—the instrument gallery, the library, the clock room itself, which had grown warm with bodies and candle-smoke and the low argument of people who could not leave one another and could not stop circling the same wound.

Edmund burned papers every evening now. Tamsin had watched him from the gallery landing, the grate light flickering orange across his jaw, feeding the fire page after page in a rhythm too steady to be grief. He did not look like a man destroying evidence. He looked like a man performing destruction, knowing he was watched, wanting to be seen burning things that were not the things that mattered. The things that mattered were punched into brass and sat in Tamsin's pocket.

*You are a clockmaker,* Tamsin told themselves, for perhaps the hundredth time since dawn. *Your work is truth. A clock that lies is worse than no clock at all.*

And then, immediately, the other voice, older and worn smooth as a stair-stone: *A clockmaker who is caught in a dead man's house with stolen evidence, whose name is a lie, whose face has already been remarked upon—who will believe you? Who has ever believed you?*

Twelve years of silences stood up inside them then, one after another, like witnesses called to a bar they knew too well.

There was the silence of the guildhall door, when Master Fenwick had said *apprentices only* and Tamsin's mother had put her hand flat against her son's chest—not daughter, never daughter, she had chosen the word herself when Tamsin was nine and had wept with relief when it was spoken—and walked away without arguing, because arguing would mean saying aloud what the whole town already whispered. There was the silence of the schoolyard, the winter the boys had followed Tamsin home calling out words in two languages, both wrong. There was the silence of the workshop, where Tamsin had learned to let Master Aldous sign their finest work because his name opened doors and theirs shut them. There was the silence of this very house, three weeks old now, wearing another man's coat and another man's reputation like a borrowed umbrella held over a head it did not fit.

Every silence had kept them safe. Every silence had cost something small enough, at the time, to pay without noticing. And here was the arithmetic they had never done until now, standing at the rain-lashed window with a dying man's key warm in their memory: the costs compounded. Silence earned interest. Each quiet year bought the next quiet year at a higher rate, until a person looked up one morning and found they had mortgaged their whole life to a creditor who never gave receipts.

The discs were receipts. That was what Mr. Harrow had understood, dying in his chair while his children circled him like weather. He had built a machine that could not be argued with, could not be flattered, could not be paid off or frightened or asked nicely to forget. The clock remembered everything, and it remembered in brass.

*But Tobias.*

Tamsin pressed their palm flat against their pocket, feeling the edges of the discs through the wool.

Mrs. Pell's son. The boy who had delivered bread to the observatory every market day, who had once, in Tamsin's first week, stood in the courtyard in the rain and helped carry the tool chest up forty steps without being asked, grinning, saying *you'll want the heavy things closest, else your arms'll give out before your patience does.* Tobias, who owed money to men who broke fingers as a form of correspondence. Tobias, whom Edmund Harrow had found drowning and offered a rope with a hook in it.

If the discs came out, Tobias came out with them. His initials were in the brass. His hand had filed the escapement teeth; Tamsin had measured the cuts and known them for a nervous amateur's work, done fast, done badly, done under someone's eye. The magistrate would weigh coercion—Tamsin would insist upon it—but insisting was not deciding, and magistrates in river towns needed convictions the way mills needed water. Someone would hang for the falsified hour if not for the death itself, and the someone would be a baker's son with gambling debts, while the man who had held the rope stayed dry above the floodline.

*So the silence protects him too,* said the old voice, smooth as oil on a pivot. *Keep quiet, and everyone stays safe. Verity keeps her inheritance lost but her reputation clean. Tobias keeps his neck. You keep your borrowed name and your borrowed place and walk out in spring the way you came in, owing nothing.*

*Owing nothing,* Tamsin thought. *That's the lie in the middle of it. I'd owe everything. I'd owe it forever.*

Below, the river spoke. It had been speaking for days—a long guttural monologue under the floorboards, sometimes a mutter, sometimes a roar—but tonight it had changed its tone. Tamsin had spent enough hours beside mechanisms to know when a sound meant strain. The river was straining.

* * *

The cry came up the stairwell at half past eleven, Mrs. Pell's voice cracked down its middle: *the boathouse—the boat's gone loose—*

Tamsin went first, because going first was the one habit twelve years of apprenticeship had beaten deeper than any silence. They took the outside stair two steps at a time, rain hitting their face like thrown gravel, lantern swinging wild. Below, the flood had climbed the stair during the evening; Tamsin had marked it at the seventh step at supper. Now the black water stood at the fourth, moving, alive, carrying branches and a fence rail and something pale that turned and turned and would not come closer to the light.

Verity was already at the bottom, hem soaked, hauling on a rope tied to the boathouse door. The skiff—one of the household's two boats, the one Edmund had not ordered chained—had torn free and was grinding itself against the pilings with each swell, and every grind sounded to Tamsin like the escapement of a clock about to strip its last tooth.

"Hold the rope!" Verity shouted. "It's the only boat—if it goes we're—"

"I have it!"

Tamsin did not have it. The rope was wet through, slick as eelskin, and the boat's weight surged against it in pulses, and the fourth step was not a step anymore but a lip of stone with the river's cold tongue lapping over it. Tamsin went down onto hands and knees on the submerged stairs, boots in the water, and reached along the rope hand over hand toward the cleat at the boathouse door, and the plan was simple: get the rope to the cleat, take the strain off human arms, let iron do what iron does best.

The river chose that moment to inhale.

Later, Tamsin could never say whether it was a wave off the main current or the wake of something vast turning in the dark, but the water rose up the stair in a single smooth wall, silent as a drawer sliding open, and took them.

Cold first—that was the mercy of it, the cold arrived before the fear, a full-body white noise that erased everything except *cold*. Then the pull. The river did not grab; it invited, patiently, enormously, drawing Tamsin sideways off the stair and into the dark between the boathouse and the tower wall, and the rope tore out of their grip, and the lantern went somewhere else entirely, and there was only the taste of the Marlow—silt and rot and the far-off sweetness of drowned orchards upstream—and the knowledge, arriving with terrible calm, that they did not know which way was up.

They had been underwater perhaps two seconds. It felt like a fiscal quarter.

*Up.* Their hands found the rope again—God, the rope, still tethered to the boat, still tethered to the house—and Tamsin climbed it the only way left, blind, burning, boot-points finding the stair edges by memory, lungs folding smaller and smaller. The surface broke over their head and they dragged in air that tasted like rain and coal smoke and life, and Verity had them by the collar, and Mrs. Pell had Verity, and the three of them fell together onto the dry steps of the inside stairwell in a heap of rope and wool and shaking.

For a long moment nobody spoke. The river spoke for them, slapping at the threshold it had nearly claimed.

"You dropped the lantern," Verity said at last, absurdly, her voice shredded. Her hands were still fisted in Tamsin's coat. She did not seem to know it. "You absolute—you dropped the—"

"I know."

"It went into the water and you went after it, you didn't even think—"

"I wasn't thinking about the lantern." Tamsin lay back against the stone, water running out of their hair, their ears, their sleeves, pooling around them like a second, smaller flood. "I was thinking about the boat."

"Then you're a fool."

"Yes," Tamsin agreed. "Probably."

But that was not true either, and lying in the dark with the river roaring its satisfaction ten feet away, Tamsin finally understood what was true, and it arrived not as a thought but as a sensation, physical, mechanical, unmistakable: the feeling of a pallet dropping into place. Of an escape wheel, held too long against its stop, suddenly released.

Some silences keep you safe. Tamsin had spent twelve years learning which ones, cataloguing them like gear trains, and the catalogue had never once been wrong. But there was another kind of silence—the kind that stood on a stair in rising water and said *not yet, not yet, wait for spring, wait for certainty, wait for safety*—and that kind did not keep anything. That kind was the river itself. Patient, enormous, and always, always hungry. The silence that said *wait* was the same silence that had let a dying man's will rot in a lawyer's drawer. The same silence that had let a frightened boy file brass teeth in the dark because the alternative was broken fingers. The same silence that had taught a half-Japanese apprentice to answer to another man's name for three weeks in a house full of people who fed them and feared them and never once asked who they were.

Silence had kept Tamsin safe the way a locked case keeps a movement safe: perfectly preserved, and never once allowed to turn.

* * *

They dried off by the library fire, wrapped in blankets Mrs. Pell fetched without being asked, clucking, pressing hot barley tea into their hands as though warmth could be administered in doses. Edmund appeared in the doorway, looked at the three dripping figures, and said, "The boat?" with a concern so precisely calibrated it might have been machined.

"Chained to the piling," Verity said. "Tamsin got the rope across before—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "Before the water took the stair."

Edmund's eyes moved to Tamsin, and for one unguarded instant something moved behind them—not gratitude, not quite; recognition, perhaps, the reluctant acknowledgment that the strange apprentice had just saved the estate its last seaworthy asset. Then the shutter came down, the cold courtesy reassembled itself, and he said, "You should change before you catch your death," and withdrew.

*He thinks he's won,* Tamsin thought, watching the empty doorway. *He thinks the flood has settled everything. The papers burned, the roads gone, the witness page altered, the old will standing, the clock still lying its slow lie in the room above us. In spring the ice goes out and the magistrate comes and by then it will all be history, tidy as a ledger, and no one will ever know the hour was stolen.*

Their hand went to their pocket. The discs were there, dry, patient, indifferent. Six small truths, waiting to be read aloud.

Above them, faint through two floors of stone and timber, the master clock ticked. Tamsin counted without meaning to—twelve ticks to the minute, steady, metronomic, false. Every tick a small perjury. Every minute an hour stolen from a dead man's mouth.

"Tamsin." Verity's voice, low, from the chair beside them. Her hair had dried in salt-stiff ropes; the firelight made her look older than her years and tired past them. "Tomorrow. Whatever you're going to do—do it tomorrow. Before the water takes anything else. Before he burns the right papers instead of the wrong ones." She stared into the fire. "I've been silent too. I know what it costs. I'm telling you it costs more every day. Like interest."

Tamsin turned to look at her. "You'd stand with me? In front of the magistrate? Knowing what it means for Tobias?"

"I'd ask for mercy for Tobias," Verity said. "I wouldn't ask for silence. There's a difference, and I think—" she hesitated, and then finished it plainly, "—I think you're the only person in this house who's known that longer than I have."

Mrs. Pell, gathering the tea things, paused at the door. She did not turn around. "Whatever it is you two are whispering about," she said, "you'll want a good night's sleep first. And Tamsin—" her voice caught, steadied, "—your clothes are drying by my stove. All of them. Even the ones with the tools in the pockets."

She knew. Of course she knew. Mothers of sons who file escapements learn to recognize pockets that matter.

Tamsin slept four hours in the clock room, on the cot beneath the great dial, with the brass key on a cord around their neck and the discs in a oilcloth wrap beneath their pillow. Twice they woke to the tick, counted it, and slept again. On the third waking, the window showed the grey beginning of a drowned dawn, and the river below had risen to the third step, and somewhere downstream a church bell that belonged to a village already underwater rang seven o'clock all the same, faithful and futile, and Tamsin lay listening to it and thought: *that is what a clock is for. Not to be right about the hour. To be right whether or not anyone is left to hear it.*

They rose. They dressed. They laid the tools out on the bench in the order of use—pinions, pliers, the new-cut pallet steel they had been hiding for a week, the files, the lamp—and then they unwrapped the oilcloth and set the six brass discs in a row beside them, dates up, initials up, gleaming in the lamplight like a row of small blunt verdicts.

One more silence, and the discs would drown with the tower or rot in a drawer or end up in Edmund's grate, and the world would go on believing a lie that fit comfortably in its mouth.

Not this one. This silence ended today.

Tamsin picked up the key on its cord, kissed it once—an old habit from their mother, who had kissed every tool she loved—and went to ring for the household.

"Everyone to the clock room," they told Mrs. Pell, whose eyes went wide and then, slowly, wonderfully, understanding. "Bring Edmund. Bring Verity. Bring the ledgers and the lamps and whatever courage you've got left in the kitchen."

"And what shall I tell them it's for?"

Tamsin looked up at the great dial, at the hands frozen in their beautiful, criminal patience, and heard themselves answer in a voice that did not shake:

"Tell them the clock is going to tell the truth."
