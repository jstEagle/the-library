## 18. The Clock Tells the Truth

The water reached the second-floor windowsills by morning.

I stood at the tower's east casement and watched the river take the garden whole—the rose beds Mrs. Pell had tended gone under without a sound, the sundial showing only its crown like a drowning man's hand. The rain had stopped in the night, which was somehow worse. A storm argues with you. This was the river simply deciding, patient as arithmetic, that Marlow's Bend belonged to it now.

Behind me, the household had gathered in the clock room because there was nowhere else to gather. The lower floors were lost; we lived in the tower now, among the instruments and the smell of oil and cold brass, and the great master clock filled the round chamber like a heart filling a chest. Edmund Harrow stood by the fire with his arms folded, watching me the way a man watches a surgeon who is about to open something of his. Verity sat with her hands laced in her lap, very still, her face arranged into nothing at all. Mrs. Pell hovered near the stair with Tobias behind her—Tobias, whose knuckles were white around a rag he kept wringing as though it might confess for him. And on the table by the window, where I had laid them out an hour ago like relics, sat eleven punched brass discs.

Eleven entries in a register no one knew existed until yesterday. Eleven dates. Eleven small initials stamped by the machine itself whenever a hand entered the escapement case—a key turned, a tooth filed, a pallet shifted. Mr. Harrow had built his own witness into the clock, and the clock had never once looked away.

"You understand," Edmund said, and his voice was level in the way ice is level, "what you are about to do."

"I'm going to repair the clock," I said. "That is what I was sent here to do."

"The clock keeps time as this house requires it to keep time."

"No." I turned from the window. "A clock doesn't keep time as anyone requires. That's the whole of it. That's why your father built one instead of trusting men."

His jaw tightened. Behind him, Verity's eyes came up and met mine—one glance, quick as a spark off flint—and then she looked down again, giving me nothing, taking nothing, our bargain sealed in silence since the night on the stair.

I crossed to the movement.

It rose before me, the great going-train of the master clock, wheels within wheels, the pendulum hanging dead-still because two nights ago I had stopped it myself rather than let it go on lying. Without the pendulum the room felt wrong, the way a body feels wrong when you put your ear to a silent chest. Around the escapement, my tools lay in their places on a cloth: pliers, files, the burnisher, the little brass pallets wrapped in wool. My master's tools—or the guild's tools, or whoever they belonged to; I had stopped being sure of anything except my hands.

My hands. They were shaking. I want to be honest about that, because everything after depends on it. They shook as I pulled off my coat and rolled my sleeves, shook as I tied back my hair, shook when I looked down at the escapement and saw again what someone had done to it—the escape wheel with three teeth filed to a shallower curve, the pallet stone ground a hair too deep, so that each swing of the pendulum released the train a fraction late, tick after tick after tick, minutes bleeding away into hours until the clock ran slow enough to move a man's death backward through the morning and unmake a will with it.

Someone had done murder with a file. Not with poison, not with a knife—with patience, with craft, with the same trade I loved. That was the part that had kept me awake. Not fear. Grief. Someone had taken the one honest thing in the world and taught it to lie.

"Miss Ishikawa." Edmund's voice again, closer now. He had come to stand at my shoulder, and I could smell the wet wool of him, the smoke of burned papers still in his cuffs. "Whatever you found in there—whatever you think you found—consider what it costs. Not you. You're a servant; servants are forgiven their curiosities. Consider what it costs the others."

I did not look at him. I picked up the first pallet.

"It costs the truth its price," I said. "Everything does."

* * *

I began with the escape wheel.

This is work that cannot be hurried and cannot be shown off, and I had spent twelve years learning it in a shop where every mistake was attributed to my blood and every success to my master's name. So perhaps it should not surprise anyone—but it surprised me—that when I set the file aside and took up the graver, the shaking stopped.

Not all at once. It went the way a pendulum goes still: swinging shorter, shorter, shorter, until there was only the point of the tool and the brass beneath it and the small bright world between them. I drew the restorer's edge along the first filed tooth, rebuilding the curve tooth by tooth against the untouched teeth beside it, matching the original profile the way you'd match a stranger's handwriting from a single surviving letter. The shavings came away fine as eyelashes. The lamp threw my shadow huge across the vaulted ceiling, and I thought: *let it be big. Let it fill the room.*

Mr. Harrow had watched me work once, from his chair, saying nothing. He had pressed a brass key into my palm and said, *Make it keep true time. For what comes after.* I had thought then that he meant his death. Now, holding the key's twin memory in my mind—the hidden register, the punched discs—I understood he had meant more than that. He had meant this exact hour. He had known, dying, that the truth would need hands, and he had chosen mine.

The third tooth took longest. Filed deepest, nearly to the root, and I had to braze a sliver of new brass into place and shape it wet with oil, my breath held so long my vision sparked at the edges. When it was done I held the wheel up to the lamp and turned it slowly, and the light ran along the teeth—even now, even now, even now—all the way around.

Mrs. Pell made a sound, small, like a door opening somewhere far off.

Then the pallets. I unwrapped them from the wool and fitted the true pair—I had cut replacements days ago, working at night, telling myself I didn't know yet why—and seated them in the crutch with pins finer than fingernails. Oiled the pivots. Checked the drop, checked the lock, checked the recoil, my fingers reading the mechanism the way Mrs. Pell read bread dough, by touch, by years, by love if I am honest, because that is what it is, loving a thing enough to know it better than it knows itself.

And then there was only the pendulum.

It hung in its case like a held breath. To start it was to start everything—to set the clock running true for the first time in months, to let it begin telling the truth aloud in front of everyone, irreversibly, the way a bell cannot be unrung.

Edmund spoke from very close behind me. "If you do this," he said quietly, "there will be no coming back for any of us."

"That," I said, "is what makes it worth doing."

I reached into the case and set the pendulum swinging.

*Tick.*

The sound went through the room like a stone through water. Everyone heard it differently—I could see that. Mrs. Pell heard absolution. Tobias heard a cell door. Verity heard her father's voice. Edmund heard, I think, the exact pitch of his own defeat, and stood very straight to meet it.

*Tick. Tick. Tick.*

Even. True. The beat of it filling the tower, filling the flooded house below, going out through the stone into the drowned town like a pulse remembered. I watched the dial's minute hand begin its slow climb and I timed my own breath against it, four ticks in, four ticks out, until I was no longer shaking at all. My hands hung at my sides, steady as a mason's. Steadier, I thought, than any master's ever were in this room. Because a master has a name to protect. An apprentice has only the work.

"Now," Verity said. Just that. One word, quiet, but it carried the weight of every word she had swallowed since the night she'd caught her brother in this room with his sleeve rolled back and the file warm in his hand.

I turned to the table and picked up the discs.

* * *

They were small things, really. Brass, the size of coins, each one punched through with holes in patterns I had learned overnight to read—day, month, hour, and the initial of the hand that had opened the case. Mr. Harrow's design, built into the winding mechanism years ago, recording faithfully while the whole house slept. He had trusted no one, and so he had made the machine trustworthy instead.

"There is a register inside this clock," I said, and my voice sounded strange to me, too loud in the ticking room. "Your father installed it. Every time the escapement case is opened, the machine marks a disc. Date, hour, and initial. There are eleven discs. I have read all of them."

"You have no right—" Edmund began.

"I have the right of the work," I said. "You sent for a clockmaker. Here is the clockmaker's report."

I held up the first disc to the lamplight. "The fourth of September. Initial E.H. The case opened at half past eleven at night. Nothing altered—not yet. Only looked at." I set it down. "The ninth of September. E.H. again. Three teeth filed. The tenth. E.H. The pallet ground. The seventeenth, the twenty-third, the thirtieth—" my voice stayed level but something under it was burning— "each time adjusted again, because a lie must be fed or it dies. The last disc is dated the eighth of October. Two days before your father died. E.H."

The fire cracked. Somewhere below, water moved through the house with a long soft sound, like the building breathing underwater.

"Those could be anyone's initials," Edmund said. "Half the county signs E.H."

"Not half the county opens this case," I said. "The register records the key used. Your father's key. Which was kept"—and here I paused, because this was the part that would cost someone, and I had promised myself I would pay my own debts before charging others'—"in the drawer of your writing desk, Edmund Harrow. Locked. And the drawer was not forced."

Silence. The kind that has weight and temperature.

Tobias Pell made a noise like a man starting to speak and stopping, and every head turned to him, and Mrs. Pell stepped in front of her son the way a wall steps in front of a window.

"He was made to do it," she said. Her voice shook and did not break, and I loved her for that, and grieved for her, both at once. "He owed money to men you know, Edmund Harrow, men who came to him and said, do this small thing with a file and your debt drowns instead of you. He didn't understand what it was for. He's a boy. He filed brass because he was told it was brass and nothing else."

"Tobias." Edmund's voice had changed—softer, almost gentle, and that gentleness was the most frightening thing in the room. "Say nothing. Whatever this girl claims, say nothing, and I'll see you're provided for—"

"Don't," Tobias said.

It came out cracked, barely a voice at all. But he said it, and he stepped out from behind his mother, and he looked at Edmund with the gray exhausted face of someone who has been carrying a stone for months and has finally, finally been given permission to set it down.

"I filed the teeth," he said. "He told me to. He stood there while I done it. I got his letters still—he wrote instructions, he thought burning them would be enough but I kept two, I don't know why, I suppose because—" His breath hitched. "Because keeping them was the only power I had."

From inside his shirt he drew out two papers, creased soft as cloth from months against his skin, and laid them on the table beside the discs.

Verity rose. She did not look at her brother. She looked at the papers, and then at the clock, and her face—for the first time since I had known her—came entirely undone and reassembled itself into something fierce and wet-eyed and certain.

"The machine remembers," she said quietly. "Papa always said it. We thought he was being sentimental."

"He was being precise," I said.

And then, because the moment demanded it—because truth left sitting is only another kind of furniture—I took the last disc, the one dated the eighth of October, and I held it up where the lamplight shone through its punched holes, and I read it aloud one final time, date and hour and initial, the clock's own testimony, dictated by gears and delivered by an apprentice with steady hands.

When I finished, the only sound in the tower was the pendulum, striking its true and even count, and beneath it, faintly, the river rising.

Edmund Harrow looked at the clock for a long moment. Then he laughed—short, disbelieving, a man hearing his own sentence read in a language he'd assumed no one else spoke.

"You'll have to prove it to someone who matters," he said. "Not to servants and women in a flooded tower."

As if in answer, a horn sounded on the river—two long notes, hoarse through the rain-washed air—and Mrs. Pell went white and gripped the stair rail, and Verity crossed to the window and leaned out over the black water, and came back with an expression I had never seen on her face: hope with its coat still dripping.

"A boat," she said. "Blue lantern at the mast. That's the magistrate's launch—from the county seat."

Edmund's laugh died in his throat.

"They're coming up the tower stair," Verity said, turning to me, and her hand found my shoulder and gripped it hard. "Tamsin. Whatever happens next—you tell it exactly as you told it just now."

The knocking began at the tower door, heavy and official, and the clock above us ticked on, keeping honest time over all our heads.
