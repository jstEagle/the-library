## 19. Magistrate in a Wet Coat

**Tamsin**

They came up the tower stair in single file, boots ringing on iron, and the first of them was a small square man in a coat so soaked it had given up pretending to be wool and become simply weather. Water ran from his hem and pooled around his shoes, and he stood in it without complaint, surveying the clock room—the scattered family, the drowned lamplight, the escapement laid open on the table like a dissected animal—as though he were taking inventory of all of us.

"Magistrate Crane," he said. "County of Marlow's Bend, by way of forty minutes of river I'd rather not repeat." He set down a leather satchel that sloshed faintly. "Someone sent a signal from this tower last night. Three flashes, blue glass. That's a magistrate's call, and whoever made it knew the code."

Every eye in the room went to Verity.

She did not flinch. "I made it," she said. "From my father's papers. He kept the codebook with his charts."

"You'll be wanting to explain why," said Crane.

"I'll be wanting to explain a great many things," said Verity. "So will others." And her eyes moved—not to Edmund, which would have been expected, but to me.

Crane unwound a wet scarf with the deliberation of a man who intended to be here for hours. "Then we begin," he said, "with the clock."

* * *

**Edmund**

Edmund Harrow had rehearsed this. Not the particulars—no one could have rehearsed a magistrate dripping on the floorboards—but the shape of it. He had spent thirty years learning how power arrived: usually wet, always cold, and asking questions whose answers you had already decided. So when Crane gestured him into the chair nearest the lamp, he sat like a man with nothing to arrange, because everything was already arranged inside him.

"State your name and station."

"Edmund Harrow. Eldest son of Alistair Harrow, deceased four days past."

The words *deceased four days past* he delivered flatly, and only afterward understood what the clock room would make of them—because the clock itself now told a different hour than the one Edmund had purchased. He watched Tamsin Ishikawa's hands go still on the table at the phrasing, and he understood that they had heard it too.

"Your father's death," Crane said. "There is a dispute as to its time."

"There is a dispute manufactured as to its time," Edmund said, "by an apprentice who has been in this house under a false name."

That much was true, and truth was the best timber. He built with it quickly. "This person arrived calling themselves Master Aldous Finch of the Guildhall. I have the letter of summons. The guild will confirm no such master exists under that name—or rather, that the name belongs to a man currently abed with fever forty miles east. Everything this—" a pause, chosen carefully "—this person has touched since is contaminated. Including your clock, Magistrate."

Crane looked at Tamsin for a long moment. Then back at Edmund.

"The clock," Crane said, "has been keeping time since before you were born. It will keep time after we are all silt. What interests me is not whether the repairer used their own name. It is whether someone taught the clock to lie." He opened his satchel, produced paper, pen, an ink bottle he unstoppered with his teeth. "We will come to names. Yours first, though. Where were you on the night your father died?"

"In my rooms."

"Witnesses?"

"My sleep."

"No one saw you near the clock room?"

And there it was—the question with the trapdoor beneath it. Edmund felt the boards give. He could lie; he had lied well all his life. But the discs were in evidence now, punched brass, dated and initialed by the machine, and lying against them was lying against a witness who never blinked and never forgot.

"I visited the clock room," he said. "Nights. To check on it. My father was dying and obsessed with the thing. I wanted it sound for the reading of the will."

"And did you adjust it?"

The lamp hissed. Rain worked at the shutters. In the pause Edmund heard the whole house breathing—Verity too still, Mrs. Pell too pale, Tobias Pell pressed against the far wall as if he could wear through it and drop into the flood below.

"I adjusted it once," Edmund said. "To correct drift."

"Toward slow or fast?"

"Slow," he said, before he could stop himself, and heard the trapdoor close behind him.

Crane wrote. Then he looked up, and his voice lost its procedural dryness and became something almost gentle, which was worse. "Mr. Harrow. You are either the most careless conspirator in this county or you are being managed by a machine. Sit there. Don't speak unless asked." He turned. "The apprentice. Ishikawa, is it? Your true name?"

* * *

**Tamsin**

My true name. Twelve years of borrowed coats and borrowed letters, and now a wet magistrate asked for it as casually as asking the hour.

"Tamsin Ishikawa," I said. "Apprentice clockmaker. My mother was of Kagoshima; my father was English and gone before I could ask him anything. Master Aldous Finch took me in at twelve and taught me wheels, teeth, springs, and silence. The letter named him because I asked him to let it. I needed the work. No guild will certify me, and no house will open its door to a name like mine."

Crane's pen scratched. "You admit the deception."

"I confess it freely and I'd do it again," I said. "But hear this first, Magistrate, before you weigh my lies: I have weighed them myself, every night since I climbed these stairs, and I know exactly what they're worth. They bought me entry to a dying man's clock and cost no one anything. The other lies in this room have cost a fortune, a birthright, and very nearly a boy's neck. If justice must sort us, let it sort us by what our silences did—not by whose accent they wore."

I had not planned the speech. It came out level and complete, the way a movement comes out of the bench after a hundred honest hours, and I heard Mrs. Pell make a small sound behind her hand.

Crane studied me. "The guild's loss," he said at last, and I could not tell if it was mockery, and decided it was not. "Now tell me about the machine."

So I told him. I laid the sabotaged pallet on the cloth where he could see it, showed him the filed teeth under his own glass, explained in plain terms what every clockmaker knows: that a wheel altered by a hair alters every hour it reports. I told him the master clock's strike had been made to run slow, deliberately, patiently, over weeks—that any hour recorded by it in that time was a forgery wearing a true face.

"And the register," Crane said. "These discs."

I brought out Mr. Harrow's brass key and the box of punched brass, and I explained what the old man had built into his own clock without telling his children, his lawyer, or perhaps anyone living until he put the key into an apprentice's hand: a hidden train that marked each disturbance of the escapement—date, nature, duration—into soft brass. The clock had been testifying for months. We had simply not known how to read it.

"Read them," said Crane.

I read them. One by one, into the lamplight: the dates of each tampering, matched against the ledger of the house, against comings and goings, against the nights certain people claimed their rooms. The room got colder as I read, though the lamp burned steady. When I reached the final disc—the one cut the night before Mr. Harrow died—I stopped.

"Continue," said Crane.

"It's initialed twice," I said. "The machine records the hand at the pendulum. Two hands were here."

Whose initials?

One was T.P. — Tobias Pell.

The other was E.H.

* * *

**Verity**

Verity had imagined this night a dozen ways and none of them prepared her for how *small* it was. Not thunder—lamplight, ink, a wet man asking quiet questions. Justice, she thought, does not arrive like the flood. It arrives like a clerk.

She gave her testimony cleanly: the codebook, the signal, the discovery of the discs, the night she had watched from the stair and seen her brother's hands on the pendulum. She did not soften it. She had spent her whole life watching this house and saying nothing, and she found the words came out of her now like water finding a breach—years of pressure behind them.

But when Tobias Pell was called forward—a thin young man with his mother's kind mouth and none of her steadiness—and began to stammer out his guilt, *yes, the filing, yes, the initials, they made me do it, Mr. Edmund held my debt over me, I owed the Ashford rooms sixty pounds, they bought the debt, they said my mother would be put out, they said—*

"That's enough," Edmund said sharply.

"It isn't," said Tamsin.

And Verity watched the apprentice turn to the magistrate with those calm dark eyes and say, in a voice like a mainspring letting down slowly, precisely:

"Magistrate Crane. Before you take that confession as you'd take any other, I want coercion entered into the record. Tobias Pell did not inherit this crime; he was sold into it. Someone purchased his debts specifically so that his hands could be commanded. That is not an accomplice speaking—it is collateral, walking and talking. If this court takes his freedom, then the man who bought it walks away having paid sixty pounds for two ruined lives, and I will not stand in a room where that arithmetic passes for justice."

Silence. Rain. The clock ticking overhead, honest as bread.

Crane set down his pen. "You understand," he said to Tamsin, "that mercy argued in this room tends to be remembered against the arguer."

"I'm already remembered for worse lies," said Tamsin. "Let them remember this one good truth alongside them."

Verity looked at her brother. Edmund sat very straight, and his face was the face he wore at funerals—correct, composed, and entirely absent—and she realized, with something between grief and satisfaction, that he was not looking at the magistrate at all. He was looking at the clock.

He had done everything right, her brother. Bought the debt, coached the boy, filed the schedule fine as a hair, arranged the hour of death to unravel the will. And he had lost to a machine that never slept, never forgot, and never once cared what he wanted. She almost pitied him. Almost.

"Take the confession," Crane said finally, "and take the coercion with it. Both go in the record." His eyes moved to Edmund and rested there like a hand. "All of it goes in the record, Mr. Harrow. Every date. Every initial. Whether charges follow—against whom, and in what measure—is a matter for assizes, and the assizes will have this testimony entire. I don't clean rivers. I report what's in them."

He dipped his pen again.

"Now. The death itself. The hour matters to the inheritance. Apprentice—you've restored the clock to true time. Tell me what hour it *actually* struck for your father."

* * *

**Mrs. Pell**

Mrs. Pell stood at the back with her hands folded, and she watched her son answer questions in a voice that shook, and she did not go to him, because going to him would mark him further, and she had spent his whole life learning the exact weight of things not done.

But when the magistrate asked whether anyone could speak to Tobias's character, she stepped forward into the lamplight, wet coat or no wet coat, and said:

"I can. Thirty-one years I've kept this house. I've seen that boy greedy once, for an orange, when he was five, and sorry for a week after. Debt is not character, sir. Fear is not character. And if the law can't tell the difference, then the law is a clock with filed teeth, and I'd think a room like this one would know what that's worth."

Crane looked at her over his spectacles for a long moment. Something passed across his face that might, in a drier man, have been called feeling.

"Noted," he said softly. "In full."

She stepped back. Beside her, Tamsin Ishikawa—half-blood, liar by necessity, keeper of the true hour—shifted slightly, so that their shoulder stood just behind hers. It was nothing. It was a shoulder. It was everything the Harrow house had never once offered her, and she breathed, and the rain went on.

* * *

**Tamsin**

It ended past three in the morning. Statements signed, discs sealed in the satchel, Tobias released to his mother's keeping pending assizes, Edmund confined to the east wing—"not arrested, Mr. Harrow, merely *untrusted*"—while Crane, refusing all offers of beds, established himself in a chair by the clock with a blanket and the air of a man who trusted no room he wasn't sitting in.

Verity found me at the workbench afterward, where I was putting the escapement back together for the third time that week, because my hands needed work more than rest.

"He'll face assizes," she said. "Maybe not for the worst of it—the coercion complicates everything, you made sure of that. But he'll face it."

"Yes."

"You spoke for Tobias when it cost you nothing to stay silent."

"It cost plenty," I said. "Just not to me."

She almost smiled. Then: "The magistrate asks for you at dawn. There's the matter of the summons letter. The false name. He says it must be addressed 'before anything else is,' whatever that means."

I fitted the last pallet, eased the beat true, and listened to the clock take up its old argument with eternity—one tick, then another, neither more nor less than honest.

"Then dawn it is," I said.

Below us the flood still held the valley, but somewhere in its gray churn the river had begun, very quietly, to fall.
