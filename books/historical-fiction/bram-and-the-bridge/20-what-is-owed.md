## 20. What Is Owed

The magistrate's boat departed at half past nine, and by ten the tower smelled of lamp oil and wet wool and the particular sourness of a family that had just been unmade in front of witnesses.

Verity stood at the long window of the upper room, watching the boat diminish down the flooded lane—because it was a lane now, the lower road gone entirely beneath brown water, the village a scatter of rooftops like islands in an archipelago that hadn't existed a month ago. The magistrate sat stiffly in the stern under an umbrella Mrs. Pell had insisted upon, his clerk beside him with the depositions wrapped in oilcloth. Somewhere in that packet was Verity's future, copied out in a fair hand: the testimony of the clockmaker, the register discs, Edmund's confession given in a voice so flat it might have been dictated to him.

She had not wept. She had noticed that, sometime during the third hour of testimony, and filed it away the way she filed everything—the way she had been filing things since she was nine years old and learned that her father's study was safest entered without expression. *You did not weep. You stood with your hands folded. You answered in fewer words than were offered you.* It was a strange ledger she kept, her own behavior tallied like accounts, as if someday she would be audited.

Perhaps she would be. The town would talk. The town had already begun; she could imagine the sentences crossing counters and kitchen tables in Marlow's Bend below. *The Harrow girl. The old will thrown out. The son implicated. And the foreign apprentice—well, half-foreign, they say, look at the eyes—standing up in the tower and making the clock confess.*

Her father had named her Verity because her mother had wanted a name that meant something plain and hard to live up to. Verity had spent thirty-one years assuming it meant telling the truth when asked. It had taken one flood and one clockmaker to teach her it meant the other thing: going first, when no one had asked at all.

* * *

Edmund was packing.

She found him in his rooms at noon—not because she had looked for him but because the whole house seemed to orient itself around him still, doors left open along his route, voices dropping as he passed, and Verity had simply followed the current of quiet until it deposited her outside his door. He had one valise open on the bed. His manservant—gone now, fled by the last ferry before the bridge closed, which in retrospect told her something about how long Edmund had been planning—had left the packing to him, and he was doing it badly, folding coats into rectangles that would not survive the journey.

"You're staying for the reading," she said from the doorway. "The formal one. Mr. Gorse won't release the estate without it."

"I'll sign whatever's put in front of me." He didn't turn around. "That's rather the skill I've demonstrated, isn't it."

"Edmund."

"What." He said it to the mirror now, working a cravat loose, and she watched her brother in the glass—fifty-one years old, gray at the temples, hands that had held ledgers and instruments of estate management all their life and had never once, so far as she knew, held a file against brass teeth. That had been Tobias Pell's hand. But the order had been Edmund's. Everyone in the tower knew whose order it had been.

"The magistrate asked me directly," Verity said. "Whether I would press charges. He asked me in front of you."

"And you said no." Edmund's eyes came up to hers in the mirror. They were their mother's eyes, which was a cruelty neither of them had ever remarked aloud. "I've been trying since dawn to decide whether that was mercy or strategy, and I can't make it resolve. It's genuinely bothering me."

"It was arithmetic."

"Ah." A thin smile. "My discipline. Stolen."

"Sit down and I'll explain it to you."

He sat. That surprised her more than anything else that week—that he obeyed her, folding himself into the chair by the cold fireplace like a man twice his age, like a man who had discovered overnight that his spine had opinions of its own. She remained standing. Not from drama; her legs simply preferred it.

"Father's fortune is real," she said. "It exists. It will exist after any trial. If I press charges, you go to trial, and Tobias goes to trial, and the town spends a year being fed on our ruin, and at the end of it the fortune is smaller, the name is filth, and Tobias Pell has spent his best years in a cell learning nothing except how to hate us. Whereas—" she let the pause sit, "—if no charges are pressed, Tobias owes a gambling debt to men in the village who will break his fingers over it, and I am about to be the richest woman in Marlow's Bend, and I can buy that debt for pocket change and burn the paper in front of him."

Edmund was very still. "You'd pay his debts."

"I'd pay what he owes *me*. Which is a version of what he owes them, laundered through my discretion." She heard her own voice and marveled at it—it was Father's voice, or near enough, the cadence he used at the toll table when the bridge receipts came in low. "You taught me this, Edmund. All those years of watching you balance cruelty against expedience. I only ever differed with you about where the money should go."

"And where does it go?"

"To a boy who made a stupid choice at twenty-two because a rich man leaned on him." She paused. "And to a housekeeper who has kept this household standing for twenty-six years and whose son I will not take from her twice—once to prison and once to shame."

Something moved through Edmund's face then, quick and involuntary, like a fish under ice. She thought—for one vertiginous moment she thought—it might be grief. Then it was gone, and he was only her brother again, tired and diminished and still, at bottom, a man counting.

"You could ruin me anyway," he said quietly. "Everyone would applaud."

"Yes."

"But you won't."

"No."

"Why?"

And there it was—the question she had been circling all morning, the one the magistrate's question had cracked open in her. Why mercy? She thought of all the answers she might give. That punishment was a luxury for people who hadn't grown up in this house. That Father had ruled by consequence for forty years and died alone in a chair facing a wall. That she had spent her whole life watching people be *made examples of*, and had never once seen an example improved by it.

"Because you expected it," she said finally. "Mercy would be the one inheritance you never calculated for. Let it weigh on you. That's sentence enough."

She turned and left before he could answer, and in the corridor she allowed herself, for exactly four steps, to lean her forehead against the damp plaster wall. Then she straightened, because Mrs. Pell was coming up the stair with linens, and the household had rules even when the world was underwater.

* * *

In the afternoon the water reached the orchard wall.

Verity saw it from the clock room window, which was where she had taken to spending hours she couldn't account for—sitting in the chair her father had occupied while dying, watching the mechanism Tamsin Ishikawa had rebuilt run true. There was something narcotic in it. Twelve years of her father's clocks running slow, running wrong, running to suit him; and here was this machine ticking with the flat indifferent honesty of a heartbeat, each second identical to its neighbor, asking nothing of anyone. She understood now why Tamsin sat with it so often. It was the only conversation in the house that didn't lie.

At two o'clock precisely the brown sheet of the river slid over the top of the orchard wall with almost no sound—a hush, a widening, and the apple trees stood ankle-deep in their own reflections. Below in the house she heard Mrs. Pell calling instructions about moving the stores up another flight, and Tobias's answering shout, hoarse and obedient. He had barely spoken since the magistrate's visit. He had sat through his own naming like a man hearing his diagnosis, and afterward Verity had found him in the scullery with his hands in a basin of water, scrubbing at nothing, saying over and over, too low for comfort, *she's not pressing charges, she's not pressing charges,* like a prayer he didn't believe a god had authored.

She owed him nothing, legally. That was the beauty of her plan and also, she suspected, its danger. What you gave freely bound harder than what was extracted.

She was still thinking about that when the stair door opened behind her and Tamsin came in with the tool roll under one arm and that careful, watchful stillness they carried everywhere, like someone perpetually entering a room a half-second after permission was granted.

"Heard the river take the wall," Tamsin said.

"You've excellent ears."

"Clockmaker's ears. Everything in this house ticks; I notice when something doesn't." They set the tools down on the bench, hesitated, then sat across from her, and for a moment both of them simply listened to the escapement divide time into honest pieces.

Verity had rehearsed nothing. This was unlike her, and she decided to be unlike her all the way through.

"I'm offering you a partnership," she said.

Tamsin didn't move. Didn't blink. Just that level gaze, dark and unhurried, waiting the way they always waited—as though silence were a country they held citizenship in and everyone else was merely visiting.

"The estate includes the observatory, the clock, and the maintenance endowment Father set up in '81," Verity went on. "Which means it includes you, functionally—you've been maintaining it for months without title or proper wage, under a borrowed name, and I'd like to make that theft right. I'm proposing the position of Keeper of the Clock. Wages, rooms, your name in the guild ledger as holder of the post. And beyond that—" she heard her own voice pick up speed and slowed it deliberately, "—a workshop. Instruments. Whatever you need to become what the guild won't let you become by the front door. I can't get you a guild card, Tamsin. I can get you everything a guild card gets a man, and more, and we can make the town swallow it."

Silence. The clock ticked. Downstairs, furniture scraped across floorboards as the stores went higher.

"You'd want the clock kept true," Tamsin said at last. "Permanently."

"I'd want it kept true more than I want almost anything. It's the only monument Father deserves—a machine that tells the truth in spite of him." Verity leaned forward. "Say you'll stay. Say it plainly. I've had enough of this house speaking in code."

But Tamsin only rose, crossed to the window, and looked out at the drowned orchard for a long moment—and Verity, who had spent her life reading faces, realized she could read nothing at all, and that this, perhaps, was the answer taking shape.

"There's something you should understand about me," Tamsin said, still facing the water. "Everything good that's happened to me in this house happened under someone else's name. Your letter asked for Master Okabe. Your father trusted Master Okabe. Even the ledger—" they touched the repair book on the bench, gently, the way one touches a wound, "—the first entry is in his name. If I stay now, today, the day after the magistrate's boat—I'd be staying as a debt paid. As the person who did you a service worth keeping. And every morning after, some part of me would wonder whether Verity Harrow keeps a clockmaker or keeps a promise."

"That's not—"

"I know it's not how you mean it." They turned, and there was something in their face Verity had never seen there: not the guardedness, not the watchfulness, but a kind of sorrowful certainty, like someone looking at a beautiful locked door and knowing they had to leave before finding the key. "You've been generous past all measuring. That's exactly why I can't answer you yet. Ask me again when I've signed my own name in that ledger in front of this whole household—not as evidence, not as testimony, just as a man writing down what he did. Ask me then, and I'll hear you properly."

Verity sat back. Something in her chest ached with a frustration that took her a moment to identify as admiration.

"You're refusing me politely," she said.

"I'm postponing you honestly. There's a difference. Most people never learn it."

A laugh escaped her—short, unwilling, the first in weeks—and it startled them both. Outside, the rain had thinned to mist, and the river lay over the orchard like a held breath, patient, certain of itself, in no hurry to reach anywhere it wasn't already going.

"Then I'll ask you then," Verity said. "But I warn you—I've inherited a great deal recently. I'm becoming accustomed to getting what's owed me."

Tamsin smiled—the small, real one, the one that transformed their whole face and vanished again like a coin dropped in deep water—and picked up the tool roll, and began, with steady hands, to oil the movement that had finally learned to tell the truth.

Below them, far off toward the village, a bell buoy rang once against the flood, and Verity thought: *spring is coming, and the bridge will open, and everyone in this house will have to choose a road.*

She only hoped she'd asked her question in time.
