## 21. The Name I Keep

The magistrate's boat left on the morning tide, and with it went the last excuse for the household to pretend that anything in Marlow's Bend was still the shape it had been before the winter.

I stood at the tower window and watched the wake flatten against the pilings below, gray water folding over gray water, until the boat rounded the bend and was gone. Behind me, in the clock room, the master clock ticked—true now, honest to the second, its escapement rebuilt by my own hands under the eyes of a family that had watched me dismantle their father's lie tooth by filed tooth. The sound filled the stairwell like a pulse. For three days it had been the only thing in the house anyone trusted.

Verity found me there an hour later.

She came up the spiral stair the way she always did now—no hesitation at the turn, no pause at the door she had once kept carefully shut between us. She carried two cups of Mrs. Pell's chicory on a chipped saucer, and she set them down on the workbench among my files and tweezers as though she had every right to be there, which, I supposed, after everything, she did.

"You've been up here since dawn," she said.

"The train needs oiling. It hasn't run true since—" I stopped myself. *Since Edmund put his hands on it.* Some sentences were not worth finishing anymore. "It wants attention."

"Everything in this house wants attention." She wrapped her hands around her cup but didn't drink. Outside, the river had begun its slow retreat, leaving a brown tidemark on the tollgate stones like a bruise fading. "Tamsin. I want to say this properly, so I'm going to say it badly first and then fix it."

"That's very like you."

"Don't." But there was no edge in it. She looked out the window at the drowned gardens, the willows standing hip-deep in floodwater, and when she spoke again her voice was quieter. "Stay."

One word. She made it sound simple, the way powerful people can make enormous things sound simple, because they have never had to carry them.

"Mrs. Pell will stay on as housekeeper—you'll have the whole east wing if you want it, though God knows why you'd want the east wing, it smells of Edmund's pipe smoke. There's the town clock too, the one over the corn exchange; it's been dying for years and nobody in this town knows how to hold a screwdriver. You'd have work. Real work. Your name on the guild rolls if I have to buy the guild to do it." She turned to face me then, and I saw that she had rehearsed none of it after all—the badly-first version was all there was, and it was better for that. "And I would rather not do what comes next alone. Whatever comes next. Rebuilding the estate. Facing the town. All of it."

I let the silence sit between us for a moment, because I had learned from her family that silence is never empty—it is always saying something—and I wanted mine to say what I meant before my mouth muddled it.

"I can't," I said.

Her jaw tightened. "Because of the guild. Because of what they'll say about a—"

"No." I set down my cup. "Not because of fear, Verity. Please don't make it that. Fear I could live with; I've lived with it since I was nine years old and learned which doorways to stand near and which to avoid. If fear were the price, I'd pay it. I've paid worse for less."

"Then why?"

"Because staying means becoming someone else again." I said it plainly, and heard, as the words left me, how strange they sounded in this house where everyone was so exactly who they appeared to be—Edmund coldly himself even in ruin, Verity stubbornly herself even in mercy, old Mr. Harrow so relentlessly himself that he had built a machine to speak for him after his heart stopped. "I came here as Master Aldous Wren. I signed his name on your gate book. I wore his coat and answered to his title and let your father believe he was trusting a man who does not exist. And then the magistrate came, and I stood in this room and gave my testimony, and do you know what I said when he asked my name?"

Verity shook her head slowly.

"'Wren,'" I said. "'Aldous Wren, journeyman.' Even then. Even with the truth of everything else sitting on the table between us like a corpse, I gave him a lie because it was the lie already in the ledger and it was easier." I looked at my hands—my father's hands, my mother's patience in them, the burn scar from the first gear I ever cut at eleven. "I've been borrowed my whole life, Verity. Borrowed by the guild, who take my work and sign it with a rounder name. Borrowed by my master, who is a good man but whose goodness requires my invisibility. Borrowed by this house, which needed a stranger and got one wearing a costume. And I am so tired of being a coat that other people put on."

The clock ticked through four seconds, five, six. Verity hadn't moved.

"So you're leaving," she said. Not accusing. Testing the weight of it.

"I'm leaving *as myself*. That's the difference. It's the only difference, and it's the whole world."

* * *

Mrs. Pell caught me packing that afternoon—not packing, really; I owned almost nothing. A roll of tools. Two shirts. My mother's letter, soft as cloth from handling, folded into the lining where it had lived through the whole flooded winter. The brass key I had not yet decided about.

She came into the little room they'd given me under the eaves carrying linens I neither needed nor asked for, and she began folding them into my bag without discussion, the way she had done everything all winter: bread set beside me without comment, kindness offered as though it were simply weather.

"Tobias walks to Ashcombe tomorrow," she said. "For the assizes. He's pled guilty to the filing—they have his confession and the discs leave no room for argument—but Verity's paid his debt and spoken for him, and the magistrate said coercion counts for something even in a wet county like this." Her hands kept moving, smoothing, tucking. "Two years, likely. Maybe eighteen months if the harvest's bad and they need field hands."

"I'm sorry," I said, and meant it, and knew how small the words were.

"He filed the teeth because a man in a good coat told him his mother would lose her place otherwise, and his mother believed her boy was clever enough to survive anything." She finally looked up. Her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady. "You know what I keep thinking? That clock lied for five months and nobody died of it. But it lied about the hour my employer drew his last breath, and somehow that's worse than any theft. Time's the only thing we're all given equal shares of, and that man stole from a dead man's portion." She pressed the last folded sheet flat. "You fixed it. Whatever else happens to Tobias, you fixed it, and I won't forget that, and neither should you."

"Mrs. Pell—"

"You'll write," she said. It was not a question. "From wherever you go next. Not to tell me news—I don't need news, I've had enough news for one winter. Write to tell me your name. Every time. Sign it full, every letter, so I know it's really you and not some coat you've put on." She closed the bag and patted it once, twice, like blessing it. "That's all I ask. That's the whole debt."

I could not speak for a moment. When I could, I said, "Yes, ma'am," and she snorted—actually snorted, the first undignified sound I ever heard her make—and left me alone with the folded linens and the sound of the true clock keeping time above us both.

* * *

Edmund was in the library.

I had not sought him out. I had, in truth, arranged my entire final day to avoid him, taking the long gallery instead of the short corridor, timing my descent to the hour he took his constitutional by the drained fountain. But the house had other ideas, or perhaps the house had simply grown tired of our choreography, and there he was in the doorway of the library with a ledger under his arm and his father's ring—Verity had let him keep it, one mercy among many—on the wrong hand, the left, where it sat like a key to a door that no longer existed.

"Wren," he said. Then, catching himself, with a grimace that might have been apology in a more flexible man: "Ishikawa."

"Mr. Harrow."

We regarded each other across the width of the corridor. He had lost weight since the testimony; the good coat hung on him now the way my borrowed coat had once hung on me, and I understood with a lurch that we had spent this whole winter wearing each other's condition—he the impostor in his own house, I the impostor in his father's employ—and that only one of us had chosen it.

"I want to say," he began, and stopped, and started again, and I watched a proud man perform the most difficult mechanical operation of his life: the disassembly of his own justification. "I believed I was right. About the will. About Verity. I believed Father had gone soft in the head and that protecting the estate from her inexperience was duty, and I let that belief license everything else. The ledger. Tobias. The clock." His voice did not break, but it thinned, wire drawn to gauge. "You, of all people, must understand how a man can convince himself a mechanism is broken when it is only telling him something he cannot bear to hear."

"Yes," I said. "I understand that exactly."

He nodded, once, as though I had returned a tool he'd lent. "Then there's nothing else. Except—" and here he hesitated, and I braced for resentment, for the cold courtesy returning like frost, "—the repair. The public repair. You did it in front of us, knowing what it would cost you to be seen doing it. Knowing what I'd say, what the town would say. I have thought about that every night since, and I cannot decide whether it was courage or cruelty, and I find I don't care which. It was the finest piece of work I have ever witnessed, and I wanted you to hear me say so while I am still the sort of man who says such things."

"Thank you," I said, and found I meant it, and found also that forgiveness was not required for the meaning. Both things could be true. The clock had taught me that much: two weights, one movement, each pulling opposite, and still the hands go forward.

He stepped aside to let me pass. At the library door I paused, because there was one thing left, and I had promised myself I would not leave it unsaid.

"Your father asked me to make the clock keep true time 'for what comes after.'" I did not look back at him. "He knew, Mr. Harrow. Before he died, he knew what you would do, and he loved you anyway, and he built the machine to stop you anyway, because he understood that loving someone and letting them steal are different duties. I think about that a great deal. What it costs, to hold both."

The silence behind me lasted long enough that I thought he would not answer. Then, quietly, in a voice I had never heard him use:

"Goodbye, Ishikawa."

* * *

They gathered in the clock room on my last evening—all of them, unbidden, which surprised me until I understood that of course they would. This room had become the house's true heart. The drawing rooms were for the family the Harrows had pretended to be; this room, with its brass and its dust and its great slow heartbeat, was for the family they had become.

Verity had the estate ledger open on the workbench. Mrs. Pell stood by the door with her hands folded. Even Tobias, home for one night before the assizes, hollow-eyed and grateful and ashamed, had come up the stairs to stand at the back, as far from the mechanism as the room allowed, as though the clock might yet bear witness against him personally.

"There's a formality," Verity said. "There has to be. The repair ledger has to show who restored the movement, when, and under whose authority—it's how the guild traces liability, and how the estate proves the clock's testimony was given by competent hands. It's been waiting for a signature since the night you rebuilt it." She turned the ledger toward me. The page lay open. At the top, in the estate clerk's copperplate: *Master clock, restoration of escapement, performed [blank], by [blank].*

Both blanks. The date I could fill from memory. The name—

I took out my pencil. And I found that my hand, which had shaken on the flooded stair, which had held steady through the public repair and the magistrate's lamplight and every terrible hour between, shook now. Not from fear. From the opposite of fear—from the sheer unfamiliar weight of writing a true thing in a place designed for true things.

*Tamsin.*

The pencil moved. *Ishikawa.*

*Ishikawa,* full and complete, every letter, the way my mother had written it on the papers that brought us across the sea, the way my father had carved it inside the case of the little watch he left me, the way no ledger in Marlow's Bend or anywhere else had ever held it. *Journeyman clockmaker.* Not apprentice—the guild could argue that point with the evidence of their own eyes. *Restored to true time, this 14th day of March, 1894.*

I signed it in full view of the household, and the nib scratched, and the clock ticked, and when I finished, nobody applauded, because nobody needed to. Mrs. Pell wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand and pretended she hadn't. Tobias lifted his chin a fraction, the way a man does when he sees a debt honored that he thought forgotten. Verity read the name twice, moving her lips, learning it.

"Tamsin Ishikawa," she said aloud, testing it, giving it back to me like something recovered. "Well. It's about time this house learned whom it owes."

"It doesn't owe me anything."

"It owes you everything, and it will survive the embarrassment." She closed the ledger. "Where will you go?"

"North, first. There's a railway works at Calder Junction that hires by skill and asks no questions about faces. After that—" I shrugged, and the shrug felt like the first free gesture of my life. "After that, whatever door opens. I have tools. I have a name. It turns out that's nearly everything."

"And the key?" Verity's eyes went to my pocket, where the brass key rode. "Father's register key. It belongs to the house, technically. I'd have every right to ask for it back."

"You would."

"But you're going to keep it."

"I'm going to keep it," I agreed. "Not as theft. As—proof. That I was here, and that the truth was here with me, and that a dying man trusted a stranger with the only thing that mattered. Someday I'll need to remember that trust is possible. The key will remind me."

Verity studied me for a long moment, and then she did something I did not expect: she laughed, short and real, the first laugh I had ever heard from her that wasn't aimed at anything. "You're the most stubborn person I have ever met, and I grew up with Edmund."

"Thank you," I said. "I intend to take that as the compliment it is."

* * *

I left before dawn, because I wanted to cross the bridge in the dark and come out the other side into light, and because goodbyes are easier on empty streets.

But the house would not quite let me go silently. As I came down the tower stair with my bag on my shoulder, I passed the little alcove where old Mr. Harrow's chair had stood all those months, watching me work. It was empty now—Verity had it in the library, she'd told me, facing the window, facing the river—and in its place someone had set a single chair, plain and new, positioned exactly where his had been, angled toward the clock room door.

On the seat lay a parcel, brown paper, tied with string. Inside: a watch. Small, silver-cased, plainly made, but when I opened it the movement sang—a lever escapement, beautifully finished, and engraved on the inside of the case in a hand I recognized from the ledgers: *To T. Ishikawa, who taught this house to tell the time. — V.H.*

I stood in the cold alcove a long moment, holding another maker's work in my hands, and then I wound it, set it by the tower clock's strike, and put it in my breast pocket, over my heart, where my mother's letter used to live before I moved the letter to my bag. Some things belong against the skin.

The front door was unlocked. Of course it was. I let myself out.

* * *

The bridge stood dripping in the first gray light, its planks washed pale by the winter's drowning, the tollgate open for the first time since autumn. A ferryman's boy was sweeping the near span, and he looked up as I passed and touched his cap, and I realized he was whistling—whistling the noon bells, the new pattern, the true count that the whole town had spent a week relearning. News travels faster than floods recede.

Halfway across, I stopped. Below me the river ran high and quick, carrying the winter away branch by branch, fence rail by fence rail, all the floating wreckage of the season the water had ruled. Behind me the observatory tower rose black against the brightening east, and as I stood there the wind shifted and brought me the sound I had been waiting for without knowing I waited: the great clock gathering itself, the train turning, the escapement releasing—one tick, two, three, twelve—and then the bell, swinging through its full honest twelve, each stroke rolling out over the water and under my feet and away down the valley toward places I had never seen.

Twelve strokes. No more, no fewer. True time, kept by hands the world would now have to learn to credit.

I settled my bag, touched the brass key in my pocket once—goodbye, old man; thank you—and walked on, and the bridge took my weight without complaint, plank by plank, toward the far bank and the road that opened beyond it, wide and muddy and entirely, magnificently unknown.
