## 22. Noon on the Bridge

The river let go of Marlow's Bend on a Tuesday, grudgingly, the way an old man releases a grudge he has held so long it has become part of his character.

I heard the news from Mrs. Pell, who came up the observatory hill with a basket and a face trying very hard not to be a weeping one. "Tollgate's open," she said. "Ferryman says the pilings held. Says a cart could cross by Thursday if the planking dries." She set the basket down on the workbench among my scattered files and said, too quickly, "I've put in a loaf. And the last of the quince paste. Don't argue with me, Tamsin Ishikawa, I've earned the right to fuss."

Three weeks had passed since the magistrate in his wet coat had taken our testimony by lamplight; two since Edmund Harrow, pale and precise even in ruin, had been escorted across the temporary ferry with his accounts in a locked case and his solicitor's letter in his hand. Tobias Pell had stayed—had worked off his fear in honest labor, hauling timber for the bridge repairs until his shoulders looked like a man's instead of a debtor's. Verity had paid the debt in full before the magistrate could name its terms, and had done it without ceremony, the way she did everything, as though mercy were simply another entry properly recorded in a well-kept ledger.

And I had stayed too. Not because anyone asked. Because the clock needed a fortnight of watching after the rebuild—an escapement that has been lied to will run true, but it takes time to trust again, and I am a person who understands that kind of convalescence.

Also, though I would not have said it aloud then, because I was leaving was harder than I had imagined any leaving could be.

* * *

Thursday came grey and clean, the sky wrung out like a washed shirt. I packed in the early light while the tower still smelled of lamp oil and yesterday's fire.

There was not much to pack. My tools, each one wrapped and nested—I had rebuilt the roll twice, checking, because a clockmaker's bag is a biography and mine was thin as yet, only four years of entries. The borrowed coat I folded and left on the chair in the little room under the clock platform, brushed clean, with Mrs. Pell's mended seam showing at the cuff where I had worn through it hauling brass. I would not need a master's name where I was going. I had, at last and at cost, one of my own.

The brass key I kept.

I had thought about leaving it. It belonged to the house, arguably—to the dead man's careful paranoia, his punched discs, his hidden register. But Verity had pressed it back into my hand the morning after the reading of the final will was upheld, closing my fingers over it with both of hers.

"He built a machine that couldn't be lied to," she said. "You're the only other one. Keep it. If you ever meet a clock that's lying, you'll know what it costs to make it tell the truth." Then, dryly, because she was always dryly something: "And it's terrible sentimental value for me. I'd only keep it in a drawer and feel obligated to visit it."

So the key rode in my waistcoat pocket, against my breastbone, a small warm weight of other people's secrets survived.

Mrs. Pell met me at the tower door with the basket I hadn't packed and wouldn't be allowed to refuse. Tobias stood behind her on the step, awkward as a colt, holding my second-best screwdriver which I had lent him a month ago in a moment of weakness.

"For the road," he said. "In case." In case of what, neither of us could imagine—a screwdriver-shaped emergency on the open plain—but I took it and told him the escapement teeth he'd filed were the best work of apology I'd ever seen, and he went red to his ears and looked at his boots.

Verity came last, down the spiral stair with her ledger under her arm, because Verity carried that ledger the way soldiers once carried banners.

"The partnership offer stands," she said. "Indefinitely. I want that in your notes somewhere, so when you're famous you can't pretend I didn't ask."

"You weren't asking. You were acquiring."

"I was doing both. It's called management." She looked past me, up the tower's stone flank to the belfry louvres, and her voice dropped its armor for exactly one sentence. "He'd have liked you, you know. My father. He collected people who could not be bought, and he found so few."

Then she straightened, and the moment was gone into the ledger, recorded, closed. "Go on, then. The ferryman won't wait, and neither will the tide."

I embraced Mrs. Pell. I shook Tobias's hand like a colleague's, which made him stand taller. Verity I saluted with two fingers off my brow, apprentice to master of estates, and she laughed—one short bark, surprised out of her—and waved me off down the hill.

* * *

The bridge in spring was a different animal from the bridge in flood.

All winter it had been a rumor, a black spine of timber visible through rain-streaked glass, groaning at night when the wind came up the valley. Now it lay easy on the water, planks silvered and drying, the tollgate standing open with its arm raised like a salute. The ferryman—no, there was no ferry now; the tollkeeper himself leaned on the gatepost, chewing a stem of something, and nodded me through without a word. Perhaps he knew me by reputation. Perhaps everyone in Marlow's Bend knew me by reputation by now; three weeks of testimony will do that. *The clockmaker's apprentice,* they called me in the town below, except that some of them, I had begun to notice, said *the clockmaker*, full stop, letting the rest fall away like a coat no longer needed.

Halfway across, I stopped.

It was not sentiment, whatever it looked like. It was habit—the same instinct that stops a watchmaker mid-motion when something in the movement's song changes pitch. Under my feet the planks hummed faintly with the current. Ahead, the road ran north along the far bank toward towns I had only seen on maps: Ashcombe, Wetherell, the junction at Cold Harbour where, the ostlers said, a guildhall stood with a clock tower of its own, forty years old, keeping famously poor time.

Behind me, above the red roofs of Marlow's Bend, the observatory tower stood against the rinsed sky.

I turned and looked at it properly, the way you look at a place you are leaving for the last time—not to memorize it but to file it, accurately, in the drawer where a craftsman keeps finished work.

The old man had watched me from his chair that first day while the clock struck twelve bells and thirteen ticks, and he had said nothing, and I had thought his silence was the silence of a dying man who no longer cared. It had taken me a season to understand that his silence was the silence of a man testing. He had been listening to whether the stranger in the borrowed coat would hear the lie. Everything after—the key, the register, the trap laid in brass—all of it had hung on that first held breath, waiting to see what my ears would do.

My ears had done the only thing they knew how. They had refused the thirteenth tick.

That was the whole of my inheritance, and nobody could contest it in any court.

Above the tower, the belfry louvres stood open to the spring air, and inside them the movement I had rebuilt ticked with the particular steady voice I knew better than any human voice I'd heard that winter—the voice of an escapement releasing its energy one honest tooth at a time, asking nothing, concealing nothing, spending itself evenly into the day.

*Keep honest time,* Mr. Harrow had whispered, pressing the key into my palm, *for what comes after.*

What came after was this: a bridge, a morning, a road.

* * *

I reached the crown of the span as the tower gathered itself to strike.

I felt it before I heard it—the faint tremor coming down through the suspension rods, through the timbers, into the soles of my boots, the great wheels overhead beginning their hour. Then the first stroke rolled out over the water and broke apart on the far hills and came back changed.

Twelve strokes. No more, no fewer.

A year ago—God, eight months ago—I would have counted them with my heart in my throat, listening for the extra tick the way a sailor listens for weather. Today I let them fall around me like coins into a purse, and did not count, because counting is for clocks you doubt. I stood on the high plank of the bridge with my bag on my shoulder and the sun coming through the scoured clouds, and I listened to a machine tell the truth over a whole town, and the truth was simply this: it is noon, it is spring, you may go.

The tollkeeper touched his cap on the far side. "Fine morning for the north road," he said. "Where you headed, if it's no imposition?"

"Cold Harbour," I said. "They say their tower clock loses six minutes a week."

"Long walk for a broken clock."

"It's not broken," I said, and could not keep it entirely out of my smile. "It's lying. There's a difference, and it's my favorite difference in the world."

He laughed uncertainly and let me pass, and the road opened ahead, wide and muddy and rutted by winter carts, running between hedgerows just breaking into leaf. A lark went up somewhere to my left. My boots found their rhythm. The bag straps settled into the old familiar bite across my shoulders, and the brass key knocked gently against my breastbone with every stride, like a small patient heart reminding me whose hands it trusted.

At the top of the first rise I stopped once more and looked back. Marlow's Bend lay small and complete in its river bend, roofs smoking pleasantly, fields stitched bright green to the water's edge, and above it all the tower, keeping on.

Somewhere down there, Verity was opening her ledgers on her first proper day of owning her own name and everything under it. Somewhere down there, Tobias was sanding a new doorframe, and Mrs. Pell was putting quince paste into somebody else's basket, and Edmund was learning, in whatever cold room they kept him, what it cost to fight a clock. All of it would go on without me, correctly, the way good mechanisms do.

I touched two fingers to my brow—apprentice's salute, master's farewell—and turned my back on the tower, and spoke my own name to the empty road, quietly, just to hear how it sounded in the open air, unowned, unhidden, owed to no one.

"Tamsin Ishikawa."

It sounded like a clock striking noon.

Then I walked on, north, into the wide magnificent unknown, carrying my tools, my key, and nothing else at all—and behind me, fainter with every mile but never quite gone, the tower kept honest time.
