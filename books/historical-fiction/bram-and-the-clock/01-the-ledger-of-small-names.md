## 1. The Ledger of Small Names

The dead came in on the tide, and the paperwork came in after them, thinner than it ought to be.

Theo Fairweather had learned this in his first month at St. Aldhelm's Royal Victoria Hospital, two years ago now, when he was still new enough to believe that a discrepancy was a mistake and not a policy. A barge would dock at the quay at whatever hour the Navy pleased, and stretchers would come up the ramp in the grey light, and somewhere behind them a clerk at the embarkation office would type out a manifest with a number on it — *forty men, wounded serious* — and the number would be wrong. It was always wrong. Forty meant fifty-three. Fifty-three meant sixty-one. The war arrived at their gate arithmetic-mad, and no one downstream ever bothered to correct the sum.

It was Theo's job to receive the manifests, check them against the admission books, and pass them upward to the Medical Directorate with his initials in the margin. He did this faithfully. He did it in blue-black ink, in a hand so neat that Sister Agnes once held a page to the window and said, *You could rule a plough-furrow by your letters, Mr. Fairweather.* And then, every evening, after the wards had settled and the orderlies had gone down to their tea, he unlocked the bottom drawer of his desk, took out a ledger bound in cheap marbled cardboards — the kind schoolboys use — and wrote down the names the manifests had left off.

He called them small names. Privates mostly. Gunners, stokers, pioneers, lads from mining villages whose surnames were longer than they'd had years. The officers' names always made the lists; officers were tallied like cargo, carefully, because somebody's colonel would ask. But a private killed on the barge between Boulogne and the quay — dead before the gangway came down — might vanish entirely. No regimental number on the manifest. No next of kin forwarded. Just a body wrapped in blanket, an admission book entry reading *unknown male, other ranks,* and then, three days later, a plot in the military cemetery with a wooden cross and a number for a name.

Theo could not bear the numbers.

So he asked questions. He asked the stretcher-bearers what was sewn into the coats. He asked the chaplain who had prayed over which man. He wrote to regimental depots in his beautiful clerk's hand and got back, sometimes, a service record, a mother's address in Wigan or Cardiff or a street in Portsmouth that had been bombed flat. He pieced men together the way the surgeons pieced them — fragment by fragment, working by lamplight, trusting that a whole man was worth the labour.

And into the marbled ledger they went, in order of arrival: *Pte. J. Whelan, 2/R. Munster Fus., d. aboard, 14th inst., buried Plot 9 Row C Grave 11.* Below him, *Gnr. A. Petrie, R.F.A., d. of wounds, ward C, no kin traced.* Below him, *Stoker H. Dengate, d. on quay, effects: 1 pipe, 1 photo (woman, unnamed), 3/6d.*

Three-and-six. A man's estate, entire.

Theo had filled forty pages now. He kept the ledger not because he believed anyone would ever want it but because he could not stand the idea that these men had passed through the world and left less mark than weather. History, he understood, happened elsewhere — in ministries, in war councils, in rooms where men moved flags across maps with little brass handles. Here at the edge of England, history merely washed ashore, cold and heavy, and it was left to small people to carry it up the beach. Very well. He would carry it. Someone had to write down that Private Whelan had existed, had laughed at something once, had a mother in Wigan who was owed more than a number on a cross.

He did not think of himself as brave or even unusual. He thought of himself as a clerk, and clerks, he believed, held up more of the world than generals did, though nobody sang about them.

The evening of the twelfth of March, 1916, began like any other, which is to say it began with rain coming sideways off the Channel and the boiler coughing in the basement like a consumptive old man.

* * *

Ward C smelled of carbolic and wet wool, and beneath those, faintly, of the sea itself, as if the men had brought it in with them and couldn't put it down. Thirty-two iron beds, twenty-nine occupied. Theo moved down the row with the water jug, filling each in turn, because the night orderly was down with his own bad chest and someone had to do it, and because moving among the beds was the only part of his day that felt like company.

"Fairweather." Corporal Hendry, in the third bed, propped himself on an elbow. His arm was gone above the elbow and he had decided, with the ferocious good sense of the young, to make jokes about it before anyone could pity him. "Tell me the truth. Am I in your little book?"

"You're not dead, Corporal. My book is strictly for the dead."

"Pity. I'd have liked to be in a book." He lay back. "My mum keeps scrapbooks. If you're in a scrapbook, you're real."

"You're real," Theo said. "I've the admission form to prove it. Signed, dated, countersigned."

"There's a comfort." But Hendry's eyes went to the ceiling, and Theo knew the shape of the thought underneath: *real for how long, and remembered by whom.* He finished the water and moved on, and let the man have his silence.

At the far end of the ward, near the window, lay the night boat's worst cargo — a boy of nineteen or so, unnamed still, brought in two days ago with a head wound that had opened his life like a drawer and spilled everything out of it. He didn't know his name. He didn't know the year. He surfaced sometimes and said words in a northern accent — *whippets*, once, and *our Nellie* — and then sank again. The manifest had listed thirty-eight men landed; the admission book showed thirty-nine. One man too many, arriving without a story attached.

Theo stopped at his bed. The boy's breathing was shallow but steady, and his face in the lamplight looked unbearably young, the jaw soft, the lashes long. Somewhere there was a Nellie. Somewhere a yard with whippets in it. The information existed; it was only temporarily unattached to him, like luggage gone astray on a railway.

"I'll find you," Theo told him quietly, because he said it to all of them and half-believed it was a vow and half-believed it was nothing at all. "You'll have your name back."

From the nurses' station at the ward's head, Sister Agnes watched him over her spectacles. She was sixty if she was a day, straight-backed as a mast, with hands that could turn a dying man like a page. She had run this hospital's worst ward since before the war, when its patients were merchant sailors with broken bones and bad livers, and she had opinions about everything, delivered rarely and finally.

"You talk to them," she said, as he passed.

"They can hear more than people think."

"That one can't hear anything." She nodded at the boy. "You talk to yourself, is what you do."

"Then I'm in excellent company. He never argues."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth that on another woman would have been a smile. "Matron wants the returns by eight. And there's a barge signalled — due on the midnight tide. You'll have work before morning, Mr. Fairweather."

"How many?"

"The signal said forty."

Theo felt the familiar small dread, the arithmetic already running. Forty would be fifty. Fifty would be more. "I'll sharpen my pencil," he said, and Sister Agnes bent back over her charts, and the rain kept coming at the windows like something that wanted in.

* * *

His desk sat in a converted linen closet off the admissions corridor — a shelf, a lamp, a chair with one short leg shimmed with folded cardboard. The walls, where plaster showed between notices, he had papered with reference sheets: regimental numbers and their depots, the postal codes of every county that sent men through the port, a list of chaplains and which ones answered letters promptly. It was, in its way, a map of England made entirely of administration, and Theo loved it the way other men loved photographs of home.

He worked through the evening returns. Then, at ten, with the hospital settling into its creaking quiet, he did what he did every night: unlocked the bottom drawer and set the marbled ledger beside the official day-book, the one lawful record and the other not-quite-anything, and copied across the day's findings.

Tonight there was only one addition. Gnr. Petrie had died on Tuesday; tonight a depot reply had come, four weeks late, confirming his number, his battery, his mother's address in Perth. Theo entered it in full, ink drying slow in the cold, and under *effects* he wrote what the earlier entry lacked: *photo recovered: mother and child, named on reverse 'Jeannie, do not forget.'*

*Do not forget.* Well. That was the whole of Theo's religion, written in a gunner's hand on the back of a photograph. He blotted the page carefully and sat for a moment with his hands flat on the desk, listening to the building breathe around him — the boiler, the wind, the far-off clink of the night kitchen.

Loneliness, he had found, was not the absence of people. The hospital was thick with people, day and night, more people than he'd ever known in his peacetime life. Loneliness was being the only one who counted a thing. Everyone else saw forty men come off a barge; he alone stood at the quay with his notebook doing sums that ended in ghosts. There was no one to check his figures against, no one to tell. In peacetime there had been— well. In peacetime there had been Elias.

He did not let himself think the rest of it. Two years of practice had built a door in his mind and he had learned to close it gently rather than slam it, because slammed doors made noise in the memory.

Elias Bram, Captain, 9th Battalion, missing presumed dead, autumn 1914. Theo had written the confirmation letter himself, at the depot's request, because his hand was trusted — his beautiful furrow-straight hand — and he had written *regret to inform* in ink he did not remember dipping the pen into. Somewhere in an officer's file in London was a copy of that letter, and somewhere in Theo's bottom drawer, folded into the back cover of the marbled ledger, was a much older piece of paper: a menu card from the Crown Hotel, August 1911, scrawled on the blank side in Elias's looping, unmistakable script.

He had not read it in months. He did not read it now. He simply knew it was there, the way you know the tide is there behind the sea wall, and he locked the drawer, and the clock over the corridor door said eleven-forty, and the midnight barge was coming.

* * *

They heard it before they saw it — the change in the harbour's sound, water slapping stone in a new rhythm, engines working low. Theo pulled on his coat and went out into the rain with the stretcher parties, down the slip road to the quay, because he always went; because the manifests were born here, wrong from the first breath, and he liked to catch them early.

The quay lamps burned in cones of rain. The barge came alongside with a grinding of fenders, and the ramp came down, and the stretchers began.

Theo stood at the head with his notebook, tallying as they passed. Twelve. Twenty. Twenty-six. The bearers' boots rang on wet stone. The wounded were quiet, mostly — men this exhausted don't cry out — faces grey under the lamps, blankets steaming faintly where the rain hit blood-warm wool. Thirty-one. Thirty-five.

Thirty-nine.

Forty, said the signal. Forty, said the embarkation officer, meeting them at the ramp foot with his clipboard and his mackintosh and his air of a man doing sums about other people's lives. "Forty landed, all accounted for," he said. "Sign here, Mr.—"

"Fairweather." Theo signed. He always signed; refusing accomplished nothing and slowed the men getting indoors. Then he stepped past the officer, as he always did, and walked the line of stretchers waiting to go up to the wards, counting bodies against the count.

Thirty-nine.

He counted again, walking slowly, rain running off his hat brim onto the open page. Thirty-nine men, and one stretcher empty except for its blankets, folded back neat as you please — and one bearer, a Navy rating, standing oddly apart, looking at Theo instead of at his burden-strap.

"Where's your forty?" Theo asked him.

The rating didn't answer at once. Up the slope, the last stretchers were going into the lighted doorway of the receiving hall, and the rain filled the pause with static. Then the man said, in a voice roughened by salt and something else, "There's a coat."

"A coat?"

"He said—" The rating stopped, glanced back at the barge, at the dark water beyond it, as if the sea might be taking notes. He lowered his voice. "He said to find the clerk. The one who writes things down."

The rain came harder. Down the channel, invisible, the tide was turning, and somewhere out in the black water the morning's ship was already thinking about sailings.

"In the coat," the rating said, "there's a letter. Addressed to you."

Theo stood very still on the wet stones, notebook open in his hand, forty names' worth of arithmetic dissolving behind his eyes — and heard himself say, in a voice that did not feel like his own:

"What coat? Whose coat?"
