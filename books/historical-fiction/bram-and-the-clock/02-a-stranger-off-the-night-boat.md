## 2. A Stranger Off the Night Boat

The night boat came in late, which was the first wrong thing.

I knew its schedule the way other men knew their prayers — the eleven-forty from the opposite coast, lights doused, running dark through the mine-channels, unloading her wounded and her dead before the tide could think about turning. By half past midnight I would have my ledger open, my pen uncapped, and Sister Agnes would have the ward lamps trimmed low so the men who came off the gangway wouldn't see how many of them there were. That was the order of things. The war had taken nearly everything from us in this town, but it had not yet taken the schedule.

So when the bell in the signal tower rang at ten past two, I put down my pen mid-stroke and left a name half-written on the page — *Pvt. R. Hollis, left leg* — and felt the small violence of that interruption more sharply than I should have. A clerk learns to guard his lines the way a surgeon guards his incisions.

"Theo." Private Wren, one of the stretcher parties, stood in the doorway of my records room with rain on his shoulders and something in his face I hadn't seen before. Not fear exactly. Confusion wearing fear's coat. "Boat's in. But she wasn't meant to be. And she's brought us a man nobody signed for."

"Nobody signed for him?"

"No papers. No kit. No unit." He rubbed the back of his neck. "Half drowned when they pulled him off the quay. He went into the water, they say. Walked straight off the edge like he intended to."

*Intended to.* I took my coat and followed him out into the wet dark, and I remember thinking — with the part of my mind that never stops filing, the part that keeps the ledger even when my hands are empty — that a man who walks into the Channel in November has made his decision, and our job is only to write down the result.

The quay was chaos of the wrong kind. Orderly chaos I could have managed; I had learned to read a casualty landing the way a farmer reads weather. But this was disorder — the boat's crew refusing to answer questions, the pier master shouting about manifests that didn't exist, a military policeman waving his lantern at crates that had no labels. And at the center of it, on a stretcher no one seemed to be carrying anymore because everyone had decided someone else's job, lay the man.

He looked dead already. That is a thing clerks are not supposed to say, but we think it constantly, and we are right often enough that the thought has earned its keep. His lips were grey-blue. Water had come out of him twice on the stones, and his clothes — civilian clothes, bad ones, the kind of coat you buy second-hand from a man who bought it second-hand from a corpse — were soaked through and stiffening with salt. No identification disc. No paybook. Nothing but the man himself and the sea's refusal of him.

"Who found him?" I asked.

"Harbour watch. He was face-down by the ladder. They thought he'd gone under for good, but he hadn't." Wren hesitated. "There's something else, sir. He wouldn't let go of his coat. Unconscious, mind. They tried to cut it off him and he came round fighting like a mad thing. Cracked a man across the jaw. Then he went under again, and his hands were still locked in the cloth."

I crouched beside the stretcher. Up close he smelled of salt and bilge and something beneath both — fever, perhaps, or just the particular exhaustion of a body that has been running longer than bodies are built to run. He was young. Younger than me, probably. His hands were ruined, the knuckles split and re-split, the nails broken to the quick, and I remember noticing that the ruin was old — weeks old, months — the hands of a man who had been doing hard work in hard places long before he ever met the Channel.

"Get him inside," I said. "Annex bed. Don't log him yet."

Wren gave me a look. *Don't log him yet* was against everything I stood for, and he knew it. "Not log him?"

"He'll have papers somewhere. We'll wait until we know what we're writing." Even as I said it, I heard how thin the excuse was. The truth was simpler and stranger: some instinct, the same instinct that had taught me which names mattered and which names got lost, told me that whatever I wrote about this man first would follow him. And he had arrived with nothing written. It seemed suddenly important to preserve that.

We carried him up through the receiving doors, past Ward C where the night's proper cargo was already laid out in rows — the mangled procession I had walked a hundred times without flinching — and into the annex, which was really an old mortuary overflow room, cold-tiled, rarely used since the new wing opened. We got his coat off him at last, once his hands had been pried loose, and laid him under three blankets with hot bottles at his feet, and Agnes began working on him with the brisk mercy she spent on every man who came through her doors, as if each one were a son she had decided in advance not to lose.

It was while we turned him — to get the wet shirt from under his back — that the coat made its sound.

A seam giving way. That was all. A small tearing whisper of thread, and then something sliding inside the lining of the collar, down along the sleeve, and landing with a soft weight on the tile floor.

A letter. Oilskin-wrapped, tied with waxed cord, flat as a hymn book.

Agnes picked it up and held it out to me without a word, because there was nothing to say. Letters sewn into coats belonged to spies and smugglers, to men who expected to be searched. I turned it over under the lamp, and the oilskin was worn soft at the corners from travel — real travel, months of it, carried against a body through places I did not want to imagine.

My name was written on the front.

Not *T. Fairweather, Records Clerk.* Not any address. Just: **Theo** — in a hand I would have known if I had seen it written in steam on a window, in sand, in spilt flour on a table. The tall, leaning letters. The *T* crossed with a single impatient stroke. The little hook on the *o* at the end, like the tail of a comma, like a signature trailing off into something it never said.

Elias Bram's hand.

I want to be honest here, because honesty is the whole reason I ever kept the ledger at all: I did not believe it. Belief would have required accepting too many impossibilities at once, and my mind, trained on facts and columns, simply refused. Bram was dead. Bram had been dead for two years — officially, formally, irrevocably dead, with a notice in the gazette and a memorial service in the garrison chapel and a line in somebody else's records closing his account. Dead men do not write letters. Dead men especially do not write letters addressed to hospital clerks in Channel ports, sewn into the coats of strangers who walk into the sea.

And yet my hand — my own hand — was shaking so badly that I could not work the knot in the waxed cord. Agnes took it from me, cut it with her scissors, and pressed the letter back into my palms, and said, in her flat Scottish way, "Read it in your own room, lad. Not here."

But I couldn't wait. I broke the fold and read it standing over that unconscious stranger, with the lamp hissing and the tide muttering against the sea wall, and what it said I will carry to my grave, though the paper itself did not survive the year:

*Theo —*

*I don't know if this reaches you, or in what state, or whether you will burn it, which would be sensible. But we made a promise once, in peacetime, when promises were cheap. I am calling it in.*

*If a man comes to you carrying this, his name is Anselm. Trust him as you would trust me. Do not write his name down anywhere. Do not tell anyone, however kind, however trustworthy, especially anyone in uniform. Above all, whatever you hear about me in the meantime — believe the opposite.*

*Believe the opposite, Theo. That is all I ask. That is the whole of the promise.*

*— E.*

I read it three times. The words did not rearrange themselves into sense. *Believe the opposite.* The opposite of what? Of the official story, whatever that was — I realized with a cold turn of the stomach that I did not even know the official story, that Bram had died at a place whose name was never given, in circumstances never described, and that I had mourned him the way I mourned everyone the war took: privately, briefly, and in ink.

On the table the stranger stirred. A sound came out of him — cracked, briny, barely a word at all — and Agnes leaned down with her ear to his mouth the way she bent over dying men, patient as stone.

"What is it?" I asked. "What's he saying?"

She listened. Her brow folded. She listened again, and then she straightened and looked at me with an expression I had never seen on her face in four years of wards and wounds and last breaths — an expression almost of apology, as if she were sorry to be the one handing me something so heavy.

"One word," she said. "Over and over. Since we took the coat off him." She glanced down at him, this man with no name and no country and no papers, who had swum the coldest sea in Europe with another man's letter stitched against his neck. "He keeps saying *alive.*"

The lamp hissed. The tide turned below the wall. And somewhere out in the dark water, I understood, a door had opened that I would spend the rest of this story trying not to drown in.

"*Alive,*" Agnes repeated softly. "Whoever he means, Mr. Fairweather — he's telling someone that they're alive."

I looked down at the letter in my hand, at the leaning letters, at the little hooked *o* trailing off into everything it never said, and I heard myself ask the question I already knew the answer to, the way a clerk reads back a figure he knows is wrong:

"Alive. Who?"
