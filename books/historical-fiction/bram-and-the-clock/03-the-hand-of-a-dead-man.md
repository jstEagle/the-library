## 3. The Hand of a Dead Man

The letter was still warm.

Not from the fire — there had been no fire — but from the man's body. It had spent the crossing sewn against his chest, and now it lay in Theo's palm carrying that heat like a borrowed thing, like something alive that shouldn't be. Theo sat on the edge of the linen cupboard where he'd retreated to open it, knees up, the door wedged shut with a mop handle, and he did not move for a long time.

He knew the hand before he knew the words. That was the terrible part. Two years of official death and grief and a small grave-shaped silence in his chest, and his eyes still went to the paper and said, *him*, before his mind could object. The long ascenders. The way Bram crossed his *t*s with a single impatient stroke, like a man waving off an objection before it was raised. The ink pressed hard enough to dent the page from behind — you could read the letter with your fingertips if your eyes refused.

*Theo.*

Just that, first. His name alone on the line, as if the rest were an afterthought. As if Bram had stood at a table somewhere — where? a cellar? a ship's cabin? — and written the name first and then not known how to go on.

*I don't know what they've told you, so I'll tell it plain. I am not dead. I have been many things these two years, most of them things I am not proud of, but dead is not one of them. If you are reading this then Anselm reached you, which means I can trust you with what follows, because no one reaches Theo Fairweather by accident. You keep too careful a door.*

Theo laughed. It came out wrong, a dry click in a throat gone tight, and he put his fist against his mouth until it passed. Around him the cupboard smelled of starch and carbolic and the ghost of last winter's influenza, the ordinary smells of the ordinary world, and none of it made sense anymore. None of it could sit next to the handwriting.

*They will have told you I defected. That I crossed over and took their pay and turned my coat. Some of that is even true in its way, which is why it works. But the men whose names are missing from your lists — you keep the lists, Theo, I know you do, you always kept everything, you kept my letters and my bad poetry and God knows what else — those men are not missing. They are alive. Every one of them that we could reach. And if you ever want to know how, or why, or what it cost, then do for me what I cannot do for myself.*

*You promised me once, in another life, that if I ever needed to disappear you would make it so. I need the opposite now. I need to come home. I need someone on this side of the water who remembers who I was before the war decided for both of us. I need you to be the record, because I can't be. Records survive. Men don't.*

*Burn nothing. Tell no one. Trust Sister Agnes if you must trust anyone, and trust no one else.*

*If you've forgotten me, burn this and I will understand. If you haven't —*

The sentence stopped there. No signature. Just the space where a signature should be, and then, lower down the page, smaller, as though written later when the lamp was guttering:

*E.B. Ask him about the forty-third.*

The paper trembled. Theo realized it was his own hand doing the trembling, and he set the letter flat on his knee and pressed it down and breathed the way Sister Agnes had taught the young orderlies in '14: in for four, hold, out for four. In. Hold. Out. The war outside went on regardless — a gull screaming off the harbour wall, a stretcher party's boots on gravel, the far-off flat cough of guns across the water that everyone in the town had learned to hear without hearing.

Two years. Two years since the telegram with its printed condolences and its regretted-to-inform. Two years since he'd stood in his landlady's parlour holding a piece of government stationery and understood, in some wordless animal way, that the world had been divided into two kinds of people: the ones history happened to, and the ones who wrote it down. He had assumed, without ever saying it aloud, that he would always be the second kind.

And now a dead man's handwriting said otherwise.

* * *

When he finally came out of the cupboard, the ward had settled into its night rhythm — lamps turned low, moaning reduced to breathing, one man near the window murmuring a woman's name on a loop, *Edith, Edith,* like a rosary. Theo checked him, adjusted his blanket, wrote nothing in the ledger, moved on. His hands worked. That was the mercy of hospital work; it asked only that your hands know their business while the rest of you was elsewhere.

Elsewhere was a linen cupboard and a letter and a question with teeth: *Ask him about the forty-three.* Forty-three what? Men? Names? He thought of his ledger, the small black book locked in his footlocker under his cot, three years of names the officers' reports couldn't be bothered with — privates and gunners and stokers, boys from Leeds and Cardiff and a farm outside Cork, men whose mothers wrote asking and got back form letters. He had never counted them. He should count them. The thought arrived whole and cold and he pushed it away, because counting them meant something, meant the letter knew about the ledger somehow, and that was a door he wasn't ready to open in a corridor where anyone might walk past.

Anselm was where they'd left him: the far cot by the sluice room, screened off, charted under a false name Theo had invented on the spot — *A. Norton, no regiment, found drowned, harbour wall.* The lie had come out of his pen smoothly, frighteningly smoothly, and he had told himself it was administrative tidiness, nothing more. Keep the paperwork consistent. That was all. That was a thing a clerk could believe.

The man lay very still. They'd stripped the soaked uniform off him — Sergeant's stripes, no visible insignia beyond that, the cloth salt-stiffened and reeking of the Channel — and Theo had watched the matron's face as she catalogued the wounds: shrapnel along the ribs, half-healed, dressed badly with someone's improvised kit; a bullet graze above the ear, crusted; feet ruined. Not fresh wounds. Weeks old. This man had been walking a long time on feet like that, and every mile of it had been toward this port, this ward, this clerk.

Sister Agnes appeared at Theo's elbow without sound, as she always did, a grey wraith with forearms like a dockworker and eyes that had seen everything twice.

"He'll live," she said. "If infection doesn't take the feet. Who is he?"

"A. Norton." Theo heard his own voice deliver the lie again and hated how steady it sounded. "No papers."

"Mm." She looked at him for a moment longer than the answer deserved. Thirty years of nursing gives a woman instruments more sensitive than anything in the surgical theatre. "There's a constable been round asking after a body washed up by the east pier. I told him we'd had nothing. Was that correct?"

Theo's mouth went dry. "Yes, Sister."

"Good." She turned to go, then paused. "Whatever's sewn into a coat comes out in my theatre or nowhere, Mr. Fairweather. I saw the stitching. I've been mending sailors' coats since before you were born, and nobody stitches a letter into a lining unless it's worth hanging for." She said it the way she said everything — flatly, without drama — and left him standing in the lamplight with his heart going like a triage drum.

* * *

It was near two in the morning when Anselm woke.

Theo was writing up charts at the duty desk, pretending the work required concentration, when the sound came — not a cry, just a change in the breathing from behind the screen, the wet ragged pull of a man surfacing from somewhere deep. Theo was around the screen before he'd decided to move.

Grey eyes, fever-bright, fixed on him with a focus that had nothing of confusion in it. This man woke knowing exactly where he was and exactly whom he'd come to find. His lips cracked apart. Theo leaned close, close enough to smell salt and sickness.

"Fairweather," Anselm whispered.

"Yes. I'm here."

"You're — " A swallow. Pain crossed the ruined face like weather. "You're the clerk. He said — the one who keeps the names."

"I keep the ward records," Theo said carefully.

Something moved at the corner of the man's mouth. Almost a smile. Almost amusement, from a man with holes in him. "That's what he said you'd say." A cough took him then, deep and ugly, and Theo held the basin and waited through it, and when it passed Anselm's hand shot out and gripped Theo's wrist with a strength that seemed impossible in the wasted arm. His fingers were ice.

"The letter," he rasped.

"I have it."

"Read it back to me. The end. The last part."

"*E.B.,*" Theo recited, low. "*Ask him about the forty-three.*"

Anselm closed his eyes. Exhaled. Something in him unclenched — some tension held so long it had become architecture, and now came down all at once. When he spoke again the whisper had changed register. It was almost peaceful.

"Then it's done. Then I got through." His grip tightened. "Tell him — no. Don't tell him yet. First you have to say it. You have to say the promise, out loud, so I can carry it back to him. He won't believe me otherwise. He'll think I bought my way through and forged the rest. Say it."

Theo's pulse hammered in his ears. The ward breathed around them. Somewhere a clock in the matron's office ticked with idiot regularity, marking out the ordinary hours of an extraordinary night.

"I don't know what you mean," Theo said. "What promise?"

But he did know. Even as he said it he felt the memory rising like water into a lock — a summer evening four years ago, grass, cheap wine, Bram lying on his back on the downs above the town with his cap over his face, laughing at nothing, twenty-six years old and invincible. *If either of us ever needs to disappear, the other makes it so. No questions, no permission sought. Sworn.* They'd sworn it with linked little fingers like schoolboys and then lain there laughing at themselves, and Theo had gone home that night warm and foolish and had never once, in four years of war, thought of it seriously. Promises made in peacetime weren't real. They were decorations. Everyone knew that.

Everyone except, apparently, Elias Bram.

"The summer before the war," Theo said slowly. "The downs above the town. We — "

"Yes," Anselm breathed. "Say it. All of it."

Theo looked at the door. Looked at the screen. And then, in a voice barely louder than the ticking clock, he said it: "If either of us ever needs to disappear, the other makes it so. No questions. No permission sought."

Anselm's eyes filled — not with tears exactly, more with exhaustion finding its level. "Sworn," he echoed.

"Sworn."

"Then he's right about you." The grip on Theo's wrist loosened, slid, fell back to the blanket. The fever was pulling the man under again, dragging him down in stages. "Listen — listen — there are more letters. Coat. Other pocket. Names. He said you'd know what — what they're for —"

"Rest. They'll keep till morning."

"No." The eyes opened again, fierce, and for a second Theo saw the soldier underneath the wreck, the man who had walked miles on destroyed feet. "Not till morning. Corvane's coming."

The name landed like ice water. "Who's Corvane?"

"Intelligence." Anselm's whisper was fraying at the edges. "He knows a ghost is moving along the coast. He doesn't know which port yet. Days. Maybe less." The eyelids dragged down. Fought up one last time. "And Fairweather —"

"Yes?"

"He made me promise. If I got through, the first thing — " A breath. The last of his strength gathering itself for one word, and Theo bent closer, closer, until his ear nearly touched the man's cracked lips —

"Alive. Tell him — you know. Tell him you know he's —"

The sentence never landed. Anselm's eyes rolled, his head turned, and he was gone — not dying, the breathing stayed strong and deep, but swallowed whole by the fever, by the weeks of it, by whatever ocean he'd been swimming since before he ever boarded the night boat. His hand twitched once on the blanket and stilled.

Theo straightened up. The ward breathed. The clock ticked. Outside, far down the coast, the guns went on with their idiot patience.

Alive. The word sat in Theo's chest like a swallowed coal. Two years of a grave-shaped silence, and one syllable had come along and lit a fire inside it. He wanted to laugh. He wanted to be sick. He wanted to run down to the harbour wall and shout it at the black water — *he's alive, you bastards, you wrote him off and he's alive* — and he wanted, simultaneously, with equal force, to burn the letter unread and go back to being the man who wrote down other people's histories, safely, at a desk, on the far side of the glass.

Disbelief and hope, and between them no referee. Only a promise, sworn on a hillside in peacetime by two fools who hadn't known what they were promising, and a stranger's frozen hand on his wrist saying *say it, say it, so I can carry it back.*

He went to the sluice room, ran cold water over his face until his thoughts stopped sliding, and came back. Anselm slept on, breath rising and falling. Theo fetched the ruined coat from where the theatre orderly had dumped it for burning, turned it inside out under the lamp, and found the second pocket's stitching with his thumb.

Inside: five more pages, folded tight, sealed with wax. And beneath them, folded separately, a strip of paper covered in Bram's unmistakable hand — not a letter this time, but a column of names. Theo counted them without meaning to, the way you count shellfire by instinct, the way you count anything in wartime.

Forty-three.

His own private ledger, the one locked in his footlocker, the one no living soul knew about — he thought of it suddenly, completely, the way you think of a locked door when footsteps stop outside it. Three years of names the officers left off the lists. Three years of small names, kept faithfully, kept uselessly, kept for no reason he could ever explain to anyone.

And here they were. Or ones like them. Written in a dead man's hand, months ago, sealed in wax, carried across a sea on a dying man's chest — arriving tonight, at this hospital, at his desk.

Theo stood very still in the lamplight, the strip of names trembling in his fingers, and understood that the choice he'd believed he was making — whether to help a stranger — had never actually been offered to him. The letter had been addressed to him before the boat ever sailed. Before Anselm ever swam. Possibly before the telegram announcing Bram's death was even printed.

Someone out there had planned on Theo Fairweather's devotion the way engineers plan on load-bearing stone.

Down the corridor, boots on gravel — unhurried, military, two sets — and a voice at the night desk asking Sister Agnes, politely, whether the matron might spare a few moments for Major Corvane of Intelligence.
