## 4. What We Promised in Peacetime

The letter lay under Theo's pillow all that first night, and by morning he had read it so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth. He knew the hand on the envelope before he knew anything else — that slanted, impatient script, the *B* that always looked like it was trying to leave the page. Two years of official telegrams and printed condolences had not been able to kill that handwriting in his memory. Grief had dulled everything else: the sound of Bram's laugh, the exact colour of his eyes, whether he took sugar. But not the hand.

He should have burned it. He told himself that, sitting in the linen room with his back against a shelf of folded sheets, the hospital's morning noise rising around him like weather. Burn it, because Major Corvane's men were already walking the wards with their notebooks, because Anselm was hidden four corridors away among the dead men's trolleys, because the letter itself was enough to hang them both.

Instead he read it again. And instead of Corvane's face or the gallows or the tide, what came to him — unbidden, insistent, the way memory comes when you are most afraid — was the summer. The last good summer. 1913, when Elias Bram was twenty-four years old and a lieutenant only because his father had bought the commission, and Theo was nineteen and still believed that history was something that happened to other people, in other countries, in books.

* * *

They had met badly, which is how the best things start. Bram had come down from London to spend a fortnight with his aunt at the big grey house above the harbour, and on his second evening he'd walked into the Anchor & Rope, seen Theo behind the bar filling glasses for his uncle, and asked — in that voice that made questions sound like verdicts — for "whatever the fishermen drink, but colder."

Theo had said, "You don't want what the fishermen drink."

"I beg your pardon?"

"You want what they drink when they're celebrating. It's different. Same bottle, different reason." And then, because nineteen-year-olds are fools with perfect instincts, Theo had poured him the celebration pint without being asked.

Bram drank half of it, set it down, and said, "You've just cost me a shilling and taught me more than Sandhurst did. What's your name?"

"Fairweather. Theo Fairweather."

"Terrible name. You'll grow into it." He put out his hand across the bar. "Elias Bram. Also terrible. We'll survive each other."

That was August. By September they were inseparable, in the way that happens only in the last summers, when everyone secretly knows the world is about to end and no one says so. Theo worked days at the harbour office — clerking, tallying cargo, learning ledgers the way other boys learned football — and every evening Bram appeared like weather off the sea, dragging him out: swimming off the point until their lips went blue, racing the evening boat along the quay, once, memorably, stealing a dinghy neither of them could sail and being towed home in disgrace by a crab boat whose captain laughed so hard he refused payment for the salvage.

Bram talked constantly. About London, about the regiment, about books he'd read too fast and arguments he'd started on purpose. He had a theory about everything and held none of them gently. But there were hours — usually very late, usually after the third pint — when the performance dropped away and something quieter showed underneath: a boy who had been sent to school at eight and had never once, as far as Theo could tell, been asked what he wanted.

"You're the only person," Bram said one night, lying on the warm shingle below the lighthouse path, staring up at stars that hadn't yet earned their winter names, "who has never wanted anything from me. My aunt wants me married. The regiment wants me punctual. My father wants me to be him, but younger. You just want me to hold the other end of the boat."

"Somebody has to. You row like a wounded heron."

"I'm serious, Fairweather."

"I know. That's what frightens me."

Bram rolled his head sideways on the pebbles and looked at him, and Theo felt the look go through him like cold water — not romantic exactly, though it was partly that, and neither of them ever pretended otherwise, but something larger and less manageable. Recognition. The terrible luck of finding, at nineteen and twenty-four, the person you would have chosen if choosing were allowed to be permanent.

"Here's my problem," Bram said. "I'm going to be a soldier. It's decided; it's been decided since I could walk; it's the family trade like your uncle's is beer. And if there's a war — and there will be a war, everyone building these lovely fleets isn't doing it for the exercise — then one day someone will tell me to do something, or know something, or be somewhere, and it will go wrong. Not cowardice. Nothing dishonourable. Just — wrong. Wrong in the way that only becomes clear afterwards, when the paperwork's done and the decisions are all official." He picked up a pebble and threw it at the dark where the sea was. "And when that day comes, I will need to disappear. Properly. Papers, a name, a road out. And there won't be anyone I can ask, because everyone I know will be either the army or my family, and those are the same thing wearing different coats."

Theo said nothing. The sea worked at the shingle below them, patient as arithmetic.

"So here's what I want," Bram went on, and now the performance was back, the grin arriving ahead of the words like cavalry. "I want a vow. A drunken, binding, absolutely ridiculous vow. If I ever need to vanish, you make it happen. No questions, no letters, no hesitation. You're the ledger man. You can make a man exist or stop existing with a pen. I've watched you do it to crates of brandy."

"That was clerking. That was entirely legal."

"It was smuggling, Theo, we both know it was smuggling, your uncle pays duty on perhaps half of what crosses that quay. My point is—" he sat up abruptly, and the shingle crunched, and suddenly it wasn't funny anymore, and Theo would remember for the rest of his life how fast Bram could turn from light to earnest, like weather — "my point is that if the day comes, I'll have maybe an hour, and I'll need to know, absolutely know, without sending word first, that there is one person in England who will open a door."

"And if it's me?" Theo said quietly. "If I need to disappear?"

"Then I burn my career to the waterline getting you out." He said it instantly, no pause, and that instant was the thing Theo kept coming back to for two years afterward, turning it over like a coin, checking it for wear. "Come on. On it. Swear it properly."

"Bram—"

"On it. Hand on the bottle. That's the sacrament; I don't make the rules."

So Theo put his hand on the warm neck of the whisky bottle, laughing, and Bram put his hand over Theo's, and they swore it — clumsily, solemnly, drunk on cheap spirits and youth and the enormous confidence of people who have never lost anything — that whichever of them needed to disappear, the other would make it so. No questions. No hesitation. One door, always open.

Then Bram kissed him, tasting of salt and whisky, and the vow dissolved into the ordinary heat of that extraordinary summer, and neither of them spoke of it again. There didn't seem to be any need. Promises like that are furniture in the house of a life; you don't discuss the chairs.

* * *

Eleven months later, war.

Theo remembered the whole country going strange within a week — flags appearing overnight like fungi after rain, boys from the harbour queueing outside the recruiting office as if tickets were being sold. Bram had gone straight back to his regiment, of course. At the station, shaking hands like strangers in front of Bram's aunt and half the town, they had managed only: "Write when you can." "When I can." Everything else stayed folded up inside the vow, unspoken, absurd now, a drunken thing said on a beach by boys who'd thought the future was a place you could map.

But the letters came. Dozens of them, over two years — from the training camp, from the mud, from rest billets and hospitals and one unforgettable page written during an actual bombardment, shrapnel holes in the paper that Bram had circled and annotated in the margin: *evidence, for your files.* Theo kept them all in a biscuit tin under his bed, and answered every one, and between the lines of the official prose — *all quiet here, weather foul, food indifferent* — ran the other conversation they'd never stopped having. Bram signed the early letters *E.B.*, then *Your E.*, then finally, in the last ones, simply *E*, as if even three letters were more identity than a man could safely carry into the century they lived in.

The last letter came in March, 1916 — no. Theo corrected himself, standing in the linen room with the new letter under his coat. The last letter had come in the spring of 1914, before the war. The last *news* came later: the telegram in the autumn of 1916, the printed card, Sister Agnes's hand briefly on his shoulder in the corridor, the way the ward had seemed to tilt. Missing, presumed killed. Then, six weeks after that, the second notice, the one nobody talked about but everybody read twice: Captain E. Bram reported to have deserted his post, believed to be assisting the enemy. *Enemy collaborator.* His name struck from the regimental list. His photograph taken down from his aunt's hallway, though Theo happened to know she kept it in a drawer with her gloves, because grief makes hypocrites of everyone.

Two years. Two years of Theo hating him — efficiently, dutifully, the way you hate a dead traitor, which is to say with a third of yourself and no conviction whatsoever. Because Theo had known, hadn't he? Some animal part of him had known from the first week that the story was wrong. Desertion. Bram. The man who raced the evening ferry along the quay for the sheer stupidity of it, who held the other end of the boat. Cowardice was the one accusation that fit him like another man's coat.

And now a stranger had been carried off the night boat with no papers and a letter sewn into his coat lining, and the handwriting was the handwriting, and the letter said:

*Theo. If this reaches you, the vow stands. I need the door opened. Forgive the method — I couldn't trust the post. Trust the bearer. He is why I am alive, and he is hurt, and he knows nothing except that you keep names. All my love, always, E.*

*P.S. — You'll have read what they say I am. Read your own ledger instead. Count the names that shouldn't be missing.*

Theo had read it eleven times and it had rearranged him eleven times, and now, on the morning after, in the smell of carbolic and clean linen, with the hospital waking around him and Corvane's men somewhere in the building asking their precise, courteous questions, Theo understood that the summer had never ended. It had only been waiting. The vow had been waiting inside him like a seed in dry ground, and all it required was water.

No questions. No hesitation. One door, always open.

He thought of Bram's face on the shingle, lit by the moment the joke turned true. He thought of his own voice saying *and if it's me?* and the answer that had come without a pause — and he understood, with a clarity that felt almost like vertigo, that Bram must have carried that same instant with him for two years. Must have bet his life on it. Had bet it, tonight, on a half-drowned batman and a needle and thread and the faith that a clerk in a Channel port kept his promises.

Theo stood, folded the letter into the lining of his own coat — mirroring the journey it had made across the Channel, hiding it in the same place, as if the garment might remember — and squared the shelf of sheets in front of him out of pure habit.

Down the corridor, boots. Measured ones. The intelligence man's walk, unhurried, a man reading a room the way Theo read a ledger.

Anselm was awake. The night nurse had whispered it to Theo ten minutes ago: fever breaking, eyes open, trying to speak. Trying to say something. One word, she thought, over and over.

Theo buttoned his coat over the letter and went to hear it.
