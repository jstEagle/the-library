## 5. The Intelligence Man

Major Corvane arrived on the morning boat, and the town knew it before he reached the quay.

He had not announced himself. He did not need to. There was a quality to his arrival that preceded him the way weather precedes a storm — a stillness in the harbourmaster's office, a clerk suddenly very interested in his own paperwork, two military policemen who had been loitering near the fish market since dawn straightening their caps as though they'd been waiting all their lives for this particular Tuesday. Corvane came down the gangway alone, carrying a single leather case, and looked at the town the way an auditor looks at a ledger that has already told him it contains a lie.

He was perhaps fifty, grey at the temples, with the kind of face that seemed assembled rather than grown — precise jaw, precise mouth, eyes of such pale blue they read as colourless until you caught them directly and felt something turn over inside you. His uniform was immaculate but deliberately unremarkable: no swagger stick, no medals displayed beyond what regulation required. Men who wanted to be seen wore decoration. Corvane wanted to be underestimated for exactly as long as it suited him.

The porter at the Grand Hotel had a room ready before Corvane gave his name. This was not luck. A telegram had come three days ago from a War Office address that officially did not exist, requesting accommodation for a Major V. Corvane, arrival date unstated, payment guaranteed. The telegram had also requested — in the same flat bureaucratic tone — that no mention be made of it to local military authorities.

So when Colonel Haversham's adjutant telephoned the hotel at noon to ask whether any officers had recently taken rooms, the porter said no, sir, none at all, and discovered only afterward that his hands were shaking.

* * *

Corvane spent his first hour in the town doing nothing that looked like work.

He walked the length of the front. He bought a newspaper he did not read. He stood for eleven minutes — a witness would later say twelve, another nine; Corvane himself would have said eleven, because Corvane counted everything — outside the gates of the Naval Hospital, watching ambulances come down the hill from the station yard.

What he saw was efficiency. That was the first thing worth noting. The port handled its dead and broken with the brisk competence of a place that had been doing it for two years and had stopped pretending it was temporary. Wounded men off the night boats, sorted on the quay by rank and severity, moved up to the hospital or across to the convalescent sheds with a minimum of fuss. The paperwork, he was told by a cheery corporal he'd bought a drink within four minutes of meeting, was handled mostly by clerks, since the medical officers had their hands full keeping men alive.

"Clerks," Corvane said.

"Oh, aye, sir. Good ones, too. Sister Agnes runs a tight ward, and there's a Mr. Fairweather does the registers — you'd never catch him a day behind. Man knows every name that's come through here since '14, I'd swear it."

"Does he," Corvane said, and smiled, and the corporal, recalling the smile later, could not have said why it had made him want to go home early.

Corvane returned to the hotel and unlocked his leather case.

Inside, arranged with the tenderness other men reserved for photographs of their children, were papers. He laid them out on the writing desk in order: forty-three casualty summaries, each stamped and countersigned and each, upon investigation, corresponding to no grave, no burial record, no next-of-kin notification, and no man. Forty-three soldiers who had been wounded at various points along the northern sector between March 1915 and January 1916, entered into hospital ledgers, marked evacuated — and then erased. Not dead. Not discharged. Not transferred to any record any subsequent inquiry could find. Simply gone, as though the war had opened a small door beneath each of them and let them drop through.

Above the stack, weighted by his pen, lay a single sheet of paper bearing one line in his own handwriting:

*Forty-three names. One route. One hand.*

They had given him the file in February with the expectation that it was a clerical error compounded by wartime chaos. Records went astray. Men went astray. France was a machine that consumed paper faster than flesh, and somewhere in its gears whole battalions of documents vanished monthly. But Corvane had spent nineteen years doing this work — first in India, then in the shadowed offices of Whitehall where the real wars were conducted with fountain pens — and he had learned that errors were lazy and this was not. Errors scattered. These names converged. Every one of the forty-three had passed through hospitals on the coast. Every one had last appeared in a ledger entry written in a period of days surrounding a sailing of some small vessel, a fishing boat, a mail packet, nothing official, nothing logged.

Someone was moving men off the books and out of the country. And whoever kept the books had either failed to notice or chosen not to look.

Corvane did not believe in coincidence. He believed in patterns, and patterns had authors.

* * *

At half past two he presented himself at the office of Colonel Haversham, commanding the garrison, with credentials that turned the colonel's ruddy good humour to careful formality in under a minute. Corvane watched the transition with professional appreciation. Haversham was a soldier of the old sort, honest, blunt, protective of his command, and precisely the kind of man whose cooperation you obtained not by demanding it but by making him feel consulted.

"You'll understand, Major, if I ask plainly," Haversham said, when Corvane had explained — in terms so general they constituted almost no information at all — that he was investigating irregularities in casualty documentation. "Irregularities of what nature? If there's something wrong in my jurisdiction, I'd take it kindly to be told."

"There is something wrong in your jurisdiction, Colonel." Corvane set three of the forty-three pages on the desk, squared to the edge, aligned. "Three names. I'll leave them with you. You may verify through your own channels that no such men exist anywhere in the records of the British Army — not wounded, not buried, not demobilised, not deserted. They have been subtracted. Subtraction on this scale is not an accident. Accidents are singular. This is a system."

Haversham read the names. Corvane observed him do it: the colonel's lips moving slightly, the frown deepening at the second name, the small involuntary glance up at the third — because the third name meant something to him, Corvane noted. Private D. Ellery. The colonel had known an Ellery, or an Ellery had served under him, or an Ellery had once drunk his whisky in some mess tent in another life. Corvane filed the reaction away. Everyone had a file. The colonel's was thin, which made him useful.

"And your suspicion, Major? If I'm to give you the run of my port, I'd like to know what I'm giving you the run of it *for*."

"Suspicion is not my trade, Colonel. Evidence is. What I have is a pattern of erasure spanning ten months, terminating at ports along this coast, and one further item." He paused, and Corvane never paused accidentally; the pause was a tool, honed to cut exactly as deep as intended. "A report, unconfirmed, from a French source, of a man moving along the littoral. No papers. No regiment. Seen twice near Calais, once at Boulogne, and — as of six weeks ago — possibly here. The locals call him *le fantôme*. The ghost."

"The ghost," Haversham repeated, with the flat scepticism of a man who had heard about ghosts since 1914 and buried enough real men to distrust the supernatural.

"A deserter, most likely. Or a spy." Corvane rose, collected his hat. "Or both. Deserters who move with this much discipline are rarely merely cowards, Colonel. Cowards hide. This man travels. There is a difference, and the difference has a purpose, and the purpose is what I intend to find."

"And if he's found?"

"Then he will be asked questions," Corvane said pleasantly, "until the answers are sufficient."

* * *

By evening he had done three more things, each small, each deliberate.

He visited the records annex and requested — courteously, with the charm of a man paying a compliment — copies of the hospital admission registers for the preceding fourteen months. The clerk on duty, a nervous lance-corporal named Petch, fetched them without question and would remember afterward, with the particular misery of the retrospectively suspicious, that the major had thanked him by name. Petch had never told him his name. Corvane had read it off the duty roster taped inside the door while shaking his hand.

He walked the morgue annex, alone, for four minutes, and stood looking at the row of slate slabs and the high salt-stained window with an expression the attendant could not classify and did not enjoy trying to.

And he wrote two letters. The first, to the address that did not exist, reporting: *Arrived. Town cooperative. Hospital records extensive and well-kept — note: unusually well-kept. Beginning systematic review tomorrow.* The second letter he did not send. It was addressed to no one. It contained a list of the hospital staff, compiled from the duty rosters, with annotations in the margin. Against one name — *Fairweather, T., clerk* — he had written a single word, and the word was not an accusation. It was a question mark.

Because here was what troubled him, and Corvane admitted it to himself with the dispassion of a surgeon noting an anomaly on a plate: the forty-three names should have surfaced sooner. Someone, somewhere along two years of paper, should have noticed. And the hospital registers — he had skimmed three volumes that afternoon, fast, the way he read everything — were immaculate. Too immaculate. Every omission accounted for, every gap explained in a neat marginal hand, every discrepancy pre-answered before any investigator arrived to ask.

Either the clerk was the most conscientious recorder on the Channel coast, or he was the best liar Corvane had encountered since Antwerp.

Both possibilities interested him equally. In nineteen years he had learned that the truth usually wore the second costume.

* * *

He dined alone at the hotel, at eight, and was in bed — lights out, case locked, boots polished for morning — by half past nine, because discipline was not a virtue he practised but a structure he inhabited.

He did not sleep immediately. He lay in the dark and reviewed the day the way he reviewed everything, forward and back, hunting for the flaw in his own conduct the way other men hunted for flaws in the world's.

The ghost was real. He was certain of it now, more certain than his superiors, who had funded this inquiry with the reluctance of men paying for a roof inspection after the storm has passed. The French source had been vague, but vagueness in intelligence was itself information: the source had seen something it couldn't explain, and things that couldn't be explained were always, eventually, explicable. A man with no papers moving north along the coast toward England, following the tide lines like a creature reading a map only it could see. Not fleeing the war. Moving *through* it. Toward something.

And the forty-three names were the wake he left behind him.

Corvane turned onto his side. Below the window, the town settled into its noises — the gulls, the far hoot of a night boat coming in, the sea working patiently at the harbour wall as it had worked for a thousand years and would work for a thousand more regardless of what anyone recorded or erased.

Somewhere out there, a man had spent two years pulling soldiers out of the machine, one at a time, quietly, without glory, without record — and had done it so well that the machine itself had failed to notice its own losses. Somewhere in this town, someone had helped, knowingly or otherwise. Someone with access to ledgers.

Tomorrow he would begin at the hospital. Tomorrow he would shake hands with the clerk Fairweather and read the man's face the way he read documents — slowly, twice, and against the original.

In the dark, Major Corvane permitted himself the closest thing he ever came to pleasure: the small, cold satisfaction of a hunt properly begun.

He slept well. He always did.

Across the town, in the hospital on the hill, a light burned late over a ledger of small names, and neither man yet knew how completely their histories were already entwined — the hunter and the keeper, each holding half of the same story, each about to learn that the ghost they sought was standing, in one way or another, much closer than the tide.
