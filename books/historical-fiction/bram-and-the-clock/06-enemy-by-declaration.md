## 6. Enemy by Declaration

The notice went up on the hospital board at half past seven in the morning, between a memorandum about lime juice rations and a requisition form for more splints, and it changed everything.

Theo was carrying a tray of dressings when he saw the small knot of orderlies gathered by the board, and something in their stillness — the particular silence of men reading bad news — made him set the tray down on the nearest trolley and cross the ward.

The notice was typed. That was the first thing he noticed, absurdly: the typing, clean and official, the double-space of a Whitehall machine, the smudged ink of a copy of a copy of a decision that had already been made somewhere far from this cold ward smelling of carbolic and seawater.

*It is hereby notified for general information that Captain Elias J. Bram, Royal Sussex Regiment, previously reported killed in action in the autumn of 1914, is confirmed to have deserted His Majesty's forces and to have entered the service of the enemy. He is to be considered alive, at large, and a traitor to the Crown. Any person giving aid, shelter, or assistance to the said Bram commits an offence under the Defence of the Realm Act and will be liable to court-martial.*

Below it, a second notice, smaller:

*Intelligence officers are authorized to conduct inquiries at all ports and hospitals. Full cooperation is expected.*

Theo read it twice. The words did not change. *Deserted. Enemy service. Traitor.* The letters sat on the page with the flat confidence of type, and type, Theo knew better than almost anyone alive, could say anything. Type had said his father had never served. Type had struck forty-three men from the lists. Type was a machine for making the world agree with whoever fed the paper into it.

And yet his hands had gone cold around the edge of the trolley.

"Bad business, that," said Corporal Hewitt at his elbow, nodding at the notice. "Officer like that. You'd wonder what they've got on a man to make him go over."

"You'd wonder," Theo agreed, and his voice came out steady, which surprised him. Steady was a thing you could do with a voice even when everything behind it was falling. He'd learned that at his father's inquest, years ago. He picked up his tray. "Dressings don't change themselves."

He walked the length of Ward C as though it were any other morning. He changed a dressing on a boy from Ashford with a shattered knee, and told him the bone was knitting well, which was true. He noted temperatures. He wrote a letter for a gunner whose right hand was gone, taking down his words for his mother in a careful clerk's hand, and when the gunner asked him to add *and I am in excellent spirits*, Theo added it, because it was what the man wanted his mother to carry, and there was a kind of truth that lived above fact.

All the while, under the routine, one thought turning over and over like a stone in surf:

They had declared him a traitor. Which meant they did not know where he was. Which meant they were frightened of him — you did not post notices about dead men unless a dead man worried you. And a man who worried the War Office enough to be branded from beyond his own grave was either guilty of something terrible or knew something terrible, and Theo had spent three years learning the difference between what the ledgers said and what had happened.

Anselm was lying in the morgue annex with a fever and a bullet wound, three doors from where Theo stood, and helping him was now, by the letter of the law, treason.

Treason. The word sat oddly, like a coat cut for another man's shoulders. Theo tried it on while he wound bandages. *Theodore Fairweather, clerk, traitor.* It didn't fit. What he had done so far — moved a dying man out of the receiving room before the morning intake arrived, laid him among the dead where no one looks for the living — was not treachery against anyone. It was the oldest thing in any hospital: you keep the man breathing until you know his name.

Except he did know the name now. That was the whole trouble.

* * *

Sister Agnes found him at eleven, in the records room, ostensibly to deliver a list of stores. She closed the door behind her, which she never did.

"You've seen the board," she said. It wasn't a question.

"I've seen the board."

"The intelligence people telephoned ahead from London last night. They'll be here today or tomorrow. A Major Corvane." She pronounced it carefully, the way she pronounced the names of medicines she didn't trust. "Two of them, perhaps three. They want the admission registers for October and November. They want to interview all staff who handled the night boats."

"Then they're looking for him already." Theo kept his voice level. "For Bram."

"They're looking for a ghost along the coast, is what the telephone call said." Agnes set the stores list on the desk without looking at it. Her hands were folded in front of her apron the way they always were, the hands that had run this hospital through two years of the worst tides of wounded in England, hands that did not shake. "Thirty years I've kept this place, Mr. Fairweather. Thirty years, and in all that time I have learned one rule that never fails: when officials come asking after a name, the name is in danger, and it doesn't matter from whom."

"And if the name deserves the danger?" Theo heard himself ask. "If he really went over —"

"Then he deserves a trial, not a noticeboard." She said it flatly, without heat, and it landed harder than heat would have. "I nursed under Bram's mother's cousin at Chatham, if you want the whole of it, and the Brams were many things but never that. But my opinion isn't evidence, and neither is yours. The question is simpler than you're making it." Her eyes went to the locked cabinet where the morgue annex key hung. "There is a man in my dead-house who has no papers, no regimental number, and no explanation. When Corvane arrives — and he will arrive, today, I'd stake the chapel fund on it — that man cannot be found there. Or anywhere."

"Sister —"

"Don't thank me and don't involve me further than I've involved myself, which is already too far." She turned to go, then stopped at the door with her hand on the frame. "The fever broke at six this morning. He took water. Whatever you're going to do with him, do it while he can walk."

Then she was gone, and the door clicked, and Theo stood alone in the records room with forty-three names in a drawer and one name in his chest and the whole apparatus of the state beginning, somewhere up the line, to roll toward this town.

* * *

He went to the annex at the noon lull, when the wards sank into the heavy doze that followed dinner and the corridor emptied except for the slow patrol of one elderly porter.

The morgue annex was a brick lean-to against the north wall, built in a hurry in ' fourteen and never improved: two slabs, a drain, a high window fogged with salt, and a smell of carbolic laid over something older and colder. Anselm lay on the second slab — Theo had made up a pallet over it with blankets from the convalescent store, an act that felt like blasphemy and was probably the opposite — and his eyes were open.

Fever had burned him down to the architecture: hollow cheeks, the blue-shaved skull of a man whose hair had been cut at sea, hands quiet on his chest. But the eyes tracked Theo across the room, and they were clear.

"Water," Anselm said. His voice was gravel.

Theo poured it, held the mug, watched him drink. Up close the man was younger than his voice — thirty, perhaps, though the wound and the sea had aged him overnight. There was a scar through one eyebrow, old. On the third swallow he began to cough and Theo took the mug away until the spasm passed.

"Slowly."

"You're the clerk." Anselm's eyes had gone to Theo's hands, of all things, as if he'd been told about them. "Fairweather. The letters were to Fairweather."

"They were." Theo set the mug on a stool and pulled the other blanket higher on the man's shoulders, an ordinary nursing gesture, and only afterward understood what he had just done in front of a witness: he had chosen. "You gave the ward sister quite a fright, arriving without papers."

"I gave myself one." A ghost of a smile. It hurt him; he let it fade. "How long have I been here?"

"A night and a day."

"And has the board been read?"

Theo looked at him. "You know about the board."

"I've known about the board since before it was printed." Anselm shifted, wincing, and got his shoulder against the wall so he could sit half upright. The movement cost him; Theo watched sweat stand out on his forehead and waited. "Captain said it would come. Said they'd call it treason, because that's the word that makes strangers afraid to open their doors. He said—" Anselm stopped, and something moved through his face that was not pain from the wound. "He said, tell Fairweather they'll make me a monster on paper first. Paper's cheap. He talks— talked— about paper the way you'd know about."

"Does he." Theo's voice was very quiet. "Because I've spent two years believing he was dead, and one morning believing he's a traitor, and I have nothing in between but a letter I haven't finished reading because I keep hoping the next line will explain everything, and it doesn't. So you'll forgive me if I don't take the word of a man who washes up on the tide with sewn pockets."

"That's fair," Anselm said. And then, simply, without drama, the way men state facts that have kept them alive: "I'm his batman. Three years. I'd follow that man into hell and back out again, and I have, more or less, which is why I'm leaking on your slab. He is not a traitor. Whatever that paper says — whatever they make you read at whatever trial they'd give you — he is not that. If you need to know one thing before you decide whether to hand me to the major coming for us, know that."

"I don't know you."

"No." Anselm's eyes were steady. "But he knows you. He says you keep names nobody else keeps. He says—" and here the batman closed his eyes as if reciting cost him, or as if the recitation were sacred, "—he says, *if anyone can be trusted with the truth, it's the man who writes down what everyone else agrees to forget.*"

The room was very cold. The high window let down a grey light that made everything look already archived, already past. Theo became aware that he was gripping the edge of the slab, knuckles white, and made himself let go.

"The major," Theo said. "When he comes, what does he find?"

"Nothing," said Anselm. "If you're the man in the letters."

* * *

Moving a living man out of a morgue in daylight was impossible; moving him at dusk was merely dangerous. Theo spent the afternoon manufacturing the ordinary out of thin air, which was, he realized, precisely the skill his whole strange life had trained in him.

He altered the annex register first. The dead-book recorded admissions to the mortuary, and the mortuary received its sad freight daily from the boats. At 3:40, in his own unremarkable clerk's hand, he entered: *Unknown male, drowned, recovered East Quay, transferred to St. Aldhelm's Chapel crypt pending identification.* There was a chapel crypt two parishes over used for unclaimed dead; transfers happened; no one audited grief. Then he took the genuine transfer docket from the file of a man who truly had gone to the crypt last Tuesday — a poor drowned sailor no one claimed — and copied it, changing only the date and the name that wasn't there. Forged, whole and entire, in eleven minutes, by a man who had never forged anything in his life and discovered that the pen did not shake until he made himself look at what he'd written.

His father's lesson, arriving late: the record is not the world. The record is only ever someone's hand.

At five, in the grey failure of light that passes for afternoon on the Channel in autumn, Theo brought a wheeled laundry hamper to the annex door and helped Anselm into clean orderly's trousers and a blanket, and the two of them did the hardest twenty yards of work Theo had ever done — a feverish man, a weak leg, a corridor, a doorway, the cold yard, the boathouse path — while somewhere beyond the wall a church bell counted six and Theo counted his own heartbeats and thought, absurdly, *so this is what it is, then. This is the shape of my treason. A hamper and a blanket and a man heavier than he looks.*

The boathouse sat at the end of the hospital's long garden, below the sea wall, disused since the rowing club gave it up in ' fifteen. Its door stuck; its interior smelled of tar and rope and old water; its single window faced the blackening sea. Theo had laid a cot there that morning, stolen in installments — frame from stores, mattress from the convalescent hut, blankets from three sources so no one count would come up short. He'd told himself he was preparing for a contingency. He had known he was lying.

He got Anselm onto the cot. The batman's breath was ragged; the journey had opened something; Theo cleaned and redressed the wound by lamplight with the same hands that had falsified a register an hour earlier, and found the two acts sat together in his chest without quarrel, which frightened him more than either alone.

"They'll search the hospital," Anselm said, when he could speak. "Not today maybe. Soon. Corvane's methodical — Captain described him."

"He's described the major too?"

"Once. After Calais." Anselm's eyes found Theo's in the lamp glow. "He said: *beware the man who believes the ledger more than the living.*"

Theo sat back on his heels. Outside, the tide was coming in, filling the dark with its patient industry, the sound it had made all his life beneath every other sound, the sound under his father's silence, under the ward's long nights, under everything: the water keeping its own accounts.

He thought about the noticeboard. *Deserted. Traitor. Enemy.* Typed twice-spaced, authoritative, final. He thought about a letter sewn into a drowning man's coat, addressed in a hand he knew intimately, and how the two documents could not both be telling the truth, and how only one of them had risked anything to reach him.

History happened elsewhere — he had believed that his whole life, believed it the way you believe the weather, without ever choosing to. It happened to ministers and generals and men like Bram, and Theo recorded its drippings in a ledger no one would read.

But history had just washed up on his own shore, feverish and hunted, and was asking him, through a cracked voice, in a boathouse that smelled of tar: *whose side are the records on?*

"I need to check the wards," Theo said, standing. "If anyone asks, you're a convalescent porter on the night shift and the boathouse is storage. Don't open the door for anyone but me or Sister Agnes. Eat what I leave. And if the worst comes —" he hesitated — "there's a loose plank behind the boat racks. It goes down to the shingle."

Anselm nodded slowly. Then: "Read the rest of the letter tonight, Mr. Fairweather. All of it. You'll want to know what he asks."

"I know what he asks. He asks me to help him come home. He asked two years ago, in peacetime, when none of this could have mattered less." Theo pulled his coat on. At the door he paused, hand on the latch, and heard himself say the thing he had been circling all day: "I made him a promise once. Drunk, ridiculous, summer before everything. I'd forgotten it until yesterday. He didn't."

He stepped out into the wind. Behind him, faint through the warped wood, Anselm's voice: "That's the captain, sir. He never forgets a debt."

Theo walked back up the garden path toward the lit windows of the hospital, where a major from London might already be signing the visitors' book, and where the noticeboard hung with its clean terrible type, and he found that the fear which had lived in him all day had done something unfamiliar overnight: it had set, like plaster, like concrete, into a shape that could bear weight.

They could call it treason. Let them. He had forty-three names in a drawer that said otherwise, and tomorrow — before Corvane's polished questions reached him — he was going to find out exactly what those names had to do with the ghost of Elias Bram.

The tide turned below the sea wall and began its long pull toward morning.
