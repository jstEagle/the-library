## 7. Anselm Wakes

The first thing Anselm Voss learned about waking was that it could be refused. He tried, twice. The ceiling above him was whitewash gone grey at the seams, and the light came through a high window in bars of morning, and none of it was the boat, and his body understood this before he did — but his mind kept reaching for the water, for the cold green slap of it, for the moment when the deck went sideways and Captain Bram's hand closed on his collar like God deciding otherwise.

He surfaced the third time because someone was counting.

"Fourteen shillings six," a voice said, low, near his feet. "That's what your boots cost to replace. I've written it down."

Anselm turned his head. A young man sat on a stool with a ledger open across his knees — a narrow-faced clerk with ink on his fingers and a face built out of careful lines, as if someone had drawn him with a ruler and then felt guilty about it and added kindness at the corners.

"You count everything," Anselm said. His throat sounded like a drain.

"Everything," the man agreed. "Theo Fairweather. You've been asleep two days. You've been dead once, briefly, which Sister Agnes says doesn't count against your record." He closed the ledger. "You said a word. Before you went under again. Do you remember it?"

Anselm remembered. He had been carrying it since Dieppe like a stone in his teeth. *Alive.* The whole war reduced to one word, and he'd spent it on a stranger in a hospital because a dead man had told him to.

"Bram," he said instead. "Where are my clothes?"

* * *

They had burned the coat. Theo told him this while helping him sit up, and Anselm felt something go out of him that wasn't quite hope — more like the shape hope leaves behind. The coat had held three letters. Two were sewn into the lining; one had been against his chest, wrapped in oilcloth, addressed in handwriting that even half-drowned and feverish he had recognized the way you recognize a voice calling from another room.

"They're safe," Theo said. "All three. I didn't read—" He stopped. Anselm watched the lie assemble itself behind the clerk's eyes and decide against being born. "I read them. I'm sorry. I read them."

"Good," Anselm said. "Then we don't have to waste time."

Theo looked at him then — really looked, the way a man looks at a locked door he has suddenly realized is his own. "You know what's in them."

"I carried them. That's not the same as knowing, but it's close enough." Anselm flexed his hands. They worked. Everything worked, more or less, which surprised him; the sea had tried hard to keep him. "Captain said: if I can't say it, let the paper say it. If the paper's lost, let Fairweather find it. Are you Fairweather?"

"I'm Fairweather."

"He talked about you like weather himself," Anselm said. "Like something he could set his watch by."

* * *

The telling took the whole day, in pieces, because that was how Anselm had learned to survive — not in narratives but in loads, one crate at a time. He had been Bram's batman for eleven months before Ypres, and then something else afterward, something without a name that appeared on no muster roll: batman, courier, gravedigger, witness.

"The first time he saved me was ordinary," Anselm said. It was evening now; Theo had brought tea that tasted of nothing and bread that tasted of less, and they sat in the small store-room off Ward C where the linen was kept and no one asked why a clerk spent an hour with an unnamed patient. "Mud at St. Eloi, the kind that takes men whole. I went in to my knees, then my waist, and the captain came back for me against orders. Pulled me out by the same collar he grabbed at the boat. He said—" Anselm smiled at the ceiling. "He said, 'Voss, you weigh more than your file.' That was all. No heroics. He just made it a joke so I wouldn't have to be grateful."

"And the second time?"

Anselm was quiet a while. Down the corridor a ward bell rang twice and stopped. Somewhere a man was singing very softly, a song with no country, just tune.

"The second time was after he was dead," Anselm said. "Officially. August of '15. I was on a working party near the coast, and there was a list — forty-three names, men marked *missing, presumed.* You'd know some of them if I said them. Some of them were already dead, truly dead, and some of them were in a cellar in Boulogne eating soup out of the same tin." He looked at Theo. "Your names. Your ledger names. Don't look surprised — he knew about you. He knew about the book."

Theo's hand had gone still on his knee. "Nobody knows about the book."

"He did. He said history keeps two sets of books, and the honest ones are always private." Anselm leaned his head back. The fever was gone but its echo remained, a tiredness with architecture, walls and floors of it. "The second time he saved me, I was meant to be on that list too. My name was written down by a man whose job was writing men down. And Bram crossed it out. Not with a pen. With a boat."

* * *

He slept, and dreamed of the cellar, and woke to lamplight and Theo still there, which told Anselm something about the man that the ledger never would.

"There were others," Anselm said, in the dark. "Before me. Men he moved along the coast like parcels, except parcels don't have mothers waiting in Dover. Each one got a letter. He wrote them himself — you'd know the hand. He said the letters were proof, someday, that the official version was a lie. Proof needs somewhere safe to live." He nodded at Theo's coat. "You're where they live now. That makes you part of it. I'm sorry about that."

"Don't be sorry." Theo's voice had changed since morning — something had been taken out of it, some softness of illusion, and something put in its place that Anselm recognized because he'd heard it in his own voice once, in a cellar in Boulogne, the night he chose. "Tell me about the third letter. The one against your chest."

"That one's different. The first two name men who are safe. The third names men who aren't yet." Anselm sat forward, slowly, testing his ribs, finding them willing. "There's a ship. Small, filthy, wonderful. It runs on the morning tide when the moon is wrong, and it costs more than money. Bram is on the coast near here — hurt, hiding, alive, and every hour the wrong people get closer. The letter is a map. The letter is a promise. The letter says—" and here he stopped, because saying it aloud made it heavier, made it real, made it a thing that could break. He said it anyway. "The letter says *bring Fairweather.*"

The lamp guttered. Neither of them moved.

"He said you'd want to know why," Anselm added. "And he said the why is: you promised. In peacetime. When promises were easy."

* * *

Later — much later, past the hour when hospitals pretend to sleep — Theo brought blankets and a question he'd clearly been carrying all day.

"Why you?" he asked. "Why does a captain spend his life on one batman?"

Anselm pulled the blanket to his chin. He thought about lying, found he hadn't the energy or the desire.

"Because I saw him cry once," he said. "At a burial detail, winter of '14. Nobody else saw. And he knew I saw, and he didn't punish me for it, didn't perform, didn't explain. He just handed me his cigarette case and said, 'Don't let them make this normal, Voss.' " Anselm looked at the dark window, at the grey sea beyond it that had almost killed him and was, tonight, the road home. "So I haven't. That's the whole job. That's what I am to him — the one man who remembers he isn't made of marble. Every man should have one. You're his other one, I think. He just hasn't seen you in years."

Down the corridor, boots. Measured ones. A man walking a ward at night who was not night staff — Anselm heard it in the stride, the stride of inspection, and beside him Theo heard it too, going very still, his hand drifting to the pocket where three letters lay like eggs in a nest.

"Who," Anselm whispered, "is that?"

"No one," Theo said, in the voice of a man who has just learned to lie badly and is practicing. "New intelligence officer. Arrived today. Major Corvane."

Anselm closed his eyes. In the cellar in Boulogne, Bram had taught him one rule above all others: *when the hunters change their habits, the hunt has changed.*

"Fairweather," he said quietly, as the boots paused outside the door, "how fast can you hide a dead man's postman?"
