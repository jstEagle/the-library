## 8. The Missing Forty-Three

I did not sleep. Sleep was a country I had lost my passport to somewhere around midnight, when I laid Anselm's letters out on the morgue annex table in a fan, like a card hand dealt by a dead man.

The annex was the right place to do it. No one came here but me and the cold. The lamp threw a yellow circle across the tin surface and the letters sat inside it like moths pinned to a board — seven of them, each in that handwriting I would have known with my eyes burned out. The forward lean of it. The way Bram crossed his *t*'s like fence rails. He used to say a man's hand was his face when he wasn't looking, and I had spent two years not looking at this face, because looking at it meant he was alive, and he was not alive, and I was a man who trusted records.

The letters were all addressed the same way. Not to me. To no one. Each began with a number and nothing else.

*No. 9.*

*No. 14.*

*No. 22.*

I had thought, when Anselm first pressed them into my hands on the night boat's stretcher, half-drowned and whispering *alive* like a man paying off a debt, that they were personal effects. Keepsakes. A dying courier's cargo of sentiment. Now, under the lamp, with my own ledger open beside them, I understood they were something else entirely.

They were receipts.

* * *

Let me tell you what my ledger is, since you have come this far with me. It is a school exercise book, blue-ruled, bought for a penny at Wetherell's before the war made pennies seem quaint. On its pages I write the names that official reports omit. The men who die in the night between one list and the next. The ones shipped out before anyone thinks to ask their names. The drowned who arrive without papers, identified by the laundry mark on their shirts if they are lucky, by their boots if they are not.

Forty-three names, as of last Tuesday. I know the number the way some men know the score of a match. Private Alfred Tunn. Private James Okafor-Reed. Gunner Wilf Sallow, who told me a joke about a donkey two hours before he died and I laughed and have hated myself since for laughing, though Sister Agnes says laughing was the kindest thing I could have done for him. Forty-three men whose names exist now only because I wrote them down, because history, I believed, happened somewhere else — in cabinet rooms, along fronts, in places where men in good coats decided which deaths were worth ink.

I believed that right up until tonight.

*No. 9.* I turned the first letter over. It was sealed with plain wax, unmarked, and opened easily, the paper soft from seawater. Inside, in that fence-rail hand:

*Delivered. He walked badly but he walked. Tell the clerk the boat went out light and came back heavy. — E.B.*

That was all. No name. No date. Just the number, the phrase, and initials I had traced on envelopes in another lifetime.

I set it down and picked up the second. *No. 14.* Same construction, same initials, slightly different words: *Delivered. Feverish but mending as of Tuesday. The clerk should keep his book dry.*

My mouth went dry enough for both of us.

*The clerk.* Me. He knew about the ledger. He had known — how long? Since when?

And then, because my hands are clerks' hands and they do what they are trained to do even while the rest of me is shouting, I laid the letters in order by their numbers and pulled my exercise book toward me and did the only thing that has ever made sense to me in a crisis.

I cross-referenced.

* * *

Number nine in Bram's sequence. I ran my finger down my ledger, past the smudge where rain had got into the ward one November night, past Tunn, past Okafor-Reed, and stopped.

Private Daniel Voss, 2nd Battalion. Admitted with a shattered knee, listed by the medical officer as *untransportable*, entered on nobody's casualty list because he hadn't died and hadn't been transferred and therefore, administratively speaking, had ceased to exist. I remembered him. Twenty-two years old, red hair, terrified of the dark and too proud to ask for a candle. One morning his bed was stripped and the ward sister said he'd gone down to the boats and I said, *which boats,* and she said, *the ones that go, Mr Fairweather,* and that was the end of the conversation, because in wartime a man can vanish between a bed and a harbour and no one is required to explain it.

He was the ninth name in my ledger. And the ninth letter said: *he walked badly but he walked.*

I sat very still. Somewhere above me a pipe knocked twice, the building settling its bones, the ordinary sounds of an ordinary hospital, and I could not make them feel ordinary.

Number fourteen. Private Thomas Ellery. Listed as *missing, presumed drowned* after the retreat — a phrase I have come to loathe the way other men loathe slurs, because *presumed* is a word that buries men without digging a grave. Ellery had come through our ward in June, coughing, half-starved, telling anyone who'd listen that he'd been separated from his unit and had walked three days along a drainage canal. Then he was gone too, moved on before his papers caught up with him, another administrative ghost. Fourteenth name in my book. Fourteenth letter: *Delivered.*

Number twenty-two. Gunner Peter Hask. Number twenty-six. Lance-Corporal Amos Bright. Bright! I had written him down in March with a note beside his name — *jokes, sang, asked after his mother in Deal* — because that is what my ledger is for, not just names but the small proof that they were people. And here was a letter saying *delivered*, saying *tell the clerk*, saying, in Bram's voice across two years and an ocean of official paper: *these men are not missing. They are found. You were writing down the wrong story.*

Or the right one. God help me, I didn't know yet which.

* * *

By three in the morning I had matched twenty-nine of the seven letters' worth — no. Let me be exact; exactness is all I have left tonight. There were seven letters, numbered nine, fourteen, twenty-two, twenty-six, thirty-one, thirty-eight, and forty-one. Seven letters. But each letter named more than its own number. The ninth letter mentioned "the four from the marsh ward." The thirty-first spoke of "the pair off the minesweeper." Bram, or whoever wrote these, had grouped them, and when I followed the groupings back through my book, counting, recounting, checking like the obsessive I am proud to be —

Forty-three.

Every name. Every single omitted, forgotten, administratively erased name in two years of my penny exercise book, accounted for. Not dead in ditches. Not drowned off the record. Evacuated. Moved along the coast in fishing boats and coal barges and whatever else Marta-the-smuggler's world provided, ferried out of the machine that had mislaid them and set down somewhere they could heal, or at least somewhere they could be a man with a name again instead of a gap in a column.

I put my head in my hands on the tin table and the metal was cold against my forehead and I stayed like that for a long time.

Because you have to understand what this did to me. For two years I had built a small church out of that ledger, and I was its only congregation, and the faith of it was bitter: *history happens elsewhere, and all we little people can do is pick up what it drops.* Every name I wrote was a protest, yes, but a protest in the dark, to no one. I thought I was recording the war's carelessness. Its waste. I thought the omissions were the system failing men.

They weren't failures. They were rescues. Someone had looked at the same gaps I saw and seen not negligence but opportunity — a machinery so clogged with its own paper that a determined man could pass living soldiers through its holes like coins under a table. The ledger and the ghost were the same story. I had been keeping the receipt book for a smuggling operation I never knew existed, mourning men who were alive, and the whole time the author of my grief had been signing his work with initials I knew better than my own.

Elias Bram. Officially dead since 1914. Officially — as I had learned that afternoon from a memorandum Corvane's office circulated, all stiff collars and sealed edges — a defector, a collaborator, a traitor whose name officers were instructed to speak of, if at all, with contempt.

And privately, secretly, in seven salt-damaged letters: the man who had carried my forty-three out of the dark, one bad knee and one fever and one pair of miners at a time.

* * *

"You've found it."

Anselm's voice, cracked and low, from the doorway. I hadn't heard him wake. He stood leaning against the frame in a hospital shirt far too big for him, grey-faced, holding the doorframe the way a man holds a rail in heavy weather. His eyes went to the fan of letters and something in his face eased, the way a load eases when it's finally set down.

"How long have you been standing there?"

"Long enough." He came in sideways, favouring his ribs, and lowered himself onto the bench across from me without asking, which told me something about how much strength he had. Up close he smelled of carbolic and salt. "You count things," he said. "He said you would. Said you were the only man on this coast who'd notice forty-three names missing from nowhere."

"He knew about the ledger." My voice came out flat. "Two years of knowing."

"He knew about you longer than that." Anselm took one of the letters — the ninth — and held it almost tenderly, like a bird with a broken wing. "Mr Bram talked about you. In the boats, in the safe houses, all those nights waiting for tide. He'd say, *there's a clerk in the port hospital who writes down every name the army loses. If I'm ever a name, he'll have me.*" He looked up. "You were his proof, sir. That somebody ashore kept honest books. That's why the letters come here. That's why I come here."

"Why does anything come here?" I heard the crack in my own voice and hated it. "Why me? Why not — anyone? A priest, a newspaper—"

"A newspaper prints what it's told. A priest prays for what he's given." Anselm smiled, wincing. "A clerk writes what's true. Mr Bram says truth needs a place to live, and you're it. The ledger's not your hobby, sir. It's the house."

I wanted to argue. Some part of me, the part that had spent two years believing history happened elsewhere, reached automatically for the objection — *I'm just a hospital clerk, I write in a penny exercise book, none of this is mine* — and found the objection had rotted through. Because the letters were mine now. The numbers were my numbers. Whatever Bram had built out of holes in the system, he had built it on the foundation of my book, and either I had been an unwitting accomplice for two years or I was being invited, right now, at four in the morning, in a room full of dead men's chill, to become a willing one.

"I mourned him," I said. It came out smaller than I intended. "Two years. I stood at a memorial service in St Aldhelm's with his mother's name read out alongside his, because she couldn't bear to come herself, and I — I grieved him, Anselm. Properly. And all that time—"

"All that time he was carrying your names out of France on his back." Anselm said it gently. "Yes, sir. And he let you grieve, because grieving you was the price of keeping you safe, and he paid it every day, and he never once stopped being sorry. He told me that too. He said, *tell Fairweather I'm sorry, and tell him sorry isn't the half of it.*"

I looked down at the letters. At the ledger. At the forty-three names sitting in their ruled lines like birds that had flown home to find their cage still standing.

"They'll call him a coward," I said. "Corvane's lot. Collaborator. Traitor. I read the memorandum. Defected, it says. Took money, it says."

"What does your book say?" Anselm asked.

And that was the question, wasn't it. My book said Private Daniel Voss walked badly but he walked. It said Thomas Ellery was delivered. It said forty-three men whom the Empire had written off as losses were, by the grace of one officially-dead officer and a smuggler's fleet, alive. My book said the opposite of everything the sealed memoranda said, and my book was the only witness in the country that had been present, page by page, for the entire two years.

"The morning lists will go out at six," I said, because practicality was the ledge I had to grab. "Sister Agnes expects the returns. Corvane is interviewing staff today — he was in Matron's office yesterday evening, I saw his car. If he's hunting a ghost along this coast, and the ghost's correspondence is sitting on a morgue table—"

"Then the letters need to be somewhere they aren't." Anselm tried to stand and failed, and sat back down, breathing carefully. "There's more, sir. Before you decide anything. The last letter — the forty-one. It's not like the others. Open it."

I hesitated. Then I broke the wax.

Inside, one line, and this time it was addressed to me by name, and the handwriting had lost its fence-rail confidence, the letters slanting where a wounded man had written lying down:

*Theo. If Anselm reaches you, I am close, and hurt, and out of road. You made me a promise once, in peacetime, and I have no right to call it in — but I am calling it in. Come to the lighthouse when you can. Bring the book. — E.B.*

The lamp flickered. Outside, far off toward the harbour, a ship's horn sounded the change of watch, and the tide, which governs everything in this town including, it appeared, my life, had begun to turn.

I closed the ledger. Forty-three names, and now a forty-fourth reason.

"Get your coat, Anselm," I said. "We're going to need Sister Agnes."
