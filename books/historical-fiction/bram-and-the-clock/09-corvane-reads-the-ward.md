## 9. Corvane Reads the Ward

Major Corvane arrived at half past seven, before the morning shift had properly begun, and stood in the receiving hall of the hospital reading the notice board the way other men read a newspaper.

He had chosen his clothes carefully, which was to say he had chosen them not to be noticed. A major's uniform, yes, but the older cut, the one that said *I have been at this too long to care about appearances*. His boots were polished but not mirror-bright. He carried a leather folder under his arm and nothing else — no escort, no clerk, no visible weight of authority. He wanted, on the first morning, to be underestimated.

The hall smelled of carbolic and wet wool. A porter hurried past with a trolley of bandages and Corvane stepped aside for him, murmured *good morning* as though they were equals. The porter blinked, confused, and Corvane noted the confusion. Good. Men who did not expect courtesy from intelligence officers gave truer answers when it came.

The notice board held the usual: duty rosters, a Red Cross appeal, a list of comforts wanted — socks, tobacco, writing paper. And pinned slightly askew at the corner, a casualty information sheet, the kind distributed to clerks for transcription. Corvane read it twice, then took from his pocket a folded paper of his own and compared them line by line.

Four names. All four present in both documents. He made no mark, showed no expression. But he had now confirmed what the leaked copies from Boulogne had suggested: this hospital's records office transcribed accurately. Which meant the omissions he was hunting had not been made here by accident.

They had been made here on purpose. Or — the more interesting possibility — corrected here after someone else had made them elsewhere.

He returned the paper to his pocket and went to find the matron.

* * *

Sister Agnes received him in her office, a small room dominated by a cabinet of medicines kept under lock and a photograph of a young man in Royal Army Medical Corps uniform, dead nine years before the war and mourned as though it were yesterday.

"Major Corvane." She did not offer her hand. "I had a telegram yesterday saying you might come."

"The telegram understates my pleasure at being here." He sat without being asked, arranged the folder on his knee. "You run a large establishment, Sister. How many beds?"

"One hundred and forty. We are carrying one hundred and eighty."

"And your staff?"

"Thirty-one nurses, eleven orderlies, two clerks, a cookhouse of nine, and whatever the Navy sends us when it feels like it."

"You'll forgive the questions. I like to know the shape of a thing before I look inside it."

"Most men do," Agnes said.

She was older than he'd expected — sixty, perhaps, with the forearms of a woman who had done forty years of lifting and the eyes of one who had watched every sort of man die. She stood while she spoke, hands folded, and Corvane understood within thirty seconds that he would get nothing from this room by charm. She had outlived charm. She had probably outlived better men than him.

So he went straight instead. It was the compliment such women understood.

"A leak of casualty records has moved through three Channel ports in five months," he said. "Names that appear nowhere in official returns. Someone is keeping a second ledger, Sister — or correcting the first. This hospital is one of four points where the false and true records intersect."

"And you think my clerks are forging casualty lists between tea breaks."

"I think someone in this building touches paper every day that other people's sons are written on."

For the first time she looked at him fully, and Corvane had the sensation — rare, unpleasant — of being measured and found to be a known quantity.

"My clerks work eighteen-hour days," she said. "One of them, Mr. Fairweather, keeps records so accurate the War Office wrote to commend him. If there is a discrepancy anywhere, I promise you it will be in the direction of more names, not fewer. Now. Is there something wrong with a wounded soldier in this hospital, Major? Because if so I would like to know which bed, and if not, I have rounds."

There it was — the question he had come to answer, asked directly, and he could not answer it, because he did not yet know. He smiled, gathered his folder, and stood.

"No soldier is wrong yet, Sister. I'm here to make sure of it. You'll see me about the wards today. Consider me furniture."

"Furniture," Agnes repeated, "does not usually introduce itself," and she opened the door for him.

* * *

He began with Ward C because the night boat had docked at eleven-forty and the admissions book showed six men brought in from it, and men off night boats were always the freshest source of whatever the tide carried.

By nine he had spoken to four of them. He did it lightly, sitting on the edge of beds, producing cigarettes, asking after their regiments, their wounds, their homes — and then, folded into the small talk so smoothly most of them never registered it: *who else came off the boat with you? Anyone you didn't know? Anyone hurt worse than the rest?*

Three of the four had seen nothing. The fourth, a private with a splinted arm from the Manchester regiment, hesitated.

"There was a fellow," he said. "On the quay. Not one of ours, we thought. They carried him in last, after everyone else had gone up. Big man. Half drowned, looked like. They put him somewhere — I don't know where. Didn't see him again."

"Did anyone say his name?"

"Matron's lot don't tell you much." The private drew on the cigarette and coughed. "Funny thing, though. One of the chaps said he heard him say a word before they took him down the stairs. Just the one word."

Corvane waited. He had learned long ago that waiting was the most underrated tool in his trade.

"*Alive,*" the private said. "Said *alive*, like it was news. Like it mattered."

Corvane thanked him, left the cigarette tin on the blanket, and walked out into the corridor with his pulse elevated by exactly four beats. He permitted himself that. Four beats was a reasonable expenditure for confirmation.

A man with no papers, carried in last from the night boat, whose first word was *alive*. The ghost was not moving along the coast anymore.

The ghost was in the building.

* * *

He spent the middle of the day doing what he thought of as reading the ward — not the patients but the staff, the way a man reads a room for exits. He watched who avoided his eye and who sought it. He learned the nurses' names from their aprons and used them, warmly. He helped carry a kidney dish. He praised the coffee in the mess and drank two cups of it.

By noon he had a working map of the hospital's human terrain. The orderlies were tired and closed. The young nurses giggled when he flirted and told him nothing. The senior sister on Ward A was loyal to Agnes and would need handling separately. And the clerk Fairweather —

Fairweather was interesting.

Corvane observed him through the open door of the records office for perhaps ten minutes without speaking. A thin man, early thirties, with the careful hands of someone who handled paper the way surgeons handled instruments. He worked fast and checked everything twice. When a nurse brought him an admission slip, he read it, frowned at something, asked her a quiet question, corrected the slip himself, and filed it.

Meticulous. Commended by the War Office. And — Corvane checked his file notes — the same Theo Fairweather who had signed the transcription sheet for the Boulogne intercepts three months ago. Innocently, almost certainly. Most men were innocent. But meticulous men who touched intersecting records were either the solution or the problem, and Corvane would find out which before he left the building tonight.

He knocked on the doorframe.

"Mr. Fairweather? Major Corvane. May I take a quarter hour of your afternoon?"

* * *

The interview lasted fifty minutes, which Corvane counted a success. Fairweather answered everything — his length of service, his methods, his system of cross-checking against field returns — fluently, precisely, and with the flat helpfulness of a man reciting a timetable. He volunteered nothing. His hands never stopped moving, squaring papers that were already square.

Corvane tried the oblique approach.

"You must develop feelings about names," he said. "After a while. All those lists."

Fairweather looked up. First time his eyes had gone still.

"Names aren't a matter of feeling, sir. They're a matter of record."

"Of course. Only — I've found that men in your position sometimes keep little books of their own. Notes. Nothing improper. A way of carrying the weight."

The pause lasted one and a half seconds. Corvane counted it.

"If I kept a diary, Major, there'd be no time to write it in."

"No," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "There never is."

He rose, thanked him, praised the state of the files, and left the records office knowing two things. First: Fairweather was lying about something, though possibly only about being afraid, which nearly everyone was. Second: the fear in the room had a direction. Fairweather's eyes had flicked, twice, toward the east stairwell — once when Corvane mentioned the night boat, once when he mentioned unauthorized persons.

The east stairwell led down.

* * *

Down, Corvane discovered, was the morgue annex, the kitchen stores, and the old boathouse passage — disused since the hospital expanded, according to a porter who talked freely in exchange for nothing more than being listened to. Down was also cold, and a half-drowned man with no papers needed warmth, unless someone had thought to give him a blanket and a hiding place among the dead.

At four o'clock Corvane descended the east stairwell alone.

The annex corridor ran sixty feet, lit by one bulb. Storage racks along one wall; the mortuary door at the end, shut. He walked slowly, letting his boots announce him, and stopped halfway, listening. Somewhere to his left, behind a rack of empty cratess: breathing. Controlled, deliberate, the breathing of a man trying not to breathe.

"Hello?" he called, mildly, into the dark of the storage alcove. "Anyone down here?"

Silence. The breathing stopped altogether, which was itself an answer.

Corvane did not go to the alcove. That was the trick of it — the moment he went, he committed; and if he committed alone, in a basement, against a man who might be anything, he lost the advantage he had spent all day constructing. No. What he wanted was not a body. He wanted the machinery around the body: who fed it, who moved it, who signed for it. A ghost caught was just a corpse. A ghost traced backwards was a conspiracy, and conspiracies had names on lists, and lists were his profession.

So he called out again — *"Anyone there?"* — heard nothing but the pipes, and turned back up the stairs with the unhurried tread of a man satisfied with his day.

Behind the crates, Anselm let his breath out in a thread and pressed his bleeding side harder against the cold stone.

* * *

He passed Sister Agnes at the top of the stairs. She had a chart in her hands and the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she disliked.

"Major. You look pleased with yourself."

"I've had an educational day. Your hospital is admirably run." He paused, as if the thought had only just occurred to him. "Tell me — do you keep bodies below? In the annex?"

"We keep the dead where the dead can rest," Agnes said. "Which is more than some institutions manage."

"Of course. And living men? Ever had occasion to put one down there? For observation, say?"

The question hung in the corridor air. Thirty years of service stood behind Agnes's face, thirty years of charts countersigned and rules obeyed and reports filed clean, and Corvane watched her decide — saw it happen, actually saw it, the fractional settling of her shoulders, the way a ship decides on a heading.

"No," she said. "No living man has ever been in my annex."

It was the first lie she had told in uniform, and it came out steady as scripture, and Corvane — who heard lies daily and knew their music perfectly — heard the truth underneath this one: that she would tell it again, and worse ones, and would count the cost later.

He smiled and bowed slightly.

"Good evening, Sister. I'll be at the Pier Hotel if anything occurs to you. Anything at all. My door doesn't lock."

Agnes watched him walk away down the corridor, upright, unhurried, a man entirely certain of his ground, and she stood holding her chart until her hands stopped trembling.

Then she went to find Theo Fairweather, because whatever was lying behind those crates below her wards had less than a day to stay hidden — and there was one key left in this building that Corvane hadn't thought to ask for.
