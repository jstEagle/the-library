## 10. Sister Agnes's Silence

The matron's office smelled of carbolic and camphor and, beneath it, the faint sweetness of the lavender Agnes kept in a dish on the windowsill because her mother had done the same, thirty years ago, in another hospital on another coast where the wounded had come home from a different war. Agnes Wren had buried more sons than she could count now. She kept no ledger of them. She didn't need one.

She sat at her desk with her hands folded — she always folded her hands when the counting started, as if in prayer, though she hadn't prayed properly since 1914 — and she did the arithmetic she did every evening. Not the ward returns, not the linen counts. The other arithmetic.

Four hundred and eleven.

That was as close as she could get. Some of them had come through her hands so briefly that they'd left nothing but a weight on a stretcher and a smell of salt and cordite, and those she couldn't number with any certainty. But four hundred and eleven boys had died at St. Brendan's since August of '14, and Agnes had signed the certificates for every one of them, and she had written to every mother in her own careful hand, the hand that had once been praised for its elegance by an inspector from the Royal College who had never once watched her hold a boy's head while he went.

The letters all said the same thing. *He did not suffer long. He was brave. He thought of you at the end.*

Some of it was even true.

She unfolded her hands and looked out the window. The harbour lay grey under a sky the colour of old pewter, and the tide was coming in over the mudflats, and somewhere down on the quay the gulls were making their argument about the morning's catch. A troopship's smoke stood up straight against the horizon like a thing planted there.

She was sixty-one years old. She had been matron here for eleven years, and before that a sister for nine more, and before that a probationer who had fainted at the first amputation and been sick behind the coal shed and come back inside and washed her hands and asked to be taught. She had served under three commandants, survived two inspections that should have ended her career, and nursed through the scarlet fever winter of ought-nine when they'd run out of beds and laid children in the corridors. Her record was clean. That was the phrase. *A clean record.* Thirty years of it, unspotted, and she had understood lately — these last weeks especially, watching Major Corvane move through her wards like a man taking inventory of things he intended to own — that a clean record was simply the story obedience tells about itself.

* * *

The major had come again that afternoon.

He arrived without warning, as he always did, his motorcar standing at the gate with its driver smoking in the road, and he came up the drive with his stick tapping a rhythm Agnes found herself hating — tap-tap, pause, tap-tap — a metronome for something she refused to name. He wore his greatcoat open despite the cold and he smiled at her in the doorway the way a man smiles at a door he has already decided to open.

"Sister Agnes." He removed his cap. His hair was iron-grey and precise, cut short as a verdict. "You're looking well."

"I'm looking tired, Major. It's the honest answer, so you may as well have it."

"Honesty." He stepped past her into the hall, uninvited, his boots ringing on the stone. "I find it rarer than courage, in my experience. Courage is common enough. Every boy on that quay has some. But honesty—" He turned, and his eyes went down the corridor toward Ward C, where Theo Fairweather was moving among the cots with his tray of dressings, and lingered there perhaps half a second too long. "Honesty has to be taught. Or bought."

"We're busy, Major. There are forty men in that ward and two orderlies."

"Then I'll be brief." From inside his coat he took a folded paper — not an official form; Agnes knew official forms the way a carpenter knows wood, and this was not one — and held it between two fingers. "We recovered a uniform yesterday. Bloodied. River-washed. It belonged to a private soldier, Anselm Voss, listed drowned off the night boat three nights ago. Curious thing about Private Voss: he appears in no casualty list. No hospital intake. No burial record. As if the sea swallowed him whole and tidy." He refolded the paper slowly, creasing each edge with his thumbnail. "And yet someone carried him out of that water. And someone dried him, and fed him, and hid him. Someone with access to linen, to food, to a building full of quiet rooms."

"The sea gives back what it likes," Agnes said. "I've seen it give back boots from Dunkirk."

"Mm." The smile again, thinner. "Sister, I want to tell you something about myself, because I admire you and I think we understand each other. I don't believe in evil. It's a lazy word. What I believe in is disorder — small disorders, tolerated, which grow. A name left off a list. A key unaccounted for. A lie told kindly by a good woman who thinks kindness is the same as goodness." He put the paper away. "Disorder spreads, Sister Agnes. My work is simply tidiness. When I've finished, everything will be in its proper place, and everyone will know exactly what happened, and no one will have to feel guilty about anything ever again. Doesn't that sound restful?"

"It sounds like a hospital run by an undertaker."

For the first time, something moved behind his eyes that was not a smile. "Rest well," he said, and tapped his way back down the drive.

* * *

Afterward she went to Ward C.

She told herself it was a routine round, and she performed it as one — checking the dressing trolley, speaking to the man in the third cot whose fever had broken in the night, adjusting the screen around the soldier who'd lost both eyes at the Somme and who always turned his face toward her footsteps now, tracking her by sound alone. But her feet carried her, at the end of it, to where Theo Fairweather stood at the window at the far end of the ward, doing nothing, holding nothing, just standing in the grey light with his hands loose at his sides, and she saw in the set of his shoulders a tension she recognized from thirty years of reading bodies: a man carrying something heavy and deciding where to set it down.

"Fairweather."

He turned. He had his father's face, she thought — not the resemblance people meant, the family resemblance, but the other kind, the one that showed only at certain angles and certain hours. Gerald Fairweather had stood at that same window in 1901, waiting for a list, and she had liked him then and failed him later, and she had never decided which of those facts mattered more.

"You look pale," she said. "Sit down before you fall down."

"I'm all right, Matron."

"No one is all right. Sit."

He sat on the sill, and for a while neither spoke. Below them, the laundry yard steamed; two women wringing sheets looked up at the window and away again. Down at the water, the tide was nearly full.

"The major came to see me too," Theo said quietly.

"I know. I watched him find you. He has a way of finding people, like weather."

"He showed me my father's file."

Agnes said nothing. She had wondered how long it would take Corvane to dig that particular spade out of the ground. She had been there, in a junior way, in the year of it — young Sister Wren, copying registers, when the order came down that a certain clerk's record was to be closed and consolidated and the original correspondence destroyed as redundant. She had copied the register entry herself. *Closed. Consolidated.* Four words for a man's whole life, and she had written them in her elegant hand because she was told to, and she had thought, even then, even at twenty-two, that the words felt like a lid going down on something breathing.

"He wasn't a traitor," Theo said. It came out flat, rehearsed, a sentence he'd been saying to himself until it held his weight.

"No," Agnes said. "He was a clerk who saw something he shouldn't have, in a year when seeing was dangerous. And when they erased him, they erased him thoroughly, because thoroughness is what they do instead of admitting fault." She looked at him. "You keep a book, Fairweather."

It was not a question. He went still in the way of a man who has heard a floorboard give beneath him.

"I've kept hospitals for thirty years," she said. "You think I don't know which of my staff writes in the margins? You write small, you write fast, you hide it in your casualty returns, and you think because no one reads the margins, the names aren't real." She let the silence sit. "I read the margins, boy. I've read them for two years. Forty-three names, and not one of them in any list I've ever seen cross my desk. I checked. Don't look so surprised — checking is most of what matrons do."

"Matron—"

"Don't confess to me. Confession is for priests and prisoners, and I'm neither." She glanced down the ward — the blind man turning his face toward their voices, the fevered man sleeping — and lowered hers. "But I will tell you what I told no one else, and then I'm going to do something I've never done, and we'll speak of neither again."

* * *

They walked out along the cliff path above the boathouse because indoors had ears now — she was sure of it; she'd found her inkwell moved a quarter-inch on her desk and she moved nothing in that room but her own hands, so either she was mad or someone searched while she slept, and Agnes Wren had never been mad in her life.

The wind came hard off the Channel and pushed at their coats. Below, wedged into the rocks beneath the old sea wall, the hospital boathouse sat with its doors shut and its paint flaking, a low dark shape the colour of the stone it leaned against. It had been built in her first year as matron, for the bathing cure, back when doctors still believed sea air could mend lungs. No one used it now except gulls and rats and, once a season, herself, when she went down to sit on the slipway with her flask of tea and watch the water and remember that the world had once contained summers.

"Gerald Fairweather trusted the wrong person," she said as they walked, pitching her voice under the wind. "Not a friend. An institution. He believed that if he put the truth in the right hands, the right hands would hold it. They dropped it. That's all treachery usually is — dropped, not thrown. People imagine betrayal as a knife. Mostly it's a parcel nobody signs for."

"And you?" Theo said. "Do you trust the institution?"

The question hung there, insolent and frightened at once, and she found she loved him for asking it.

"I trust what I have buried," she said. "Four hundred and eleven boys, Fairweather, and I signed every certificate, and I wrote every letter, and I told every mother *he thought of you at the end*, and do you know what I have learned? That the lists are the war's version of the truth, and the lists are wrong. Not sometimes. Structurally. By design. A list is a machine for deciding who counted, and the men who make the lists decide first, and then the numbers follow." She stopped walking. Below them the tide worried at the slipway, patient as a debt. "Your ledger says forty-three names that no list contains. The major says there's a ghost moving along this coast pulling men out of the water. You'll forgive an old woman for noticing those are the same sentence."

Theo stared at her. In the wind his eyes were bright and wet and trying not to be either.

"You've known?"

"I suspected. Knowing comes later, and costs extra." She started walking again, faster now, before her courage could cool the way tea cools. "Here is what I know for certain. Two nights ago, an orderly reported blankets missing. One blanket, actually — a field blanket, grey, army issue. And a basin, and a length of bandage unaccounted for, and a tin of condensed milk from stores. Small thefts. Disorder, the major would say." She smiled, and it was not a warm smile. "I signed the stores sheet covering all of it. My signature, my responsibility, thirty years clean. So you see, boy, I made my decision before you came to ask. You're late."

* * *

At the top of the boathouse steps she stopped and took the ring of keys from her belt.

She had worn that ring for eleven years. It was part of her, the way the habit of folding her hands was part of her, the way the lavender was part of her — the small iron liturgy of a life spent keeping things locked and accounted. Dispensary. Drug cupboard. Records room. Linen store. Morgue annex. And at the bottom of the ring, on a separate loop she'd added herself years ago, the boathouse keys, which opened a building containing nothing worth stealing, which she kept anyway because a matron keeps everything, because keeping was the whole of what she was.

She worked the boathouse keys off the loop. Her fingers were stiff — sixty-one years and thirty winters of lye water — and it took longer than she wanted it to, and Theo stood watching with an expression she refused to look at directly, because if she looked at it she might stop.

"There's a boat under the canvas," she said. "Old, but the hull's sound; I had it tarred in the spring, God knows why — call it sentiment. There's oil for the lamp and a stove that lights if you swear at it properly. Water doesn't run there, so carry it in. And the floorboard at the back, third from the wall — it lifts. Underneath there's a box. Blankets, candles, and a bottle of brandy I confiscated from a chaplain in 1915 and have been saving for an occasion I hoped would never require it."

"Matron." His voice cracked on the word.

"If you thank me I'll strike you across the ear with this ring, promise or no promise." She pressed the keys into his palm and closed his fingers over them, and his hand was cold and shaking and younger than it had any right to be. "Listen to me now. I have buried sons, plural, though I bore none — every boy who died on my wards died in my arms or near enough, and I wrote to their mothers, and I lied to their mothers, gently, because the truth was a luxury the war had requisitioned. I have spent thirty years being obedient, and obedient got me a clean record and a dish of lavender and four hundred and eleven funerals." The wind took a strand of her grey hair and whipped it across her face and she left it there. "I am done being tidy. Whatever happens next — whatever that man with the stick digs up, whatever they take from me — you remember that an old woman chose this with her eyes open. I was not deceived. I was not coerced. I read the margins, and I decided the names matter more than the record, because the record is theirs and the names are ours."

"The names are ours," Theo repeated. Quietly. Like learning a prayer.

"Now go down and light your stove, and swear at it as instructed." She turned back up the path. Three steps, and then she stopped, because there was one thing more and she had carried it up from the morgue annex in her chest all afternoon. She didn't turn around. It was easier said facing the sea.

"The boy in the annex. Voss." The wind filled the pause. "When he wakes — and he'll wake, he's got a farmer's constitution under all that navy wool — you tell him there's a woman in this hospital who buried four hundred and eleven sons, and she has room in her heart for one more living one. Tell him he eats at my table tonight. The dead can spare us the company."

She walked on up the cliff path, tapping her own rhythm now against the turf with her stick — tap-tap, pause, tap-tap — and she did not hear whether he answered, because the wind had risen and the tide below was turning, sliding silver-grey over the mudflats, coming in the way it always came, indifferent and enormous and keeping its own accounts. Behind her, faintly, she heard the boathouse door open on its stiff hinge, and the sound of it went through her like a bell through water.

In her office that evening she moved the lavender dish aside, took out a fresh sheet of paper, and began, in her elegant hand, a letter to no one — a record of one day's small treasons, dated precisely, to be burned or kept as the times required. At the bottom, after the stores figures and the missing blanket and the keys, she wrote the only line that mattered:

*Let the record show that Sister A. Wren knew, and chose.*

Outside, far down the coast toward the ruined lighthouse, the last light went off the water, and three nights suddenly felt like very few.
