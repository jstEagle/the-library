## 11. A Message in the Tide Line

The tide left its offerings every morning along the shingle below the hospital wall: kelp in black ropes, a ship's fender, once an entire church pew from God knew where, sitting upright on the stones as if waiting for a congregation that had drowned. Theo walked the tide line most mornings before his shift, because it was the only hour the port belonged to him and to no one's war.

On the fourth morning after he hid Anselm in the morgue annex, the sea delivered a message.

He almost missed it. It was nothing — a scatter of pale things arranged among the stones, the kind of debris the Channel threw up by the yard. But his eye had been trained for two years now on what didn't belong, on the small wrongnesses that official eyes slid past, and something about the arrangement snagged him like a fishhook.

Six pieces of driftwood, each about the length of a finger, laid in a line above the wrack mark. Beside them, three limpet shells. And threaded through a knot of dried bladderwrack, a strip of oilcloth — the kind used to wrap signal flares, the kind that kept paper dry through a crossing.

Theo stood very still. A gull screamed at him from the groyne, offended by his stillness.

He looked up the beach and down it. Empty except for a boy digging for lugworms two hundred yards off, bent double over his fork, interested in nothing but bait. The fog was coming in off the water in long grey fingers, softening the cranes at the quay, softening everything.

Theo crouched. He did not touch anything at first. He had learned that much from Major Corvane, who had spent an afternoon in the records room explaining, with terrible patience, how a man could be undone by the evidence of his own hands. *Everything you touch is a confession, Mr. Fairweather.* Corvane had said it as a courtesy. As a warning dressed as a courtesy.

But the arrangement of the wood was already speaking, and it spoke in a language Theo knew better than any cipher manual could teach.

* * *

In the summer of 1913, before everything, Elias Bram had spent a rainbound week at the Fairweather house recovering from a fever caught on maneuvers, and had filled the enforced idleness by teaching Theo the games he'd learned at staff college. Not cards. Games of arrangement. He would take whatever was at hand — matchsticks, buttons, the sugar lumps Theo's mother hoarded against shortage — and lay them out in patterns that meant nothing until suddenly they meant everything.

"Anyone can write a letter," Bram said, moving a button a half-inch left with the deliberation of a chess player. "Letters get read. Letters get *found*. But if you and I agree on a grammar first, then a spilled box of matches on a windowsill says more than any letter ever could."

"And who agrees this grammar with you?" Theo had asked. "You can't post instructions ahead."

"You're not listening." Bram tapped his own temple. "The grammar lives here. You don't send the code. You send the man who carries the code in his skull. That's why it works. That's why it can't be broken — there's nothing to break."

"What does the button mean?"

"The button means *wait*. The matchstick across it means *wait for me*. If the matchstick lies under the button—" he slid it there, "—it means *don't wait, I'm not coming*."

Theo remembered laughing at the vanity of it. *When would we ever need this? We live in England, Elias. What could possibly happen to us that we couldn't simply say aloud?*

Bram hadn't laughed. He had looked out the rain-streaked window at the garden going green and grey, and said, quietly, "That's rather the point of a promise, Theo. You make it for the day you can't say things aloud."

Now, four years later, on a cold shingle beach with fog swallowing the horizon, Theo counted.

Six lengths of driftwood. In their grammar — the one they'd built that rainy week, extended and refined over letters in the first year of the war, when Bram was stationed close enough to pass through the port and they'd spent one golden afternoon on this same beach laying out signals with pebbles while the world beyond the Channel burned — six marks in a line meant a number. Six. Three shells beside it: three days. The oilcloth meant *written word follows*, or *look closer*, depending on which version of the grammar —

No. There was only one way to know. The oilcloth was the invitation to look closer.

Theo glanced once more up and down the beach. The boy was still digging. The fog had thickened. He picked up the knot of bladderwrack, felt the weight of something stiff inside it, and walked — not fast, not slow, a man merely taking his morning exercise — back toward the hospital wall, where a drainpipe offered cover enough to open it.

Inside the wrack, wrapped in the oilcloth, folded to the size of a stamp: a scrap of paper gone soft at the edges with salt.

Four words, in handwriting Theo would have known blindfolded, upside down, in the dark, in hell. The hand that had written him jokes from training camp. The hand that had signed the promise they'd made drunk on the vicarage lawn, both of them nineteen and immortal. The hand that had stopped appearing in casualty lists two years ago and started appearing instead in Theo's ledger, in the gaps, in the absences, in the forty-three names that were missing from the record and present in Theo's book.

*Light-house. Third night. Tide.*

That was all. No signature. No endearment. Bram was too careful for endearments now; endearments were fingerprints.

But beneath the four words, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper, there was a fifth mark — a small cross, like an anchor crossed with a sword. Their old sign. The sign Bram had scratched into the bark of the Fairweathers' apple tree the day he'd made the vow, drunk enough to be solemn: *If ever either of us needs to vanish, the other makes it so. No questions asked. No price refused.*

Alive. Asking. Coming home.

Theo put the paper in his mouth.

It was the only place he could think of. His hands were shaking too badly to trust a pocket, and the annex, the ward, the records room — all of them had eyes now, because Corvane had made sure of it. He chewed the paper twice, swallowed it down with the taste of salt and ink and two years of grief reversing themselves all at once, and stood at the hospital wall with his heart going like a field telegraph.

Then he was sick into the channel of the drain, quietly, thoroughly, and wiped his mouth, and went in to work.

* * *

The morning passed in a blur of ledgers and lint. Theo admitted eleven men off a destroyer that had found a mine, wrote their names in the admission book in his careful clerk's hand, and then wrote them again — all but one — in the other ledger, the private one, the one that lived under the floorboard beneath his cot. The eleventh man had come in without identification, unconscious, and the admitting sister had penciled *unknown* beside his bed number, and Theo had thought: *not unknown. Unrecorded. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole war.*

Anselm was awake when Theo got down to the annex at midday.

The morgue annex was a stone lean-to against the north wall, cold as a cellar, used mostly for overflow stores since the new mortuary was built. Theo had installed Anselm there behind a screen of stacked stretchers, with blankets, a spirit stove, and a bucket, and had told himself the irony of hiding a living man among the dead was a joke someone else could appreciate later, if there was a later.

Anselm was sitting up against the wall, grey-faced but lucid, working his way through a bowl of Sister Agnes's barley broth with the concentration of a man performing surgery on himself.

"How's the chest?" Theo asked.

"Sewn together," Anselm said. His voice was gravel and rust. "Your matron sews like she's angry at the wound."

"She is. She's angry at everything the war has done to boys' bodies. It comes out as excellent suturing." Theo crouched, checked the dressing, checked the fever line on Anselm's forehead with the back of his hand. Still warm, but holding. "I need to ask you something, and I need you to think carefully, because I believe your life depends on getting it right."

Anselm set down the bowl. Up close, in the annex's grey light, his face was a map of hard travel — a scar through one eyebrow, sunburn peeling on his nose, eyes that had learned to measure exits. "Ask."

"When Bram sends messages — arrangements, rendezvous — does he use objects? Things laid out in patterns?"

Something moved behind Anselm's eyes. Recognition, and then caution, and then a decision. "Tide lines," he said. "Rocks. Fence posts. Once, a butcher's window in Boulogne — sausages and kidneys laid out so only the right person would count them right." He paused. "You've found one."

"This morning. On the shingle." Theo kept his voice low. The annex wall was thin and the coal store next door was sometimes occupied. "Lighthouse. Third night. Before the tide."

Anselm let out a breath that seemed to empty him of half his remaining strength. "Third night," he repeated. "Then he's close. Close enough to reach the beach himself, or near enough to trust a runner." His hand closed on Theo's wrist — a grip surprisingly strong for a half-drowned man. "Mr. Fairweather. Listen to me. If Bram sent for you, it's because he trusts you with the only thing he has left, which is his own skin. And if the major's men find that message site before you've cleared it—"

"I cleared it. Wood in my pocket, shells scattered, oilcloth eaten."

"*Eaten?*"

"It seemed safest."

Anselm stared at him, and then — for the first time since the night boat — he laughed. It was a wrecked sound, half cough, but it was laughter, and it transformed his face entirely. "Oh, he'll like you," he said. "He always said you were the steady one. Steady men do the maddest things, he said. Everyone else is mad all the time, so nobody notices."

Theo felt heat climb his neck. "What did he say about me? Exactly. When?"

"On the boat. Crossing from — never mind where from. He was delirious half the time, fevered, but he talked. About a clerk who kept names the army threw away." Anselm's grip tightened. "He said, *if I die on this water, find Fairweather. He'll know what to do with the truth. He's been keeping it safe for me for two years without knowing it.*"

The annex went very quiet. Somewhere above them, the hospital bell rang the hour, and the sound came down through the stone like a stone itself.

"He knew," Theo said. "About my ledger. All this time, he knew."

"He arranged it," Anselm said gently. "Who do you think made sure those forty-three men passed through *your* port, *your* wards, on their way out? He couldn't tell you. Telling you would have killed you and the men both. So he did the next best thing. He made you the record." He released Theo's wrist. "You weren't keeping a ledger, Mr. Fairweather. You were keeping a promise you didn't know you'd made."

* * *

Theo spent the afternoon being two men.

The first man was the clerk: efficient, unremarkable, invisible in the way that only truly good clerks are invisible. He processed paperwork. He answered Corvane's assistant's polite questions about stationery requisitions with the tedium they deserved. He drank tea in the records room and discussed filing systems with a corporal who was almost certainly reporting on him, and gave the corporal nothing whatsoever to report.

The second man was counting hours.

Three nights. Sixty-odd hours between now and the rendezvous. He ran the arithmetic in the back of his mind while his hands moved forms from tray to tray. The lighthouse was four miles along the coast — two hours' walk in good conditions, longer in the dark, longer still if he was carrying anything, which he would be. Blankets. The spirit stove. Food. Whatever a man needed who had been dead for two years and intended, tonight, to stop being dead.

And Bram himself was wounded. Anselm had said it plainly: fevered, half-delirious on the crossing. If Bram was coming to the lighthouse, he was coming damaged. Someone would need to move him from the lighthouse to wherever the ship would take him aboard, and that someone, presumably, was Theo, and the distance from the ruined lighthouse to any usable landing point was—

He stopped himself. One problem at a time. Bram's grammar had always been economical; the message said *lighthouse, third night, tide*, and nothing else, which meant the rest would be arranged at the lighthouse or not at all. Marta, perhaps. The name Anselm had given him, the smuggler woman, the thread that connected the ghost to the boats. If Marta was part of this, then boats existed, and routes existed, and the impossible had infrastructure.

At four o'clock, Sister Agnes appeared in the doorway of the records room with a linen inventory list that did not require her personal delivery, and stood beside his desk while he pretended to check it.

"Well?" she said, not looking at him.

"Third night. The lighthouse. Before the morning tide."

Her jaw tightened — he saw her bite down on whatever her first response was, and swallow it. Thirty years of discipline. "The major dines with the port commandant tomorrow evening," she said. "I heard the adjutant arranging it. He'll be away from the hospital from seven until late."

"And the night after?"

"Night rounds are mine. They have always been mine." She set the inventory list on his desk with unnecessary precision. "Whatever you're planning, Mr. Fairweather, plan it so that no one else's name is in it but yours. That is not kindness. That is tactics." She turned to go, then stopped. "The boy in the annex asked me today whether I believed a man could come back from being declared dead."

"What did you tell him?"

"I told him I'd buried forty-one sons of other women in this uniform, and not one of them had ever managed to come back and apologize for the trouble." Her voice didn't change at all. "So I am prepared to be surprised."

She left. Theo sat with the inventory list and his own heartbeat, and understood that the net around them had just acquired, silently, another ally — and that allies were also liabilities, and that every person who knew was a door Corvane might one day kick open.

* * *

That night, after lights-out, Theo lay on his cot in the clerks' quarters and listened to the foghorn moaning off the point, one long note every ninety seconds, patient as a pulse.

Under the floorboard, the ledger waited. Forty-three names. He had taken it out earlier and read them again, all of them, running his thumb down the columns the way a priest runs a thumb down a rosary. Privates and sergeants, a sapper, two stokers, a chaplain's assistant. Men the lists had given up. Men who were, according to every official document in the country, dead — and who were therefore, according to the same logic that made Bram a traitor, ghosts, desertions, errors to be corrected.

Except they weren't errors. They were evacuations. Bram had been running men out of the war one and two at a time for two years, and Theo's ledger — his sacred, secret, lonely ledger — had been the passenger manifest all along.

*You were keeping a promise you didn't know you'd made.*

Tomorrow he would prepare. Tomorrow he would lay in supplies, chart the coastal path, work out how a wounded captain and a convalescent batman and a hospital clerk were going to reach a ship that existed only as a rumor in a smuggler's mouth. Tomorrow he would face the arithmetic of what it would cost and who would pay it.

Tonight, he allowed himself one minute — he timed it against the foghorn, four cycles — to feel it. Not hope exactly. Something older and less comfortable than hope. The feeling of a door you had mourned as bricked-over discovering, in the dark, that it had been unlocked all along, and that on the other side of it someone was knocking in a rhythm only you would recognize.

*Light-house. Third night. Tide.*

Two nights now. One after tomorrow.

The foghorn sounded. Theo closed his eyes and began, methodically, to pack the list in his head: blankets, bread, the spirit stove, the flask, the ledger itself — yes, the ledger, because if he was going to meet the author of the missing names, he was going to bring the names.

Outside, the tide turned and started its long pull toward the morning, and somewhere along the dark coast, in a ruin that had watched the sea for two hundred years, a dead man was — if the sea was kind, if the patrols were blind, if the grammar held — waiting for him.
