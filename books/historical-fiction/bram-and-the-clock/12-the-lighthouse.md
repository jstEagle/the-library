## 12. The Lighthouse

The fog came in off the Channel like something with intent.

Theo had walked the coast road a hundred times in peacetime — picnics at the point, Bram's arm slung loose around his shoulders, both of them laughing at nothing — but he had never seen it like this: the world reduced to eleven feet of chalk track and the sound of the sea working somewhere below, patient and enormous. The foghorn out on the mole boomed every forty seconds, and Anselm counted them under his breath, one to forty, then boom, then again, as if arithmetic could hold the night together.

They had left the hospital by the morgue annex door, which was the whole joke of it — two living men walking out through the house of the dead because it was the only door without an orderly on it. Theo carried the canvas bag with blankets, bread, the spirit stove, and beneath his coat, flat against his ribs where his heart could keep an eye on it, the ledger. Anselm carried a hurricane lamp turned down so low it was less a light than a rumor of one.

"Forty," Anselm whispered. "Boom."

"You don't have to count it."

"It keeps me from thinking about other things." He paused. "Mostly I think about how easy it would be for someone to shoot us out here and roll us off the cliff, and no ledger in the world would ever know our names."

"Cheerful."

"I was Bram's batman for three years," Anselm said. "Cheerfulness was never in my job description. Keeping him alive was. Look where that got me."

The path bent inland around the old quarry, then out again onto the headland, and there it was: the lighthouse, or what remained of it. It had been dark since before the war, its lamp room shattered in some Victorian storm and never repaired, so that it stood against the sky like a broken tooth — a stub of tower with jagged glassless windows that caught even this starless night and gave back nothing. The sea had undercut the rocks below it. The grass grew right up to the ruin's door and stopped, as if afraid.

The note had said third night, tide, and nothing else. No time. Bram's codes had never needed clocks; they ran on the water, and the water kept better hours than any sergeant major.

Theo stopped twenty yards out and put his hand on Anselm's arm.

"If it isn't him," he said quietly, "if this is Corvane, if this is anyone else—"

"Then we run back along the cliff path and you push me first, because I'm slower."

"That's not funny."

"It wasn't a joke." Anselm lifted the lamp a fraction, and in the dying amber Theo saw the other man's face: young under the damage, resolute in the way of people who have already lost everything once and found it survivable, barely. "Mr. Fairweather. I drowned twice getting that letter to you. Once in the water and once in the ward when they were deciding whether I was worth saving. I'm not going to die now on a foggy hillside for nothing. Come on."

They went to the door.

It stood open. Not forced — open, unhinged entirely and leaned against the wall outside, which meant whoever was inside had wanted, for whatever reason, to be able to leave fast and see the approach while doing it. Theo felt the hairs rise on his forearms. Inside, the darkness smelled of gull lime and old oil and, faintly, of woodsmoke. Very fresh woodsmoke.

There was a glow behind the spiral stair. A small fire burned at the base of it, laid neatly on a sheet of tin — someone who knew about fires, about not leaving traces. And beside the fire, on an upturned crate, sat a woman.

She was perhaps fifty, wrapped in a fisherman's sweater that had been mended more times than most soldiers' lives, with grey hair cut short as a man's and hands that looked like they had hauled nets since childhood. She was eating bread and cheese with perfect calm, and she did not stand, and she did not smile, and the pistol lying across her knee was pointed nowhere in particular and everywhere all at once.

"You're late," she said. "Or early. With the tide it's hard to say which, and I don't much care." She tore another piece of bread. "Which one of you is the clerk?"

Anselm, POV

Anselm knew her.

Not well — three meetings in two years, all of them at night, all of them brief — but you do not forget a woman who once pulled you out of a burning fishing smack off the Dutch coast while shells fell like bad weather, and who complained afterward, while stitching your scalp with sailmaker's thread, that you bled on her good boat.

"Marta," he said.

Her eyes moved to him, and something shifted in them — not warmth, exactly, but recognition, the acknowledgment between professionals that the other party has, so far, failed to die. "The batman. So Bram's message was true. I owed him nothing unless you were alive, and now I find you are, which is inconvenient, because I'd already spent the money I didn't owe you."

"We need to see him," Anselm said. "Where is he?"

"Where you can't get at him until I say." She set down the bread, wiped the knife on her skirt, and picked up the pistol with the ease of long habit, holding it loosely now, almost conversationally. "Sit down, both of you. You'll forgive me if I don't offer tea."

Theo sat. Anselm noted that — the clerk sat immediately, without hesitation, folding himself onto the cold stone with his bag hugged to his chest like a schoolbook. Either very brave or very frightened, and after four days of watching him Anselm suspected the difference was mostly bookkeeping.

"You understand our position," Marta said. "A man comes to me with a story about Captain Elias Bram. Dead man. Traitor. The whole coast is plastered with his description and a price on it that would buy my boat twice over. I am asked to believe this dead traitor is alive, is not a traitor, and requires passage for himself and two associates on a night I have cargo already booked." She shook her head slowly. "In my trade, a story like that gets you drowned. Usually by your own people, as a precaution."

"So why are you here?" Anselm asked.

"Because Bram saved my brother's life in 1914 at Antwerp, and never told me, and I found out from a letter written by a man who was supposed to be dead." Her voice stayed flat, but her thumb moved once along the pistol's grip. "I hate debts. They float. So I am here to settle one, and I will settle it properly, which means I must be sure. Absolutely sure. Because if either of you is a plant — if Major Corvane, whose name I assure you I know, has sent a clerk and a half-drowned batman out here as bait — then everything dies tonight. My route. My people. Bram. All of it."

"We're not bait," Anselm said.

"Everyone says that." She stood, finally, and crossed to Theo, and held out her free hand, palm up. "The ledger."

Theo's arms tightened around the bag. "Why?"

"Because it's the only thing you have that proves anything. A clerk who keeps private lists of missing men, that's what Bram's letters described. If you're real, the ledger is real. If the ledger is real, you're real. Show me."

"The ledger has forty-three names in it," Theo said, and his voice, though quiet, had iron under it, and Anselm thought: there it is, that's what Bram saw in him. "Names of men who died and were never reported. Men whose mothers waited for letters that never came. It is not a passport and it is not a password. I will not hand it over to be looked at by a stranger on a dark night as if it were a train ticket."

The silence stretched. Out at sea, invisible, the foghorn counted forty seconds and boomed.

Then Marta laughed — one short bark, genuine surprise — and lowered the pistol, and tucked it into her sweater like a woman putting away scissors.

"Well," she said. "You'll do."

"That's it?" Anselm said.

"That's it. Corvane's people would have handed it over instantly. Bait always cooperates; it's the tell." She crouched by the fire and fed it a twist of paper. "But understand me, both of you. Being sure of you is the smallest part of tonight. There is still the matter of trust, and trust is a thing I test, not a thing I give. So we are going to play a game. Bram taught me the rules. He called it the truth game, and he is, far as I know, the only man alive who ever won it."

Theo, POV

The game was simple, and brutal.

Marta sat them down on either side of the fire and said: one question each, from me, and you may not answer with anything but the truth, and I will know a lie because I have heard every lie told on this coast since 1914 and they all sound the same — smooth.

To Anselm she said: "Why did you come back? You were off the continent. You could have gone anywhere. Why carry letters into the one port crawling with intelligence men?"

And Anselm, after a silence in which Theo could hear the sea chewing the rocks below, said: "Because he carried me. Twice. Once at Antwerp and once at the Yser, literally on his back, with my leg opened to the bone. There is a debt that doesn't float. I came back to pay it, and because—" His voice broke slightly and he mastered it. "Because someone should know what he actually did. The truth, written somewhere, by someone. Even if it only ever gets read by strangers."

Marta nodded once, as if ticking an entry in her own ledger.

To Theo she said: "You've never met the man you're doing this for. Two years dead, declared a traitor, and you walk out at midnight carrying your precious book for him. Why?"

And Theo found, to his surprise, that the answer rose in him fully formed, the way answers only do when they have been sitting in you for years wearing different clothes:

"Because in 1913, on this same headland, in daylight, a young officer made me a promise over bad claret: that if ever the record and the truth disagreed, he would come to me, and I would choose. Everyone else in my life chooses duty first and calls it honor afterward. I wanted, just once, to be the person who chose differently. And because—" he hesitated, then finished it, "—because I have spent two years writing down the names of men nobody would write down, and I think, I have begun to think, that those omissions were not accidents. That they were him. That my ledger and this ghost are the same story, and I want to see how the sentence ends."

The fire popped. Marta looked at him for a long moment with an expression Theo couldn't read, and then she reached into the breast of her sweater and drew out a folded paper and held it toward the flames.

"No!" Theo was on his feet before he knew it.

She let the paper hover an inch above the fire, close enough that he could read his own handwriting mirrored upside-down — a page of the ledger, torn out, stolen? — and watched him the way a cat watches a bird decide.

"How easily," she said softly, "they panic. How quickly a man of principle runs when the principle catches fire." Then she withdrew the paper, unharmed, and held it out to him. "Bram's instructions. In case you were the wrong kind of man. You weren't. Sit down, Mr. Fairweather. The game is done, and you've won it, and now comes the part nobody wins."

Theo took the paper with hands that would not quite stop shaking. It was not a page of his ledger — it was a copy, Bram's unmistakable hand reproducing Theo's own list, name for name, date for date, in the neat copperplate of a man who had studied his friend's records from a distance, across two years, across the impossible geography between them. At the bottom, one line of instruction:

*He will bring the book. Tell him everything. He has earned the ending.*

"What ending?" Theo asked. "Where is Bram? Is he here? Is he close?"

Marta began packing the fire away, smothering it with sand, killing the light methodically, businesslike, her back to them.

"He's close," she said. "Half a mile, in the old salt house, with a fever that should have killed him a week ago and a wound that will if we don't move him tonight. And I'll take you to him at first light, when the patrol rotates and the east path opens for twenty minutes. Those are the terms, and they aren't negotiable."

"And the ship?" Anselm said. "The morning tide — is it real?"

Marta stood and brushed off her skirts, and in the last red ember-light her face was unreadable.

"Oh, the tide is real. The ship is real. But there's a thing you should know before you meet him, and I'd rather you heard it from me than from him." She picked up her pistol and checked it, out of habit or out of something darker. "He isn't planning to board it. Bram means to send you both away and stay behind — he's decided he's the price that buys your clean exit, and knowing Elias, he's already written the noble version down somewhere with his damn beautiful handwriting." She jerked her chin toward the door, toward the fog and the ruins of the night. "So you have until first light, gentlemen, to change his mind. Walk fast. The east path won't wait, and neither, these days, does he."

Theo folded the copied page into the ledger, into the exact place where the ending belonged, and followed her out into the fog — already composing, in the part of his mind that never stopped keeping accounts, the argument he intended to win.
