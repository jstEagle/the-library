## 13. Marta's Price

Marta Voss had smuggled eleven men out of France and two out of Belgium, and she had learned long ago that the dangerous ones were never the soldiers. The soldiers were grateful and half-dead and did as they were told. The dangerous ones were the clerks. The men who kept records. Men like the tall one standing at her door in the salt spray with his coat buttoned wrong and his eyes too steady for a hospital man.

"You're late," she said.

"The tide was against us." He had a boy with him — Anselm, the batman, still gray around the mouth from fever but on his feet, which was more than Marta had expected when she'd heard he'd gone into the water off the night boat. "You said three nights hence. This is the second."

"I said come to the lighthouse in three nights. You came to the lighthouse tonight. That makes you eager, and eager men get my people killed." She stepped back from the doorway of the salt house and let them in, because the fog was thickening and the patrol boat would sweep the point within the hour, and because she wanted to look at Fairweather's face in better light.

It was a plain face. A bookkeeper's face. She'd imagined someone harder.

"Sit," she said. "Both of you. Don't touch the stove; it smokes black and the coastguard knows the color."

The salt house had been a fish-curing shed before the war and smelled like it still: brine and old blood and the tarred rope coiled along one wall. Marta put a lamp on the table between them and turned the wick low. In that light Anselm looked younger than she knew him to be. Fairweather looked like a man doing arithmetic behind his eyes and not liking any of the sums.

"He's alive," Anselm said. "You said if I got him here, you'd—"

"I said nothing of the kind. *He* said things. I only didn't contradict him." She let that sit. "But yes. Captain Bram is alive. He is four miles from where you're sitting, in a cellar under a chapel that hasn't held a congregation since the artillery came through. He has a wound in his side that went bad and then, thanks to a Frenchwoman who deserves a medal she will never receive, went good again. He can walk. He cannot run. He cannot swim. If anything goes wrong at the pier, he dies on the stones, and so do you, and so do I, and probably the Frenchwoman."

Fairweather's hands were flat on the table. Steady. She noted that. "Take me to him."

"No."

"Then take him a message."

"I'm not a post office."

"Then what do you want?" It came out of him without polish, without the clerk's carefulness, and Marta thought: there it is. Under the ledger-keeping face, a man who had already decided and was only now letting the rest of him know.

She poured three fingers of rough calvados into tin cups and pushed them across. "Money," she said, "is what other people want. I want something better. I want paper."

* * *

She laid it out for them the way she laid out everything: plainly, once, no repetition.

"A ship sails on the morning tide, day after tomorrow. Fishing boat on the outside, something faster underneath when it clears territorial water. The master owes me, and the master's crew are all men who owe me, which is safer than loyalty. But a boat is not an escape. A boat is just a smaller place to be caught." She took a breath. "Every port between here and Boulogne has a check. Harbor police, military police, the major's people — and make no mistake, the major's people are everywhere this week, which is why we're talking in a dead man's smokehouse. At each check they ask for papers. Not identity papers — those can be bought in any town in France. Discharge papers. A man in uniform with discharge papers is a man going home, and nobody stops a man going home. Nobody wants to be the clerk who delayed some poor devil's leave and had it written up."

"And you have none," Fairweather said.

"I have blank forms. Genuine blanks, taken off a quartermaster who owed me money instead of his life, which tells you how long ago. What I don't have — what no forger alive can give me — is real entries. Names, numbers, units, dates of wound, dates of discharge, signatures of officers who actually exist and whose signatures the checking clerks have seen. Because your Major Corvane has done one thing very well since he arrived: he's taught every checkpoint on this coast to distrust anything that looks too clean. A perfect forgery is suspicious now. Only truth passes."

The lamp fluttered. Somewhere outside, the tide was turning; she could hear it change under the fog, the way you learn to hear it after enough years of living by water.

"So," she said. "I need papers for three men. Bram, Anselm, and one more — because two discharged men traveling together looks like desertion, and three looks like a draft. And I need them true. Real names, real units, real wounds, real discharges. Entries that were never made, made correctly." She looked at Fairweather until he looked up. "That's why Anselm fetched you and not a priest. You keep a ledger, Mr. Fairweather. The whole coast whispers about the clerk who writes down the names the Army forgets. Forty-three names, or near enough. Men treated in your hospital and vanished from the record. Dead on paper, alive somewhere in the dark, moving north along my line like beads on a string."

Anselm had gone very still beside him.

"You knew," Fairweather said to him. Quiet. Not accusing. Worse than accusing — realizing.

"I knew the captain wrote letters," Anselm said. "I carried them. I never read the ledgers."

"There were no ledgers," Marta said. "There was a list, and the list lived in Bram's head, and every name on it matched a bed in your ward. He chose your hospital, Mr. Fairweather. Two years ago. Before you ever saw a stranger off the night boat. Your omissions weren't accidents of bureaucracy. They were his shadow, and yours."

She watched the sentence land. Watched him go white, then settle, the way deep water settles after something enormous passes beneath it. She had broken men with less. This one absorbed it and asked the next question, which told her everything she needed to know about whether he would do.

"What happens to the names? If I give them to you."

"They become three living men with honest papers."

"And the other forty?"

"Stay exactly where they've always been. Nowhere. Which is where your Army put them."

"That's not good enough."

"It's the only offer on the table."

* * *

He stood then and walked to the door and looked out at nothing, because there was nothing to look at — fog and the sound of water — and Marta drank her calvados and waited. She had watched men argue with themselves before. It was best done alone.

Anselm hadn't touched his drink. "If it costs him the ledger," he said softly, "he won't do it. You should know that. He'd give you his hands first."

"Then it's fortunate," Marta said, "that I'm not asking for his hands."

"You're asking for worse. That book is his religion. I've seen him write in it. It's like watching a man pray."

"Then he'll understand me perfectly," Marta said, "because I'm asking him to pray for something that walks out of here alive instead of something beautiful written down."

Fairweather came back from the door. He sat. He turned his cup a quarter-turn on the table, a small precise gesture, a bookkeeper squaring a column.

"I'll do it," he said. "On conditions."

Marta laughed despite herself. "Conditions. From a man hiding in a smokehouse."

"One. The names I use must belong to men already safe — men who came through your line months ago, settled, past any checkpoint Corvane might audit. I won't spend a living man's name on danger. Two. When this is over, whatever happens to me, the full ledger gets published. All forty-three. Bram's forty-three and mine together, in one record, with what he did written plainly. No more ghost. If Elias survives this, the world learns what he survived *as*, not the lie the Army printed."

"And if the world learns it and calls it treason anyway? They've already declared him a collaborator. Truth doesn't always wash a man clean, Mr. Fairweather. Sometimes it just gets him hanged honestly."

"Then it gets him hanged honestly," Fairweather said, "and I'll stand at the foot of the scaffold with the true account in my hand. I promised him once that if ever he asked, I'd help him come home. Home isn't only a country. It's being known. I can't give him the country yet. I can give him the knowing."

Marta looked at him for a long moment, this quiet man with his terrible arithmetic, and thought again that the clerks were the dangerous ones. Then she reached into her coat and brought out the packet: oilcloth, tied with waxed cord, holding the blank forms and the pen nibs and the bottle of iron-gall ink that would brown convincingly with age.

"The forms," she said. "Names and details by tomorrow dusk — no later, the master sails whether I'm ready or not. Write them in a hand that isn't yours. Tired, hurried, a man writing with his off hand in bad light. Officers' signatures slanted left; the checking clerks see right-slanted forgeries ten times a night and nothing else." She stood, and the deal was closed the way her deals always closed: by her standing up. "One more thing, and you'll want to hear it before you decide how much of yourself to spend. Bram doesn't know you're coming tomorrow. He thinks I'll refuse you. He told Anselm to carry you to safety if I did, and to tell you—" she paused, weighing whether the words were currency she could afford to spend, and decided they were, because sentiment properly placed was worth more than gold on this coast, "—to tell you the promise was paid long ago and he'd release you from it. His exact sense was: *Theo kept faith with a dead man long enough.*"

Fairweather didn't move. Didn't speak. Anselm looked down at his untouched drink.

"So," Marta said, shouldering the oilcloth packet and pushing open the door onto the fog and the smell of the incoming tide. "Come at dusk tomorrow. Bring the papers. And bring a coat for the captain — he's been wearing a dead Frenchman's jacket for five weeks and complains about it even now, which is how I know he intends to live." She stepped out into the white air, then turned back once, because there was one thing left and it was hers to say. "Mr. Fairweather. The cellar chapel. There's a sentry on the lane, a woman with a red scarf; show her nothing, she'll show you everything. And walk slowly. Men who walk fast toward Elias Bram usually arrive to find him pointing a gun, and he's a poor shot these days, which makes it worse for everyone."

She heard them follow her up the cliff path, footsteps muffled in fog, and below her the tide came in over the flats, patient and enormous, keeping its own ledger of everything the shore gave it and everything it took away.

Two days, she thought. One ship. Three sets of true lies.

And somewhere back along the coast, in a warm room full of filing cabinets, Major Corvane was already awake, reading.
