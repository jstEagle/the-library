## 14. Forging the Living

The boathouse smelled of tar and old rope and, beneath that, of salt rot — the smell of things the sea had given up on. Marta had left a lamp burning low in the corner, its wick trimmed to a coin of light, and she had left paper too: three sheets of good rag stock, a bottle of ink gone thick with age, and a pen with a nib worn to a slant that matched no hand I owned.

"You'll want to practice first," she'd said, before slipping out into the dark like something poured rather than walking. "A forger's first document is always his confession."

I sat down at the upturned skiff she used as a desk and I did not practice.

That was the thing I would remember afterward — not the fear, not even the guilt, but the terrible stillness of my own hands as they opened my ledger to the middle pages and began, without tremor, without prayer, to kill.

* * *

Understand what the ledger was to me before you judge what I did to it.

I had started it in the autumn of 1914, in the second week of my clerkship, when a sergeant from the Sussex regiment died in Ward C at four in the morning and his name appeared on no list anywhere. The matron wrote him into the ward book; the ward book went into the registry; the registry went into the war, which swallowed it whole. Somewhere in France there was a mother who would wait years for a letter that was never coming, because a clerk two desks up from mine had decided a private's death was not worth ink.

So I bought a ledger of my own. Cheap paper, marbled boards, fivepence from the stationer on Quay Street. And every name the lists forgot, I wrote it down. Rank, regiment, date, wound, hour. Where he was from, if anyone knew. What he said, if he said anything worth keeping. *Private A. Dunn, 2nd Buffs, says tell Nellie the dog will remember her.* That sort of thing. Small names. The history that happens to other people, written small enough to fit.

Three years of it. Forty-three names by that winter — forty-three men who existed nowhere else on earth except in my handwriting and in God's, if God kept books, which I had begun to doubt.

And now Marta wanted me to take those names and forge them into discharge papers. To make living men out of dead entries. To turn the one true record I possessed into a lie.

She had put it plainly enough, back at the salt house, her knife lying on the table between us not as threat but as punctuation. "Your Bram needs papers or he needs a coffin, and I only know how to arrange one of those. Discharge documents, travel passes, a medical board certificate. Real names, real regiments, real wounds — your ledger has all of it. The War Office checks nothing harder than plausibility, and plausibility is all you've got, clerk. Ink it."

Ink it. As if it were a word and not a wound.

* * *

Anselm slept in the stern of the beached dinghy under a fisherman's tarpaulin, fever still riding him but lower now, his breathing the slow bellows-sound of a body that had decided, provisionally, to continue. Every quarter hour or so he stirred and said a name — not Bram's, someone else's, a name I did not know — and settled again. Even unconscious, he was carrying people.

I worked by the coin of lamplight with my coat off and my sleeves rolled, and I told myself I was only practicing.

The first sheet I ruined entirely. My natural hand is a clerk's hand — upright, legible, modest, the hand of a man who expects his writing to be read by superiors. A medical board certificate wants authority. It wants the looping confidence of a major who has signed ten thousand such papers and thinks of them as less than letters home. I wrote *Examined this day and found unfit for further service* six times, and six times it looked like what it was: a hospital clerk impersonating an officer, a mouse wearing a lion's signature.

On the seventh attempt I stopped trying to write like a major and started trying to write like a man who was tired. That was the trick, I realized — the real signatures on the real papers I had stamped and filed for three years were never confident. They were hurried. They were the marks of men who wanted their tea. Authority, it turned out, looked exactly like impatience.

My heart was going hard. Not from fear of discovery — though that was there, a cold stone low in my stomach — but from something worse. From how good it felt. There is a pleasure in transgression that no honest work ever gives you, and I hated the pleasure, and I could not stop feeling it, and my pen moved.

* * *

Here is what forgery actually is, for anyone who wonders. It is not invention. Invention is easy; invention is a game. Forgery is theft of the true.

Every fact I set down that night was true. *Fairweather, T. J., Clerk, Royal Army Medical Corps* — that was me, real rank, real number, certifying what I had no right to certify. *Bram, E. A., Capt., 9th Norfolks, invalided, gunshot wound left shoulder, trench fever, recommended for immediate passage.* All of it true except the one thing that mattered: that no board had ever sat, no examination ever happened, no authority existed behind the signature except a fivepenny ledger and a promise made in peacetime over bad wine.

I used Private A. Dunn's service number for Anselm. Dunn had been dead two years; no one would come looking for a corpse's papers, and if they did, they would find a man whose wounds matched the record because I would write the record to match the man. I gave Anselm Dunn's regiment, Dunn's home county, Dunn's next of kin — and then I stopped, pen lifted, because Dunn's next of kin was *Nellie*, and somewhere in Kent there might still be a woman and a dog, and I was spending the dead like coins.

"Forgive me," I said aloud, to a man I had never met. The lamp flame leaned as if listening.

Then I wrote Nellie's name anyway, because Anselm needed it more than Dunn's ghost did, and because I was learning — fast, horribly fast — how a forger's conscience works. It does not fall silent. It negotiates. *This life for that memory. This lie for that truth. He is dead either way; the living man is bleeding.* By the second page I was negotiating fluently. By the third I was not bothering to negotiate at all.

That was the part that frightened me. Not the risk. The ease.

* * *

Somewhere past two in the morning, Anselm woke.

I heard the tarpaulin shift and then his voice, cracked and low: "Is it done?"

"It's nearly done." I kept writing. "Sleep."

"I dreamed about the letters." He hauled himself up on one elbow, and in the lamplight his face was all hollows and salt-burn, a face the sea had tried to return and half succeeded. "Bram's letters. You've read them?"

"I've read them."

"Then you know what he did. The forty-three." His eyes fixed on me with a feverish intensity, as if my answer were a rope and he was deciding whether to hold on. "Everyone called it desertion, or worse. Corvane calls it treason. But you've seen the names. You keep them yourself."

"I keep them," I said. "I didn't know I was keeping his."

"No more did he." Anselm lay back, wincing. "He started writing them down after Ypres. Men the lists lost. He couldn't save them all — God knows he couldn't save one in ten — but he could get them off the field, through the lines, onto boats, and he could make sure somebody, somewhere, knew they hadn't just evaporated. He said—" the fever pulled at him, and he closed his eyes to steady the words— "he said a man isn't dead until everyone agrees to stop saying his name. So he kept saying them. To strangers. To smugglers. To whoever would carry the letters north."

"To me," I said.

"He didn't know if it reached anyone. Two years, Mr. Fairweather. Two years of writing letters and sewing them into coats and handing them to drowning men, never knowing if any of it landed." Anselm's breath caught. "You're the landing. Do you understand? Everything he did was aimed at you and he never knew if you existed."

I put down the pen. My hand, I noticed, had not shaken once all night, and it did not shake now, and some traitorous part of me was proud of that.

"Tell me about the ship," I said.

"Marta's contact. A collier out of Rouen, running empty, takes what cargo it's given. Dawn tide day after tomorrow, but the boarding party comes at first light, so we go in the dark." He recited it like scripture, because it was — the liturgy of every escape he had helped run. "Papers checked at the quay. One officer, one batman, medical transit. Your forgeries have to survive thirty seconds of lamplight and a bored lieutenant."

"Thirty seconds," I said. "I can do thirty seconds."

"You'd better do thirty years," Anselm said, "because that's how long they hang you for, if Corvane catches it." He said it without cruelty, the way a man reports weather. Then, softer: "Why are you doing this? You barely know him anymore. The Bram you promised things to in peacetime — that man's been dead twice over. Once when they listed him, once when he let them."

I looked down at the ledger open on the skiff, at the forty-three names marching down its pages in my careful clerk's hand, and at the forged certificate taking shape beside them in my borrowed, tired, authoritative scrawl, and I understood that the two documents had become the same document. That they had perhaps always been the same document, and I had simply been too devout to see it.

"Because he kept saying the names," I said. "Someone had to answer."

Anselm held my gaze a moment longer. Then he nodded, once, the way soldiers nod — not agreement, exactly. Recognition. He pulled the tarpaulin up and was asleep again inside a minute, the way the truly exhausted sleep, dropping like a stone through water.

* * *

I finished the third document as the sky behind the boathouse shutters went from black to iron.

It was the hardest one, though I hadn't expected it to be. The first two were for Bram and Anselm — lives spent to buy lives, a fair exchange even a puritan could audit. The third was different. Marta's price had a third clause, delivered with the flat pragmatism of a woman quoting freight rates: a pass for herself, in a clean name, because the net was closing on the coast and Marta intended to be elsewhere when it snapped shut. She had saved Bram twice already, she said. She was owed a door out, and I was the man with the ink.

So I invented a person. That was the sin I hadn't prepared for. Not stealing a dead man's identity — Dunn wouldn't mind, Dunn was beyond minding — but manufacturing a human being out of nothing. *Marta Lindqvist, civilian, subject of a neutral power, certified for passage.* I gave her an age, a port of origin, a reason to travel. I gave her a widow's mourning band in ink, a brother in Copenhagen, a grief I fabricated wholesale and handed her like a coat. Somewhere in my chest a small voice observed that I had just written a fiction and signed it as fact, and that this was precisely what governments did daily at scale, and that the difference between me and the War Office was that my lie was trying to save three people instead of spending ten thousand.

The small voice sounded suspiciously like Major Corvane. I told it to be quiet.

I blotted the last page. I stacked the three certificates square. And then I sat for a long time with the ledger itself, because there remained one final act, and I had been avoiding it all night.

The ledger had to move. If Corvane took me, he would search my rooms within the hour, and the ledger could not be found — not because it named the escaped, but because it named the omitted, and forty-three omissions read in sequence amount to an accusation. Marta had said to burn it. *Paper is evidence, clerk. Ash is loyalty.*

I carried it to the lamp.

I stood there with the marbled boards in my hands, three years of small names, the only church I had ever properly attended, and I found that I could not do it. Not because of sentiment — well, yes, because of sentiment, I am not going to pretend otherwise — but because burning it felt like agreeing with the world that those men had never existed. The whole point, the entire point, had been to refuse the erasure. I would not perform it myself, even kindly, even tactically.

Instead I wrapped the ledger in oilcloth, bound it with twine, and hid it where I had hidden things since I was a boy who read too much — up inside the chimney flue, above the shelf of the boathouse stove, where smoke had long since blackened everything and no soldier searches for treasure in soot. If I lived, I would come back for it. If I didn't, some future clerk sweeping out this ruin would find forty-three names and wonder, and wondering would be enough. Wondering was how my ledger had started. Let it propagate.

When I climbed down, my fingers were black to the wrist, and I laughed — actually laughed, alone, in a smuggler's boathouse at dawn, with treason drying on the table behind me. Grief and exhilaration taste identical in the mouth. No one tells you that.

* * *

Marta came back with the grey light, letting herself in without sound, and stood over the three certificates reading them upside down with the speed of a customs man. Her finger stopped on the third.

"Lindqvist," she said. "Widow. Copenhagen." Something moved across her face too quick to name — amusement, or its opposite. "You gave me a dead husband."

"You seemed like the type who'd use him well."

For a moment I thought she'd strike me. For a moment I hoped she would; it would have simplified things. Instead she rolled the papers tight, slid them into an oilskin wallet, and tucked it inside her coat against her ribs, and said, "The salt house, tonight, after full dark. He'll be there. Bring yourself and the boy and nothing else — no ledger, no letters, nothing written. Anything written is a sentence."

"It's hidden."

"Nothing is hidden. Some things are merely unfound." She paused at the door, silhouetted against the brightening water, and looked back at me with an expression I would spend a long time failing to interpret. "You know what you are now, clerk?"

"A forger."

"Worse." She stepped out into the grey. "You're a witness. Those are the ones they hunt longest."

The door swung shut. The lamp guttered. Out on the flats the tide was turning, dragging the night's debris back toward the sea, and I stood in the cold with ink on my hands and soot to the elbows, a man who had crossed a line in the dark and found, disturbingly, that the country on the other side looked almost exactly like the one he'd left — except that now, everywhere he looked, the names were moving.

Tonight, the salt house. Tonight, Elias Bram, alive and angry and two years late.

I washed my hands in the tide pool until they stopped being black, knowing they never would again.
