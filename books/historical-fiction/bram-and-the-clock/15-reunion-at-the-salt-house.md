## 15. Reunion at the Salt House

Marta gave me directions the way she gave everything — grudgingly, and only once.

"Follow the tide line east until the groyne with the black pilings. Then inland, along the creek, past the wrecked punt. The salt house sits alone. Don't use a lamp. If you meet anyone, you're a clerk checking drainage for the War Office. You look like a clerk." She looked me over. "God help you, you even smell like one."

"And Bram?"

"He'll be where I leave him." She pulled her collar up. "If he's still breathing."

She didn't offer to walk with me. I understood by then that Marta did not do anything twice, and guiding me had been once.

I left Anselm at the boathouse with Sister Agnes's keys in his fist and instructions to bar the door against God himself if God came wearing a major's uniform. His hand was steady now. He'd eaten half a tin of something Marta called fish and I chose not to call anything. Before I left he caught my sleeve.

"Tell him I carried them," he said. "All of them. Tell him none got wet but the ones the sea took anyway."

"I'll tell him."

"Tell him it wasn't far enough. Tell him—" He stopped, jaw working. "Just tell him alive. That's all any of it means. Alive."

I went out into the dark with that word sewn into my coat next to the letters, heavier than paper had any right to be.

* * *

The flats at night are a country without law. The tide had gone out miles, leaving mud that looked solid and wasn't, channels that looked empty and ran deep, and above it all a fog coming off the water in slow rags. I walked the line where the sea had been, following the groyne Marta described, and every step I rehearsed what I would say.

I had been rehearsing for two years without knowing it.

The first drafts were forgiving. *Elias. Thank God.* Then the war revised them. *Elias, you bastard, I buried you. I mourned you properly, with the good ink, the way I don't do for just anyone.* By the time I reached the black pilings the drafts had grown teeth: *Two years. Two years, while I kept your handwriting in my head like a religion, while I watched your name go on the memorial wall with the paint still wet.*

The creek turned inland. The wrecked punt lay on its side like something that had tried to escape and failed. And beyond it, low against the darker sky, the salt house — a long shed with a sagging roof, once used for drying fish, now used, apparently, for drying captains.

No light showed. That frightened me more than a light would have.

I knocked the way Marta had shown me — three, pause, two — and heard nothing, then something: a scrape, a weight shifting. A voice I had not heard in two years except in memory, and memory had made it kinder, said:

"If you've come to finish it, come in and be quick about it. I'm poor sport."

I pushed the door open.

Inside there was a lantern turned so low it was more suggestion than light, a heap of nets, a stove breathing iron-warmth, and on a pallet against the wall a man propped on one elbow with a pistol in his hand that he could not have fired straight if his life depended on it, which it did.

He was gaunt the way the drowned are gaunt. Fever had burned through him for days — I could read it in his eyes before he spoke, in the shine of skin across cheekbones that had once been full. His hair had gone grey at the temples and he was thirty-four. The uniform he wore belonged to no army; it was a fisherman's oilskin over a shirt that had been white in some previous life. His right leg was bound in bandages that had been changed by someone who knew her work and hated doing it.

But it was him. It was Elias. Not the portrait on the memorial wall, not the ghost of the coast reports — him, breathing, squinting against even that guttering flame, alive in defiance of every document I had ever filed.

"Theo," he said.

The pistol dropped from his hand onto the pallet as though his fingers had simply resigned.

I had crossed the whole flat in fury and arrived, in the doorway, unable to spend any of it. We stood — he half-stood, swaying, catching himself on the wall — and then he closed the distance between us in three staggering steps and held onto me the way drowning men hold onto wreckage, and I found I was holding back, and that we were both shaking, and that neither of us said anything worth writing down because there is nothing worth writing down at such moments except *alive, alive, alive.*

* * *

I got him back onto the pallet the way I'd moved wounded men a hundred times, hands finding the business of it before the heart caught up. The leg was bad. Not dying-bad, not yet, but the kind of bad that turns septic on a whim, and when I unwound the bandage he watched my face with terrible calm, reading the verdict there.

"Well?" he said.

"You'll keep it," I said. "If the fever breaks and you get somewhere warm and you stop lying in a salt house with a hole in your thigh."

"Ambitious of me."

I cleaned and repacked the wound while he talked — or rather, while I worked and he filled the silence the way he always had, even at school, even before the war, narrating to keep the fear at a civilized volume. But tonight the narration had an audience of two years' arrears, and I let him run through the first hour of it with my head down and my hands busy, and then I tied off the bandage, sat back on an upturned crate, and said:

"No. Stop. Start again from the part where you died."

He flinched. Good, I thought. Feel that.

"It was Passchendaele," he said quietly. "You know that much."

"I know what they told me. Which was that Captain E. J. Bram fell leading his company, October 1914, and that his effects would follow, and that his family"—his family being me, functionally, though neither of us ever said it—"was notified with regret."

"The company broke. You know how it breaks — not all at once, like a bone, but like rope, strand by strand. We were down to forty men and I was the only officer standing and the orders coming back were insane, and I made a decision." He looked at his own hands. "I walked toward the German lines with my hands up. And then, when they sent me back with a stretcher party under flag to collect our wounded — I didn't return."

I waited.

"There were nineteen of our men lying out there in the mud the recovery parties had written off. Too exposed. Not worth the risk, the arithmetic said. I knew where every one of them lay because I'd crawled the ground at night. So I went and got them. One at a time. Through the lines, down the canals, into Belgium, into Holland. Nineteen men the army had already stopped counting." He smiled, and it was the worst smile I have ever seen. "That was the first month. It got easier after that, which should tell you everything about the world."

"The list," I said. My voice came out strange. "Forty-three names."

"Forty-three," he agreed. "Including the nineteen. Some I took from no-man's-land. Some from hospital trains that were going the wrong direction. Some simply walked out of camps I helped them out of. Anselm twice — did he tell you? He never mentions the first time. He thinks I don't remember."

"He remembers for both of you."

"Yes." Something warmed in his face, briefly. "Good. That's good."

And there it was, laid out between us: the ledger on my side, the man on the other, the same story told in two hands. I should have felt vindicated. I had spent two years believing those names mattered, and here was the proof, sitting up feverish on a fish-drying pallet. Instead what I felt was the other thing, the thing underneath, and it came out of me before I could dress it in clerk's language:

"You let me bury you."

"Theo—"

"You let me stand at the memorial and hear your name read out. You knew I was here. You knew I was at this hospital, ten miles from your smuggling routes, four years of tide from your lighthouse rendezvous. You wrote to strangers. You wrote to *Anselm*. You sewed letters into dead men's coats and sent them across the Channel, and not one of them was addressed to me." My voice was rising and I let it. There was no one for miles. "I mourned you, Elias. I did it properly. Do you understand what that costs a man like me? I don't feel things cheaply. When you died I sat in the records room after hours and I wrote your name in my private ledger in red ink, the ink I save for—"

I stopped. He was looking at me with an expression I couldn't read, and it silenced me more effectively than an argument.

"Say it," he said.

"You could have told me."

"I couldn't." And then, with the honesty of a man too sick to construct anything better: "I was afraid you'd say yes."

"What?"

"To helping me. To coming with me. To forging papers, hiding men, whatever I asked. I'd seen you at the pier that last leave, Theo. I'd seen you watching the boats with that look — the look of a man who belongs somewhere else and is too decent to admit it. If I'd written to you, you'd have torn your careful life open to follow me into it, and I couldn't bear that. So I let you have the clean grief instead. It seemed kinder." He laughed, without humor. "That was the calculation. Dead Bram hurts, but living Bram might cost you everything. I chose what hurt less. For you. That's what I told myself, anyway. Late at night, in attics and coal holds, I told myself it was for you."

"And now?"

"Now Corvane has your scent and your ledgers are compromised and you've forged discharge papers with your own sacred hand, so I rather think the clean life is gone regardless." He reached out — his hand was burning, fever-hot — and gripped my wrist. "So now I'm going to be selfish. Now I need you. Both things true at once, Theo. That's what nobody tells you about love and war. They're not opposites. They arrive together, in the same boat, and you take delivery of both."

I looked at his hand on my wrist. Two years of anger, and it dissolved — not into forgiveness, forgiveness would come later, would take years, would probably never fully arrive — but into something older and sturdier than anger, something that had been waiting underneath it the whole time, patient as the tide.

"I'm not angry that you needed me," I said. "I'm angry that you decided for me. Don't ever do it again. That's the term. That's the price of passage."

"Agreed."

"Swear it."

"On the ledger," he said, "and on the forty-three, and on the fact that I am, apparently, alive — sworn."

I repacked the stove. I changed the dressing again an hour later when the fever spiked and he shivered and muttered names — some from the ledger, some I didn't know, some that were clearly from before the war, from school, from summers — and I sat through all of it holding his hand down when he tried to rise, saying the same thing each time: *"You're at the salt house. You're safe. Theo's here."*

Near dawn the fever broke in a rush of sweat, and he surfaced, lucid, exhausted, and looked at me with eyes that were almost clear.

"Marta says a ship, morning tide, day after tomorrow," he said. "Anselm sails with me?"

"Anselm sails with you."

"And you?"

The question hung there. Outside, the first grey was coming into the sky, and the tide was coming back across the flats, filling the channels one by one, and somewhere behind us in the town Major Corvane was awake too, I had no doubt, precise and charming and closing in.

"That depends," I said, "on whether you can answer one question honestly."

"Try me."

"When this is over — when the ship sails and the war does whatever it does — are you coming home? Actually home. Not a letter sewn into a coat. Home."

He was quiet a long moment. The stove ticked. The tide hissed far off across the mud.

"I've been dead two years, Theo," he said at last. "Dying taught me exactly one thing worth knowing." He took my hand, fever-heat and all, and held it. "Yes. Whatever shape the world takes afterward. Yes."

I bent to change his dressing one more time, and that was when we heard it — boots on the creek path, more than one pair, moving fast, and a voice carrying across the flat in the grey half-light, calling my name.
