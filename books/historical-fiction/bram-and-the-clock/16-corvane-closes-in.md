## 16. Corvane Closes In

The uniform was folded badly.

Major Corvane noticed that before he noticed the blood, and the noticing itself told him something worth knowing about the man who had done the folding. Soldiers fold well or not at all; clerks fold in halves, precise as bookkeeping. This had been folded by someone who cared about tidiness but had run out of time to practice it — a man working against a clock he could not see, only hear.

He stood in the linen store behind Ward C with the door shut and the lamp turned low, and let the room speak first. It always spoke, if you gave it silence enough. A shelf of folded sheets. A basin with a ring of dried soap. A window latched from inside, which meant no one had left through it. And on the second shelf, beneath a stack of pillowcases placed with just slightly too much care, a hospital orderly's grey tunic, buttons done wrong by one, soaked through the chest with blood that had gone the colour of iron shavings.

Corvane did not touch it for a full minute. Touching ended conversations; looking continued them.

Then he lifted it, turned it, and read it the way other men read letters.

Entry wound through the fabric, high on the left side — a graze, not a penetration, or the wearer would not have walked out of here under his own power. The blood volume said survived. The position of the stain said the man was moving when hit, falling forward, catching himself. There was a smear along the right cuff where a hand had pressed against something — stone, most likely, or the gunwale of a boat. And there, at the collar, a repair: darned with navy thread on grey wool, the work of a soldier's mother or a soldier himself too poor to care about matching. He had seen that same darning stitch once before, eleven days ago, on a report photograph of clothing recovered from a beach at Gris-Nez.

Anselm Vey. Corporal, 2nd Buffs, missing since March. Batman to an officer whose file Corvane carried in his breast pocket like a coal he'd learned not to feel.

He set the tunic down exactly as he had found it, adjusted the pillowcases back over it by half an inch so that a hurried search would find nothing and a patient one would find everything, and turned off the lamp.

* * *

By noon he had the route.

It assembled itself the way these things always did — not from any single piece but from the friction between pieces. A porter at the fish market remembered a handcart taken out after curfew on Thursday and returned Friday with the axle greased. A girl selling mussels had seen two men and a third who "walked like a sack of meal" go along the sea wall toward the old salt house, and she told him this freely, pleased to be asked, because Corvane asked the way a doctor palpates: firm, impersonal, impossible to resent until afterward. The harbourmaster's log showed a dinghy signed out in the name of a man who had been dead since Loos. And the boathouse at the eastern point — the hospital's own boathouse, keyholder recorded as Matron A. Shelton — had fresh oil on its floorboards and a crate of ship's biscuit that no fishing crew would stock, because ship's biscuit kept, and men who were hiding planned to keep too.

He spread it in his mind the way he had once spread maps in Flanders: not as territory but as intention. Two men into the salt house. One wounded — carried, then walking, then carried again. Provisioned at the boathouse, which meant the boathouse was the fallback, the bolt-hole, the place you ran to when the salt house burned. And beyond the boathouse, the water. A boat on the morning tide, some smuggler's hull with a name that changed twice a year, bound for a coastline where a dead captain could stop being a rumour and start being a problem.

Three nights hence, if the tide tables meant anything, and they always meant something. Corvane had checked them twice. Low water at 4:12, dawn at 5:40, the window between them narrow and dark and exactly the kind of window such men loved, because they believed darkness made them invisible when all it ever made them was quiet.

He wrote none of this down. Reports could be read over shoulders; shoulders could belong to anyone. He kept it in his head, which was the only filing cabinet in England he trusted.

There remained the matter of the clerk.

* * *

He found Theo Fairweather where he expected to, at the admissions desk at six in the evening, entering names into a ledger with the small furious neatness of a man writing scripture. Corvane watched him for a moment from the corridor before announcing himself — watched the pen move, pause, move again; watched the young man's lips shape each name silently as he wrote it, as though the ink needed the help of the voice.

Men who did that were dangerous. Corvane had learned to fear tidy men with soft voices long before the war taught him to fear everyone else.

"Mr. Fairweather."

Theo looked up. His face did what faces usually did at the sight of Corvane — a flicker, quickly mastered, of the expression people wore upon finding something dead in the road. Good. Fear was usable. Fear that had been mastered was even more usable, because mastered fear wanted reasons to stay mastered.

"Major." Theo closed the ledger. Not slammed it — closed it, deliberately, a hand laid flat on the cover like a man stilling a table during an earthquake. Corvane noted the gesture and filed it.

"I wonder if I might trouble you for ten minutes. Somewhere we can speak without an audience."

"There's a supply room—"

"Not a hospital room." He smiled, and made sure the smile reached exactly as far as it was designed to reach and no further. "Walk with me. The quay is pleasant at this hour, and I find I think better near water."

They went. Theo fetched his coat without argument, which Corvane noted also: compliance without protest was either innocence or discipline, and he would know which within a quarter hour.

* * *

The tide was going out. It did the quay what it always did — uncovered things. Ropes, weed, the black teeth of old pilings, a bicycle frame furred green. Corvane walked to the end near the customs house and looked at the water for a while before speaking, because he had learned that silence made civilians fill it, and what civilians used to fill silence was almost always the truth.

"You've worked here three years," he said at last. "Admissions clerk, casualty records, occasional nursing duty when the boats come in heavy. You are, by the testimony of every officer I've spoken to, meticulous, discreet, and indispensable."

"That's kind of them."

"It isn't kindness. Kindness is when I say it to your face at your funeral." He let that sit a moment, then turned. "Mr. Fairweather, I'm going to tell you a thing I don't tell many people. I am very good at my work. Not clever — clever is cheap, the war is full of clever men dying of it. *Good.* Which means that when I stand in front of a man, I can generally tell within a few sentences whether he is hiding something, and if so, how large it is."

Theo's face held. It held well — better than the mussel girl's, better than the porter's. But holding is not the same as calm; a dam holds too, right up until the sound it makes.

"And how large is mine?" Theo said.

"Oh, yours is enormous." Corvane said it almost gently. "You're carrying it beautifully, mind. Better than most. But a man who closes a ledger with his whole palm before a stranger has finished his first sentence — that's not discretion, Mr. Fairweather. That's weight."

The gulls were working the exposed mud below the quay. Out past the harbour mouth a trawler came in on the last of the light, lamps already lit, riding low.

"I'm investigating the leak of casualty information," Corvane went on. "Forty-three names, over nineteen months, removed from official lists and surfacing elsewhere — letters home, parish registers, one memorial plaque in a Kent churchyard with names on it the War Office never carved. Someone in this town, or passing through it, has been extracting men from the record. Some of those men may be alive. All of them are officially absent without leave, presumed deserters, and their officers have been writing to mothers who write back asking why their sons won't answer. Do you understand what that does to a woman, Mr. Fairweather? To receive no reply for a year and a half and then learn her son's regiment lists him as a deserter?"

"I can imagine," Theo said quietly.

"I don't think you have to imagine. I think you keep a list of your own." He said it flatly, without accusation, the way one remarks on weather. "Every clerk does eventually. The ones who last. Names that fall through — men nobody claims, paperwork that dies in transit. A private ledger. A memory aid. Nothing criminal in it. I'm not here for your little book."

He watched that land. Watched the pulse jump at Theo's throat and the face refuse to follow it. *There*, he thought. *Not innocence, then. Discipline.*

"What I'm here for," Corvane said, "is Elias Bram."

The name did what no question could have. It went through the young man's composure the way the bullet had gone through Anselm Vey's tunic — a graze, not a penetration, but enough to draw everything to the surface. Theo did not speak. That was the tell, really. An innocent man would have said *who?* or *the traitor?* or something, anything, because innocent men cannot bear silence; guilt knows silence is the only shelter left.

"He was declared dead in the autumn of 1914," Corvane continued, pacing slowly now along the quay edge, hands clasped behind him, a man strolling. "Two years ago this month. Gallipoli dispatch, then amended. Since then he has been formally reclassified — I'll spare you the vocabulary; the vocabulary matters less than the sentence, and the sentence is that assisting him is treason. Not misconduct. Not bending of rules. Treason, tried at bar, hanged at dawn. I want you to hold that word a moment, Mr. Fairweather, because I suspect no one else in your life has said it to you plainly and meant it."

"You have the wrong man," Theo said. "I haven't seen Captain Bram since—"

"Since the summer of 1913. Yes. I know. You knew him in peacetime; there's a photograph of the two of you at a regatta, taken by his sister. I have a copy. I have rather a lot of copies of things, it's my one extravagance." He stopped walking and faced him fully. "I'm not accusing you of seeing him. I'm telling you that he is going to try to see *you*. Men like Bram collect people the way other men collect stamps — carefully, by type, and for years. He collected you in peacetime. He will spend you in wartime. It's what he does. It's what he's been doing along this whole coast, and I have watched him do it with a patience I confess I admire."

The tide sucked at the pilings below. Somewhere behind them the hospital bell rang the hour, and neither man moved.

"Here is what I offer," Corvane said. "Once, plainly, and I will never repeat it. Full immunity. Your ledgers, your omissions, anything you've signed, carried, hidden, or failed to notice — all of it dies tonight, unspoken, unpunished, unwritten. Your record stays clean. Your matron's stays clean. The orderly who greased a handcart axle he shouldn't have — clean. All of it, gone, in exchange for one thing."

Theo's voice, when it came, was steady in the way of a rope under load. "Which is?"

"Elias Bram. Before the morning tide. Not his body — his surrender. You bring me word of where he is, or you bring him to me, and by Friday you are a man with no debts. I will even see to it that your friend Anselm Vey is treated as a prisoner of war instead of a deserter. He's a good soldier in a bad story; that much I'd give you freely."

* * *

He had prepared the next part for days, and now that he reached it he found he took no pleasure in it, which was itself informative. Once, long ago, he would have. He noted the absence of pleasure the way he noted everything, and proceeded.

"Do you know what happened to your father?" he asked.

The quay went very still. Not the town — the quay, and the boy standing on it.

"He drowned," Theo said. "1909. The *Marguerite*, off Boulogne."

"The *Marguerite* was found," Corvane said. "That's the part the inquiry never managed. Found, six weeks later, hull intact, cargo missing, no crew. There was a file. It crossed four desks and was closed by a fifth, and the closing note says — I have it memorized, I've read it perhaps forty times — *'inconvenient; recommend no further action.'* Your father's name is in that file. Not as a victim. As a witness. He saw what was in the hold, reported it to the customs house at Folkestone, and the report itself is the only surviving document, because every copy above it was destroyed by men protecting a shipping arrangement that certain persons preferred continue." He paused. "Your father was erased, Mr. Fairweather. Not by the sea. By a ledger. By men who understood, as I understand, that the record is the battlefield where everything else is decided. They wrote him out, and the world obliged, and you grew up believing the water took him."

Theo had not moved. His face had gone the colour of the paper in his ledgers, and his eyes were fixed on the middle distance, on the black line of the harbour mole, seeing something that was not the harbour at all.

"Why," he said finally, and his voice cracked on the single syllable, and he mastered it, and Corvane felt something that in a lesser man might have been called respect. "Why are you telling me this."

"Because you deserve to know it," Corvane said. "That's true, so I'll say it first. And because I need you to understand that I am not your enemy. I am a man who spends his life pulling names out of fires that other men started and called policy. I found your father's file by accident, in 1914, doing routine work, and I have kept it — not destroyed it — because I thought someday it might matter to someone, and I confess I hoped it would matter to you." He took a slow breath. "I can reopen it. I can make them carve your father's name back into the world — inquiry, restitution, apology, whatever fragments of it survive after six years. It's within my reach. Few things are, but that is. For Bram, I'll burn the file myself."

Below them the mud lay open and gleaming, all the quay's buried things briefly visible in the last light: rope, weed, iron, bone.

"That's not immunity," Theo said softly. "That's ransom."

"Yes," Corvane said. "It is. I'd rather you heard me say it than dress it up as patriotism. I have served the version of duty that wears flags, Mr. Fairweather, and I've buried enough of it to know that flags are what men hide behind when the truth won't fit. What I serve now is plainer. Elias Bram has moved forty-three soldiers across borders using forged paper and stolen names, and among the men he's moved there are three who were not soldiers at all, and one of those three passed intelligence to the German naval attaché in Rotterdam that cost eleven lives on a mine-layer in February. Bram doesn't know that. Or he knows it and weighed it, which is worse. Either way, the ghost must stop, and the man must answer, and you are standing between me and both." He stepped closer, and lowered his voice to something almost kind. "You keep a ledger of forgotten names. So do I. Mine has your father in it. Come midnight tomorrow, one of us decides what his is worth."

He left him there. He did not look back — looking back was theatre, and theatre was for men who doubted — but he listened, the way he listened to rooms. And what he heard, beneath the gulls and the sucking tide and the far-off bell, was the sound he had come to the quay to listen for: nothing. No footsteps following. No voice calling after.

Silence, in a man like Theo Fairweather, was not acquittal.

It was a decision being made.

* * *

At the customs house Corvane paused under the lamp, took out his watch — nine minutes past eight — and performed the small nightly arithmetic that governed his life now. Sixty hours to low water. Fifty-nine to dawn. A salt house to raid, a boathouse to watch, a matron to interview again, and one clerk to break or believe.

He wound the watch, put it away, and stood a moment in the lamplight thinking not of Bram, nor of the boy on the quay, but of the file upstairs in his quarters, tied in government string, bearing a dead man's name that belonged to a living son.

*Inconvenient; recommend no further action.*

He had written that closing note himself, six years ago, at another desk, in another life — and had told the boy a truth shaped around it, the way you shape a key around a lock.

Some lies, he had learned, were just files waiting to be reopened.

Across the harbour, in the dark where the lamplight gave out, the morning tide was already turning.
