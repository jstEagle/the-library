## 17. The Father's File

Major Corvane did not take me to an interrogation room. He took me to his office, which was worse.

An interrogation room is honest about its intentions — bare walls, hard chairs, the geometry of pressure made visible. His office was comfortable. There was a fire going, though it was nearly June. There was a decanter of sherry he did not offer me. There were two chairs before the desk and he gestured me into the one further from the window, so that I sat with my back to the light and he sat with my face in it. He had arranged us that way without appearing to arrange anything at all. That was Corvane. You never saw the machinery; you only felt yourself moving inside it.

"Tea?" he asked, as if we were two neighbours sheltering from rain.

"No, thank you."

"Fairweather." He said my name slowly, tasting it, the way a man tests a coin between his teeth. "It's a Sussex name, isn't it? Fairweather. Very old. Very respectable." He opened a drawer. "I've been reading about your family."

The fire ticked. Somewhere down the corridor a trolley rattled past on its way to the wards, and I thought of Anselm lying in the morgue annex with his stitches pulling, listening for boots, and I made myself sit still and look bored. Boredom is the best armour I know. In a hospital you learn it early: the clerk who looks interested invites questions; the clerk who looks dull is furniture.

"I'm not sure my family warrants reading," I said. "My mother kept hens."

"Your mother kept hens," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "And your father kept books." He set a folder on the desk between us. It was thin. Government grey, string-tied, the corners soft with handling. "Alfred Fairweather. Customs and Excise, Rye sub-division. Twenty-two years of service. And then—" he paused, delicately, like a surgeon reaching the interesting part of the X-ray, "—not twenty-two years of service. Then nothing at all."

I had not heard my father's name said aloud in eleven years. My mother never said it. The aunts never said it. In our house after 1905 he existed the way the dead exist in rooms where no one will draw the curtains: present, unmentioned, filling every silence. To hear a stranger speak him plainly, in that mild civil-service voice, was like hearing a nail drawn out of a wall. You hadn't known how much the picture had been hanging there until the plaster came away.

"He retired," I said.

"He resigned," Corvane corrected. "Under inquiry. And then he didn't retire, and he didn't work, and when he died in 1910 there was no pension, no record of cause beyond a local certificate, and no entry in any departmental history. A man who served this country for twenty-two years, Mr. Fairweather, and the file simply — ends." He tapped the folder once with one pale finger. "Except that it doesn't. Because I went looking."

"You have an odd idea of leisure reading, Major."

"I have an odd job." He smiled. It was not a warm smile but it was an honest one, in its way; it told me exactly what he was. "May I?"

He untied the string himself, without waiting for my answer, and laid the folder open.

* * *

I want to describe what was in it accurately, because accuracy is the whole point of me, or it always has been, and because what was in it was almost nothing — and the almost-nothing did more damage in four minutes than the war had done in two years.

A service record, complete through 1903. Commendations. A note commending him for detecting undervalued manifests at Rye — my father, who could smell a false invoice the way other men smelled rain coming. Then 1904: a marginal annotation in violet ink, initials only, E.C.B. Then a transfer request, denied. Then a second transfer request, denied. Then a letter of resignation dated March 1905, and beneath it a memo saying the resignation had been accepted "with regret" — and beneath *that*, a single sheet, typed on a machine different from all the others, unsigned:

*Subject removed from establishment. No further correspondence to be entered. Name not to appear in departmental publications.*

Name not to appear. As if the man himself were an error of typography.

"There was an inquiry," Corvane said. "In early 1905. Irregularities in cargo declarations — French spirits, mostly, some arms shipments that officially weren't arms shipments. Your father flagged them. Someone senior did not wish them flagged. When he would not withdraw his objections, the inquiry reversed itself. It stopped investigating the cargoes"— he turned a page, and I saw the violet ink again, circling, striking through — "and began investigating him. They found what inquiries always find when they go looking for it. Ambiguity. A payment received in good faith that could be made to look otherwise. A friendship with a wine merchant of uncertain politics. Nothing proven. Nothing needing to be proven. They didn't need proof, Mr. Fairweather. They needed only the absence of his name."

I sat very still. The fire had got at some resin in the logs and was spitting softly, and the room smelled of pine and old paper and sherry I wasn't offered, and eleven years collapsed into the width of a desk.

"They erased him," I said.

"They tidied him," Corvane said. "It is not quite the same thing, and the difference matters to my work." He closed the folder but did not retie it, leaving it lying open-faced on the desk between us like a body on a slab. "But yes. From the department's point of view, Alfred Fairweather ceased to exist in March 1905, and no official document ever says why, and so far as history is concerned, nothing happened to anyone."

I thought of my ledger then. I couldn't help it. I thought of the small black book in the locked drawer under my counter, forty-three names in my own cramped hand, names the casualty lists had swallowed — Private Hessler, gone between one list and the next; Gunner Mowbray, entered as *died of wounds* when I had personally carried his tea an hour after he was recorded dead. I had started it the first winter out of grief and stubbornness, and I had kept it out of habit, and I had told myself for two years that it was a private thing, a modest thing, a clerk's small rebellion against clerical error.

It was not a private thing. It had never been a private thing. It was inheritance.

"Do you know why I keep records, Major?" I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect.

"I imagine you're about to tell me."

"Because someone has to be the one who doesn't lose them." I looked at the open folder. "You people write history with a pencil. Everything can be rubbed out if it inconveniences the margin notes. My father spent twenty-two years proving that cargo was what it said it was, and the moment he proved it about the wrong cargo, he became cargo himself. Filed away. Marked *not to appear*. So yes — I keep a ledger. Names in, names kept. It isn't sentiment. It's the only audit anyone will ever run on men like you."

Corvane listened to all of it with his hands folded and his face giving back nothing but attention. When I finished he nodded slowly, as if I had confirmed something he'd written in a report months ago.

"That is very nearly a confession," he said. "You understand that. You've just described your ledger to an intelligence officer as an act of opposition."

"It isn't opposition. It's bookkeeping."

"In my experience, Mr. Fairweather, they are the same profession wearing different coats. Every archive is an argument. Every record takes a side." He leaned forward slightly, and for the first time the charm dropped away entirely, and underneath it was something older and colder and, oddly, more bearable, because at least it was real. "Which brings me to why we are here, and why I showed you your father instead of beginning with Captain Bram."

"Then show me the rest," I said. "You didn't bring me here for a séance."

* * *

He drew a second folder from the drawer — thicker, newer — and opened it facing me, and turned pages with the unhurried competence of a man who knew each one by heart.

Photographs first. A quay, taken from above. A boat, hull number partially scraped. Then faces: Marta Lindqvist, sharper-eyed than her smuggler's squint suggested, caught mid-stride on a fish-steps. Two men I did not know. Then a third photograph, grainy, long-lensed, and my chest went tight before my head caught up, because I knew that walk, that slight forward lean as if always about to correct a subordinate's saluting arm.

Bram. Taken within the month. Alive, upright, here — on this coast, in this town, close enough for a telephoto lens.

"Captain Elias Bram," Corvane said. "Declared killed in action, autumn 1914. Subsequently identified as having defected to the enemy. Currently designated a collaborator under standing orders I don't need to read you." He let the photograph sit. "You knew him. Before the war. The vicar remembers you at the same table at the Harrow Inn, summer of 1913. There's a photograph in the parish magazine."

"We knew each other," I said.

"Well enough that he wrote to you."

And there it was. Not a question — a placement of pieces. I watched myself refuse to flinch and felt the flinch happen somewhere behind my ribs instead.

"If he did," I said, "a dead man's handwriting is a curiosity, Major. Not evidence."

"It becomes evidence when the letters name men." He turned another page, and there, transcribed in his own neat hand, were excerpts — dates, ports, and names. Names. I read three lines down before I could stop myself, and then I read them all, because I am what I am, and the names reached off the page and took me by the collar. Hessler. Mowbray. Okafor-Lowe. Denny. Forty-three chances for me to recognise a line of my own ledger reproduced in an intelligence officer's files.

"You see the problem," Corvane said gently. "Your little black book and this man's operation agree with each other line by line. Either you have been keeping records of his smuggling for two years — which makes you an accomplice — or he has been evacuating men your own government lists as dead, which makes him something else again, and makes your ledger the most dangerous piece of paper on this coast. I haven't decided which story I prefer. Both hang him. Only one hangs you."

"The men were alive," I said. It came out before strategy could catch it. "The forty-three. They were alive, and the lists killed them twice."

"Perhaps," Corvane said. "Or perhaps a traitor found that dead men make excellent cover, and found that a sentimental clerk in a port hospital was keeping him a private register of exactly the men he needed to disappear." He closed Bram's folder. "That is the other possibility, Mr. Fairweather. You should sit with it tonight. Loyalty makes wonderful camouflage. I've seen better men than Bram hide entire networks inside other people's grief."

He was lying, or half-lying, testing me with a poisoned version of the truth — I understood that even then, even with my blood up. But understanding a trap does not keep your leg out of it. It only means you feel the teeth.

"Why tell me any of this?" I asked. "Why my father? Why the file?"

"Because," Corvane said, "you and I are the same kind of animal, and I'd rather not destroy you. You keep names because your father's name was kept from you. I read files because somebody read mine apart when I was nineteen and decided what I was before I'd decided it myself. We both learned the same lesson, Mr. Fairweather: whoever holds the record owns the man." He rose, came round the desk, and stood looking down at the grey folder with my father's life folded flat inside it. "So here is what holding the record buys you. Tomorrow morning I interview the night staff. By evening I will have the stranger who came off the boat, or I will have you, and I would genuinely rather have you standing in a witness box testifying against Bram than lying in a cell next to him. Your father was destroyed by an argument between men senior to him that he was never permitted to enter. Don't repeat him. Hand me Bram, hand me the woman, and this file—" he touched the folder once, lightly, almost kindly, "—goes home with you. Reopened, restored, amended. Alfred Fairweather, cleared, in the printed record, permanent ink. History can give your father back, Mr. Fairweather. I'm the only one offering."

The fire had burned low. Through the window behind him I could see the harbour, grey under a scud of cloud, and the morgue annex roof just visible beyond the chapel — a slate rectangle I could not stop my eyes finding, though I gave them nothing else.

"You've made one error," I said, standing.

"Only one? I'll take that."

"My father didn't resign because he was broken," I said. "He resigned because they asked him to sign a paper saying the cargoes were clean, and he wouldn't. He told me that the summer before he died. I was fifteen and I thought he was a bitter old man talking about ledgers." I buttoned my coat, and my hands were steady, and I have never been less afraid in my life, because terror and clarity had finally become the same feeling. "You think the file is leverage, Major. It isn't. It's instructions. He already told me what to do with men like you. It took me eleven years and your folder to hear him."

For a moment — just a moment — something moved behind Corvane's eyes that might have been respect, or might have been the pleasure of a fisherman feeling the line go taut in the direction he wanted.

"Then we understand each other," he said.

"Not remotely," I said. "But we've been introduced."

He saw me to the door himself. In the corridor, Sister Agnes was coming with a tray of dressings, and she did not look at me, and her face was perfectly composed, and thirty years of composure cost her something, I understood later, that she never got back.

Outside, the tide was turning. I walked down to the waterline where the gulls were working the mud, and I stood a long time with my shoes darkening at the toes, thinking about my father's name typed on a page by a machine that no longer existed, in ink that had never dried so much as hardened.

Then I went back through the wards, past the counter, and unlocked the drawer, and took out my ledger, and copied every name onto a second sheet in a hand deliberately not mine — because whatever happened tomorrow, whatever Corvane took from the drawer or from me, the names themselves were going to be somewhere he would never think to search.

I was folding the copy into my boot when the orderly Pell came through at a run, white to the lips, and gripped my sleeve, and whispered the words that took the choice out of everyone's hands but mine:

"They've found blood on a uniform in the laundry, Mr. Fairweather. The major's men. They're searching the boathouse now."
