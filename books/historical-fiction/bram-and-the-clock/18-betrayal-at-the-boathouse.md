## 18. Betrayal at the Boathouse

I knew the orderly's face before I knew his name. Pellow. He changed the pans on the convalescent ward and emptied the ash bins and never once looked a dying man in the eye if he could look at his boots instead. There was no contempt in it. That was the thing I understood later, lying flat under an overturned boat while soldiers kicked through Marta's nets — there was no contempt in him at all. Only fear, worn smooth as a river stone from years of handling.

I had seen him watching me. Two days ago, when I carried water across the yard with my side still stitched and pulling, he had watched me the way a man watches a door he is afraid to open. I had told Theo we should move sooner. Theo had said one more night, one more dressing change, Bram too feverish to travel in daylight. And Bram himself — God help us — had agreed, because even ghosts get tired of running.

So we stayed. And Pellow opened the door.

* * *

It began well, which I have learned is how all disasters begin. The tide was going out and the mudflats stank of salt and rot and diesel, and the boathouse sat at the end of its rotten walk like something the sea had given up on. Marta had the kettle on. That detail will stay with me longer than the gunfire: Marta, who had smuggled men past blockades and through mined channels, standing at a rusted stove warming a blackened kettle because she said feverish men drink more when the tea is hot.

Bram lay on the camp bed by the wall, propped on blankets, his wound dressed in sailcloth strips because we'd run through everything else. He was gray but lucid. He had spent the last hour quizzing me about the hospital — Sister Agnes's health, whether old Hargreaves still tended the boiler, whether the chestnut tree in the courtyard still bore, though half-blinded, conkers no one ate. A man taking inventory of what he intended to come back to.

"Theo," he said suddenly. "Where is he?"

"Forging your new life," I said. "He'll be here before dark."

"He hates forging."

"He does it anyway."

"Yes," Bram said softly. "That was always Theo." He turned his head toward the window, where the light was failing over the water, and for a moment he looked not like a traitor or a ghost but like the officer who had pulled me out of a trench at Hooge with his coat already burning, and again at Ypres when the stretcher-bearers wouldn't go and he went himself. Twice he saved me. I counted it like money because it was all I had.

Marta poured tea. She moved around the small space with a economy that made everything she touched look planned, and I remember thinking — I swear I remember this — that nothing bad could happen in a room where Marta was pouring tea.

Then the dog started.

She kept a dog, a yellow brute of uncertain breeding named Kip, and he slept under the pier. At half past five he began to bark — not the sharp complaint of gulls or strangers on the walk, but the low continuous roar of an animal announcing an army.

Marta set down the kettle without a sound.

"Out," she said.

"Marta—" Bram started to rise.

"If you argue I will shoot you myself." She was already moving, kicking open the trapdoor to the mud-flat below, where a dinghy lay keeled over in the ooze. "Down. Downstream, not out — they'll have the channel. Keep to the pilings until the third bend, then cross on foot. Anselm, you carry him or you both die here."

"There's time," I said stupidly. "Maybe it's only—"

Lamps came on down the shore. Not one. Six. Sweeping the mudflats in long white strokes, patient as lighthouse beams, closing.

"Go," Marta said.

* * *

Getting Bram down the trapdoor was like lowering a sack of wet sand that kept trying to help. His legs would not hold and his arms shook and every jolt wrung a sound out of him that he strangled down to nothing, because he was Elias Bram and he would not make noise while soldiers hunted him, even now, even bleeding through sailcloth. I got my shoulder into his armpit and half-dropped, half-carried him into the mud, and the cold of it closed over my boots like a mouth.

Above us, the trapdoor slammed. Then boards scraped — Marta dragging the boat crate over it, making it look like clutter, buying seconds with furniture.

We went downstream along the pilings, bent double, Bram's breath sawing in my ear. Behind us the boathouse door opened. I didn't see it; I heard it. Boots on the walk. A voice — Corvane's voice, I knew it from the hospital corridors by then, that mild, courteous instrument — saying something I couldn't make out. Then Marta's voice, clear and carrying across the water, pitched deliberately loud so we would know she had them:

"Gentlemen. You're trespassing on my property, and my property has rights."

A soldier laughed nervously. Corvane did not.

We reached the third bend. Bram collapsed against a piling, sliding down into the mud, and I knelt over him ready to haul him up and he caught my wrist with shocking strength.

"She won't give us up," he whispered. "You know that."

"I know it," I said, which was true, and which was the whole horror of it.

"We go back—"

"If you go back she has died for nothing." I said it brutally because gentleness would have killed us both. "The crossing. The ship. That's what she bought. We don't waste it."

He looked at me for a long second, and behind his eyes I watched him do the arithmetic that officers do, the arithmetic that turns people into expenditures, and I hated him for it and loved him for it, because I was doing the same sum.

Boots on the walk above. Then, muffled by boards:

"Madam, I'm going to ask you once more. Where are the two men?"

"What two men?" Marta said. "I trade in oysters and brandy, Major, whichever pays better. The only men who come here are the ones who pay."

Something clattered — a search, rough-handed. Kip was barking wildly now and someone kicked him and he yelped and went silent and I felt Bram flinch beside me as if the boot had caught his own ribs.

"You've been seen ferrying a wounded man," Corvane said, conversational. "Twice this week. I admire the enterprise. I'll be honest with you — I'm not interested in you. I'm interested in Captain Bram, and in whoever is helping him. Help me and you'll be trading oysters tomorrow. Refuse, and you'll be trading them from a cell until spring, and then somewhere worse."

Silence. Long enough that I raised my head an inch.

Then Marta laughed. It rang off the pilings, huge and unafraid, the laugh of a woman who had been threatened by customs men and naval patrols and at least two husbands.

"Major," she said, "you have searched my nets, frightened my dog, and put your filthy boots on my clean floor, and found nothing but a cold kettle. Come back in summer. I'll show you where the oysters are."

A pause. Then Corvane, mild as milk: "Take her in."

* * *

They brought her out between two privates and she did not struggle, which somehow made it worse — she walked like a woman keeping an appointment, chin up, scanning the water with those flat gray eyes. And I will swear before any court that she looked directly at our stretch of shadow beneath the pier, found us in the dark the way she found channels other smugglers drowned in, and gave the smallest shake of her head. *Stay.* One motion. Then the lamps swallowed her.

They did not find the dinghy. They were looking at the boathouse, at Marta, at the obvious, and we were already crawling through the mud on our bellies like animals, Bram dragging his leg, both of us painted brown to the eyebrows, while behind us the search party spread out with lamps and the yellow dog Kip stood at the end of the walk howling at the men who had taken his woman — howling, I thought, the way I wanted to and couldn't, because sound traveled over water and Marta had bought our silence along with everything else.

We crossed the flats at the third bend on our feet, then our knees, then our bellies again, and somewhere in that mile of stinking mud Bram stopped speaking altogether, which frightened me more than the lamps, because Elias Bram silent was Elias Bram deciding something.

At the sea wall we hauled ourselves up into the black root-smell of the elm hedge and lay gasping, and that was when he spoke.

"Pellow," he said.

"What?"

"The orderly. Fairweather described him to me — nervous, spectacles, family in Dover. It was Pellow. It had to be. Someone told Corvane about the ferrying, and it wasn't Marta and it wasn't Agnes and it wasn't Theo, and I was the only secret left." He closed his eyes. "A frightened man with a family. Corvane collects them. They're cheaper than informers."

"Then we run," I said. "Now. Tonight. Before the ship—"

"No." He opened his eyes and there was something in them I hadn't seen since before Ypres. Not defeat. Reckoning. "Anselm. Listen to me. If they took Marta, they took her tonight, and by morning she is in a room with Corvane, and Marta is brave but she is not immortal, and Corvane knows how to take a person apart starting with the parts they love least. Which means by morning he knows about the ship."

"Then we take another—"

"There is no other. There is this one, on the morning tide, and Theo doesn't know any of it, and he is walking to this pier at dawn with a forged ledger and a heart full of promise straight into whatever Corvane has built for him." Bram pushed himself up the bank on his elbows, mud raining off him, and his voice cracked on the next words. "I let him mourn me once. I will not let him walk into that quay blind. Get me to the salt road. Get me to Fairweather."

"It'll kill you."

"Then it kills me moving," he said, "instead of waiting."

* * *

We came into the town after midnight, two mud-creatures stitched together, Bram's arm over my neck, his weight increasing with every street as the fever climbed back into him. Searchlights still swept the harbor. Somewhere a whistle blew and we flattened into a doorway and a patrol passed close enough that I could hear the soldiers complaining about the cold, boys' voices, and I thought of Pellow's boys in Dover and understood him entirely and forgave him nothing.

The hospital annex was dark except for one window on the top floor. Theo's window. He was awake. Of course he was awake; he had probably never gone to bed, sitting up all night among his beautiful terrible names.

Getting Bram up the back stairs nearly finished both of us. Twice he fainted and twice I slapped him awake with a hand that shook. At the top, the door opened before I knocked, and Theo stood there in his shirtsleeves with the ledger under his arm and his face already going from question to answer, because Theo always read a room faster than anyone, and behind him I saw the papers spread on the table, hours of careful forgery, discharge documents for a dead man, everything ready, everything perfect, everything too late.

"They have Marta," I said.

Theo did not move for a moment. Then he stepped aside, and took Bram's other arm without a word, and together we lowered him onto the cot, and only when Bram was down — gray, shuddering, muttering an apology into the pillow that no one asked for — did Theo look up at me with an expression I had never seen on that quiet clerk's face.

"How long," he said, "before Corvane connects her to us?"

"A night, maybe less. If she's broken already — hours."

Theo set the ledger on the table beside the forged papers. He looked at them the way a man looks at the two halves of his own life. Outside, far down the harbor, the tide was turning; you could hear it change in the pilings, a long sigh under the town.

"Then we don't wait for dawn," Theo said. "We go now — and Anselm, whatever happens on that quay, the ledger goes with me."

I did not yet understand what he meant by that. I would, by morning. By morning the whole town would understand it.
