## 19. Running the Quay

### THEO

The fog came off the Channel like something poured, thick and grey and smelling of coal smoke and salt, and I had never in my life been glad of weather before. Now I blessed it. Every foot of murk between the boathouse and the quay was a foot Corvane's lanterns couldn't cross.

Anselm weighed nothing. That was the horror of it — a grown man, a soldier, reduced to a bundle of coat and bone that I could half-carry, his arm over my shoulders, his boots dragging furrows through the wet shingle. He was conscious, barely. His eyes opened at intervals like a man surfacing from deep water, took in nothing, and closed again.

"Left," Bram said behind me. "There's a slipway."

I had imagined this moment for two years — Bram coming home — and in every version of it he was strong. He was not strong. He moved like a man made of sticks and wire, one hand pressed to the bandage at his side where Marta's needlework had already soaked through once, and when he caught up to me on the slipway his breathing was a thin, torn sound.

"You can't run," I said.

"I can fall forward with purpose," he said. "It comes to the same thing."

We came up off the shingle onto cobbles, and the town opened around us, blind and sleeping. Somewhere ahead the fog glowed faintly yellow where the searchlights worked the harbour mouth, sweeping slow arcs, patient as lighthouse keepers who had learned a new trade.

* * *

### BRAM

Fairweather had got old. That was my first coherent thought, and it shamed me, because he'd have said the same of me and meant worse. Twenty-six years old and old. But his hands were steady under Anselm's arm, and his eyes did the arithmetic of the fog faster than mine did, and I thought: *this is the man I chose. Out of everyone in the world, drunk on summer and bad claret, I chose correctly.*

"The customs shed," he whispered. "Then we're exposed all the way along the quay."

"How far?"

"Four hundred yards. Maybe five."

Five hundred yards of open stone with a ship at the end of it. I had crossed no-man's-land twice and both times the ground had at least been *meant* to kill me honestly. This was worse. This was my own country trying to be quiet about it.

We reached the customs shed and pressed into its lee. Theo lowered Anselm against the boards, and the boy — he was hardly more — opened his eyes properly for the first time since the boathouse.

"Bram," he rasped. "You shouldn't be carrying me."

"You've carried me twice," I said. "We're square only if we finish."

His mouth did something that wanted to be a smile. "Captain's arithmetic."

"Bateman's is better. Keep yours open."

Down the quay, a searchlight swung and stopped. Held. The beam leaned against the fog forty yards short of us, a pale wall of it, and I heard boots — several pairs, unhurried, the walk of men searching rather than chasing.

Theo's hand found my sleeve. "They'll sweep the whole length. We can't wait them out; the tide won't."

I looked at Anselm. At Theo. Five hundred yards.

"When the light moves," I said, "we don't run. Running makes noise. We walk. Fast, straight, like men going to work."

"At four in the morning?"

"We're fishermen," I said. "We're stupid with grief about the weather."

The light crawled on. And we went.

* * *

### THEO

Walking fast while your heart tries to claw out through your ribs is a particular skill, and I learned it on that quay. Fifty yards. A hundred. The ship sat low at her berth at the end of everything, riding lights hooded except one green eye at her masthead, and I fixed on that green eye the way a drowning man fixes on anything.

Anselm's feet began to fail at two hundred yards. I felt it before I heard it — his weight shifting, his rhythm breaking — and then he went down on one knee on the stones with a sound like a struck bell, and the fog carried the noise further than fog should carry anything.

Bram didn't hesitate. That is what I will remember until I die: he didn't hesitate. He dropped, hauled Anselm's arm across his own neck, and lifted him — this man who could barely stand, whose wound had bled through twice tonight — and lifted him, and walked.

"You'll open the stitches," I hissed.

"Then sew them again at sea," Bram said through his teeth. "Count steps. Don't stop counting."

I counted. It kept the fear in a box. Three hundred and ten. Three hundred and forty. And then, out of the fog behind us, flat and unhurried and unmistakable:

"Gentlemen."

Major Corvane stood thirty yards back, alone, his cap dry despite the mist, a pistol held low against his thigh like an afterthought. Behind him, shapes. Many shapes.

"That's far enough," he said. "It's been far enough for some time."

* * *

### BRAM

I set Anselm down gently, because even now — especially now — I would not let him be dropped by anyone, least of all me. Then I turned.

"Major." My voice held. Two years of holding worse things had taught it to.

"Captain Bram." Corvane's face in the fog-light showed nothing at all, which was how I knew how much it showed. "Dead men make poor runners. All that grave-dirt on the lungs."

"The grave was always your department."

He almost smiled. "Mr. Fairweather." He shifted his gaze past me, and I felt Theo stiffen beside me like a wire pulled taut. "You've had a long night. Put the ledger down and step aside, and history will record you were deceived by a traitor. It's kinder than the alternative, believe me."

"I don't," Theo said. His voice shook and did not stop him, which is the bravest thing a voice can do. "Believe you, I mean. I don't."

"There's a ship behind you," Corvane said, reasonably, terribly reasonable, "and soldiers behind me. Arithmetic, gentlemen."

Behind me, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of a ship's bell. One strike. The tide.

* * *

### THEO

The bell changed everything and I understood later that Corvane heard it too. Ships keep no appointments. Whatever negotiation he wanted, the tide was taking his options away minute by minute, and a man like Corvane hates negotiating against a clock he doesn't own.

"Forty-three names," I said.

He blinked. First crack in the porcelain. "What?"

"The names Bram took out of France. The ones your lists call deserters and cowards and missing. He carried them out one by one while you wrote him dead. Forty-three names, Major, and every one of them alive because of the man you're pointing that gun at."

"They are *irrelevant*—"

"They're the whole war," I said, "as far as I'm concerned."

And then Bram moved. Not toward escape — toward Corvane, one staggering step, arms loose and open, and every rifle in the fog came up, and Corvane's hand twitched upward with them—

"—and there," Bram said softly, "is your dilemma, Major. Shoot a dead man in front of witnesses, or explain to your superiors why the ghost they sent you for was real, and walking, and *right*."

The fog held its breath. The green eye of the ship burned at the end of the quay. Somewhere behind Corvane, a soldier shifted his weight, uncertain, and I watched doubt move down the line of them like wind through barley — because Bram was right, and they knew it, and men will die for orders but they hate shooting at questions.

The gangway was eight yards away.

"On three," Bram breathed, without moving his lips. "Take Anselm. Don't look back. Promise me—"

"You promised *me*," I said, "in peacetime. Promises made sober bind tighter."

"One," he said.

Corvane raised the pistol. "Enough. Enough of this theatre—"

"Two."

The bell rang again, closer, urgent.

"Three."

* * *

### BRAM

I have no memory of the running, only of the water slapping beneath the pier, and Theo's shoulder driving into mine, and the gangway iron cold under my hands. Shots cracked behind us — high, wide, the shots of men who had not quite been ordered to kill — and then we were on the deck and hands were pulling Anselm aboard and someone was shouting in a language I pretended not to know.

Theo was beside me, chest heaving, blood on his cuff — Anselm's, not his — and below us on the quay Corvane stood alone in the lamplight, pistol still raised, watching. Not firing. Calculating.

"He'll follow," Theo said.

"No," I said, and gripped the rail to stay standing. "He'll do worse. He'll write it down."

The mooring lines splashed free. The deck tilted, gentle, monstrous, final, and the quay began to slide away through the fog with the major still standing on it, small and precise and already composing whatever report would come next.

Theo's hand found mine on the rail. His fingers were freezing and shaking and they did not let go.

"The ledger," he said. "The true names. If I go with you, nobody reads them. Nobody ever knows those men lived."

I saw it land in him — the thing he'd been carrying since the boathouse, since the lighthouse, maybe since the day he started writing names officers left off. Truth or duty, and the gangway between them rising and falling with the swell.

"Theo—"

But he was already turning to the mate, already speaking fast in that clerk's voice that had carried forty-three men out of oblivion, already asking — I caught only fragments — *the pilot boat, the mail packet, anything, please.*

He pressed the ledger into my hands, still warm from his coat.

"Keep it safe," Theo Fairweather said, stepping back toward the gangway as the gap widened beneath his feet. "I'll take the names home."
