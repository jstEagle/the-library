## 20. The Morning Tide

The fog came off the water like something poured.

Theo ran with his hand on Anselm's arm and Bram's weight on his shoulder, and the pier assembled itself out of the grey one plank at a time — the bollards first, then the crane, then the gangway of the *Meridian*, black against the lighter black of the hull. Somewhere behind them the searchlights swept the quay in slow, patient arcs, the way a man pats his pockets for a thing he already knows is lost.

"Left," Bram rasped. "There's a gap in the crates."

"I know the gaps," Theo said. "I've been walking this pier since I was nine years old."

He had never once walked it like this — at a dead run, before dawn, carrying a man the War Office had declared both dead and treasonous, toward a ship that would leave on the tide whether they reached it or not. The ledger was inside his coat, bound flat against his chest with two of Sister Agnes's bandage pins. Forty-three names, plus eleven more he'd added in Marta's boathouse by lamplight while Bram slept. Fifty-four names. He could feel them through the cloth like a second heartbeat.

Anselm stumbled and Theo caught him under the arm. The batman's wound had opened again somewhere between the salt house and the quay; there was blood on Theo's cuff now that was not his own, and it was warm, which meant Anselm was still alive, which meant they still had time. He kept saying that to himself, in the flat clerk's voice he used for arithmetic: *he is warm, therefore alive; the tide is at six-forty; therefore we have time.*

The foghorn sounded, long and low, and out on the water something answered it — the deep-throated moan of a ship getting up steam.

"There," Anselm breathed. "I can hear her."

"You can hear everything," Theo said. "That's not the same as us reaching her."

They came around the last stack of crates and the gangway was simply *there* in front of them, twenty feet of wet timber rising to the rail where a single figure stood with a lantern held low, and for one whole second Theo let himself believe it might be Marta, escaped somehow, come to see them off—

Then the figure raised the lantern higher, and the light fell on Major Corvane's face, and Theo understood that everything since midnight had been permitted rather than prevented.

* * *

Corvane did not look triumphant. That was the worst of it. He looked tired, and neat, and faintly apologetic, as though they had all been kept waiting past an appointment and he regretted the inconvenience. His greatcoat was buttoned to the throat. Behind him, half-lost in the fog along the rail of the pier, stood four soldiers with rifles slung — not raised. They didn't need raising. There was nowhere to go but the water.

"Gentlemen," Corvane said. "Or perhaps I should say: Mr. Fairweather, Captain Bram — though I'm told that name is no longer yours — and the man whose uniform I found folded in your morgue annex like laundry." He glanced at Anselm, who had gone very still beside Theo. "Sergeant Anselm Voss. You were reported killed at Ypres. Your mother receives your pension. I imagine she'll be pleased you're alive, if no one tells her the circumstances."

"Don't say her name," Anselm said quietly.

"I say whatever names are useful," Corvane said, without heat. "That is my profession. It appears to be yours as well, Fairweather. Names, I mean. Yours are just kept in a different book."

Theo shifted Bram's weight. Bram was barely standing; he'd spent the last fifty yards mostly carried, his breath coming in short wet pulls that Theo felt through his own ribs. Fever had him again. His eyes were bright and wrong.

"The tide's at six-forty," Theo said. "It's ten past. If you're going to arrest us, arrest us quickly, because that ship leaves with or without us and I'd rather not have wasted the run."

Corvane almost smiled. "You misunderstand the situation entirely, and I think deliberately. No one is being arrested. Look around you. Do you see handcuffs? A cell? This is a pier, Mr. Fairweather, at dawn, and I am a man having a conversation." He set the lantern down on a bollard so the light fell upward on all their faces, turning them into something theatrical. "Captain Bram will board that ship or he won't, depending entirely on what happens in the next few minutes. Sergeant Voss likewise. You, however, are not going anywhere. You're going to stand here and listen, and then you're going to make the only choice that has ever actually been available to you."

"And what choice is that?" Bram said. His voice was wrecked, but underneath the ruin of it there was still the old officer's edge, the tone that had made young lieutenants stand straighter. "You've got rifles and fog and the high ground of legality. What exactly do we get to choose?"

Corvane looked at him for a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice had changed — lower, more careful, the voice of a man setting down a heavy object.

"Do you know how many men went missing from this sector between March 1915 and last month? Not killed — missing. Men whose bodies were never recovered, whose names appear on no casualty list, whose families received nothing but silence and eventually a printed card." He paused. "Forty-three."

The number hung in the fog. Theo's hand went, without his willing it, to the ledger beneath his coat.

"Yes," Corvane said softly. "I know about the ledger. I have known about it for some weeks. You think I came to this town for the ghost stories? I came because someone in this port has been keeping a private record of every man the Army lost track of, and because forty-three missing soldiers is not a clerical error — it is an operation. Somebody has been running men off this coast for two years. Smuggling them. Evacuating the ones who couldn't be explained, the ones who'd seen things, said things, belonged to the wrong people, asked the wrong questions." He turned fully to Bram. "You. It was you. And the beautiful part — the part I genuinely admire, and I don't admire much — is that you hid it inside the paperwork itself. Every man you saved became an omission. A blank line. You made absence into an alibi."

"He wasn't a traitor," Theo said. The words came out of him before he could weigh them. "Whatever the file says. Whatever they declared. He was pulling men out of places the Army had written off, and the only crime he committed was making the Army look like what it is."

"Which is precisely why it's treason," Corvane said. "Not the boats, Mr. Fairweather. Not even the forged discharges — though those were competent work, better than competent. The crime is the demonstration. One man proved the record can be beaten. That cannot be allowed to be a lesson anyone learns." He bent and picked the lantern up again. "But here is what I am prepared to do, because I am not a monster, merely employed."

He looked at Theo directly, and Theo felt the full weight of the intelligence man's attention settle on him like a hand on the back of the neck.

"The captain boards the ship. The sergeant boards the ship. The ship sails on the morning tide, and neither of them is ever officially anything again — no arrest, no court-martial, no firing squad in some French courtyard for the newspapers to enjoy. In exchange—" Corvane drew a breath. "In exchange, you give me the ledger. All of it. Every name, every date, every route, every contact. And you stay. You walk back up that gangway ramp with me, and you spend the rest of this war and probably several years after it explaining yourself to men like me, in rooms like mine, until we are quite certain the lesson dies with you."

"No," Bram said.

"Bram—"

"*No.*" Bram pushed himself off Theo's shoulder and stood, swaying, upright, and for a moment despite the fever and the ruined body there was something of the peacetime officer in the set of his shoulders. "You don't get to buy him, Corvane. Whatever you think you know, you don't understand what he's holding. Those aren't routes and contacts. Those are men. Fathers. Sons. The only proof some family in Sheffield or Cardiff will ever have that their boy didn't just vanish into the machine." His breath caught. "You want the ledger so you can burn it. So the blanks stay blank forever."

"I want the ledger," Corvane said, "because a record that exists outside the record is more dangerous than any boat."

"Then you understand each other perfectly," Bram said, "because that's exactly why it has to survive."

And Theo, standing between them with the fog beading cold on his face, understood suddenly that he had stopped listening. Not from disrespect — from clarity. Something had finished assembling itself in him during the run down the quay, during the nights of forgery, during the hearing-to-come that he could already feel waiting for him like weather on the horizon. It was the same clarity he'd had at fourteen, copying his father's name out of the parish roll because no one else would, and it said:

*They keep offering you a choice because they believe a choice is all they need.*

*Refuse the shape of the question.*

"Major," Theo said.

Corvane turned.

"You've made an error of accounting, and I'd expect better from a man in your profession." Theo's voice was steady. He was surprised by how steady. "You've assumed the ledger is mine to trade. It isn't. I've known that for months, actually — since the night I copied the first name into it — I just hadn't found the language. A ledger of the forgotten doesn't belong to the man who keeps it. He's not its owner. He's its custodian. Custodians don't sell the collection. They don't even get to choose what happens to it. They get to make sure it passes on intact."

"So you refuse," Corvane said. "And my men take all three of you, and the ledger with you, and I read it at leisure over breakfast."

"That's the other error," Theo said. "You think the ledger is paper in my coat."

He watched Corvane go very still.

"There are three copies," Theo said. "One is in my coat. One left this town yesterday in a mail sack addressed to a newspaper office in London, in an envelope that will be opened whether I'm free or in a cell, because I paid a woman named Marta enough smugglers' gold to guarantee delivery, and Marta does not break contracts, only bones. And the third—" he paused, and found he was smiling, and could not stop it, "—the third went into the tide line at the lighthouse three days ago, wrapped in oilcloth, weighted, timed, in a place only one other living person knows about. If I am arrested, if I disappear, if I die of a fever contracted mysteriously in custody — the third copy surfaces. You cannot burn what you cannot find, Major. You taught me that. Or rather, the Army taught me that, when it burned my father."

Corvane's face did not move. But behind his eyes something recalculated, rapidly, and Theo watched it happen and knew he had finally, finally landed a blow on ground the man could not fortify.

"Your father," Corvane said slowly, "was a different matter."

"My father was exactly this matter. A man erased because erasure was cheaper than truth. I keep a ledger because somebody once kept a file on my father and then decided the file was more dangerous than the man." Theo stepped forward, one step, close enough that the lantern-light touched them both. "So here is my offer, and it is the only offer on this pier, and you should hear it before your pride makes you do something stupid with those rifles."

He pulled the ledger from inside his coat. Held it out flat on both palms, the way he'd seen men surrender swords in engravings.

"You take it. Tonight. Freely given — no trade, no conditions, no immunity. I'll walk up the ramp with you right now and answer every question you have until you're sick of my answers. In exchange, nothing changes for Bram and Anselm — not your terms, your real terms, the ones you came here intending to honor because you need this closed quietly. They sail. The ship leaves on the tide. And the copies I've placed become worthless, because the custodian gave it up willingly and the story ends with the record restored, not destroyed. You get your clean conclusion. The Army gets its blanks back. And I—" he heard his own voice catch and steadied it, "—I get to stop carrying it alone."

"Theo." Bram's hand closed on his arm, grip fierce despite the fever. "Don't. Don't you dare. Two years I built that ledger into your hands—"

"You built it into the world," Theo said. "I'm just the postman." He looked at Bram then, fully, and let everything he couldn't say in front of Corvane live in his eyes — the anger and the love and the summer field and the vow and the forty-three mornings of copying names by lamplight. "You promised me once that if either of us ever needed to disappear, the other would make it so. Well. I'm making it so. Both directions. Go home, Elias. Come back when the war lets you. That's the promise kept — not broken. Kept properly."

Bram stared at him. And then, slowly, with the terrible grace of a man accepting a wound he knows is mortal, he took his hand off Theo's arm and straightened as much as his body allowed.

"He always was the better accountant," Bram said to Corvane, hoarsely.

Corvane looked at the ledger for a long moment. Then at Theo. Then out at the fog, where the ship's horn sounded again, closer now, impatient.

"The copies," he said. "You'll tell me where they are."

"They'll tell *you* where they aren't," Theo said. "That's the arrangement. Take it or take all three of us and lose anyway."

For a moment — one suspended, salt-heavy moment — Theo genuinely believed the man would order the rifles up. The fog pressed in. The soldiers shifted. Anselm, beside him, had moved almost imperceptibly so that his body stood between the nearest muzzle and Bram.

Then Corvane exhaled, took the ledger out of Theo's hands with the care of a man lifting something consecrated, and stepped aside from the gangway.

"Board," he said. "Both of you. Now, before I recover my judgment."

* * *

Bram gripped Theo's collar at the foot of the gangway and pulled him close, forehead nearly touching forehead, breath hot with fever against the cold air.

"Read the names aloud," he whispered. "Wherever they put you. Until they can't bear it. Promise me."

"Already promised," Theo said. "Peacetime rules apply."

Bram laughed — a broken, wet, wonderful sound — and then Anselm was shaking Theo's hand with both of his, hard, wordless, and then they were going up the ramp together, the rescuer leaning on the rescued, two shapes dissolving into the fog at the top, and a sailor's voice calling the line aboard, and the gangway timbers shuddering as it began to rise.

Theo stood on the emptying pier and did not watch the ship go. He watched Corvane instead, standing at the bollard with the ledger held against his chest like a man who has won a document and lost an argument, and he saw the exact moment the major realized the third copy might not exist at all — that Theo had gambled everything on a bluff dressed as arithmetic — and saw him decide, visibly, deliberately, never to find out.

"Mr. Fairweather," Corvane said at last, without turning. "You understand there will be a hearing."

"I'm counting on it," Theo said. "I have fifty-four names, Major, and I intend to say every one of them out loud, in order, as many times as it takes."

The foghorn sounded. The tide turned. And somewhere out in the grey, a ship carrying two men who did not officially exist slipped past the harbor mouth and into the open sea, while Theo Fairweather turned and walked back up the pier toward the lights of the town — unbound, unguarded, and utterly certain of what was coming for him next.
