## 1. The Weight of Water

The sea gave up its pearls at dawn or not at all.

Ansel Eastwick had learned this before he learned his letters — that the oysters opened their best secrets in the first grey hour, when the tide turned and the harbor lay still as held breath. By the time the sun cleared the headland and struck sparks off the water, the beds had been worked over by every diver in Saltmere, and what remained was shell and silt and disappointment.

So he went down in the dark.

He stood on the gunwale of the *Petrel*, Meridian's smallest workboat, and let the rope harness settle across his shoulders the way a yoke settles across an ox. Below him the water was black glass, faintly starred with the last of the night's reflections. He could see the lamp of old Piet Voss's boat two hundred yards east, a smudge of orange bobbing in the dark, and beyond it nothing but the slow breathing of the sea.

He spat over the side, took the stone in both hands, tipped forward, and fell.

The cold hit him like a hand pressed flat against his chest. It always did, no matter how many hundreds of times he'd made this descent, no matter how well he knew the water's moods. Twelve years of diving and the sea still greeted him each morning like a stranger — polite, indifferent, ready to be offended.

He sank. The weight stone pulled him down through layers of temperature and light: black to bruised grey to a green so deep it felt like looking into the inside of his own skull. His ears equalized with small practiced pinches of his nose. At forty feet the harbor floor came up to meet him, pale sand ribbed like a washed bedsheet, and the oyster beds spread out below in their long cultivated rows — Meridian's rows, every shell in them counted and owned down to the last nacreous secret.

Ansel released the stone and let it rest on the bottom beside him, its rope trailing up toward the light like something climbing out without him.

He worked.

His hands moved on their own now, after twelve years; he barely had to think of them. Palm along the sand, feel for the hinge of a shell, judge by weight and by the set of it whether the oyster held a pearl worth carrying up or one better left for the company's discard pile. A good diver could tell from outside the shell. Ansel could tell from the way it sat in the mud. There were perhaps four men in Saltmere who could do that, and two of them were past fifty, and one of those was blind in one eye from a bad ascent years ago.

He filled the net bag at his hip. One, three, seven shells. His lungs began their slow argument with him, a tightening behind the breastbone that most men would have answered immediately, kicking for the surface.

Ansel stayed down.

This was the part the other divers whispered about at the tavern, when they thought he couldn't hear — though Ansel heard everything in Saltmere, because hearing was the one thing Saltmere paid him for without knowing it. *Eastwick boy stays under too long,* they said. *Got gills, that one. Got no sense.* They meant it half as praise and half as warning, and they were right both ways, because the truth was simpler than either: down here, in the cold and the pressure and the dark, there was nothing owed. No ledger. No clerk with his neat columns. The sea didn't keep accounts. The sea only kept you or didn't.

He pried loose an eleventh shell, felt its weight — good, heavy, that dense little promise inside — and finally let his body have what it wanted. He kicked for the surface, rising through the green and the grey and the black, and broke out into air with a gasp he kept as quiet as he could, because noise carried over water at dawn and Piet Voss was close enough to hear.

*Voss.* The name sat strangely in his chest this morning, the way it sometimes did. Piet was no kin to Maren Voss — the harbor was full of Vosses, half of them related to each other in ways nobody could untangle — but names were names, and hers still surfaced in him at odd hours like an oyster rising in a storm-churned bed.

He hauled himself onto the gunwale, dripping, and began the morning's arithmetic. Eleven shells, conservatively four bearing pearl. At Meridian's rates — and Meridian's rates were the only rates, since Meridian owned the beds, the boats, the shucking shed, and Ansel himself down to the last hour of his indenture — four good pearls might clear him six days of debt. Six days off a balance of nearly four thousand.

At that pace he would be free at fifty-one.

Ansel wrung out his hair and did not sigh, because sighing was a luxury, and luxuries were the first thing the company taught you to strike from your personal column.

* * *

By full light the harbor had woken into its usual industry, which was to say its usual pretending. Saltmere lived off Meridian and hated Meridian and could not exist five minutes without Meridian, and everyone in town had arranged themselves around this contradiction the way a body arranges itself around an old injury — carefully, unconsciously, favoring the side that hurt least.

Ansel rowed the *Petrel* in past the breakwater, past the great company warehouse with its crane arm folded like a sleeping bird, past the counting house with its shuttered windows and its brass plaque polished bright enough to shame the sun. MERIDIAN TRADING COMPANY — CHARTERED BY THE CROWN. Beneath the words someone had once scratched something else into the paint, years ago; the company had painted over it twice, and the ghost letters still rose through on damp mornings. Ansel had never been able to read them. He suspected nobody really wanted to.

The quay was already busy. Fishwives with baskets. Carters arguing about axle grease. Two company clerks in their grey coats walking the boards with ledgers under their arms, checking, always checking — the tonnage of fish, the count of barrels, the hours logged against each diver's name in the great book that lived in the counting house and governed every life in Saltmere more surely than the king's law did.

"Eastwick." One of the clerks raised a finger without breaking stride. "Quota's raised. Nineteen shells a man this week."

"Nineteen." Ansel kept his voice flat. "Was fourteen yesterday."

"Company found new buyers." The clerk was already ten feet away, already talking to someone else. That was how Meridian delivered news: in passing, the way you'd toss a fish head to a gull, not caring if it caught it.

Ansel tied off the boat and carried his net to the shed, where the shuckers — mostly women, mostly older, their hands scarred into permanent half-curls by decades of knife work — would open his catch and weigh his fortune in front of him with the tenderness of bankers.

Old Sella took the net from him and peered inside. "Eleven," she said. "Light morning."

"Tide's wrong."

"Tide's always wrong lately, according to you." But there was no malice in it. Sella had shucked oysters beside his mother once, before. She weighed the shells, marked his number in her own book — everyone in Saltmere kept books; it was contagious — and slid two extra empty shells into the discard pile where he could see her do it, which was her way of saying what she would never say aloud.

"Thank you, Sella."

"Don't thank me. Thank the tide." Her knife flashed. "And eat something, boy. You look like a rope that's been left out in weather."

He ate something. Bread and salt fish on the seawall, watching the mail packet come in from the south, white sail catching the early sun. The packet came weekly, bringing letters and orders and occasionally a stranger, and the whole harbor paused for it the way a congregation pauses for the doors to open. Today it came in clean and quick, and even at this distance Ansel could see it rode higher than usual — few crates, then. Passengers, maybe.

He chewed his bread and felt the small familiar ache underneath everything, the one that never quite healed and never quite worsened. Hunger, but not for food. He'd stopped being able to name it around his seventeenth year and had simply learned to carry it, the way divers learn to carry the pressure in their ears — not cured, just managed, equalized with small practiced gestures nobody noticed.

Twelve years old when his father drowned. Thirteen when his mother signed the indenture. He'd stood in the counting house between her and a man named Gorrick with ink on his fingers, and he remembered the sound of the pen scratching, and he remembered thinking that the scratch sounded like something small dying. His mother had smiled at him afterward on the quay, a smile assembled entirely from pieces, and told him it was a fine bargain, a fair bargain, the best anyone could do, and she had died two winters later with the debt still standing, because grief is expensive and medicine costs money and Meridian was happy to extend credit at terms the crown would have called usury if Meridian hadn't helped write the crown's maritime law.

Four thousand crowns. Give or take. The number changed weekly, and never downward by much.

Ansel finished his bread, brushed the crumbs from his knees with the thoroughness of a man performing a small ritual, and went back to work.

* * *

Three more dives before noon. The quota sat in him now like a stone of its own — nineteen shells, when the season's average ran twelve — and he made his calculations the way he made all his calculations: silently, thoroughly, and alone. Other divers cursed openly, gathered on the quay, talked of petitioning the harbormaster. Ansel listened to all of it and said nothing, which was its own kind of speech in Saltmere, and everyone understood it correctly: *the Eastwick boy doesn't make trouble.* The Eastwick boy has four years left on his indenture. The Eastwick boy wants his papers clean when freedom comes.

All true. None of it the whole truth.

The whole truth was that silence was the first thing the sea had taught him and the second thing Meridian had taught him, and somewhere in the space between those two teachers he had become a man who heard everything and offered back almost nothing. He knew which fishermen sold to the company at cut rates because they owed gambling debts to the company's own tally-man. He knew which clerk skimmed from the shed weights and which clerk covered for him and why. He knew that Director Hale took his coffee on the counting house balcony at eight sharp and that the shutters of Hale's office stayed lit past midnight maybe twice a month, and that both times, within the week, a ship's insurance claim had been settled in Meridian's favor.

He knew these things the way the seabed knew the anchors that dragged across it — taking the marks, keeping the shape of them, saying nothing.

Because saying something, in Saltmere, was how his father had ended up in the water.

That was the story nobody told straight, though everyone told it sideways. Bram Eastwick had been a diver too, the best of his generation, and Bram Eastwick had objected — loudly, publicly, at the tavern, at the shed, once at the counting house steps itself — to the company's practice of re-grading pearls downward after purchase. *You bring up a jewel and they call it a seed,* his father used to say, and then laugh, because laughing was safer than the alternative, until one November night he went out alone on the *Petrel*, against regulation, against sense, against the weather, and the sea kept him.

Accident, said the coroner, who was paid by the company.

Bad luck, said the tavern, quietly, in the way people say things they don't believe.

Ansel had been twelve. He had stood on this same quay and watched them bring the boat in empty, and he had learned in that single hour what his father had spent a lifetime failing to learn: that the water wasn't the dangerous part of Saltmere.

He dove again. The afternoon beds yielded poorly — nineteen was going to be hard, maybe impossible, unless the evening tide was generous — and the cold worked deeper into him than usual, and when he surfaced the last time the light was going long and gold across the harbor, and the counting house windows had caught fire with it, blazing orange, beautiful, indifferent.

He looked at them a moment longer than he needed to.

* * *

Evening found him on Hetta Marlow's steps, because evening always found him there; he lodged in her attic room and ate at her table and had done for nine years, since she'd taken him in a week after his mother's funeral with a look that asked no questions and a bowl of stew that answered none.

"You're gray around the mouth," she said, setting the pot down harder than necessary. "Nineteen shells, is it? Whole quay's grumbling."

"The whole quay can grumble. Grumbling's free."

"And you're not." She sat across from him, a broad woman gone narrow with age, hands like tree roots. "Eat."

He ate. Through the window behind her, the harbor lay darkening, lamplit here and there, the packet boat riding at anchor with a single light at its masthead. Something about it caught at him again — that high-riding hull, the passenger rig — and he mentioned it without meaning to.

"Packet came in heavy with people today, I thought."

Hetta shrugged. "Mail day. Somebody's somebody arriving, somebody's somebody leaving." She ladled herself more stew. "There was a gentleman asking at the Anchor this afternoon, Tilda says. Asking questions."

"What kind of questions?"

"That's the thing." Hetta's spoon paused. "Nobody could say exactly. Questions that got answered with shrugs, Tilda says. Stranger type. City coat." She looked at him across the table with eyes that missed very little and admitted less. "You'll hear about it tomorrow, I expect. Everything gets heard eventually in this town."

Everything except the things that mattered, Ansel thought. Those stayed sunk.

He finished his supper, thanked her, climbed the narrow stair to his attic room, and stood a while at the small window with the cold coming through the gaps in the frame. Below, the harbor breathed. Out at the breakwater the tide was turning, and he could feel it turning in him too, that old pull, that unnamed hunger that had followed him down every dive of his life and risen with him unsatisfied every time.

Somewhere out there, he thought — beyond the headland, beyond the shipping lanes, beyond the whole tidy cage of Saltmere — the world went on without ledgers. People walked around in it owing nothing to anybody. He had no proof such places existed. He believed in them anyway, privately, the way a drowning man believes in air.

Then he saw it: far out past the breakwater, low on the black water where no boat should be riding at this hour — a lantern. Not swinging with any swell. Held steady. And moving, deliberately, once — twice — side to side, like a hand writing something on the dark.

Like a letter.

Ansel stood very still in his cold attic room, and the hunger in his chest, quiet for twelve years, lifted its head.
