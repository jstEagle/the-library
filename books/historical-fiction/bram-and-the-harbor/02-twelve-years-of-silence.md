## 2. Twelve Years of Silence

The sea gave nothing back that morning, and Ansel Eastwick had learned long ago not to ask it to.

He sat on the edge of the drying shed with a rag in one hand and his father's knife in the other, working the salt crust from the blade the way he did every evening after the last dive boat came in. The knife had belonged to Bram Eastwick for nineteen years and to Ansel for twelve, and in all that time Ansel had never once sharpened it past its old edge. It was worn now, the steel thinned to a whisper of itself, but he could not bring himself to grind away what remained of his father's hand on the hilt. So he cleaned it, and oiled it, and let it dull another day.

Saltmere at dusk was a town made of rope and rumor. The drying racks clacked in the offshore wind like slow applause. Somewhere down the strand, children were daring each other into the shallows, their shrieks carrying over the water in bright broken threads. The Meridian counting house stood black against the last of the light, its windows already shuttered, though everyone knew the clerks worked by lamplight until the ninth bell — because Mr. Hale liked the town to see the light burning, liked them to understand that somewhere in Saltmere there was always arithmetic being done against them.

Ansel finished with the knife. He wrapped it in oilcloth and slid it into the box under the floorboards where he kept everything else his father had left: a brass diving weight stamped with the company mark, a coil of writing that might have been letters or might have been accounts, and one small pearl, misshapen and gray as a rain cloud, that Bram Eastwick had pulled from the deep on the morning of the day he drowned.

Ansel had never sold it. He did not know what it was worth. He suspected it was worth nothing, which was perhaps why the company had let him keep it.

* * *

His father had died on an ordinary Tuesday, six fathoms down off the Gull Rocks, in water so clear the men on the surface swore they could see him the whole time. They said it the way people say such things — as if clarity were comfort, as if watching a man stop moving were somehow better than not seeing him go.

Ansel was four years old. His memories of that day were not memories so much as impressions the years had pressed flat: his mother's hands going still over the washing; the strange quality of the silence that moved through the divers' quarter like weather; a neighbor woman kneeling in front of him and saying his name twice before he understood she meant for him to answer.

Bram Eastwick had been the best diver in Saltmere. Everyone said so, even the company men, who rarely praised anyone they couldn't bill. He could hold his breath longer than any man on the coast, could read the sea floor through ten feet of green murk, could feel a shell bed under the sand the way a blind man reads print. And on that Tuesday he had gone down healthy and come up dead, and the company's physician had written *rupture of the lung, sustained in the ordinary course of employment*, and the company had paid out nothing, because the indenture papers — which Ansel would later read more times than he could count — contained a clause about hazards assumed voluntarily by the signatory.

Hazards assumed voluntarily. Ansel was eleven the first time he understood those words, and twenty-four now, and he still felt something turn over in his chest when he thought of them. Voluntary. As if his father had woken that morning hungry for the deep. As if a man with two children and a wife took a breath and walked into cold water for the pleasure of it.

What his father had been hungry for was the bonus. The deep beds beyond the Gull Rocks paid triple rate, and Bram had a daughter with a bad lung and a son who would need shoes by winter, and the company paid triple rate precisely because the deep beds killed men — killed them often enough, and cheaply enough, that the mathematics still favored Meridian. Ansel understood this now the way he understood tides: not as a revelation but as a fact of the world, constant, impersonal, reliable.

Grief, he had found, did not stay grief. It wore smooth, like a stone in a current, until what you carried was no longer sharp enough to cut you but heavy enough that you never forgot it was there. He carried his father every day. He carried him when he checked the seams of his diving suit, when he counted his breaths on the way down, when he surfaced slow — always slow, always against the burning in his chest — because the physician's phrase *rupture of the lung* lived in him like a splinter. He carried him in the knife wrapped in oilcloth. And most days the carrying cost him nothing he noticed. That was what worn-smooth meant. You only felt the weight when you tried to set it down.

* * *

His mother lasted four years after the drowning. Not in body — she lived eleven more — but in will, and Ansel marked the difference precisely, because the night her will broke was the night she signed him away.

He was eight. Sela Eastwick had buried a husband, watched a daughter's lungs fail by increments, and run out of ways to feed three mouths on a widow's portion of nothing. The company did not starve widows outright — that would have been poor practice, and Meridian was nothing if not good practice — but it let the ledger do its quiet work. The rent on the company row house crept up. The scrip at the company store bought less each season. The debt, which had begun as funeral costs and back-rent, grew like something alive in the dark, compounding at rates Ansel would spend half his life trying to compute.

In the spring of his eighth year, a clerk named Pellings came to the house. Ansel remembered him exactly: a narrow man with ink-stained cuffs and a voice like a receipt being read aloud. He sat at their table without being offered a chair, because he knew he didn't need one. He spread papers on the wood and smoothed them flat with both hands, and then he explained to Sela Eastwick, gently, in the tone men use for women they have decided are simple, what a wonderful opportunity this was for young Ansel.

Apprenticeship. Training. Board and lodging provided. A trade of real value — pearl diving, naturally, the boy showing promise, all the Eastwick men did. And at the end of it, freedom. A settled account. A clean slate.

Ansel remembered standing behind his mother's chair, his chin just above her shoulder, looking at the papers upside down. The handwriting was beautiful. That was the thing he could not get past, even then, even at eight: the handwriting was beautiful, looping and confident, and it turned his whole life into a page someone else had already finished writing.

His mother's hand hovered over the signature line. It hovered a long time. Ansel could feel her trembling, a fine vibration through the chair-back, like the shiver a boat gives when the anchor catches.

"Twelve years," she said. "You said seven before."

"The terms were adjusted," Pellings said, "given the outstanding balance." He said it the way you'd note a change in the weather. Then, softening by a single degree: "Mrs. Eastwick. The alternative is the debt court. I don't think anyone wants that. The boy especially wouldn't want that. Debt court boys don't learn trades. They learn walls."

Ansel did not know what debt court was. He knew only that his mother's hand came down, and the pen scratched, and the sound it made was very small for the size of what it did.

Afterward she knelt and held him hard enough to hurt, her face buried in his neck, and she said — he would keep these words all his life, worn smooth like everything else but never gone — *"I'm buying you back, my love. However long it takes. I'm buying you back."*

She had four years to try. She took in washing, mending, shelling work at a penny a bucket. She sold her wedding ring to a factor who cheated her and she went back the next week anyway because cheating was still coin. She got the balance down — Ansel saw it once, in a moment of clerk carelessness, the figure reduced by nearly a third — and then her own lungs began to fail, the same slow drowning his father had suffered all at once, and the doctor's visits went on the account, and the medicine went on the account, and everything, always, went on the account.

She died in Ansel's twelfth year, in the same room he'd been born in, in a house owned by the company that owned him. At the end she gripped his wrist with surprising strength and looked at him with eyes that were still fierce underneath the gray, and she did not say *I'm sorry*. She said, *"Don't let them make you small in your own mind. Whatever they own, they can't own that unless you give it."*

Then she said, *"Buy yourself back,"* correcting the pronoun with the last of her breath, and she was gone.

* * *

The signing at the counting house had happened two weeks after Pellings' visit — the delay, Ansel learned much later, was deliberate, a courtesy period that existed only in the clerk's description of it. On the appointed morning, Pellings collected him from the row house himself, walking him through streets Ansel had known all his life and that suddenly seemed to belong to somebody else. The harbor was full of light. The packet boats knocked gently against the quay. Everything went on being beautiful, indifferent to the small transaction underway inside the boy walking through it.

The counting house smelled of lamp oil and paper and, beneath it, faintly, of brine that no amount of scrubbing could remove — the smell of the harbor soaked into the building's bones, as if the sea itself had been made a party to the contract.

There was a table. There were witnesses: two factors whose names Ansel never learned, a man from the harbormaster's office required by statute, and Mr. Aldous Hale, who was not yet director then but already had the look of one — a compact, careful man with pale eyes that did arithmetic while they watched you. Hale had said almost nothing during the signing. He had stood at the window with his hands clasped behind his back, looking out at the water, and only at the end, when the wax was cooling on the page, had he turned and regarded Ansel with something that was not quite kindness and not quite contempt and was, Ansel understood decades too late, appraisal.

"A strong family resemblance," Hale had said. "Your father was our finest man underwater. We expect great things, Eastwick."

We expect great things. Ansel had been eight, and those words had sounded like sunlight. He had walked home taller than he'd left. It had taken him years to hear the sentence correctly: not a compliment but a quota. We expect great things. We expect yield. We expect, in time, your father's death repeated profitably.

The indenture ran twelve years. He was eight months from the end of it now.

Eight months. He turned the fact over nightly, the way a miser turns a coin. Eight months, if the account stayed clean. If no fines were assessed. If the physician certified him sound. If the company — whose every kindness so far had been a hook baited with arithmetic — chose, in its perfect discretion, to release him.

Perfect discretion. Another clause he knew by heart.

* * *

That evening, the lamps of the counting house burned as they always did, and Ansel sat in the loft above the drying sheds where he lodged, and he did the thing he did most nights, which was nothing at all, magnificently.

Below him, Saltmere closed up shop. The tavern on the strand lit its windows and began leaking fiddle music and argument into the dark. The tide came in with its patient industry, filling the harbor, lifting the boats, erasing the mud flats where the day's failures were written. A dog barked itself hoarse at something invisible and gave up.

Twelve years of silences, Ansel thought. He had become fluent in them the way other men became fluent in languages. He knew the silence that kept a diver safe on a crowded boat (never mention how deep the beds really run). He knew the silence that kept a debtor fed (never mention what the store scrip actually buys). He knew the silence around the Gull Rocks, which no one spoke of and everyone thought about; around the clause in the papers; around the question of how many widows the company had made and how many of them it had then, in its generosity, employed.

And he knew the oldest silence, the one that predated all the others, the one he had inherited along with the knife and the gray pearl: the silence of a boy who had learned, at eight years old, at a table smelling of lamp oil, that the world was written down by other people, in beautiful handwriting, and that his name appeared in it chiefly as a line item.

He was not bitter. That was the strangest part, the part he examined sometimes like a man probing an old scar to check it's still numb. Bitterness required energy, and energy was rationed here, and besides — bitterness implied surprise, and he had not been surprised by anything Meridian had done since the age of eight. What he felt was closer to resignation, resignation's quieter cousin, the thing that lets a man rise before dawn, dive well, eat plain food, sleep dreamless, and do it again. Resignation worn smooth. Habit holding the shape of a life the way a mold holds the shape of a statue whose bronze was melted down long ago.

But underneath — and he admitted this to himself only in the dark, only in the loft, only in the voiceless way one admits things that cannot be spoken even inwardly — underneath there was hunger. Small, stubborn, unkillable. Hunger for a year with his name written in his own hand. Hunger for a morning when the first thing he decided was where he would go, not where he was sent. Hunger for a life that was his own line item, entered in his own book, at his own price.

Eight months, he told the dark ceiling. Eight months, and then we'll see.

He banked his lamp. Below, the counting house lights went out one window at a time, and the last thing visible before full dark was the Meridian flag dropping limp against its pole as the wind died — the company sleeping, the town sleeping, the harbor breathing in and out with the patience of something that had swallowed better men than any of them and kept their secrets.

Ansel slept. He did not dream, or if he dreamed, it was only of water, clear and green and going down forever, which was not a nightmare to him anymore but simply home.

Three days later, the mail packet came in from the south, and with it a stranger in a gray coat who asked questions no one in Saltmere would answer.

Ansel watched him from the loft window, and felt, for the first time in twelve years, the silence begin to crack.
