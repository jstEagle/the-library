## 3. A Stranger at the Quay

The mail packet came in on the morning tide, and Saltmere came down to meet it the way a body comes to meet a wound — slowly, all at once, without wanting to.

Ansel Eastwick saw it first from the loft window of the counting house, where he had been copying freight ledgers since before the gulls woke. The window was a finger's width too small for his shoulders; he had learned its geometry as a boy, pressing his face to the gap while the clerks below believed him somewhere else. From here he could see the whole sweep of the harbor: the fish quay with its silver-scaled morning chaos, the company pier where Meridian's colors hung limp in the windless air, the long gray line of the breakwater reaching out like an arm that had forgotten what it was holding back.

And now, cutting through the flat pewter sea, the packet — a two-masted coaster flying the postal pennant, her canvas patched in three places, her hull riding low with letters and cargo and whatever else the world beyond Saltmere decided to send them this week.

Packets came twice a month. Packets were routine. Ansel knew their schedule the way he knew the tide tables, because everything in Saltmere ran on schedules, and knowing the schedules was the only kind of power a man in his position could accumulate.

But something about this packet made him stop copying.

Perhaps it was the way she came in — not the usual wide, cautious approach through the shoal channel but straight in, bold, as if her master knew the water personally or didn't care to know it at all. Perhaps it was the single figure standing at her bow, still as a piling, watching the town come toward him with the fixed attention of a man reading a document he intended to keep.

Ansel set down his pen. Ink pooled at the nib and dried there, forgotten.

* * *

By the time the packet warped alongside the public quay, half of Saltmere had found reasons to be outdoors. That was how the town did things — no one would admit to curiosity, so everyone invented errands. Old Pell dragged a barrel of tar down the ramp he had no use for. The Teller sisters appeared on the fish steps with baskets they bent over industriously, though Ansel could see from the loft that both baskets were empty. Even the harbor master, Grole, who considered himself above spectacle, had materialized at the top of the quay ladder wearing his good coat and an expression of official indifference so studied it looked like pain.

Ansel watched all of it from his window, and understood all of it, and despised himself a little for being part of it. Twelve years in this harbor had taught him the difference between watching and looking, and he was watching now — cataloguing, filing, keeping the silences that kept people safe. Including, usually, himself.

The stranger came down the gangway alone.

He was not what Ansel expected. Saltmere's imagination, working ahead of events as it always did, had conjured a revenue officer or a magistrate's man — someone with a sword and a swagger. What stepped onto the wet stones of the quay instead was a lean, weathered man of perhaps fifty, dressed in a plain traveling coat the color of driftwood, carrying a single leather satchel and nothing else. No trunk. No servant. No papers displayed. He moved like a sailor gone soft around the edges — careful feet, rolling slightly even on solid ground — and he wore his hair long past fashion, iron-gray, tied back with a cord.

He stopped at the foot of the gangway and looked at the town.

That was the thing Ansel noticed, the thing that kept his face pressed to the gap in the window frame when routine said he should be back among the ledgers. The man wasn't looking at the harbor, or the fish quay, or the company pier with its tall-windowed counting house. He was looking at the town itself — the crooked lanes climbing the hillside, the salt-bleached roofs, the chapel spire leaning eastward like a man listening hard. He looked the way a man looks at a place he has heard described many times and is checking against the description.

Then he smiled, briefly, at nothing, and walked up the quay.

* * *

Ansel should have gone back to work.

He told himself so, twice, with the particular firmness he used for arguments he was losing. The ledgers would not copy themselves, and the second clerk, Weir, had a nose for idle minutes the way sharks had a nose for blood — one whiff and he'd be up those stairs, smiling his wet smile, noting the idleness in some mental column where notes on divers were kept alongside notes on cargo. Ansel's debt was arithmetic; his conduct was collateral. Everyone at Meridian understood this without it ever being said aloud.

But the loft window overlooked the quay, and the stranger was crossing the quay, and Ansel stayed.

The man's first stop was the customs shed, where Grole received him with the elaborate courtesy of a small official confronting a large uncertainty. Their voices carried across the water-gap in fragments, flattened by the morning air.

"—on business," the stranger was saying, "of a private nature."

"Private business in Saltmere is generally pearl business or fish business," Grole returned. "Which are you?"

"Neither." A pause. "I'm looking for someone."

"Saltmere has four hundred souls. You'll have to narrow it."

"I expect I will."

Ansel could not see the stranger's face from this angle, only the tilt of his head, unhurried, taking Grole's measure the way a diver took the measure of a current before committing to it. There was no threat in the posture. There was no apology in it either. It was the stance of a man who had asked questions in harder places than this and been refused in most of them and had outlasted every refusal.

Grole stamped something. Papers rustled. The conversation sank into administrative murmur — landing fees, health declaration, the length of stay.

"How long?" Grole asked, and Ansel realized he had been holding his breath for the answer along with half the town.

"Until I find what I came for."

"That's not a duration."

"It's the honest one."

A silence followed that Ansel could feel through the window glass — Grole deciding whether offense was worth the trouble, concluding, as Saltmere always concluded, that it was not. The stranger emerged from the shed with his papers and his satchel and his unhurried walk, and turned up the hill toward the taverns, and the town exhaled in forty doorways at once.

* * *

The morning proceeded. It had no choice.

Ansel went back to the ledgers and copied figures for an hour — tonnage, pearl weights, the small brutal sums by which men's years were converted into company property. His hand did the work. His mind stayed on the quay, turning over the stranger like a stone to see what lived underneath.

At midmorning Weir came up with the day's correspondence and stayed, as he always stayed, to look meaningfully at the clock and the window and Ansel in turn.

"You'll want to know," Weir said, in the tone of a man dispensing charity, "that Hale's asked after you. Wants the dive reports from the spring beds before noon."

"They're finished." Ansel slid the pages across the desk. "They were finished yesterday."

"Hale says they're late."

"They were finished yesterday," Ansel said again, evenly, and watched Weir decide — visibly, the way a scale tips — whether to make something of it. Weir was twenty-three and hungry and would have made something of it if there had been anything to make. There never was. That was Ansel's craft, honed longer than his diving: to be smooth water, unremarkable, the sort of man whose file at Meridian grew fat with numbers and thin with incidents.

Weir took the reports and left. Below, through the floorboards, Ansel heard the counting house going about its business — pens scratching, the great safe opening and closing, Hale's voice once, clipped and dry, giving an order nobody repeated. Hale. Director of the Saltmere station. A man who spoke of the harbor's people the way farmers spoke of weather — as a force to be planned around, never consulted.

Ansel thought of Hale, and then of the stranger asking Grole about private business, and felt the first cold thread of the unease that would follow him all day.

Because Saltmere had secrets. Every harbor town did, but Saltmere's were load-bearing. Six years of them, laid into the town's foundations like ballast, and everyone — the fishermen, the tavern keepers, the widows on the hill — had adjusted their lives to the slight list those secrets gave the place. Nobody spoke of the Voss ship. Nobody spoke of the year the insurance inquiry came and went and came to nothing. The silences had grown so old and so well-tended that most people had forgotten they were silences at all, and simply called them the way things were.

A stranger asking questions was a pick struck against all of it at once.

* * *

At noon, Ansel carried the spring-bed reports down himself.

It was partly obedience and partly strategy — better to deliver papers on his own feet than let Weir carry them and embroider the journey — but mostly it was the chance the descent gave him: past the clerks' desks, past the map room, through the corridor where the company's older records slept behind green baize doors. He did not slow. He did not look. Twelve years had taught him to pass locked doors with his eyes forward and his thoughts arranged elsewhere, and he exercised the discipline now, walking through the corridor's hush like a man wading through shallow water, disturbing nothing.

Hale received the reports in his office, which smelled of lamp oil and the particular staleness of a room where windows were opened rarely and opinions rarely at all.

"Punctual," Hale said, without looking up. He was a spare man, gray at the temples, with hands that were always clean and always folded. "The spring beds yielded under quota."

"The storm weeks cost us six dive days."

"I know what they cost." Hale signed something. "You'll extend the autumn rotation. Two additional weeks at the outer banks."

Ansel kept his face still. Two additional weeks meant two additional weeks, and the outer banks meant deeper water and colder, and the ledger between him and freedom did not care about lungs. "Yes, sir."

"You're steady, Eastwick." Hale looked up then, and for a moment his eyes held Ansel with the mild, weighing attention he gave to cargo he was assessing for purchase. "Steady men do well here. Unsteady men find their terms extended. You understand the principle."

"Perfectly, sir."

"Good." Back to the papers. Dismissed.

Ansel walked out with his spine straight and his stomach tight, hearing beneath Hale's words the polite arithmetic that governed his life: obedience credited, curiosity debited, freedom payable in full upon completion of term, penalties accruing. One more year. Three hundred and seventy-one days, though he kept the count in a corner of his mind where pride kept other men's birthdays.

He was halfway back to the stairs when he saw the stranger again — from above, through the tall counting-house windows, standing on the quay below and looking up at the building. Not at the windows generally. At the building, with the same checking look he had given the town, as if confirming that the description matched.

Then he turned away and was gone up the hill, and Ansel stood on the stairs with his heart beating oddly fast, telling himself it meant nothing, knowing it didn't feel like nothing.

* * *

The afternoon gave him an excuse to be outside, and he took it without shame: the pearl sheds needed the day's catch weighed and graded, and that was Ansel's work by right of seniority among the divers, and no clerk could do it, and no one questioned why the head diver preferred the sheds' salt-smelling dimness to the loft's ink-smelling one.

So he was at the sheds when the stranger began his rounds.

Ansel saw the pattern within the first hour, because patterns were his trade. The man worked the quay methodically, the way a dredge worked a bed — patient, thorough, indifferent to what it caught that wasn't wanted. At Pell's tarpaulin he paused and asked something; Pell's answer was a shrug that could have meant anything and therefore meant no. At the net-menders' bench he tried again; old Sorrel laughed at whatever it was, a real laugh, and then answered with a fisherman's favorite reply, which was a different question. At the weigh-house the stranger lingered longest, speaking to the tally-man, and Ansel watched the tally-man's shoulders climb around his ears like a man pulling a coat tighter against weather.

Nobody answered him. That was the remarkable thing, the thing Ansel turned over and over. Saltmere was a gossiping town, a town that could tell you the marital history of every gull on the breakwater, and here was a stranger walking its quay asking questions and receiving, universally, the town's oldest and finest product: silence. Polite silence, rude silence, laughing silence — but silence, uniform, practiced, produced without apparent coordination, as if the whole harbor were one instrument played by one hand.

And that was what chilled him, standing among the pearl bins with the afternoon light going long and gold across the water. Because silences that uniform weren't natural. They were maintained. And things maintained needed maintenance, and maintenance required someone maintaining — and Ansel had spent twelve years learning exactly how much effort Saltmere spent, daily, in a hundred small ways, keeping certain names unsaid.

The stranger worked his way toward the sheds.

Ansel kept his eyes on the pearls in the grading tray — small moon-colored lives, each one the compressed patience of some wounded oyster — and listened to the footsteps come up the ramp, unhurried, and stop.

"Good afternoon."

Ansel looked up. Up close, the stranger's face was seamed and sun-cured, with eyes a pale gray-green, like the sea before rain. They rested on Ansel without urgency, taking in the diver's calloused hands, the company brand stitched at his collar, the grade of his stillness — and Ansel understood, in one cold drop of recognition, that this man read silences too, and read them professionally.

"Afternoon," Ansel said.

"Long way to come for oysters." The stranger nodded at the trays. "Though I suppose that's the trade."

"It pays the town's bills."

"And the company's." The pale eyes didn't move. "My name is Corliss. I've just come off the packet." He let that sit, then added, mildly, as if remarking on the weather: "I'm looking for a family. Voss."

The name dropped into the shed's dimness like a stone into deep water, and Ansel felt the splash go all the way through him — down past his steady hands, past his trained face, down to the sealed place where six years of grief and silence sat in the dark wearing each other smooth.

He kept grading. His fingers did not shake. Later he would be proud of that; now he simply performed the motion, pearl by pearl, while his blood rang.

"No Vosss left here," he said. His voice came out level. He was almost surprised. "Old name. Gone now."

"Gone where?"

"Drowned out, most like. It happens in harbors." He met the pale eyes and gave them what everyone else had given them, and hated the taste of it even as it left his mouth: "You'll not find them on this quay."

Corliss regarded him for a long moment. Something moved behind the gray-green — not triumph, not suspicion exactly. Recognition. The look of a man who had thrown a line expecting to snag kelp and felt, instead, something alive take hold on the far end.

"Of course," Corliss said quietly. "Forgive the intrusion. These harbor towns — everyone's related to everyone. Easy to mistake grief for history." He touched his cap brim. "Thank you for your time."

He went back down the ramp and away along the quay, unhurried as ever, and Ansel stood among the pearls with his heart slamming and the taste of the lie still on his tongue.

*Grief for history.* The man had seen it. However carefully Ansel had closed the door, Corliss had marked which door it was.

* * *

Evening came down on the harbor like a lid.

Ansel walked home through lanes he had walked ten thousand times, past windows already glowing, past knots of townsfolk speaking too loudly about nothing — the storm damage, the herring prices, the scandalous price of lamp oil — while beneath every conversation, audible to anyone who had ears tuned to it, ran the same low frequency: *the stranger, the stranger, the stranger.*

At Hetta Quill's boarding house, his landlady had soup on and questions ready, disguised as soup-related inquiries.

"He came by the tavern at supper," Hetta said, ladling. "Sat in the corner with broth he didn't eat and listened more than he drank. Tam Swale swears he asked after shipping records. Wreck records." She set the bowl before Ansel and looked at him with her quick black eyes — Hetta missed nothing that happened under her roof or on her street, and filed it all in a ledger of her own. "Wreck records, Ansel. In *this* town."

"People ask after wrecks. Sailors are superstitious."

"Tam Swale isn't superstitious. Tam Swale is frightened, which is different and worse." She wiped her hands on her apron and stood over him a moment, and for a heartbeat Ansel thought — absurdly, dangerously — that she was going to say the name. That the silence might crack right there over the soup. But Hetta had kept the town's silences longer than Ansel had been alive, and she kept this one. "Eat while it's hot," she said instead, and went back to her kitchen, and the clock ticked, and outside the wind came up off the water carrying the smell of the coming night.

Ansel ate mechanically and did not taste it.

In his narrow room under the eaves, he lay awake in the dark with the harbor sounds in the walls — halyards ticking against masts, the far dog, the sea working the breakwater in its sleep — and turned the day over and over like a coin he couldn't stop biting.

*A family. Voss.*

Six years since the town had said the name aloud in his hearing. Six years since the ship went down with all hands — with Maren Voss aboard, or reported aboard, or said to be — and the insurance men came and the inquiry closed and the silence settled over the harbor like fog over a grave, and Ansel had done what he always did, what he had been doing since he was nine years old and the sea took his father: he had kept the silence, worn the grief smooth, folded the girl he'd promised things to under the breakwater into the same dark drawer where he kept everything the water had taken.

And now a stranger stood on the quay asking after the drawer by name.

He did not believe in omens. Divers couldn't afford them; the sea offered signs enough without inventing meanings. But lying in the dark he could not shake the feeling of the day having a shape — the packet arriving bold through the shoals, the man checking the town against a description, the pale eyes finding Ansel among all the town's closed faces and recognizing, precisely, which one had flinched.

Something was coming up from the deep. He could feel it the way he felt groundswell through a boat's keel before the wave showed itself — pressure first, then the swell, then the breaking.

Tomorrow, he told himself, he would go to the sheds and grade pearls and say nothing, and the stranger would ask his questions and receive the town's silence, and in a week or a month the packet would take him away, and Saltmere would settle back into its list, its ballast, its beautiful terrible quiet.

He fell asleep near dawn believing it, the way you can believe anything if you hold the belief very still.

He woke to the sound of voices in the street below — Hetta's, sharp, and another voice answering, low and unfamiliar — and through his window, gray in the early light, he saw Corliss standing at the gate of the boarding house, looking up at the house's windows one by one.

Counting them.
