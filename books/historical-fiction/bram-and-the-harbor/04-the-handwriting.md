## 4. The Handwriting

The counting house smelled of ink and cold iron the way it always did before the ledgers were opened, and Ansel Eastwick had learned to love that smell the way a prisoner learns to love the sound of his own chains — not because it was sweet, but because it was his.

He climbed to the loft at half past six, as he did every morning but the Sabbath, and took down the day's dive roster from its nail by the shuttered window. The paper was stiff with salt damp. He read the names by the gray light coming through the slats: Toma Brand first bell, the Keswick brothers second, himself on the third and last rotation, when the light came slantwise through the water and the pearls sat up in their beds like coins someone had forgotten. His name was written in the clerk's neat hand — *A. Eastwick, indent., third rot.* — and beneath it, as beneath every name, the small notation that meant nothing and everything: *debt standing.*

Twelve years of debt standing. His father's boat, his father's funeral costs, his mother's signature pressed into wax in a room that had smelled of tallow and fear. The company called it *advancement against future labor*, four words that meant a boy of eleven sold to the water until the water or the arithmetic released him.

Ansel folded the roster and was reaching for the tally slate when he heard the door below open — early, too early for the clerks, who arrived with the sun well clear of the harbor wall — and then footsteps on the stairs. Not the clerk's shuffle. Not Hale's deliberate tread, each boot-fall placed like a stamp on a document.

These steps were lighter, uneven, the gait of a man used to the roll of decks.

Ansel set the slate down and stood very still among the shadowed shelves, where the old voyage records slept in their oilcloth, and watched the stairwell.

The stranger from the quay came up into the loft without knocking. Up close he was younger than he'd seemed from the window yesterday — thirty perhaps, maybe less, worn older around the eyes — with a face like a rope left out in all weathers, browned and cracked and still holding. His coat was good wool gone gray at the cuffs, cut for a man of means and traveled hard. He carried a leather satchel flat against his chest the way some men carry a child.

"You're Eastwick," he said. Not a question. "The diver."

"I'm the loft keeper this hour," Ansel said. "Diving's not till third rotation."

"Third rotation." The man smiled without warmth. "You even count your hours like an inventory." He looked around the loft, at the shelving, the tallied barrels below through the floor grating, the harbor beyond the small window where the morning fog was lifting off the water in rags. Then back at Ansel. "Corliss," he said, and offered no more than that. No vessel, no port of origin, no business.

"Meridian doesn't see visitors before eight."

"Meridian," Corliss said, tasting the word, "sees exactly what it wants to see, and not one thing more. That's rather why I'm here." He moved along the shelf, trailing one finger over the oilcloth bindings, reading spines the way another man might read faces. "You've been here twelve years. That's a long time to learn a house."

"It's long enough to know you don't touch the records."

Corliss withdrew his finger. Something shifted behind his eyes — not apology; calculation, revised. "Fair enough." He unslung the satchel and held it against him again. "I'll be plain, since plainness seems to be the only coin this town accepts. I came on the mail packet. I'm asking after a ship. The *Vespera*. Out of Saltmere, six years gone, listed lost with all hands off the Serrano shoals."

The name went through Ansel like cold water through cloth.

He kept his face still. He had twelve years' practice keeping his face still — under the water especially, where a flinch cost air, and air was the only wage a diver truly counted. But something must have moved anyway, some flicker in the muscles around his eyes, because Corliss leaned forward slightly, like a man who has heard the fish take the hook far down in dark water.

"The *Vespera*," Ansel said. "That was before my time on the deep rotations."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's the only answer there is."

"Is it." Corliss reached into the satchel and drew out a letter — a single sheet, folded twice, travel-worn, its edges soft as cloth from being handled. He held it out across the space between them. "Then this will mean nothing to you."

Ansel should have refused it. He knew that the way a diver knows, three fathoms down, when the current has turned against him — some animal arithmetic older than thought. Take nothing from a stranger. Answer nothing. Keep the silences that keep you safe.

He took the letter.

* * *

It was the handwriting that undid him.

Not the words — he made himself read the words last, made himself hold the page at arm's length first like a man checking whether a blade is sharp — but the hand itself. A slanted script, urgent, leaning eastward as if every word were walking into a wind that blew from the west and would not stop. The letters were tall and narrow, the crossbars struck through with a quick impatient flick, the tails of the *g* and the *y* looped once and cut short, as if the writer could never spare time for ornament. And the capital *M* — he would have known that capital anywhere, that *M* like two wave-crests meeting, drawn in one motion, always slightly too large for the line —

Maren Voss had written her name ten thousand times in that hand. In the margins of his father's tide almanac. In the sand below the breakwater with a bent nail. On the slate outside her mother's door: *gone to the beds, back by the bell.* On the note she had pressed into his hand the night before her ship sailed, which he had read so many times the creases had gone soft as skin, and which he had burned two winters ago in the stove of Hetta's kitchen because hope kept too long turns to rot, and he could not afford rot, not in that house, not in his chest.

Six years dead. Drowned with all hands off the Serrano shoals. There had been a memorial board nailed to the customs post, twenty-two names in official paint, hers fourth from the top because the list ran alphabetical within families and Voss came after Vance. He had walked past it every morning for a year and taught himself not to look, the way he had taught himself not to look at the berth where his father's boots used to sit.

And now the handwriting was in his hands, alive, leaning into its wind.

*They watch the harbors,* the letter said, in that voice he could hear as clearly as if she stood behind him in the loft. *But they do not watch the mail packets, because no one important rides the mail. I am well. I am more than well. What I have found is worth everything it cost. Do not come to me — send me the man I can trust, and let him be patient, and let him say nothing to anyone, above all to no one who wears Meridian's colors.*

There was no signature. Only that capital *M*, two wave-crests meeting.

Ansel became aware that his hands were shaking. He stilled them the way he stilled them underwater — deliberately, muscle by muscle, from the shoulders down — but the shaking moved inward instead, into the cage of his ribs, where no discipline of twelve years could follow it.

"Where," he said, and his voice came out lower than he intended, scraped, "did you get this."

"In a port I won't name, from a courier I won't name, in a chain I won't describe." Corliss took the letter back gently, folding it along its soft old creases. "The chain ends with me. It began with the woman who wrote it."

"She's dead."

"Yes," Corliss said. "She's been dead for six years. Officially. There's a memorial board, I understand. Very tidy paint." He tucked the letter away and looked at Ansel squarely. "Dead women write remarkable letters, Master Eastwick. This one has been traveling for eight months to reach a man in Saltmere who would recognize the hand. Eight months. Through four ports and two wars' worth of checkpoints. Because the woman who wrote it said there was exactly one person in this whole gray harbor town who might read it and not run straight to the counting house to sell it for a year off his debt."

The words landed like a weight belt dropped on stone.

"And how does she know that," Ansel said quietly, "about my debt?"

"Because she grew up here too. Because she watched them take her own father's boat for arrears. Because—" Corliss stopped himself, and something almost gentle crossed the rope-cracked face. "Because she remembers a boy who dove for her ribbon off the breakwater pilings in winter, and brought it up bleeding from the ears, and laughed about it after."

The memory rose whole and sudden, the way drowned things rise when the storm turns the beds: Maren, fourteen, her red hair whipping in the harbor wind, her mother's good ribbon blown off the quay rail and into black February water. *Don't you dare,* she'd said, and he'd dared, because she had asked it like a challenge and he was fifteen and stupid with wanting to be brave in front of her. Three dives. The last one down past the piling bases where the light gave out. His ears had rung for a week. She'd wrapped the wet ribbon around his wrist and tied it there and said, *Now it's yours too, so you have to keep it safe forever, that's the rule.*

He had kept it. Until the burning, with the note. Two winters ago. He had told himself it was prudence. He knew, standing in this loft smelling of ink and cold iron, that it had been grief wearing prudence's coat.

"If she's alive," Ansel said slowly, "and writing letters, then she knows things about the *Vespera*. About how it sank."

Something flickered across Corliss's face — there and gone, a lantern shaded behind a hand. "Everyone knows how the *Vespera* sank. It struck the Serrano shoals in weather."

"Then why does a living drowned woman need a chain of couriers to carry her letters?"

For a long moment Corliss said nothing. Below them, through the grating, Ansel heard the first clerk arrive, keys jingling, the day's machinery beginning to turn. The fog was lifting off the harbor; he could see the mail packet at its mooring, gulls working its rigging.

"Because what she found," Corliss said at last, carefully, "is worth more than her life. Worth more than yours. Worth more than this entire town's obedience, which I begin to suspect is the most expensive thing in it." He shouldered the satchel. "I'm not asking you anything today, Eastwick. Today I'm only asking whether the hand on that letter was familiar."

Ansel looked at the harbor. At the water lying smooth over the pearl beds, over the shoals, over whatever else lay down there in the cold keeping its silence. Twelve years he had survived by knowing which silences keep people safe.

"No," he said.

Corliss studied him for a moment. "No?"

"I don't recognize it," Ansel said, and heard his own voice lie smooth and level as still water, and marveled dimly, somewhere beneath the roaring of his blood, at how easily it came — as though the skill had been waiting in him all along for something worth spending it on. "I was eleven when the *Vespera* sailed. Children don't remember handwriting."

Corliss held his gaze a moment longer. Whatever he saw there — the truth, or the magnificent architecture built over it — he inclined his head. "Of course. Forgive me." He started toward the stairs, then paused, one hand on the rail. "I lodge at the Anchor and Chain. I'll be in Saltmere some days yet. These things take time." His eyes went briefly to the shelving of voyage records, the oilcloth spines, the tallied history of the company's ships. "Most things in this town are a matter of records, I find. And most records keep poorly."

Then he was gone, his uneven seaman's tread fading down the stairs, and the counting house settled back around Ansel like water closing over a diver.

He stood a long while among the shadows. Through the small window the fog burned off the harbor in gold rags, and the *Vespera*'s name surfaced in him unbidden — not from any ledger, he realized, but from Maren's own mouth, the summer before she sailed, laughing on the breakwater: *the *Vespera*, isn't that fine, evening star, they named her ship for the evening star, and she swore she'd send him letters in a hand so distinctive no clerk could ever forge it, and he'd laughed and said no one writes letters anymore once they're rich—

Two wave-crests, meeting.

His third rotation bell rang out across the water, thin and bright. Ansel took up the tally slate with steady hands, and wrote his name, and went down to the harbor to breathe water for the company, and all the while, beneath the practiced stillness of his face, something old and buried turned over in the deep and opened its eyes.
