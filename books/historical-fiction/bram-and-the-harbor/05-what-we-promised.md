## 5. What We Promised

The letter was still folded in his shirt when sleep finally took him, and in the dream he went back to the breakwater — because he had never really left it.

* * *

Six years before, the summer air over Saltmere had smelled of tar and ripe samphire, and Ansel Eastwick had been nineteen years old and certain of almost nothing except that Maren Voss was leaving on the morning tide.

He remembered the whole of that last day in pieces, the way a diver remembers a wreck: not as a shape but as fragments surfaced one at a time, each more vivid than the whole could ever have been. The smell of her father's boat — pitch and herring and old rope — as they'd helped load the last crates. The way the harbor light came slantwise through the rigging and laid gold bars across the cobbles. The sound of the Voss family's trunks being dragged down the gangway, each one announcing departure like a bell.

The Meridian Trading Company had bought the Voss shipping interest lock, stock, and hull, and with it had bought the family's passage east — a clerkship for Maren's father in the Saltmere office's sister house across the water, a "generous resettlement," a future measured in ledgers instead of tides. Old Voss had signed because there was nothing left to sign for. The company had a way of making surrender look like paperwork.

"You're sulking," Maren said that afternoon, sitting on the bollard by the customs shed with her boots dangling, watching him coil rope that did not need coiling.

"I'm working."

"You're coiling rope badly and glaring at my ship."

"It isn't your ship. It's a Meridian packet. It has your name on the manifest and your father's debt in its belly, which is not the same thing."

She laughed at that — the short, unguarded laugh she saved for when no one respectable was listening, the laugh that made two fishermen by the fish-house turn their heads. "No," she agreed. "It isn't the same thing." Then, quieter: "It's the same thing enough."

That was the trouble with Maren. She never lied to soften anything. She had been the only person in Saltmere who talked to Ansel as though his silences were choices rather than defects — as though he might, on some occasion, elect to speak, and she would be there to hear it. Everyone else had learned to read his stillness and fill it with their own assumptions. Maren treated it like deep water: worth respect, worth sounding, occasionally worth diving.

They had grown up sideways into each other, the pearl diver's indentured son and the merchant's daughter, meeting where neither of their worlds had jurisdiction — under the breakwater, out past the drying nets, in the drowned hour between tides when the harbor belonged to gulls and children. She had taught him letters from her father's discarded invoices, chalked on the seawall. He had taught her which rocks held crabs and how to hold her breath until the world went soft at the edges. Between them they had built a vocabulary of the harbor that belonged to no ledger: names for the five kinds of fog, for the exact color the water turned before a storm, for the feeling of swimming through a school of sprats so dense it was like flying through rain.

Now she was leaving, and the vocabulary would belong to him alone, which was a kind of drowning.

* * *

The packet sailed on the dawn tide, which meant the last night belonged to them whether anyone approved or not.

Her father had gone to bed early, worn out by goodbyes and brandy. Her mother sat up packing linens with red eyes. And Maren had appeared at the foot of the divers' stairs just after dark, hood up, hands jammed in her pockets, saying only, "Well?" as if Ansel were the one who had kept her waiting.

They walked the length of the quay first, past the taverns where the evening noise spilled out warm and golden, past the Meridian counting house with its shuttered windows and its brass plate catching the lamplight — *MERIDIAN TRADING COMPANY, CHARTERED* — past everything in Saltmere that had already decided both their futures without asking either of them. She walked it slowly, touching things. The iron mooring ring her grandfather had cast. The seam of the wall where she'd chalked the alphabet at eight years old. She touched them the way you touch a sleeping animal you're about to leave behind.

Then out along the breakwater arm, to the place where the stones gave onto the low ledge above the tide pool they had called, in their private geography, the Deep Window — because on clear nights you could look straight down through twelve feet of green water to the sand below, and the sand looked up at you like a face.

She sat with her knees drawn up. He sat beside her. For a long time neither spoke, and the silence between them was not the careful kind he wore like armor everywhere else in town. It was the other kind, the rare kind — the silence of two people who had run out of need for words and were resting in what remained.

"The water's warm tonight," he said at last, because someone had to say something and the sea was safer than either of them.

"It's always warm to you. You're part seal."

"Your father says I'll be a company man yet. Another ten years on the debt and I'll be diving pearls in a Meridian collar with my name stamped on the back."

"Your father dove pearls too."

"My father dove pearls and the sea took the payment." He heard his own voice go flat and stopped himself. That was the discipline: grief spoken plainly, once, then put away. His mother had taught him that, or the years had. "Sorry."

"Don't apologize for him." She turned to look at him fully, and even in the half-dark her eyes had that quality they always had — direct, unsparing, lit from somewhere internal. "Ansel. Don't ever apologize for missing him. Everyone in this town apologizes for feeling things. It's how the company likes them. Quiet men make cheap labor."

"And loud ones?"

"Loud ones get bought. Like Papa." She looked away, out at the harbor lights doubled in the black water. "We're all cargo here. Some of us just travel first class."

The tide was coming in below them, filling the Deep Window, and the first stars had come out over the headland. Somewhere behind them a tavern fiddler was mangling an old reel, and the sound carried thin across the water like something trying to remember itself.

"I wrote to you," she said suddenly. "While we were loading. Three letters. They're terrible. You can't read them."

"I read everything you write."

"You'll regret that." But she pulled them from inside her coat anyway — three sheets folded small and soft from handling, and he saw that she had carried them against her body all day, and something in his chest turned over slowly, like a stone shifting in a current.

He didn't open them then. He put them inside his shirt, next to his skin, where he could feel the weight of them, and she watched him do it and nodded once, satisfied, as if a transaction more binding than any contract had just closed.

* * *

What happened after that, he would spend six years turning over, examining from every angle, holding up to different lights. Not because he regretted it. Because he needed to know exactly what had been promised, and promises made in the dark by nineteen-year-olds are drafted in a language neither party fully speaks.

She had taken his hand. That much was simple fact. She had taken his hand and laced her fingers through his, palm to palm, and said, "I want to tell you something and I want you to hold still while I say it, because if you do your diver thing and go quiet I'll lose my nerve."

He held still.

"I'm not going to be a clerk's daughter across the water," she said. "Papa thinks I'll keep his household and marry some junior factor with damp hands. Mama thinks I'll write homesick letters for a year and then settle. They're both wrong. I'm going to learn how their ledgers work — actually work, the real entries, not the ones they show the harbormaster. And then I'm going to come back and buy back Papa's ships, or take them, whichever costs less. And when I do—" Here her voice caught, just slightly, the first crack in all that certainty, and he felt it in his own chest like a hairline fracture in glass. "When I do, I'll need one man in Saltmere I don't have to explain myself to. One pair of eyes that knows this harbor better than the company does. One person whose silence is worth something."

"Maren—"

"Let me finish. I've rehearsed this. I've rehearsed it eleven times and I always lose my nerve at this exact spot." She laughed shakily. "This is the spot. This is where I say it."

The tide filled the Deep Window below them. A night bird called once off the headland. He waited, and the waiting felt like the moment at the top of a dive, lungs full, the whole blue weight of the world about to close over him.

"If I send for you," she said, "you come. Whatever the letter says, however strange it sounds, whatever it costs — you come. And I swear to you, Ansel Eastwick, on the water and on your father's memory and on every crab we ever caught out of that pool — I will send. However long it takes. However far they move me. If I am alive, I will find a way to put a letter in your hand, and if you get it, you come."

"And if you don't send?"

"Then I'm dead," she said simply, "and you should live your life."

He wanted to argue with that. He wanted to tell her that living his life had never once been on offer, that the arithmetic of his existence had been settled when his mother pressed her thumbprint into the indenture paper — twelve years of his breath sold by the pound to the Meridian Trading Company. He wanted to tell her that a promise like hers was a luxury item, imported, fragile, and that boys with debts couldn't afford to keep faith with girls who talked about taking ships back.

Instead he said: "I'll come."

And then, because it was true and because the dark allowed it and because he was nineteen and the sea was listening: "Maren. Wherever they take you — whatever they make you — come back to me. Not for the ships. For me. Come back and I'll meet you here, at this pool, at any tide you name, and I'll be early."

She kissed him then, salt-stung and fierce and brief, and pulled back before it could become something that would make leaving impossible, because Maren Voss understood economics even at nineteen, understood that some expenditures had to be deferred.

"There," she said, voice unsteady. "Now it's a contract. Witnessed." She nodded at the sea, at the sky, at the black shoulder of the breakwater. "Everything important gets witnessed by water in this town. It's the only thing here that keeps its word."

They stayed until the moon stood high and the tide began to fall, talking about nothing — the fiddler's terrible reel, the new customs officer's ridiculous hat, whether seals dreamed. Ordinary words, spent freely, the currency of people who believed they had years of them ahead.

At the stairs below her lodging house she stopped and gripped his hand hard, once, like a sailor testing a knot.

"One year," she said. "Give me one year to get settled and learn the ground. Then watch the mail packet. Every week. Promise me you'll watch for it."

"I promise."

"And Ansel—" She hesitated on the step, half-turned, the lamplight from the street finding her face. "Whatever you hear about me afterward. However the letters change. However strange I sound. Read the handwriting first. My hand is mine. No one else writes like me — Papa used to say I wrote like a spider falling downstairs. If the hand is mine, then I'm alive, whatever the words say." She smiled, quick and crooked. "Remember that. The hand before the words."

Then she was gone up the stairs, and the door closed, and Ansel Eastwick stood alone in the street with three letters warming against his chest and the taste of salt on his mouth, watching the windows of her house go dark one by one like a fleet putting out to sea.

* * *

The packet sailed at dawn. He was on the quay for it, of course. So was half of Saltmere, because departures are theater and the town never missed a performance. The Voss family stood at the rail looking smaller than they had on land, the way people always did when the water got between them and you. Maren found him in the crowd — he knew the moment she did, because she went very still, and then she raised one hand, not waving, just open, palm out, the way you show an empty hand to prove you carry nothing but what you mean.

He raised his. Palm to palm across forty yards of harbor water and six years of distance that neither of them could measure yet.

The packet cleared the breakwater. The sails filled. And Saltmere turned back to its fish and its ledgers and its careful silences, and Ansel went down the divers' stairs into the cold green morning and worked until his hands were numb, because that was the only prayer he knew.

For the first year he watched the mail packet come in every week, standing at the quay with his heart doing something foolish in his chest. Nothing came. The second year he watched less often but no less hard. In the third year, word came east with a returning factor — the Voss ship lost with all hands off the Correnne shoals, a storm, a tragedy, a paragraph in the shipping news — and Ansel read the notice nailed to the harbor board four times while the words refused to arrange themselves into meaning, and then he walked out to the breakwater and sat above the Deep Window until dark, and the tide came in and went out and did not care, and the water below him kept its blank, patient, witnessless face.

Drowned, they said. Lost with all hands. The company sent flowers. The company sent flowers and a condolence note in beautiful copperplate and closed the file.

He had believed it. That was the wound under all the others — not that the sea had taken her, but that he had believed it, that he had stood at her parents' empty window and mourned her honestly, completely, for years, had let the promise dissolve into grief the way salt dissolves into water, harmless, invisible, gone. He had told himself that a dead girl's contract expired with her, that the hand before the words had gone into the same gray water as the words themselves.

Six years of believing it. Six years of the tide coming in and going out over the Deep Window, indifferent, keeping the only kind of faith the sea knows — which is none at all.

* * *

He woke in his room above Hetta's kitchen with the letter from Corliss still folded against his ribs and the dream still on him like wet cloth — her hand rising palm-out over the rail, the fiddler's broken reel, her voice saying *read the handwriting first*.

He lay still in the gray light before dawn and listened to the harbor waking below: the first carts, the gulls, the creak of the drying nets, all the ordinary machinery of a town that had buried a living girl and moved on.

*My hand is mine. No one else writes like me.*

He drew the letter out and unfolded it again in the half-dark, and held it to the window where the first light was coming up gray-green over the water, the exact color of the Deep Window at slack tide.

The script slanted hard to starboard, letters tumbling after one another like a spider falling downstairs.

Alive. Whatever the words said. Alive.

And underneath the shock, underneath the joy that hit him like cold water and the fear that came right behind it, one thought rose with terrible clarity, the way truth rises from depth — slow, unstoppable, bending toward the light:

She had kept her side of the contract. Which meant the tide had come due on his.

He dressed in the dark, pocketed the letter, and went down to the harbor to find Corliss — and to find out what, after six years, the words finally said.
