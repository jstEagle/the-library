## 6. Enemy of the Crown

The notices went up before the fish were landed.

Ansel saw them first from the water, treading in the gray chop off the oyster beds, waiting his turn on the diving line. The harbor master's men moved along the quay with a hammer and a fistful of paper, nailing sheets to every post where notices usually announced tide tables or the price of salt. Paper was expensive. Meridian did not spend paper on tides.

He dove anyway. He had learned long ago that a thing seen early and said nothing about was a thing that stayed small. He worked the bottom with cold hands, thumbs finding shell by shape, and let the harbor above him do its business unwatched. When he surfaced, Tam Ossler was already on the quay reading, lips moving.

That alone told Ansel it was serious. Tam could not read.

By the time Ansel came up the ladder with his basket, a crowd had gathered at the customs post, thick and quiet in the way Saltmere crowds were never quiet — no banter, no wagers, just men and women staring at a single sheet as if it might change under their eyes.

He shouldered through to the front.

HARBOUR NOTICE — BY ORDER OF THE MERIDIAN TRADING COMPANY, CHARTERED AGENT OF THE CROWN

BE IT KNOWN that one MAREN VOSS, of this port, presumed lost at sea aboard the *Gilded Ann* in the year past, is hereby declared ALIVE and ABSENT, and further is declared an AGENT OF FOREIGN POWERS acting in contravention of the Company's lawful charter and the interests of the realm.

She is NOT to be approached. She is NOT to be harbored. Any person receiving intelligence of her whereabouts is bound by law and contract to report the same to the Company's harbor office WITHOUT DELAY.

REWARD: FORTY POUNDS STERLING for information leading to her apprehension. Ten pounds for information concerning any person aiding her, abetting her, or concealing knowledge of her.

By order of DIRECTOR HALE.

Below, in smaller print, as if embarrassed: *God save the King.*

Forty pounds. Ansel's indenture — twelve years of his lungs, his hands, his father's grave — had been valued at sixty. A woman's life was worth two-thirds of a diver, and they'd priced the informing cheap enough that half the town could afford to be tempted.

He read it three times. Not because he failed to understand it the first time. Because *Maren Voss* in official ink looked nothing like Maren Voss in memory, and he needed to see how badly the words would fail.

*Presumed lost.* Alive. *Agent of foreign powers.*

The crowd around him breathed. Somewhere behind him a woman whispered, "Voss's girl? Little Maren?" and someone shushed her hard, the way you'd shush a shout near ice.

Corliss stood at the edge of the gathering, hat down, hands folded, watching the notice with the mild interest of a man reading shipping news. He caught Ansel's eye for exactly one heartbeat. Then he turned and strolled toward the packet office, unhurried, a stranger with nowhere urgent to be.

Ansel carried his basket to the company scale with legs that felt borrowed.

* * *

The weighing shed smelled of brine and the tallow candles Meridian burned even in daylight, because clerks who couldn't see each other's faces couldn't compare notes. Pell, the tally clerk, sat behind his grille with the harvest ledger open, thin fingers poised.

"Light day," Pell said.

"Tide's wrong," Ansel said.

"The tide," Pell said, "is always wrong when the quota isn't met." He wrote something. Ansel watched the pen add what would become interest, compounding quietly against a debt that had been compounding since he was eleven years old. He knew better than to argue arithmetic with Meridian. Arithmetic was their religion, and Pell was one of its minor priests.

But today Ansel heard himself speak before he had decided to. "Did you post those notices?"

Pell's pen stopped. He looked up, and there was something in his face Ansel had never seen there before — not suspicion, not boredom. Reluctance. "Company business."

"Six years." Ansel kept his voice flat, the voice he used for weather talk. "Six years she's dead, and now she's an enemy agent. Which is it?"

"It is whatever the ledger says it is." Pell closed the ledger with soft finality. "And you, Eastwick, are in the wrong column to be asking."

"What column is that?"

"The obedient one." Pell's eyes flicked to the door, then back. His voice dropped half a tone, so slightly that a man who hadn't spent twelve years learning silences would have missed it entirely. "There's ten pounds in it for knowing things, boy. And forty more for the woman. There is nothing in it for asking me questions. Do you understand the difference?"

Ansel understood. It was a warning wearing the clothes of a threat, or possibly the other way around, delivered by the last man in Saltmere he'd have expected to deliver it. Pell, who once docked him two shillings for a miscounted oyster.

"I understand," Ansel said.

"Good." Pell reopened the ledger. "Your basket. Scale. Go home."

Walking back through the town, Ansel counted the notices. Seven of them. Customs post, packet office, chandlery, tavern door, chapel yard wall, harbor steps, and — this was the masterstroke, and he hated Hale for it — the boarding house where the divers took their meals, nailed at exact eye height across from the bench where the men ate. Forty pounds hung over every bowl of chowder in Saltmere.

At the tavern door, Brann the cooper was arguing loudly with no one in particular that the whole business was scandalous, that poor Voss, rest him, must be spinning in his grave, that a girl driven to foreign powers could only have been driven there. It was a performance, and it was flawless, and Ansel noticed that Brann performed it while casually shifting his body to block the view of the notice behind him — because someone had written something small in charcoal at its bottom corner. Ansel got close enough to read it as he passed.

*A lie floats. Truth dives.*

He kept walking. But the words went down into him like a dropped stone, sinking past fear, all the way to some hard bottom he hadn't known was there.

* * *

Hetta had lit the fire before he arrived, which meant she had news, which meant she wanted him fed before he had opinions. Twelve years of landlady logic. She set a plate of smoked fish and bread in front of him without ceremony and sat opposite with her mending.

"You've seen them," she said. Not a question.

"Notices."

"Seven." Her needle didn't pause. "I counted too."

"Hetta—"

"Eat first."

He ate. She let him. Outside, the wind had come up from the northeast, dragging a smell of old ice across the harbor, and the window rattled once in its frame like something impatient.

"They posted one at the chapel," Hetta said finally. "Reverend Aldous let them nail it to God's own wall, and then he preached Sunday on render unto Caesar, which everyone understood to mean shut your mouth. Mrs. Corvey has already been to the harbor office twice. She won't say why."

"Mrs. Corvey sold us out to the press gang in '19."

"Mrs. Corvey would sell her own teeth." Hetta bit thread. "My point is not Mrs. Corvey. My point is—" she set down the mending, and her hands, which never stilled, went flat on the table, "—that I knew your mother, Ansel Eastwick, and your mother made one bargain in her life, and it was with the devil's counting house, and she wept doing it. And I know you. You sat across that table from Pell today and asked him questions with your face. Your face, boy, is the worst-kept ledger in this harbor."

Ansel put down his fork. "What did my face say?"

"It said *she's alive*." Hetta held his gaze. "It said it in front of forty people, and two of them can read, and one of them is Mrs. Corvey."

The fire popped. Down the street, a dog began to bark at nothing, or at everything.

"If they think you know something," Hetta went on, quieter, "they won't arrest you. Arrest makes questions. They'll do what they did to Jem Tarrow — call in his debt early, every shilling, payable in fourteen days, and let arithmetic do the drowning. You've got a year left, Ansel. One year of obedience between you and your own name. Don't spend it on a face."

"How do you know about the year?"

"I know everything in this house's walls." She picked the mending back up, and just like that she was only his landlady again, needle flashing, eyes down. "Whatever it is you're carrying, and I'm not saying you're carrying anything, you'll want to decide soon whether it's heavier than your freedom. That's all. Eat your fish."

But upstairs, in his narrow room with its one window on the harbor, Ansel could not eat peace. He stood at the glass and looked down at the lights of the counting house burning late — Hale's windows, always lit, the company watching the town the way the lighthouse watched the shoals.

*Agent of foreign powers.*

He laid his forearm against the cool sill and thought about what the phrase actually meant. It meant Maren had chosen a side. Against Meridian. Against the charter, the ledgers, the arithmetic — against the machine that had priced his father's drowning as a deductible loss and her supposed death as an insurance settlement. Whatever flag she served now, she had gone to war against the thing that owned him.

Which made her, technically, an enemy of everything Ansel belonged to.

Which made helping her treason.

He tested the word the way he'd test a bad shell — turning it, listening to its weight. *Treason.* The crown's word. Meridian's word, printed under crown authority, chartered, sealed, legitimate. Everything legal was on their side: the notices, the reward, the debt, the gallows waiting at the end of a rope of perfectly lawful hemp.

And yet.

*A lie floats. Truth dives.*

His mother had taught him to swim in this harbor. His father had died in it. Maren had stood on the breakwater in the last summer of peacetime and made him promise, and the promise had no flag on it, no seal, no clause about foreign powers. It had only her hand in his and the sound of the water underneath.

Fear was in him — real fear, cold as the deep water, the fear of Jem Tarrow's fourteen days and of ropes and of Mrs. Corvey's honest, greedy eyes. He let himself feel all of it, the whole freezing weight of it.

And beneath the fear, steady as ballast, something else rose: defiance, quiet and total, the same stillness he found at forty feet down when the light went gray and only his own will kept him working. They could post a hundred notices. They could price his silence, weigh his loyalty, tax his hope with interest compounded quarterly.

They could not tell him what was true. He had been under the water. He knew how to find what sank.

A lantern blinked out in the harbor — once, twice — and Ansel's breath stopped, watching. Only a fishing boat coming in late, swinging its lamp on the turn. Nothing. Not yet.

But tomorrow, he knew, he would go back to the counting house. He would climb to the archive loft, and he would open the file on the *Gilded Ann*, and he would learn what Meridian was so desperate to bury under forty pounds of other people's greed.

The decision made itself with the ease of a body deciding to sink or rise — one breath, and gravity takes its side.

He slept badly, and dreamed of slanted handwriting.
