## 7. The Counting House

The Meridian Trading Company kept its ledgers the way the sea kept its drowned — locked away, catalogued, and never spoken of above a certain depth.

Ansel Eastwick had been in the counting house perhaps two hundred times in twelve years, and for two hundred times he had looked at nothing he was not meant to see. That was the first rule of debt: you did not stare at the arithmetic of your own captivity. You signed where the clerk's finger rested, you counted your quota aloud when asked, and you kept your eyes on the floorboards, which were scrubbed pale as bone and told no stories.

Tonight, for the first time, he looked up.

The counting house sat on stilts above the high-tide line, its timbers groaning with each swell that worked beneath the pilings like an old man turning in bed. Ansel had come after hours because the company wanted him after hours — Hale's summons had been specific, delivered by a junior clerk with ink-stained cuffs and a habit of not meeting anyone's eyes. *The divers' quarterly tallies require verification. Eastwick will attend.* It was routine work, the kind of chore given to men whose hands could read numbers even if their schooling could barely read letters. Ansel had checked figures for Meridian since he was fourteen, hunched over this same table while clerks drifted home to their suppers.

But tonight there was a stranger's voice still ringing in his skull, and a letter folded in his memory that would not stay folded.

*Corliss,* the man had called himself. Corliss with his clean boots and his questions that everyone in Saltmere knew better than to answer. Corliss, who had stood at the quay that morning asking after ships lost six years ago, and who — when the harbor crowd had closed around him like water closing over a stone — had turned and found Ansel watching from the loft window of the counting house, and had held his gaze one beat too long.

Ansel should have gone home. He knew every version of the smart thing to do, had spent twelve years learning which silences keep people safe, and the smartest silence of all was the one you kept about yourself.

Instead he had come to the counting house at dusk, and now it was full dark, and he was alone with the files.

* * *

The room smelled of tallow smoke, salt rot, and the particular staleness of paper that has been handled by too many careful hands. A single lamp burned at the tally table, its flame bending whenever the wind found a gap in the clapboard. Beyond the lamplight the shelves went black — row upon row of pigeonholes, each labeled in the clerk's copperplate: *Manifests 611–618. Wages & Bonds. Charter Correspondence. Insurance, Foreign.*

That last shelf had never interested him before.

He told himself, as he dipped his pen and began checking the third quarter's dive weights against the ledger, that he was here for the work and nothing else. He told himself that the letter Corliss carried meant nothing to him, that Maren Voss was six years drowned, that a slanted hand on old paper proved nothing except that grief made forgers rich. He wrote his neat column of figures. He blew on the ink. He listened to the sea knocking under the floor like something asking politely to be let in.

Then, without deciding to, he set down the pen.

It is a strange thing, the moment before a first transgression. Later — if there was a later, if he lived to have later moments — Ansel would try to remember whether his heart had hammered or gone quiet, and he would find he could not say, because in truth it had done neither. It had simply stopped being his heart for a few seconds, become instead some borrowed organ belonging to a man he did not yet know. His body rose from the chair. His feet crossed the boards. And his eyes went to the shelf labels the way a diver's eyes go to the shape rising out of blue water: *that is not supposed to be there, and yet it is, and I am looking at it.*

*Insurance, Foreign. Insurance, Coastal.*

The Voss ship had been coastal. The *Lark's Mercy*, they had called her — a pretty name for a tramp coaster out of Saltmere harbor, the ship Maren had boarded with her father the spring the pearl beds soured, bound south for the Meridian depot at Callow's Reach. She had gone down off the Teeth with all but three hands lost. The three who lived had been paid off and scattered within the month; that much Ansel remembered, because even at sixteen he had noticed how quickly the company bought silence, and had filed the noticing somewhere deep where it could not hurt anyone.

Maren had been on the passenger manifest. Sixteen years old, listed under her father's booking. Drowned, said the notice in the harbor gazette. Drowned, said the priest at the memorial service, over a coffin filled with ballast stones because the sea gives nothing back.

Drowned, said everyone, for six years — until a stranger walked the quay carrying letters in a hand Ansel would know blindfolded, in the dark, at any depth.

His fingers hovered over the pigeonhole marked *Wrecks & Losses, 609–612.*

The lock was a simple thing, brass-faced, older than Ansel himself. He knew the shape of these locks because he had watched clerks work them ten thousand times, and he knew — because a boy who grows up poor learns everything about everything whether anyone means him to or not — that the key for the general files hung on the board behind the head clerk's desk, fourth hook, unlabeled, because no one bothered stealing from the past.

The key was warm. That detail would stay with him: the key should have been cold, midnight-cold, and instead it lay against his palm almost like skin, as though the whole room were alive and complicit.

He turned the lock. It gave with a click so small the sea swallowed it whole.

* * *

Inside the pigeonhole: a dozen folders, tied with red tape gone pink with age. He did not need light to find the right one. His hands found it the way hands find things the mind refuses to lead them to — by trembling, by eagerness, by the terrible animal intelligence of want.

*LARK'S MERCY — LOSS REPORT & CLAIMS.*

He untied the tape. The knot fought him, then surrendered, and the folder opened like something exhaling.

The first pages were dull as prayer. Surveyor's report. Position of the wreck, estimated. Weather. Cargo manifest — salted fish, timber, tin, and forty crates of Meridian goods insured through the Callow's Reach office at a valuation that made Ansel's breath catch. He read the number twice. Then a third time. He was a diver; he knew what things were worth at the bottom of things. Forty crates of that description, at that valuation, was double what the *Lark's Mercy* could have carried even stuffed to her rails.

Unless the cargo existed only on paper.

He turned the page and the lamp guttered, and for one black instant he saw nothing at all and felt the whole future tilt beneath him — *burn the folder, tie the tape, go home, be nobody, live* — and then the flame steadied and he read the next line and there was no going back anywhere, ever again.

*A note, initialed H.H., recommending settlement of claim in full and without survey, citing "the inconvenience and expense of raising."*

H.H. Hollis Hale. Director of the Saltmere station. The man who signed Ansel's wage dockets. The man whose voice from the counting house stairs could make grown divers study their boots.

Without survey. Ansel knew enough of shipping law to know what those four words meant. Ships that sank were surveyed. Cargo claims of this size were contested for years, litigated down to the last bolt of cloth. But the *Lark's Mercy* had been settled in eleven weeks, in full, without a diver ever going down to confirm she existed at the bottom of the Teeth — which was strange, because divers were cheap in Saltmere, cheaper than honesty, and Ansel himself had been sent down on murkier errands.

And beneath the settlement note, clipped with a brass fastener gone green, a copy of the insurance policy itself. Issued eight days before the *Lark's Mercy* sailed.

Eight days.

A ship sinks with double her cargo value aboard, on paper. Her owners insure her fat and fresh a week before departure. The claim is paid without question. The survivors are scattered. The dead are mourned with ballast stones. And a girl goes into the water —

— and comes out somewhere, six years later, writing letters that make the Crown call her an enemy agent.

The folder shook in his hands. Not fear, or not only fear. It was the shaking of a structure giving way — the whole careful architecture of his twelve years, built on the foundation that the sea took what the sea took, that drowning was weather and bad luck and God's indifference, that Meridian was a hard master but a lawful one. He had hated the debt. He had never once suspected the wreck.

Somewhere below the floorboards the tide turned, and the pilings sang a low new note, and Ansel Eastwick stood alone in the counting house holding proof that the company had murdered a ship full of people for money, and understood with perfect clarity that he could never again be the man who had walked in through that door.

* * *

He heard the step on the stair with perhaps four seconds to act.

Four seconds is nothing ashore. Four seconds underwater is a career. Ansel's hands moved with the speed the sea had taught them: folder closed, tape knotted — wrong, the knot was wrong, leave it — shoved home into the pigeonhole, door shut, lock turned, key back on the fourth hook as the third stair from the top creaked its familiar creak. He crossed to the tally table in two strides, sat, took up his pen, and bent his head over the quarterly weights as the door opened.

Hollis Hale stood in the doorway with a storm lantern raised, and the light came across the room and lay against Ansel's face like a hand testing for fever.

"Eastwick." Hale's voice was mild. Mildness was worse than anger; everyone in Saltmere knew that. "Working late."

"The quarterly weights, sir. The clerk said—"

"I know what the clerk said." Hale came in slowly. He was a broad man gone soft at the edges, with the pallor of someone who had spent thirty years indoors making money from other people's lungs. He set the lantern on the tally table, and its light swung across the shelves behind Ansel, and Ansel felt the swing of it like a blade passing near his neck — across *Wages & Bonds*, across *Charter Correspondence*, across *Insurance, Coastal* — and stopped.

For a long moment Hale looked at the shelves. Then he looked at Ansel, who looked back with twelve years of practiced blankness, and inside that blankness his heart was doing something he had never allowed it to do in the water: screaming.

"You've finished the third column," Hale observed.

"Two lines short of finished, sir."

"Hm." Hale picked up the ledger, read Ansel's figures upside down with the ease of a man who had spent his life reading other men's debts, and set it down. "Your arithmetic is good, Eastwick. It always has been. It's one of the reasons your file interests me." He smiled without warmth, the way a lock smiles. "A year left on your indenture. A man with good arithmetic and a year left can afford to think about the future. Don't spend the thinking time badly."

"No, sir."

"Lock up when you're done. The key stays under the loose brick." Hale lifted the lantern and went, and the stairs took his weight one groan at a time, and the night outside swallowed the sound of his boots on the shell road.

Ansel sat without moving until the tide under the floor had knocked twice more. Then he looked at the third column, at the last figure his pen had written before his feet had carried him across the room — a dive weight, ordinary, innocent — and beside it, unbidden, his hand wrote one more mark. A small slanting stroke, half a letter, nothing. Except that it leaned the way certain handwriting leaned, a way he had first learned tracing letters in harbor sand twenty years ago, a way no living person in Saltmere wrote anymore.

He had touched the file. He had read the file. He had lied with his stillness to Hollis Hale's face and been believed, or not been believed, and had no way of knowing which.

The lamp burned low. Out past the breakwater, far out where the deep water started, a light appeared — a lantern rising, hanging, sinking. Rising. Hanging. Sinking. Too regular for a fishing boat. Too patient for the coast guard.

Ansel put out his own lamp and stood in the dark with his heart going like a diver's hammering ascent, thrilling and terrible, knowing already — the way you know the pull of a current before it takes you — that tomorrow he would have to answer Corliss after all.

The light out on the water sank one last time, and did not rise.
