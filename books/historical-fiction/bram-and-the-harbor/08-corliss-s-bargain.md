## 8. Corliss's Bargain

The rain came in off the water an hour before dusk, thin and needling, the kind that didn't fall so much as hang in the air and wait for a man to walk through it. Ansel crossed the quay with his collar up and his head down, and that was why he nearly collided with Corliss, who was standing in the lee of the fish-packing shed as though he had grown there, patient as a post.

"You walk like a man who knows where he's going," Corliss said. "Even in the rain."

"Everyone in Saltmere walks like that. It's a small town. There's nowhere else to go."

"May I walk with you a moment?"

Ansel looked at him. The stranger's coat was good wool gone soft at the elbows, his boots salt-stained, his face the kind that made a lie of itself — pleasant, forgettable, a face built for asking directions. But his eyes didn't forget. They were doing the work of a ledger, Ansel thought. Adding him up.

"You may walk," Ansel said. "I can't promise conversation."

They went along the seawall, past the drying racks where the herring hung like silver coins on strings, past the chandlery where old Piet was shuttering his windows against the weather. The lamps were being lit one by one down the harbor road, each flame a small yellow struggle against the gray. Somewhere behind them, the mail packet's crew sang something tuneless and foreign as they loaded for the morning tide.

"You recognized the hand," Corliss said. Not a question.

Ansel kept walking. His boots found the cobbles by habit; his mind had gone somewhere cold and high, like the moment after a dive when the water closes over your head and the whole world becomes pressure and silence.

"I asked you a question, Mr. Eastwick."

"You asked nothing. You made a statement. There's a difference, and the difference matters in a town like this." Ansel stopped at the corner where the seawall met the harbor road, where the lamplight reached and no farther. "I don't know what you think you saw in my face. I've spent twelve years learning to keep my face. If something slipped, it slipped because you caught me tired, and I'll not be caught tired twice."

Corliss smiled. It was a small smile, and it did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "Twelve years," he said. "That's a long apprenticeship in silence."

"It's the only trade Saltmere teaches."

"And yet." Corliss drew something from inside his coat — not the letter this time, Ansel noted, not the one with the slanted script that had turned his stomach to cold iron in the counting house loft. This was a folded paper, plain, sealed with nothing but a crease. He held it out. "And yet you looked at that handwriting the way a man looks at a face he buried."

The rain ticked against the paper between them. Ansel did not take it.

"I buried no one," he said.

"No. That's rather the point, isn't it?" Corliss's voice dropped, not to a whisper — a whisper would have been an admission — but to something level and quiet, the voice of a man stating the price of fish. "Maren Voss is alive, Mr. Eastwick. She has been alive for six years. And she has spent those six years gathering proof of what Meridian Trading did to the *Cormorant's Pride*, and to her father, and to the forty-one souls the company wrote off as weather."

The name hit him like cold water in the ears. Ansel had not heard it spoken aloud in six years. In Saltmere, the drowned were spoken of in the past tense for a season, then not at all; grief here was a tide that went out and did not come back. He had learned to say *the Voss girl* only in the safety of his own skull, and even there the words had worn smooth, like a stone turned over so many times it lost its edges.

"You're mad," he said. "Or you're testing me. Either way—"

"I'm neither. I'm a courier. It's a dull trade, mostly. You carry paper and you say nothing and you are paid to be forgettable." Corliss tucked the paper away again, unhurried. "But this run is different. This run needs someone on the inside. Someone Meridian trusts. Someone who can stand in the counting house and look at a ledger and know what he's looking at."

"I dive for pearls. I don't read ledgers."

"You stood in the loft above the clerk's room for the better part of an hour last week. You looked at the wreck file for the *Pride* until Tam the junior clerk noticed you looking. You think no one sees you, Mr. Eastwick, because you've been invisible so long you've started to believe it yourself. But the company counts everything. It's the only prayer they know." Corliss tilted his head. "How much do you owe them?"

The question was obscene in its plainness. Ansel felt it land in the place where the debt lived — that number he carried the way other men carried a bad tooth, always present, always aching when the weather changed. Twelve years of interest on his mother's bargain. One year left, if he was careful. If he was silent. If he was nothing.

"That's my business."

"It's the company's business, which makes it mine." Corliss stepped closer, out from under the shed's lee, letting the rain hit him as though he didn't notice weather anymore. "Here is the bargain, and then I'll leave you to your silence, which I can see you love like a wife. Maren needs the original file. Not a copy — the clerk's copies are doctored, the originals are not, because Hale is a thief but he is a lazy one. She needs it carried out of that building and into certain hands. And she has asked —" and here, for the first time, something moved in the courier's face, something almost like pity — "she has asked whether the diver's son would help. Those were her words. *The diver's son.* She remembers you."

The harbor road stretched away in both directions, lamplit and empty. Ansel became aware of his own breathing, the way a diver becomes aware of it when the air runs short.

"She's dead," he said. "The company says she's dead. The Crown says she's an enemy agent. Both of those things can be true, and neither of them makes her a friend of mine."

"No," Corliss agreed. "But you made her a promise once. Under the breakwater, the night before the *Pride* sailed. I know because she told me, and she is not a woman who spends memories where they aren't true." He let that sit in the rain between them. "I don't need your answer tonight. I need it by the morning packet, because after that I'll be gone, and the offer goes with me, and it will not come again. Think of it however you like. Think of the debt. Think of the file. Think of forty-one people the company drowned for an insurance settlement and called it an act of God." He turned up his collar. "Or think of a girl who kept your name for six years in a place where keeping names is how people die."

"I'll think of nothing," Ansel said. "I'll dive tomorrow, and the day after, and in one year my debt is paid, and I will walk out of Saltmere with clean hands. That is the whole of my ambition, and it is not small to me. It is everything I have."

"Yes," Corliss said. "That's exactly what I told her you'd say." He nodded once, courteous as a clerk, and walked away down the harbor road, and the lamplight took him and gave him back and took him again until the dark had all of him.

Ansel stood a long time in the rain.

* * *

Hetta had left a lamp burning in the hall and a plate covered with a cloth on the kitchen table — salt fish and bread and a smear of butter going rancid at the edges. The house smelled of peat smoke and the lavender she hung against moths, and the rain worked at the shutters with a patience like a debt collector's.

Ansel ate without tasting. He washed the plate. He climbed the narrow stair to his room under the eaves, where the ceiling sloped so low he could touch it lying down, and where the window gave a slice of the harbor — the masts, the riding lights, the black shoulder of the breakwater beyond.

He lay down in his clothes. He did not sleep.

The trouble with silence, he thought, staring at the beams, was that it was never empty. Twelve years of it had taught him that. Silence was a hold full of cargo, and everything he had ever refused to say was stacked in the dark down there, and tonight the hatch had come open.

*The diver's son.* She remembers you.

He had been seventeen the last time he saw her, all elbows and salt-scars, and she had been seventeen too and certain of everything, the way only the daughters of shipwrights are certain. Under the breakwater, with the *Cormorant's Pride* riding at anchor and her father's voice carrying down the quay — *Maren! Girl, we sail on the morning tide, and you'll be aboard or you'll swim after* — she had gripped his wrist hard enough to leave marks and said, *Keep something for me. Anything. A stone, a word, I don't care. Keep it and I'll come back for it.*

And he had kept — God help him — he had kept everything. Every year on the anniversary of the wreck he had walked out to the breakwater and stood where the sea had taken her, and said nothing, and kept nothing, and kept everything. It was the one silence he had never managed to make smooth. All the others he had worn like tools. This one had worn him.

*Forty-one souls the company wrote off as weather.*

He had seen the file. He had seen the surveyor's report with its careful, clerkish hand — *hull sound, cargo sound, crew adequate* — dated nine days before the *Pride* sailed into a storm the harbor pilots had warned against, a storm the company's own agent had ordered them into with a hold full of insured timber. He had seen the number. He had seen the number and he had put the file back and walked down the stairs and out into the street and told himself it was not what it looked like, because the alternative was a world in which everything he had given Meridian — his father's drowning, his mother's signature, his own two hands and lungs and the twelve best years of his life — had been poured into the same cup as that.

Rain on the shutters. A riding light swinging at anchor. Somewhere out there, a woman alive who should have been six years drowned.

He turned onto his side. He made himself think of the debt. One year. Three hundred and sixty-odd days of keeping his head down and his mouth shut and his face smooth, and then the gate opened and he walked out free, and freedom was not a small thing, freedom was the whole cathedral of his ambition, and he would not — he would *not* — trade it for a ghost's errand and a stranger's smile.

But the arithmetic would not sit still. Because if the file said what it looked like it said, then Meridian had not merely drowned forty-one people. Meridian had drowned them, and insured them, and mourned them publicly, and raised the harbor's rates to cover the loss, and every coin Ansel had ever earned in their water had been washed in it. His debt was not clean. His debt was the company's debt wearing his name. One more year of obedience was not freedom. It was interest.

He got up. He lit the stub of candle on the sill and looked out at the harbor. The packet rode at her moorings, dark and patient, ready for the morning tide. Corliss would be aboard her, or ashore at the inn, and either way the offer was dying with the night, and it would not come again.

*Keep something for me.*

He had kept it. Six years of it. And now it had come back for him, the way kept things do.

Ansel pressed his forehead against the cold glass and watched the rain crawl down the panes, and somewhere in the small hours the resistance in him did not break — it was too well-built for that, twelve years in the making — it simply went quiet, the way the sea goes quiet before a change of wind, and in that quiet he heard the truth he had been diving to avoid all night: that he had already decided. He had decided in the loft, when he looked at the file too long. He had decided six years ago at the breakwater. Corliss had not come to offer him a bargain. He had come to collect one.

The candle guttered. Down the harbor road, the inn lamps went out one by one.

And Ansel Eastwick, who knew which silences kept people safe, lay back in the dark and understood that tomorrow he would have to walk into the tavern where the whole town's ears were drinking, sit among the men who owed Meridian and the men who feared it, and find Corliss before the morning packet sailed — because he could not let the offer die, and he could not say yes aloud, and the only thing left between those two impossibilities was a room full of listening neighbors and a courier who was leaving on the tide.

He did not sleep. Outside, the rain stopped, and the sea began to speak in its new voice, and it sounded, to a diver's son, exactly like a countdown.
