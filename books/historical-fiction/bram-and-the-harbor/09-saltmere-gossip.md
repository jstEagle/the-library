## 9. Saltmere Gossip

The Anchor & Eel had a way of arranging itself before Ansel ever touched the door. He could stand on the cobbles outside and know the shape of the room from the sound alone — who had come early, who was drunk already, who was drinking quietly enough to mean business. Tonight the sound was wrong in one particular: too many low voices, not enough laughter. A town that talked about the weather and the pearl prices was a town at rest. A town that dropped its voice when the door opened was a town with something to hide, or something it feared would be overheard.

He pushed inside and let the warmth and the lamp-smoke close over him like water.

The packet had come in that morning — the mail packet out of Greyharbor, riding low with autumn freight — and with it the usual small tide of strangers: two cloth merchants, an insurance man whose boots were too fine for Saltmere mud, and Corliss. Ansel found him without looking for him, because he had trained himself years ago to find anyone Meridian might care about. The stranger sat at the corner table nearest the window, half in shadow, nursing a glass of small beer as if he intended to make it last until spring. A traveling man's coat. A traveler's stillness. And beneath both, something patient that did not belong to either.

Ansel took his usual place at the long table by the hearth, where the divers sat, where he had sat since he was fourteen and allowed his first pint. Dennen was there, big-shouldered and slow-spoken, with his burned hand cradled around his cup. Old Pell, who had forty years of bottom-time in his joints and walked now like a ship in a swell. The Kestrel brothers, Tam and Rye, young enough to still laugh about the diving instead of just surviving it. They made room for him the way they always did — a shift of elbows, a nod, no questions. That was the courtesy Saltmere paid its silences.

"Packet came in heavy," Dennen said, to no one and everyone.

"Heavy with letters," said Pell. "Heavy with nothing else. Prices are flat again."

"Flat." Rye Kestrel spat into the fire. "That's the word they use when they've decided what we'll be paid and want us to feel grateful it isn't less."

"Mind your mouth," Dennen said mildly.

"Why? Half the room works for them and the other half owes them. Nobody here is going to carry tales to Hale that Hale doesn't already have written down in a ledger somewhere."

Tam laughed, but it was the thin kind of laugh, and it died fast. Ansel drank and watched the fire and let the talk move past him the way current moves past a rock.

* * *

He had learned listening the way other boys learned knots — from necessity, and early. His father had been a diver, and divers' houses were quiet houses; men who spent their days holding their breath came home wanting very little talk. But his mother, before the bargain, before the company owned everything down to the name on the grave marker, had taught him the other lesson: *people tell you everything, Ansel, if you don't interrupt the telling.* She'd say it while shelling mussels, eyes down, hands moving. She'd been the best-informed woman in Saltmere and nobody had ever thought of her as knowing anything at all.

So Ansel sat among the divers and listened to the town assemble itself, voice by voice, the way a harbor assembles itself out of fog.

At the bar, Joss Ambrose — chandler, churchwarden, a man whose loyalty could be purchased with a favorable mooring fee — was telling the publican, loudly, that the new notices posted at the customs shed were "nothing but routine, routine every year," which was interesting, because Ansel had read those notices at dawn and there was nothing routine in them. *By order of the Charter Court: information respecting enemy agents operating against lawful commerce… rewards offered…* Someone had drawn a crude figure beside the printed lines, a stick-man with a hangman's loop, and someone else had tried to scrub it off and only made it darker.

"Routine," said a voice from the far end of the bar — Marta Quill, who ran the ferry skiff and feared no one living. "If it's routine why is the customs officer been down twice today to count the same three boats?"

Joss's laugh was a hinge that needed oiling. "Marta, you see conspiracy in a change of tide."

"I see paper where there wasn't paper yesterday. Paper means somebody in Greyharlor is frightened, and frightened men with charters are worse weather than any storm I've ferried through."

Ansel turned his cup slowly. *Frightened.* So the notices weren't only theater. Hale had put them up because something had reached Greyharbor — a report, a rumor, a name — that had made the company reach for the oldest tool it owned: a town that policed itself. You didn't need constables in a place where every neighbor was a witness.

He looked, without moving his head, at Corliss. The stranger had not reacted to Marta's words. But he had shifted the smallest degree forward, the way a man leans toward a fire, and Ansel understood that Corliss was doing exactly what Ansel was doing. Two listeners in one room, each pretending to belong to the speaking half of it.

* * *

"Young Eastwick." Pell's voice, close, gravelly. Ansel turned. The old diver was studying him with eyes gone pale as oyster shell. "You've been quiet tonight."

"I'm always quiet, Pell."

"Aye. But you're quiet different tonight. Quiet like a man counting." Pell drank, set the cup down with care. His burned hand trembled, just slightly, on the wood. "Twelve year you've worn that debt. My girl asks after you sometimes. Says a man your age shouldn't look like he's carrying ballast below the waterline."

"The debt ends next year," Ansel said. It was the safe answer, the answer he gave whenever anyone probed, and it was even true — twelve years served, one remaining, the arithmetic posted in the counting house in the clerk's neat copperplate. What the arithmetic didn't say was that indentures had a way of extending themselves: a damaged season, a broken gauge, interest compounding politely in columns he wasn't permitted to audit.

"Ends," Pell repeated, tasting the word. Then, lower, so the Kestrel brothers' chatter covered it: "You know old Havelock? Worked the beds off Crag Point?"

"Had the stroke. Six years back."

"Six years back. And his debt — what happened to his debt when he couldn't dive?"

Ansel said nothing.

"They carried it," Pell said. "Carried it against his house. His widow's scrubbing floors in Greyharbor now so she doesn't lose the roof over her sister's children. That's how debts end at Meridian, boy. Like tides. They go out and they come back and they never once ask permission." He pushed himself up, joints cracking like line under strain. "Watch yourself. Quiet ones get watched longest."

He shuffled toward the bar, and Dennen leaned over to fill the space. "Old fool talks like the company's the devil," he said comfortably. "It's just a company. Pays fair enough, mostly. Keeps the harbor alive. Where'd Saltmere be without it?"

"Drowned," said Tam Kestrel, grinning. "Like everything else it's ever had anything to do with."

The laugh that followed had an edge in it, and Dennen's face closed like a shutter, and Ansel saw — clearly, the way you see the bottom suddenly when the light swings right — that the whole table had stepped around a wreck without naming it. The Voss ship. Six years gone with all hands, and no one at this table had ever once, in six years, spoken her name in his hearing. Not Maren's name. Not her father's. Not the ship's. As if the silence itself had been assigned, entered into a ledger, and everyone in Saltmere had signed.

*Belonging,* Ansel thought, watching Dennen order another round with too much heartiness, *is just surveillance you've agreed to.*

* * *

The evening wore on. Voices rose and fell and mapped the town more precisely than any chart Ansel had ever seen in the counting house.

There was Brann — and Ansel felt his pulse knock once at the name — the smuggler, holding court near the dartboard, telling some inflated story about running brandy past the revenue cutter in fog. Brann laughed loudest at his own lies, and his eyes, while laughing, went around the room like a man checking his anchors. Everyone knew Brann moved things the company didn't list. Everyone also knew that half the constabulary drank his brandy. He was, in his way, part of the harbor's official trade.

There was the customs officer, Fenwick, red-faced at the end of the bar, insisting to anyone trapped nearby that the reward notices were "Greyharbor's business, not mine, I just post what comes down the line." Nobody believed him. Fenwick believed nothing himself except that his pension depended on believing nothing.

There was Widow Crane, who took in washing, whispering to the publican's wife behind the settle — and though Ansel caught none of the words, he caught the direction of the widow's glance twice: once toward the corner table where Corliss sat, once toward the door, as though measuring distances. Word about the stranger was already moving through the town's blood, faster than any packet boat. By morning, every household in Saltmere would know that a soft-spoken traveler had come asking questions about shipping schedules and old wrecks, and had paid for his beer with Greyharbor coin.

And there — arriving late, shaking rain from a good coat that hadn't earned it — was Wick, the junior clerk from the counting house. Nineteen, eager, forever hovering at the edge of adult conversation like a gull waiting for scraps. He took a stool too near Joss Ambrose and began, in the confiding stage-whisper of the young and self-important, to talk.

"—director's own instruction, mind, extra copies of the register this quarter, and Mr. Hale's had the strong-room keyed different, and there's been talk—" Wick paused for effect, savoring his proximity to power, "—talk right up on the second floor, about irregularities. About papers being where they shouldn't, or not where they should."

Ansel's cup stopped halfway to his mouth, and he completed the motion as though it had never hesitated.

"Talk," Joss said indulgently. "Wick, my boy, clerks always think talk in a stairwell is intelligence."

"It's more than talk." Wick lowered his voice further, and the tavern, obligingly, seemed to lower its noise around him — or perhaps Ansel's hearing simply narrowed to a single channel, the way it did underwater, when the world above went distant and one sound became the whole world. "The wreck files. The old ones. Someone's been through them. Pages out of order. And Mr. Hale has asked the registrar for the Voss manifest — the *Voss*, mind — private-like, through channels. Now why would a director six years after the fact want—"

"Wick." Joss's voice had changed. Not loud. Just final, the voice of a man closing a shop. "Some cargo's not worth hauling. Take my advice and leave manifests where they lie."

Wick blinked, wounded, and retreated into his beer. But the damage was done, and done precisely, and Ansel sat with his heart going slow and enormous in his chest like a bell struck once under water.

*Hale has asked for the Voss manifest.*

After the stranger came. After the letter. After Ansel had stood in the counting house loft with the file open under his lamp for the length of perhaps forty stolen heartbeats — long enough to see that the salvage report contradicted the survivor testimony, that the cargo declared lost matched exactly the cargo later sold at Greyharbor auction, that a company inspector's signature had been witnessed by a man who had, per the harbor rolls, been dead a month by then. Forty heartbeats. He'd put everything back exactly, squared the pages, turned the key. He'd told himself it was clean.

And now the clerk was saying the file had been disturbed.

Either Hale was paranoid — and men like Hale were never paranoid about the right things, only the wrong ones — or Ansel had left a fingerprint somewhere in that tidy room, some displacement of dust, some wrongness in the ordering of pages that only a man who knew the file by heart would feel. Or — and this thought arrived cold and settled in like ballast — Wick was lying. Testing. Hale's little terrier let loose in a tavern full of divers to see which ear pricked up.

Ansel made himself laugh at something Tam said. Made himself drink. Made himself be, for the benefit of whatever eyes the counting house had in this room, exactly the Ansel Eastwick who had lived here twelve unremarkable years: reliable, silent, no ballast below his waterline at all.

* * *

Corliss left first, slipping out between one of Brann's stories and the next, unhurried, a traveler retiring for the night. Ten minutes later — ten minutes measured in the small change of other people's conversations — Ansel followed.

The rain had begun again, a thin salt drizzle. Lamplight lay in yellow coins on the wet cobbles. At the end of the lane, where it bent toward the lodging houses, Corliss waited under the eaves of the rope-walk, hat dark with mist.

"You heard the boy," Corliss said quietly. No greeting. Courier's manners — time was money, words were weight.

"I heard him."

"Then you know your director is pulling threads. When a man like Hale pulls threads, he finds what he's looking for whether it's there or not. Your friend doesn't have the luxury of your caution anymore." Corliss studied him, and in the lamplight his face was older than his voice. "The packet sails on tomorrow's morning tide. After that, the next mail is a fortnight out, and by then the notice boards will have photographs."

Photographs. Ansel's stomach turned over slowly. He hadn't known there were photographs.

"I can't give you an answer tonight," Ansel said.

"No," Corliss agreed. "You'll give it to the tide."

He melted into the dark toward the lodgings, and Ansel stood alone in the drizzle with the whole tavern's worth of voices still turning in his head — Pell's warning, Wick's whisper, Marta's *paper means somebody is frightened* — and underneath them all, steady as surf, the thing he had refused to say aloud in that room full of neighbors who loved him the way a net loves fish.

They would talk about him tomorrow. They would talk regardless. The town's belonging was the town's watching, and he had spent twelve years paying for his place in it with silence, and the silence had bought him exactly nothing that mattered — not his father's name cleared, not his mother's roof kept, not Maren's grave honored, because Maren had no grave, only a ledger entry and a lie.

But there was one more thing Wick had said, and it was the thing Ansel carried home through the rain like a coal cupped in both hands: Hale wanted the manifest. Through channels. Private-like. Which meant the director was not yet certain. Which meant the file was still where Ansel had left it — unless Hale moved it first, tomorrow, behind the new lock, beyond reach forever.

One year left on the indenture, the clerk's copperplate promised. One year of obedience for a freedom that Pell's story said would never come.

Or one night, now, before the tide turned, to take the truth out of that locked room and put it where six dead men and one drowned-and-living girl could finally spend it.

Ansel reached Hetta's gate and stood a moment in the dark, listening to the sea count down.
