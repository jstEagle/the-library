## 11. Debt and Interest

The summons came on a Thursday, in the manner of all Meridian's summons: a folded slip of paper delivered without comment, left where Ansel would be sure to find it. This one was tucked into his slate box at the drying shed, wedged between the nacre shells he'd graded that morning, so that when he lifted the lid the white rectangle lay against the grey like a small cold fish.

*Ansel Eastwick to attend the counting house. Two o'clock. Mr. Quill.*

No reason given. There never was.

He read it twice, set it down among the shells, and went back to grading pearls as if his hands were not suddenly aware of themselves — of every motion, every small wet click of shell against shell. That was the first trick of surviving Meridian: you did not let your body learn your fear before your mind had decided what the fear should be. A man who flinched at paper invited questions. Questions were how nets were drawn tight.

Still, the name *Quill* sat in him like a swallowed stone for the rest of the morning.

Quill was the company's chief clerk, keeper of ledgers, architect of the polite arithmetic by which every soul in Saltmere owed Meridian something. He was a narrow man, dry as old rope, with ink-stained cuffs and a voice pitched so low and even that listeners leaned in to catch it — which, Ansel suspected, was the whole design. Quill never raised his voice because he never needed to. The numbers did the shouting.

At noon Ansel ate his bread on the quay, watching the tide come in over the flats, and made himself think about nothing at all. It was a skill he'd learned young, the emptying-out, the stillness of a pool at slack water. His mother had called it *keeping your bottom clean* — a diver's phrase. Silt up, you saw nothing. Silt down, you could see all the way to the truth of a thing.

The trouble was that lately his silt kept stirring. Since Corliss had come to Saltmere with his questions and his letters, since Ansel had seen Maren Voss's slanted hand crawling across that page like something alive, the bottom of his pool would not stay settled. He kept reaching for stillness and finding currents instead.

At half past one he washed his hands, put on his good coat — the one without pearl-marks on the sleeves — and walked up from the quay through the town toward the counting house.

* * *

Meridian House stood at the head of the harbor like a man standing with his back to the sea and his eyes on everyone else. Three storeys of grey stone, windows tall and narrow, roof ridged like a knuckle. The company's flag hung limp above the door — blue field, white meridian line, the symbol stitched into every sail, every crate-stamp, every ledger page in Saltmere. Ansel had known it longer than he'd known his own face in a mirror.

Inside, the air smelled the way money thought: paper, wax, lamp oil, the faint vinegar tang of fresh ink. The entry hall was quiet in the particular way that expensive rooms are quiet — hushed not by emptiness but by carpet and heavy doors and the understanding of everyone within it that loudness was for people who had something to gain by it.

The under-clerk at the desk looked up, looked down at his list, and nodded. "Go up. Second door on the left."

The stairs creaked in places they had creaked for twelve years. Ansel knew this building the way he knew the reef paths — which boards gave, which corners you could stand in unseen, which office windows overlooked the harbor and which faced only the blank wall of the warehouse next door. He had been coming here since he was nine years old, first clutching his mother's hand, then alone, then as part of the furniture. Twelve years. One more year, said the number in his head, unbidden. *One more year.*

The second door on the left was open. Mr. Quill was at his desk with his spectacles low on his nose, writing in a ledger whose columns ran straight as pilings. He finished his line before he looked up. He always finished his line.

"Eastwick." He indicated the chair opposite with the end of his pen. "Sit."

Ansel sat. The chair was hard and deliberately lower than the visitor's chairs in other offices; he had never noticed until someone told him, years ago, and now he always noticed.

Quill set down the pen, removed his spectacles, and folded them with the care of a man folding a map of somewhere valuable. Without them his eyes were smaller, older, unexpectedly sharp — two beads of tide-pool glass.

"Do you know," he said, "what day this is?"

Ansel considered several answers and rejected them all. "No, sir."

"Neither do I, precisely. That is my point." Quill drew a slim folder from a drawer and laid it on the desk but did not open it. "There are so many days in these books. Signings, renewals, assessments, deaths. A clerk learns to measure time in obligations rather than calendar. Your family's account, for instance, opened on the ninth of March, thirty-one years ago. Your father's indenture. Then your mother's. Then yours, upon her death — transferred, as the contract provides, to the natural issue of the debtor." He said *natural issue* the way another man might say *the rain*. "Twelve years ago this spring."

"Yes, sir."

"Which means—" and here Quill opened the folder at last, turning it so Ansel could see the figures, neat as stitching, "—one year remaining."

There it was. The number he carried everywhere anyway, written out in another man's ink. Ansel looked at it and felt nothing he could name, which was itself a familiar feeling.

"One year," Quill repeated softly, "if."

Ansel waited. With Quill there was always an *if*, and it always came after the pause, placed like a pearl on velvet.

"The contract provides for discharge upon completion of term," Quill went on, "provided the account stands clear. Provided there are no outstanding debts against the indentured party. And provided—" he tapped the page once, a nail clicking on paper, "—the conduct of the party has been satisfactory throughout the final period of service. Clause nineteen. You'll find it near the bottom, in smaller type. They always put the important things in smaller type."

"I've read clause nineteen, sir."

"Have you. Good. Then you know that 'satisfactory conduct' is not defined." Quill smiled. It was not an unkind smile; that was what made it terrible. It was the smile of a man looking at weather. "I have watched men leave this building free and men leave it in chains, and the difference between them was rarely the size of their debts. It was the quality of their last year. Obedience, Eastwick, is a currency. It appreciates. Carelessness depreciates. A man one year from freedom has exactly one year of currency left to spend, and no credit."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, a door closed and footsteps crossed the entry hall and faded.

"May I ask why I've been called, sir?" Ansel kept his voice level, curious rather than anything else. "If it's just the anniversary—"

"It is never *just* anything." Quill rose and went to the window, hands clasped behind him, and stood looking down at the harbor as if taking its inventory. "You are one of the best divers this company has employed in twenty years. I say that as fact, not praise. Facts are the only honest currency, in my experience. Your yields are consistent. Your losses—" meaning lost gear, lost days, the small wastage of any man's labor — "are negligible. In short, you are worth more to us in the water than your debt is worth on paper. Do you understand what that means?"

"That the company is content with my service."

"It means the company has *no reason* to notice you." Quill turned from the window. "Contentment, Eastwick, is the finest condition a debtor can achieve. The noticed man drowns. The unnoticed man finishes his term. I have signed the discharge papers of forty-one men in my time here, and buried the appeals of nine, and I will tell you freely what separated them: the nine gave somebody above stairs a reason to look at them closely in their final year. A quarrel. A question asked too loudly. A name mentioned that ought not to have been mentioned." 

The pause after that stretched just long enough.

"A stranger in town," Quill said, "asks a great many questions. Mail packet brought him Tuesday. Corliss is the name he gives. He has been to the taverns, the chandlers, the customs shed. He shows letters. He is, we understand, looking for people who knew the crew of a certain ship." Quill returned to his desk, sat, and laced his fingers together. "Now. Why would I tell a pearl diver this?"

Ansel's heart performed one long, slow roll, like a hull taking a swell broadside. He kept his face still. Twelve years of practice, and it held.

"Because you want me to keep to my work, sir."

"Partly." Quill picked up his pen, examined the nib, set it down again. "Partly because strangers asking questions make work for everyone, and work makes irregularities, and irregularities make notices in ledgers, and notices—" the bead-glass eyes came up, mild as morning, "—can attach to a man's account. Even a man with one year left. Even a good diver with negligible losses." He slid the folder across the desk. "Sign the acknowledgment. It says only that you have been informed of your term's status. Routine."

Ansel read it. He read everything they put in front of him, always had; it was the one rebellion available to the ill-treated, to know the shape of their cage better than the jailers bothered to. The text was three lines. He signed. Quill blotted the signature, folded the paper away, and produced — with a small flourish that in another man might have been warmth — a second document.

"And this?"

"Your harvest quota for the season. Revised." Quill's tone did not change at all. "The board has raised expectations across all berths. Yours increases by fifteen percent. Effective immediately."

"Fifteen percent." Ansel heard his own voice go flat and let it. "That's two full dives a week beyond my current schedule."

"It is arithmetic," Quill agreed pleasantly. "Debt and interest, Eastwick. Everything is debt and interest. You borrow breath, you pay it back in pearls." He replaced his spectacles, took up his ledger, found his place. "That will be all. Work well. Stay unnoticed. In one year, you will walk out of this building a free man, and I will genuinely wish you joy of it. I sign those papers gladly. It is the only part of my work I like."

Ansel stood. At the door, Quill spoke again without looking up, the pen already moving.

"Oh — Eastwick. If the stranger Corliss approaches you, as he has approached others, you will report it. To me directly, not to the watch. Consider it clause nineteen, informally applied." The pen paused. "A man's final year should be quiet, don't you think? Quiet, and profitable, and short."

Ansel said, "Good afternoon, Mr. Quill," and went out, and down the creaking stairs, and out into the ordinary daylight, which had gone strange around the edges, the way light goes when you surface too fast.

* * *

He walked home the long way, along the tideline, because he could not yet trust his legs to carry him anywhere useful and the water had always been the only thing that slowed his blood. The tide was falling, leaving the flats shining and pocked, gulls picking at the margins. Out past the breakwater the packet moorings rode empty; the next mail boat wasn't due for days. But Corliss had come on the last one, and Corliss was still in town, lodged above the Anchor and Chain, asking his careful questions in his careful voice, showing letters in a hand Ansel knew better than his own.

*Report it,* Quill had said. Report it to me directly.

So that was the shape of it. Not a reminder. A leash, measured out politely, one year of slack. Quill hadn't summoned him to warn him off — or not only that. He'd marked him. *Noticed.* The word sat in Ansel's chest like a swallowed fishhook. For twelve years he had been invisible by craft, a man whose silences were load-bearing walls, and in one afternoon the chief clerk had drawn a circle around his name in the great book and said, in effect: *we will be watching this line.*

And the quota. Fifteen percent. He turned the figure over as he walked, because figures were safer than feelings. Two extra dives a week, deep-water beds mostly, the far grounds where the water stayed cold even in summer and the light died ten fathoms down. They were pricing his final year in advance. Keep pace, and the year would grind him thin but pass. Fall behind — miss a dive, come up short a weight of shell — and the shortfall would go on the account, and the account would grow interest, and interest would buy the company one more year, and then another. He had seen it done to Danner's boy, to old Metcher. The cage described in polite arithmetic: you never saw the door close. You only noticed, some winter, that the bars had multiplied.

His hands had gone cold. He looked at them, diver's hands, scarred at the fingertips, steady even now, and thought: *they will not get another year. Whatever it costs, they get one year and no more.*

Which was, he understood dimly, exactly the kind of thought Quill's speech had been designed to provoke — the dangerous kind, the kind that made a man stop counting his way to safety and start weighing other currencies. Obedience was a currency. So was silence. And so, apparently, was whatever it was that had made him stand in the counting house loft six days ago and recognize Maren Voss's handwriting with his whole body, like a bell struck underwater.

He reached the stretch below Hetta's house and stopped. The light was going amber behind the western hills; lamps were coming up along the quay road, one by one, obedient little flames. From the harbor came the slap of water against pilings, patient as a clock.

And there — out past the point, where the cove folded into dark — a light. Brief. Deliberate. A lantern shown and shuttered, shown and shuttered: two flashes, a pause, two more.

Ansel stood very still on the tideline, silt settling fast now, the pool of him suddenly, terribly clear.

Nobody signaled from the cove at dusk. Nobody but fishermen running late, and they used lights, not shutters. Two and two. He counted the pattern again as it repeated, his heart keeping its own count against his ribs, and thought of Quill's mild voice — *a man's final year should be quiet* — and of clause nineteen in its small type, and of a slanted hand on paper, alive somewhere beyond that point in the dark.

One year, they had promised him, if he stayed obedient.

The lantern flashed again. Twice, a pause, twice. Waiting for an answer.

Ansel stood on the falling tide with the company's arithmetic in one pocket and six years of a promise in the other, and began, slowly, to walk toward the boat shed — where, he happened to know, an old signal lantern hung on its nail, wick trimmed, oil filled, for reasons no one had ever asked him about.

The cage had described itself in polite numbers all afternoon.

It had forgotten to count the dark.
