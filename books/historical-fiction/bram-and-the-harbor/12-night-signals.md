## 12. Night Signals

Ansel told himself he was only walking.

That was the lie he carried out of the counting house yard at half past eleven, past Hetta's darkened windows where the lamp had gone cold hours ago, down the lane that smelled of kelp and tar and the low tide's rotten breath. He was only walking. The night was fine. A man could walk. Saltmere was a small town hemmed by water on three sides, and men walked its edges every evening to think, to smoke, to escape their wives and their debts. Nobody marked a man who walked.

But his feet knew where they were going, and his feet were not liars.

The lantern signal had come at dusk — three long flashes from the headland above Cutter's Cove, a pause, then two short ones. Ansel had seen it from the loft window of the counting house while he pretended to check the tide tables for the morning ledger. Three long, two short. It was nothing. It was fishermen signaling set nets, it was the pilot boat talking to the point light, it was any of a hundred ordinary conversations the harbor held with itself after dark. He had told himself all of that too.

Then it repeated. Three long, two short. And then — this was what undid him — a fourth flash, single, deliberate, held so long it must have scorched the shutter-man's fingers. Like a hand raised in greeting. Like someone saying *I see you watching. I know you saw.*

Corliss's voice came back to him across three days of careful distance: *If you change your mind, the cove answers when the moon is under. You'll know the signal. You'd know her handwriting anywhere; you'll know this too.*

He had refused Corliss cleanly, or he had believed he had. He had said no with the same flat care he used to decline a second pint or an invitation to gamble, the refusal of a man whose neck was already worth something to people who collected debts. He had walked back to Hetta's, eaten his supper, blown out his candle, and lain in the dark listening to his own heart like a man listening for footsteps on stairs.

Now here he was, walking. With the tide tables folded in his coat pocket and a coil of good line over one shoulder because — he told himself — a diver never went to the water without line. It was habit. It meant nothing. His hands had decided things his mouth hadn't agreed to yet, and his hands had been trained by the sea, which does not ask permission.

* * *

Saltmere after dark was a different town wearing the same bones. By day it belonged to Meridian — the warehouse doors, the counting house shutters, the company colors flying fat and orange over the quay, even the gulls seemed to file grievances in triplicate. But at night the town reverted to something older. Fishing nets hung in the lanes like sleeping animals. Cats conducted their own commerce. And in the doorways and upper windows, behind curtains that everyone pretended were opaque, the town watched itself, and kept what it saw.

Ansel moved through it the way he had learned to move through everything: without sound, without haste, without expression. Twelve years in Saltmere had taught him that a man's face was public property and his thoughts were the only land he'd ever own. He passed the Shipwright's Arms and heard the late crowd singing something bawdy about a duchess and a donkey, and he let the noise cover his steps until he was past the last of the houses and out onto the cliff path where nobody sang at all.

The moon was under, as promised — a thin rind of cloud where it should have been, the sky lit only by stars enough to make the path a gray ribbon stitched between blackness above and blackness below. The sea made its endless argument against the rocks, patient, repetitive, certain of winning eventually. Ansel knew that sound better than his mother's voice now. His father had gone into it before Ansel could write his own name, and the sea had never explained itself, never apologized, never once given anything back that it took. Meridian had at least sent a bill for the funeral.

He thought of that sometimes — the arithmetic of grief as the company practiced it. Six shillings for the coffin, offset against four months of the dead man's outstanding advance. His mother had signed the indenture papers with the same hand she used to salt fish, steady until the last stroke, and then the pen had stuttered and left a blot like a small dark wound on the page. Ansel had been nine. He had thought the blot was the saddest thing he'd ever seen. He had not yet learned that the whole document was the wound, and the blot was only where it showed through.

Three long, two short. If they signaled again tonight, that would be the shape of it.

His heart was going hard now, harder than it ever did on the deep dives, and he noticed that with a kind of clinical surprise. On the dives his pulse was a tool he governed — slow it before you drop, slow it on the way down, spend it like coins. Here on the path with nothing chasing him, his body had mutinied entirely. Because this was different, his body understood what his mouth still argued about. Diving was dangerous but lawful. This — whatever this was beginning to become — was the other kind of danger, the kind Meridian handled not with safety rules but with constables and rope.

The path bent around the shoulder of the headland and Cutter's Cove opened below him, a bite taken out of the coast, its beach pale shingle whispering with each withdrawal of the waves. There was no boat. There was no fire, no camp, no shape of anyone waiting. There was only the old netting shed at the cove's edge, abandoned since the herring failed, leaning into the wind like a man listening.

And then the light.

Three long flashes from beside the shed. A pause. Two short.

Ansel stood very still on the path. Below, the light waited through five seconds, ten, and gave the fourth flash — the single long one — and went dark.

*I see you watching.*

His hands tightened on the coil of line until the hemp bit grooves into his palms. He could still turn around. That was the thing about lines — you crossed them one step at a time, and every step before the last one looked exactly like standing still. He was only walking. He was only curious. He only wanted to know if the signal was Maren's people or smugglers or revenue men laying bait, and wanting to know was harmless, knowledge was harmless —

But he had recognized the handwriting in Corliss's letter. Slanted, urgent, the letters leaning east like grass in a wind off the water, and the moment he'd seen it six drowned years had come up gasping. Maren Voss, who could whistle gulls onto her fist. Maren, who had dared him to dive the wreck of the old *Peregrine* when they were twelve and had jumped in first when he hesitated. Declared drowned. Listed now — he'd read the harbor notice with his own eyes — as an agent acting against the charter of the Meridian Trading Company, reward offered, information solicited. Enemy of the Crown, as far as a piece of paper could make a girl who once cried over a broken-winged tern and fed it sardines for a month until it flew.

They had made her an enemy. Which meant, somewhere in the arithmetic Saltmere ran on, they had made Ansel's memory of her contraband too.

He went down the path.

* * *

The shingle gave him away immediately — there was no quiet way across it, and he stopped trying, letting his boots announce him the way an honest man would. If there were watchers in the shed, let them hear a man arriving openly. Deceit, he had found, worked best dressed as plainness.

"Three long and two short," said a voice from the shed's shadow before he reached the door. "You counted them right, coming down. Most would've run."

"I nearly did," Ansel said. "Twice."

A match scraped and touched a lantern wick, turned low, hooded on the seaward side so it threw light inward only. The man holding it was Brann — Ansel knew him by sight the way everyone in Saltmere knew Brann, the coaster captain who appeared on manifests nowhere and paid cash everywhere, weathered as a bollard, with a beard going to rust and eyes the flat gray of a mackerel's back. What Ansel didn't know was why Brann would be flashing lanterns in patterns meant for him.

"You know me?" Brann asked.

"Everyone knows you."

"That's not what I asked." The lantern rose slightly. "Do you *know* me. Can you vouch me."

"No," Ansel said honestly. "I can't vouch anyone. I've learned what belonging costs here."

Something moved at the corner of Brann's mouth that might have been approval. "Good answer. Wrong answer, maybe, for what I hoped, but honest." He tilted his head toward the shed interior. "Come in out of the wind, diver. Let's talk where a light won't travel."

Inside, the shed smelled of ancient tar and rotting net and mouse. Brann hung the lantern on a hook driven into a beam older than either of them and studied Ansel with the unhurried attention of a man appraising cargo.

"Corliss spoke to you," Brann said. "Three days back, by the packet stairs."

"He spoke. I listened. I said no."

"You said no." Brann nodded slowly. "And then you learned my signal somehow, and walked four miles of cliff path at midnight carrying dive line, to say no again in person?"

Ansel felt heat climb his neck. "How did you know I'd seen it? The signal tonight — how did you know I was watching at all?"

"Because we've been watching the watchers, lad. There's a man up on Gallows Hill most nights lately, glassing the harbor for Meridian. Hale's new careful. And there's you, three evenings running, in the counting house loft window, looking not at your ledgers but at the headland, with a face on you like a man doing sums he doesn't want the total of." Brann let that sit. "Corliss says you're the one who'd know her hand. Says you went white as drowned meat when he showed you that letter, and lied about it badly, and he's been in this trade long enough to know a bad liar is better than a good one. Good liars are for sale. Bad liars are feeling something."

"I'm not feeling anything," Ansel said, and heard how unconvincing it was, and hated it.

"Mm." Brann reached inside his coat and brought out an oilskin packet, small, tied with waxed cord, and did not offer it — simply held it, weighing it in his palm like a pearl of bad news. "There's a woman on a ship I supply, out past the point. She was born in this town. She has reason to believe one boy she grew up with is still in it, and that he's got eyes inside the counting house and a memory longer than his fear." He paused. "She may be wrong about the memory part. Fear grows faster than memory. I've seen it the other way once or twice, but rarely."

"What do you want from me?"

"Tonight? Nothing. Tonight I want to look at you and decide whether you're worth drowning for." Brann said it flatly, without drama, and that was what made Ansel's stomach drop — the man wasn't threatening him, he was pricing risk, the same way Meridian priced pearls, by weight and luster and chance of flaw. "You're indentured. Twelve years served, by my count. One year left on your paper. A year is not nothing. A year is a whole life, if the wrong rope finds the right neck."

"One year," Ansel agreed quietly.

"So walk away. That's my counsel, freely given, and it's the only free thing you'll get from me. Walk home, keep your face empty, take your freedom in twelve months, and let the dead rest. The dead have rested this long." Brann's gray eyes didn't blink. "Or else tell me why you climbed down here instead, and we'll go on from there."

The wind worried at the shed's loose boards. Out on the water, waves folded and unfolded along the shingle, patient as clerks. Ansel looked at the oilskin packet in Brann's hand and understood suddenly that whatever it held had been written by that slanted, eastward-leaning hand, and that it had been aboard ship for weeks, perhaps months, waiting for a recipient who might never exist — written on spec, on hope, by a woman declared dead who had refused to cooperate with her own erasure.

"Why did she do it?" he asked. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Fake the wreck. Or — survive it, whichever way it truly went. Why not just stay dead safe somewhere?"

Brann was silent a moment. "Because the wreck wasn't weather," he said. "And she's the only living soul who knows it for certain, and knowing a thing like that sits in you like a swallowed anchor. You can't sail well with it. Believe me. I've carried smaller truths across bigger seas and they near capsized me." He extended the packet at last, holding it out over the space between them. "This isn't proof of anything. It's a question. She wrote it before she knew if there was anyone left in Saltmere worth asking. Take it or don't. But take it or don't *now*, diver, because the tide's turning and I'm gone with it, and I don't come back to this cove twice."

Ansel looked at the packet. His heart filled his ears like surf. Every prudent year of his life — twelve of them, each one stacked like ballast — told him to shake his head, to thank Brann for his counsel, to turn and climb the path and be in his bed before Hetta woke, and serve his final year, and collect his freedom papers, and be a small free man in a small free life.

He thought of the blot on the indenture papers. The small dark wound where the truth showed through.

He took the packet.

It weighed almost nothing and more than anything he'd ever lifted, and his hands, trained by the sea not to tremble, trembled anyway.

"There's a signal," Brann said, already moving to snuff the lantern, businesslike now, the transaction closed. "When you're ready to be more than a pair of eyes — if that day comes — hang a white rag on the third piling of the old ferry stage, and within three nights there'll be a light asking. Three long, two short. Same language." At the door he stopped, a shape against the star-gray, and his voice dropped into something almost gentle. "One more thing, gratis. The clerk you'll want to mind is young Tam Weller — round face, always chewing his pen. Hale promoted him past his appetite, and hungry men sell cheap. Mind Tam."

Then the lantern was out and Brann was a footstep, then a shingle-scrape, then a hull kissing water somewhere below in the dark, oars wrapped in cloth, pulling away with barely a seam of sound. Ansel stood alone in the rotting shed with a dead lantern and a dead town between him and home, and the oilskin packet burning a hole against his chest like a coal he'd chosen to carry.

* * *

He didn't open it on the beach. He didn't open it on the path. He made himself wait, made himself do the thing properly — checking his backtrail along the cliff, noting that Gallows Hill's watcher had a lantern glow tonight and was facing the harbor, not the cove — because if he was going to be a criminal he intended at minimum to be a competent one. But by the time he reached the bench of wind-cropped turf above the cove where he and Maren had once sat eating stolen plums and ranking the townspeople by how much they'd scream if dropped in the harbor, discipline lost its grip.

He knelt. He broke the waxed cord with his thumbnail. Inside the oilskin was a single sheet, folded twice, and even by starlight he knew the hand before he read a word — the letters leaning east, hurrying somewhere, alive.

*Diver,* it began, and he made a sound that in daylight he would have been ashamed of.

*Brann tells me the town stands and the counting house stands and the water is probably still cold, so some things are constant. I won't waste paper pretending this letter might find anyone else. If it's you, then you know I didn't drown, and you know or will soon know that the drowning was made, not suffered. I need what the counting house keeps. Not copies — the file, the true one, with the insurance signatures. I can't come for it until I have somewhere to bring it and someone ashore who wants the truth more than they want to live comfortably. That's a large request of a man with a year left on his paper. So I ask it as what I actually am, which is not an enemy agent, whatever their notices say. I am the girl under the breakwater, and I am calling in the promise. You owed me one truth for every lie I covered at supper. I counted. You owe me forty-one.*

*Don't answer with words. Words get read. Answer the way we answered each other before either of us could write.*

Underneath, drawn small and sure, was a shape he had not seen in six years and had never once forgotten: the little spiral-and-crossing mark she used to scratch into the breakwater stones with a nail, their sign, meaning in their private grammar neither of them had ever needed to define — *still yours, still here, still coming.*

Ansel sat on the cold turf above Cutter's Cove with the letter in his hands and the whole machinery of the Meridian Trading Company sleeping uneasily a mile behind him, and he understood with perfect clarity that he had crossed the line some steps ago — back at the loft window, back at the quay, back perhaps twelve years, back at a signing table where a pen blotted — and that the only thing left to decide was not whether but how far.

Forty-one truths. She had counted. Of course she had counted.

He looked down at the cove where the lantern had flashed, then out at the black water where a schooner he couldn't see was carrying her away from him again, and he did the only answering that mattered. In the morning — no. Not the morning. Tonight, before Hetta woke, before the town stirred, he would pass the old ferry stage, and on the third piling he would hang a strip of white canvas torn from his dive weight bag, and then he would walk into the counting house as he did every day, empty-faced, obedient, and begin learning how to steal.

The wind came up off the water and turned the letter's pages, eager, like something reading over his shoulder.

Behind him, far up on Gallows Hill, the watcher's lantern blinked — and turned, slowly, toward the cove.
