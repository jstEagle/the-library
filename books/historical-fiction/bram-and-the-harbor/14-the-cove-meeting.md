## 14. The Cove Meeting

The tide was going out when Ansel slipped past the last of Saltmere's lamps, and it seemed to him that the whole town exhaled with it — the water drawing back from the mudflats in long silver sheets, the moored boats settling on their lines like sleeping animals shifting their weight. He had chosen his clothes badly, he realized halfway down the cliff path: the dark coat was good for hiding, but it drank the sea-spray and grew heavy, and by the time he reached the shingle below Gull's Tooth he was carrying an extra few pounds of damp wool, as if the night itself had decided to tax him.

He stopped among the rocks and let his eyes adjust. This was old discipline, diver's discipline. The first mistake underwater was to look too hard; the eye chased what it wanted and missed everything else. So he stood still and let the cove come to him — the black tongue of water between the headlands, the overturned skiff hauled up above the tideline, and, farther out where the channel ran deep, a low shape that had not been there an hour ago. No lights. No anchor light, no riding lamp, nothing that any honest vessel would show. Just a darkness sitting on darker water, patient as a held breath.

Ansel counted to two hundred before he moved. It was not superstition; it was arithmetic. If anyone had followed him from the town, they would need those seconds to make their own decision about cover, and in that window they would betray themselves — a loose stone, a cough, the particular clink of a company man's boot nails. He heard nothing but the sea working the shingle, that endless dry rattle like someone slowly pouring grain from one sack into another, forever.

He crossed the beach quickly, coat open, the oilskin packet flat against his ribs where he'd lashed it with a strip of linen. Twice in the last three days he had taken it out and read what was inside, and twice he had nearly burned it. The first page alone — the manifest amendment in Clerk Wray's cramped hand, dated four days *before* the Voss ship sailed, authorizing a "survey adjustment" at a berth the ship never visited — was enough to hang a man. Not enough to prove anything, maybe. But enough to hang.

At the waterline he stopped again and made the signal Corliss had taught him: three strikes of the shuttered lantern, pause, one more. The light showed only as a pulse against his own cupped hand, a red heartbeat he might have imagined.

Then he waited, and waiting was the worst of it, because waiting gave his mind room.

*You could still go back.* That voice had been with him all evening, reasonable as a clerk reading out figures. Nothing had been proven. He could walk home along the shore road, put the packet under the third floorboard of his room where he kept his mother's ring, and in a year his indenture would end clean, and he would be an ordinary free man of Saltmere with salt-scars on his hands and a share in whatever pearls the beds still gave. Twelve years he had paid for his father's drowning with silence. One more year of obedience and the debt died of natural causes.

But the voice had grown thinner lately, and he knew why. It was the same thinning that happened to a diver's fear at depth — not because the danger lessened, but because at some point you passed beyond the place where turning back was cheaper than going on, and the body understood this before the mind admitted it. He had passed that point somewhere between the counting house loft and tonight.

Out in the channel, the dark shape answered. A single lantern unshuttered, covered, unshuttered — quick, quick, slow. Then a boat came off it, oars muffled with rags, sliding across the cove water so quietly that the sound arrived before the sight of it: the knock of wood against swell, the drip and pull.

The boat grounded six feet from dry shingle and a man stepped out into the shallows without troubling to keep his boots dry, which told Ansel something about him before anything else did. He was broad without being tall, wrapped in a fisherman's jersey gone stiff with salt, and he wore a beard cropped close enough to show the line of a jaw set like a cleat. His eyes went past Ansel immediately — up the cliff path, along the headland, back the way Ansel had come — and only then returned to him.

"You're the diver." Not a question. The voice was flat northern-coast, vowels worn round.

"I'm Eastwick."

"Brann." The man did not offer a hand. He nodded at the packet instead, or rather at the place where it was hidden. "On you?"

"Yes."

"Good. Get in."

Ansel didn't move. "The exchange happens here. I was told here. Shingle, tide's first ebb."

Brann looked at him with something that was almost appreciation. "And I'm telling you different. We don't pass papers standing on a public beach with a town full of Meridian's watchers behind every shutter. You come out to the schooner, you hand them to me aboard, you come back. Half an hour."

"That wasn't the arrangement."

"No," Brann agreed mildly. "It was mine, though, and I'm the one standing in the water." He jerked his chin toward the skiff hauled above the tideline. "Or we take that. Your choice. But the papers cross my hands on my deck, or they don't cross at all. Courier's rule. I don't take delivery where anyone with a spyglass and a grudge can watch me pocket treason."

Ansel weighed it. Every instinct built by twelve years in Saltmere said that stepping onto that boat meant surrendering the one thing keeping him alive — distance. On land, he could run. In a boat, he was cargo.

But he thought of Corliss saying, in his careful foreign accent: *She trusts Brann with her life. She trusts you with her truth. Try to be worth half of each.*

He pulled the skiff down to the water. It was lighter than it looked, hull patched with tarred canvas, and Brann dropped into the stern sheets without being asked and took the tiller as if he'd been born holding one. Ansel shipped the oars and pulled. The schooner resolved as they approached — sixty feet, flush-decked, hull blacked, rigging cut down for speed rather than cargo. She flew nothing. A vessel belonging to no flag, which on some coasts meant pirate and on others meant freedom, depending entirely on which side of her gunwale you were standing.

A hand caught the skiff's gunwale as they came alongside, and a second man — young, gap-toothed, silent — lifted Ansel aboard the way a dockhand lifts a crate, assessing weight and setting it down. Then the skiff was made fast and Ansel stood on another man's deck for the first time in twelve years, and the strangeness of it went through him like cold water in a boot.

There were perhaps eight crew visible, and not one of them looked at him directly, which was its own kind of courtesy. Brann led him aft to the small shadow of the mizzen, where a hooded lamp sat on a coil of line throwing light no further than a table's width. Brann gestured. Ansel sat. The packet came out from under his coat, and for a moment he simply held it in both hands, feeling the weight of paper and wax and everything else.

"Corliss says there are four documents," Brann said. "Manifest amendment. A payment order through the Meridian house at Corvane. A letter — internal — discussing the survey adjustment. And a copy of the insurance claim filed after."

"You know them well enough."

"I ask better questions when I know what I'm carrying." Brann crouched opposite him, forearms on knees, close enough that Ansel could smell tar and old smoke on him. "Here's what happens now, diver, and I'll say it once. I take these aboard, catalog them, seal them, and they go below in a lead-lined case that goes nowhere near this coast again until it's in a certain hand in Corvane. From the moment I touch them, they stop being yours. Whatever happens to you after tonight — if Hale's men drag you off the quay tomorrow — you know nothing, you saw nothing, these papers never existed. That's the arrangement. That's the only arrangement."

"And her?" Ansel heard his own voice come out lower than he intended. "What's her part in the arrangement?"

Something shifted in Brann's face — not softening exactly; a recalculation, like a man revising a chart. "You've not met."

"No."

"She speaks of you anyway." Brann said it without emphasis, the way he might report weather, and it landed on Ansel like a depth charge all the same. Six years. She had been gone six years, declared drowned, declared traitor, and she spoke of him. "That's her business. Mine is the papers." He put out his hand, palm up. Open. Waiting.

Ansel looked at that hand. Broad, scarred across the heel of the palm in the way of a man who handled line for a living. It occurred to him, with the strange clarity that comes at the edge of great risk, that he knew nothing about this person worth knowing. He did not know Brann's price, his politics, or his god. He did not know whether the lead-lined case existed or whether the papers would be sold back to Meridian within the week at ten times their weight in pearl. Everything Corliss had told him rested on Corliss's word, and Corliss's word rested on Maren's, and Maren's rested on a promise made under a breakwater by two children who had believed the world was larger than it proved to be.

Trust extended on nothing. On faith alone. It went against every law Ansel had learned in Saltmere, where survival meant trusting only what you could hold, weigh, and hide.

And yet — he had been owned since he was eleven years old by a company whose every document was stamped and witnessed and countersigned, and where had that gotten him? Drowning quietly in arithmetic. Meanwhile here was a smuggler with no papers at all except the ones Ansel carried, asking to be believed.

He put the packet in Brann's hand.

Brann did not snatch it. He received it, actually received it, the way you accept something entrusted, and broke the seal right there in the lamplight. He checked each document against some list he kept behind his eyes, handling the old paper with unexpected gentleness — the insurance claim especially, which he held longer than the rest, tilting it toward the lamp.

"Four days before she sailed," he said quietly. Reading the date on the manifest amendment. "They signed this before the ship left harbor."

"So it appears."

"Don't give me *appears*, diver. I've carried enough lies to know the difference between a fact and its costume." Brann folded the papers back along their original creases, precisely. "This is a fact. Whoever wrote this knew the ship was going to sink before it sank." He looked up. "Do you understand what you've been carrying? Not evidence. A confession. They just never expected anyone to read it."

"They didn't expect me to be able to read it," Ansel said. "I'm a diver. I count pearls and sign where the mark is."

Brann laughed once, short and genuine, and sealed the packet into an oilskin sleeve, then into a metal case that he did indeed line with dull grey sheeting. He worked fast, sure-handed, talking as he went in a low stream that was half instruction and half something else — the running commentary of a man deciding how much truth a stranger could carry.

"The schooner stands off this coast three nights, then moves. After that, contact comes through Corliss or not at all. If you're taken, we were never here, and the papers were lost in a crossing. Nobody trades you out, diver. Understand? There's no rescue written into this. You're spending yourself."

"I understand."

"Do you?" Brann finished the clasps and looked at him straight. "Because I've seen men say they understand and then drown anyway. Not because the water beat them. Because at the bottom of it they found out they'd never decided what they were dying for, and the finding out broke them mid-stroke." He tapped the metal case once with two fingers. "So tell me. What are you dying for? Papers? Revenge on Hale? Or is there a name in it?"

Ansel thought of the breakwater. Of a girl with ink on her fingers who had once told him that the sea keeps every promise because it forgets nothing, only buries things where the tides can find them again. He thought of her handwriting, slanting forward like a person leaning into wind.

"There's a name in it," he said.

"Good." Brann rose, and the interview was over; the whole change in him was immediate and total, the courier replaced by the captain. "Back to your skiff. Keep off the shore road, take the cliff steps past Hetta Fenn's — the watch doesn't trouble that side after midnight, the steps stink of her fish sauce and they'd rather breathe brine." He paused. "Tell Corliss the next delivery wants to be bigger, and it wants to be soon. What you brought tonight proves intent. Intent hangs clerks. To hang directors, we need the wreck file itself. Originals. And that lives in the counting house, behind Hale's own door."

"I know where it lives," Ansel said. "I've seen the shelf."

Brann stopped halfway through lifting the lantern and looked at him again, longer this time. "You've been inside already."

"Once."

"Once is how men get caught," Brann said. "Twice is how men get hanged. Make sure the third time is worth it." He turned and called softly, and the gap-toothed sailor appeared to see Ansel down to the skiff, and then Ansel was pulling away across the black water with the schooner dissolving behind him like something he might have dreamed.

* * *

Halfway back to shore he shipped oars and drifted, and let himself feel it — really feel it — for the first time since he'd left his room. His hands were steady on the oar looms, which surprised him. Inside, nothing was steady. Something had come undone and something else was assembling in its place, rough and unfinished, and he could not yet name whether it was a man being built or broken down.

*She speaks of you anyway.*

He had not asked Brann what she said. Some part of him had understood that asking would cost more than he could pay tonight, that hope was a currency he needed to hoard until morning.

He found the cliff steps by memory and climbed them slowly, the town coming up around him in patches — the harbor bell buoy, the yellow eye of Hetta's kitchen window burning late as always, the counting house dark against the stars with its one guard lamp burning over the door like a cyclops asleep with one eye reluctantly open. Behind that door, on the third shelf of the locked cabinet in Hale's private office, lay the original file on the wreck of the *Voss Star*. Manifests. Survey reports. The claim. And whatever else Meridian had bothered to keep as proof of its own innocence.

Brann wanted originals. The magistrate in Corliss's plan — if the plan even had a magistrate yet — would want originals. Everyone wanted the thing that lived behind Hale's door, and everyone assumed the diver would simply go back in and fetch it, as though the first time had not taken six years off his life.

Ansel stood in the lee of Hetta's wall and let his breath slow. The sensible course was to wait. Let the schooner move on. Let Corliss find another courier, another diver, some braver instrument. He had done his part; he had spent himself as promised; the confession was aboard a black ship bound for Corvane. He could stop now and survive.

Then he remembered the way Brann had held the insurance claim up to the lamplight — gently, the way you hold a dead man's letter — and said, *they signed this before the ship left harbor,* and Ansel understood that he had already gone too far to stop, that stopping was just drowning done slowly.

He was reaching for Hetta's side door when it opened.

She stood in the frame in her shawl, a candle in one hand, and she did not look startled, and she did not look away from him, and for a long moment neither of them said anything while the candle flame leaned between them in the sea wind.

"You're bleeding," Hetta said at last, nodding at his knuckles, scraped raw sometime during the landing without his noticing. "Come in before the gulls follow the smell of you." And she stepped aside to let him pass, saying nothing about the hour, nothing about the cliffs, nothing at all — which frightened Ansel more than any question could have.
