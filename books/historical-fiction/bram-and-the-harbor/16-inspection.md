## 16. Inspection

The summons came at half past seven in the morning, delivered by a boy too young to know what he was carrying.

Ansel was on the quay, coiling rope with the other divers, when the lad tugged his sleeve and held out a slip of cream-colored paper. Meridian's seal pressed into the wax — the meridian line, the sun above it, the whole company's arrogance rendered in miniature. Ansel read it twice because the first time his eyes would not hold still.

*Mr. Eastwick is requested to attend the director's office at nine o'clock regarding certain irregularities of record.*

*Requested.* Hale never requested. The word sat on the page like a knife laid flat on a table.

"Bad news?" asked Tam Ossler beside him, not looking up from his coils. Tam had dived beside Ansel's father once, years ago, and had never quite stopped treating Ansel as a man owed something.

"Just bookkeeping," Ansel said, and folded the paper into quarters, and then into eighths, until it was small enough to be nothing.

He finished the rope. He ate the bread Hetta had packed him, though it turned to wet sand in his mouth. He watched the tide come in over the flats with the patience of a man who had spent twelve years learning that panic was a luxury the water did not permit. Down there, under forty feet of green dark, fear killed faster than any director could. You breathed when the rope told you to breathe. You moved when the current allowed. Everything else — every scream in the chest, every animal demand to surface — you folded away and set aside like a tool you'd need later.

Nine o'clock was a depth he could work.

* * *

The counting house smelled the way it always did: ink, tallow, cold stone, and beneath it all the faint brine of ledgers carried up from damp cellars where the older records slept. Ansel had walked this corridor a thousand times to settle accounts, to sign for gear, to have his debt recalculated each quarter in numbers that always grew no matter how many pearls went into the company's trays. Today the corridor felt narrower. The clerks at their desks did not look up. That was its own kind of message — everyone knew, or guessed, or had been told to see nothing, which amounted to the same thing.

Hale's office occupied the corner of the second floor, two windows giving onto the harbor as if the harbor itself were an asset under review. A junior clerk — Pellwick, young, quick-handed, the sort whose pen moved before his thoughts did — opened the door for him without meeting his eyes.

"Eastwick."

Hale stood at the window with his back half-turned. He was not a large man; he didn't need to be. He had the stillness of deep water, the kind that looked calm because everything in it had already learned what happened to things that struggled. His coat was dove-gray, immaculate. On the desk behind him lay a single folder, closed, squared precisely to the edge of the blotter.

"Sit," Hale said, without turning.

Ansel sat. The chair was hard and deliberately low, so that any visitor looked slightly upward at the desk. Ansel had noticed this years ago and had decided, in the private ledger he kept behind his eyes, that furniture was the cheapest form of power ever invented. Knowing that made it easier to sit straight anyway.

"You dive tomorrow's beds?" Hale asked, still facing the window. Below, the packet boat was warping in from her coastal run, gulls screaming over her wake.

"Yes, sir. The eastern shoal. Quota's forty weight."

"Mm." Hale turned. He had a face built for ledgers — long, ruled, with small eyes that totaled whatever they fell upon. "You've been with us twelve years now."

"Eleven years, ten months, sir."

"Counting the months." Something that was almost a smile touched the ruled mouth. "That's a diver's habit. Counting what's owed." He crossed to the desk but did not sit, standing instead with one hand resting on the closed folder, the way a man rests a hand on a dog's head. "Do you know why I've asked you here?"

"Certain irregularities of record, sir. That's all the note said."

"It did say that, yes." Hale studied him. The silence stretched, and Ansel let it stretch, keeping his breathing slow and even, keeping his hands loose on his knees, keeping his face in the particular blankness the sea had taught him — not sullen, not eager, just present. Under the water you did not look at the eels and they mostly did not look at you.

"The Voss file," Hale said.

Two words. They went through Ansel like a harpoon through the chest, and he felt the old discipline take hold the way it always did — the instant of white shock, then the folding-away, the setting-aside, the body going quiet while the mind worked the problem. *Breathe when the rope tells you to breathe.*

"The Voss ship," Ansel said. "The *Fairwind*, sir? She went down six years back. Off the Serran point."

"She did." Hale's fingers drummed once on the folder. "Her owner filed a claim against us — cargo insurance, hull underwritten through our marine office. The claim was honored. Six hundred crowns." He said the figure the way another man might name a dead relative: flatly, with the grief worn off. "Recently there has been some irregularity in the records touching that claim. Pages consulted out of sequence. A file removed from the archive room and returned late. Returned *damp*, Eastwick."

"Damp, sir?"

"There was rain three nights ago," Hale said. "You were in the building that night. You often are — fetching your tally sheets, settling your account. You're a careful man. Careful men are permitted liberties." His voice did not rise. It never seemed to need to. "But careful men also understand that liberties are loans. And loans accrue interest."

"I don't know anything about the file, sir." And here was the strange thing, the thing Ansel would examine later with a kind of horrified professional admiration for himself: his voice came out level. Not just level — *mild*. Slightly puzzled, the way a man sounds when asked about weather in a town he's lived in all his life. "I was in that night for my tally sheet, same as always. Old Merrick signed me through — he'll have it in his book. I took my papers from the front rack and went home. Rain caught me on Harrow Lane. I remember it because Hetta scolded me for tracking salt water across her floor, and Hetta doesn't scold unless she means it."

All true. Every word of it true, which was the trick his father had taught him without meaning to: *a lie stands best on legs of truth.* He had been in the building. It had rained. Hetta had scolded him. Only the shape of the evening — the order of events, the hour he'd climbed to the archive shelf — had been rearranged, and a rearranged truth is the hardest thing in the world to catch.

Hale watched him for a long moment. Somewhere below, a hatch cover slammed; gulls rose in a ragged white scatter past the window.

"You were fond of Maren Voss," Hale said.

There it was. The second hook, baited and dropped quiet, and Ansel felt his heart begin the old hammering and folded it away, folded it down into the green dark where it belonged.

"Everyone was fond of Maren Voss," he said. "She mended nets better than the net-mender. She could gut a herring faster than boys twice her size. Half the town cried at her father's funeral, and she wasn't even in the ground yet herself when—" He stopped. Not abruptly. Gently, the way you stop when a memory presses on a bruise, and let the silence do the work. "She drowned, sir. I helped drag the point for three days looking for her. I found her shoe. If someone's been reading her file out of turn, I'd guess it's grief, not theft. Grief makes people do odd things with paper."

It was a beautiful answer and he knew it even as he gave it, and knowing made him sick. It offered Hale a motive — grief, diffuse, town-wide, untraceable to anyone. It reminded Hale that Ansel had suffered too, publicly, verifiably. And underneath it ran a blade of accusation pointed anywhere but at himself. *Grief makes people do odd things.* Let the director go looking among the mourners of Saltmere. There were hundreds.

Hale's hand lifted from the folder. For the first time he sat, settling into his chair with the creak of good leather, and steepled his fingers.

"Your indenture concludes in one year," he said. "Next autumn. Do you remember the terms?"

"Freedom dues of eight crowns and my diving kit. Provided no breach of conduct is recorded."

"No breach of conduct," Hale repeated softly. "You've kept a clean book for twelve years, Eastwick. Twelve years is a long time to keep anything clean in Saltmere. I'd hate to see the last year spoil it. One irregularity, properly recorded, and the clock begins again. You know that. Everyone knows that. It's why nobody steals from us — not because they're honest, but because they're arithmetic." He smiled then, fully, and it was worse than the not-smiling. "I'm telling you this as information, not accusation. Because the file will be reviewed, and reviews find things, and sometimes the things they find belong to people who merely *stood nearby*. You understand me?"

"I understand you, sir."

"Good." Hale drew the folder toward himself and squared it again to the edge of the blotter, a small precise violence. "Return to your beds. Dive well. And Eastwick—" Ansel paused at the door. "If you should happen to hear anything — gossip, tavern talk, a stranger asking questions — a man named Corliss arrived on yesterday's packet and has been remarkably curious for a mail-carrier — you'll bring it to me. Discreetly. There's a crown in it for useful ears, and more than a crown in it for loyal ones."

"I'll listen, sir."

"I know you will," Hale said. "Listening is what you're for."

Ansel inclined his head, closed the door behind him with the softest click, and walked the length of the corridor on legs that felt borrowed. At the stairhead, young Pellwick glanced up from his copying, met Ansel's eyes for half a heartbeat — a flicker of something, curiosity or pity or simple appetite — and looked back down at his page.

Ansel descended into the salt air and did not exhale fully until he reached the water.

* * *

The eastern shoal took him under at half past eleven, and for forty minutes the world was simple.

Down there there was only the rope in his fist, the burn of the pressure in his ears, the pearl beds opening below him like gray flowers. His fingers knew the work without him. Find the shell, feel its weight, twist free, into the bag. Surface on the pull. Breathe. Go down again. The sea held no directors, no folders squared to blotters, no questions shaped like kindnesses. It only held the oldest law there was: come up or drown.

Twice, hanging in the green half-light between breaths, he let himself think about what Hale had actually said — stripped of the velvet, laid bare like a gutted fish.

*Reviews find things.* Which meant the file had been missed, or meant to be missed, or meant to be found missing by someone else. Which meant either that Hale suspected him and lacked proof, or that Hale suspected nothing and was casting a wide net over the whole harbor to see what thrashed. The damp pages — that was the wound. Someone had noticed the damp. Ansel turned the fact over in the cold water, examining it from all sides the way he examined a shell for flaws. Who counted the archive? Merrick, the old under-clerk, half-blind and devoted to his lists. And who assisted Merrick since spring?

Pellwick.

The boy with the quick pen and the glance at the stairhead.

Ansel surfaced, spat salt, handed up his bag, and went down again. Forty weight of shell by dusk. He filled it. His shoulders burned, his lungs tasted of iron, and through all of it the performance continued — the steady hands, the mild face, the jokes with Tam Ossler on the deck of the tender, the easy slouch walking home along the quay as the lamps were lit. He waved to the night-watchman. He bought a heel of cheese from Brann's cousin's stall and remarked on the weather. Every step of it performed, every word of it placed like a stone in a wall, and behind the wall something pacing, something counting the days.

One year on his indenture. One year, and Hale had just told him — smiling — how easily it could become twelve again.

* * *

Hetta had supper waiting: barley soup, dark bread, a candle burned halfway down because she refused to waste light on a man she claimed ate like a seagull. She watched him across the table with her sharp gray eyes while he told her about the day — the beds, the quota, the price of cheese — and left out, smoothly, entirely, the morning upstairs.

"You saw the director today," she said.

It was not a question. Ansel's spoon paused over the bowl. "News travels."

"News travels faster than ships in this town. The boy who carried the note told the fishwife's girl, who told the cooper, who told me before noon." She tore her bread, unhurried. "Irregularities, they're saying. In the counting house."

"Tavern talk."

"Mm." She ate. Outside, wind worried the shutters; rain was coming again off the Serran water. "My husband was summoned to that office once. Thirty-one years ago. Same words — *irregularities*. He'd shorted a manifest by accident, a barrel of tar, nothing. He confessed it inside five minutes because he was an honest man and thought honesty was protection." Her spoon clicked against the bowl. "They doubled his term for the confession. Honest men make the best examples, Ansel. That's the whole secret of Meridian. They don't punish guilt. They punish *usefulness*."

Ansel looked at her then, really looked, and saw that she knew. Not everything — perhaps not even much — but enough. Enough to be sitting at this table saying the word *confession* with the weight of thirty-one years behind it, watching him the way she watched weather coming in off the marshes.

"Hetta—"

"Eat your soup," she said. "A man who lies well needs his strength." And when he went very still, she only refilled his bowl, calm as a lighthouse, and added, so quietly it was nearly lost under the wind: "Whatever it is you're carrying, boy, carry it lower. The clerk who took your tally signature Tuesday — he read it twice. Men don't read their own tallies twice."

She stood, gathered the bowls, and left him sitting in the candlelight with the blood gone cold in his arms.

Through the shutter-crack he could see the harbor, black now, the packet boat riding at anchor with her riding lamp burning steady — the same packet that had brought Corliss, the same packet that in some few days' time, if the plans held, would carry more than mail. And somewhere behind him in the dark of the town, in a room smelling of ink, a young man with a quick pen was writing down the hours of Ansel Eastwick's evenings, and getting paid by the entry.

One year on his indenture. It suddenly seemed a very long time, and a very short one, and both at once far too little.

Upstairs, Ansel lay awake listening to the rain, doing what he did best.

Keeping silence. And learning, finally, what it weighed.
