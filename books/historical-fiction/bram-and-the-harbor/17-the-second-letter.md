## 17. The Second Letter

Corliss came at the hour when the counting house emptied and the harbor turned the color of pewter, when the clerks went home to their suppers and the gulls settled into their argument over the fish offal. Ansel was sweeping the loft — a task he did not need to do but performed anyway because it gave his hands something honest while his mind worked at the problem of Brann and the harvest — when the floorboards below him creaked in a rhythm he had learned to know.

Three steps, pause. Three steps, pause.

Corliss walked like a man who had spent years measuring rooms for exits.

Ansel set the broom against the wall and went down the narrow stair. The counting house smelled of ink and lamp oil and old paper, that dry sweetness of ledgers that would outlive everyone who had written in them. Corliss stood by the tall front windows with his hat in his hand, looking out at the masts as though he were a man idling away an evening. He was not idle. He had never once been idle. Even standing still, Corliss seemed to be counting — boats, men, minutes.

"You shouldn't come here," Ansel said quietly. "Not twice in one week."

"Twice in one week is nothing." Corliss turned. In the fading light his face looked older than it had on the quay, the lines around his eyes carved deeper, like channels worn into stone by water that never stopped running. "I've called on men every day for a fortnight without any of them knowing I existed. That's the trade. But tonight I'm not calling. Tonight I'm delivering."

He held out an envelope.

It was small. Cheap paper, the kind sold loose from the chandler's shop for a farthing, the kind fishermen used to write home from ports they'd drunk through. It was sealed with plain wax, unmarked. And across its face, in ink gone slightly brown where the sea air had touched it, was a single line:

*For Ansel Eastwick.*

He knew the hand before he knew what he was reading. He knew it the way you know a voice through a wall, the way your body knows weather before your mind does. The *A* leaned hard to the right, as if the letter itself were walking into wind. The crossbar of the *t* slanted upward, always upward, impatient even in ink. The *k* in Eastwick had a little flourish at the end of its leg, a small unnecessary beauty, because Maren Voss had never been able to write anything without making it lovely, even a laundry list, even a lie if she'd ever been forced to tell one.

His hands did not move.

"Take it," Corliss said.

"I—" Ansel heard his own voice come out strange, flattened. "Where did this come from?"

"It doesn't matter."

"It matters."

Corliss considered him for a long moment. Outside, the lamplighter was making his rounds along the quay, and his flame passed behind Corliss's shoulder like a small captive sun. "It came from her," Corliss said at last. "That's all you're entitled to ask, and more than most get. Take it. Read it. Then decide what you are."

Ansel took it.

The paper weighed nothing. That was the terrible thing about it — how little it weighed. Twelve years of debt had weight; the wreck file hidden beneath his mattress had weight; the fear that lived now in his chest like a second heartbeat had weight enough to bend him. This was nothing. A few grams of rag paper and cheap wax. And his hands shook holding it as though it were made of lead and lit fuse.

He broke the seal.

* * *

*Ansel,*

*I have started this letter eleven times. Brann tells me paper is dear where we're going, so I'll spend it plainly.*

*You don't owe me anything. I want to say that first, before anything else, because whatever Corliss has told you — and he will have told you only what serves the work, it's how he keeps himself alive — I don't want you deciding anything out of an old promise made to a girl under a breakwater in peacetime. That girl is six years drowned. What's left is harder and less pretty and asks more than she ever would have.*

*But I read your name in Brann's report and I sat down on the deck like a fool and cried until the mate asked me if I was ill. So I won't pretend to be only the courier's principal, only a name on a manifest. You were my whole childhood, Ansel Eastwick. You taught me which mussels were safe and which would poison you. You held my hair back when the fever came. You promised me the tide would bring me home and I believed you, and then the tide brought me somewhere else entirely, and I stopped believing in promises because believing got people killed.*

*The documents you sent — I've read them four times. I knew Meridian scuttled her. Knowing and proving are different countries and you have built me a bridge between them. Hale signed the order himself; the insurance claim matches the survey dates; there's a clerk's initials on the margin who is still in Saltmere and can be made to swear it. Do you understand what you've done? Six years I have carried this alone, telling myself the proof didn't exist or had rotted, and you reached into the company's own heart and pulled it out. You, who they own. You, who kept your head down so well that no one remembers you have eyes.*

*Don't do anything else. That's the second thing I want to say. The file you copied is enough to begin. When I come ashore for the rest — and I will come ashore, Brann is arranging it — I want you nowhere near the quay that night. Stay in your bed. Lock your door. Let the work be mine; I've been paid in grief already and I'm owed.*

*Except.*

*Except that I know you, and I know you will ignore this paragraph entirely, and some small rotten part of me wrote it only so that I could imagine you reading it and disobeying.*

*The third thing. There is no gentle way to put it. If you help me past this point, you are not a diver anymore. You are a man the Crown has a name for. They hanged a man in Kingsport last spring for less than what you've already done. I need you to look at that plainly, the way you look at deep water, and decide with your eyes open whether you can hold your breath that long.*

*And the fourth thing, the thing I swore I wouldn't write, and here is the candle burning down and me writing it anyway:*

*I loved you. At sixteen, at seventeen, in the way you love someone when you don't yet know that loving is a thing you survive. I loved you and I sailed away and I let the sea take my name because it was safer than keeping yours. Every hard thing I have learned to do since, I learned by pretending you were dead too. It worked. It worked for six years. And then a smuggler handed me a scrap of paper with your handwriting on it — just your name, Ansel, just three syllables in that careful hand of yours — and everything I had buried stood up out of the grave and started walking.*

*Come to the breakwater. Not now. Not yet. But when this is finished — however it finishes — come to the breakwater, and I will be there if I'm alive, and if I'm not alive then I'll have died trying to keep a promise I told you not to make.*

*The tide turns both ways. I used to think that meant nothing. I think now it might mean everything.*

*M.V.*

*P.S. — Your handwriting has gotten worse. All those years writing figures instead of words. I could read it anyway. I could always read you.*

* * *

He read it standing in the dark of the counting house, because he had not moved to light a lamp, because moving felt dangerous, as though the letter might change its mind and fly out the window like a bird if he shifted his weight. He read it a second time by feel, the words already half memorized, the shapes of them printed on the inside of his eyelids. Then Corliss struck a match and lit the desk lamp without asking, and the yellow light filled the room, and Ansel read it a third time properly, aloud in his head, hearing her voice in every line — the way she said *plainly*, the way she argued with herself mid-sentence, the way she ended things before they could end her.

"You've read it?" he said finally. His voice sounded scraped.

"No one reads another man's letter," Corliss said. "Unless he's paid to. I wasn't."

"She says stay away from the quay."

"She says a great many things." Corlliss lowered himself onto the corner of the clerk's desk, unhurried, a man settling in for weather. "She also says *come to the breakwater*. She also says she loved you. Women write letters the way the sea moves — several currents in the same body of water. You learn to navigate or you drown."

"She wants me safe."

"She wants you hers," Corliss said, not unkindly. "Those aren't the same, but they overlap more than either party usually admits." He studied Ansel across the lamplight. "How many times have you read it?"

"Three."

"You'll read it a dozen. Everyone does. First letter after a resurrection — it's scripture. You'll find new sentences in it for days. Sentences you'd swear weren't there the first time." He paused. "They weren't. That's how it works."

Ansel folded the letter along its original creases, precisely, the way he did everything, and then unfolded it again immediately because he could not bear to have it closed. "Why did she write to me? Why now?"

"Because you sent the copies. Because Brann reported your name. Because—" Corliss stopped himself, and Ansel watched him weigh something, watched the scales move behind his eyes. "Because I told her I thought you'd come further. That's between us, that opinion. She may yet prove me wrong."

"And if I refuse? If I take her advice and lock my door?"

"Then she comes ashore with fewer friends than she needs, and the odds go from bad to arithmetic." Corliss stood and retrieved his hat. "I'm not here to beg. I've done this work too long to beg. I'm here because she asked me to deliver a letter, and because once — a long time ago, in a country you've never seen — someone delivered a letter to me, and it ruined my life, and I've been grateful ever since." He set the hat on his head. "Read it twelve times, Eastwick. Then sleep on it. Brann will want his answer within two days, and Brann's answers have a way of becoming prices."

He let himself out. The door clicked shut behind him with the softness of a man who had entered and exited ten thousand rooms without anyone remembering his face.

* * *

Ansel did not remember climbing back to the loft.

He was there, though, sitting on his cot with the lamp he kept for mending nets turned low, the letter open on his knee, and the harbor sounds coming up through the floorboards — the slap of water against pilings, the far bell of the point buoy, the creak of the Meridian warehouse swallowing the cold. He read it again. He read the fourth paragraph and skipped the fifth and went back and read the fifth. He read the postscript four times, because the postscript was unbearable, because the postscript was her, wholly her, teasing him across six years and an ocean as though no time had passed at all, as though drowning had been a season abroad rather than a death.

*Your handwriting has gotten worse.*

He looked down at his own hand, at the diver's calluses and the ink stains, at the fingers that had spent twelve years recording other men's numbers. He thought of all the things he had written in that time. Manifests. Receipts. His name in the debt ledger, quarterly, the interest recalculated in a clerk's neat hand above his signature. Thousands of lines of figures and not one sentence worth keeping. Not one line that anyone would read a dozen times.

*I could always read you.*

Outside, the moon was rising over the point, laying a silver road across the harbor water, and Ansel understood suddenly why sailors called it that — because every man who saw it wanted to walk it, wanted it to lead somewhere other than where it led, and the wanting was the whole point. The road went nowhere. The wanting went everywhere.

He read the letter a fifth time. A sixth. Somewhere in the seventh reading he began to cry — silently, the way he did everything, tears running down into the collar of his shirt while his face stayed perfectly composed, the skill of a boy who had learned at eight years old that grief made noise and noise drew attention. He wept for the girl under the breakwater with salt drying white on her shoulders. He wept for the woman on the schooner who had learned to bury him alive. He wept for the eleven false starts of that letter, for the candle burning down, for *here is the candle burning down and me writing it anyway* — because he knew exactly what that cost her. He knew what it cost to spend something you couldn't replace.

A ninth time. A tenth.

By the eleventh reading he was no longer crying. By the eleventh reading something else had happened, something quieter and more dangerous, the way the worst weather arrives quietest. He was reading it now not as a letter but as a map. *There's a clerk's initials on the margin who is still in Saltmere.* He knew whose initials those were — Pellworth, junior clerk, careful, ambitious, frightened of Hale the way small dogs are frightened of boots. *Brann is arranging it.* A date would come. A signal. And Brann, Brann would want payment, and Ansel's quota was short, and the pearl beds beyond the shelf held pearls enough if a man were willing to dive depths that squeezed the breath out of him—

He stopped.

He caught himself doing it — turning her love into logistics, her ache into arithmetic — and understood that this was what twelve years in the counting house had made of him. Even this. Even her. He almost laughed.

Then he read the twelfth time, slowly, and let himself simply feel it, letting it hurt the way a broken bone hurts, clean and total. *Everything I had buried stood up out of the grave and started walking.* He knew the feeling. It was happening to him now, in a loft above a counting house owned by the men who had tried to kill her.

Below, footsteps on the quay — a constable's measured beat, passing, fading. Ansel blew out the lamp. In the dark, the letter was a pale rectangle on his knee, and he pressed his palm flat against it as though taking a pulse.

Two days. Brann would want his answer in two days, and Brann's answers had a way of becoming prices.

Ansel lay back on the cot with the letter against his chest and stared at the black beams overhead, and began — carefully, methodically, the way he planned a dive — to calculate how many pearls the deep water beyond the shelf might hold, and what a man's life was worth per fathom, and whether the sea would accept the payment he was about to offer.

He did not sleep at all.
