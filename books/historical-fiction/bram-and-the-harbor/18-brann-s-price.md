## 18. Brann's Price

The tide was going out when Corliss came for him, and it took the light with it.

Ansel had spent the morning in the shallows off Gull Spit, working the easy beds with the other divers, letting his hands do the work while his mind turned over the letter folded against his chest. *To Ansel Eastwick, who will know my hand.* He had read it a dozen times by lantern light and another dozen times in daylight behind the cover of a ledger, and each reading left him more certain of two things: that Maren Voss was alive, and that she remembered him. Both certainties were dangerous. Both were also, he was beginning to understand, no longer things he could set down.

Corliss found him at the rack where the divers hung their wet suits to dry. The stranger wore his fisherman's coat like a man who had learned to wear whatever a place required of him, and he stood looking out at the water as though he had all the time in the world.

"Brann wants to see you," he said quietly. "Tonight."

"Where?"

"The old salt house past the marsh cut. You know it?"

Ansel knew it. Everyone in Saltmere knew it — a rotted-beam ruin from the days before Meridian's charter, when the harbor had belonged to whoever could float a boat. Nobody went there now except boys daring each other and gulls.

"What does he want?"

Corliss's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "What men like Brann always want. He wants to talk about money."

* * *

Ansel told Hetta he was walking the spit to clear his head, which was true enough in its way, and she looked at him over her mending with those pale eyes that missed nothing and said only, "Wear your coat. The wind's coming around." He wondered, not for the first time since the night on her stairs, whether her kindness was cover or warning. He put on his coat either way.

The marsh path was treacherous in the dark — mud that grabbed at his boots, channels that gleamed black where they shouldn't be, the smell of rot and salt thick enough to taste. Twice he stopped and listened. Once he heard nothing but the wind combing the reeds. Once he heard something that might have been a footstep and might have been his own heart.

The salt house rose out of the dark like the ribcage of some drowned animal, its roof half gone, its walls leaning together as if conspiring. A single shuttered lantern burned inside, and the light leaked through the slats in thin gold lines.

"You're punctual." The voice came from the shadows near the far wall, low and gravelled, the voice of a man accustomed to being obeyed quickly or not at all. "I like punctual. Punctual means careful, and careful means you might live long enough to be useful."

Brann stepped into the lantern light, and Ansel got his first good look at him. He was shorter than Ansel expected, broad through the shoulders, with a face weathered to the color of old rope and eyes that moved constantly — to the door, to Ansel's hands, to the dark beyond the broken window — never resting anywhere longer than a breath. A scar split one eyebrow. His hands were enormous and looked gentle, which Ansel knew from experience was the most dangerous combination a man could have.

"You're the diver," Brann said. It wasn't a question. "Maren's letter says you're the best in Saltmere. Says you can hold your breath longer than any man she ever saw, and that you once brought up eleven pearls in a single day when the company quota was six."

"That was a good day."

"Don't joke with me, boy." There was no heat in it, which made it worse. "Sit."

There was an upturned crate near the lantern. Ansel sat. Brann remained standing, circling slowly, and Ansel realized he was being examined the way a buyer examines a horse — teeth, legs, temperament.

"Here's where we are," Brann said. "Your friend Corliss has been carrying letters back and forth like a schoolboy passing notes, and that's fine for letters. But what Maren needs now is weight. Paper. Proof. She tells me there's a file in the Meridian counting house that proves they scuttled her father's ship, and she tells me you've seen it."

"I've seen part of it."

"She tells me you can get the rest." Brann stopped circling and faced him directly. "And I'm telling you that getting it isn't free. Nothing is free. Not my boat, not my crew, not the risk we take running papers for a woman the Crown has named an enemy agent. Do you understand what happens to me if we're caught? They don't hang smugglers anymore, mostly. They press them. There's a hulk off the coast where men go in and don't come out, and nobody asks questions because smugglers are the one kind of criminal everyone's happy to lose."

Ansel said nothing. In the counting house he had learned that silence invited men to fill it, and fill it they did.

Brann seemed to appreciate the silence. He nodded slowly, as if Ansel had passed some small test. "So. The price." He held up one huge finger. "First: the file. All of it. Every page, every entry, every signature. Not copies — originals. Copies can be forged. Originals hang men." A second finger. "Second: you get it yourself. No intermediaries. If Corliss goes into that counting house, he's a stranger and strangers get noticed. You're the indentured boy who's been in and out of those offices for twelve years. You're furniture. Furniture doesn't get noticed." A third finger, and here Brann paused, and his restless eyes settled on Ansel's face and stayed there. "Third. Payment for services rendered. My fee. For the boat runs, the crew, the risk, and the standing between Maren's people and the Crown's people. That comes due when she sails. And it comes due in coin."

"How much?"

Brann named a figure.

Ansel felt the number land in his chest like a stone dropped down a well. He heard it strike bottom and echo. It was more than he earned in four years. It was more than he would earn in the year remaining on his indenture, even if the company paid him fairly, which the company did not.

"That's not possible," he said.

"Everything's possible." Brann crouched down so their eyes were level, and up close Ansel could see the fine network of broken veins across his nose, the salt-cracked skin of a man who had spent forty years on cold water. "That's the first lesson of this trade. Everything is possible. The question is never whether. The question is what you'll pay."

"I don't have it. I won't have it. My indenture—"

"I know about your indenture." Brann waved it away like smoke. "Meridian owns your debt until next spring. I know. I make it my business to know who owes whom in every harbor I work." He straightened up and walked to the broken window, looking out at the black marsh. "But you're forgetting something, diver. You're forgetting what you're sitting on."

"There's nothing. I own nothing. That's rather the point of an indenture."

"You own your hands," Brann said. "And your hands are worth more than you think. Maren's letter didn't exaggerate. Eleven pearls in a day. I've been in this trade twenty years and I've bought pearls off divers in six ports, and I know what a man like you is worth in a season." He turned back. "The harvest. The autumn beds open in three weeks. Meridian takes its quota, but every diver skims — I know that too, don't insult us both by denying it. A man with your skill, diving without a company clerk watching the tally, diving deep where the big shells sit..." He let it hang.

Ansel's stomach tightened. "The deep beds kill men."

"The deep beds pay men," Brann corrected. "There's a difference, and the difference is usually how badly a man needs the money." He crouched again, and this time his voice dropped, lost its edge, became almost — almost — kind. "Listen to me, boy. I've watched a hundred men stand where you're standing. Men who owe too much and love too much and have too little time. Some of them walk away, and I don't think worse of them. Walking away is the sane thing. But the ones who stay — the ones who pay my price — they always ask me the same question, so I'll save you the trouble. You want to know if it's worth it. Whether a woman, a promise, a piece of paper, is worth what it's going to cost you." He tapped his own chest, twice, over the heart. "I can't answer that. Nobody can. But I'll tell you what I tell them all: the sea doesn't care why you dive. It only cares how long you stay down."

* * *

Ansel walked back along the marsh path with Brann's number burning in his head like a coal he'd swallowed.

Three weeks. The autumn harvest opened with the neap tides, and the company would work the shallow beds hard for a month — every diver, every day, quotas raised because the season was short and the London buyers were impatient. During the harvest, the clerks counted everything. Every shell, every pearl, every hour. Skimming during harvest was theft, and theft by an indentured man was not a fine. It was the gallows, or the press gang, or — if Hale was feeling merciful, and Hale was never feeling merciful — another ten years added to the debt.

And the deep beds. The outer banks beyond the safe line, where the water went from green to black and the pressure squeezed a man's chest like a fist and the cold reached into the bones and stayed there. Old Tam Breech had dived the deep beds for three seasons and come up rich and come up dead, both, in that order. Ansel's own father had drowned in water barely deeper than a man is tall. The sea didn't care why you dove. It only cared how long you stayed down.

He climbed the path to Hetta's house and stood outside in the dark for a long moment, looking at the lit window, at the shadow of her moving behind the curtain, and he thought: *she lost a son to this town, to this company, to this water, and she still opens her door every night.* He didn't know yet what that meant. He only knew it steadied him, the way a fixed light steadies a boat.

Inside, Hetta looked up from her mending. Her eyes went over him the way they always did — quick, thorough, missing nothing.

"You've been talking to someone who frightened you," she said.

Ansel sat down across from her. The fire ticked. Somewhere down the street a dog barked twice and gave up.

"Hetta," he said, "how much would a man get for pearls? Good ones. Sold private, not through the company."

She didn't ask why. That was the thing about Hetta; she never asked why, which meant either that she didn't care or that she already knew, and Ansel had begun to suspect it was the second. She set down her mending and considered him for a long moment.

"A really fine pearl — round, lustrous, the size of a pea or better — might bring thirty crowns from the right buyer. More in the city. Less if the seller looks desperate, and sellers always look desperate." She picked the mending back up. "A dozen fine pearls would buy a boat. Two dozen would buy a life. Why?"

"No reason."

"Mm." The needle flashed. "The autumn beds open soon."

"They do."

"The deep beds too."

Ansel looked at her sharply. She didn't look up from her sewing.

"Everyone knows the deep beds pay best," she said mildly. "Everyone also knows they kill. My Thomas—" the needle paused, just for a beat, then resumed, "—my Thomas wanted to try them, the year he died. I talked him out of it. He drowned in the shallows instead, on an ordinary Tuesday, in water he'd swum since he was five." She bit the thread and tied it off. "So I don't know what the lesson is, Ansel. Maybe there isn't one. Maybe the sea just takes what it takes and the rest is arithmetic." She gathered her mending into its basket and stood. "Go to sleep. You look like a man who's going to need his strength."

* * *

He did not sleep. He lay in his narrow room under the eaves and listened to the wind come around the way Hetta had promised, and he did the arithmetic she had named.

Brann's price. Three weeks of harvest. The deep beds. Eleven pearls in a day, Maren had written, and Brann had repeated it like scripture, but that had been a good day in the shallows, in green water, with the sun overhead and the boats close by. The deep beds gave perhaps two hours of usable light and water, and the shells there were bigger but scarcer, and the cold came up from below like a living thing with intentions.

He thought about the letter hidden under his floorboard. *I have not forgotten the breakwater,* she had written. *I have not forgotten anything.* Six years she had carried that. Six years of being dead, of being hunted, of building a new self out of whatever materials exile offered, and through all of it she had kept his name the way you keep a seed in a dry pocket — not planted, not discarded, just kept.

Against that, he had a number. Brann's number. And a deadline. And a body that was, he admitted to himself honestly, lying in the dark, the only asset he had ever truly owned.

He got up before dawn, as he always did, and went down to the water.

The harbor lay gray and still, the tide slack, the boats sleeping at their moorings. Out past the point, past the safe line marked by the leaning stake, the deep beds waited under thirty feet of cold green-black water, and somewhere beneath them, in the dark silt, the shells sat with their slow-made treasures, indifferent to everything — to Meridian, to the Crown, to debts and promises and the shortness of human lives.

Ansel stood at the edge of the quay and filled his lungs and timed his heart, the way his father had taught him when he was seven years old, standing in the shallows: *breathe slow, boy, breathe slow, the sea rewards the patient.*

Three weeks. He had three weeks to pull a fortune out of the bottom of the sea, or watch the only person who had ever kept a promise to him sail away unavenged, carrying proof that would die in her hands for lack of weight behind it.

The sun came up over the harbor wall, and the first bell of the harvest season rang from the company tower, and Ansel Eastwick turned toward the counting house to sign the harvest rolls — and to learn, once and for all, exactly where the deep beds' biggest shells were marked on the company charts.

He did not yet know that the junior clerk, Pell, was watching him from the window above, and writing down the time.
