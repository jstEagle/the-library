## 19. The Deep Dive

The charts were wrong.

Ansel had known it since the moment he'd copied them two nights before — known it the way a diver knows a shell is empty before his fingers close on it, some animal arithmetic beneath thought. The company surveyors had marked the deep beds off the Gullet in tidy blue circles, forty fathoms, they said, a diver's graveyard annotated with the casual confidence of men who had never put their heads under water. But Ansel had been diving these waters since he was small enough to fit in a coracle, and he knew that the Gullet's floor did not fall away evenly. It stepped down. There was a ledge at thirty-two fathoms where the old shell beds had once run fat and endless, before Meridian's boats worked them to bone — and past the ledge, the bottom dropped into cold black nothing where no sane man followed.

The shells he needed would be at the edge of the ledge. Or below it.

He told himself, rowing out in the gray hour before dawn with the mist lying on the harbor like a held breath, that he would only look. That was the bargain he struck with himself, the same bargain every diver in Saltmere struck when the quota came due and the beds ran thin: *I will only go down and see.* The seeing was never the dangerous part. The dangerous part was what a man decided at the bottom, alone, with his lungs already half spent and the shells glittering at the edge of the dark like coins scattered by a careless god.

The Gullet lay two miles past the harbor mouth, where the headland split the tide and the water ran green-black even on bright days. Ansel shipped his oars above the bed and let the coracle drift, and for a long moment he simply looked down.

He had loved this once. It was important, in the hour before he might die, to remember that he had loved it — that there had been a boy crouched on these same rocks with his father's spare goggles strapped over his face, breathing through a reed in the shallows, certain that the sea was the kindest of his parents because it was the only one that ever let him come home. His father had taught him the first rule of the deep before any other: *the water doesn't hate you, lad. It doesn't love you either. It just is. You're the one bringing the wanting down with you.*

His father had gone down off this very bed, eleven years ago, chasing a shell worth more than a year of the family's debt. They had brought up his body at low tide, his hands still closed, empty.

Ansel stripped to his wool undershirt, checked the stone weight on his line, checked the knife at his belt, checked the mesh bag that would hold perhaps sixty good shells if the day were kind. Then he sat on the gunwale and breathed.

Slow. Deeper than slow. He pulled air down into himself the way his father had shown him, filling from the bottom as if his lungs were a jar being dipped into a cask, and he counted. Twenty breaths. Thirty. His heart slowed until he could feel each beat arrive separately, deliberate as a clerk stamping a ledger. At the top of the fortieth breath he packed his lungs full, tipped forward, and let the stone take him down.

* * *

The water went from green to gray to a darkness like closing eyes.

Thirty fathoms. The pressure found his ears and squeezed; he equalized, pinching his nose, and felt the small pops travel up through his skull like distant signals. The light thinned to a color that had no name, a dusk that existed nowhere in the world above. Below him the ledge appeared out of the murk, pale and scalloped, rimmed with dead coral the color of old teeth.

He turned along it, pulling himself hand over hand down the guide line, and the shells began.

Not enough. Not here. The bed had been worked ragged — he could read the story in the bottom the way clerks read their ledgers, the diver-marks of a hundred harvests scored into the silt, the broken shells discarded by men in a hurry, the whole shelf picked clean with the thorough, joyless efficiency of everything Meridian touched. A few shells huddled in the crevices. He took them. Sixteen. Seventeen. Not a tenth of what Brann demanded.

The ledge ended.

Beyond its lip the bottom fell away, and Ansel hung there at the edge of it, thirty-two fathoms down, his chest beginning its first quiet argument against him, and looked into the dark.

It was not truly dark. Down there the water held a faint luminescence, a ghost-gray suggestion of floor far below, and the shells on the deeper slope caught even that little light and gave it back — he could see them, scattered thick across the drop-off where no diver bothered to follow, hundreds of them, a fortune lying in the cold. Of course they were there. Everything valuable in Saltmere lay somewhere difficult, guarded by depth or debt or the company's lawyers.

*I will only look,* he told himself again, and heard the lie in it, and went over the edge anyway.

* * *

The cold came first, a cold that pressed through wool and skin and found the marrow, a cold with weight behind it. Then the pressure found his chest and began its real work — not pain, exactly, at first, but a slow gathering compression, as if the sea had set its hands flat against his ribs and was leaning. Forty fathoms. The guide line ran out through his grip. He had tied a red rag at forty-five; his father's rule, older than any company regulation, the mark past which the ledger of a man's life balanced badly.

The bottom rose toward him out of the gloom and he leveled off above it, hovering like a spirit over ground it has no right to walk, and began to gather shells.

They were beautiful. That was the obscenity of it. Each one sat in the silt with its nacre turned upward, holding that last gray light, and his numb fingers pried them loose and fed them into the bag and his mind kept counting against his will — twenty-two, twenty-three — while his chest counted too, a different arithmetic, the burn building behind his sternum like a fire being banked coal by coal. Thirty shells. The diaphragm began to jump, small involuntary spasms, the body's oldest alarm, the bell that rang in every drowning man's skull: *breathe now, breathe now, breathe now.* He knew the trick of it. You didn't fight the bell. You acknowledged it, thanked it, sent it back below. Thirty-four. Thirty-six.

The red rag on the line was somewhere above him. He had lost track of how far.

Forty-one shells. Enough — more than enough, nearly half a fortune in a bag, and the sensible voice in him, the voice of Hetta and Corliss and everyone who needed him alive, said *now*, and he reached for the line.

And saw the shell.

It lay a body's length beyond him, apart from all the others, larger than any pearl shell he had ever taken from these waters, sitting alone in the silt with its lip flared open like a hand extended. In the gray light it seemed almost to be lit from inside. Twelve years of diving and he had never seen its like, and some part of him that was not greedy but only curious, only faithful to the boy with the reed and the goggles, wanted it — wanted to bring up one thing from the deep that was not owed to anyone.

His vision had begun to narrow. He registered it distantly, clinically: the edges going soft, the tunnel forming, his heartbeat now audible in his ears like surf. He should have been rising. Every second he hung there the sea took another portion of his air and gave back nothing.

He went for the shell anyway.

His fingers closed on it. And in the same instant his foot caught in something — a snag of cable, old netting, some rope-rotted relic of a drowned boat — and when he wrenched free his hand opened, and the shell tumbled away down the slope, and he lunged after it without deciding to, pure want dressed as reflex, and the movement cost him.

The spasm hit mid-reach.

It was not like the warning twitches. This was his body seizing the tiller entire, the great muscles of his chest clamping down hard as a fist, demanding air that wasn't there, and he got nothing — a dry, collapsing effort, lungs empty, throat shut, and suddenly he understood with a clarity that felt almost peaceful that he had misjudged the distance. That he had, in the ledger's language, overdrawn.

The dark at the edges of his vision stopped being an edge and became a tide.

He grabbed the line. His hands were clumsy, faraway things. He kicked for the surface and the kick moved him the way a dream moves a runner, vast effort, small result, and the pressure leaned on his chest harder, delighted, and somewhere very far above him there was a surface, there was air and morning and Hetta's kettle and Maren's slanted handwriting, and none of it could reach him down here where the only law was the law of the water, which did not hate him, did not love him, only weighed.

*Breathe now,* said the bell, and this time there was no sending it back.

He thought, with the strange tidiness of a drowning mind: *so this is where Father was.* Not terror at the end but recognition, a son arriving late at the room where his father had stood, understanding at last why the dead man's hands had been closed. Not clutching at pearls. Clutching at the wanting itself, refusing to let the sea have the last of it.

*No,* Ansel answered the water. It was not a word so much as a contraction of everything left in him, gathered and aimed. *Not here. Not yet. I have a promise to keep.*

He hauled on the line. His vision was a coin now, a shrinking silver disc with the dim brightness of the surface at its center, and he climbed it hand over hand, and the spasms wracked him and he rode them, and the cold let go of his limbs degree by degree as he rose, and the disc widened, widened —

He broke the surface into a world so loud and bright it was violence. Air tore into him in great ratcheting gulps and he hung on the coracle's gunwale and coughed salt and shook and breathed, breathed, breathed, each breath arriving like a gift he had not earned, and the morning sky wheeled overhead, pale and indifferent and impossibly beautiful.

* * *

He did not remember climbing aboard. He came back to himself in the bottom of the coracle with the sun well clear of the headland — he had been under far longer than he'd meant, or unconscious longer than he'd know — and the mesh bag beside him, heavy. He lifted it with shaking hands and counted by touch. Forty-one shells, plus the one that had come up clenched in his fist after all, though he had no memory of retrieving it. Forty-two. Nearly half of what Brann asked.

The largest shell sat on top of the pile, its nacre holding the sunlight, and inside it, when he finally worked the hinge, a pearl the size of a thumbnail, gray-violet, perfect.

His father had died within sight of this water. Pell was writing down his comings and goings. Hale was setting his watch. Every shell in the bag was a coin stamped with risk, and the price of the next dive like this one would likely be paid in full.

Ansel lay back against the gunwale and let the coracle rock and looked up until his eyes stung.

The sea had watched him the whole time. He understood that now in a way he hadn't as a boy. It had watched without intervening, the way Saltmere watched, the way Meridian watched, the way the whole world watched a poor man gamble his life for his debts — and it had rendered no verdict except the one he carried up in his own fists. The water didn't judge. It only kept account. What he was owed, what he owed, whether the balance justified the breath — those entries were his to make, and he had made them, and he was still alive to read them.

"Twelve more," he said aloud, to the gulls, to the deep, to whoever kept the ledger. "And then we're done bargaining."

He rowed for shore with his back to the sun, and did not see the skiff that lay to beyond the Gullet's point, nor the man in it lowering a spyglass, nor the way the man consulted a pocket watch before turning for the harbor — where Hale was waiting, patient as tide, to hear what the watcher had seen.
