## 20. Hale's Suspicion

The watch began on a Tuesday, though Ansel would not learn that until much later — would learn it the way one learns the shape of a current, by what it dragged and where.

He rose before dawn as he always did, took his coffee standing at Hetta's kitchen window as he always did, and walked down to the quay through lanes still dark at their edges. The routine was twelve years deep. It had grooves worn into it like the steps of an old stair, and he moved along those grooves without thinking, which was precisely why he noticed when something ran against them.

The boy was mending nets on the fish-house steps.

Ansel had seen him there before — a lean, freckled lad of fifteen or sixteen with quick hands and a habit of watching boats come in. That was nothing. Half the idle boys of Saltmere watched boats. But this morning the boy's eyes came up when Ansel passed, tracked him to the counting house door, and dropped back to the nets only after the latch clicked. And there was something in the tracking that Ansel recognized, because he had done it himself a thousand times: the flat, patient attention of someone paid to notice.

He hung his coat on its peg and stood a moment in the dimness of the divers' room, listening to the building settle. *You are imagining it,* he told himself. *A guilty man sees watchers in every window.* But guilt, he had learned long ago, was not always wrong. Sometimes it was just early.

* * *

Director Hale received the report in his office above the counting house floor, where the windows were thick enough to turn the harbor green and the whole enterprise of Meridian Trading lay beneath him in ordered rows of desks.

"Name?" he said.

"Tullin, sir. Osric Tullin. Second-year clerk, ledgers." The junior clerk stood with his cap in both hands, sweating despite the chill. He was a narrow young man with ink-stained cuffs and the desperate posture of someone who had rehearsed this moment all night. "I wasn't sure I ought to come forward."

"You were right to come." Hale did not look up from the ledger before him. He turned a page. The scratch of his nib was the only sound. "Tell me again. Slowly."

Tullin swallowed. "It was the twelfth, sir. Evening. I'd stayed late on the shipping abstracts — Mr. Quennel wanted them for the morning post — and the floor was empty except for me. Then I heard footsteps in the records gallery. Someone moving among the strong-room files. I thought it might be a rat at first." A weak attempt at a smile; Hale's silence killed it. "I went up. Eastwick was at the cabinet. The wreck registers, sir. V-series. He heard me and closed the drawer, but I saw—" He stopped, steadied himself. "I saw him put something back. Papers. He said he was looking for a bill of lading from the March consignment. For his own quota records."

"Did you believe him?"

"I — I don't know, sir."

"That is an answer." Hale set down his pen at last and looked up. His face was famously mild — a soft, gray, unhurried face, the face of a man who signed things — and it was famously unwise to mistake that mildness for softness. "What did you do afterward?"

"I went back to my desk. I didn't want him to know I'd seen. He passed me on the stairs. He wished me good evening." Tullin's hands worked around the cap. "Sir, I've thought about it every night since. Whether to say anything. Mr. Eastwick is respected here. The divers speak well of him. I kept telling myself there must be some explanation. But then—"

"Then?"

"Then last week I saw him talking to the stranger. Corliss. Down by the breakwater, after dark. Twice." Tullin hesitated. "And the foreign mail has been odd lately. Redirected packets. And there's a man named Brann who runs oysters out of the north cove at strange hours, and—"

Hale raised one finger, no more, and Tullin stopped as if the finger had been a hand across his mouth.

"Osric," Hale said gently. "Do you know what I value most in a clerk?"

"Laccuracy, sir?"

"Punctuality," said Hale. "Accuracy second. Punctuality third." He let the little joke sit a moment, dry as dust. "But beneath all three, discretion. You have shown discretion in coming to me quietly rather than gossiping at the Anchor. That is to your credit, and I do not forget credit. Neither do I forget debts." He opened a drawer, counted out coins, and slid a small purse across the desk. "For the abstracts you stayed late over. Overtime ought to be paid."

Tullin stared at the purse. It held more than a month's wages.

"From now on," Hale continued, "you will keep the shipping abstracts entirely yourself. All of them. You'll work the evening hours — you seem suited to evenings — and you'll note anything irregular. Anything at all. Boat movements. Names. Who speaks to whom." He smiled, mild as milk. "Think of it as accuracy, extended."

"Yes, sir." Tullin took the purse as if it might bite.

"And Osric." Hale returned to his ledger. "Eastwick is a valuable man. Twelve years in the water for us. Whatever he was doing in the files, there may be a simple explanation, and if there is, no harm done and none spoken. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"Not entirely, sir."

"Good," said Hale. "That's the correct answer. Close the door on your way out."

* * *

Hale sat alone for a long while after the clerk had gone, the green light climbing the wall behind him.

He did not believe in simple explanations. In thirty years of charter trade he had found that men's actions were almost never simple and almost never innocent, and that the two facts together made management straightforward. Ansel Eastwick diving into the wreck registers six years after the *Voss* went down — that was not a man hunting a misplaced bill of lading. That was a man who had been asked a question, or had asked himself one, and could not leave it alone.

And questions, in Hale's experience, arrived from outside. They came on mail packets, in the pockets of strangers who asked about tides and shipwrecks in bars. This Corliss person had been in Saltmere five weeks now, lodging at the Anchor under a pretext of buying dried fish that no sane merchant would sustain at his prices. Hale had read the man's papers — adequate forgeries, probably purchased in the Free Ports — and had decided early on to let him move freely. Watched things revealed their connections. Provoked things revealed their purpose too soon.

But Eastwick was different matter. Eastwick was Meridian's own — indentured, invested, twelve years of the company's money sunk in his training and his debt. If Eastwick had turned, then something had reached him that Hale had failed to see, and that offended Hale more than the transgression itself. One kept accounts. One expected them to balance.

He pulled a fresh sheet toward him and wrote four instructions in his small even hand:

*Watch rotation on Eastwick — three men, no pattern they can learn.*
*Tullin to evening abstracts, all harbor movements logged.*
*Quiet inquiry re: Corliss — send to the agent at Kingsport on the Thursday packet.*
*Nothing further on V-series. Seal the register cabinet. Key to me alone.*

He considered a fifth line — *dismiss Eastwick* — and did not write it. Dismissal was a blunt instrument, and blunt instruments left marks. No. If Eastwick had picked up a thread, Hale wanted to see the whole weave before he cut anything. A diver in deep water should be allowed to keep swimming until the moment one chose to haul the line. It was kinder that way, in a sense. Kinder, and more informative.

He blotted the page, rang for his secretary, and went down to the floor to wish the clerks a pleasant morning, shaking hands, asking after a sick mother, being, in every visible particular, the same mild gray man as always.

Only the key ring at his belt held one new weight, and no one remarked on it.

* * *

The days that followed had a texture Ansel could not name at first, like a change in the water pressure before a storm swell — nothing visible, everything felt.

The freckled boy appeared at intervals that were too regular to be chance and too scattered to be provable. On the quay at dawn. Outside the Anchor at dusk. Once, absurdly, mending the same stretch of net on the same fish-house step, and doing it badly, because nets were not his trade and his hands betrayed him. Ansel began taking different routes to work, an old habit from his father's day — his father had claimed the company watched its divers the way a farmer watched a field, not out of malice but out of appetite — and when he varied his path, the boy varied his, half a street behind, unhurried, incurious, damning.

*They know.*

He made himself say it plainly, sitting on Hetta's cellar stairs in the dark on Wednesday night, because terror left vague grew enormous, and terror made specific could be managed. *They know you went into the files. They don't yet know why.* That was the gap he had to live inside: between what Meridian suspected and what Meridian could prove. The file was still hidden — wrapped in oilcloth, wedged behind the loose brick under Hetta's coal chute, where no clerk would ever look. The letters from Corliss were burned. The lantern signal from the cove had been answered once and never repeated; he and Brann had agreed on that from the first, one exchange only, then silence until the meeting itself.

So the evidence of his guilt was buried. What remained was suspicion, and suspicion was weather. You could sail through weather.

He tested the theory on Thursday by being magnificently ordinary. He dove the east beds, brought up a respectable catch, argued amiably with the tally-master about shell weight, drank one pint at the Anchor and left before the songs started. He tipped his cap to the freckled boy. He even stopped and asked the lad how the fishing was, and enjoyed — if *enjoyed* was the word — the way panic crossed the boy's face like a cloud.

"Fishing's fine," the boy said.

"What's your name?"

"Webb." Too quickly. "Jem Webb."

"Fine weather for it, Jem Webb," Ansel said, and walked on, and did not let his shoulders climb toward his ears until he was well around the corner.

But ordinary was a costume, and costumes chafe. At night he lay awake cataloguing errors. Had he left the cabinet drawer a hair open? Had anyone seen him cross the gallery? Had Tullin — yes. Yes, there had been a light burning late that night, a clerk's desk lamp below the stairs, and he had assumed the floor was empty because he had wanted it empty. *You saw what you needed to see.* Twelve years of keeping silences, and he had grown careless in the one season carelessness could hang him.

* * *

Corliss noticed it too, in his own unhurried way. They met Friday at the chandlery, openly, because they had agreed that after a certain point concealment looked worse than acquaintance — a pearl buyer and a fish trader might reasonably discuss prices.

"You've grown a shadow," Corliss murmured over a barrel of brine-cured herring. He was a gray, neat man with seamed cheeks and eyes the color of old rope, and he spoke of surveillance the way other men spoke of rain.

"I know."

"How many?"

"One that I've marked. There'll be others I haven't."

"There are. Two, most likely. A big fellow who drinks at the Anchor and loses gracefully at cards, and a woman who sells winkles near the customs shed." Corliss selected a herring, examined it, put it back. "Hale's work. He doesn't spend money on watchers unless he means to spend more later."

"Then we move the meeting up."

"No." Flat, immediate. "The schooner can't make the cove before the appointed week regardless of tide, and Maren won't risk a half-plan to save a full one. We hold the schedule." Corliss glanced up, and for a moment something almost kind moved behind the rope-colored eyes. "I know what it costs, waiting with a hand on a hot iron. But hear me, Eastwick: Hale suspects. Suspicion is a fishing line with no hook yet. If we panic, we tie the hook on ourselves. You go to work. You dive. You quarrel about weights. You drink your one pint. You are the dullest man in Saltmere, and you stay dull until the tide tells you otherwise."

"And if they come for me first?"

"Then you're an indentured diver who looked at some paperwork." Corliss paid for his herring, slow-counting the coins. "Worth a fine. Worth a flogging, maybe. Not worth the gallows — not unless they can prove what you actually carried out of that room, and they can't, because it isn't there anymore." He tucked the parcel under his arm. "Suspicion without proof is a cage with the door standing open. Walk softly and stay inside it until you choose to walk out."

Easy words, Ansel thought, from a man whose papers were forged and whose escape route floated at anchor beyond the point. But he took the advice, because it was the only advice, and spent the weekend performing dullness so thoroughly that by Sunday evening he half believed in it himself.

* * *

Hetta knew. Of course Hetta knew.

She had known since the second morning, when she'd seen the freckled boy on the fish-house steps and recognized the posture — she'd married a man with that posture once, a watcher for the customs house, may God rest him. She said nothing to Ansel. Saying things too soon was a waste of saying. Instead she adjusted, quietly, the way she adjusted everything in her house: extra salt fish laid in, the cellar brick re-mortared with her own hands while Ansel was on the water, a word dropped to her niece at the harbormaster's office about the comings and goings of certain hired men.

And she watched Ansel himself, this young man she'd lodged for nine years, who ate like a bird and paid like a prince and kept his grief locked up like company silver. She watched him grow thinner and quieter and more careful, and she thought: *he's carrying something heavy, and he thinks he carries it alone.*

She had carried things alone herself, once. She knew what it did to a person — how it wore the grooves deeper year by year until the grooves were all there was.

On Sunday night, hearing him come in past midnight from God knew where, she lay in her bed above the kitchen and listened to him move through the house on sock-feet, holding his breath on the third stair, and she made her decision the way she made all decisions: completely, and in advance of any consultation.

*Soon,* she thought. *Before it breaks over him. A body can't carry stone in secret forever; either you help him set it down or it breaks his back.*

She slept better than she had in months. Below her, in the dark, Ansel lay awake staring at the ceiling, counting watchers he couldn't see, and did not know that the house around him had already chosen his side.

* * *

Monday morning brought the summons.

Ansel was in the drying shed, coiling lines, when the office runner appeared in the doorway — a child of ten with the Meridian crest stitched on his cap, looking everywhere except at the diver he'd been sent to fetch.

"Mr. Eastwick. Director Hale would like a word. At your convenience." A pause. "His convenience, sir."

The shed seemed suddenly very loud with surf-sound that existed only in Ansel's ears. He finished the coil in his hands — deliberately, making his fingers obey — hung it on its peg, and followed the boy across the yard.

Through the counting house windows the clerks bent over their ledgers in the green morning light. Among them, at the far desk by the stairs, a narrow-shouldered figure with ink-stained cuffs did not look up as Ansel passed. Did not look up with a stillness that was itself a kind of looking.

At the foot of the stairs to Hale's office, Ansel stopped for one breath, laid his hand flat against the cold plaster, and felt his own heartbeat answer through his palm — steady, deep, diver's slow, the rhythm that had kept him alive in dark water more than once.

*Steady,* he told himself. *Deep water. Slow air.*

He climbed the stairs and knocked on the director's door, and from within came Hale's mild voice, warm as ever, calling him in.
