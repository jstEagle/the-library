## 21. What Silence Costs

The rain had been falling since noon, a thin grey drizzle that made the cobbles of Rope Walk shine like fish scales, and Ansel came home to it with his boots wet through and his mind still circling the counting house like a gull over a gutted catch. He had not meant to go back. That was the thing he kept telling himself as he climbed the outside stair to Hetta's rooms — *I did not mean to go back* — and yet his hands remembered the weight of the file drawer, the give of the paper, the way the lamp in Hale's office had guttered when he passed beneath it like something watching.

He let himself into the hall quietly, though there was no need. Hetta's kitchen was loud tonight: water boiling, a kettle singing its one note, the smell of barley and smoked herring thick enough to lean against. She heard him anyway. She always heard him. Twelve years of listening for other people's silences had taught Ansel that some people could hear silence too, and Hetta Brannow was one of them.

"You're dripping on my floor," she said without turning from the stove.

"It's my floor too. I pay rent."

"You pay rent for the room. The floor is mine." She ladled stew into two bowls — she always made two now, had stopped asking whether he'd eat weeks ago — and carried them to the table. "Sit down before you fall down. You've got the look of a man who's been swimming in deep water with his coat on."

Ansel sat. The stew was good. It was always good, and he ate it the way he did everything these days, automatically, while part of him stayed behind in that loft window, replaying the moment he'd slipped through it with a handful of copied pages sewn flat inside his shirt.

Hetta sat across from him and didn't touch her own bowl. She folded her hands instead — broad hands, knuckles swollen from decades of laundry taken in from the company officers' houses, from scrubbing floors at Meridian's guest quarters until they gleamed for men whose ships she would never be allowed aboard. Her hands were the first thing anyone noticed about Hetta, and the last thing they forgot.

"Junior clerk," she said. "Tam Pellowe."

Ansel looked up. "What about him?"

"He was here Tuesday. Asking after you. Wanted to know your hours, your habits, whether you had visitors." She said it the way she said everything, level as a tide table. "I told him you were dull as ditchwater and twice as predictable. He seemed satisfied." A pause. "Was he right?"

The steam rose between them. Somewhere out in the street a cart rattled past, and under it, faint but constant, the harbor breathed — masts knocking, rigging ticking, the whole town held in the sound of water working against wood.

"Hetta—"

"I'm not asking what you've done." She raised one hand, and he saw that it wasn't quite steady. "I've kept boarders for nineteen years. Divers, clerks, a customs man once, God rest his foolish soul. You learn which silences are empty and which ones are full. Yours has been full for a month. Full as an egg."

Ansel set down his spoon. Outside, the rain thickened against the window glass, and the lamplight caught the wet and threw small trembling rings across the ceiling.

"There's a woman," he said finally. "Maren Voss."

Hetta's face did something very small and very controlled. "The Voss girl. The drowned one."

"She didn't drown."

The words came out of him plainly, and once they were out he felt lighter and more naked than he had in six years. Hetta watched him across the table with eyes the color of old pewter, and she did not gasp or cross herself or glance at the door. She simply waited, the way the sea waits, for him to go on.

"The ship went down because Meridian scuttled it. Insurance. I've seen the file. Cargo declared lost that never left the warehouse — I checked the ledgers myself, the numbers don't close, they never closed, nobody wanted them to close. And Maren got off at Fennwick before the ship ever put out. She's alive, Hetta. She's been alive this whole time, and the company knew, and they let everyone in this town stand on the quay and mourn her father and light candles for her while they pocketed the payout."

His voice had gone low and hard, and he heard in it his father's voice, which frightened him, because his father had spoken like that only once — the night before the *Kestrel* took him down and kept him.

Hetta rose. Not abruptly; nothing about Hetta was abrupt. She crossed to the dresser, poured two fingers of plum brandy into two small glasses — the bottle she kept for wakes, he realized, the bottle she kept for wakes — and brought them back.

"Drink."

"I don't—"

"Drink, boy. Then listen."

He drank. The brandy burned all the way down and pooled warm in his chest like a coal.

"My son's name was Joss," Hetta said.

Ansel went still.

"Joss Brannow. Born in this house. Died at twenty-three." She turned her glass slowly on the table, not drinking, just turning it, watching the lamplight move in it. "You'll not have heard of him. Nobody hears of them. That's rather the point of Meridian, isn't it? They drown men so quietly the water itself seems guilty."

"Hetta—"

"Joss signed on to the *Fairwind*, cargo run to the southern ports. Good wages, better than diving, he said. His father dead, me alone, a boy wants to do better by his mother." Her voice stayed level. It was the levelness that made Ansel's throat tighten. "Ship came back short-handed. Said Joss fell overboard in a squall. No body. Company paid out three months' wages and a printed card with a black border, very tasteful, very official."

She lifted her glass and drank, a small precise swallow.

"Two years later I took laundry in from a Meridian purser, and drunk men talk around women with irons in their hands, they think we're furniture. He said the *Fairwind*'s run that season was carrying contraband on the side — company men skimming, selling off the manifest. And a diver named Brannow who'd found out and asked questions." She set the glass down. Turned it again. "Asked questions, Ansel. That's all my son did. Asked questions of the wrong men on a dark deck in the middle of the sea."

The rain filled the pause. Down in the harbor, a ship's bell rang the half hour, muffled by weather.

"They wrote 'lost overboard' in the book," she said. "Same hand that writes everything. Same neat little hand that writes 'drowned' for your father and 'drowned' for Maren Voss and 'lost' for every man who ever stood between Meridian and its money. They have one word for murder, boy, and they use it the way other people use punctuation."

Ansel found he couldn't speak. He had known, abstractly, that Saltmere was full of such stories — every harbor town is — but knowing it the way you know a thing in a book was different from sitting across a kitchen table from a woman who had folded her grief like linen and stored it away for nineteen years, taking it out only now, for him, because his own was showing at the edges.

"Why are you telling me this?" he managed.

"Because Tam Pellowe came to my house asking after you, and I lied to a company clerk, and I want you to understand that I knew what I was doing when I did it." She met his eyes. "I've been waiting nineteen years for someone to ask the questions my boy asked. I'd begun to think no one ever would. And then my boarder starts coming home late with salt-water eyes and a face like a man carrying something breakable, and I thought: *there*. There he is. There's the fool."

A sound escaped Ansel that was almost a laugh. "Fool."

"Fool," she agreed. "Brave fools and stupid fools look identical from a kitchen, believe me. I've fed both." She leaned forward, and her swollen hands lay flat on the table between them like things set down after a long carry. "Now. Corliss. The stranger at the quay. He's yours?"

Ansel hesitated — twelve years of habit, the reflex that had kept him alive — and then he let it go, all at once, like a diver opening his fist above the bottom and letting the shell drop.

"He's hers. Her courier. There's to be a meeting. She needs the wreck file — the original, not my copies — and then she sails on the morning packet and it's done, and Meridian comes apart somewhere far from here where it can't hurt anyone in this town."

"And you?"

"I stay. Someone has to keep the evidence standing. Someone has to be here when the questions come." He said it lightly, and Hetta's face told him exactly how lightly it had landed.

"Yes," she said dryly. "And I'm the harbormistress. Boy, they will take you apart the way they took apart Joss, politely, in a ledger, and no one will even get your name wrong at the funeral."

"Maybe."

"*Maybe,* he says." But there was no anger in it. She studied him a long moment, and whatever she was weighing, she weighed it thoroughly, the way she weighed everything. Then she stood, took up the lamp, and nodded toward the back of the kitchen.

"Come and see."

* * *

The cellar door was behind the pantry, painted the same grey as the wall, the kind of door you walked past for years without seeing. Hetta went down first, holding the lamp high, and Ansel followed into cool air that smelled of earth and salt brick and apples in straw.

It was deeper than he'd expected. Deeper than a house that size had any right to hold. Along one wall ran bins and barrels; along the other, shelving loaded with preserves in ranks, hundreds of jars, each labeled in Hetta's firm upright hand. But at the far end, past a rack of drying nets hung with hooks, the wall was stone — older stone, rougher — and set into it, half hidden behind a standing cupboard that swung aside on oiled hinges, was a low doorway into darkness.

Hetta held the lamp up to it.

"Salt cellars under this whole row of houses. Old smuggling ways, from before Meridian bought the charter and pretended the coast was respectable. This passage runs forty yards and comes up under the net-loft on Cutwater Lane. There's a hatch. It's rusted shut, but rusted shut is a condition, not a fate." She looked at him. "There's another way out at the far end, down to the tideline through the old drain. My husband put in the door. He was a smuggler before he was a laundryman, and he believed in exits the way other men believe in scripture."

"Why are you showing me this?"

"Because if they come for you, they'll come at night, and they'll come quietly, and the front stair is the first place they'll look." Her pewter eyes caught the lamplight and held it. "You'll come down here. You'll wait. And when it's clear, you'll go out the way Joss should have had someone offer him — a way out that doesn't pass a single company window."

Ansel touched the cold stone of the doorway. It felt like touching something patient.

"Hetta. If they find out you helped me—"

"They won't. And if they do—" she said it simply, folding the words flat, "—then they'll learn what I've had nineteen years to learn, which is that they can only take from me what I agree to stop hiding." She turned and started back up the stairs, then paused on the third step, her back to him, the lamp throwing her shadow huge and crooked up the wall. "One thing more, and you'll hear it once. When it's done — when she's on the packet and the file is where it needs to be — you come up those stairs and you eat my stew and you tell me how it ended. All of it. Every word. Because I buried my boy with a lie on the headstone, Ansel Eastwick, and I am owed a true ending before I die. Do you understand me?"

"Yes," Ansel said. "I understand."

"Good." She climbed. "Then bring your bowl up. Stew doesn't keep its virtue waiting on brave fools."

He followed her up, and paused at the top with his hand on the door, looking back once at the dark mouth of the passage. Forty yards of old stone between him and the tideline. Nineteen years of a mother's patience packed into a cellar like preserves against winter.

Outside, the rain had stopped. The clouds were tearing open along the harbor, and through them, far out past the breakwater where the water went black, he thought he saw it — a single point of light, low on the horizon, blinking. Steady. Twice. Pause. Twice.

Not a star. Stars didn't keep time.

The lantern signal from the cove — earlier than Brann had promised, earlier than anything should be — and Ansel felt his heart begin the slow hard knock of a man stepping off a known shore. He pulled his coat on. Behind him, Hetta was already setting a second helping of stew to warm, saying nothing, hearing everything.

"Going out?" she asked, mild as milk.

"For a walk."

"Mm." She handed him a dark lantern from the hook by the door, unasked, unexplained. "Mind the wet stones."

He took it. Their eyes met for half a second — the whole bargain sealed in it, the whole terrible alliance — and then Ansel Eastwick went down the outside stair into the night, toward the cove, carrying a dead boy's mother's lantern and a living girl's promise, and behind him the cellar door stood open in the lamplight like a held breath.
