## 22. Plans on Paper

Hetta's cellar smelled of turnips and lamp oil and forty years of keeping other people's secrets. Ansel came down the ladder last, after a full circuit of the alley behind the boarding house — a habit now, checking the shadows the way he once checked his air lines before a deep dive. The storm lamp was already lit on the pickle barrel that served as a table. Corliss sat hunched beneath the low ceiling beams like a heron folded into too small a cage, and Hetta stood at the foot of the ladder with her arms crossed, watching him come down as though she were still deciding something about him.

"Door's barred," she said. "Window shutter latched. If anyone asks, I'm salting down cabbage and you're helping me because you're too stupid to refuse an old woman."

"That's not far from true," Ansel said.

Corliss had cleared a space on the barrel top and weighted down the corners of a chart with three jars of pickled onions and a candle stub. The chart was old, soft as cloth at its folds, and Ansel recognized it with a small cold shock: it was a harbor chart of Saltmere, but drawn from memory, from the sea side, the way a smuggler or a pilot would know it. Every shoal marked. Every channel. The tide races off the breakwater rendered in careful pencil strokes, annotated in a hand he did not know.

"You've been busy," Hetta said to Corliss.

"I've been careful," Corliss said. "There's a difference, and it keeps men alive." He smoothed the chart with one long finger. "Now. Before we speak of anything else — understand what we're planning. Not a rescue. Not a raid. A meeting. Maren comes ashore for one purpose: the original wreck file. Copies have gone out on Brann's boats, copies will go out again, but copies can be argued with. A court abroad will want the company's own ledger paper, with their own seals, in their own clerk's hand. That document exists in exactly one place."

"The counting house," Ansel said. "Third floor, east strong room. I've seen it."

Both of them looked at him. He realized he had never actually told them how well he'd seen it — how many nights since that first terrible glance he had lain awake reconstructing the room in his mind, shelf by shelf, lock by lock, the way a diver memorizes a wreck before ever putting his hands on it.

"You've seen it," Corliss repeated carefully.

"Through a door left open six inches while Pellworth fetched brandy for Director Hale." Ansel kept his voice level. "Iron-banded cabinet, double key. One key lives on Hale's ring. The other hangs in the clerk's alcove behind the third ledger case, disguised as the key to the stationery press. Pellworth thinks no one knows. Pellworth drinks after ten o'clock and sleeps at his desk on Fridays when the packet brings the southern mail and there's nothing to count until morning."

The lamp flame bent in some small draft. Corliss smiled slowly, the way ice forms — all at once, where you weren't looking.

"Twelve years," Hetta murmured, mostly to herself. "Twelve years they think they own him, and he knows their house better than their own clerks do."

"They don't think," Ansel said. "That's the whole architecture of the place. Nobody thinks inside those walls. They count. Counting doesn't require believing anything." He looked down at the chart. "So we take the file. Then what?"

* * *

Then, it turned out, everything depended on tides, and tides did not care what any of them wanted.

Corliss laid it out with the patience of a man who had done this kind of arithmetic before, in worse cellars than this one. Maren's schooner could not lie off Saltmere — Meridian's charter gave the company authority over harbor approaches, and a strange vessel lingering would bring the revenue cutter down on them within a day. Instead she would stand off beyond the point, past the Gullet race where no cutter liked to go, and send her boat in under cover of dark on a single night, chosen in advance and not to be changed except by weather.

"One night," Corliss said. "One boat. She comes ashore at the breakwater gap at slack low water — two hours either side of which the current through the gap is fast enough to swamp a loaded dinghy. She takes the file. She goes back out. The packet for the mainland sails on the morning tide regardless, because the packet always sails, and if she is aboard it, she's a passenger with papers and no one aboard that ship works for Meridian. By the time Hale learns what's happened, she's in open water and the file is in a foreign consulate's safe."

"And if the tide's wrong," Hetta said, "or the wind's wrong, or the cutter's in?"

"Then we wait for the next slack water and pray the file hasn't been missed yet." Corliss's finger traced the breakwater on the chart. "Which is why the theft must happen the same night. No earlier. If Ansel lifts the file days ahead, every hour is another hour for someone to open that cabinet and raise the alarm. Same night, same hours. He takes it at the change of watch — between half past one and two, when Pellworth is drunk asleep and the night porter makes his rounds along the quay front instead of upstairs. Maren's boat comes ashore at slack water, which falls at—" he checked a scrap of paper covered in figures— "quarter past three. That gives us an hour to move it from the counting house to the water."

An hour. Ansel turned the number over and felt its weight, the way he weighed a stone before diving with it. A quarter mile from the counting house to the breakwater gap, over cobbles that rang underfoot, past the customs shed, past the taverns that would be emptying their last drunkards into the street. Carrying a leather folio that might as well have been branded with the word *thief*.

"It's not enough time if anything goes wrong," he said.

"Nothing ever gives you enough time," Hetta said. "That's what wrong means."

But Corliss shook his head. "It's not enough time carrying it through the streets, no. So we don't carry it through the streets." His finger moved to a mark on the chart, a small cross behind the fish market. "The salt cellar. Hetta's."

Ansel looked up sharply. Hetta's face had gone very still — the stillness of a woman being volunteered for something.

"There's a drain," Corliss went on, gently now, reading her. "A culvert that runs from the market square down under the fish sheds and out through the seawall below the high-tide line. It was cut for draining the salting floors. It comes up inside your property, behind the third row of racks. I know because I helped cut part of it twenty years ago, before your time here, when this town ran on different traffic." He paused. "I'm sorry to bring it back to your door, mistress. But it's the only path in Saltmere that doesn't pass a single lantern."

Hetta was quiet for a long moment. In the lamplight Ansel watched her jaw work, watched her decide — and understood that she had decided long ago, probably the night she found him coming in at dawn with salt marsh mud to his knees and said nothing, and had been waiting since then only to learn what shape her silence would need to take.

"My son Tomas worked the counting house ledgers for nine years," she said at last, to no one in particular. "They took his wages against his father's debt and then took his health, and then when his lungs went bad from the ink dust they docked him for lost hours until he owed more dead than alive. I buried him owing Meridian money." She set both hands flat on the barrel. "You'll use my cellar. You'll use my drain. And when this is over, if any of you are still standing, you'll drink a toast in this house loud enough for the dead to hear. That's my price."

"Agreed," Corliss said softly.

"And one more thing." Her eyes came around to Ansel, sharp as a gaff hook. "After the boat goes out. After she's away. What happens to you, boy? Because I've been doing sums of my own, and I don't like the answer."

* * *

He hadn't answered her then. He answered it now, walking the length of the breakwater alone in the gray afternoon, letting the wind off the water push at him like a hand testing whether he'd fall.

Because that was the flaw in the plan, the flaw none of them would say aloud in the cellar, and it was shaped exactly like himself. Maren came ashore, took the file, sailed on the morning packet, and vanished into a world where Meridian's writ didn't run. Corliss had a courier's talent for disappearing; he'd proven it twice over. Hetta was an old woman with a cabbage story. But Ansel Eastwick was a known quantity — indentured, debt-bound, seen daily by men who knew his face and his gait and the exact hour he reported to the dive boats. The moment the empty cabinet was discovered, there would be exactly one diver in Saltmere with reason and opportunity and, once anyone asked the right questions, knowledge.

He could run. The thought came the way it always came, patient and reasonable, wearing the voice of self-preservation. Fish north with Brann's people. Disappear into the archipelago the way hundreds of debt-men had before him, becoming rumor, becoming nobody. The file would still do its work abroad. Maren would still be safe.

But then the debt didn't die; it just transferred. Hetta's cellar, Hetta's drain, Hetta's name written all over the escape route — Meridian would squeeze her for harboring, and an old woman's boarding house was worth perhaps two years of her son's phantom debt. And Corliss, if caught. And the whole edifice of the thing resting on a stolen document that no honest court in Saltmere had ever seen, dismissed as the forgery of runaway criminals unless someone stayed to swear to it, to stand in a hall and say *I read it, I carried it, I am the witness.*

Justice outside the law still needed a body to stand inside it.

He reached the end of the breakwater where the lighthouse stood white and blind-eyed in the daylight, and he leaned on the wet granite and let the spray come up over the stones and soak his sleeves, and he thought about a girl under this same wall years ago making him promise something enormous with the smallest possible voice, and how he had said yes without knowing that yes would take twelve years to arrive at its meaning.

"All right," he said to the water. It was the closest thing to prayer he had.

* * *

The second meeting happened two nights later, and this time Corliss brought paper.

Real plans, drawn small and precise on sheets torn from a navigation log: the counting house floor plan from memory — Ansel corrected two details with a pencil stub, moving the night porter's route, marking the loose tread on the stairs that sang under weight — and the timing table, and the signals. Everything reduced to ink. Fragile hope given structure, given times and fallbacks and a sequence of small decisions that could each be made in the dark by someone whose hands were shaking.

"Say it back," Corliss said, when the sheets were spread across Hetta's kitchen table with the shutters closed and the kettle steaming for cover-noise.

"Half past one, I go up through the loft window as usual — that part's ordinary, I've done it a dozen times this season for the harvest tallies." Ansel ticked it off. "Pellworth asleep by eleven, guaranteed by the southern mail and the bottle I've watched him finish three Fridays running. Key from behind the third ledger case. Cabinet opens stiff at the bottom hinge — I'll oil it Thursday when I'm in for my wage tally. File is a grey folio, cord-bound, stamped *Voss Line — Claim 44*. I take the folio and leave the folder of current correspondence so the stack height stays true. Down the back stair. Out through the yard gate, which bolts with a hook-and-eye I can lift from either side. Culvert behind the fish market. Your cellar by quarter past two."

"Then?" Hetta prompted.

"Then I sit in your cellar with the file wrapped in oilcloth until the boat signal — three flashes from the point, green shuttered lantern, answered by two from your seaward window. I walk it down to the gap. Slack water at quarter past three. Maren ashore, file handed over, she walks back to the boat, boat pulls for the schooner beyond the Gullet. Packet sails at seven. Done."

"And if the signal doesn't come?" Corliss asked. "Any reason — fog, patrol, a changed mind at sea?"

"Then the file stays in your flour bin until the next arranged contact, and I go home and sleep like an innocent man."

"Good lad." But Corliss wasn't smiling. He turned one of the sheets toward the lamp — the last one, the one Ansel had been avoiding with his eyes all evening. It showed the breakwater gap and the beach below it, and a single line of text: *Courier to remain visible at gap until boat is clear.* "One more thing has to be said plainly, and Hetta's right that it should be said in front of witnesses. If the patrol comes — and Hale has been doubling the night rounds since the inspection irregularities began — the man standing on the beach with the evidence is the man who hangs. Maren must not wait for you. The boat must not wait for you. Whatever happens on that sand, that boat leaves at slack water with her aboard. Swear it."

The kettle hissed. Hetta's knitting had stopped entirely, which from Hetta was the loudest sound in the room.

"She won't agree to that," Ansel said.

"She agreed to it weeks ago, on her own ship, in words I'll not repeat. It's you who has to agree to it. That's why I'm asking in front of a witness." Corliss's eyes were steady and not unkind, which somehow made it worse. "Swear it, Ansel. Or we build a different plan, and the different plan is worse."

Ansel looked at the line of ink on the paper. *Courier to remain visible.* As though he were cargo to be jettisoned. As though the whole architecture of these pages — the tides, the keys, the oiled hinge, Hetta's drain running cold and secret under the town — had been built around a hole shaped exactly like him, a hole everyone had politely agreed not to name until now.

"I swear it," he said, and his voice held, and he hated how easily it held, twelve years of practice holding.

Hetta reached over and squeezed his wrist once, hard, saying nothing, which from her was a speech.

* * *

Corliss left first, slipping out through the culvert itself to prove it passable — he emerged twenty minutes later smelling of brine and looking quietly triumphant, and Ansel made the crossing himself afterward in the dark just to know his own body could do it, shoulders scraping stone, breath echoing strangely, coming up behind the salting racks into the smell of fish and lamp oil like a man surfacing.

Afterward there was only the walk home, and the streets of Saltmere at midnight, and the harbor lying black and patient beyond the rooftops.

At the corner by the customs shed he stopped. From there he could see the counting house, a darker mass against the stars, its upper windows blind. Somewhere inside it slept a grey folio with a cord binding and a stamp that named a drowned girl, and somewhere beyond the Gullet, past the racing tide, a schooner rocked at anchor carrying the living version of that word *drowned*, and between the two ran a line of paper and moonlight and a quarter mile of cobbled street that four people in a lamplit kitchen had just finished measuring to the minute.

It seemed impossible that the town could sleep through it. It seemed impossible that the gulls on the moorings could keep making their small greedy sounds, that the tide could keep breathing against the pilings with its ancient indifference, when everything was about to change. But that was the sea's oldest lesson, the one his father's trade had taught him before any other: the water does not care what you are about to attempt. It only cares whether you have prepared.

He had prepared. God help him, he had prepared.

He was halfway down his own street when he saw the light.

It was a lantern, moving along the quay front where no lantern had any lawful business at this hour — not the porters' pattern, not the watch's measured swing. It moved quickly, then stopped. Moved again. Stopped directly opposite the counting house steps, where it hung motionless for a long, long moment, a small yellow eye staring at the dark building.

Then it went out, and the man carrying it became one more shadow among shadows, and Ansel stood pressed into a doorway with his heart going like a diver's pulse at depth, understanding that whatever the plan was on paper —

the town had begun writing its own.
