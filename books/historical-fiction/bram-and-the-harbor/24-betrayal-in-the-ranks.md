## 24. Betrayal in the Ranks

The rain had come in the night and left Saltmere smelling of wet rope and cold iron. Ansel Eastwick walked to the counting house with his collar up and his ledger under his arm, and he did not look at the harbor notices, because he had learned that a man who looks too often at a thing draws other eyes to it.

He was early. He was always early. Twelve years of indenture had taught him that punctuality was a kind of armor — the first virtue a company buys from you, and the cheapest.

The counting house smelled of tallow and ink as it always did, of paper gone soft with sea damp. Junior clerks hunched at their desks like gulls over scraps. Ansel nodded to Pellam, the youngest of them, a narrow boy of nineteen with ink-stained cuffs and an ambition that showed in his face the way a bruise shows through skin. Pellam had been friendly lately. Too friendly, perhaps, though Ansel had not thought so at the time. He had answered the boy's questions about the loft stairs, about which nights the director worked late, about how long the file room stood unattended on inventory days — all of it framed as idle curiosity, all of it answered because refusing to answer would have been its own confession.

"Morning, Eastwick." Pellam's smile was quick and bright. "Rough crossing for the packet yesterday."

"They say the storm's not done." Ansel set his ledger down. "Tide'll be high by noon."

"Mm." Pellam dipped his pen. "Hale's in early. Wants the quarterly tallies before the divers come up."

Ansel's hands did not shake. He had trained them not to, years ago, when he learned to hold still while a conch closed on his fingers in the dark below. But something behind his ribs went quiet and attentive, the way the water goes quiet before a squall line crosses the horizon.

"Hale's in early," he repeated mildly. "That's unlike him."

"He's been in early three days running." Pellam said it lightly, but his eyes came up and held Ansel's for half a second longer than they needed to. "Man must have something on his mind."

*Three days.* The inspection had been eight days ago. Eight days since Ansel had sat across from Director Hale and lied with a steady voice about why his name appeared in the loft visitor's book on a night no entry should have been made. Hale had smiled at the end of it and said, *Thank you, Eastwick. That will be all,* and Ansel had walked out believing — wanting to believe — that the lie had held.

Now he counted backward through the week like a man counting coins and finding one short. Three mornings of early arrivals. A junior clerk suddenly curious about stairways and schedules. And somewhere in the middle of it, a silence around him that he could not name yet but could feel, the way a diver feels a current change forty feet down — nothing visible, everything altered.

He opened his ledger and began copying figures, and all morning the numbers swam like fish just out of reach.

* * *

Pellam had sold him on Tuesday.

That was how it happened, though Ansel would not learn the shape of it for some time. It had not been dramatic. There had been no bag of coin slid across a tavern table, no whispered bargain in an alley. It had happened in Director Hale's office at the end of the day, with the shutters half-closed against the gray light, and it had taken less than ten minutes.

"You've been observant, Pellam," Hale had said, pouring two fingers of brandy and not offering any. "That's rare in a man your age. Most clerks see only what's put in front of them."

"I try, sir."

"And what do you observe about Eastwick?"

Pellam had hesitated — a real hesitation, the last one he would permit himself — and then he had laid it out the way he'd learned to lay out figures: neat, itemized, damning. Eastwick in the loft after hours. Eastwick asking about the file room schedule. Eastwick's friendship with the stranger Corliss, who asked questions at the quay that no honest man needed answered. Eastwick's landlady Hetta Brannock, whose son had drowned in Meridian service and who had stopped attending chapel the same month Corliss arrived. And finally — delivered with the small flourish of a clerk who knows his arithmetic is correct — Eastwick's name appearing twice in the wreck-file requisition log, once in a hand that was not his own.

Hale had listened without moving. When Pellam finished, the director had turned his glass slowly on the blotter, watching the brandy climb the sides.

"Do you know what I would have done at your age, Pellam? I'd have gone straight to Eastwick. Confronted him. Thought I could buy my way into his confidence, or frighten him out of whatever he's doing." He set the glass down. "It took me twenty years to learn better. You don't close a net while the fish can still see it. You let him run to the end of his rope. You watch every knot he ties. And then—" he smiled, and it was the smile of a man balancing books, "—then you pull, and everything comes up together. The diver, the courier, the landlady, and whoever they're all working for. One haul. Clean."

"Should I keep watching him, sir?"

"No." Hale rose and looked out the shuttered window toward the harbor, where the morning tide packet was a smudge of white against the gray water. "You'll do nothing differently at all. That's the whole art of it. If Eastwick so much as suspects you, he'll go to ground, and I'll lose the thread back to whoever is paying Corliss to carry company papers across the water." He turned. "You understand? From this moment, you are exactly what you were yesterday. Friendly. Helpful. Bored."

"Yes, sir."

"One more thing." Hale crossed to the door and opened it, dismissing him. "There's a girl involved. Maren Voss. Officially dead, officially an enemy agent. If she comes ashore — *when* she comes ashore — I want her taken alive. The Company pays better for live proof than for corpses. Remember that."

Pellam remembered. He remembered it all the way home, and told himself it was loyalty, and knew even as he told himself that it was fear wearing loyalty's coat.

* * *

By Thursday, Ansel could no longer pretend the water hadn't changed.

It was nothing he could point to. That was the cruelty of it. No one followed him — or if they did, they were good enough that he couldn't catch them, and he had spent twelve years learning to catch eyes in mirrors and shop windows. No one questioned him. Hale passed him in the corridor on Thursday and wished him good morning by name, which Hale almost never did, and the courtesy landed on Ansel like a hand on the back of the neck.

He tested the edges carefully. He mentioned to Pellam that he might stop by the counting house Saturday to check the tide tables, and watched whether anyone was waiting there when he came. No one was. He took a different route past the customs shed and saw nothing. He left word for Corliss through the fishwife Marta — a nothing message, a request for more tobacco — and the tobacco arrived on time, wrapped in the same brown paper, with no sign it had been opened.

Everything was normal. That was what frightened him.

He had grown up on silences. His mother had taught him which ones kept people safe; the harbor had taught him which ones meant danger. And this — the smooth surface, the absence of friction, the way the days moved without a single snag — this was the silence that meant someone was holding their breath and waiting.

Friday evening he climbed to his room above Hetta's house and found her sitting on the landing outside his door, mending a shirt that belonged to neither of them, her needle working in the lamplight with the patience of a woman who had raised sons and buried one.

"You're walking like a man listening for footsteps," she said, not looking up.

"I'm walking like a tired man."

"Mm." She bit the thread. "Hetta Brannock has buried a husband and a boy, Ansel Eastwick, and she has lived under Meridian's roof her whole life, and she will tell you something for free: when the counting house goes quiet, it isn't resting. It's aiming." She folded the shirt, rose stiffly, and paused beside him. "Whatever's coming, don't be standing where you were yesterday when it lands."

She went down the stairs. Ansel stood a long time in the dark of the landing, listening to the harbor breathe below, and understood that she knew — or guessed enough — and had chosen, again, to say only what could not be proven against her.

* * *

Saturday night, Corliss came to the breakwater.

He came the long way, along the shingle below the sea wall, hood up against a wind that carried salt and the first bite of real winter. Ansel met him where the old capstan rusted into the stone, and for a while they simply stood looking at the black water, two men pretending to smoke in the dark.

"The packet sails Tuesday, dawn tide," Corliss said quietly. "Maren comes ashore Monday night. Everything holds."

"Does it?" Ansel kept his voice flat. "Something's wrong in town. Hale's been early three days. The clerks are watching me sideways. Even Hetta feels it."

Corliss was silent a moment. "Feelings aren't evidence, Eastwick."

"No. They're warnings. I've lived off them twelve years." He turned to face the older man. In the dark he could make out little of Corliss's face — the gray stubble, the scarred knuckles wrapped around a pipe he never lit. "I'm asking you plainly. Is there anything you know that you haven't told me?"

"If there were, it would be because telling you would endanger you without helping you."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I'm authorized to give." Corliss's tone was not unkind, which somehow made it worse. "Listen to me. Every job like this has a week where the air goes bad. Where you're sure the walls have ears. Half the time it's nothing — nerves, coincidence, a director with indigestion. The other half—" he shrugged, "—the other half it's real, and knowing sooner wouldn't save you anyway. What saves you is discipline. Same routes. Same hours. Same bored face. Can you do that for three more days?"

Ansel looked out at the water, where far off a buoy light blinked its slow idiot rhythm, and thought about the file hidden in the loft wall, and the letters sewn into his coat lining, and the promise standing at the end of all of it like a lighthouse.

"I can do it," he said. "But if it goes wrong—"

"It won't go wrong," Corliss said, and Ansel heard, beneath the certainty, the sound of a man saying what he needed to believe. "Monday night. Breakwater. Then it's out of our hands and onto the water, and Meridian can choke on its own ledgers."

They parted without shaking hands. Ansel watched the darker shape of the man dissolve into the shingle shadows, and stood alone a long while, and the wind came off the sea and found every seam in his coat.

* * *

Sunday, Pellam brought him tea.

That was the detail that broke something open in Ansel's chest — not a search, not a summons, not a hard question, but a cup of tea carried up to the loft with both hands and a shy, apologetic smile. "You looked half-dead Friday," Pellam said. "Thought you could use it."

"Thank you," Ansel said, and drank it, and hated himself for the suspicion that bloomed with every swallow, and hated Pellam more for making the suspicion necessary.

Because that was the thing about betrayal — he had seen it work before, in the harbor, in the barracks of hired divers, in his own mother's face the year she signed him over. It never came with a knife. It came with kindness, timed precisely, offered by the person whose warmth you had begun, stupidly, to rely on. The company did not need to torture a confession out of you. It only needed to make you comfortable enough to stop checking the locks.

He sat with the cooling tea and forced himself to think it through like figures in a ledger, because figures did not flinch.

Item: Hale early three days. Item: Pellam's questions about stairs and schedules, weeks ago, filed away and now returned with interest. Item: the inspection itself — Hale had let him go with a smile, and Hale did not smile at things he believed. He smiled at things he intended to use. Item: no arrest, no summons, no search. Which meant either innocence — unlikely — or a trap being built to catch more than one fish.

And the largest figure of all, the one he kept circling: if Hale knew, then Hale knew about Monday night. About the breakwater. About Maren.

He set the cup down with a care that cost him everything he had.

* * *

That evening he did the hardest thing he had done since the deep dive. He did nothing.

He ate Hetta's stew and praised it. He wound the clock. He read four pages of a shipping almanac by candlelight and understood none of them. He lay in the dark listening to the wind work at the shutters, and he made himself rehearse Monday like a diver rehearsing a descent: the walk to the breakwater, the signal, the meeting, the documents, the walk back. Same routes. Same hours. Same bored face.

And underneath the rehearsal, quieter than the wind, the question kept surfacing like a body in the water: *if it's a trap, do I still go?*

He turned the question over until midnight and could not drown it. Because there was no version of Monday in which he stayed home and lived with himself afterward. If Hale knew, then going was a gamble with gallows odds. If Hale didn't know, staying home was the betrayal — of Maren, of Corliss, of the boy he had been under the breakwater six years ago with salt drying on his shoulders and a promise in his mouth. Either way the water was rising. The only choice left was how he went under.

Near dawn he slept at last, an inch deep, and dreamed of the wreck file lying open on a desk, its pages turning themselves, and every page blank except the last, where someone had written his own name in Maren's slanted hand.

* * *

Monday morning broke clean and cold, the kind of treacherous beauty the coast specialized in — hard blue sky, white light on the water, gulls wheeling over the fish market like confetti thrown by a cruel hand. Perfect weather for a sailing. Perfect weather for an ambush.

Ansel dressed, checked the letters in his coat lining, and went down to find Hetta standing in the kitchen doorway with her arms folded, blocking the exit the way a small woman can block a door entirely.

"You'll take bread with you," she said. "And you'll eat it walking, like always, so the pattern holds." She pressed the loaf into his hands, and her grip tightened over his for one breath longer than bread required. "And Ansel. Whatever happens tonight — my cellar door doesn't lock from the inside anymore. I had it changed."

He looked at her. She looked back, level and dry-eyed, a woman who had spent a lifetime learning to say enormous things in the smallest possible words.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't thank me. Come back and need it." She stepped aside. "Go on. The tide won't wait, and neither will whatever's waiting for you."

He went out into the bright cold morning, past the quay where the tide packet rode at anchor with her cargo half-loaded, past the counting house where the windows caught the sun and gave back nothing, past Pellam at his desk, who looked up and smiled and wished him a good day.

Ansel smiled back, and walked on, and did not let himself wonder — not yet, not where his face could betray him — whether the boy's smile was the last honest thing in Saltmere, or the first lie of the trap already closing around them all.

Above the harbor, on the customs shed roof, a man with a spyglass lowered it slowly and made a note of the time.
