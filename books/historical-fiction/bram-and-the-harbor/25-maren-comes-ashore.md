## 25. Maren Comes Ashore

The schooner *Kestrel* stopped a league out from Saltmere harbor and killed her lights, because no ship that meant well ever came into that harbor in the dark.

Maren Voss stood at the rail while they lowered the boat, and she made herself look at the town.

That was the discipline of it. Six years of charts and ciphers and other people's coastlines, six years of learning to read water by the color it turned under a moonless sky, and still the hardest thing she had done tonight was look directly at the place she had been born and not flinch. Saltmere lay along its crescent of shore like something asleep — but not the way she remembered it sleeping. The lamps were fewer. She counted them without wanting to. Where the fishing fleet had once rafted three deep along the north quay there was only one line of boats, small ones, and beyond them the masts of Meridian's packet stood up tall and proprietary against the stars, like a man who has moved into a house whose owner drowned.

"You can still go back," Brann said beside her. He said it lightly, which was how he said everything, but his hand was on the gunwale and his knuckles were white in the lamplight he'd shuttered. "No one would think less."

"Everyone would think less," she said. "You'd think less. You'd write it in your report."

"I'd write that you were sensible."

"You'd write that I turned back." She pulled her coat tighter. It was a good coat, foreign-cut, and she had worn it precisely because no one in Saltmere owned anything like it; now, looking at it, she saw it for what it was — a flag. Tomorrow it would be a fisherman's oilskin from Hetta's line or nothing at all. "Put me ashore, Brann."

The sea between the schooner and the shore was black glass, and the oars made almost no sound in it. That was the thing about salt water, she thought, as the boat slid through it — it never held a grudge, it only held you. It had tried to hold her once already. She had been twenty years old and the *Voss Star* had gone down off the shoals with her father's cargo and half her father's crew, and the company had written *lost with all hands* in a clerk's fair hand and paid her mother's claim at two-thirds value, and the sea had taken her and then — because she had learned to fight even the things that owned her — given her back.

She had never told anyone the whole of what happened that night. Not the enemy service that fished her out and found her useful. Not Corliss, who carried her words like coins he was afraid of dropping. Not, above all, the boy on the breakwater who had made her promise to come home, and whom she had let believe her dead because dead girls couldn't be interrogated, and living ones with her name couldn't set foot in a port where Meridian's charter ran.

Six years. She wondered if he still walked with his shoulders hunched, like a man perpetually ducking under a lintel that wasn't there. She wondered if he still kept his pearls — no. He would have sold them. He'd had a debt.

The keel touched sand with a sound like a struck match.

* * *

They came ashore in the cove south of the point, where the shingle gave way to hard wet sand and an old net-drying rack leaned against the wind like a drunk against a wall. Two of Brann's men got her bag up the beach; the third stood facing the water, watching for the boat that shouldn't come. Everything was done in whispers and hand signs, quick and practiced, and Maren stood among them like a woman at her own funeral — present, addressed by no one, understanding everything too late.

"Signal goes up at the second bell after midnight," Brann murmured, close to her ear. "Green twice, pause, green once. If your diver answers, you meet him at the breakwater, you take the papers, and you are back on this sand before the tide turns or I leave without you. I mean that the way it sounds."

"You've left people before."

"Once." He looked at her, and in the dark his face was just a suggestion of itself, weathered and amused and not amused at all underneath. "He volunteered to stay. There's a difference, and I've spent four years trying to convince myself of it. Don't make me carry a second one."

"And if Hale's watch is on the harbor?"

"Then your diver doesn't signal, and we do this again on the spring tide, and you spend another month writing letters to a man who may be dead by then. Or worse — employed." He handed her the shuttered lantern. "Two flashes means all's well. Any other number of flashes means run."

She took it. It weighed nothing and everything.

"One more thing," Brann said. He hesitated, which he almost never did. "When you see him — whatever you built in your head during six years of cold cabins, put it down gently. People aren't letters. They get altered in transit."

"Is that from experience?"

"It's from being altered in transit." He touched two fingers to his brow, a salute halfway to mockery, and waded back toward the boat.

Maren watched until the darkness swallowed it. Then she turned inland, toward the town that had raised her and would hang her if it recognized her, and began to walk.

* * *

Saltmere had changed the way a face changes during a long illness — the bones the same, everything over them altered.

She knew it was dangerous to come near the streets at all. She did it anyway, because there is a kind of hunger that isn't for food, and she had starved on it for six years. She kept to the backs of the buildings, the ginnels she had run through as a girl, the places where the fish-cellar doors opened onto alleys and the smell of brine still rose up old and familiar enough to hurt. And through these gaps and shadows she looked at her hometown sideways, in slices, and each slice was wrong.

The chandlery on Fish Street had become a Meridian supply office. Its sign — her godfather's sign, the painted herring he'd been so proud of — was gone, and in its place lettering so official it looked printed: MERIDIAN TRADING CO. — STORES & OUTFITTING. NO CREDIT BEYOND ACCOUNT.

No credit beyond account. Her godfather had extended credit to half the widows in Saltmere on nothing but his own memory of their husbands. She stood in the alley behind his old shop until her eyes stung and made herself move on.

The divers' row was worse. Those little houses had always leaned together like conspirators, roofs touching, walls shared, one stove's smoke warming two families. They still leaned, but they leaned alone now — shutters closed at an hour when Saltmere had never once closed its shutters, because the fleet rose at dawn and everyone slept with the noise of preparation in their ears. Now the windows were dark and tight and quiet, and the silence coming off them had the texture of obedience. A town doesn't learn to sleep early, she thought. A town is taught.

At the corner of the ropewalk a board had been nailed up, new wood, pale as a scar. Even before she got close she knew what it was; she had read enough harbor notices in enough ports to know the shape of authority's prose. She risked the open ground, crossed fast, low, and read it by feel more than light, her eyes finding the words the way they'd find rocks in surf.

BY ORDER OF THE CHARTER MAGISTRACY AND THE MERIDIAN TRADING COMPANY... PERSONS OF ENEMY ALLEGIANCE... INFORMATION AS TO THE WHEREABOUTS OF ANY SUCH PERSON... REWARD OF TEN POUNDS...

Ten pounds. Six years ago her father's whole boat and season had fetched perhaps sixty. She was ten pounds of paper on a board in the town where she'd been christened.

There was a name at the bottom, in the position of authority, that she didn't know: HALE. Underneath, smaller, the standing notices — tide tables, tariff schedules, a warning against unauthorized diving beyond the shallows. Unauthorized diving. Ansel's whole life was unauthorized; it had been since he was eleven years old and went down after his father's lost grapnel and came up with a pearl in his fist. She could see him so clearly in that memory that she had to put a hand flat against the ropewalk wall. Eleven years old, blue-lipped, grinning, holding the pearl up to the light like a stolen sun, saying: *this one's yours, Ren. For your dowry.* Twelve years old and already keeping accounts of love because love was the only currency Meridian hadn't figured out how to tax.

She pushed away from the wall and moved.

* * *

The breakwater was exactly where she had left it, which felt like an accusation.

Everything else had shifted — the town, the fleet, the shape of power, herself most of all — but the breakwater ran out into the dark water on its ancient spine of stone, armoring the harbor mouth against the sea, indifferent as scripture. She came to it along the shingle below the seawall, boots in her hand, because the stones here were the kind that reported footsteps to anyone listening, and she walked out along its crest only when she reached the section hidden from the quay by the capstan house.

The wind was stronger here, tasting of open water, and it pressed her coat flat against her and made her glad of it. Below her, on the harbor side, the tide packet rode at her moorings, half-lit, half-loaded, patient as a debt. Beyond her, on the seaward side, the black water breathed against the armor stones — the same slow exhale it had made on the last night of her old life, when a boy had sat her down right about here, on the flat slab the fishermen called the Parson, and refused for an hour to say what he'd brought her out to say.

*I'm not going to say goodbye,* he had finally said, furious with himself, she'd realized later, for taking so long. *Goodbye is for people who've finished. I'm going to say: come back, and when you come back, come here first. This rock. I'll know where to find you. Whatever else changes.*

*Whatever else changes,* she had repeated, sixteen and certain the world would do her the courtesy of holding still.

And then, quieter, the pearl pressed into her palm, warm from his hand: *and this time you keep it. I've got others. I'll always have others. But I want there to be one thing in the world that's yours and mine and nobody's business.*

She had thrown it into the sea rather than carry it through customs. She remembered his face — horror, then laughter, then something that had no name but that she had spent six years calling by different names depending on how honest she was willing to be that night. *Now it's really ours,* he'd said. *Only we know where it lies.*

She sat down on the Parson. The stone was cold through her clothes, and the cold felt correct, earned, a penance she was owed the chance to perform. Below her, somewhere in that dark water, a pearl lay in the silt that no dredge had ever found, because only two people in the world knew where it lay, and one of them had spent six years pretending the other was dead.

She took out the lantern and uncovered it, aimed it out over the water where the *Kestrel* waited invisible, and flashed: green twice, pause, green once. Then she shuttered it and waited.

Waiting was the work, she had learned. All the romance of espionage was actually waiting — in doorways, in fog, in borrowed identities, in the space between sending a message and learning whether it killed anyone. She was good at waiting. She had made a profession of it. But she had never waited anywhere that mattered before, and the minutes here had weight; they sat down next to her on the stone.

The second bell after midnight came across the water from the packet's deck, thin and round. Then the town bell answered, deeper, slower — and wrong. Off its old rhythm. She frowned into the dark, counting, and heard it: the bell was ringing short. The night watch in Saltmere had always rung the hours long and lazy, a sound you could set your heart by. Someone had changed it. Small thing. Everything was small things. A shortened bell, a missing sign, a shuttered window, a notice board pale as a scar — a town altered one small thing at a time until the people inside it could no longer remember what it had originally agreed to be.

Her hands had gone numb. She tucked them under her arms and watched the harbor for the answer signal and thought about what she would say.

She had composed a hundred openings in six years. In the cold cabins Brann had mentioned, she had rehearsed them — angry ones, forgiving ones, funny ones, the confession she'd never earned the right to make (*I let you grieve me, Ansel, I chose it, it was safer*) — and now that the moment was possibly minutes away, every one of them fell apart like wet rope. What remained was embarrassingly simple. *You came. You're here. You kept the rock.*

A light blinked far out on the water. Green — twice.

Then a third flash.

Not green. White. One flash, white, from close in — from inside the harbor, from the direction of the capstan house at the landward foot of the breakwater.

Brann's voice in her ear on the sand: *any other number of flashes means run.*

But it wasn't the number that froze her. It was the color. Lantern glass in Saltmere was cheap and yellowed; every fisherman's lamp she'd grown up under burned warm. White light, steady, deliberate — that was company issue. Meridian's patrol lanterns burned white.

The third flash hung in her vision like an afterimage, and beneath her feet, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of boots on stone — two sets, unhurried, spaced like men who had all the time in the world because the world had already been arranged for them.

Maren snuffed the lantern and slid off the Parson into the shadow of the capstan house, her heart going like a diver's pulse at depth, and the last thought before the darkness took her was not of escape routes or the *Kestrel*'s rendezvous but of a boy's voice, twelve years old, laughing over black water:

*Only we know where it lies.*

Someone knew more than they should.
