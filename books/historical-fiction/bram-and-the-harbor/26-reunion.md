## 26. Reunion

The breakwater had not moved in six years, but Ansel had.

He crouched now where he had once sprawled, a boy's careless length replaced by a man's coiled patience, and the granite blocks pressed cold through his knees the way they never had when he was fourteen and invincible beneath the moon. The tide came in low and slow, licking at the barnacled feet of the stones, and beyond them Saltmere harbor lay black except for one lantern swinging on the customs sloop — Hale's lantern, he had no doubt of it, though it hung innocent as any other light in any other port.

Maren would come from the north end of the strand. Corliss had said so with his eyes, if not his mouth; the courier had learned to speak without speaking during their weeks of letters and signals and the long terrible arithmetic of trust. *The breakwater, an hour past midnight, when the patrol turns at the salt house.* Ansel had counted the patrol's turns all evening from Hetta's cellar, counting to keep from shattering, and each time the boots went by overhead he thought: *not yet, not yet, not yet* — until finally it was yet, and he was climbing out through the coal chute into air that tasted of iron and storm-wash.

Now he waited, and the sea breathed against the stones, and every nerve in him was a struck match that refused either to catch or go out.

Six years. He had rehearsed this moment so many times that rehearsal had worn it thin as coin — her face, her voice, the first thing she'd say. In some versions she wept. In others she struck him for waiting so long. In the worst ones she never came at all, because Brann was a smuggler and smugglers sold people as readily as pearls, and this whole night was a net drawn tight around Ansel Eastwick's throat.

Then a shape detached itself from the dark at the northern end of the strand, quick and low, and stopped.

Not Maren's walk. He almost laughed at himself — six years, and he expected her to walk out of the sea unchanged? But then the shape came closer, bent under the wind, and there was something in the set of the shoulders, the way the head turned first toward the harbor lights and only then toward him, checking danger before comfort, and his heart did something that hurt more than drowning.

"Maren," he said. Just the name. It came out broken in half.

She froze, ten yards off, a wraith in oilskin and a hood dark as wet slate. For a long moment neither of them moved. A patrol lantern bobbed along the quay behind them, its glow sliding across the water like a hand feeling for something in the dark, and they both stood stone-still while it passed — two pieces of the breakwater, two more rocks the tide could ignore.

When the light swung away she crossed the distance in four strides and pulled back her hood, and there she was.

Thinner. Harder. A scar through one eyebrow he didn't know. Her hair cut short and rough, the color of it gone duller than the copper he remembered, and her eyes — her eyes were the same, gray-green and furious and bright, and they went over his face as if reading a letter she'd been told was destroyed.

"You got old," she whispered.

"You got dead."

Her breath caught. For one heartbeat he thought she would strike him after all, and he found he wanted it, wanted anything that wasn't this unbearable stillness between them. Then her hand came up — not to strike, but to touch, fingertips finding his jaw as if confirming he cast shadow, held weight, existed. Her fingers were freezing. They shook.

"I watched your ship sail," he said. His voice was failing him, cracking like ice. "I stood right here. I watched her go past the point and I told myself, *she'll write, she'll come back for the herring season, she'll—*" He stopped. Swallowed. "They gave us a funeral, Maren. Hetta sewed the mourning bands. Your mother's grave has a stone with your name on it and I've swept the moss off it every spring since."

"Ansel—"

"*Drowned,*" he said, and the word tore coming up. "Six years you let us bury you."

"Yes." No flinching. That was Maren, always — she took the blow full in the face and stood inside it. "Yes, I did. And I'd do it again, and I'll hate what it cost you until I die, and none of that is apology enough, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. Not here. Not with their boots on the quay every twenty minutes." Her voice dropped lower, fierce and quiet. "I need you to be angry later, Ansel. Be angry on the boat, be angry across an ocean, fill whole pages of it and send them to me. But tonight—" her hand was still on his jaw, and it trembled, and her composure cracked down the middle the way ice cracks, all at once, catastrophically — "tonight I need my friend."

He broke then. There was no dignity in it. He pulled her against him and she came, hard, her arms locking around his back with a strength that had nothing soft left in it, her face buried in his neck, and he felt her shaking and realized he was shaking too, both of them rattling together like rigging in a gale. She smelled of tar and salt and something underneath that was simply *her*, unchanged by drowning or exile or whatever she had been these six years, and the smell undid him worse than the touch.

"They said Voss ship went down with all hands," he managed into her hair. "Twelve souls. I read the manifest. Your name was on the manifest, Maren. In ink."

"I know whose name was on it."

She pulled back just far enough to look at him. In the dark her face was mostly shadow, but the harbor's distant lantern caught the edge of her cheekbone, the wet track down it that she made no move to hide.

"The *Vesta* went down," she said, "because Meridian scuttled her. You've seen the file. Don't tell me you haven't — Brann says you bled into that counting house like a tick. Insurance against hull and cargo, and twelve names to make it true." Her jaw tightened. "I wasn't supposed to be one of them. I was supposed to be dead in the water like the others. Instead I got fished out by a free-trader running contraband up the coast, and I spent three years deciding whether to stay dead or come back as a weapon." A sound that was almost a laugh, bitter as brine. "I chose weapon. It pays worse."

"And the agent lists," Ansel said. "The notices on the harbor board. *Enemy of the Crown, acting against charter interests—*"

"Meridian wrote the Crown's definition of enemy. Their charter lets them do that — you of all people know how much law fits inside a company seal. I've spent three years carrying proof of their wreck to anyone who'd take it. Courts abroad. Rival houses. Newspapers, God help me, men who print things for money instead of burning them." She searched his face. "It hasn't been enough. Paper alone isn't enough. It needed someone inside Saltmere who could reach the original file, who could swear to it, who knew these waters and these ledgers and these *people*. It needed—" she stopped, and her voice went strange, "—it needed to be you, and I swore to Corliss I wouldn't ask. That you'd built a life here and I had no right to burn it down twice."

"You asked anyway. With a letter."

"I asked anyway," she admitted. "I'm a coward about exactly one thing in this world."

Lantern light swept the quay again. They dropped together behind the seaward rank of stones, shoulders touching, and listened to the patrol pass — two men, unhurried, one complaining about the weather, the ordinary music of a town that didn't know it was a battlefield tonight. Ansel found Maren's hand in the dark and held it, and neither of them spoke until the light died around the corner of the salt house.

"One year," he said quietly.

"What?"

"My indenture. One year left on it. Twelve years I've paid, dawn dives, half my catch, half my father's debt stacked on top of mine because Meridian keeps books like spiders keep webs. One more year and I'm a free man on paper." He turned her hand over, traced the new calluses on her palm with his thumb. "I want you to understand what I'm telling you. If we're taken tonight, there's no year. There's a rope off the customs house beam and a name added to the harbor notices, and everything I am gets filed away in one of their ledgers as a loss written off against their costs."

"I know."

"And you're staying anyway. On this shore. To put papers in a magistrate's hand."

"I'm staying anyway."

"Why?" It came out harder than he meant. "You could be over open water by Friday with everything Brann carries. Let the file rot in their vault another season, let another diver's son sign his life into their book—"

"Because the next diver's son is *you*," she said, "and I have already let one person I love drown on paper. I will not do it twice. Not even to save him." Her grip tightened painfully. "That's the whole of it, Ansel. That's the ugly, stupid, unforgivable arithmetic. You taught me to dive. Did you think I'd forgotten which of us could hold his breath longer?"

A laugh escaped him — sudden, helpless, absurd — and he pressed his forehead against hers and let it shake out of him silently, because laughing on the breakwater at midnight was as dangerous as screaming. Six years of grief and fury and the thousand small betrayals of her absence, and here at the end of it all she was still the girl who'd once dared him to touch the bottom of the channel and then sat on the rocks cursing him for winning.

"The promise," she whispered.

His chest closed. "Don't—"

"No. Listen to me. Under this exact wall, before the packet took me, you said—" her breath hitched; she pushed through it — "you said, *whatever happens, whoever comes asking, if you send for me I'll answer.* Those words. Word for word. I've kept them alive six years like a coal in my fist, and I sent, Ansel. Through Corliss, through Brann, through every dangerous stranger I could find. And you answered."

"I answered," he said.

"That's the promise. Everything else—" she gestured, a small sharp motion that took in the harbor, the company, the gallows waiting patiently in all their futures — "everything else is weather."

Boots on the strand. Close — too close, the patrol turning early, cutting along the base of the breakwater instead of the quay, and Ansel flattened them both into the angle where the great stones met, Maren's back against his chest, his palm covering the back of her skull, both of them folded into a space no wider than a coffin. The lantern came on. Ten yards. Five. He could hear the men breathing, smell the pipe tobacco on them, see the wet gleam of the granite inches from his own nose.

"—telling you the third watch doubled since the Voss business," one was saying. "Hale's spooked. Something's moving and he doesn't know what."

"Hale's always spooked. Man sleeps with his ledger."

The laughter receded. The light receded. Ansel counted to two hundred before he let them breathe.

"We can't stay," Maren murmured against his collarbone. "Corliss said quarter hour and we've spent double. Where does Hetta's cellar open?"

"Coal chute, rear alley, third door past the chandlery. There's a fish crate against it — leave the crate."

"Brann puts back to sea before dawn light. Once the file reaches the magistrate and the packet sails, I go with the tide or I don't go at all." She pulled back and looked at him, and in the darkness her expression did something complicated — hope and dread braided into one cord. "And you? When it's done. When the warrant's signed and Hale's finished. What happens to Ansel Eastwick?"

"I stay," he said. "Someone has to stand in the hall and swear to it. The evidence means nothing from a dead woman's mouth — that's the whole trick of what Meridian did to you, they made you untestifiable. So I'll testify. Me, alive, indentured, owned down to my own two hands." He heard his own voice go steady and wondered distantly whose it was. "Let them take the year. Let them take all of it. You'll be past the point with the file in your kit and the truth loose in the world, and that buys more than my freedom ever would."

"No." Flat. Instant. "No, that's not—"

"It's exactly yes, and we have no time to argue it, and you know I'm right, and I can see you knowing it." He cupped her face in both hands. Her skin was freezing; his probably was too. "Six years ago I watched your ship sail and hated myself for every word I didn't say. I'm not doing that again. So here are the words, Maren Voss, and then you're going to run for that alley like the devil's own tide is after you, because it is."

The harbor clock began to strike. He spoke fast and low beneath it.

"I love you. I loved you at fourteen under this wall and I loved you at fifteen sweeping moss off a stone with your name on it, and I love you now, scar and all, weapon and all. Whatever happens on that quay tomorrow — whatever they take — that's yours. Take it aboard with the file."

For a moment she couldn't speak at all. Then she kissed him, brief and fierce and tasting of salt, and broke away with her jaw set like a woman boarding a ship in a gale.

"Survive," she said. "That's an order from an enemy agent. Survive, and sweep my mother's stone again next spring, and I'll come back when it's safe and argue with you about everything you just decided without asking me." She was already moving, low along the stones, hood up, a ghost reassembling itself out of the dark. At the seam of shadow where the strand met the alley she paused, one heartbeat, and looked back.

Behind her, up the slope of the quay, a second lantern had appeared where no patrol should be.

It didn't move like the watchmen moved. It stood at the harbor steps, still, deliberate, angled toward the breakwater — toward them — and beside it, faintly, the shape of a second man raised something to his eye. A glass. Or a signal shutter.

"Maren," Ansel breathed. "*Run.*"

She ran.
