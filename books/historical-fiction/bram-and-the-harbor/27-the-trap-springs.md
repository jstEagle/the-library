## 27. The Trap Springs

The breakwater at night was a spine of wet stone, and Ansel knew every vertebra of it.

Twelve years of diving had taught him the harbor the way other men knew their mothers' faces — the way the current leaned east after slack tide, the way the stones grew slick with weed past the third elbow, the places where the gaps between blocks ran deep enough to swallow a boot to the knee. He led Maren along the seaward side, below the line where the lamplight from the quay reached, moving when the patrol lanterns swung away and freezing when they swung back. She followed without question, her hand light on his sleeve, and it struck him — absurdly, in the middle of all this fear — that she trusted his silences more than most people trusted their own voices.

"Boat," she breathed against his ear.

He heard it a moment later: oars muffled with rags, the soft knock-knock of wood against water coming up the channel between the breakwater and the moored fleet. Not one boat. Two.

Ansel went cold in a way the sea had never made him cold.

"They knew," he whispered. "Hale knew before you ever set foot on the stone."

Maren's face was a pale smudge in the dark. Six years had changed her — thinned her, hardened the line of her jaw, put a scar through one eyebrow that he would ask about later, if there was a later — but her eyes were the same, gray as harbor water before weather, and they were moving now, fast, counting boats and lanterns and exits the way she had once counted gulls from her father's deck.

"He let us come ashore," she said quietly. "Corliss warned me. He said if the letters stopped being answered on schedule, assume the hand behind the silence is holding a pen of its own." Her jaw tightened. "I thought we had until dawn."

Lanterns bloomed at both ends of the quay at once.

For a heartbeat nobody moved — not Ansel, not Maren, not the dark shapes detaching themselves from the warehouse doorways with the unhurried confidence of men who had been paid to be certain. The whole harbor seemed to hold its breath: the water slapping stone, a hawker's abandoned barrow creaking, somewhere far off a dog barking itself hoarse at nothing.

Then Corliss stepped out of the shadow of the capstan house.

Ansel hadn't seen him since the cove meeting — the courier had gone to ground days ago, as arranged, waiting for tonight to row them out to Brann's schooner beyond the point. He came forward now into the lantern light with his hands loose at his sides and his voice pitched to carry, mild and almost pleasant, the voice of a man asking directions.

"Gentlemen. It's a foul night for fishing."

"Hold there," said the nearest of Hale's men — a broad figure with the company knot knotted at his shoulder, not constable's colors. Meridian kept its own law after dark.

"I am holding," Corliss agreed. "Though I'll tell you what I told your colleagues in Kingsport — I'm a mail carrier by trade, and I've a sworn obligation to the packets. Whatever you're hunting tonight, I'd take it kindly if you hunted it away from my route."

"There's no packet due," the man said.

"No," Corliss said. "There isn't."

And then everything happened at once, and Ansel would spend the rest of his life trying to make the pieces sit still in his memory.

Corliss kicked the lantern.

It left his hand like something thrown in anger and landed on the wet stones between himself and the patrol, and the oil spread fire in a bright ragged tongue across the breakwater's spine, and every eye in the harbor — every eye trained on the darkness where two fugitives stood — flinched toward the light. Human nature. Even Hale's men, drilled and paid, were human first.

"Run," Corliss said, not turning his head, not raising his voice. "The marsh path. *Now.*"

Ansel grabbed Maren's wrist and pulled her down off the landward edge of the breakwater, dropping the four feet to the shingle below, her boots hitting stone beside his with a crack that seemed loud as a cannon. Behind them the lantern fire roared up and men were shouting, and above it all Corliss's voice, still mild, still pleasant, saying something about the sanctity of His Majesty's mail that Ansel could not hear the end of because they were running.

Shingle gave way to mud, mud to the salt-crusted turf at the marsh margin, and the turf grabbed at their ankles with every step. Behind them a pistol cracked — once, twice — and Ansel flinched each time though the balls went nowhere near them; they were aimed upward or wide, warning shots, because dead fugitives could not be questioned and Hale wanted questions answered.

Or so Ansel told himself, and ran harder.

* * *

They did not speak for an hour.

The salt marsh west of Saltmere was a drowned country of channels and hummocks, black reeds standing ten feet tall in the dark, the ground treacherous in a way that changed with every tide. Ansel knew it the way he knew the breakwater — not from maps but from years of gathering samphire and gull eggs as a boy, from learning which green mats of weed floated over six feet of sucking mud and which floated over nothing. He took Maren's hand and put it on his belt at the small of his back.

"Hold on. Step where I step. If I stop, stop."

She held on.

Twice they dropped flat as lanterns swept the channels behind them — the pursuit was organized, spreading out along the marsh margin in pairs, calling softly to one another in a code of whistles that told Ansel these were not tavern men given a shilling and a torch. These were Meridian's own watchers, the ones who checked the pearl weights, the ones whose faces he had catalogued for twelve years without knowing why.

The third time the whistle-code sounded close, Maren tugged him sideways into a channel cut, shoulder-deep in cold black water, and they stood chest to chest among the reed roots while a pair of searchers passed eight feet above them on the bank, close enough that Ansel could smell the tar on their boots.

In the water, Maren began, very silently, to shake. Not with fear, he realized. With laughter.

"What?" he breathed.

"You used to do this," she whispered, barely a thread of sound. "When we were thirteen. You'd drag me out of bed before dawn and stand me in a creek until my feet went blue, and swear it was the only way to learn the harbor." Her eyes caught what little light the sky allowed. "I hated you for it. And here I am, alive because of it."

"We're not alive yet."

"No," she admitted. "But it's a fine old habit, keeping me breathing."

Something loosened in Ansel's chest that had been wound tight since the night Corliss first showed him her handwriting. He turned his face toward the channels and listened until the whistles faded northward, then eased them out of the cut and onward, mile by mile, hummock by hummock, deeper into the drowned country where no lantern would follow until morning.

* * *

They found high ground around the hour when the cold turns from bracing to dangerous — a shelf of old shell midden above the tide line, ringed with stunted pines, where generations of marsh eel-fishers had built fires before them. Ansel risked none. Instead he dug into the cache he had kept there since summer, wrapped in oilcloth against exactly this kind of need: a blanket, a tinderbox, a clay jug of water, a twist of dried fish, and bread gone hard as ship's biscuit.

Maren looked at the cache, then at him, and said nothing for a long moment.

"You planned for this," she said finally. "Before I ever came ashore. You kept a bolt-hole ready."

"I kept supplies," Ansel said. "Whether it was for you, or for me, or just because Hetta taught me never to trust a week that's going well — I couldn't say." He broke the bread and handed her the larger piece, an act so automatic he didn't notice it, and she noticed it completely.

They ate sitting shoulder to shoulder, wrapped in the single blanket, watching the distant smear of Saltmere's lights across three miles of black marsh. The pursuit lanterns had drawn back to the town. Somewhere out on the water, past the point, Brann's schooner would be riding at anchor under no lights at all, waiting for a signal that would not come tonight.

"Corliss got out," Maren said. It wasn't a question, but Ansel heard the plea folded inside it.

"The last I saw, he was standing over a burning lantern quoting maritime law at six armed men." Ansel turned the bread in his fingers. "He bought us the whole breakwater. But Maren — those weren't hirelings fumbling in the dark. They took him *alive*. They wanted him alive."

"Yes." Her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a woman reading a chart with a hole in it. "Corliss knows things. Names, dates, the routes. He knows enough to hang everyone he's ever carried letters for." She paused. "But he doesn't know the wreck file exists. He doesn't know what's in it. And he doesn't know about the magistrate. That was my secret, kept even from my own courier, because secrets travel in chains."

Ansel heard what she was doing — building the architecture of damage control aloud, tallying what a captured man could cost them — and he understood it, and he hated it, because underneath the tallying she was grieving and neither of them could afford to say so.

"He'll give them nothing," Ansel said.

"He'll give them nothing that matters," she corrected gently. "There's a difference, and it will cost him to know it."

The tide turned somewhere out in the dark, the channels beginning their slow reverse whisper. Ansel fed a few sticks into a tiny fire after all, screened by stone and pine, because Maren's hands had gone bloodless white and warmth was no longer a luxury. The flames threw small shadows across her scarred brow, and he found himself staring and made himself stop, and stared anyway.

"Six years," he said. "I stood at your family's grave marker and read 'lost at sea' and I believed it, because believing it hurt less than doubting it. Do you understand what that did? Every year on the anniversary I dove the wreck site. Alone. Past safe depth. Looking for—" He shook his head. "I don't know what. A ring. A hull plank. Something that would let me stop hoping, because hoping was drowning me slower than the water would have."

Maren watched him over the fire.

"And instead," he went on, "you were alive the whole time. Alive and working against the company that owns my debt, and I was diving pearls for the same company, polite as a clerk, keeping my little silences." His hands had closed into fists without his permission. "I'm angry at you. I want you to know that. I've earned the right to be angry for at least a day."

"Take two," Maren said. Her voice cracked on it, just barely. "I wanted to write. God knows I wrote — forty letters that never went anywhere because any letter to Saltmere was a letter to Meridian. You were safer thinking me dead. I told myself that so many times I almost believed it." She reached over and took one of his fists, worked it open finger by finger, and held his hand flat between both of hers like something rescued from deep water. "Almost."

The fire popped. Out across the marsh, a curlew called and was answered.

"The file," she said, when she could trust her voice again. "You saw it. Tell me exactly what it said, every entry, every initial. Not tonight's version. The true one."

So Ansel told her — the insurance ledger, the double entry for the *Voss Star*, the survey report dated *before* the sailing, the initials H.M. in the margin that belonged to Hale himself. He told it in the flat precise way he had been taught to recount cargo, and Maren listened the same way, interrupting twice only to confirm dates. Between them the little fire burned down to coals, and the story sat over it like iron in a forge.

When he finished, Maren was quiet for a long time.

"My father suspected," she said at last. "That's the whole of it, Ansel. He suspected, and he said so in a letter to the harbormaster at Kingsport, and the letter was intercepted, and three weeks later the *Voss Star* went down with all hands — except the girl hiding in the cordage locker, who swam two miles in November water because dying would have been convenient for them." She looked up, and the firelight in her gray eyes was hard and steady. "I have spent six years collecting proof that no court would touch, because Meridian's charter makes it the law in every port it owns. And now I'm sitting in a marsh with a pearl diver who stole the proof out of their own strongroom."

"Freed diver," Ansel said. "As of tonight, probably."

That startled a short laugh out of her — sharp, surprised, real. Then it died, and she looked at the distant lights of Saltmere, and her face did something complicated.

"One year," she said quietly. "Your indenture had a year left. I remember you counting it down under the breakwater like a man counting bars."

"It has no years left now," Ansel said. "Whatever it has, it isn't years."

"Ansel—"

"Don't." He said it gently but firmly, the way he'd say *hold on* in a current. "I know what you're going to offer. Don't offer it. I didn't steal that file to buy my passage out. I stole it because a girl I promised things to drowned for a company's arithmetic, and eleven sailors drowned with her, and my father drowned before that, and somebody has to stand up on dry land and say the numbers out loud." He fed the last stick to the coals. "Tomorrow, when it's light, we signal Brann from the outer channel. You go aboard with the ledger pages I copied, and I take the original file to Magistrate Corven's door."

"That's suicide dressed as principle."

"It's testimony," Ansel said. "Dressed as principle, yes. Maybe. But it's also the only version of this story where you get clean away *and* the truth survives ashore. If we both run, Hale burns the file, calls you a spy forever, and Saltmere goes on kneeling for another fifty years." He met her eyes. "I've done my kneeling."

Maren was silent. The curlew called again, closer, and the tide whispered through the channels, and somewhere far behind them a single lantern still crawled the marsh margin, patient as arithmetic.

"You've changed," she said at last, wonderingly. "Underneath everything. The boy I knew apologized for taking up room."

"The harbor did that," Ansel said. "It teaches you that silence keeps people safe. It just never mentions what the silence is keeping them safe *from*." He pulled the blanket higher around her shoulders. "Sleep. I'll watch. First gray light, we move."

She slept eventually, really slept, the sleep of someone who had decided to trust — curled against his side with the copied ledger pages sewn into her coat lining and the whole sea between her and the gallows. Ansel sat watch with his back against a wind-bent pine, the wreck file's contents laid out in his memory page by page, and watched Saltmere's lights burn across the water.

Panic had carried them this far. It would carry them no farther. From here on it had to be resolve — cold, deliberate, load-bearing, like a keel.

He counted the lights on the quay. One of them was moving, slow and regular, along the breakwater where Corliss had stood. Guarding it now, or searching it, or simply making sure the world knew the company owned even the stones.

*Sleep,* he told himself. *Tomorrow needs you awake.*

But he sat up all the same, watching the tide come back into the channels they'd crossed, filling their trail behind them inch by inch — and it was only as the first birds stirred that he understood the flaw in tomorrow, the thing panic had kept him from seeing all night:

Brann's schooner waited beyond the point. But the point was watched now too — had to be, if Hale had known about the breakwater. And Magistrate Corven's door was in Saltmere. In the town. Behind the patrols. Behind the quay where, in less than a day, Hale would be interrogating the one man who knew Ansel Eastwick's face, name, habits, and debts.

Somewhere across the black water, in a lit room above the harbor, Corliss was running out of hours.

And when they ran out, Hale would not need to hunt the marshes at all.

He would simply go home, and wait.
