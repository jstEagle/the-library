## 28. Marsh Night

The marsh did not let them go easily. It took them in ankle-deep and then knee-deep, sucking at their boots with every step, as though the ground itself had been paid to hold them. Ansel went first because Maren's eyes were not yet adjusted to the dark and because he knew these channels the way he knew the counting house stairs — by failure, by falling into them once and never again. Cordgrass hissed around their thighs. Somewhere behind them, faint as a held breath, Saltmere's harbor bell counted the hour, and then the wind took even that.

"Here." He stopped where a hummock of higher ground rose out of the reeds, a shelf of packed peat no bigger than a room, ringed with the black skeletons of old shrubs. It was dry. It was hidden. It was the best the marsh offered, and the marsh offered little.

Maren lowered herself down beside him with a sound that was half laugh, half groan. "Six years," she said, "and I'd forgotten how a place could hate you and shelter you in the same breath."

"That's Saltmere."

"That's everything I grew up in."

They sat shoulder to shoulder, not quite touching, both of them still listening for pursuit. The patrol lanterns had gone west along the seawall an hour ago, chasing the shape Corliss had made of himself so that these two shapes could slip east into the mud. Ansel tried not to think about what being taken meant for a man with no town, no family, no charter to hide behind. He failed, then set it aside the way he set aside everything that would stop his hands from working. There would be time for grief later. Grief kept.

The cold came up through the peat like something living. Maren shivered once, hard, and Ansel pulled off his coat — heavy wool, company-stamped, smelling of brine and pearl-oyster slime — and put it around her shoulders without asking. She looked at him in the dark. Neither of them said anything about it. Some transactions were older than speech.

"You're thinner," she said finally.

"You're alive. We'll call it even."

That earned him a real laugh, brief and low, quickly smothered against her wrist out of habit. Six years of being hunted had taught her to laugh the way divers learned to breathe: only when there was air to spare.

* * *

Ansel built a fire the way Hetta had taught him for nights on the marsh road — a scooped pit below the tide line, driftwood dried in the heart, flame no higher than his hand, screened on three sides by cut turf so the light fell nowhere but up. Anyone watching from the seawall would see nothing. The sky above the pit glowed faintly orange, a small captive dawn.

Maren held her hands over it and told him.

She started at the beginning, which surprised him; he had expected her to start at the part that mattered, the way people do when they've rehearsed a story too long alone. But she started with the *Meridian Star* riding low in the water at the quay, six years ago, taking on cargo for the northern run. She started with her father signing papers he didn't read because Meridian's clerk had a soft voice and her father had a tired one.

"The hold was wrong," she said. "I was thirteen, I wasn't supposed to be aboard, but I used to sleep in the stern locker when my father ran late. That night I was curled up back there and two of the crew came down with a lamp. They weren't fixing anything. They were fitting something. A plate — iron, hinged, bolted inside the hull just above the waterline. And a mechanism. I didn't know what it was then. I know now. A slow lever, weighted. Saltwater does the rest after enough hours."

Ansel watched the fire. His hands were very still, which was how he listened — he had learned young that stillness made people keep talking, and he wanted every word of this.

"They scuttled their own ship?"

"With forty-one souls aboard, twenty-two of them company men who were owed wages the company preferred not to pay." Her voice stayed level, but her hands around her knees had gone white. "Insurance against the loss. And insurance of a second kind — my father had begun keeping his own books. Small ones. Cargo that arrived without ever being entered. Pearls that left the harbor lighter than they'd been weighed. Hale's pearls, Ansel. Your quota, all those years — some of it never touched a ledger at all. My father knew. He'd started writing letters north."

"And the wreck ended the letters."

"And the wreck ended the letters." She fed a twig to the fire and watched it catch. "Except the sea is a poor conspirator. It doesn't always finish what it's asked."

* * *

She told the survival plainly, without drama, which somehow made it worse.

When the plate gave way — three hours out, in the dark, exactly as designed — the ship went down fast by the bow. She had been awake in the stern locker. She got out through the lazarette hatch. She remembered the water as cold beyond meaning, and then as nothing, and then as daylight on gray swells among floating crates of company china, absurdly intact, bobbing like toys.

A fishing boat out of Kell Harbor picked her up, half-dead, tangled in net cork. The fisherman's wife nursed her through fever. By the time she could stand, the newspapers had already given the sea its verdict: *Meridian Star lost with all hands; suspected rogue weather.* All hands. She had read her own name in the list of the drowned, printed in a stranger's tidy type.

"I could have come home," she said. "I thought about it. Walked as far as the crossroads outside Kell before I turned back. But if I came home alive, I was a loose thread. Forty-one dead, Ansel, and one girl walking around who'd seen the plate go in. Hale doesn't leave threads. You know that better than anyone — you've spent twelve years learning which silences keep people breathing."

"So you became someone else."

"I became useful to people who hate Meridian as much as I do." She said the word *hate* carefully, like setting down something hot. "There are trading concerns in the north that have been bled by Meridian's charter for thirty years. They were delighted to find a survivor with a memory and a grudge. They trained me. Taught me ledgers, codes, how to copy a shipping manifest so well a clerk can't tell it from the original. How to be invisible in harbor towns. I've spent six years being a ghost with excellent handwriting."

"That's what the letters were. Copies. Forgeries."

"Evidence. Every letter I sent through Corliss was baited — real dates, real figures, things that could be checked against what Meridian thinks is private. I've been building a case for six years, Ansel. Not revenge. A case. Revenge burns up. A case can be handed to someone."

"To a magistrate," Ansel said slowly. "One who isn't bought."

"If one exists."

He thought of Magistrate Corrow — ruined years ago by a Meridian lawsuit over a drowning inquiry, pensioned down to a saltmarsh backwater to rot politely. He thought of the man's face when Ansel had laid the wreck file on his desk at midnight, and how long he had looked at it before speaking. He kept that thought for later. First he needed the rest.

* * *

"Why me?" The question had been sitting in his chest since the cove, since Brann's report first carried his name north. Now he let it out. "Six years, Maren. You could have stayed dead. It's safer. Instead you send a stranger to my quay with letters in your hand — the one hand in the world that could crack me open. Why?"

The fire popped. Out in the marsh, a bird cried twice and went silent.

"Because you're the proof I don't have," she said. "Listen. Everything I know, I saw as a child hiding in a locker. It's good, but it's testimony from a dead girl — a dead girl who's been an enemy agent for six years. Hale will say I invented it all. Half the world will believe him. What breaks him is paper from inside. The wreck file. The double ledger. The insurance claim with the impossible timing. Paper that lived in Meridian's own counting house, handled by Meridian's own hands." She turned to look at him fully for the first time since the fire was lit. "You've been inside that counting house since you were eleven years old. You know where everything sleeps. And you owe them nothing but a debt they engineered — your mother signed away your childhood over a drowning *they caused*, Ansel. I found that out last year. Your father's boat. The *Gannet*. Same clerk. Same soft voice."

The world tilted slightly. Ansel found he had gripped a fistful of peat.

"My father's drowning wasn't—"

"There's no record. Of course there's no record. But the pattern holds — a diver who asks questions, a storm that comes conveniently, an insurance settlement paid to a widow who signs an indenture in the same week." Maren's voice cracked, just once, and she mastered it. "I'm not telling you to make you hate them. You've earned the right to your own hatred. I'm telling you so you understand that when I reached for you, I wasn't reaching for a tool. I was reaching home."

He couldn't speak for a while. The tide muttered somewhere out in the dark, patient as arithmetic.

"I almost refused Corliss," he managed at last. "First day. He offered and I walked away and lay in my loft all night staring at the rafters."

"What changed?"

"A file. I went back into the counting house and read the wreck file, and it confirmed everything you just told me, and I understood that I had been keeping silences for twelve years for a company that drowned my father and wrote it down as weather." He shook his head. "No. What changed was smaller than that. What changed was a line in your first letter. *Tell me if the breakwater still stands.* Like the rest of it was business and that one sentence wasn't."

"It wasn't," Maren said quietly.

* * *

The fire burned down to a red eye. Ansel fed it the last of the driftwood and they moved closer together, because the cold demanded it and because neither of them pretended otherwise anymore. Her head found his shoulder. His arm went around her under the borrowed coat. Mud-splattered, salt-stiff, exhausted, they fit against each other with the ease of two people who had been shaped for this years ago and stored apart.

"When you went down with your father's dive line," she murmured, "the summer before the indenture. I stood on the quay and watched the water for two hours."

"I know. Hetta told me. Afterward."

"I decided then that if the sea took everyone I loved, I'd take something back from the sea. It took me six years to realize the thing worth taking back wasn't from the sea at all." She was quiet a moment. "It's from Meridian."

"Then we'll take it."

"Don't say it lightly."

"I'm not saying it at all. I'm doing it. Tomorrow I go back into the counting house for the original file — Corrow won't move without it, and he shouldn't. Copies can be called forgeries. The original has Hale's own seal on the insurance annex." He felt her stiffen against him. "Don't," he said. "I've been in and out of that building twelve years. Hale watches me, yes. But he watches me the way a man watches a clock he believes is honest."

"And if the clock is lying?"

"Then it strikes once," Ansel said, "and stops."

Above the dead turf screen, the sky had begun its slow turn toward gray. The tide had shifted; he could hear it in the changed voice of the channels, water moving back toward the harbor, draining the marsh flat by flat. In perhaps two hours the packet would begin making ready at the outer quay, and Corliss — if Corliss was still free, if he had bought them their hours at the price they feared — would need to get word to Brann that the exchange moved tonight.

Maren lifted her head from his shoulder. In the dying firelight her face was hollowed and fierce and entirely awake.

"One more thing," she said. "Before we plan anything else, you should hear the part I haven't told anyone. Not Corliss. Not Brann. The reason the wreck file matters more than even Corrow understands." She drew a breath. "The plate in the *Star*'s hull wasn't fitted by crew. The two men I saw that night — Ansel, one of them signed the manifest as supercargo. He rode every northern run Meridian insured after that. He's still on their rolls. He's in Saltmere now."

The cold went through Ansel that had nothing to do with the marsh.

"Who?"

"Hale's junior clerk," Maren said. "The one who sold us out."
