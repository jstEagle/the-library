## 29. Corliss in Chains

The cell smelled of tar and old rope, which meant it had once held fish, which meant the Meridian Trading Company had converted a brine-house into a place for keeping men the way they kept ledgers — flat, stacked, and audited twice daily. Corliss appreciated that about his captors. It was good to know what you were dealing with. A man who understood he was an entry in a book could predict how he would be amended.

His wrists were chained to a ring bolted low in the wall, so that sitting was possible only if he folded himself like cargo. They had taken his coat, his boots, the little brass compass he'd carried through two wars and three aliases, and — this was the loss that stung — the hollow heel of his right boot, where four letters in Maren Voss's hand had ridden from Saltmere's quay until a junior clerk with quick hands and quicker cowardice had found them.

He had counted the hours by the change of light through the barred vent near the ceiling: gray to white, white to gold, gold back down to gray. One night and one day. Somewhere out there, past the counting house and the harbor and the breakwater where two people had once made a promise under an indifferent moon, Ansel Eastwick was either running or already taken. Corliss laid odds on running. The diver had the look of a man who knew how to hold his breath.

*Hold it longer than they think you can,* he thought, testing the chain. *That's the whole art.*

The lock turned at dusk.

They came in threes, always threes — Hale in front because vanity required it, a constable behind because fear required that, and between them a narrow man with a leather case whom Corliss had not seen before. The narrow man set his case on the single stool, opened it, and began laying out instruments with the unhurried care of a surgeon or a jeweler. Pliers. A small iron brazier on legs. A coil of wire. A wooden wedge, sanded smooth.

Corliss looked at these things the way one looks at weather coming in over open water. Not with hope of avoiding it. Only with interest in its timing.

"Master Corliss," Hale said. He had a voice built for counting houses, dry and precise, each word weighed against its cost. "You've been in Saltmere nine days. You arrived on the mail packet with no luggage worth mentioning, no trade, no letters of introduction, and you've spent those nine days asking questions in taverns and lofts that polite men don't ask." He drew the stool closer and sat, unhurried, knees nearly touching Corliss's own. "I am going to tell you how this evening goes, and then we are going to have the conversation I've been looking forward to since the clerk handed me your letters."

"I'd offer you a chair," Corliss said, "but you brought your own."

Hale ignored this. "This evening goes in stages. In the first stage, my colleague — Master Quill, formerly of His Majesty's customs enforcement — will ask you questions. If your answers satisfy him, the evening ends and you take breakfast tomorrow as a man awaiting trial for espionage, which is unpleasant but survivable. If your answers do not satisfy him, Master Quill will proceed to methods that are not sanctioned by any charter but are, let us say, tolerated in harbor towns where the Crown's attention is thin." He folded his hands. "I want to be honest with you, Corliss, because dishonesty is expensive and I deal only in what pays. No one is coming for you. Your confederates cannot reach you here. The woman you serve is beyond the tide line by now, if she ever existed at all. What you carry in your body — names, times, routes — is the last asset of a failed operation. Assets get liquidated."

"That's a great deal of speech," Corliss said, "for a man who claims to deal only in what pays."

A flicker crossed Hale's face — not anger. Something more dangerous: recognition. Men of the ledger hated meeting their own kind on the wrong side of the arithmetic.

Quill began.

He asked the questions Corliss expected, and Corliss gave him the answers Corliss had prepared before he ever stepped off the packet. Name: Josiah Corliss, factor's agent out of Greywater, discharged for debts. Employer: none living; he had worked, he said, for a shipping syndicate now dissolved, whose principals had paid him to recover documentary evidence of fraud committed against them by Meridian — a civil matter, a matter of money, nothing political. The woman Voss he had never met; her name had come to him secondhand; the letters were payment for services rendered, given him by an intermediary whose face he had seen once, by lantern light, at a distance.

None of it was true. All of it was *consistent*, which in interrogation is worth ten truths, because truth has edges and consistency doesn't. Quill circled the story four times, approaching from different angles, probing for the seam where the lie met itself. Corliss felt the circling like a hand feeling along a wall in the dark, and kept his breathing slow, and gave each answer at the same unhurried pace, and looked at the middle distance where a man remembering would look.

There was one moment — Quill asked what the intermediary had worn on his feet, a question with no purpose except to catch a man inventing — and Corliss felt the whole structure tremble, and answered, "Boots. Harbor boots, salt-stained. He stood with his weight on his heels, like a man who works on deck," and saw the question pass. He did not let himself feel relief. Relief is a door left open.

"You understand," Quill said mildly, setting down his notebook, "that I have done this work longer than you have done whatever it is you do. And I can tell you are not a factor's agent. A factor's agent would have broken by now."

"Then break me and be done," Corliss said. "I'm tired, I'm cold, and your director repeats himself."

Hale laughed — one short syllable, genuine, quickly put away. Then he nodded to Quill, and the evening entered its second stage.

* * *

Pain, Corliss had learned long ago, is a language, and every language can be translated. The trick was never to refuse translation — refusal breaks men faster than iron — but to become fluent in it quickly and speak it back in your own words until the pain grew bored. *So this is the shoulder saying its piece,* he told himself when Quill took the arm out of true with a sound like wet canvas tearing. *Very well. The shoulder has made its argument. I remain unpersuaded.*

He screamed. He permitted himself screaming because screaming was expected and withholding it invited escalation, and because a scream, oddly, is private — no one hears what you actually mean by it. What he meant by it was: *Ansel, run. Hetta's cellar, the marsh path, the eel boats. Run while I spend this.*

Because that was the transaction, though no ledger recorded it. Every minute Quill spent on Corliss was a minute not spent on the quay, on the marsh roads, on the cove watch. Every hour of Corliss's stubbornness was an hour purchased for a diver's son and a drowned girl. He had made worse trades for better prices. He thought of the compass in Hale's pocket and hoped the man would keep it; it had never once pointed anywhere useful.

They asked about the letters. Who wrote them. Where they were written. How many copies existed. Whether others had been sent abroad. Corliss gave them the intermediary again, consistent as a heartbeat, and Quill's pliers found the webbing between two fingers, and Corliss screamed his translation and held the line.

Somewhere past midnight the questions changed character, and this was the moment Corliss had dreaded more than the iron, because character-change means the interrogator has stopped fishing and started aiming. Quill leaned close and asked, quietly, about the counting house. About the loft above it. About whether anyone else had been up there. About the wreck file for the ship called *Meridian's Grace*, and whether its absence from the archive had been noticed, and who might have taken it.

And Corliss, hanging by his ruined shoulder from the ring bolt, understood with perfect clarity that they did not know. That the junior clerk had sold Corliss and the letters, but had not seen Ansel's face in the archive, or had seen it and not connected it, or had connected it and not yet spoken. That Ansel still walked free partly unbetrayed, and that everything now depended on whether a broken courier could keep one name out of his mouth.

Names are the hardest thing. Facts can be buried under consistency, but a name must simply not exist, and pain makes all sorts of things exist.

"Eastwick," Quill said. "The pearl diver. Nine days you watched him. Why?"

"He was convenient," Corliss said. "A diver knows the water. I needed someone who could move cargo by night. I approached him. He refused." This much was nearly true, and nearly-true is the strongest timber. "He wanted no part of it. Ask him yourself, when you find him. He'll tell you he sent me away."

It was beautiful, in its way — offering them Ansel as a refuser, an innocent, a man whose guilt was only proximity — knowing they would eventually lay hands on the boy anyway and hear him deny it, and perhaps believe. It was also the most dangerous card in his hand, played early because there was no later left.

Hale studied him for a long time. "You're spending yourself," he said at last, almost gently. "Buying minutes for people who are already caught. I've seen men do it. It looks noble from inside. From out here it looks like waste."

"Everything looks like waste," Corliss said through cracked lips, "from the side that's paying less."

* * *

They left him at dawn, or what passed for dawn through the vent — a graying of the dark, the gulls starting up over the harbor. Quill packed his instruments without triumph. Professionals don't celebrate; they bill. Hale lingered at the door.

"The magistrate has signed nothing yet," Hale said. "He is old, he is cautious, and he owes me the courtesy of proof. By the time he convenes anything, the tide will have turned and I will have your diver and whatever papers he's carrying, and your whole enterprise will resolve into a line in an expense column." He paused. "You could shorten all this. One name, spoken clearly, and Quill sets your arm tonight."

Corliss lifted his head. The motion cost him; he paid it. "Director Hale," he said, "in thirty years of this work, I have known exactly four kinds of men. Men who break fast. Men who break slow. Men who die without breaking. And men like you, who never need breaking, because they were hollow all the way down." He smiled with the half of his mouth that still worked. "Guess which kind buys breakfast tomorrow."

Hale looked at him the way one looks at a bad investment one is nonetheless forced to carry on the books. Then the door closed, and the bar fell, and Corliss was alone with the gulls and the arithmetic.

He took stock as best he could. Right shoulder dislocated and badly reset by Quill's rough art. Left hand ruined in two fingers — he flexed them and was rewarded with a white flare, but they moved, which meant they might heal, which meant someday he might hold a pen, which was the only instrument he had ever truly needed. Ribs: uncertain, painful to test, so untested. He was forty-nine years old, and he estimated — with the detached amusement of a man auditing his own estate — that he had perhaps three days of usefulness left before fever or the shoulder finished the account.

Three days. He turned the figure over.

Enough. It would have to be enough, because the plan he had glimpsed forming in Hale's mouth — *the tide will have turned and I will have your diver* — told him the endgame was now, that Ansel and the girl and the papers were moving toward some door, and Hale was standing in front of it, patient as a debt. Which meant Corliss's remaining capital was simple: he was a loose thread Hale could not afford to leave visible in front of a magistrate. As long as Corliss lived, unsilenced and untried, there was a witness in chains whom the magistrate's caution would require acknowledging. They could not hang him quietly before trial; Hale's whole strength was procedure, procedure was his armor, and armor cuts both ways.

So: stay alive, stay loud, stay *visible*. Demand a magistrate. Scream about charters and the Admiralty and the Crown's postmasters until the town heard there was a prisoner in the brine-house who kept talking about paper. Make himself a public embarrassment, because embarrassment delays, and delay was the only coin he had left to mint.

He began immediately. He sang. Badly, deliberately, an old Greywater stevedore's song with verses that named no one and insulted everyone, and when the constable rattled the door he stopped just long enough to call out, cheerfully, that he wished to make formal complaint before a justice of the peace regarding theft of personal property, specifically one brass compass, and that he would recite the complaint hourly until heard. Then he sang again.

From the vent above, the gulls wheeled over the morning tide, and far out past the breakwater a shape was moving that Corliss could not see and did not need to — a packet taking on cargo, or a fishing boat riding low, or a schooner standing off the shallows with a lantern dowsed. It didn't matter which. What mattered was that somewhere on the water, right now, a decision was being made by a young man with strong lungs and a soft heart, and Corliss knew — the way an old courier knows weather — exactly which two doors that decision stood between, and how narrow the space between them, and how little time.

*Choose the harder door,* he willed across the stones and the harbor water, too weak now to sing, whispering it into the dark like a prayer to no god in particular. *The safe one leads back to their books. Choose the door that costs you.*

In the counting house above the harbor, a lamp went out.
