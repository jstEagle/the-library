## 30. The Choice

The marsh gave them nothing but itself: black water, bent reeds, the smell of rot and salt, and a sky the color of an old bruise going green at the edges. Ansel crouched in the lee of a half-sunk skiff that some fisherman had abandoned years ago, its hull furred with moss, and watched the lights of Saltmere glitter across the flats like a string of coins dropped on dark cloth.

Maren sat with her back against the skiff's ribs, knees drawn up, arms wrapped around them. She had not stopped shivering since they'd waded in from the breakwater, though she'd stopped pretending it was only the cold. Somewhere behind them, toward the harbor, a bell was still ringing — three strokes, pause, three strokes. The signal Hale used when he wanted every constable and every hired man on the company's payroll to converge.

They had heard boots on the boardwalk as they ran. They had heard Corliss shout — one short, hard syllable, cut off like a rope snapped taut. Ansel carried that sound in his chest now, next to his heartbeat, where it would stay.

"They'll work him over tonight," Maren said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"Corliss knew what he was walking into." Ansel said it because it was true, and because saying it aloud was the only way to keep it from becoming a prayer. "He built himself for this. A courier who can't be broken is worth ten ships."

"He's still flesh."

"I know." Ansel looked at her. In the last gray light he could see her face clearly for the first time in six years — the sharpness of it, the new lines around her eyes that hadn't come from laughter, the small scar at her jaw he didn't know the story of and might never ask. She was real. That was the miracle he kept having to rediscover, the way a diver keeps rediscovering that air exists. She was real, and she was cold, and she was hunted, and everything that happened next depended on him.

"We should keep moving," he said. "There's a channel through the marsh that comes out past the oyster beds. Brann keeps a boat moored near the third marker stake — I saw it when we came through. If we take it now, tonight, we can reach the fishing fleet before dawn."

Maren turned her head slowly. "The fishing fleet."

"Old Tam Verrick owes me. He doesn't ask questions; he never has. He runs herring north twice a week, and his hold is big enough to hide a woman and a strongbox of papers. By tomorrow night you'd be forty miles out, past the patrol line, and nobody in Saltmere would ever have to know you came ashore at all."

"And you?"

Ansel felt the question land the way a stone lands in still water — no splash, just the slow spreading of rings. He had known it was coming. He had been carrying it since the breakwater, since the moment he'd pulled her back from the lantern light of the patrol and felt, absurdly, joyfully, alive.

"I go back," he said.

* * *

She stared at him. Then she laughed — a short, bitter sound with no humor in it at all, the laugh of someone who has been lied to by professionals and recognizes the craft.

"You go back. Into the counting house. Into Hale's hands. After tonight, after the trap, after Corliss—" She stopped, pressed her lips together, started again with terrible calm. "Ansel. They will hang you. Not trial-and-release hang you. Not make-an-example hang you. They will hang you from the customs house beam before the week is out, and they will call it piracy or sedition or theft of company property, and the town will watch because the town always watches, and Meridian will own your debt right down to your corpse."

"My debt dies with me either way," Ansel said. "That's the one thing about indenture nobody advertises."

"That is not funny."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. But it's arithmetic, and I've lived my whole life inside their arithmetic, so let me finish the sum." He shifted closer, until his shoulder touched hers, until he could feel her shaking through the contact. "Listen to me. If we run — if I put you on Verrick's boat and vanish into the marshes or sign onto some tramp steamer out of a port two hundred miles south — then what do we have? You're free. Maybe. For a while. And Meridian goes on. Hale goes on. The wreck file sits in that iron cabinet in the counting house, and the Voss ship stays a tragedy, an act of God, a storm that drowned eleven people who were only cargo to begin with. Your father's name stays a lie. My father's name stays a lie. Every diver who goes down on Meridian's ropes and comes up bleeding stays a lie."

"The evidence—"

"Is paper," Ansel said. "Paper in a cabinet. Paper that means nothing unless someone stands in front of a magistrate and swears to it. Paper that means nothing unless there's a man willing to say: I read it, I know it's true, and I will pay whatever they charge me for saying so." He took a breath. "I've spent twelve years learning which silences keep people safe. I'm very good at silence, Maren. It's the only trade Meridian ever taught me. And I am telling you that if I run now, I will spend the rest of my life being good at it, and it will eat me alive from the inside, and I will deserve it."

The bell across the water had stopped ringing. In its place there was only the marsh — wind in the reeds, the tick of settling water, somewhere far off the cry of a night bird that sounded almost human.

"There's another way," Maren said at last, and her voice had changed. Quieter. Careful. The voice, Ansel realized, of a woman who had spent six years planning operations, weighing risks, spending lives like currency because the cause required it. "You don't have to be the one who stands up. Give me the file. I have people — not just Brann. People in the capital, people in the Admiralty courts, journalists, a member of Parliament who owes my organization more than he wants anyone to know. I can burn Meridian down from London. You don't need to stand on a dock and let them arrest you. That's — Ansel, that's martyrdom, and martyrdom is just vanity with better lighting."

"It's not martyrdom if it works."

"It won't work without a witness here. And you know it." She closed her eyes. When she opened them again, something in them had cracked — the operator falling away, leaving the girl from the breakwater, the girl who had once told him she hated the sea and loved it anyway, the way you hate and love a parent. "I buried you," she whispered. "Do you understand that? Six years ago I stood on a deck in a foreign harbor and I wrote your name in a book of the dead, because writing it anywhere else hurt too much. I made myself stop hoping. I made myself into a person who doesn't hope, because hope is a liability in my line of work. And now you're sitting in the mud telling me you want to die for a filing cabinet, and I am asking you — I am begging you — don't make me bury you twice. I won't survive it. I'm not sure I survived the first time."

Ansel reached out and took her hand. Her fingers were ice.

"Maren." He waited until she looked at him. "What did you come back for?"

"You know what I came back for."

"Say it anyway."

She exhaled, long and unsteady. "The file. The original file. The magistrate — whoever he is, whatever he is — won't move without the original document in his hands, and copies can be denounced as forgery, and Brann's people can't get into the counting house because none of them can pass Hale's door without a warrant and a search party. Only you can. You've done it once already."

"So the plan needs me alive and free long enough to steal it. And then?"

"Then you disappear. North, with me, on the packet. We both go. Let London do the rest."

"And the magistrate?" Ansel asked gently. "The one whose career Meridian broke eight years ago over the dredging contracts? The one who drinks alone and signs nothing and hates them more than he hates anyone alive? If the file leaves this coast in your pocket, what happens to him? What happens to Saltmere? Do you think Hale doesn't have successors? Do you think the charter doesn't just pass to the next company with deeper pockets and the same arithmetic? You can burn Meridian down in London, Maren, and Saltmere will still be Saltmere — owned, silent, diving on their ropes, keeping the silences that keep us safe. Unless somebody makes it different here. Here, where it happened. In daylight. On the record. With a magistrate's seal on it."

He could see her fighting it. He could see the strategist and the girl at war behind her eyes, and he did not push, because he had learned — from the sea, of all teachers — that you cannot force a thing to surface. You can only wait below it, patiently, with your hands open.

"Justice outside the law," she said finally. "That's what Corliss called it. He said you'd understand."

"Corliss understands a lot of things." Ansel's throat tightened. "Let's hope he understood this one too: that a man who gives up nothing vital buys time. That's what he's doing right now, in whatever room Hale put him in. Buying time. Spending himself so that we have until morning. I won't waste what he's buying."

Maren was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, "Tell me about the magistrate."

"His name is Aldous Wrenn. He was harbor magistrate when I was a boy — a good one, strict but fair, the kind of man who actually walked the quay and knew divers' names. Then Meridian needed the east-channel dredging contract approved and Wrenn refused to certify their survey. Within a year he'd been investigated for corruption — fabricated, all of it, but thorough — stripped of his seniority, reassigned to hearing customs disputes and stray-dog complaints. He stayed. Everyone thought he'd resign. He stayed, and he got old, and he got careful, and he signed nothing without reading it four times." Ansel paused. "My mother sold mussels to his housekeeper for years. He knew my father. He spoke at the funeral — briefly, carefully, the way careful men speak. He owes us nothing. That's why I trust him. Gratitude can be bought. Damage can't."

Maren looked out across the black water toward the distant lights of the town. When she spoke again, her voice was steady, but Ansel heard what it cost her.

"If we do this — if we go to Wrenn — there's no second option after. You understand that. Once that file touches a magistrate's desk, it's a legal proceeding, and legal proceedings have shapes, and Hale knows every shape. He'll have constables at Wrenn's door within the hour of our arrival. He'll have warrants. He may already have the magistrate himself compromised — we don't know, we can't know, we're gambling everything on the honor of a broken man."

"Yes."

"And if Wrenn fails us — if he's frightened, or bought, or simply tired — then we've handed Meridian the one thing they need: proof, in their own town, under their own nose, that we have the file. They'll take it back, destroy it, and hang us both with the paperwork neatly stamped."

"Yes," Ansel said again.

"Then say why." Her grip tightened on his hand, hard enough to hurt. "Say it plainly, Ansel Eastwick, because I have planned operations where men died, and I have learned that the ones who die willingly are the dangerous ones, and I need to hear you say you are not one of them. Tell me you mean to live through this."

And there it was — the fulcrum, the balance point on which the whole night turned. Ansel looked at their joined hands, hers frozen, his scarred white from twelve years of rope and salt water, and he searched himself honestly, the way he searched a dive site before going down: for currents, for hazards, for the truth underneath.

"I mean to live," he said. "Not because I'm afraid to die — although I am, I found that out on the breakwater tonight, my whole body screaming at me to run. I mean to live because dying is easy and it proves nothing. A dead witness convinces no one. A dead diver is just another entry in Meridian's ledger, and I have spent my entire life as an entry in their ledger, and I am finished with it. I want to stand in Wrenn's hall tomorrow morning with that file in my hands and watch Hale's face while it's read aloud. I want to hear my father's name said true in a room full of people. I want—" His voice caught, and he let it. "I want the life after. The one where I take work on the water because I choose it, and write letters, and grow old enough to be boring. I want that. So I'm not offering you a martyr, Maren. I'm offering you a plan that requires nerve, timing, and a great deal of luck. Same as any dive."

Maren laughed then — really laughed, wet-eyed and shaky, the first true sound he'd heard from her since the breakwater. She leaned her head against his shoulder, and for a moment neither of them moved, and the marsh held them like a cupped palm.

"All right," she said. "All right. Wrenn. At dawn — no, before dawn. Before Hale's people are fully awake, before the packet traffic starts. How do we get to his door without walking past the customs house?"

"There's a lane behind the chandlery that comes up under his garden wall. Hetta knows it — her late husband used to deliver wine to Wrenn's kitchen, back when Wrenn still gave dinner parties. She can walk us to the wall and knock on the kitchen door herself. Wrenn's housekeeper trusts her." Ansel hesitated. "Hetta's hiding us in her cellar tomorrow if this goes wrong. She lost a son to Meridian. She's decided which side she's on."

"Your landlady."

"My landlady." He smiled faintly. "Saltmere is smaller than it looks, and older than it seems. Half the town is waiting for someone to go first."

"Then let's not die before breakfast," Maren said. She straightened, and he watched the operator come back into her spine, the planner settle over her face like armor — but softer now, alloyed with something that hadn't been there an hour ago. "The file. Where exactly is it?"

"Second floor, records room, iron cabinet, third drawer. The key hangs on a hook behind the clerk's desk — Hale thinks nobody knows. The lock is old; I've opened it once already." He met her eyes. "I go alone. You wait with Hetta. If I'm taken, you take the copies Brann holds and run north, and you do exactly what you said — burn them down from London. Promise me that part. Promise me you won't come looking."

"I promise nothing of the kind."

"Maren—"

"I promise," she said, after a moment, "to consider it. That's the best offer you're getting from a woman who buried you once."

It was, Ansel knew, the most honest bargain he would strike all night. He took it.

They rose together, stiff and mud-caked, and stood a moment in the dark among the reeds, looking at the far lights of Saltmere — the counting house with its one lit window where a clerk worked the night ledger, the customs house beam where men had hanged before, the low dark shape of the harbor magistrate's house with its single chimney standing against the graying sky.

Somewhere out on the water, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of oars — measured, disciplined, many. A patrol boat, sweeping the flats, coming between them and the channel to Brann's boat. Between them and the fishing fleet. Closing the last door on the easy road even as they chose the hard one.

Maren heard it too. She looked at Ansel, and in the first thin light of the false dawn her face was pale and set and certain.

"Well," she said. "No boat, then. It seems the sea has voted."

"The sea doesn't vote," Ansel said, taking her hand. "It just tells you what you already decided."

They turned toward the town, toward the chandlery lane and Hetta's cellar and the magistrate's door, and began to walk.
