## 32. The Magistrate's Door

The magistrate's house sat at the end of Rope Walk, where the cobbles gave way to mud and the smell of the tanneries replaced the smell of salt. It was a narrow, two-story building with a slate roof gone green at the edges and a lamp burning in the front window — the only lit window on the street. Ansel had walked past it a thousand times in twelve years and never once looked at it directly. You didn't look at the magistrate's house in Saltmere. You looked away from it, the way you looked away from Meridian's counting house, from Hale's office, from all the places where power sat behind glass and decided what your life was worth.

Tonight he looked at it straight on.

Maren stood beside him in the shadow of the cooper's shed across the lane, her hood up, her hands buried in the pockets of Corliss's borrowed coat. She hadn't spoken since the marshes. Neither had he. There was nothing left to say that wasn't either a promise or a farewell, and neither of them could afford either one yet.

The knock was Ansel's to make. They had agreed on that in the marsh, in the dark, with the rain coming through the reeds. It had to be him because it was his name on the indenture papers, his debt, his town. Maren Voss walking up to a magistrate's door would be an arrest waiting to happen. Ansel Eastwick asking for a word was just a diver with a grievance, and Saltmere was full of those.

He crossed the lane. The mud took his boots to the ankle. At the door he paused, hand raised, and heard behind him — faint, carried on the wind off the water — the bell of the tide ship ringing the half hour past midnight. Four and a half hours until dawn. Four and a half hours until the packet sailed or didn't, until Maren was on it or in chains, until everything they'd bled for meant something or meant nothing.

He knocked.

Footsteps inside. Slow ones, uneven, favoring one leg. He remembered that about Magistrate Oren Fell — everyone remembered it, though no one spoke of it. Fell had been a young assessor once, before Meridian bought the harbor charter out from under him, before the fire that took his house and his wife and the use of his left leg on the same terrible night. The fire was ruled accidental. Everything in Saltmere was ruled accidental. That was practically the town motto.

The door opened on a chain, six inches of lamplight and one eye, gray and unblinking as a gull's.

"It's midnight," Fell said.

"I know what time it is."

"Then you know what kind of man answers his door at midnight." The chain did not slide. "Divers are abed. Smugglers are afloat. Only men in trouble come down Rope Walk at this hour, and I've no patience left for trouble. I sold the last of it years ago."

"My name is Ansel Eastwick."

Something shifted behind the eye. Not recognition exactly. Arithmetic. A name filed away against other names.

"Pearl diver," Fell said. "Meridian's boy. Twelve years on their books." He said it without contempt, which somehow made it worse — flat, factual, the way you'd read a shipping manifest. "What does Meridian's boy want at my door at midnight?"

"To show you something Meridian will burn this house down again to keep you from seeing."

The silence that followed was long enough for Ansel to hear his own heart, and the rain starting again, soft on the slate roof, and beneath both, far off, the sea working at the breakwater like a patient animal worrying a bone.

Then the chain slid. The door opened.

* * *

The room inside was smaller than Ansel expected and colder. Books everywhere — on shelves, in stacks on the floor, on a table by the cold hearth where a single candle burned down over a ledger. Fell limped to the hearth and lit the fire himself, refusing help with a motion of his hand that brooked no argument, and while he knelt there Ansel saw the full ruin of him: a big man gone soft at the edges, gray-haired, dressed in a nightshirt and an old dressing gown, moving like a ship that had taken water below the line and never quite recovered its trim.

"Maren Voss is outside," Ansel said.

Fell's hands stopped on the poker. For three seconds he did not move at all. Then he finished lighting the fire, stood, and turned around with his face carefully empty.

"The drowned girl," he said. "The enemy agent. The name on the harbor notices with the reward under it."

"She didn't drown. And she isn't what the notices say she is."

"No." Fell lowered himself into the chair by the fire, easing the bad leg out straight. "I imagine she isn't. Sit down, Eastwick. Both of you — yes, I know she's outside, I can smell a watcher on my step the way I used to smell a falsified ledger. Bring her in before someone else smells her too."

Ansel opened the door and signaled, and Maren came across the lane fast with her hood low, and then she was inside, dripping on the magistrate's floor, pushing back her hood, and Fell looked at her for a long moment with an expression Ansel couldn't read — grief, maybe, or memory, or just the particular exhaustion of a man seeing proof that the world was exactly as rotten as he'd always privately believed.

"You were the assessor on the Voss charter," Maren said. "Six years ago. You signed the insurance survey."

"I signed what I was given to sign." Fell's voice didn't rise, but something underneath it did. "By the time I understood what I'd signed, my house was ash and my wife was ash with it, and the new charter holder was sending condolences along with a generous settlement and a suggestion that grief might be better managed in a quieter town. I stayed anyway. Spite is a durable material. Now." He folded his hands over his belly. "You came here because you believe I have honor left. That is a considerable gamble. Tell me why I should take your side of it."

Maren set the satchel on the table between them.

For the next hour, she talked. Ansel watched Fell listen — really listen, the way no one in Saltmere listened anymore, leaning forward despite the leg, interrupting only to ask the questions a magistrate asks: dates, tonnage, the draft marks on the *Voss Star*, who handled the cargo manifests, who countersigned the survey. Twice he pulled the ledger toward himself and wrote. Once he stopped writing altogether and sat with his pen motionless above the page while Maren described how the scuttle charges had been placed below the waterline aft, where the survey stamp would never look, and Ansel saw the man's jaw work like he was chewing on something bitter.

When she finished, Fell was quiet for a long time. The fire cracked. Rain ticked at the window.

"There's enough here," he said finally, "to hang three men. If it were true."

"If?" Maren's voice went sharp.

"If it were *proven*." Fell tapped the satchel. "These are copies. Copies of letters, copies of manifests, a copy of a diver's sworn account of hull damage that predates the storm. Copies burn, Miss Voss. Copies get called forgeries. Copies get their authors hanged for sedition while the originals sit safe in a locked cabinet in the counting house and Hale stands at a trial looking wounded. I know how this works. I wrote the rules these men hide behind." He looked at Ansel. "You understand what I'm telling you."

Ansel's stomach dropped, but he'd known — some part of him had known since the marshes — that it would come to this.

"The original wreck file," he said.

"The original file. Every page. Survey, manifests, the internal correspondence, the payment authorizations. It exists — it must exist; Hale is too careful to destroy paper that could someday be leverage against his own partners. It'll be in the strongroom or in Hale's private cabinet, and either way it's behind locks that don't open for clerks or divers." Fell leaned forward. "Bring me that, and at dawn I will sign a warrant against Meridian Trading with my own hand, and stand in my own hall and let them try to stop me. Bring me copies, and I will wish you luck at your trial."

* * *

They stood in the doorway afterward, the three of them, looking out at the rain.

"You're asking him to walk back into the counting house," Maren said quietly. "Hale is watching him. You told me yourself — the junior clerk, the patrols, the net."

"I'm not asking anything," Fell said from behind them. "I'm telling you the price. What he does about it is between him and his conscience." He paused. "And his debt. Don't think I don't know the arithmetic, Eastwick. Your indenture has a year left on it. If you're caught in that building tonight, you won't face a magistrate — you'll face Hale, privately, and what happens after that won't appear in any record. I've seen men come out of that counting house different. Or not come out at all."

"Then it's fortunate," Ansel said, "that I know which window sticks."

Fell almost smiled. It aged his face ten years in both directions at once.

"One more thing." The magistrate's voice dropped, and the lamplight caught the scar tissue at his temple, old fire damage, smooth as wax. "Whatever you feel toward Hale — and you'll feel it, in that room, with the file in your hands — you leave him breathing. You hear me? The moment this becomes killing, we lose. The law is the only weapon we have that they can't confiscate, and the moment you sharpen it into a knife, every soul in Saltmere who's afraid of Meridian gets to be right to be afraid. I need you clean, Eastwick. Cleaner than I ever managed to be."

"I'm not a killer," Ansel said. "I'm a thief. Tonight, anyway."

"A thief with four hours." Fell glanced at the clock on the mantel, then at the rain. "The tide turns at five. If you mean to put her on that packet, the file has to be in my hands before the first light hits the water. After that—" he spread his big ruined hands— "after that, we find out together whether there's any such thing as justice in Saltmere, or only the memory of it."

Maren touched Ansel's arm. Just that. Her fingers were cold through his sleeve.

"The loft window," she said. "You said it yourself, the night we planned it. Third floor, north side, over the records room. You've cleaned that glass since you were nine years old."

"Twelve years," Ansel said. "I know every board in that building that creaks. I know which stair skips. I know Hale keeps the strongroom key on the ring in his coat, and I know his coat hangs in his office when he works late, and I know—" he stopped, hearing his own voice, steady and cold and completely unfamiliar. "I know I can do this."

Outside, the rain thickened. Somewhere down the harbor, muffled by weather, the packet's riding lanterns would be swinging at their moorings, and Brann's schooner would be standing off past the point, waiting on a signal that had to come soon or not at all.

Fell held the door wide.

"Go," the magistrate said. "And Eastwick — if you're taken, I never opened this door. I was asleep. I'm an old man with a bad leg and I sleep heavily." His gray eye held Ansel's a final second. "Make me a liar worth being."

Ansel stepped out into the rain, and the door closed behind him, and the lamp in the window went dark — the magistrate of Saltmere returning to the appearance of a sleeping town, holding his candle over a ledger in which, for the first time in six years, the names of the guilty were written on the same page as the evidence.

The counting house loomed at the far end of the quay, black against the blacker water, every window dark.

Every window but one.
