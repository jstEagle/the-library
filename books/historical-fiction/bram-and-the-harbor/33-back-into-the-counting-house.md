## 33. Back Into the Counting House

The lamp in the magistrate's window had gone dark, but Ansel carried its light inside him anyway — a small, stubborn coal he cupped with both hands as he crossed the empty fish-market square. The rain had thinned to a drifting mist that beaded on his face and made the cobbles shine like the backs of seals. Behind him the town slept its practiced sleep, shutters drawn, dogs quiet, everything arranged to look innocent because for six years innocence had been the cheapest thing in Saltmere and the only thing Meridian let people keep.

Ahead, the counting house waited.

He had walked this stretch of quay perhaps ten thousand times. He knew every plank by the sound it made under his boots, knew where the rail had been spliced after the storm of his fourteenth year, knew which mooring bollard leaned seaward because the frost had heaved it a finger's width out of true. It was the most familiar ground in his life, more familiar than his mother's grave, more familiar than Hetta's kitchen. And tonight it was enemy territory, and the familiarity was the worst part, because his feet wanted to carry him through the front door to the loft where he slept five nights in seven, and he had to keep correcting them like a man leading a horse away from a burning stable.

Every window dark. Every window but one.

That had been true when the magistrate said goodnight, and it was true now: a thin amber seam high in the eastern gable, on the floor where Hale kept his private office. Ansel stood at the rail and watched the light and told himself the arithmetic again. A man expecting a thief does not burn a lamp to announce himself. A man who has forgotten to snuff a wick does. Or a man who wants the thief to come.

Or a man who is sitting in the dark beside the lamp, waiting.

Ansel turned the possibilities over the way he turned a shell over before prizing it open. There were three doors into the record room below the office. The water door at the tide-line, barred since the break-ins of the old harbor wars, the bar itself an iron rod older than anyone living. The main stair from the counting floor, which passed directly beneath Hale's door. And the outside ladder to the loft, then down the interior stair, past the clerks' desks, past Hale's door, into the records.

No way in that did not pass the lit room.

But there was another way up, if not in. The counting house had been built in layers across forty years, and layers meant gaps. When Ansel was eleven and newly indentured, he had helped haul ledger crates up through the ceiling hatch of the record room because the proper stair was being rebuilt, and he remembered the hatch, remembered the smell of dust and whale-oil and old paper rising out of it, remembered the ladder bolted inside the wall cavity that ran from the record room's storeroom closet straight up to the loft floor. A shaft no wider than a chimney. A way in that touched none of the doors.

It also meant going up first, and coming down blind.

*You're stalling,* he told himself, and it was true, and he made himself move.

* * *

The outside ladder took his weight without complaint. Rain-slick rungs, each one known to his palms. At the top he eased open the loft window — he left it unbarred even in winter, a small disobedience he had never examined until now, and which tonight looked less like sloth than fate — and slid inside onto boards he knew better than his own heartbeat.

The loft was black and smelled of tar, rope-ends, the tallow candle he wasn't allowed, the blanket that still held some ghost of Hetta's soap. His cot stood against the far wall. Twelve years he had slept here, above the ledgers of the company that owned him, and never once, not once in twelve years, had he thought to wonder what was written two floors beneath his sleeping body.

He knelt at the hatch.

It opened without a sound; he oiled the hinges himself each autumn, along with every hinge in the building, because a diver learns that maintenance is the difference between a tool and a trap. The ladder went down into a darkness so complete it seemed to have texture. He could feel cold air rising against his face, carrying paper and dust and something else — whale-oil lamp, recently burned, faintly, hours ago or minutes.

He went down with the file-copy wrapped in oilcloth inside his shirt, and his knife at his belt, and the coal of the magistrate's lamp burning behind his ribs.

The storeroom closet at the shaft's bottom was barely wide enough for his shoulders. He counted by touch: three shelves of shipping manifests, the crate of dead divers' indenture papers that Meridian kept for its own reasons, and then the closet door, latched from the outside, opening onto the record room.

Which meant, he realized with his hand already on the latch, that when he stepped through, he would be standing in a locked closet in the middle of the one room in Saltmere he least wished to stand in, and the only way back out was the same latch, and if anyone came through the record room's outer door while he was inside it, he would be caught in a box no bigger than a coffin, listening.

He almost laughed. Courage, he was discovering, felt nothing like the stories said. It felt like fear that had been given a job to do.

He lifted the latch. The door swung inward a hand's width. No light beyond. No breath of movement. He slipped through, eased the door to behind him — leaving it unlatched, remembering at the last second, pressing it home with one fingertip — and stood in the record room of the Meridian Trading Company.

* * *

Moonless, lampless, he worked by touch and memory. Two nights ago he had found the Voss file in the dark of a different kind of hurry; now he found it again in half the time, third drawer of the wreck cabinet, behind the insurance schedules where it should not have been and therefore was. He added the original file to the copy against his chest and felt the weight of both together, paper and paper, lighter than any pearl he had ever brought up from the bottom and heavier than all of them.

And that was when the light came on.

Not in the record room. Above. Through the floorboards overhead, a wash of yellow bloomed suddenly across the joists, and he heard — unmistakably, horribly close — the click of a lamp door, the scratch of a match that had not been needed, the small settling sounds of a chair accepting a man's weight.

Hale had been sitting in the dark. Not waiting for a thief. Waiting for *him*, because Hale did not think like a constable; Hale thought like an accountant, and an accountant knows that a man who has tasted a file will come back for the original. Ansel had done exactly the predictable thing, exactly on schedule, and the lamp was not a signal to anyone but himself: it was Hale, upstairs, letting him know the room was occupied.

Except Hale didn't know Ansel was below. The lamp was bait for a different entrance. The stairs. The water door. Any route but the one Ansel had used, because Hale did not know about the shaft. Nobody knew about the shaft except a boy of eleven who had hauled crates through it once, twelve years ago, and had never spoken of it, because speaking of things was dangerous, and silence keeps you safe.

Twelve years of silences, and this one had just bought him his life.

He stood absolutely still among the drawers and listened to the house breathe. The floorboards overhead creaked as weight shifted — Hale moving to the window, looking down at the quay, watching for a man who was already inside. A drawer rolled open somewhere above. Paper rustled. Then, worst of all, nothing: long minutes of nothing, the silence of a patient man reading, or pretending to read, positioned between Ansel and every exit Ansel had planned.

The plan dissolved. Plans do that; water gets into them. What remained was the shaft, and the shaft remained only if Hale stayed in his chair until Ansel reached the closet, climbed, and pulled the hatch shut above his head.

Ansel began to move. Softly, softly, along the rows of cabinets, one silent step at a time, a man crossing a frozen pond. He had reached the closet door and gotten it open and put one shoulder into the dark of the shaft when, above him, the chair scraped back.

Footsteps crossed the ceiling — toward the door. Toward the stairs. Down the stairs.

Hale was coming down.

There was no mathematics that saved him now. Ten steps from the top of the interior stair to the record room door, and Ansel was fifteen feet from the foot of the ladder inside a shaft whose lower reach was visible from the doorway if the lamp were raised, and the lamp would be raised, because Hale was coming down to check on his bait. Ansel went up.

He went up the way he dove: fast, efficient, spending nothing on panic. Rungs under his hands, the oilcloth-wrapped files clamped between his teeth because he needed both hands, the taste of old ink and salt on the cloth. Below him the record room door opened and lamplight spilled across the floor in a long blade.

"Eastwick."

His name, spoken quietly, conversationally, the way Hale said everything. Ansel froze on the ladder, three rungs up, half into the shaft, his body pressed into the wall cavity's shadow. The light moved. It swept the rows of cabinets, paused at the wreck drawer — Ansel heard the moment it registered, the tiny hitch in the room's breathing, a drawer open that had been closed, files disturbed by a fingertip's width.

"You're in the building," Hale said, not loudly, walking slowly now, angling toward the closet. "I've known since the night you visited. Junior clerk with a memory and a grudge — you'd be surprised what a shilling buys in this town. I let you take your look. I wanted to know who you'd carry it to." The footsteps stopped. Directly below. "The magistrate's door was disappointing, Eastwick. I'd hoped for someone worth the trouble."

He knew. He knew about the magistrate, about Corliss, about all of it, and he had let it run, and tomorrow — no, not tomorrow, dawn, the tide, the packet — it would all close like a hand. Unless the papers reached the magistrate before the constables reached Ansel, and they would not, because Ansel was about to be found on a ladder like a rat in a wall.

The lamp rose.

Ansel did the only thing left, the thing the sea had been training him for since he was seven years old: he committed. He blew out his breath, made himself thin, and went up the shaft in one convulsive surge, hand over hand, boots finding rungs by feel, and behind him the light hit the closet mouth and Hale's voice changed — dropped its conversational warmth and became simply loud:

"GUARDS! THE SHAFT—"

The hatch was above him, closed. Of course it was closed; he had closed it; a man cannot climb through a hole he hasn't opened, and his hands were full of papers and his lungs were already burning from the sprint up the rungs and there was a moment — one clean, crystalline, terrible moment — when Ansel Eastwick hung in a chimney of black air with a lamp blazing below and a barred hatch above and understood that this was where courage ran out, this exact rung, this exact second.

Then he got one hand free, tore the hatch up, and rolled through into the loft as boots thundered on the exterior ladder outside.

* * *

They had circled the building. Of course they had — Hale's voice carried, and men had been posted, two of them at least, coming up the outside rungs fast and heavy. Ansel slammed the hatch. The iron bar that normally lay across it was on the wrong side, in the closet below; instead he dragged his cot across the planks and dropped onto it with all his weight, and a heartbeat later the hatch jumped under the cot's legs like a trapped animal.

"Eastwick!" A fist on the loft door — the interior door, the stair, more men below, they must have come through the building. The door handle rattled, stopped. Barred? He had barred it? He couldn't remember barring it; his body had barred it, twelve years of habit locking doors behind him, and the habit bought him another ten seconds.

Ten seconds was a tide window. You worked with what the sea gave you.

The loft window. The ladder outside — but the ladder had men on it, he could feel their weight through the wall, the whole gable trembling faintly. So not the ladder. The window and then not the ladder: the lean-to roof of the chandlery next door, its eave a body's length and a half below and slightly out, over water-black cobbles. A dive he would not ask a boy of fifteen to make off a moving deck.

He asked it of himself.

Fists hit the loft door properly now, the frame cracking around the latch. Ansel wrapped the oilcloth bundle tight against his chest with the file inside it, both bundles, everything, the whole truth of the Voss ship bound in waxed cloth against the skin that had learned to hold its breath — and he breathed out, all the way out, the last of it, the way you do before dropping through green dark toward something on the bottom — and went through the window headfirst into the rain.

For a heartbeat there was nothing under him, the purest freedom, falling. Then the chandlery's roof met his feet and he let the impact run through bent knees into the slates, slithered, controlled the slide with his palms until the eave threw him off into space again, shorter this time, and the cobbles came up hard.

He had expected to break something. He rolled instead — instinct, or luck, or the magistrate's gray eye watching after all — and came up running, wet stone flying under his boots, the shouts behind him splitting the sleeping quay wide open. Lights bloomed in windows. Somewhere a dog finally lost its mind. Ahead, the marsh gate, the sea wall, the black labyrinth of channels that Hale's men knew in daylight and hated in the dark, and that Ansel had mapped since childhood, channel by channel, eel-grass bed by sucking flat, the geography of every secret a poor boy keeps.

Behind him, at the counting house, a voice — Hale's, pitched now to carry across water — issued orders with terrible calm. Boats. Cover the water. He knows we'll want the marsh.

Ansel ran into the dark with the evidence beating against his heart like a second pulse, and did not look back at the counting house, at the loft window hanging open above the quay like a mouth that had finally, after twelve years, said something.

The tide was turning. In three hours the packet would make ready, and Maren would walk down a gangway into a trap Hale had known about for days.

Unless Ansel reached her first — and unless the marsh, indifferent old witness, decided tonight to keep one more silence on his behalf.

Behind him, across the black water, a lantern began to swing at the harbor mouth. Signal, pursuit, summons — he couldn't tell. But it was answered, one by one, from points down the coast, lights waking in the dark like eyes opening.

They were not all Meridian's.

He ran faster.
