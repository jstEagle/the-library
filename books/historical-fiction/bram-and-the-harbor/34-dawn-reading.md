## 34. Dawn Reading

The magistrate's hall smelled of cold ash and old paper, and Ansel thought it was the most honest room he had ever stood in.

It was not a grand room. Saltmere's magistrate had never been a grand man, and the Crown had never seen fit to give him more than the ground floor of a converted chandlery: a long table scarred by forty years of ledgers, a stove that breathed heat only when it felt like it, windows fogged with salt-frost so that the dawn came through them gray and shapeless, like light at the bottom of the sea. Somewhere beyond those windows the tide was turning. Ansel could feel it in his ears, an old diver's instinct, the water leaning back from the flats with a long low sigh.

He stood at the end of the table because Magistrate Corven had not asked him to sit. Hetta sat anyway, arms folded, chin up, daring anyone to remark on it. Corliss was not there — Corliss was lying in the lockup behind the hall with two cracked ribs and a face the color of wet slate, and every time Ansel let himself think of that, his hands wanted to close into fists.

The wreck file lay open on the magistrate's table.

Ansel had carried it out of the counting house three hours ago, through the dark, past Hale waiting in it like a spider in its web, and now it looked smaller than it had in the loft — a bundle of paper tied in company string, ordinary as any of ten thousand bundles Meridian produced each year. Except for what was written inside.

Corven read slowly. He read the way a man reads who has been ruined before and does not intend to be fooled again. His finger tracked down each line. He turned pages by their corners, careful not to smudge anything, and once he took off his spectacles, cleaned them on his sleeve, put them back, and started a page over again from the top.

The stove ticked. The tide sighed. Ansel counted his own heartbeats and tried not to count them against the minutes until sunrise.

"Here," Corven said at last. He did not raise his voice. He spoke the way men speak in church, or at gravesides. "The *Vespera* was surveyed in March of her last year. Hull sound. Here is the surveyor's signature — here is his fee, paid by Meridian, recorded in this ledger column. And here—" he turned a page with terrible gentleness "—is an instruction to the harbor master to amend the survey. In the company's hand. Not the surveyor's."

"Scuppered," Hetta said flatly. "Like my Tam's boat."

"Like your son's boat," Corven agreed quietly. "And insured for double her worth eleven days before she sailed." He laid the page flat, smoothing it as if it were something wounded. "And the manifest lists a cargo of tin that was never loaded. I remember the *Vespera*. Half the town lined the quay to see her off. There was no tin going aboard that day. I stood there myself. I watched her go."

Ansel's throat was tight. "She didn't sink of bad weather, then."

"She sank of arithmetic," Corven said. "Which is worse. Weather at least is honest about being cruel."

He gathered the file together but did not retie the string. He left it open, loose, as though a thing like this should never again be closed up neatly in the dark.

"The question," Corven said, "is what happens at dawn."

"The question," said a voice from the doorway, "is whether you are all still standing there when I finish my breakfast."

* * *

Hale filled the doorframe the way money fills a purse — completely, and without any softness left over. Behind him, four constables came in out of the gray morning, stamping the frost off their boots, and behind *them*, faintly, Ansel heard the creak of more men on the step. Six. Eight. Enough.

Hale's coat was brushed. His boots shone. He had shaved in the small hours, Ansel realized; he had dressed for this the way one dresses for a wedding or a hanging, and he had known exactly which it would be.

"Magistrate." Hale inclined his head precisely enough to insult. "You have company property on your table."

"I have evidence on my table," Corven said. He did not rise. He did not move his hands from the file at all, and Ansel saw that this was deliberate — that the old man's palms rested on the open pages like weights on scales.

"I have a warrant for the arrest of the persons present," Hale went on, drawing a folded document from inside his coat, "for theft, conspiracy, and aiding an enemy of the Crown. Signed before witnesses. All quite regular." He unfolded it with a flick of the wrist. "I'll thank you not to make it tedious."

"You'll forgive me," Corven said, "if I read it."

"Read it, then." Hale smiled without warmth, and his eyes moved across the room — over Hetta, over Ansel, lingering on Ansel with something that was almost fondness, which was the most frightening thing in the whole gray room. "Eastwick. You gave me a deal of trouble for a pearl boy."

"You waited up for me," Ansel said. His voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect. "I'm sorry to have missed you."

"In the counting house. At midnight. With your hand in the company's files." Hale shook his head slowly, a schoolmaster's gesture, and Ansel understood that Hale was performing too — performing patience, performing regret, performing the reasonable man surrounded by fools. He had learned performance from the same masters Ansel had. Everything in Saltmere was learned silence and learned speech, and Hale had simply been the best student in the class. "Twelve years we fed you. Twelve years, and this is the interest you pay."

"That word again," Ansel said. "Interest. You use it like scripture. But I've read your ledger now, Mr. Hale. I know how your arithmetic works. A boat scuttled. Thirty-one souls. Eleven days after doubling the insurance." He nodded at the file under Corven's hands. "That's the interest. That's what your twelve years of feeding us cost somebody else."

Something moved behind Hale's eyes — quick, quickly mastered — and Ansel knew then, in the marrow of him, that Hale had known. Had always known. That the director had signed papers with those figures in front of him and slept afterward, and shaved afterward, and brushed his coat.

Corven finished reading the warrant. He set it down on the table beside the wreck file, side by side, deliberately, so that they touched.

"This warrant names three people," he said. "It does not name me."

"It doesn't need to," Hale said. "Step aside, Corven. You've been a dead weight on this coast for twenty years and no one will mourn the paperwork."

"No," Corven agreed mildly. "But here is a difficulty." He picked up the warrant again and held it to the frosted window, reading it a second time, unhurried, while the constables shifted and the stove ticked and Ansel's heart hammered against his ribs like something trying to get out of a net. "A warrant issued by whom?"

"By the proper authority."

"The proper authority," Corven repeated. "Meaning yourself and two Meridian supervisors, since the justice of the peace for this district resigned last spring — you'll recall why — and meaning a warrant executed by constables whose wages the company pays. Which is to say, Mr. Hale, that this paper is you, arresting people, on behalf of you." He lowered the warrant. "I have spent the night reading another set of papers. Would you like to know what I've concluded?"

"I know what you've concluded," Hale said. "You concluded whatever those people told you to conclude. You're an old man in a cold room, Corven, and the world passed this harbor by thirty years ago. Meridian built every jetty you ever moored against. We pay for the lamp oil. We own half the boats, all the warehouses, and every debt from here to the headland. When we say a ship sank of weather, it sank of weather. When we say a girl drowned, she drowned." His voice dropped, and it lost its polish all at once, and underneath it Ansel heard the true thing, the frightened thing. "Where is she? Where is Maren Voss?"

The room held still. Outside, faint as breath, came the sound of the packet's bell — the morning tide, ringing twice, readying.

"You're asking the wrong room," Hetta said.

"I'm asking the only room that matters," Hale snapped, and then caught himself, and smoothed it over, and became reasonable again in the space of a heartbeat, which somehow made it worse. "Magistrate. Last offer. Give me the file and the girl's whereabouts, and I will consider this a misunderstanding. Eastwick's indenture continues. No charges. Everyone goes back to work. The tide comes in, the tide goes out, Saltmere stays standing. Isn't that what you all want? For it to stay standing?"

"It is," Corven said. "That's rather the point."

He rose then — slowly, joints cracking, an old man made of stubborn bone — and took the wreck file in both hands, and held it against his chest.

"Twenty-two years ago I ruled against Meridian in a salvage matter. Small case. Honest ruling. Within the year I'd lost my clerk to a better post in the south, my docket to delays, my wife's family to scandal, and every favor I was owed to silence. I learned what law costs in a company town. I kept the office. I stopped believing in it." He looked around at the constables — at each of them, one by one, and several of them found reasons to study the floor. "Some of you were boys when that happened. Some of you dive for pearls, or your fathers do. Every one of you has a Tam or a Vespera in your family somewhere, if you're honest. So I'll say this plainly, once."

He set the file down on the table, opened it to the amended survey, and turned it to face the room.

"These papers say thirty-one people were murdered for insurance money. They are originals. Copies exist elsewhere as of yesterday — with a shipping advocate in Corvane, sealed and posted, and I will not say where else. If this room ends with me arrested and these papers burned, the copies sail regardless, and everything done here tonight was for nothing except making yourselves accomplices after the fact. If instead this room ends lawfully — with a magistrate's warrant served on the Meridian Trading Company — then for the first time in twenty years, Saltmere gets to find out what the law is actually worth."

Silence. The stove ticked. The tide turned below the window, and the light through the frost changed by a single shade, gray to silver.

"Arrest him," Hale said to the constables.

None of them moved.

"Did you hear me?" Hale's voice cracked upward. "I said—"

"We heard you, sir." It was the youngest constable who spoke, a broad-faced boy Ansel knew from the quay, whose father dove the eastern beds. He did not look at Hale. He looked at the amended survey, at the company hand amending the company truth, and his jaw worked. "We heard you fine."

Hale stared at the boy. Then at Corven. Then, last and longest, at Ansel — and Ansel saw the moment the man understood that the room had gone out from under him, the way a drowning man sees the deck slide past overhead. To his credit, or whatever passed for credit in him, Hale did not scream. He drew himself up, buttoned his coat with two precise motions, and reached inside it.

For a pistol, Ansel thought. Everyone in the room thought it. Four constables took one step forward in perfect unison.

Hale brought out, instead, a second folded paper, and laid it on the table with exaggerated care, as if setting down a winning card in a game he had only just admitted he was losing.

"Then read this," he said softly. "Signed by the provincial court at Corvane, sealed Monday. An injunction dissolving your authority in this matter pending review — pending, Magistrate, my review — and directing the return of all company property." He smiled, thin as a knife's edge. "I sailed it here myself. While you were all reading bedtime stories."

Corven picked it up. Read it. Set it down. And Ansel watched the old man's face and saw the ruin coming back into it, twenty-two years of it, the whole gray weight of the world passing harbors by —

— and then Corven began, very quietly, to laugh.

"Monday," the magistrate said. "Sealed Monday, at Corvane. By the provincial court." He wiped his eyes. "Mr. Hale. Do you know what I did all night, besides read? I sent my clerk to the wireless shed. Do you know what the harbor office received at four this morning, before your boat even made the outer lights?" He turned the injunction over, almost gently, and tapped the wax seal. "A writ from the same court. Issued on the petition of one Maren Voss, agent of record, naming the *Vespera*'s loss a matter of Crown inquiry and staying all company proceedings in Saltmere — including, I should think, injunctions bought in haste by directors who did not check the docket times." He lifted his spectacles. "Yours was sealed at nine o'clock Monday morning, Mr. Hale. Hers was sealed at noon. Mine arrived first."

The stove ticked. Hale did not move. Outside, the packet's bell rang a third time, impatient, and beneath it — fainter, farther out in the gray water — came the muffled report of a signal gun from a schooner lying at anchor where no schooner had any lawful business anchoring, and Ansel knew, with a lurch that nearly buckled his knees, that she was early, that the tide would not wait, and that somewhere on that deck Maren Voss was watching Saltmere's rooftops come out of the dark.

"There is one flaw in your arithmetic after all," Corven said pleasantly, taking up his pen. "You taught this whole town to count. Now hold still while we do."

He dipped the pen and began, in a clear round hand, to write a warrant of his own.

Hale lunged for the table — and stopped, because eight hands and Ansel Eastwick's shoulder were suddenly, quietly, in his way.

"Run," Hetta said to Ansel, without turning her head. "The tide's turned, lad. Go and fetch her home."
