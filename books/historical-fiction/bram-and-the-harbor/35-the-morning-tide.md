## 35. The Morning Tide

Dawn came up gray and bruised over Saltmere, the color of old pewter, and Ansel Eastwick stood at the magistrate's window watching the packet take on cargo with the patience of a thing that had waited all night and would wait no longer.

The *Kestrel* rode low in the harbor mouth, her decks stacked with bales and salt-cured fish, her crew moving with the brisk indifference of men who wanted only to be gone. She would sail on the turn of the tide — a little past seven by the church bell — and she would carry Maren Voss out of this country alive, or she would sail without them both, and Hale would have what he wanted.

Ansel counted the minutes the way his father had taught him to count breath underwater. Not with panic. With arithmetic.

Behind him, the magistrate's hall smelled of cold ash and lamp oil. Quill Brannon — the man everyone in Saltmere still called *the magistrate* though Meridian's charter had stripped him of half his authority years ago — sat at his own desk for the first time in a decade, the warrant spread before him, the ink not yet fully dry. He had signed it an hour past midnight: *Warrant issued against Meridian Trading Company and its officers, on evidence of fraud upon the Crown, destruction of property and life, and conspiracy.* His hand had shaken only once, at the seal.

He was reading it again now. Not because he doubted it. Because a man who has been broken once checks every knot before he trusts the rope.

"It will hold," Maren said from the corner, where she stood wrapped in Hetta's gray shawl, hood drawn low. "If you make him read it aloud. Make him say it in front of witnesses."

"I intend to." Brann looked up at her, and there was something raw under his composure — the look of a man who had spent ten years swallowing a debt and had just been handed the means to collect it. "Hale will come. He won't send constables ahead. He'll come himself, because he'll want to watch me fold."

"Then don't fold," Ansel said.

Brann almost smiled. It was a terrible smile, worn thin. "I stopped folding the night they took my commission. Everything since has been rehearsal." He rose, straightened his coat, and Ansel saw that beneath the shabby magistrate's robe he had put on his old uniform jacket — the one with the faded braid, the one Meridian had mocked him out of. He had dressed for war in the clothes of his defeat.

Outside, the bell began its quarter-hour. Six o'clock.

Ansel crossed to Maren. Up close, in the gray light, he could see what six years had done to her face — lines around the eyes that hadn't been there at nineteen, a scar along her jaw he didn't know the story of and wouldn't ask about until they were at sea. But her eyes were the same. That was the unbearable part. The sea could change everything about a person except the one thing you'd know anywhere.

"The tide turns at seven-ten," he said. "We go down at quarter past six. You board with the fish porters, hood up. I've paid the mate — Corliss's money — to look the other way until you're aboard. Once you're on the Kestrel, Hale can't touch you without stopping a Crown packet, and he can't do that if Brann is standing on the quay holding this." He touched the warrant.

"And you?" Her voice was quiet, pitched for him alone. "You're staying."

It wasn't a question. She'd known since the marshes, since the night she'd told him everything with the rain coming down through the reeds and his arms around her because it was the only warm place left in the world. She'd argued then. She wasn't arguing now, and somehow that was worse.

"Somebody has to stand here when they ask who did it," Ansel said. "If we both run, it's two fugitives and a stolen file. If I stay, it's testimony. Brann can't make the charge stick without a witness who isn't a smuggler and isn't a magistrate with a grudge. I'm the only one left."

"You're the one they'll hang."

"They hanged my father for nothing," Ansel said. "I'd rather give them a reason that costs them something." He said it steadily, and was surprised to find it true. Not brave. True. There was a difference, and he had learned it in the deep water where fear stops mattering because your hands are busy.

Maren reached up and pulled the hood back. For one moment, in the empty hall, she let him see all of her — the girl under the agent, the drowned girl standing in the land of the living — and then she kissed him, brief and hard, like sealing a letter.

"Quarter past six," she said. "Don't be late. You've made me wait long enough for one lifetime."

* * *

They went down through the waking town like ghosts, Hetta leading, her broad back a wall between them and the street. Saltmere was stirring — gulls screaming over the fish market, the first smoke rising from the bakery chimneys, a cart of kelp rattling toward the fertilizer yards. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a town that didn't yet know its whole history was about to tip over.

At the foot of Harbor Street, Hetta stopped and took Ansel's arm with a grip like a mooring line.

"I'll be in the crowd," she said. "Front of it. When they ask who stands witness, you look for me. I buried a son to these people and I never got to say so out loud. Today I say it out loud." Her eyes were wet but her voice was iron. "Go. Get her to the water."

Corliss was waiting near the fish steps, disguised in a porter's smock, his courier's neatness buried under fish blood and sacking. He looked older than he had at the mail packet six weeks ago — the cost of his night in chains still written on him, a looseness in the left wrist where they'd bent it. But his eyes were clear.

"Mate's bought," he murmured as they fell in among the porters. "But Hale's got two men watching the quay already. The fat one by the customs shed. The young one walking the tide line. They're not stopping anyone — just counting heads. He wants to know who boards before he moves."

"He knows we'll run for the packet," Maren said.

"He knows you'll *try*. That's different." Corliss handed Ansel a canvas sack smelling of mackerel. "Under this is the second copy of the wreck file — Brann has the original, but a man like Hale burns paper. Paper needs to survive somewhere he can't reach. It goes with you, Maren. In the hold, in the oilskin, taped under the third rib starboard side. I did it last night while they were interrogating me. Let them search the cabin. The truth's in the bones of the ship."

Ansel stared at him. "You hid it before you knew we'd reach the magistrate?"

"I hid it the day I arrived," Corliss said. "A courier plans for capture. It's the whole trade." He almost smiled. "Go on. Walk her to the gangway. I'll draw the watchers' eyes."

He stepped away, staggering deliberately, clutching his bad wrist, cursing loudly about dropped crates — and sure enough, both of Hale's men turned toward the commotion. In that gap, Ansel took Maren's elbow and walked her forward, two more figures in the morning shuffle, hooded woman and pearl diver, indistinguishable, invisible, ordinary.

The *Kestrel*'s gangway creaked. At its head, the mate glanced at them, then away, exactly as paid. Maren paused at the top step.

She turned. For a heartbeat she searched Ansel's face, and he understood she was doing what he was doing — memorizing. Filing each other against the years ahead.

"Seven-ten," he said. "I'll be on the quay."

"I know," she said. "You were always going to stay. You were always the one who stayed." And she went down into the ship.

* * *

Six-forty. The church bell tolled the hour's last quarter, and the town came to the harbor the way water comes to a drain.

They came because word travels faster than constables in Saltmere — Hetta had spent the night seeing to that, a word here, a letter there, the widow's network of kitchen gossip that Meridian had never thought to spy on because it belonged to women and the poor. Divers came up from the strand, their diving knives still at their belts. Dockhands came. The bakery boy came, flour to his elbows. By quarter to seven there were three hundred people pressed behind the rope the constables had strung along the quay's edge, and every one of them knew something was happening, and none of them would say what, because knowing-while-saying-nothing was the oldest language Saltmere spoke.

And down the center of Harbor Street, boots ringing on the cobbles, came Director Aldous Hale.

He had dressed well. Ansel gave him that. Charcoal coat, silver chain of office, four constables in company colors flanking him and eight more behind — a show of force sized for a riot, which meant Hale believed there might be one, which meant Hale knew exactly how much the ground had shifted under him. His eyes swept the quay, found the magistrate's hall, and fixed on it with the flat satisfaction of a man arriving to collect.

Ansel stood beside Brann on the hall steps, the warrant in the old man's hands. Behind them, through the open door, the evidence lay laid out on the table like a surgery: the original wreck file, the insurance ledger, the surveyor's report dated three days *before* the Voss ship sailed, the seacocks ordered and paid for under a false name. Twelve years of Meridian arithmetic proving that forty-one people had been drowned on purpose for nine thousand crowns.

Heta stood at the front of the crowd, arms folded, planted like a piling.

Hale stopped ten feet from the steps. He did not look at Ansel. He looked at Brann, and smiled the way a knife smiles.

"Magistrate Brannon." He let the title curdle. "Or do we still use that word? I have warrants of my own, as it happens. For the woman Voss — enemy agent, sentenced in absentia. For the courier Corliss. And for one Ansel Eastwick, indentured diver, charged with theft of company documents, conspiracy, and treason." He produced the papers with theatrical care. "You are harboring felons on Crown ground. Step aside."

"Read yours first," Brann said, and held up his own.

For a moment Hale simply looked at the document. Ansel watched his face perform the small theater of contempt — the raised eyebrow, the thin smile — and watched, underneath it, the moment the words *against Meridian Trading Company and its officers* actually landed. Something moved behind the director's eyes. Not fear. Recalculation. A man discovering the price list had changed.

"That paper is worthless," Hale said. "You have no authority—"

"I have exactly the authority the Crown restored to me yesterday afternoon," Brann said, "when a petition arrived with forty-one names on it and evidence attached. Signed by the Admiralty court at Kingsport. You may verify it after I execute it. Or you may verify it now, while three hundred witnesses watch me do so." He raised his voice, and it carried across the water the way a bell carries, the voice of a man who had spent ten years silent and had saved all of it up. "By the authority vested in me, I am executing a warrant against the Meridian Trading Company, its officers and agents, for the murder of the souls aboard the *Voss Star* and the fraud of her insurance. Director Hale — you are named. You will stand and answer."

The quay did not erupt. It did something quieter and more terrible: it leaned forward. Three hundred people leaned, as one body, toward the sound of a door they had all been pretending was locked.

Hale recovered fast — Ansel granted him that too; the man had built an empire on recovery. "Arrest them," he said to his constables. "All three. Now."

None of the constables moved.

It happened in the space of two heartbeats: the nearest constable looked at the crowd, at Hetta's folded arms, at the divers with their knives, at the dockhands who loaded Meridian's ships and had just learned what those ships had been used for. Then he looked at his sergeant. The sergeant looked at the sea. And the whole apparatus of Meridian's law stood on the cobbles of Saltmere discovering, all together, that it had been running on habit, and the habit had just broken.

"Sergeant," Hale said, very quietly, "your name will be in my report."

"My name's already in your ledgers, sir," the sergeant said. "Third page of the pay book. We all saw it last night." He stepped back — out of the space between the magistrate and the director — and stood neutral, arms at his sides, a soldier refusing a lawful order from an unlawful man.

And then the church bell began to ring.

Not the quarter-hour. The full peal. Seven o'clock — and Ansel's head snapped toward the harbor, because the tide was early, the storm-swell pushing it, and the *Kestrel* was already making sail.

No. Not sailing. *Preparing.* Topsails loosed but not set, the mate at the rail scanning the quay — waiting, Ansel realized with a lurch, for the last of her passengers. For the signal.

Hale heard it too. Whatever calculation he'd been building collapsed into pure instinct. "Stop that ship!" he shouted, and this time he moved himself, shoving past the constables, boots hammering down the quay toward the gangway. "That vessel carries a traitor! Stop it in the Crown's name!"

Ansel ran.

He ran because there were perhaps ninety seconds between the quay and the water, because the arithmetic of tides does not negotiate, because Maren Voss had spent six years being dead and he would not let her be dead one minute longer than necessary. He vaulted the rope — the crowd parted for him, Hetta's voice roaring somewhere behind him like a foghorn, *"That's the boy! That's the Eastwick boy, let him pass!"* — and he hit the gangway at the same moment Hale hit its foot.

They met on the planks, the director below and the diver above, and for one suspended instant they were face to face, close enough to count each other's breaths.

"Step aside, boy," Hale gasped, "and I'll only ruin you."

"I'm already free," Ansel said, and it was true — Brann had voided the indenture at midnight, one signature among many — and he stepped past the reaching hands, down the swaying boards, onto the deck of the *Kestrel*, where the mate took one look at the chaos ashore, made the sailor's ancient decision that no cargo was worth a riot, and bellowed, *"Cast off!"*

Lines splashed. The hull swung. And Ansel turned at the rail to see Maren coming up from below, hood thrown back, hair loose and dark in the morning wind, alive, *visible*, standing in plain sight on a Crown packet while the most powerful man in Saltmere screamed impotently from the shrinking quay.

Hale had stopped at the water's edge. Behind him, Brann descended the hall steps slowly, warrant held high, reading it aloud now in full, his voice carrying over the water to the ship, to the town, to the gulls — *fraud, conspiracy, murder* — and the crowd had begun, softly at first, to make a sound Ansel had never heard in Saltmere in twelve years.

They were cheering.

The *Kestrel* gathered way. The harbor mouth opened before them, gray-gold in the new sun, and Maren came to stand beside him at the rail, her shoulder against his, both of them breathing hard, both of them laughing in the shaky way of people who have run out of terror and found the other thing underneath.

"He'll appeal," she said. "He'll spend everything he has left on lawyers and lies. It won't matter — the file goes to the Admiralty in Kingsport, and I'll deliver it myself — but he'll fight."

"Let him fight," Ansel said. "I'm good at deep water. I hear trials are similar."

She looked at him then, really looked, and whatever she saw made her take his hand and press it flat against the rail, over the wood, over the vibration of the ship driving east. "You could have come," she said. "You still could. The mate owes us nothing, but the sea owes me a few favors. Say the word and—"

"No." He said it gently. "The testimony stays where the crime happened. And besides—" he nodded astern, where Saltmere was falling away into its morning smoke, the breakwater, the counting house, the little gray town that had owned him body and debt for twelve years and had just, in the space of one tide, lost its grip entirely — "somebody has to be here when it all comes apart. Somebody they know. Somebody who kept the silences." He turned his palm over and laced his fingers through hers. "This time I choose to stay. That's different from being kept."

Ahead, the open sea. Behind, a town learning to shout. In the hold, taped beneath the third rib, the truth rode east in the bones of a ship.

And on the quay, growing smaller with every swell, a figure stood apart from the cheering crowd — a junior clerk in a Meridian coat, hat in his hands, watching the packet go with the expression of a man doing sums he already knows will not balance.

Ansel saw him. He didn't point. Some debts, he thought, collect themselves.

The bell of Saltmere rang on behind them, and the morning tide carried them out.
