## 37. The Ship Sails

The packet's deck tilted under Maren's feet as they cast off, and the whole of Saltmere swung sideways — the counting house, the drying sheds, the crooked line of the breakwater — all of it sliding left as though the town itself were trying to look after her.

She stood at the stern rail and did not let herself grip it too tightly. Six years at sea had taught her that a woman who clings to the rail is a woman who looks like cargo. She folded her hands instead. She watched.

Ansel was still on the dock.

He had not moved from the spot where she'd last seen him — there between the bollard and the customs shed, in his diver's coat gone gray with salt, his hands hanging open at his sides as if he'd forgotten what hands were for. The crowd around him flowed and broke like water around a piling. He stayed.

*Don't watch me leave,* she had begged him, only minutes ago, only a lifetime ago. *Watch the ship instead.*

He hadn't listened. Of course he hadn't. Ansel Eastwick had never once done what she told him to do, not when they were children diving for urchins off the north rocks, not the night under the breakwater when she'd asked him to forget her, and not this morning when she'd asked him for the one thing she could not bear to ask: *stay alive, and let me go.*

The packet cleared the harbor mouth and the wind found them properly, filling the fore-and-aft canvas with a crack like a whip, and still Ansel did not move. He grew smaller by inches. A man made of gray wool and stubbornness, shrinking against the stone.

Maren pressed her hand flat against her bodice, where inside the lining the oilskin packet rode against her ribs. The wreck file. Forty-one pages in Meridian's own hand — orders signed, ledgers balanced, a ship named *Voss's Hope* written down as an entry of profit before it had ever left the quay. Her father's ship. Her father's death, calculated to the shilling.

She carried it out of the country now. That had been the bargain, struck in mud and cold in the marshes while patrol lanterns swept the dunes above them: the evidence must go abroad, into hands beyond Meridian's reach, into courts where Hale could not buy the magistrate or hang the witness. Someone had to carry it. And someone had to stay so that the carrying would mean something — so that when the papers surfaced in London or Amsterdam, there would be a man standing in Saltmere who could say, *I saw the files myself; I took them from the counting house; here is my body as proof.*

He had chosen to be that man without hesitating. That was the thing she could not forgive and would never stop loving.

"Below if you want warmth," said Brann beside her, his voice pitched under the wind. The smuggler looked wrong on a lawful packet — too weathered, too quiet among the mail clerks and the two merchant passengers playing cards near the mast. He'd bought their passages in coin he claimed came from a legitimate cargo run, and Maren had decided not to examine that too closely. "Captain says we'll raise the headland by dark."

"I'll stay up."

Brann studied her a moment, then nodded and moved aft, giving her the rail and her grief the room it needed. He was better at silences than most men she'd known. Corliss had been good at them too.

Her stomach turned over — not from the swell. From Corliss.

She had not seen him taken. She'd heard it: the whistle from the customs walk, the shouts, Ansel's hand hard on her wrist hauling her down behind the breakwater stones while boots hammered the quay above. Corliss had turned back toward the sound deliberately — she'd seen him do it, seen him square his shoulders like a man walking into weather — and then they'd been running, and everything after was marsh water and fear.

Hale had him now. Whatever Corliss knew, Hale would spend the night extracting it, and Corliss would spend the night deciding how little of it to give. Maren had watched the courier work for three years. He was careful. He was loyal. But no one held forever.

Every mile the packet made put more water between her and whatever was happening in a cell beneath the customs house. Every mile was escape and abandonment at once, braided together so tight she couldn't tell which strand was which. Perhaps there was no difference. Perhaps that was what victory felt like when you were the one who lived.

The headland slid past on the port side, black rock wearing a crown of gulls, and Saltmere dropped below the horizon like something sinking.

* * *

They put in at Greyhaven to take aboard the London mails, and Maren went below to sleep because sleeping was discipline, and discipline was the only weapon she had left.

She dreamed of the breakwater. Not the night before last — the night six years ago, before her ship sailed, before everything. Ansel seventeen and all elbows and silence, sitting on the seaward stones with his boots unlaced, listening to her talk about the colonies the way other people talked about heaven. Her father below them on the quay, shouting up that the tide wouldn't wait for lovebirds, and both of them laughing because neither of them yet knew what tides took.

*I'll write,* she'd said.

*I know.*

*You won't answer.*

*I will.* And he'd looked at her then with that level, patient stare he had — the look of a boy who had learned early, from his father's drowning and his mother's arithmetic, that words were cheap and staying was the only currency worth anything. *I keep what I promise, Maren. Ask anyone. Ask the sea.*

The sea had answered, eventually. It had kept her when Meridian's powder charge should have scattered her across the bay along with her father and eleven others; it had given her to a fishing boat out of the Free Ports whose skipper asked no questions for coin; it had taught her that being dead was the safest thing a woman could be, so long as she didn't mind watching everyone who loved her mourn.

In the dream, Ansel was still on the breakwater, still seventeen, and she tried to tell him about the file, about Hale, about the forty-one pages riding against her ribs, but every time she opened her mouth the wind took the words and filled someone else's sails.

She woke to the packet's motion changed — longer, slower swells, open water — and grey light through the companionway hatch. Brann sat across the tiny cabin, cleaning his nails with a knife, watching her with those flat fisherman's eyes.

"You cried out."

"No."

"You said a name."

She sat up. Her body ached in the old places, the places the wreck had written on her: the shoulder that had been wrenched nearly out of its socket climbing the netting of the fishing boat, the ribs that had healed crooked. "How long until London?"

"Four days, weather holding." He sheathed the knife. "And after?"

"After, I deliver the file to people who have been waiting three years for it. After, Meridian's charter gets challenged in a court Hale can't reach. After—" She stopped. After, she thought, I find out whether a name spoken aloud in testimony can bring a man back from the gallows, or whether all of this buys nothing but a cleaner kind of mourning.

Brann nodded slowly. "The Eastwick lad. You knew him before."

"He was my closest friend in the world."

"And now?"

Maren looked at the hatch, at the grey light moving on it like water. "Now he is the bravest person I have ever known, and I left him standing on a dock."

"Aye," Brann said. "That's how it goes, mostly." He rose to go up, then paused at the ladder. "For what it's worth — the ones who stay are the ones who make the leaving mean anything. I've run cargo past a lot of towns full of people who'd sooner drown than risk anything. Your Saltmere produced a magistrate willing to sign against Meridian, a landlady hiding strangers in her cellar, and a pearl diver who walked back into the lion's den twice in one night." He shrugged. "That's not nothing. That's the opposite of nothing."

He climbed away. Maren lay back down and put her hand over her heart, over the oilskin, and let the packet rock her, and did not cry, because she had spent six years learning to save her tears for water she couldn't see over.

* * *

On the third day the weather stopped holding.

It came up from the southwest in the afternoon — not the storm that had delayed them at Saltmere, but its cousin, a hard blow with rain like flung gravel. The packet shortened sail and drove on anyway; the mails were sacred, the captain said, more sacred than comfort, possibly more sacred than lives. Maren lashed herself into the cabin bunk and held the oilskin packet against her and thought about waterproofing.

Because that was the terror she hadn't said aloud to anyone, not even Ansel, not even in the marsh: paper drowns. Men die, and their deaths are witnessed and remembered and avenged. Paper simply dissolves, and forty-one pages of proof become forty-one pages of pulp, and Hale wins forever, and Ansel's freedom — Ansel's *life*, if it came to that — will have been spent on nothing.

She had wrapped the file in waxed linen, then oilskin, then waxed linen again, and sewn the whole of it into her bodice lining with sailmaker's stitches. She checked the seams hourly. Brann noticed. He didn't comment, but he started checking the bilge pumps himself, personally, twice a watch, and once he silently handed her a second oilskin wrap "for your letters, or whatever it is you're carrying that smells like a counting house."

Near midnight the worst of it passed astern and the packet ran into long grey light under torn clouds, and the captain's mate stuck his head down and said they'd lost the mizzen topsail and gained an hour, and did anyone care, and nobody did.

Maren went up on deck into the wet aftermath. The sea had been scoured clean; stars showed in rags between clouds. She stood at the rail and let the spray hit her face and thought about her father, who had loved this exact kind of night, who used to say the sea forgave nothing but forgot nothing either, and that a sailor's job was to be the memory the ocean lacked.

Well, Papa. She pressed her hand to the packet. I'm the memory now. Both of us.

Behind her, the card-playing merchants had retreated below, and Brann stood his usual post by the mainmast, and the helmsman sang something low and tuneless to himself. Ordinary sounds. A lawful ship making a lawful passage, carrying one dead woman and enough paper to burn down a company.

She found she was smiling, there alone in the spray, and let herself do it for exactly as long as it lasted.

* * *

London announced itself first as smell — coal smoke and river mud rolling out to meet them miles before the light showed anything — and then as a forest of masts, and then as the city itself, vast and brown and steaming, a creature so large it made Saltmere seem like a toy some child had built at the edge of a bath.

Maren stood at the rail with her hood up and her face composed and felt the old fear rise, the fear of crowds, of eyes, of names. She had been Maren Voss of Saltmere, drowned daughter of a drowned man. She had been Tessa Rill of the Free Ports, seamstress, widow, nobody. In London she would need to be someone new again, and she was tired, bone-tired, of being new.

But then the packet worked its way upriver, past ships of every flag, past cranes and customs barges and a warship at anchor with its guns run out, and she saw the size of the world that Meridian's money moved through — and the size of the world that might, just might, be turned against it.

Brann joined her at the rail as they came to their berth. "You know where you're going?"

"I know the street. I don't know if they'll believe me."

"They believed you enough to fund three years of couriers," Brann said. "Belief isn't the problem. Proof is. You've got the proof." He looked at her sidelong. "You've also got a story, if it comes to that. Tell them about the boy."

"The boy?"

"The diver." He nodded toward the south, vaguely, toward everything behind them. "When the commissioners ask why a dead woman risked coming ashore in enemy waters — because they will ask, they always ask — don't give them strategy. Give them truth. Say there was a man who kept a promise for twelve years, and it shamed me into courage. Commissioners understand shame. They're full of it themselves."

The lines went ashore. Fenders squealed against the pier. Around them the packet erupted into ordinary chaos — mails hauled, parcels thrown, passengers jostling for the gangway — and none of it mattered, none of it touched her, because Maren Voss was already somewhere else entirely: on a quay four days and a world away, where a man in a gray coat was either free, imprisoned, or dead, and would remain all three at once until word crossed the water.

She waited until the deck emptied. Then she went below one last time, unpicked six sailmaker's stitches, and drew out the oilskin packet, and held it in both hands.

Forty-one pages. Her father's death warrant, signed by men who had never smelled salt. She had read every page four times; she could recite the damning entries in order; she had memorized the ledger notation — *S.V. Hope, hull & cargo, recovered value against premium* — that proved the wreck had been entered as expected profit before the ship sailed. Hale's neat initials appeared on the authorization. Hale, who had shaken her father's hand on the quay the morning the *Hope* went out.

She repacked the file in fresh oilskin, sewed it into the lining of the coat she'd wear ashore, and climbed up into the noise and smoke of London.

At the top of the gangway she paused — one breath, no more — and looked back down the river, south, the way they'd come, toward a harbor she couldn't see and a man she couldn't help and a tide that kept going out and coming in regardless of what it took from people.

*Keep what you promise,* she whispered, to him, to the sea, to herself. *Ask anyone.*

Then she stepped onto English stone, and the crowd closed around the drowned girl from Saltmere, and she began to walk.

* * *

Three days later, in a narrow room above a print shop off Chancery Lane, Maren laid the wreck file on a table before five grim-faced men who had spent a decade looking for exactly this, and one of them — a barrister with ink-stained fingers and eyes like a falcon's — read Hale's initials in the margin of the scuttling order and said quietly:

"Where, precisely, did you obtain this?"

And Maren Voss, officially drowned six years, enemy agent by charter decree, took a breath that tasted of salt though there was no sea within ten miles, and said:

"There is a man in Saltmere who will swear to it. His name is Ansel Eastwick. If anything has happened to him, gentlemen, I will make sure the world knows this document cost a life — and I will start with yours."

The barrister looked up. Somewhere outside, a church bell rang the hour, and far to the south, unseen beneath the same grey sky, a tide was turning in a harbor called Saltmere.
