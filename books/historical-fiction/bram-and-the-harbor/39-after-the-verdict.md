## 39. After the Verdict

The gallows frame came down on a Tuesday.

Ansel watched from the quay as the shipwright's crew worked the bolts loose, the timber groaning like an old man easing into a chair. It had never been used — Meridian had preferred its executions done quietly, by ledger and by sea — but the frame had stood at the harbor mouth for thirty years all the same, a piece of furniture in every Saltmere childhood. Children had played beneath it. Fishermen had tied lines to it when the proper rings were full. Now four men with hammers and a mule cart were taking it apart as if it were nothing, and perhaps that was the strangest thing of all: how quickly a thing that had shaped a whole town could become, in an afternoon, merely wood.

The magistrate's ruling had come down nine days earlier, read aloud in the hall because the hall could not hold the crowd and so most of Saltmere heard it through the open windows, standing in the street in the cold. Ansel had stood among them. He had expected to be elsewhere — in the dock, in irons, in the sentence — but the magistrate, a gray and ruined man named Aldous Crane who owed his restoration entirely to a folder of stolen paper, had looked at Ansel for a long moment before pronouncing judgment on everyone but him.

*The indenture of Ansel Eastwick is dissolved,* Crane had said. *It was dissolved the moment the company conspired to defraud the Crown of wreck duties, for a debt cannot be held valid against a man by an entity that no longer exists in law.* And then, quieter, in something that was almost not a judge's voice: *The company made you all into instruments. I will not compound the error by treating the last instrument as an accomplice.*

Hale had been taken in chains to the provincial capital. Half the counting house clerks had scattered within a week, some fleeing ahead of warrants, some simply abandoning desks that no longer meant anything. The great seal of the Meridian Trading Company — a pearl in a crowned hand — had been pried off the harbor office door and now lay somewhere, Ansel supposed, in an evidence crate or a rubbish heap, depending on how much sentiment the removal men had.

And Saltmere, which had lived inside that grip for two generations, was waking up and finding it did not know its own hands.

* * *

Hetta Kessler reopened her boarding house kitchen as if nothing had happened, which was her way of announcing that everything had.

She had fed Ansel through the trial weeks without once asking what he'd felt, because Hetta believed feelings were like salt fish — necessary, but best kept in the cellar until wanted. Now she stood over her stove in the first light, flipping barley cakes, while the table filled with people who had no reason to be up except that work existed again and they wanted to be near it: divers, rope-makers, the widow Prentiss who had sold bait for Meridian's tally for twenty years and had never once been paid in anything but credit at the company store.

"The store opens Thursday," the widow Prentiss was saying, in the tone of a woman reporting a miracle she didn't fully trust. "Cooperative. Ours. Marta Vane has the ledgers."

"Marta Vane can barely add," Hetta said, not turning from the stove.

"Marta Vane can add fine when it's her own money," said Brann from the corner, where he sat with his boots up on a second chair he had clearly decided was his by right of having bled in a cell for the cause. His wrists still bore the marks. He wore them the way other men wore medals. "You'll see. Greed sharpens arithmetic wonderful."

Ansel ate his cakes and listened. This was his old habit — listening more than speaking — but it sat differently now. Before, listening had been survival: know which silence keeps you safe. Now it was only pleasure. Nobody at this table could cost him anything. That fact still startled him each morning, arriving fresh like a stranger at the door.

"You'll want work," Hetta said to him, finally looking over. She looked at him the way she had since he was fourteen and she had caught him stealing bread from her sill and given him a job instead of a beating. "A man dries out worse than a barrel. You've been in the water since you were seven. You'll go strange if you stay on land."

"I've been thinking about the *Gannet*," Ansel said.

Brann snorted. "That leaky scow."

"She floats."

"So does a drowned man. For a little while."

"Crane awarded her in the seizure," Ansel said. "Part of Meridian's assets going to auction. She's small enough that nobody's bid on her yet. I've got the prize money from the testimony — the magistrate's fund paid witnesses." He said this last flatly, because it still did not sit right, being paid for truth. "I could buy her outright and owe nobody."

The table went quiet in the particular way of tables where people are deciding whether to say the thing.

"And do what?" Hetta asked. "Fish?"

"Dive," Ansel said. "But free. My own boat, my own catch, my own prices. There are beds out past the breakwater Meridian never touched because they're too deep for their gear and too far for their schedules. I know where they are. I've known since I was twelve." He turned his cup slowly on the table. "Meridian dove this harbor like a man robbing a room — quick, greedy, taking what was easy. There's a whole living out there nobody ever went after because the company set the price of pearls so low no one could afford to look for better ones."

"Free diving," Brann said, and there was something in his voice that wasn't mockery. "Imagine that."

"That's the whole idea," Ansel said.

* * *

He bought the *Gannet* on a Friday for less than the price of a good season's catch, because the auctioneer wanted the vessel gone and Ansel was the only bidder who inspected her hull without flinching. She was forty feet of stubborn oak, patched twice, renamed three times, and she smelled below decks of tar and old herring and, underneath everything, of the sea itself, which no amount of cargo could ever quite wash out.

Brann came to see her sail her first day out, leaning on the quay rail with his arms folded, wearing the expression of a man determined to watch a disaster and enjoy it.

Ansel worked her out of the harbor alone. The wind was light and quartering, and the *Gannet* handled like a barge with opinions, but when the main took the breeze full and the hull leaned and began to bite, something opened in Ansel's chest that he had no name for. He had been on boats all his life. He had never been on one that was his — where every order given was given to himself, where the course was his course, where the only tally he answered to was the tide.

He looked back once. Saltmere sat low and gray-gold in the morning light, the harbor mouth open like a held breath released. The space where the gallows had stood was just a patch of paler stone.

Out past the breakwater, in water the charts marked with a dismissive flourish, he went down.

It was different, diving free. Twelve years he had descended on the company's terms — quotas, tallies, a supervisor's boot on the deck above, the understood mathematics that a diver's life was worth exactly what his last haul had brought. Now there was only him and the water and the long green light, and the strange truth that the ocean had never cared whose debt he carried. The oysters down here beyond the old beds were thick and undisturbed, clustered on the ridge rocks like coins someone had hidden and forgotten. He worked until his lungs pressed, took what his hands could carry and no more, and came up into air that tasted like the beginning of something.

When he surfaced, Brann was leaning over the *Gannet*'s rail, having rowed out uninvited, grinning like a shark.

"Well?"

"There's a fortune down there," Ansel said. "Or near enough."

"There always was." Brann spat thoughtfully over the side. "Question was never whether the pearls existed. Question was who got to dive." He looked at Ansel for a moment, and then, in a voice stripped of its usual performance: "Corliss would have liked seeing this."

Ansel's hands stilled on the rope.

Corliss had been released from the capital three weeks after the verdict, walking with a cane he would probably need forever, his interrogation scars a matter of official record now — testimony, the papers called it, though Ansel knew what the testimony had actually been made of. He had refused Ansel's offer of a room at Hetta's and taken a berth north on the first packet, saying he had letters to deliver and a woman's network to rebuild and that sitting in harbors made him itch. At the gangway he had shaken Ansel's hand and held it a moment longer than the gesture required.

"You gave her the tide," Corliss had said. "Nobody takes that back. Not a magistrate, not a company, not God."

Then he had gone up the ramp, cane tapping, and Saltmere had lost sight of him, though Ansel suspected the world had not.

"He's alive," Ansel said now. "That's more than most of us had any right to."

"Aye." Brann settled himself against the rail, making clear he intended to be fed. "Now. About wages. Because if you think I rowed out here for the scenery—"

* * *

The season turned.

It turned gently, which no one in Saltmere trusted, and then it turned honestly, with rain, and the town began to learn the strange new discipline of owning itself. The cooperative store opened and Marta Vane proved, to general astonishment, that she could indeed add. The divers organized — not as Meridian's crews had been organized, by threat, but around a table at Hetta's, arguing about prices and safety lines and who would pay for the new air hoses. Some of the arguments were bitter. Old grudges surfaced like dead fish. Two families stopped speaking to each other entirely over a dispute about bed rights that predated anyone present.

But they argued as free people argue, which is to say loudly, endlessly, and without anyone being flogged for it, and Hetta presided over the worst nights with a teapot and a face like a closed fist, and somehow the arguments ended in numbers everyone could live with.

Ansel dove the deep beds. Word spread, as word does in harbor towns, and by midsummer boats were coming from two towns over to buy at his mooring, and the price of Saltmere pearls rose for the first time in a generation. He hired two young divers, brothers named Wick and Colm whose father had drowned in Meridian's service and whose mother had wept when Ansel paid them in coin rather than credit. He taught them the deep-water rules he had learned the hard way — come up slow, never chase a shell past your breath, the sea pays wages but she also collects debts of her own.

"Some of those rules," Wick said once, treading water beside the boat, "sound like things that happened to you."

"They are," Ansel said.

He did not tell the boys about the near-drowning. Not yet. Maybe someday, when the memory wore smooth enough to hold without cutting. Some silences he kept now not because they kept him safe but because they were still tender, and that was a different country altogether.

* * *

In the evenings he mended nets on the breakwater, because the breakwater was where he had always gone to think, and thinking had changed shape lately. It used to circle: the debt, the file, the lantern signals, the fear. Now it circled a single point six years distant and one ocean away — Maren on the deck of the morning packet, growing smaller, her hand raised, the folder of proof under her arm like a child entrusted to a wet nurse.

Her letters came rarely. Across a distance that wide, even hatred traveled slow; justice slower. But the word that came back through Corliss's network, through merchants, through the provincial papers that Hetta now subscribed to with fierce extravagance, was consistent: the Meridian charter was under investigation abroad. Wreck-duty fraud, they called it there, in their tidy language. Insurance conspiracy. Names were being taken. Hale's superiors were discovering, with the surprise of men who had signed documents without reading them, that they had built an empire on a scuttled ship.

Maren was doing it. Somewhere out there, in drawing rooms and port offices and whatever rooms enemy agents conducted their wars in, she was laying the folder's contents out like a hand of cards, one at a time, and watching faces change.

Ansel mended his nets and let himself feel it, cautiously, the way you test ice: hope, but only part of your weight at a time.

* * *

On an evening in late summer, Hetta found him on her step with a lamp, which meant she had something to say that wouldn't keep.

"There's a packet tomorrow," she said. "Morning tide."

"There's a packet most mornings."

"This one carries mail from the south." She set the lamp down between them, and its light made small warm islands of their hands. "And there's talk at the customs shed. Talk about a name on the passenger list for the return sailing. Talk about a woman traveling under her own name, which caused comment, because the clerk swears the name was struck from the Crown's lists months ago — struck clean, reinstated, pardoned, whatever they do to enemy agents when they stop being useful as enemies and start being useful as witnesses."

Ansel did not move. The harbor breathed beneath them, dark and patient.

"Hetta."

"I'm telling you what I heard," she said. "That's all. A name on a list. You'd want to check it yourself, knowing how clerks write." She rose, collected her lamp, and paused at her door. "I'll have the kettle on after the tide comes in," she said, which was Hetta's way of saying *go*, and also of saying she had known since the lantern night in the cove, since before, since always, and had simply been waiting for the sea to finish its business.

Ansel sat on the step a long while after her door closed, the lamp guttering beside him, the packet's mast lights already visible far out at the harbor mouth where the dark water met the darker sky.

Somewhere on that ship, moving toward him across the black water at eight knots, there might be a woman with a slanted handwriting and a promise six years old and a pardon in her pocket. Or there might be only a list, misread by a talkative clerk, and another morning of nets and dives and careful, partial hope.

He would know by the morning tide.

He picked up the lamp and walked down to the quay to wait, and the whole town behind him slept, rebuilt and rebuilding, unaware that the next chapter of its life was already hull-down on the horizon, coming in with the light.
