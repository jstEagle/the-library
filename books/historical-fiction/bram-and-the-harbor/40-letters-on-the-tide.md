## 40. Letters on the Tide

The letter came on a Tuesday, which Ansel would remember later because Tuesdays were the days Hetta baked, and the smell of her caraway bread was in the air when Tam Oller, the packet boy, came running along the quay with his cap in one hand and a bundle of post under his arm, shouting names the way he always did, as though every letter were a small fire he was delivering.

"Eastwick! Master Eastwick!"

Ansel was mending a net on the deck of the *Gannet's Sister*, the fishing boat he'd crewed on since spring. His hands had learned the work faster than he'd expected — twelve years of pearl diving gave a man fingers that could feel a knot before he saw it — and he didn't look up at first, because half of Saltmere still looked at him sideways and he'd decided not to feed their looking.

But Tam ran straight past the fish stalls and the rope walk and planted himself at the gunwale, holding out an envelope as if it might bite him.

"This one's foreign," the boy said. "Came on the packet from Corvane, then the coastal mail. There's a seal."

Ansel set down the net.

He knew the handwriting before he took it. He knew it from four feet away, from across a room, from six years of silence and one night in a salt marsh. The envelope was soft from travel, water-spotted, addressed in that slanted, urgent script where every letter leaned east like grass in wind — *Maren Voss's hand, or Maren Eastwick's now, no — Voss, she'd kept Voss* — and the seal on the flap was plain wax, unmarked, which meant she hadn't wanted anyone at any port to know whose letter it was.

Only the address told the truth: *Ansel Eastwick, by the harbor, Saltmere.* Not care of Hetta. Not care of the *Gannet's Sister*. By the harbor, as if he were a feature of the place itself, like the breakwater or the tide bell.

"I didn't read it," Tam said, too quickly. "I mean — I wouldn't."

"I know you wouldn't." Ansel fished a coin from his pocket and flipped it to the boy, who caught it with the practiced greed of the young. "Anything else on the packet for me?"

"No, master. That's the only one with a seal. Brann's got crates coming Thursday, Hetta's got a letter from her sister in Corrow — oh, and the magistrate wants you Thursday too, about the lease papers on the north shed."

Thursday, then. Everything happened on Thursdays now. The world after Meridian ran on smaller wheels, but it turned.

Ansel put the letter inside his coat, against his chest, and finished the net. It took him two hours. It took everything he had, and he was aware the whole time of the paper warming against his skin like a second heartbeat, and he was aware that this was ridiculous, and he did not care.

When the *Sister*'s catch was crated and iced, he walked out along the breakwater.

* * *

The breakwater had been rebuilt in places — Meridian's stone, patched with cheaper rock by the town council after the trial, so that the new sections sat slightly wrong, a different color, like a scar on a face you loved. Ansel found the old section anyway, the part where the granite still wore its original green felt of weed, and sat where he had sat at seventeen with a girl who smelled of tar and oranges, and opened the letter with hands that were steadier than they had been that night, but not much.

Six pages. She had never been capable of short letters; even her smuggling reports, Brann once said, ran to excess.

*Dear Ansel.*

*I have started this letter nine times. In Corvane they teach clerks to begin with business, so: business first, and then I will spend the rest of the pages being honest.*

*The consortium signed yesterday. Eleven houses, including two that Meridian thought were theirs. Your file — our file, yours as much as mine, you bled for it twice — went into evidence with the signatures attached. The charter is suspended pending investigation. Hale's name is on three separate warrants in three separate jurisdictions, and the last I heard he was selling his plate in Corvane and telling everyone he was going into tea. There is no tea. There is only running.*

*I want you to understand what that means, because I know how your mind works, how it will try to make the arithmetic come out fair. You lost a year of your life to the trial and eight months more waiting on my word. You stood in a hall and let them call you a thief before the truth arrived. You stayed ashore when every bone in you wanted the opposite, and you stayed because someone had to hold the ground while the proof crossed the sea. It crossed. It landed. It worked. Whatever it cost you, it bought this. I have watched men spend whole lives and buy less.*

*Now the honesty.*

*I am coming home. Not passing through. Coming home. The consortium has offered me the liaison post for the northern harbors — Saltmere among them, which I suspect was your magistrate's doing, though he will deny it with that face he makes like a door closing — and I have accepted, because I want to be the person who reads the charters now instead of the person they are written against. I want to sit across tables from merchants and be the reason their ink dries slower. I want, mostly, to wake up in a town where I know which silences keep people safe, because I learned the other kind abroad and it is lonelier.*

*You told me once, on this breakwater, that belonging was something the company assigned you, like a berth or a debt, and that you had never once chosen anything in your life. I have thought about that for years. I have built speeches around it in ballrooms where I was pretending to be someone else. So here is mine:*

*I am choosing you. Openly, stupidly, in writing, where anyone can read it. I choose the boy under the breakwater and the man who walked me through marsh mud at three in the morning with constables' lanterns behind us and never once let go of my hand. If you will have me, I will step off the Thursday packet, and we can decide together what the rest looks like — slowly, badly, arguing the whole way, I promise you arguments. If you need time, take it. If the answer is no, say it plainly and I will still come, because Saltmere is mine too, and I have earned the right to be difficult in it.*

*But I hope. God help me, after all these years and all this careful work, I hope like a girl on a breakwater.*

*The packet sails Thursday on the morning tide.*

*Maren*

Ansel read it once straight through, sitting very still, gulls screaming over his head. Then he read it again, slowly, the way he used to read the tide tables — line by line, committing it.

Then he laughed, once, a short helpless sound that startled a cormorant off the rocks, because of course. Of course she'd written *openly, stupidly, in writing.* Of course she'd turned a proposal of the heart into a clause in a contract, because Maren Voss negotiated everything, even love, especially love, and left the other party room to counter-sign on their own terms. *If you will have me.* As if there had ever been a version of his life, in any year of it, where the answer was no.

Below him the tide was turning, the water sliding back off the weed-slick stones in long bronze sheets. Beyond the harbor mouth the sea lay flat and pewter-colored, patient, indifferent, the same sea that had taken his father and nearly taken him and given him back Maren twice. He had made his peace with it the way you make peace with a large animal — carefully, without turning your back, knowing it owed you nothing.

The morning tide ship was due Thursday. Today was Tuesday.

He had two days to become a man who deserved the word *home* used about him in someone else's handwriting.

He sat until the light went long and gold, and then he went to tell Hetta.

* * *

Hetta took the news the way she took everything — with flour on her hands and a verdict delivered to the dough.

"Well," she said. "Finally." She punched the bread down hard enough to be heard in the street. "She'll want feeding. She was always too thin, even before. Does she still eat like a stevedore?"

"Hetta, she hasn't lived here in seven years—"

"She ate like a stevedore at fourteen and nobody changes." Another punch. "North bedroom, then. The good bed. And you're not telling her about the leak in that roof until after the packet lands, because a woman shouldn't arrive to bad news about shingles." She wiped her hands and looked at him properly, and her eyes — which had watched him for fifteen years, first as a landlady collecting rent from an indentured orphan's wages, later as a conspirator hiding smugglers' papers among her preserves — went soft in the way she reserved for perhaps four occasions a year. "You stayed, that night. When she begged you onto the ship."

"I stayed."

"You could have gone. Nobody would've blamed you. The magistrate had your freedom in his hand and Hale's men at the door."

Ansel leaned against the doorframe. "If I'd gone, the evidence was just paper in another country. Here it was a warrant, a trial, a town watching. Somebody had to stand in front of it."

"Somebody," Hetta said, turning back to the dough, "could have stood in front of it and gotten hanged. Don't think I didn't lie awake doing that arithmetic. I buried one boy in this town's service already. I did my lying awake." She shaped the loaf with quick, fierce strokes. "But you were right. I'll grant it. It's a strange feeling, granting it. Now get out of my kitchen and go see the magistrate, and wear the shirt without the patch."

The shirt without the patch had been bought for the trial, and Ansel hated it, and wore it.

Magistrate Corven received him in the hall where the dawn reading had happened — where Ansel had stood with a stolen wreck file open on the bench and watched a ruined old man weigh truth against the most powerful company on the coast and find, to everyone's surprise including his own, that truth still tipped the scale. The wreck file itself sat in a glass case near the window now, displayed like a relic, which Corven claimed was for archival purposes and everyone knew was a monument.

"The north shed lease," Corven said, pushing the papers across. "The consortium honors existing claims. You'll pay rent to the harbor trust instead of the company, which is the same money going somewhere better. Sign there."

Ansel signed.

Corven watched him with those hooded eyes, then said, without preamble: "The Voss woman takes the liaison post Thursday."

"So I'm told."

"You know what she'll be walking into. Half this coast still thinks Meridian's fall was theft dressed as justice, and the other half thinks it was justice dressed as luck, and both halves will test her. A liaison with her history — declared agent, returned from the dead, engaged—" he paused delicately, "—if that's the word, to the diver who broke into the counting house. She'll need people beside her who chose to be there. Not debt. Not gratitude. Choice." He tapped the lease. "That's what that paper means now, Eastwick. Rent to nobody. You may be the first free man in this harbor in thirty years. Try to act like it's strange."

"It is strange," Ansel said.

"Good." Corven gathered his papers, then added, almost as an afterthought, in the tone of a man closing a ledger: "Tell her the hall is open to her whenever she wants to read the old records. Some of us spent a long time unable to look at them. She should have the privilege."

* * *

Wednesday, Ansel dove.

Not for pearls anymore — the beds were held in common now, harvested carefully, the divers paid proper shares, and Ansel only went down when the boats were short-handed — but because he wanted, one last time, to visit the place where his life had actually begun: under the water, in the green silence, where a boy had once learned that the world below the surface asked nothing of him but skill and breath.

He went down past the beds to the old ground, the deep ledge where he'd nearly died the winter of the quota, filling Brann's price one desperate shell at a time. The water was cold and clear. Fish moved through the light in slow silver schools. Down there nothing had changed — no company, no charter, no warrants. The sea kept its own accounts and had never once needed a counting house.

He hung in the blue and let the pressure close around him like a held note, and thought: *this is what she's bringing home to.* Maren had spent seven years learning to breathe in drawing rooms and customs houses, holding herself under surfaces that wanted her drowned. She was surfacing now. They both were.

He came up slowly, the way the new rules demanded, and broke into daylight gasping, alive, twenty-nine years old, and grinning like an idiot at Tam Oller in the skiff above him.

"You're mad, Master Eastwick," Tam said. "Everyone says so. Deep-diving for fun."

"They're right," Ansel said, and spat salt, and laughed.

* * *

Thursday came grey and bright by turns, squalls chasing sun across the harbor, the kind of day the old divers called a coin-flip sky. The tide was early. Ansel was on the quay an hour before the packet showed, wearing the hated shirt, standing where Hetta had put him after inspecting him like cargo.

The town turned out the way it always did for the packet — fishwives, clerks, the divers in twos and threes, Tam weaving between legs — and Ansel felt their glances slide over him and away and sometimes back. He was used to it now, or he told himself so. The man who had exposed Meridian, who had stood trial, who had walked out of the hall a free man with his debt cancelled by a magistrate's ruling and his name in every broadsheet from here to Corvane. *The Saltmere Evidence,* they called the case in the newspapers, as if it belonged to the town and not to him. Maybe it did. That was rather the point.

The packet came around the light at half-ebb, riding the last of the ebb tide with her canvas catching the fluky wind, and Ansel's heart began its slow climb before he could pick out a single figure at the rail.

He watched the passengers disembark in the small crowd of arrivals: two merchants in city coats, a family with too much luggage, a clerk with a leather case. Then a woman in a plain traveling dress and a seaman's oilskin — no finery, though he knew she owned some now — came down the gangway with a document case under one arm and her chin up and her eyes moving over the quay, reading it, cataloguing it, the way she read everything.

She saw him.

For a moment neither moved. The quay flowed around them, carts and crates and shouting, the ordinary machinery of a working morning, and the two of them stood fixed in it like stones in a stream. Six years of silence between them, then one night in a marsh, then a year of letters crossing the water in sealed wax — and now forty feet of wet planking, which suddenly seemed the widest distance either had ever crossed.

Maren closed it first. She always had.

She stopped a pace away, looked him over with theatrical thoroughness, and said, "You're wearing the trial shirt."

"It's the only one Hetta approves of."

"It's hideous." Her voice cracked on the word, and her eyes were shining, and the document case slipped from under her arm and she didn't catch it, and neither did he, because he was already holding her, both of them laughing and gripping each other like people pulled from the same wreck, which, in every way that mattered, they were.

Around them the quay went on: Tam Oller yelling the packet's manifest, gulls wheeling, the tide bell ringing the turn of the water. Somewhere behind them Hetta stood with bread warm in a cloth, pretending not to watch, watching everything. Somewhere beyond the harbor mouth the deep ledges waited, and the beds, and the boats, and all the ordinary enormous work of a free town.

"I got your letter," Ansel said into her hair.

"I know. I watched you not answer it for two days. I've been composing your refusal the entire crossing, and I have to tell you, it was devastating."

"There's no refusal." He drew back enough to see her face — thinner, lines at her eyes the sea hadn't put there, the same slanted urgency in every feature. "There never was one. But I want it said properly, out loud, on the record, since you insist on contracts." He took her hands, standing on the rebuilt stone where the old stone met it, scar and original joined. "I choose you. Openly, stupidly. Anyone can read it."

Maren Voss — liaison for the northern harbors, enemy agent, drowned girl, home — closed her eyes for exactly one breath, the way she did when a negotiation ended exactly as she'd planned and she refused to look pleased too quickly.

"Witnessed," she said.

And behind them, clear over the water, the morning tide began to run.
