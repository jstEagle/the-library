## 1. What Fits in a Saddlebag

The chancery had stopped sweeping the floors.

Casper Okonkwo noticed it the way he noticed everything — as a courier noticed things, in small accumulations of wrongness that a civilian would walk past and never think of again. The marble of the Third Dispatch Hall still gleamed where the light from the high windows fell, but along the baseboards the dust had gathered into grey felted ridges, and in the corners there were leaves. Leaves, in November, blown in and left to lie, because somewhere below stairs a man with a broom had decided the republic had perhaps eleven more weeks in it and had gone home to his family.

Casper stood at the assignment desk and waited, hands folded behind his back, boots together, in the posture of a man who had no thoughts at all beyond the next road. It was a posture he could hold for hours. He had once held it through an entire audience with the Deputy Minister of Ways while the man wept openly over a map of the northern provinces. You learned, in the courier service, that stillness was a kind of currency. It bought you trust. It bought you time. On bad nights, in border towns where the loyalties changed with the weather, it had bought him his life.

The clerk behind the desk was a boy he didn't know. That was wrong too. For eleven years Casper had collected clerks the way other men collected scars; he knew their names, their coughs, which ones watered the wine and which ones would trade you a hot meal for news of the coast. This boy's collar was too clean and his hands were too soft, and when he looked up at Casper there was something in his face that wasn't quite respect. It was closer to pity, or to guilt, and neither of those belonged on the face of a man handing out roads.

"Okonkwo," the boy said, reading from a docket rather than greeting him. "Final run of the season. Three dispatches, sealed, to be delivered in order. First to the garrison at Kessel. Second to the magistrate's court at Halven."

Halven.

The word went through Casper like a needle finding the old channel in cloth. He did not move. He had spent eleven years learning not to move, and if all the rest of his training burned away from him tomorrow, that would remain: the body's obedience to the face's decision not to feel anything yet.

"Halven," he repeated, in the flat tone of a man confirming a place he had no history with. "Border town. Lantern-makers."

"There's a lantern-makers' guild hall there," the boy agreed, without interest. "Third delivery is verbal only. You'll receive it from Captain Rehn, at the north gate, before departure." The boy slid three objects across the desk. They were wrapped in oilcloth, each no bigger than a prayer book, each heavy in a way that had nothing to do with weight. Wax seals showed under the wrapping's edge — black wax, the chancery seal pressed deep, the kind of seal that meant the words beneath were not advice.

"Sign," the boy said, and turned the ledger around.

Casper signed. His hand did not shake. Eleven years of shaking deferred, all of it banked, all of it accruing interest in some drawer of him that he kept meaning to inspect and never did.

"The roads north—" he began.

"Are open," the boy said. "As of this morning. After that, Okonkwo, I couldn't tell you what they are." And then, quieter, with the pity showing again like a stain through whitewash: "They said to tell you it's the last run. Everyone's being given a last run. After this, the service is..." He made a small gesture with one soft hand, a gesture that might have meant *reorganized* and might have meant *ended* and almost certainly meant both.

Casper took the dispatches, bowed the precise degree owed to a clerk of the third rank, and walked out of the hall with his heels striking the marble at the interval of a metronome.

* * *

His rooms were three streets east of the chancery, above a cooper's shop that hadn't shipped so much as a bucket since spring. He climbed the outside stair because he always climbed the outside stair — habit was armor, and armor was the only property a courier truly owned — and let himself into two rooms he had never once thought of as home.

Home was a word for people whose lives didn't fit in a saddlebag.

He lit the lamp, set the three dispatches on the table in a row, and looked at them the way a surgeon looks at a wound he has decided not to treat. Then he did what he always did after an assignment. He took inventory.

It was a ritual by now, older than some of his scars. From the peg by the door came the saddlebags themselves, cracked leather gone dark with eleven winters of road salt, mended twice at the left strap with stitches he'd put in himself by firelight in a place he would deny under oath ever having been. He laid them open on the bed.

Coins first. He counted them out onto the blanket by lamplight, though he knew the count to the copper: thirty-four Vessani crowns, four of them clipped thin enough that any honest money-changer would weigh them and frown; nine silver pieces from across the western border, acceptable anywhere and loyal to nothing; and one gold coin of no country at all, stamped with a mountain on one side and worn nearly smooth on the other, given to him once by a ferryman who had refused to say where it came from or why he was giving it away. Total portable wealth: enough to live modestly for two months in a peaceful country. In the country as it currently was, enough to buy perhaps four days of silence from the right kind of man, or six hundred miles of road if he slept outdoors and ate what the ditches offered.

He counted them anyway. Not because the number would change, but because counting was the shape his hands made when they were steadying the rest of him.

Next the codebook. Small, bound in grey calf, indistinguishable from a book of psalms unless you knew that psalms were not printed in cipher-grids. He had carried it so long it had molded itself to the curve of his palm the way a walking stick molds to a grip. There were codes in it for every contingency the chancery had imagined: codes for *route compromised*, for *send replacement*, for *contents false*, that last one added in someone else's hand two years ago, which had struck him even then as an odd thing for a state to need and an odder thing for it to admit. He opened it now to the flyleaf, where in faded ink a former instructor had written, years before Casper owned it: *A code is a promise that two people agree to keep in public.* He had never understood what the man meant. He suspected he was about to.

And then, last, from around his neck on its cord of braided horsehair, the brass charm.

He unhooked it and set it in the lamp's light. It was no bigger than a thumbnail, cast in cheap brass that had never once been polished and had consequently gone the color of old honey. It was a lantern — a real lantern rendered in miniature, with a hinged door that actually swung, and inside it, visible through the pierced tinwork, a fleck of red glass where a flame would be. A child could have made it. A child *had* made it, probably; the Veren workshop in Halven let the youngest apprentices practice their piercing on charms, and sold them at the autumn fair for a coin apiece to travelers who wanted proof they'd crossed the border.

Seven years ago, a girl of nineteen had taken one off a nail by the workshop door, folded Casper's half-frozen fingers around it, and told him: *When peace comes, come back and show us. Light it three times in the window and we'll know you kept your word.*

Mira Veren. She'd have used a different word than *girl* if anyone asked her to describe that winter, and she'd have been right to. He had been twenty-four and bleeding from a fall in the pass that should have killed him, delirious by the time they found him in the snow with his dispatch case frozen shut, and the Verens had hidden him for four months — from the customs men, from the requisition officers, from a war that was already, quietly, beginning to eat the borders. They had asked him nothing about what he carried. That was the part he could never explain to anyone who hadn't been sheltered by lantern-makers: they asked nothing. In a country of papers and seals and questions with traps in them, they simply fed him, set his shoulder, taught him to solder, and waited for spring as if waiting were a craft too, one their family had perfected.

He had sworn, feverish and earnest and young, that he would come back with news. News, he had meant then. Good news. Peace, and the roads open, and a courier with nothing in his saddlebags but gratitude arriving to light a lantern in their window.

Seven years of assignments had gotten in the way, each one urgent, each one final, each one followed by another. Seven years of telling himself that next season. Next season. And now the service itself was ending, the republic dissolving like sugar in wet ink, and the last run of the season — the *final* run, a word the boy at the desk had used carelessly, the way people use words when they have never had to carry them — ended at Halven.

Casper closed his fist around the charm until the hinges bit his palm.

There it was, then. The inventory complete: coins, codebook, charm. Thirty-four bad crowns, nine neutral silvers, one gold coin loyal to nobody, a book of promises written in grids, and a lantern with a flame of glass that had never been lit. Everything he was, laid out on a army blanket, and it had all fit in a saddlebag for eleven years, and he had told himself this was freedom. Mobility. The courier's pride. Now, sitting on the edge of the bed in a room above a dead cooper's shop, in a capital whose floors went unswept, he examined the feeling underneath his sternum and gave it, at last, its honest name.

Dread. Quiet, professional, patient dread — the dread of a man who has just been handed three sealed packets, one of them addressed to the only place he has ever loved, in a country that has begun eating its own limbs.

He got up, packed the bags with the economy of long practice — coins against the frame, codebook in the inner flap, charm back around his neck where it lay against his breastbone like a small cold key — and forced himself to eat half a loaf and some hard cheese with the thoroughness of a man fueling a machine. Sleep would not come; he hadn't expected it to. He sat instead by the window with the lamp turned low, watching the capital he had served make its preparations for dying. Somewhere south a bell rang the hour, hesitated, and did not finish.

* * *

He was at the north gate an hour before dawn, as ordered, breath smoking, the horse line restless in the cold. The gate watch barely looked at his papers anymore; the guards had begun checking fewer documents and more faces, as if they had already transferred their loyalty from the seal to the person, which was either the death of the service or its last honest act.

Captain Ilsa Rehn was waiting under the gate arch with a lantern in one hand and, he noted, no dispatch case at all.

She looked exactly as she had looked when she signed off his field certification nine years ago: tall, grey at the temples in a way that seemed earned rather than suffered, wearing her rank the way other people wore old coats — comfortably, with no illusions about staying warm forever. She had trained half the couriers still riding. She had unmade others, quietly, with a signature and a sentence. Casper had spent nine years deciding whether he feared her or admired her and had concluded, correctly he believed, that the answer was yes.

"Okonkwo." Her voice carried without rising, the way it always did. "You have the packets."

"Three, Captain. In order. Kessel, Halven, and—"

"And the third is mine to give." She stepped close, and though there was no one within thirty feet, she lowered her voice anyway, which was its own message. "No paper for this one. Paper can be intercepted. Paper can be read on a corpse." She looked at him steadily, and in the lantern light her eyes did something he had never seen them do: they paused, as if consulting something. "You'll deliver the first two. The third instruction concerns your return. You'll hear it once."

"I'm listening, Captain."

She told him. Four sentences. Short ones, chosen like ammunition.

Casper stood very still while they settled into him, each one finding its place with the soft, terrible precision of stones dropped into deep water. The gate wind moved his cloak. The horse line stamped and blew. Above them, in the machicolations of the gate tower, a pigeon rearranged itself and went back to sleep, having no stake in any of it.

"That is all, Captain?" he said at last, and his voice was level. Eleven years of banking. The interest would come due later, on some dark road, all at once. But not now.

"That is all." She straightened, and for one moment — he would spend years trying to decide whether he had imagined it — her hand rose halfway toward his shoulder, toward the gesture she'd made at his graduation, *ride well*, and then fell short. "You were the best I certified, Okonkwo. Do you know that? Out of forty-one."

"I'd heard rumors, Captain."

"Rumors flatter everyone." She stepped back into the arch, raising her lantern, and her face went official again, stone settling back over whatever had moved beneath it. "Roads are clear to Kessel. Beyond that, no report will reach you and none of ours will meet you. Ride as if no one is coming, because no one is."

She turned away, then paused, half-turned, the lantern throwing her shadow long across the frost-silvered stones of the gateway — the whole republic's gateway, though neither of them said so.

"Okonkwo. One question, off the record." Her eyes found his, and held. "The second packet. Halven. Have you been there before?"

The charm lay against his breastbone, small and cold and silent, seven years of an unkept promise weighing less than a coin.

"No, Captain," said Casper Okonkwo. "Never."

She studied him a moment longer — long enough that the lie had time to stand up, look around, and realize it had survived — then nodded once and walked back into the dark of the arch, and the gate detail began hauling on the winches, and the doors of the north gate of Vessany opened onto a road going up into the hills, into the early snow-light, north.

Casper led his horse through, mounted, and did not look back at the capital, because couriers did not look back, because looking back was weight, and everything he allowed himself had to fit in a saddlebag.

But at the top of the first rise, where the road bent and the city was still a smudge of smoke behind him, he took out the brass charm and opened its little hinged door, and let the morning light fall on the fleck of red glass. Three taps, Mira had told him once, teaching him the workshop's signal the way you'd teach a family secret to family. Three taps on glass means safe harbor. Come home.

He closed the little door. He put the charm away. He rode north with three sealed dispatches in his bag, four sentences buried in him like shrapnel, and the growing, unswept-corner certainty that he was carrying, in perfect professional calm, the exact weight of everything that could destroy him.
