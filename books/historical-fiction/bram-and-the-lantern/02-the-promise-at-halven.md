## 2. The Promise at Halven

Seven years before the republic died, Casper Okonkwo learned that a wound is only half a misfortune. The other half is the road.

He had been nineteen, three months into his commission as a junior courier of the Second Chancery, when the border skirmish at Kolvat Marsh went wrong in the way small things go wrong — an ambush nobody had predicted, a horse screaming, a bullet meant for his shoulder finding the meat below his collarbone instead. He had cut himself free of the saddle rigging, taken the dispatch case, and walked — staggered, then crawled — away from the burning causeway while militiamen who were not yet calling themselves rebels picked through the wreckage of the escort.

By the third day the wound had gone hot. By the fifth he was delirious enough to be walking in circles, and the marsh country had narrowed around him into hills he did not know, under a sky the color of old pewter, with snow coming down in the fat, indifferent flakes of early winter. The dispatch case was still slung across his chest. He remembered being proud of that, even as his thoughts unraveled — proud that some part of him kept faith with the seal even when the rest of him could not keep faith with his own name.

That was how the girl found him: facedown in a drainage ditch at the edge of a terraced orchard, one hand still hooked through the case's strap, more dead than the dead horse he'd left behind on the road.

She did not scream when she turned him over. That was the first thing he ever learned about Mira Veren — that she was not a person who screamed. She knelt in the snow, pressed two fingers beneath his jaw, considered him for a long moment with eyes the gray-green of bottle glass, and then said, in the flat practical voice of someone triaging a lamb, "You'll ruin my father's evening," and whistled twice toward the tree line.

What came out of the tree line was not a cart or a mule but a man carrying a lantern in broad daylight — a big, slow-moving man with a smith's shoulders and a lamplighter's face, who took one look at Casper and said, "Well. He's carried post." As though that explained everything. As though that settled the question of whether a stranger bleeding in their ditch was worth the risk of a firing squad.

Casper would learn, over the winter that followed, that it did.

* * *

The town was called Halven, and it sat where the republic stopped pretending to govern — the last cluster of slate roofs before the border fens, famous for exactly one thing. Lanterns. Not tallow dips, not the crude whale-oil lamps peddled from wagons, but true lanterns: brass and tin and horn, lanterns with lenses ground in the family workshop until they threw light clean and far, lanterns that hung in lighthouse towers along the northern coast and in the chapel belfries of three provinces. The Verens had made them for six generations. There was a saying in Halven that you could walk from the fens to the sea after dark and never once be lost, so long as somewhere ahead of you a Veren lantern was burning.

The house stood above the workshop at the end of Lanternmakers' Row, and it smelled — this was Casper's first coherent memory after the fever took him and gave him back — of beeswax and hot metal and the particular mineral sweetness of polishing rouge. He surfaced from fever the way a swimmer surfaces from deep water, in stages, and each stage arrived with a sound: the rasp of a file beyond the wall; a kettle; a woman's low voice reading aloud, badly, stumbling over the long words in an almanac; and once, close beside his ear, the unmistakable tick of cooling brass, patient as a clock.

When his eyes finally agreed to focus, he was lying in a box bed under more quilts than he had seen in his entire life, and Mira Veren was sitting in the corner mending a lamp wick by feel, watching him with the expression of a farmer watching weather she had already decided to work through.

"You've been talking," she said. "For four days. Mostly numbers."

"Relay codes," he rasped. Then, horrified: "Did anyone else—"

"My father heard. My mother heard. Nobody wrote anything down." She bit her thread. "We're not interested."

"We're not," said a voice from the doorway, and there was the big man again — Bram Veren, though Casper didn't know the name yet — filling the frame the way a stove fills a room. He had his sleeves rolled and his forearms scorched pink from the braziers, and he looked at Casper the way Casper would later watch him look at a stubborn hinge: without anger, and without any intention of losing. "The republic sends us tax collectors and lets our roads rot. It sends soldiers who shoot at nothing. But it also sends mail." He held up a hand before Casper could speak. "I know what's in the case. I'm not asking. A man who guards a letter with a hole in him is a man keeping a promise to somebody, and this house has a rule about promises. We keep them, and we don't ask other people to break theirs. Eat your soup."

It was, Casper thought afterward, the strangest act of mercy he ever received, precisely because it asked nothing. They hid him not as an investment, not out of ideology, not even — entirely — out of kindness, but because the alternative was letting a wounded boy die in a ditch within sight of their orchard, and the Verens had simply never been the kind of people in whose universe that was a survivable fact. His convalescence became, without ceremony, part of the household's ordinary machinery, the way a broken gate becomes a mended one.

* * *

Winter closed over Halven like a lid.

The war — the first of the wars, the little one, the rehearsal — had made the border roads dangerous for official traffic, and so no one came looking for a missing courier whose name had already been entered in the ledgers as *lost with escort, presumed*. This should have frightened Casper. Presumed dead at nineteen is a strange gift. But instead it loosened something in him. For the first time since he'd signed his indenture papers at sixteen, no one knew where he was. He belonged to no route, no relay, no roster. He belonged, improbably, to a house that smelled of beeswax.

So he healed, and because idleness was a sin in that house second only to waste, he worked. First at the bench tasks his shoulder allowed — cutting horn panes, sorting solder, feeding the polishing wheels — and then, as January hardened, at everything else. He learned to sweat a seam. He learned why a lantern's cap must be domed and never flat (snow, Bram said, sliding off; a flat roof is a promise the weather will break). He learned to read the flame itself, which was the deepest lesson and the hardest: that a wick trimmed too short starves, and too tall smokes, and that the whole art of the family trade was not making light but *husbanding* it.

"The chancery thinks light is a switch," Mira told him one night on the workshop roof, where they'd climbed to trim the great signal lantern that guided fen-cutters through the channel markers. Her breath smoked. Below them Halven lay blue-white and quiet, woodsmoke standing up straight from a dozen chimneys. "On, off, orders given, orders obeyed. Papa says light is more like a fire you're sharing. You don't command it. You feed it, and you shield it from wind, and you pass it on before yours goes out."

"And what do you say?" Casper asked.

She trimmed the wick with a little brass snuffer, unhurried. "I say my father talks about lanterns when he means people, and it's annoying, and it's true."

He laughed, and the laugh hurt his shoulder, and he laughed anyway, and somewhere in that moment the debt he owed the family quietly changed currencies. It stopped being a debt of survival and became something he could not have named at nineteen and would carry, unnamed and exacting, for the rest of his life.

Because they did not merely shelter him. They *counted* him. Mira's mother, Sela — a small, iron-spined woman who spoke perhaps forty words a day and missed none of them — began setting his bowl at the table without comment, in the fourth seat, between Tam-of-nowhere's empty place and Bram's. When Casper noticed, weeks later, that the fourth seat had always been laid, Sela said only, "House was built for four," and passed the bread. On the long dark evenings Bram read the news aloud from month-old broadsheets, and argued with it, and taught Casper the border dialect by correcting his vowels over and over with a craftsman's patience, until Casper dreamed in it. Mira drew him maps of the fens in pencil on the backs of failed lens diagrams — *this causeway floods; this one lies; trust the cairn, not the track* — and refused, always, to explain why she knew the paths so well herself, and he was wise enough not to ask.

And on the night of the solstice, when Halven burned every lantern it owned in defiance of the year's longest darkness — the whole town guttering gold, windows and doorways and the frozen millrace lined with light — Bram Veren called Casper down to the bench, and put in his hands a lantern the length of a thumb.

It was brass, and it was perfect. A working striker folded into its base; a horn pane thin as a fingernail; a ring for a chain. On its underside, in script fine as a watchmaker's, three letters: *V.V.* — the workshop mark, the mark that went only on pieces the family kept or gave.

"For the road," Bram said. "A courier travels dark country. Now some of the light you carry is ours."

Casper held it and could not speak. He was nineteen and had been paid in coin his whole life, coin and rations and the abstract satisfactions of service, and he did not have the vocabulary for what it meant that these people had spent six generations learning to make one small thing perfectly so that a stranger might not be lost.

So he made a promise instead, because it was the only currency he had, and because the house had a rule about promises.

"When peace comes," he said, "when the roads are safe and the chancery has no more use for me — I'll come back. And I'll bring news. Real news, from the capital, from everywhere — whatever the world knows, I'll bring it here and tell it to you straight, no chancery varnish on it. Whatever happens, whatever side of it you're on—" he stumbled, because he hadn't yet learned that soon there would be no sides, only rubble, "—you'll hear it from me first. From a friend. That's my oath."

Bram studied him for a long time, the way he studied a seam that had to hold. Then he nodded once and shook Casper's good hand with a grip like a vise closing gently.

"Promises are like lanterns," he said. "Anyone can light one. The whole craft is keeping it burning."

Mira, leaning in the doorway with a lamp in her own hands, said nothing at all. But years later — much later, in a cellar beneath a ruined workshop, waiting on a promise she had half believed was a story — it was her face he remembered from that night, lit gold at the edges, watching him with those bottle-glass eyes as if she were filing the oath away somewhere careful. Somewhere it could be found again.

* * *

Spring came late that year, and when the passes cleared, the Chancery came for him — a rider with a copy of the casualty amendment, restoring Junior Courier Okonkwo to the rolls with bureaucratic embarrassment, back pay enclosed. He packed his things in a saddlebag that weighed almost nothing: some coin, spare shirts, the codebook, and the little brass lantern riding against his hip like a second heartbeat.

He said goodbye badly, as nineteen-year-olds do. He hugged Sela, which startled them both. He let Bram grip his shoulder and say only, "Trim your wick, boy," which was the family's benediction and its warning in one breath. At the end of Lanternmakers' Row he turned, and the whole household stood in the doorway beneath the workshop sign — a lantern worked in iron — and Mira raised her hand, not waving, just open, fingers spread against the morning.

He rode south and did not look back again, because looking back was a luxury couriers were trained out of, and because he believed — with the terrible confidence of the young and the loyal — that he could come back any time he chose. That the road ran both ways. That peace was coming, someday soon, and behind it a promise, and behind the promise a lit doorway in a town famous for never going dark.

Seven years later, riding north through the same hills with three sealed dispatches in his saddlebag and the little brass lantern still at his hip, Casper Okonkwo would understand, at last, how much a promise weighs when you have to carry it that far — and how the roads you trusted most are the first thing a dying country closes.

The relay station at Two Fords was a day ahead. So, though he did not know it yet, was the first crack in everything he believed.
