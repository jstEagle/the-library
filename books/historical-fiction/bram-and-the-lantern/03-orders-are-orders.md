## 3. Orders Are Orders

The north road out of Vessany City was a gray ribbon stitched through gray fields, and Casper Okonkwo rode it the way he rode everything: at a pace that looked unhurried and covered ground like a debt being collected.

He had left before dawn, when the capital still smelled of last night's fires and the barricades on Corven Street were only smoke and rumor. Three dispatches in his saddlebag, each in its oiled leather sleeve, each sealed with wax the color of dried blood. The chancery clerk who'd handed them over had not met his eyes — which was nothing new; clerks never did — but there had been something else this time, some small tremor at the corner of the man's mouth, as if he were biting back words that would cost him his post or worse. Casper had logged the detail and filed it where he filed everything: under *later*.

*Later* was a country of its own, and lately its borders kept expanding.

He rode through the morning with the city shrinking behind him like a stain drying into the horizon, and he did what he always did on the first day of a run: he took inventory. It was a habit older than his commission, from the years when inventory was all he had — an orphan's arithmetic, everything he owned measured against everything he could lose.

In the right saddlebag: the codebook, bound in plain calfskin, unmarked but for three pinpricks along the spine that meant *rotating cipher, northern circuit*. In his coat: forty-one crowns in mixed coin, folded into a money belt that had been his father's and had held more value as leather than it ever had as inheritance. In the breast pocket, over his heart where it had ridden for seven years: a brass lantern charm no bigger than his thumbnail, worn smooth at the ring where a chain had once been.

And in the left saddlebag, wrapped separately, the thing that was not inventory at all. A letter. Unsealed. Written by lamplight two nights ago in handwriting he barely recognized as his own, because he had written so little in seven years that wasn't a report.

*To Mira Veren, Halven.* That was all the direction it needed. There was one lantern-maker's family in Halven, and one daughter who had taught a fevered stranger to braid rush cord by feel because his eyes wouldn't focus, and if peace ever came she'd said — laughing, her hands moving, his clumsy ones learning — *then come tell us about it, courier, or I'll know you died somewhere interesting.*

Peace hadn't come. Something else had come instead, wearing peace's coat, and Casper carried his letter north toward a town that might already be ash.

The dispatches, then. He made himself think of them as freight. Freight didn't ask questions. Freight went from seal to hand, hand to seal, and the man between was only a bridge, and bridges don't read what crosses them.

That was the catechism. He had said it to apprentices a dozen times, in the flat reciting voice the service required. *You are a bridge. Bridges do not open.*

So why, he asked himself for perhaps the hundredth time since the chancery, did the first dispatch weigh wrong?

It was not a question he could answer honestly without handling it again, and handling it again was the kind of thing a careful man did only when the road was empty and the light was his own. The road was empty. The light was late-morning, thin and honest, falling across a fallow field where crows argued over something dead.

He reined in at the milestone, dismounted as if stretching a stiff knee, and lifted the first dispatch from the bag.

Oiled leather. Wax seal intact, the republic's crossed-keys pressed deep and clean. Weight: he had carried ten thousand dispatches in fourteen years of service and his hands knew their grammar the way a musician's fingers know frets. This one should have weighed — what? Two sheets, standard chancery stock, plus the wrapper. Perhaps a touch more if it bore enclosures. His palm said it weighed like five sheets. Like six.

Like paper folded around something that was not paper.

Casper turned it over. Turned it back. The seal was flawless. The wrapper stitching was regulation double-cross at the spine. Everything was exactly correct, and that itself was a kind of wrongness, because in a collapsing republic nothing traveled this smoothly — not men, not money, not orders. Smoothness now meant preparation, and preparation meant someone upstream had known, weeks ago, what the republic was only now discovering about itself.

He put it back in the bag. He mounted. He rode on, and told himself the weight was wet ink, thick stock, his own nerves adding ounces.

He did not believe himself. But belief, he had learned, was a luxury item, and couriers traveled light.

* * *

He stopped at noon at the shrine-well outside Dremm's Crossing, where the water came up cold and iron-tasting and the carved saint above the basin had lost her face to weather or iconoclasm — in Vessany these days it was hard to say which. He watered the horse. He drank from his cupped hand. And that was when he noticed he was being followed.

Not followed — preceded, then overtaken, then paced. The rider had been behind him since the second milestone, close enough to the hedgerows to be scenery. Now he came down the road openly, and Casper felt the particular cold that comes not from danger but from recognition of a pattern.

The rider was young. Sixteen, maybe seventeen, on a service pony too small for him, with the stiff-backed seat of someone taught to ride by drill rather than by childhood. Service cloak, service satchel, and pinned at the collar the tin badge of an apprentice courier — third class, lowest rung, the badge every courier in Vessany had once worn and most had thrown in a river the day they made full grade.

The boy drew rein six feet away. Sat very straight. Said, in a voice pitched carefully between respect and rehearsal:

"Courier Okonkwo. Tam Voss, apprentice third class. I'm assigned to you."

Casper looked at him for a long moment. Long enough that the boy's ears went pink and his pony shifted.

"Assigned," Casper said.

"Yes, sir. By order of the Northern Circuit office." The boy produced a folded chit. Casper took it without touching fingers to fingers — habit — and read it. It named him. It named the route. It authorized an apprentice observer attached to the courier's person for the duration of transit. It was signed with a stamp, not a signature, and the stamp belonged to an office that technically did not exist anymore; it had been dissolved in the spring reorganization, though everyone understood such dissolutions to be a matter of letterhead.

"Observer," Casper said.

"Shadow, sir, if you prefer. They used both words at the depot."

"Which word did they use more?"

Tam considered whether honesty was survivable. Decided — visibly, you could watch the decision happen behind his eyes — "Observer, mostly. Toward the end, shadow."

"And no explanation given?"

"No, sir."

"No explanation requested?"

A pause. "I requested one, sir. The duty officer said the request was noted." The way he said *noted* told Casper everything about how requests were received in the Northern Circuit office these days: stamped, shelved, forgotten in the general avalanche of a state coming apart.

Casper handed back the chit and looked at the boy properly. Thin face, sunburnt at the cheekbones. Hands that were careful with the reins. Eyes that moved — and this was the part Casper noted and filed — not to the road, not to the horse, but to the saddlebag. To the left saddlebag. Twice in ten seconds.

They had sent him a child to watch him, and armed the child with nothing but appetite and a tin badge.

"You've done runs before?" Casper asked.

"Bridgeton twice. Millhaven once. Relay work." Pride flickered there, quickly smothered. "Nothing north."

"No. Nothing north, at this season." Casper remounted. "Rules. You ride where I can see you. You speak when spoken to until I say otherwise. You eat when I eat, sleep when I stop, and you do not touch the dispatches, the codebook, or my horse's near-side flank, because she bites and she is a better judge of character than either of us."

"Yes, sir." A beat. "Sir — may I ask—"

"You may ask. I may not answer. Both of those are also rules."

The boy's mouth twitched, almost a smile, strangled at birth. He fell in on Casper's right, at the distance of a man riding beside someone rather than behind him, and Casper let him have the position because correcting it would cost more than it bought.

They rode. The fields gave way to orchards gone untended, apples rotting sweet and alcoholic in the grass, wasps drunk among them. Somewhere south, behind them, Vessany City burned or didn't; the road offered no opinion.

After an hour, Tam said, "Sir?"

"Hm."

"The duty officer also said—" The boy stopped. Started again, lower. "He said to tell you the schedule has been accelerated. The relay at Harrow Ford will expect you tomorrow night instead of Thursday. And that—" the boy recited it with the flat care of words memorized without understanding "—that delivery confirmation is now to be given verbally, to the receiving agent personally, at each station. Not by seal-log. Verbally."

Casper kept his face doing nothing at all. Inside, something turned over slowly, the way ice turns over on a river in thaw — silently, hugely, changing the whole surface of things.

Verbal confirmation. Seal-logs had existed precisely so that confirmation could be verified later, by clerks, by auditors, by history. Verbal confirmation meant the record was whatever the receiving agent said it was. Verbal confirmation meant deniability flowed downhill, and it pooled, as it always pooled, on the men who carried things.

"Did he say why?"

"No, sir."

Of course not. The republic had entered its final grammar: the imperative mood, subject omitted. Do. Carry. Confirm. Ask nothing.

"Then we make Harrow Ford tomorrow," Casper said. "Which means we push past Dremm tonight. Can your pony manage it?"

Something flared in the boy's face — offense, quickly converted into resolve. "She's managed worse, sir."

"They all say that." But Casper found, to his mild annoyance, that he believed the boy. There was a set to Tam Voss's shoulders that Casper remembered from mirrors long ago: the stubbornness of someone who had been given nothing and decided early that nothing would be the sum total only over his objections.

They rode on. The afternoon aged. Crows kept their argument going in the far field, patient as clerks.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a ruined tithe-barn, walls standing to shoulder height, roof long since gone to fuel somebody's desperate winter. Casper built the fire small and shielded, drilled into both of them the old rule — *a courier's fire is seen by one man fewer than he can afford* — and they ate hard bread and smoked river fish Tam produced from his satchel, caught, he admitted when pressed, from the canal at Bridgeton on his last run. Initiative. Casper approved of initiative in principle and distrusted it in children, and could not have said where the line between those things ran, only that the boy sat squarely upon it.

Tam talked, because boys talk, and because silence on the road is a currency most people can't afford. He talked about the depot at Bridgeton, about the instructor with the whistle who made them run signal drills till their throats cracked, about his mother who worked the dye-works and his father who had been a soldier in the border war and came back with a limp and a temper. He talked the way people talk when they're afraid that stopping will invite questions, and Casper listened with half his mind and kept the other half on the saddlebag.

Because he had taken it out to check the girth straps — a legitimate errand — and he had felt it again. That weight. Wrongness has a texture, and this texture was dense, deliberate, layered. Paper around paper around something with a grain to it.

And there was more. While Tam saw to the horses, Casper had held the first dispatch up against the firelight — casually, the way any courier checks his seals — and the flame had shown him something the daylight hadn't: through the oiled leather, faint as breath on glass, the shadow of the contents' edge. The packet inside was squared. Crisp corners. Chancery stock, fine paper, cut true.

Nobody cut corners that true anymore. The paper mills were on strike; the chancery had been issuing orders on stock rough enough to shave with for a month. Every dispatch Casper had carried since autumn had soft edges, deckle-roughened, hurried. This one had edges like a knife had planned it.

Prepared. Not written — prepared. Sitting in a drawer somewhere, finished, waiting for events to catch up to it.

Waiting for a war, or a purge, or a name, the way a trap waits for a foot.

"Cas— Courier Okonkwo?" Tam had come back into the firelight, leading the horses. He stood there with the awkwardness of a boy holding a question he'd rehearsed. "Can I ask you something? About the trade, I mean. Properly."

"You can ask."

"When you carry something sealed. When you don't know what it is." Tam crouched across the fire, face half-lit, and his voice dropped as if the horses might repeat it. "Does it ever — does the weight ever feel like more than paper? Like the thing matters so much it's heavier than it should be? The instructors say that's nonsense. Superstition. They say paper weighs what paper weighs."

Casper looked at the boy across the small fire, at the earnest hunger in his face, and felt something old move in his chest — the memory of being seventeen and asking a grey-bearded courier the same question at a shrine-well much like this one, and being told: *paper weighs what paper weighs, and what you're feeling is consequence, and consequence has mass.*

"It's not nonsense," Casper said. "But it isn't magic either. What you're feeling is yourself. Your own knowledge that what you carry will change things — that hands will be forced, doors opened or shut, that someone will stand somewhere after tonight and their life will divide into before-this and after-this. You carry that knowledge along with the paper. That's the extra weight. Every courier feels it. The good ones learn to carry it upright."

Tam absorbed this with the gravity of a boy being handed doctrine. Then: "Do yours feel heavy now? These?" A nod toward the saddlebag. "The ones we're carrying."

Casper fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch.

"Heavier than they should," he said.

He said it lightly, as a joke, a teacher's indulgence, and watched relief and pride cross the boy's face in equal measure. And beneath the lightness, where no one could see it, the sentence stood at attention and refused to be dismissed.

Heavier than they should be. Because the stock was too fine, and the seal too perfect, and the schedule accelerated, and confirmation verbal, and a child with a tin badge assigned to watch a fourteen-year veteran without explanation — and because somewhere upstream, in offices whose names were being struck from letterheads even as they worked, someone had written orders in advance and was waiting, patient as a trap, for the world to grow into them.

Casper rolled into his blanket with his back to the fire and the saddlebag strap wound once around his wrist, which he had never once done in fourteen years of service and could not have explained if asked.

Across the embers, Tam slept the boneless sleep of the young, one hand curled near his tin badge.

North, beyond the dark hills, lay Halven. Tomorrow night, Harrow Ford, and a receiving agent who would want his verbal yes. Casper lay awake a long while, listening to the wind work at the ruined barn, and thought about bridges.

*Bridges do not open,* the catechism said.

But bridges, he thought, are built to bear weight — and this bridge was beginning to suspect that what it had been ordered to carry was not freight at all.

It was, he finally admitted to the dark, beginning to feel exactly like a verdict — sealed, dated, and addressed to someone who did not yet know they were guilty.

He slept at last, badly, and dreamed of lanterns burning in windows of houses that were no longer there.
