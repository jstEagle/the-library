## 4. A Town That Buries Its Bells

Tam Voss had a theory about villages that went quiet.

He had learned it the hard way, in the first winter of his apprenticeship, when he'd ridden into a hamlet on the eastern road expecting bread and a warm stable and found instead a place where every door stood open to the snow and nobody answered when he called. He was eleven then, and he had walked from house to house with his hood pulled up and his heart going like a rabbit's, until an old woman had finally emerged from a root cellar and told him, not unkindly, that when soldiers passed through twice in one month, people learned to become furniture. They sat still. They put out their cookfires. They waited for the world to be over.

The village of Sern Kolvek was like that, only worse, because it had already decided there was nothing left worth taking and had buried what remained.

Tam saw it before he understood it — the way you see a face that's been crying and don't yet know why. The houses along the main street were intact, roofs whole, shutters hanging square. But the well had no rope. The church had no bell. Where the bell should have hung, in the open arch above the door, there was only a pale rectangle of stone, protected from decades of weather, shaped exactly like the absence of a bell. Even the signposts had been taken down; the crossroads at the village's edge stood naked, four roads leaving in four directions with nothing to say about where any of them went.

"They took the bells down themselves," Tam said, because Casper Okonkwo had ridden past the church without seeming to look at it, and because Tam had discovered over three days on the road that saying things plainly was the only way to make this man react.

Casper reined up and looked. He had a way of looking at things — slow, complete, as if he were reading a dispatch written across whatever stood in front of him. "Why would they do that?"

"So nobody rings them." Tam shrugged, uncomfortable under that attention. "If there's no bell, nobody can call an alarm, and nobody can call the town together either. No meetings, no mustering, nothing the occupation could point at." He hesitated. "It's not always soldiers who take them down. Sometimes it's the priest. Sometimes it's the whole town, at night, with ropes and sledges, and they bury the bell in a field and everyone swears they'll dig it up after."

"After what?"

"That's the thing." Tam nudged his mare forward. "Nobody ever says after *what*. I think because if you say it out loud, you have to admit you know how long it might be."

Casper didn't answer right away. Behind them the pale rectangle of stone slid out of sight behind a leaning chimney, and Tam watched it go and thought: *that's what the Republic looks like now. A shape where something used to hang.*

They found the inn by its smell rather than its sign — woodsmoke, and under it the sour-sweetness of turnips boiled too long. The innkeeper was a broad woman with flour in her eyebrows who took their coin without counting it, which Tam knew from experience meant she was charging them double and didn't want witnesses to the arithmetic. There were two other travelers, both asleep by the fire with their boots still on, and a dog that raised its head, considered barking, and decided against it.

"We take the room at the back," Casper said quietly. "Ground floor. Window facing the stable."

"You think someone's following us?" Tam asked, once they'd settled.

"I think it's wise to know what's behind you even when nothing is." Casper set his saddlebag against the wall, on the side away from the door, and Tam noticed — not for the first time — that he did it without appearing to decide anything at all. The man moved like water finding the low ground. Three days of riding beside him and Tam had not seen him check a road, adjust a girth, or count a coin without making it look like habit rather than caution.

That was the problem with Casper Okonkwo, Tam was discovering. Everything about him looked like habit. Which meant you couldn't tell which parts were performance.

*Which is exactly what they sent you to find out,* said the voice in the back of his skull, the one that sounded uncomfortably like Captain Rehn. *You're not his apprentice. You're his mirror.*

* * *

They ate turnip soup and black bread at a corner table, and Casper asked questions the way he always did — sideways, gentle, so that answering felt like conversation rather than interrogation.

"Where did you train? Before the chancery took you?"

"Keldermark. The orphan depot." Tam tore his bread instead of cutting it. "My mother died in the second quarantine year. Father before that, in the dock strikes. There's a fund — Republic Orphans of Service Families. It sounds better than it is. They teach you letters, riding, codes. The ones with good hands go to clerking." He held up his own hands, small and quick and scarred at the knuckles. "Mine were judged good enough for the saddle instead."

"And you wanted that?"

Tam almost laughed. "Does anyone *want* anything at twelve? They asked if I liked horses. I said yes. That was the whole examination, near enough." He caught himself, hearing the bitterness come out sharper than he'd intended, and busied his mouth with soup.

But Casper was watching him with that unhurried attention, and something in Tam — some part he hadn't meant to expose — kept talking.

"It's not the worst life," he said. "You're moving all the time. Nobody's master owns your days. When you carry sealed dispatches, people are kind to you — kinder than they've ever been to anyone in a uniform — because everyone understands that whatever's in the bag matters more than whatever's outside it. For a while, being a courier means mattering." He stopped. The fire cracked. One of the sleeping travelers rolled over and muttered at his dreams.

"And then?" Casper said softly.

And then, Tam thought, you learn that mattering isn't the same as meaning. And then you get assigned, at fifteen, to ride north with the best courier in the service — the man whose name was spoken with something like reverence even in the orphan depots, Casper Okonkwo who had carried dispatches through the Halven winter, through three blockades, through the whole slow collapsing of everything — and you are told, by a captain with gray eyes that never blink quite when your eyes do:

*Your orders are simple. Ride with him. Watch him. If he opens any dispatch before delivery, if he deviates from the route, if he makes contact with anyone from his past — you send word. Nothing more. Do not warn him. Do not follow him into anything. He is being weighed, and you are the scale.*

She had said it in a room with no windows, and then she had said the other thing, the thing Tam turned over now at night like a stone with a sharp edge: *Courier Okonkwo has served the Republic faithfully for many years. This assignment is a formality. But formalities exist because sometimes they are not formalities.*

"And then you grow up," Tam said aloud, and smiled, and hated how easily it came. "And then you stop asking."

Casper let the silence sit between them for a long moment. Then he leaned back and said, conversational as weather, "Who gave you your secondary orders?"

The spoon stopped halfway to Tam's mouth.

He recovered — he was proud of how fast he recovered, only a heartbeat, only a slight too-careful setting-down of the spoon — but he saw in Casper's face that the heartbeat had been enough. The older courier wasn't looking at him accusingly. He was looking at him the way he'd looked at the church bell's absence: reading the shape of the thing missing.

"I don't—" Tam began.

"Don't bother deciding whether to lie," Casper said. "You have a liar's face, Tam. It's a compliment — mine's better. We'd both waste the evening pretending." He turned his cup slowly on the table. "I'm not angry. I'd have done the same in your place, and I *did*, at your age, twice. Once watching a quartermaster in Drevny, once watching a senator's wife. You ride with a man, you smile, you memorize. It's the oldest trade in the service."

The fire popped. Tam's heart was going hard enough that he was sure it showed in his throat.

"If you report me," Casper said, still mild, "for whatever you think you'll see — remember one thing. Ask yourself who wrote your instructions, and whether the person who wrote them has ever once, in your experience, told the whole truth to anyone she gave them to."

"You know it's Captain Rehn." It came out flat, defeated.

"I assumed. She likes orphans. She says we're the only ones the Republic made honestly." Casper's mouth did something that was nearly a smile and mostly grief. "Eat your soup, Tam. It's getting cold, and tomorrow the road goes through marshland, and cold soup tonight is better than regretting breakfast."

Tam ate. He was surprised to find he was hungry. Fear, apparently, agreed with him.

* * *

Later, with the inn dark and Casper's breathing gone long and even across the room, Tam lay staring at the ceiling beams and conducted his private accounting, the one he performed every night now, the ledger no dispatcher had ever taught him to keep.

On one side: his orders. Simple, clean, written in another hand for another boy. *Watch. Report. Nothing more.* On the other side: three days of a man sharing his fire, teaching him the old relay calls, correcting his rein-hands without mockery, saving him the last of the dried plums twice without comment. Three days of watching Casper Okonkwo guard those saddlebags like a mother wolf and never once, not for a single hour, behave like a man with anything to hide.

Except that men with nothing to hide didn't ask about secondary orders.

Unless they simply expected them. Unless anyone good enough to survive ten years in the service slept with one ear open for exactly that question, and Casper's gentleness these three days had itself been the performance, and Tam had been read like a cheap pamphlet from the first mile.

He turned over. Through the window, the stable yard lay silvered under a half-clouded moon, and beyond it the fields where, somewhere, a bell lay buried in the dirt with its mouth full of earth, waiting for an *after* that its own town had refused to name.

*Two liars by one fire,* he thought. *And neither of us knows yet which of us is lying about what.*

He must have slept, because he woke to gray light and the sound of hooves in the yard — not theirs; the mare's ears told him that much — and to Casper already dressed, standing at the window with the curtain pulled back just the width of two fingers.

"Company?" Tam whispered.

"No." Casper's voice was strange. Not afraid. Something worse: puzzled. "A rider from the north. Chancery colors."

Tam was beside him at the window before he'd decided to move, propriety abandoned, cover abandoned, everything abandoned except the need to see. In the yard below, a mud-flecked rider was handing something down to the innkeeper's husband — a paper, sealed, the wax catching the early light dull red like old blood. The man read it, went white, and stood very still in the manner of the furniture-people, the waiting-people, the bell-burying people.

Then he looked up — directly at their window — and Tam realized with a lurch that the rider was pointing at it, and speaking, and that the words, muffled through glass, were unmistakably *the couriers*.

Casper's hand closed on Tam's shoulder, steady, but Tam could feel the fine tremor in it, and hear him say, almost to himself, in the tone of a man doing arithmetic he already dreads:

"The relay station at Ostrow is a day ahead of us. Whoever carries news faster than a courier—"

He didn't finish. Below, the innkeeper's husband began walking toward their door.
