## 5. The Seal That Shouldn't Open

The relay station at Vosk Bridge had once been a monastery, and it still behaved like one. The walls were thick enough to swallow sound, the corridors smelled of tallow and cold stone, and the brothers who kept it — or the men who wore their robes now that the order had scattered — moved through the rooms with the hushed economy of people who had long ago stopped expecting anything from the world except silence.

Casper liked it for exactly those reasons. A courier learned to love buildings that kept secrets.

He and Tam arrived an hour past dusk, riding through a drizzle that had followed them down from the high moor like something with an opinion about them. The stable was half-full — a merchant's wagons, a government mule team bound south with their drivers looking over their shoulders too often to be merely tired — and Casper chose stalls at the far end without comment. Old habit. You never tied your horse next to a man whose eyes were doing arithmetic.

Tam unsaddled in silence, which was itself becoming a kind of language between them. Four days on the road north, and the boy had not yet asked a single question he could answer himself. He watched instead. He counted. Casper recognized the technique because he had invented half of it: the way Tam noted which side of the road they rode, how many riders passed them going south, whether Casper checked the dispatch case before sleeping or after. It was competent work. It was also, unmistakably, surveillance.

He let it be. There was no point resenting a boy for obeying orders Casper himself would have given.

* * *

The refectory served barley soup, black bread, and a cheese so old it had developed ambitions. Casper ate slowly, his back to the wall, and listened.

What he heard was weather of the human kind. Two clerks from the southern circuit complaining that the pay office had gone three weeks silent. A wool factor who'd abandoned his warehouse in Kesselvane because "the garrison stopped being a garrison, if you take my meaning." A pair of pilgrims — or men dressed as pilgrims, though their boots were army boots and their piety was of the sort that required no walking — who spoke softly and left before the bread came.

The republic was not collapsing the way a building collapses, all at once and with a great noise. It was collapsing the way snow leaves a field: invisibly, everywhere at once, until one morning you looked out and could no longer say where it had gone.

"You're not eating," Casper said.

Tam looked up. In the lamplight he seemed younger than he claimed, which Casper suspected was the truth of it. "I ate."

"Eat again. Cold roads lie about how much you've had." He pushed his own bowl across. It wasn't entirely generosity; a courier who fainted at a river crossing was a courier who lost his cargo, and Casper had buried enough colleagues to know that most of them drowned in small ways first. "And then sleep. We ride at first light."

"Where to?"

"Halven."

Something crossed the boy's face — quick as a fish under ice, there and gone. "That's off the main line."

"It's on mine." Casper stood, gathered the dispatch case, and felt Tam's eyes follow it the way they always did: hungry, careful, pretending otherwise. "Sleep, Tam."

* * *

His room was a monk's cell, whitewashed, windowless but for a slit facing the river. A cot, a stool, a candle stub. Luxury, by the standards of the last month.

Casper set the dispatch case on the stool and sat across from it, as he did every night, and did what he had done every night since leaving the capital, which was nothing at all.

Three sealed dispatches. That was the whole of his commission, and the whole of its mystery. The first had been opened by him within sight of the chancery steps — standard practice, a courier verified the seals before accepting custody, and he had turned each packet in his hands under the clerk's bored gaze, confirming wax, ribbon, and docket number. The second and third he had examined the same way. All three had been correct. All three had been wrong in some way he couldn't yet name, the way a portrait can be technically perfect and still look at you strangely.

He took out the second dispatch now.

It was heavier than the others. Not dramatically — a few sheets of good rag paper, folded twice, wrapped in oilskin against rain. But weight in a dispatch was information, and Casper had spent eleven years learning to read it. This one carried a second enclosure inside the outer fold, or paper thick enough to pretend to. And the wax—

He held it to the candle.

The chancery seal of Vessany: a tower above crossed keys, pressed in the deep vermilion reserved for instruments of state. It was flawless. The keys were crisp. The tower's crenellations were sharp. The matrix had been pressed with even heat and confident pressure, and around the edge the wax had feathered into that particular starburst that only came from a seal applied while the document lay flat on a proper table.

Casper turned it over.

There, on the reverse of the fold, where the wax had bled through the paper — as wax does, as every courier knew it must — the impression was wrong.

Not absent. Wrong. On a genuine chancery seal, the bleed-through mirrored the face: keys reversed, tower reversed, the whole ghost-image inverted like a reflection in dark water. This one showed only a smear. A bloom of red with no structure in it, as though the wax had been pressed hard on top of the paper and the paper had refused to carry the image through.

He had seen this before exactly once, seven years ago, in a border town where a man had tried to pass forged requisition papers to buy powder for men who were not soldiers. Casper had been nineteen and full of doctrine, and he had reported it, and the man had been shot against the granary wall on a Tuesday morning while Casper stood witness because witnesses were part of procedure.

The memory sat down beside him like an old dog.

"No," he said aloud, to the cell, to the candle. "No. The chancery doesn't—"

But the chancery was three hundred miles south, and the republic it served was melting like snow, and here was a document bearing its perfect vermilion face and hollow heart, and here was Casper Okonkwo holding it in hands that had begun, very slightly, to shake.

* * *

There were ways to test a seal that didn't break it. Every courier learned three or four; the honest ones used them to catch bandits' clumsy copies, and the dishonest ones used them to make better forgeries, and the difference between the two groups was mostly a matter of which oath they'd taken and when.

Casper laid the dispatch flat on the stool and worked by feel, eyes half-closed, the way you read a coin in the dark.

The wax was right. That was the devil of it. Its color matched the chancery stock exactly — that deep vermilion with the faint brown undertone, the shade the Treasury guarded like scripture. Its smell, when he warmed it gently between his palms, was beeswax and resin and the ghost of the sealing room's smoke. Whoever had made this had real matrix access, or had taken a cast from a genuine impression, and either possibility was worse than simple fraud. Forgers stole faces. Casts required custody.

Then there was the paper.

He unfolded the outer wrap — carefully, along existing creases, disturbing nothing — and held the inner document near the flame without letting it near enough to matter. Good rag paper from the southern mills had a certain hand: dense, cool, faintly ribbed when tilted to light. This paper had the ribs. This paper had the hand.

This paper also had age.

Not dramatic age — no foxing, no water stains. But the fibers had relaxed the way paper relaxes after months in a file drawer, compressed and released, compressed and released. The folds had been made, pressed flat, and re-folded at least once. He knew this because on the second crease there was a hairline wear-mark, a pale thread of broken sizing, exactly where a fold wears when a document has been opened and closed and stored and handled.

A dispatch written this month, in the capital, and sealed this month, did not have month-old wear on its folds.

A dispatch written months ago did.

Casper sat very still. Outside, the river went about its business under the bridge, patient and indifferent, and somewhere below a horse stamped in the straw, and the world continued to behave as though it were sane.

Someone had written this document long before the events it described. Someone had sealed it, filed it, aged it in a drawer — and then, when the moment came, had brought it out and applied fresh wax to old paper. Pre-written history. An order that predated its own cause the way a gravestone predates the death it reports.

He thought: *the seal is a month old.*

He thought it again, precisely, because precision was all he had left: *the wax is a month old at most — fresh, soft-edged, applied recently. The paper is older than the wax by seasons.* And a courier's arithmetic was simple and terrible: documents were dated by their seals. Whatever this paper said, it said with the authority of this month, and it had been waiting in the dark, finished, for however long it took events to grow into it.

You don't pre-write history unless you already know how it ends.

Unless you're the one writing it.

* * *

He didn't remember deciding to open it. That frightened him more than anything else — more than the smear on the back of the wax, more than the worn fold. Eleven years of doctrine lived in his spine: *a courier delivers, a courier protects, a courier does not read.* He had carried orders that sent men to die and had slept soundly afterward because the sleeping was not his to do. The seal was the whole architecture of trust. Break it and everything above it comes down.

His thumbnail rested against the fold anyway.

*It's not your mail,* said the voice of Master Corvin, dead these six years, who had taught him the road codes and the folding patterns and the rule beneath all rules: *what's inside a dispatch can't hurt you, boy. Only carrying it can.*

*Whose mail is it, then?* Casper answered the dead man silently. *Mine? I'm the one it will kill if I deliver it badly. Or theirs — whoever wrote it in advance, waiting for the world to catch up?*

The candle guttered. He trimmed the wick with fingers that were almost steady.

Here was the thing he could not stop circling, the thought with teeth: the first dispatch had weighed wrong too. He'd told himself it was nothing — uneven paper, damp absorbed on the road. But wrong was wrong, and now wrong had a shape. Three dispatches, carried north by the republic's most reliable courier, into a country coming apart. What did you send ahead into chaos? Instructions. Contingencies. Lists of names.

And what names mattered, in the final winter of Vessany?

He found his thumb had moved without permission, finding the seam of the fold where the wax met paper, and he made himself stop, made himself breathe, made himself put the dispatch back into its oilskin with hands that wanted very badly to disobey him.

"Not tonight," he whispered. "Not like this."

Because there was a right way and a wrong way, even now. If he broke the seal in a monastery cell with an apprentice spy sleeping twenty feet away, he was a traitor with evidence and no plan. He'd be arrested before dawn — or worse, quietly, and the dispatch delivered by someone else, someone who wouldn't notice worn folds and hollow wax.

If he was going to damn himself, he would at least do it with the door barred and the candle low and a reason worth the price.

* * *

He did not sleep. He sat on the cot with his back to the cold stone and watched the candle burn down, and the cell filled with shadows that arranged themselves into memories he hadn't invited.

Halven. Seven years gone. The lantern workshop with its windows like honeycomb, every pane glowing, the whole street smelling of beeswax and hot brass. Old Veren with his burned forearms and his endless patience, teaching a feverish young courier to solder a seam while snow buried the roads. Mira on the stairs, sixteen and unimpressed, correcting her father's pronunciation of a foreign word and then laughing when Casper got it wronger.

*I'll come back,* he had sworn, with the solemn stupidity of the wounded and grateful. *When there's peace. I'll bring news.*

*Bring yourself,* Veren had said. *News keeps.*

He had meant it. He had meant every word, and the road north was carrying him home at last, and the republic he served had handed him sealed paper addressed, in every way that mattered, toward that town — and the paper was older than its own seal.

*What did you write about us?* he asked the oilskin packet in the dark. *Who did you write us to be?*

The candle died. In the black, the dispatch case on the stool was a small square of deeper darkness, patient as everything else in the monastery, and Casper understood, with the calm of a man watching ice crack under his feet, that whatever was in that second dispatch had been decided long ago by someone who had never met him, never met the Verens, never stood in a warm workshop in the snow — someone for whom the whole living world was just so much paper, to be folded, sealed, and filed in advance.

Tomorrow they rode for Halven.

Tonight, for the first time in eleven years, Casper Okonkwo was afraid of the mail.
