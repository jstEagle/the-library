## 6. Captain Rehn

Captain Ilsa Rehn had learned long ago that a road told you more about a man than his file ever could.

Casper Okonkwo's file was thick — eleven years of service, forty-one confirmed deliveries, two commendations and one formal reprimand for insubordination that she herself had written and then quietly buried. She had read it so many times the pages had gone soft at the folds, and it had never once told her the thing she needed to know now, riding north three days behind him: which way would he break?

The road knew. The road said he was traveling fast but not fleeing, buying horses with exact change, sleeping four hours exactly, tipping stable boys in copper because he'd always believed small kindnesses were cheaper than large suspicions. She read his passage the way other officers read dispatches — in the posture of innkeepers, the nervousness of ferry men, the single sentence scrawled in a relay ledger in his unmistakable hand: *Roads north poor. Traveling light.* He was telling anyone who checked that he could be caught. That was Casper. Even his lies were honest about something.

She rode alone. That was not sentiment; it was doctrine. Three riders drew eyes, one rider drew none, and the republic — what remained of it, which was mostly paperwork and habit — had taught her everything worth knowing about being unseen.

* * *

The orders she carried were verbal, as the worst orders always were.

Deputy Director Voss had summoned her to a room without windows in the Chancery annex, back when the Chancery still pretended it would survive the winter. He had been packing even then, she'd noticed. A man does not pack before delivering orders to a mission he expects to succeed. But Voss packed the way some men prayed — ritually, without expectation of answer — and he had spoken the words in his dry, inventorying voice:

"The courier Okonkwo carries three dispatches of the highest sensitivity to Halven and beyond. Upon confirmation of delivery, the courier is to be retired from service. So is the boy."

"Retired," Rehn had repeated, because there was a ritual on her side too, and it was to make them say it plainly.

"Killed, Captain. Cleanly, if convenient. The dispatches concern persons whose names must not circulate. A courier who has read nothing can still describe a seal, a weight, a route. In the present circumstances" — Voss had gestured at the windowless wall, meaning the war, the collapse, the whole rotting edifice of Vessany sliding off its foundations — "circumstances being what they are, we cannot audit what he knows. So we remove the archive."

*Remove the archive.* Twenty-six years in the service and she still marveled at the language, how it could make murder sound like filing. She had saluted. She always saluted. Her arm had done it without consulting her, the way a heart beats without permission.

"And Tam?" she had asked. "The apprentice is fourteen."

"The apprentice was assigned as an observer. Observers observe. If Okonkwo deviates, the boy reports it, and then the boy is also removed. One grave is cheaper than two." Voss had looked up from his ledgers then, and for a moment something almost human had crossed his face — or she had imagined it, which amounted to the same thing. "You trained him, didn't you? Okonkwo."

"I trained him."

"That will make it easier or harder. I've found it's usually both."

* * *

She caught his trail at the third relay station out of the capital, and there she found the first wrong thing.

The station master, a woman with a burn scar down one forearm and the flat stare of someone who had stopped believing in the republic sometime around the second harvest failure, mentioned it without being asked — because Rehn asked questions the way Casper paid tips: small, constant, disarming.

"Your colleague," the woman said, "he stood at the dispatch case a long time."

"Couriers do."

"He held the second pouch. Weighed it in his hand like fruit." She shrugged. "Then he bought coffee he didn't drink and rode on."

Rehn thanked her and did not write any of it down, because writing things down was how archives got removed. But she turned the detail over through a long gray afternoon of riding: *weighed it like fruit*. Casper had a feeler's hands. Eleven years of seals and papers had given him an instinct for weight the way a midwife had an instinct for trouble, and if he had paused over the second dispatch —

Well. Either the dispatch was heavier than its cover story, or Casper was already becoming the kind of problem Voss had predicted, and both possibilities meant the same thing for her orders.

She found she was angry at him, which surprised her. Not at the situation, not at Voss, at *him* — for weighing the pouch, for making himself interesting, for refusing, even now, to be simply the man she had made him. It was the anger you feel at a student who has outgrown the syllabus and doesn't know to be careful about showing it.

Or it was the other kind of anger. She declined to examine which.

* * *

She had recruited him herself, eleven years ago, out of a flood season and a border skirmish and a boy of nineteen who had carried a wounded sergeant nine miles on his back and then, when the sergeant died anyway, sat down beside the body and organized the dead man's kit so it could be returned intact to his widow. She had watched him do that — the carrying and the sitting and the folding of another man's belongings with such terrible care — and thought: *there. That is the whole of the craft in one person. He can bear weight, and he respects what belongs to others.*

She had never told him that. In the service you did not tell people why you chose them; it gave them ideas about mattering.

For six years she had been his handler. She had taught him the cipher wheels and the tap codes, the three-taps-on-glass signal for safe harbor that older couriers still used from the days when lanterns hung in windows along the northern roads. She had taught him how to sleep in a chair with his boots on and wake at a name. How to eat a full meal while memorizing a face across a room. How to lie with his whole posture and tell truth only with objects — coins, charms, the little brass lantern he kept on his watch chain, though she had never asked him about that and he had never offered. Some things you left unexamined in a courier the way you left a load-bearing wall unexamined in a house.

He had been her best. Not her most obedient — that had been a boy named Drese, long since dead of obedience — but her best. When the reprimand came, five years back, for refusing to abandon a wounded fellow courier in the eastern marshes, Rehn had written the necessary condemnation in language so bloodless that no one reading it could have believed it, including the man who signed it. Then she had assigned Casper the next good route, which was how she apologized, and how he forgave, neither of them ever saying a word about any of it. They had built a whole friendship out of things unsaid. It was, she sometimes thought, the only honest architecture the republic allowed.

And now she was following him north to put a bullet in the architecture.

* * *

At dusk she came down into the valley called Sennet Hollow, where the village had buried its own bells the year the looters came — she'd heard the story, everyone on this road heard the story, of a town that dismantled its church and sank the bronze in the marsh rather than let it be melted for someone else's war. There was firelight ahead, two figures at a small camp pitched badly on purpose, in a hollow out of the wind, and she reined in on the ridge and watched them through the long lenses of ordinary dark.

Casper sat with his back to a stone, hat low. Across the flames the boy — Tam, all elbows and borrowed confidence, fourteen going on expendable — was talking with his hands. Casper was listening the way he listened to everyone, which was completely, which was precisely the flaw in him. He gave even children the dignity of his full attention. It would kill him someday. She had assumed for years that it would kill him in some abstract future; now she had orders saying otherwise.

She watched the boy hand him something — food, probably hoarded from the last village — and watched Casper take it with that grave nod of his, and felt something in her chest do a thing she refused to name. Affection, if pressed, though she would have denied it under oath. She had attended enough of her own feelings' funerals to be quick about this one.

*They look like a teacher and a student,* she thought. *Which is what they are. Which is what we were.*

She pulled back from the ridge. There was no point closing tonight. Delivery had not been confirmed, and her orders were specific in their cruelty: *upon confirmation of delivery.* Until Casper reached Halven and put those dispatches into whatever hands awaited them, he was still useful, still the instrument. Afterward he was the archive. She was content to let the road carry him a while longer. Content. She tested the word in her mouth and found it false, and set it down.

What she actually was, riding down out of the hills toward the buried bells, was early.

* * *

In the tenth year of Casper's service — the ninth, the eighth, she lost track; the years had begun stacking like unread files — there had been an evening in the capital, after a delivery well made, when the two of them had drunk the ceremonial glass the service permitted and Casper, who almost never spoke of anything that wasn't a route, had spoken.

"Halven," he said. "There's a family there. Lantern-makers. The Verens."

"I know the town."

"You don't know them." He turned his glass, and the lamplight moved in it. "I wintered there once. Wounded. They hid me the whole season and never asked what I carried, and the daughter—" He stopped, started again. "I made them a promise. That I'd come back when there was peace, and bring news, and sit at their table as a man with nothing hidden. Do you know what that's worth? To have one table where you're just a person?"

Rehn had looked at him for a long moment. Everything in her training said: *log this. A courier with a table where he's just a person is a courier with a lever sticking out of him.* Everything in her training, and yet.

"It's worth exactly what you'll pay to keep it," she said. "That's what promises are denominated in."

He had smiled at that — the rare smile, the real one, the one that reached all the way up and made him look nineteen again. "You should try it sometime, Captain. Being a person somewhere."

"I am a person everywhere," she had said. "That's the trouble."

She had logged nothing. And now, years later, riding a dark road toward the town where that promise pointed like a compass needle, she understood that she had known even then — had known and filed it in the place where she kept the things that would eventually be required of her. Somewhere in her there had always been a drawer labeled *Halven*, and somewhere in the Chancery there was a man named Voss, and it was the nature of the service that the two drawers would one day be opened by the same hand.

*Upon confirmation of delivery.*

She wondered — professionally, coldly, the way she wondered about everything — whether Casper would open the dispatches first. She hoped not. Hoping was also denominated in payment; hope cost more than most. A courier who delivered sealed orders unopened died clean. A courier who opened them died knowing, and knowing was the part she had spent twenty-six years sparing people where she could.

But she had seen him weigh the pouch like fruit.

She thought of him opening it. She thought of his face doing what faces did — the small collapse, the rearrangement around a fact. She had watched that rearrangement perhaps a hundred times in her career, in interrogation rooms and briefing rooms and once, terribly, in a mirror. She found she did not want to watch Casper's.

*That will make it easier or harder,* Voss had said. *Usually both.*

Voss was right about almost nothing else, but he had been right about that.

* * *

Near midnight she reached the edge of Sennet Hollow itself and saw, in the last coals of the distant camp, a small glow that was not fire. Two points of it, low, moving — the boy holding something up to the embers, showing Casper. Metal, catching light. She raised her field glasses, though the distance ate detail, and caught only the shape of it: small, round, hanging from a chain.

A charm. A lantern.

Rehn lowered the glasses slowly. Twenty-six years, and the service had taught her to read documents, codes, faces, weather. It had never once taught her what to do with an omen, and so she did what she always did with the unclassifiable: she noted it, and moved on.

She made cold camp in the tree line above the hollow, boiled nothing, ate dried bread, and lay with her coat as her only blanket, watching the stars do their indifferent bookkeeping over a country that had maybe four months left in it. Below, the two couriers slept — the man who had been her best and the boy who was nobody's yet — and between them and her lay three sealed dispatches, a verbal order, and the entire question of what Ilsa Rehn was, now that the state that defined her was coming apart faster than she could define it.

She had killed couriers before. Two, in the border troubles, both strangers, both quickly grieved and longer forgotten. This one would not be a stranger. This one had sat at her own table in the capital a hundred times, had learned to sleep in chairs with his boots on because she taught him, had once handed her his whole heart in a wineglass and called it Halven.

*I'm sorry,* she thought, toward the coals below, in the direction of a sleeping man. It was the first apology she had issued in twenty years and she issued it silently, internally, in the strictest privacy, because duty was duty but she had never been a savage.

*You taught me routes,* she imagined him answering, drowsy and ironic, the way he got when he was safe. *You never taught me to die quietly.*

"No," she murmured to the dark. "I never did."

Sleep took her near dawn, dreamless, professional. She woke at first light with frost on her coat and the certainty already formed, hard as a horseshoe, that she would ride into Halven and find the delivery made and do the thing she had been ordered to do, cleanly, if convenient, and carry it afterward the way she carried everything.

She was tightening her horse's girth when she saw, far down the valley road, a single rider moving fast — not one of hers, not one of theirs, some other thread in the weave — and then, worse, saw the dust of a larger party behind it. Soldiers on the north road, moving toward Halven. Toward the town. Toward whatever the second dispatch had already set in motion.

Rehn stood very still in the cold morning, one hand on the saddle, and understood with perfect clarity that the schedule had changed. Someone had moved the timetable forward. Whatever the dispatches contained, it was already happening — and Casper Okonkwo, three hours ahead of her and riding straight into it, had no idea at all.
