## 7. Lantern Smoke

The road into Halven smelled of woodsmoke and wet iron, and Casper knew before he crested the last hill that something had gone wrong with the town the way a man knows a letter is bad news before he breaks the seal — by its weight, its silence, the way it sits in the hand.

Seven years ago that same hill had offered him Halven whole: a scatter of slate roofs along the river bend, the lantern-makers' quarter glowing at dusk like a constellation brought down to earth, every window in the Veren workshop district burning soft amber against the coming dark. It had been winter then too, and he had been bleeding through his boot, half-frozen, half-dead, and the town had looked to him like salvation does to drowning men — impossibly bright, impossibly far, and then suddenly close enough to touch.

Now he reined the horse to a halt beneath a bare alder and counted what was wrong.

No smoke from chimneys, though it was cold enough that even the poorest house should be burning something. No carts on the north road. No children — and there had always been children in Halven, running the towpaths, daring each other near the millrace, their voices carrying up the hill like birdsong. No bells. The chapel bell had rung the hours when he'd lived here; he remembered being woken by it his first morning, furious and feverish, and old Aldous Veren laughing at him over breakfast: *That bell has been wrong since my grandfather's day, courier, but we keep ringing it out of stubbornness.*

The bell tower stood empty now. Not silent — empty. Whatever they'd done with the bell, they'd done it deliberately.

And above the town, on the ridge where once there had been only a shepherd's hut, flew a banner he did not recognize: black field, white chevron, the mark of one of the provisional garrisons that had sprouted across Vessany's northern counties like mold on bread. Not republican colors. Not quite rebel colors either. The colors of men paid to hold ground for whoever paid them next.

Casper sat with this for a long moment, letting the horse blow and stamp, and did the arithmetic he had been doing since the relay station three days ago. Two dispatches left. One sealed so tightly it might as well have been welded shut. One whose chancery seal predated its own paper. And at the bottom of the northern road, in the town below him, the family to whom he owed a winter of his life.

*Deliveries first,* said the voice of his training, Captain Rehn's voice, cool as well-water. *Feelings after.*

But Rehn wasn't here, and feelings were all he had, and so he did what no proper courier would do: he turned off the road.

* * *

He came down into Halven the back way, along the drainage cut behind the tannery where the mud froze in ridges hard as teeth, leading the horse because the path would not bear its weight. He wore his courier's grey under a drover's coat bought two towns back, the dispatch satchel redistributed into an ordinary grain sack slung across his shoulder, and if anyone stopped him he was a drover named Marten Kovic out of Lowbridge, illiterate, incurious, paid to move horses and nothing else. He had worn the lie comfortably enough on the road. Wearing it inside Halven felt different. It felt like wearing a mask made of his own face.

The lower streets were occupied in the way towns are occupied rather than destroyed: doors shut but not broken, shutters closed but not nailed, a market square swept bare except for a knot of soldiers around a brazier who watched him pass with the flat professional attention of men paid to watch everything. There were perhaps thirty of them visible. Enough to hold a town this size. Not enough to have taken it without help.

He passed the chandlery where Mira Veren used to buy beeswax by the hundredweight, haggling with the chandler until the man surrendered just to make her stop, and found the door boarded shut with fresh planks. Passed the schoolhouse, boarded. Passed the house of the widow Anselm, who had fed him soup his second week in hiding and asked nothing about the blood on his trousers, and saw that hers alone remained open — and standing in her doorway, arms folded, watching him with eyes that went from his face to the grain sack and back again with terrible precision, was the widow herself, older, thinner, and unmistakably alive.

He should have walked past. Every instinct trained into him across eleven years of service said walk past, you are a drover, drovers do not know widows in occupied towns. But she gave the smallest shake of her head — not *no*, he realized, reading it correctly a half-second too late, but *not here* — and tilted her chin toward the alley beside her house, and Casper, cursing himself in three languages, walked past like a stranger and doubled back through the alley like a fool.

She let him in through the kitchen and threw the bolt behind him.

"Marten Kovic," she said, looking him up and down. "You were never much good at faces. You still walk like a man counting exits."

"Frau Anselm—"

"Hush." She put a finger to her lips, then pressed a cup of something hot and bitter into his hands — chicory, mostly, with maybe a ghost of real coffee hoarded against exactly this kind of morning — and listened at the window for a full minute before she spoke again. "Thirty soldiers, maybe forty. They came six weeks ago. Before that it was the county militia, and before that nobody, and I will tell you now what everyone in this town knows and no one in this town says aloud."

"The Verens."

Her face did something complicated — grief and fear and a fierce old anger all trying to occupy the same features at once. "Boarded up since the first snow. Soldiers came at night, official-looking ones, with papers. Said there was evidence the family had aided enemy agents. Spies, they said." Her mouth twisted around the word. "*Spies.* In a house where the daughter can't lie to save her life and the father hasn't left his workshop for anything but church in twenty years."

"They took them?"

"That's the thing." She lowered her voice further, though the kitchen was already quiet enough to hear the frost forming on the glass. "Nobody saw them taken. The workshop was boarded from outside — boards over the doors, boards over the windows, wax seals on the boards like it's evidence in a trial. But Aldous Veren built that house, courier. Built it and rebuilt it twice, and there are ways in and out of it that only a lantern-maker would think of, because a man who works with fire thinks about ways out of burning buildings." She looked at him then, long and level, and Casper felt the ground shift under him the way it shifts when someone says your own secret back to you in a stranger's voice. "I'm an old woman and I mind my own business, and my business is that I'm low on flour and high on curiosity, and neither of those things concerns a drover passing through."

"No," Casper said carefully. "Of course not."

"But if such a drover were to go look at the boarded-up workshop," she continued serenely, taking her cup back and rinsing it in the basin as though he were already gone, "he might notice that the seal on the back board is broken and reset, badly, by a soldier who didn't know wax. And he might notice that the snow against the cellar grate has been disturbed from the inside more than once. And he might remember — if he were the sort of man who remembers — that the Veren girl used to signal her father from the market with three little taps on the lamppost glass, three taps meaning *come home safe*, a game they played since she could walk. And if that drover waited until the changing of the watch, which happens at moonrise, and went round by the tannery cut..." She set the cup upside down to dry. "...he'd find the watch on that street is two boys more interested in the brazier than the street."

The chicory had gone cold in his hands. "Why are you telling me this?"

Frau Anselm dried her hands on her apron, slowly, and when she looked up her eyes were wet and furious at once. "Because seven years ago I fed a bleeding boy soup and asked nothing, same as the Verens took him in and asked nothing, and because whatever is written on those papers the soldiers carry, I knew Mira Veren when she cried over a broken birdwing lantern at eight years old, and I am telling you that family never spied on anyone in their lives." She straightened. "And because somebody boarded up the best people in this town and called it justice, and I am too old and too frightened to do anything about it but talk to drovers. Now get out of my kitchen before I regret my charity."

* * *

The changing of the watch came at moonrise, as promised, and the moon rose late and thin behind cloud, and Casper crouched in the shadow of the tannery's drying sheds with the grain sack heavy against his back and his heart going like a drum in a parade.

The Veren workshop had not changed its bones. Even boarded, even dark, he could read it the way he'd learned to read it that winter — the long gallery where the finished lanterns hung in rows like fruit, the glazing room with its south-facing windows, the kiln chimney standing up against the sky like a raised finger. The boards over the front door bore a paper seal, official, stamped with the black chevron. The boards over the back door bore a seal too — and Frau Anselm's eye had been good. The wax had been melted and reset, carelessly, the impression blurred, the work of a bored soldier following orders he didn't understand.

Casper crossed the frozen yard in the intervals between the sentries' turns toward their brazier, and knelt at the cellar grate, and saw that the frost on the iron had been smeared from within.

He did not knock. Knocking was for people with rights. Instead he did the thing his body remembered without asking his permission, the thing he'd watched Mira do a hundred times that winter from the workshop gallery, tapping her father's shoulder or the lamppost or the window glass: three taps, pause, three taps. *Come home safe.*

Silence. Long enough that doubt began its cold climb up his spine. Then — faint, from somewhere deep under the boards and the stone and the years — a scrape. A held breath. And then, muffled almost past hearing, three taps answered from the other side.

He worked the grate loose with his knife, and it came away easier than it should have, because it had been loosened already, oiled, maintained, kept ready by hands waiting on the far side of seven years and six weeks of occupation. He slid the grain sack through first, then himself, down into darkness that smelled of wax and lamp oil and cold earth, and had barely found his footing before small strong hands caught his arm and a voice he had carried across a republic like a coal carried in cupped palms whispered, shaking:

"You're late."

Mira Veren stood in the blackness close enough to touch, and she was thinner than memory, and older than twenty-four had any right to make a person, and there was gunpowder residue on her fingers and a bandage tied around her left forearm, and she was — he understood in one staggering instant — absolutely, completely real.

"You're supposed to be a story," she said, and her voice cracked on it. "Papa said I made you up. Seven years, Casper Okonkwo. Seven years and you come through the cellar grate smelling of horse."

"I came as fast as—" He stopped, because excuses were ash, and because her hand was gripping his sleeve now with a strength that had nothing gentle in it. "Is your father—"

"Wounded. A soldier's bayonet, the night they came. He walks with a stick and he shouldn't walk at all, but try telling him." She pulled him deeper into the cellar, past racks of unfinished lanterns wrapped in oilcloth — the whole inventory of a season's work, hidden like contraband in its own home — to the far corner where a single shuttered candle burned behind tin, and where an old man lay propped on sacks of sand, gray-faced, one leg splinted, watching the darkness with eyes that sharpened the moment Casper entered the candlelight.

Aldous Veren studied him for a long moment. Then, in the dry voice Casper remembered from a thousand mornings of porridge and bell-tower complaints: "You got tall. And you've lost the limp. Sit down before you fall down, boy, and tell me why my daughter has been guarding a hole in the wall like a fox at its den for six weeks, and what's in the sack, and don't say *nothing*, because couriers who smell of nothing don't come through cellar grates."

So Casper sat, in the cellar of the family the republic had named traitors in a dispatch whose seal lied about its age, and began — carefully, leaving things out, knowing even as he did it that he was lying to the only people in Vessany he had never lied to — to explain what had brought him home.

Above them, boots crossed the yard. Paused. Moved on.

Mira's hand found his in the dark and gripped it, and did not let go.
