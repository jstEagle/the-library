## 8. The Daughter in the Cellar

The lantern told the truth better than any newspaper, and Mira Veren had stopped believing newspapers sometime around the second week of the occupation.

It was a small thing, the lantern — a stub of tallow in a tin case her grandfather had soldered before the border wars, the kind of object the workshop would never have sold because it was too humble and too well-made, which is to say it was made the way her family made everything, as if someone's life might depend on it in the dark. The flame sat low and steady behind smoked glass, throwing a circle of amber no wider than a supper table across the cellar's low ceiling. Beyond that circle, the cellar went on into blackness where the pickles had once been and the winter apples and the great clay jars of lamp oil that were now nearly empty, and beyond all that, the old coal chute that ran up beneath the street like a throat.

Mira had learned to measure time by the oil. When the lantern burned at its lowest honest setting, a full jar lasted six days. She had three jars left when the soldiers boarded the workshop above her head, and she had counted them the way other people counted money or sins.

Her father lay on the pallet against the far wall where the foundations kept some of the ground's cold out, or perhaps only shared it more slowly. Tomas Veren was sixty-one years old and had built lamps for two embassies and a cathedral, and now he breathed in a particular shallow rhythm she had memorized the way she had once memorized the proportions of a good wick: one long breath, a pause too long, then two short ones, like a man climbing stairs he wasn't sure would hold him. The splint on his left leg was her own work, done by lamplight in the first terrible hour after the beam came down, and she did not let herself think about whether she had set the bone properly, because if she thought about it she would have to decide whether to un-splint it and try again, and her hands were not her father's hands, and they both knew it, and neither of them said it.

"Papa," she said softly, "the light's getting thin."

He opened his eyes. They found her the way they always did now, a half-second slow, swimming toward her through pain.

"Then trim it," he said. "You'll go through the oil like a government official."

She laughed — a real laugh, small and startled out of her, the first in what felt like weeks — and heard him take quiet satisfaction in having produced it. That was his way. Even flat on his back with his leg broken in two places and his workshop nailed shut above him, Tomas Veren managed his household from the pallet like a captain managing a ship from below decks, issuing opinions with such calm authority that disaster seemed merely poorly organized.

"There's bread," she said. "Half of it's gone green at one end, but the other end doesn't know yet."

"Optimist."

"Realist." She cut slices with the small knife she wore now on a cord at her waist, because a girl who had grown up in a house of fire and glass had learned early that you never moved through a workshop without a blade, though she had never expected to need it for rationing bread in a root cellar while soldiers walked the floorboards overhead.

Which they were doing even now. Boots, four of them, crossing and recrossing the kitchen above in the pattern she had come to know as *searching* rather than the pattern that meant *looting* or the one that meant nothing at all. The searching pattern was slower. It stopped at corners.

She put down the knife and looked up at the ceiling beams and waited, and her father watched her wait, and neither of them spoke, because the first rule of the cellar — established the night she'd carried him down it, half-conscious, while the town crier read the proclamation about spies — was that sound traveled up worse than it traveled down, but it still traveled.

The boots moved on. A door slammed somewhere in the front of the house that had been her childhood, and then there was only the wind coming off the hills through the broken gable window, and the small sound of the lantern, which was not really a sound at all but something you imagined when you had listened to a flame long enough.

*The family that helped spies.* That was what the crier had called them, standing in the square with the new flag hanging behind him like wet laundry. Mira had been in the crowd. That was the foolishness she could not forgive herself for — she had gone out for salt, of all things, salt and a bolt of canvas for awnings, ordinary errands on an ordinary morning, and she had stood in Halven's little square between the fountain and the baker's and heard her family's name spoken aloud with the word *treason* attached to it, in the flat administrative voice men use when the words are not theirs and the consequences will never be theirs either.

Spies. Her father, who could not lie convincingly about whose turn it was to scrub the acid vats. Her mother, dead six years, dragged into it posthumously, accused of carrying messages to the northern partisans as if her ghost had grown legs. And the third name on the list, the name that had made the cold walk up Mira's arms in the middle of a crowd of several hundred people:

*A courier, believed still at large.*

Casper.

* * *

Seven years was a long time to keep a promise, and longer to keep believing in one.

She had been seventeen the winter he fell through their door — literally, half-frozen, with a wound in his side that had soaked through two blankets before her father got a cauterizing iron anywhere near it. He had come over the pass ahead of the worst snows, riding a horse that died in their yard the next morning, carrying dispatches nobody ever explained and a fever that made him talk in three languages, two of which her father claimed not to recognize and one of which turned out to be poetry.

They had hidden him eleven weeks. The war that year had come within thirty miles of Halven and then thought better of it, and in that strange held-breath season, the Veren household had consisted of three people pretending to be four: Mira and her mother and her father, and the young courier who slept in the loft above the glassworks and came down each evening to eat soup and be quizzed by Tomas on the relative merits of different oils until Casper admitted, laughing, that he had never in his life considered what burned inside a lantern, only what it let him see by.

That admission had offended her father so deeply that he spent the rest of the winter educating him. By spring, Casper could trim a wick, judge an oil by its smoke, and explain why a good lantern is honest and a bad one lies. He had learned it all with the seriousness he brought to everything — as if knowledge were a debt being repaid, as if every hour of safety they gave him was being entered in a ledger somewhere behind his dark, careful eyes.

It was her mother who finally asked him, one night near the thaw, what he intended to do about that ledger.

"I don't understand," Casper said.

"You're keeping accounts," her mother said. She was peeling apples; she never stopped working, her hands. "Debts. I've watched you do it all winter. Every meal, you nod like a merchant signing a bill. So. What do you intend?"

And Casper, who was twenty-three and had been a state instrument since he was fifteen and had plainly never been asked what he *intended* about anything, looked at the table for a long moment. Then he looked at Mira — not at her parents, at her, sitting by the annealing oven with her mending in her lap, pretending very hard not to have been listening — and he said:

"When peace comes, I'll come back. With news. Good news, if I can manage it. And I'll knock on your door and tell you everything the war took away and gave back, and if there's anything in the world I can do for this family, you'll only have to ask."

Mira had laughed at him then. Kindly, but she'd laughed — because he said it so formally, like a speech rehearsed on horseback, and because her mother was smiling into the apples, and because seventeen-year-olds believe that promises made in kitchens are the cheapest kind.

But he had touched the brass charm at his collar when he said it — the little lantern her father had pressed into his hand at the border, the one every Veren carried and gave away only once in a lifetime — and Mira had known, in the way you know weather, that he meant it absolutely. That was the trouble with Casper Okonkwo. He did not have the talent for meaning things halfway.

He had left in March, walking south with his leg healed crooked and his dispatches intact and her father's lessons packed somewhere in his skull alongside whatever it was the Republic kept in him. At the gate he had turned around and taught her the signal, right there in the lane, in the gray early light, with snowmelt running in the ruts.

"Three taps," he said, knocking his knuckle gently against the glass pane of the gatehouse lamp. "Any glass — window, lantern, bottle. Three taps, then show a light. Three taps means *it's me, it's safe, open.* Anyone can knock. Three taps is ours."

"And the light?" she had asked.

"The light means I'm alive," he said, "and that I remember."

Then he had gone down the road and become small and then become nothing, and the war had rolled over the pass and away again, and seven years later the Republic of Vessany had sent soldiers instead of news.

* * *

The nights were the hardest, and also the only time she allowed herself to think.

Days had structure. Days were broth and bandages and listening at the chute for the changing of the patrol, days were keeping her father's spirits from bleeding out faster than his leg, days were the enormous silent labor of not making noise in a house designed for noise. But nights, when Tomas slept and the lantern was banked to a bead of blue at its heart and the whole ruined weight of her life settled onto her chest like snow on a roof — nights, she let herself count.

One hundred and eleven days since the proclamation. Forty-two since the workshop was boarded. Nineteen since the soldier had put his boot through the display window and she had watched, from behind the curtain of the neighbor's back room where she'd been buying milk with the last of the coin that didn't matter anymore, her family's finest pieces — the embassy lamps, the cathedral study models, the little brass night-lights her mother had designed — scattered and crushed on the cobblestones by men who wanted her to see it happen.

They hadn't found her. That was the arithmetic that saved her sanity: they had named her family traitors, arrested the cousins in Brenn, searched the workshop twice, and still not found the daughter, because Halven was a town of lantern-makers and lantern-makers know what light shows and what shadow hides, and her father had built this cellar's hiding places himself, years ago, without ever telling anyone why. *A man who makes lamps for governments,* he used to say, *should keep a door governments don't know about.*

But her father was failing. Not the leg — the leg would heal or it wouldn't, and he treated it with a fatalism that infuriated her — but something deeper, some slow interior collapse that happened to strong practical men when the work of their hands is taken and smashed in front of their town. Some mornings he woke and lay staring at the ceiling beams and she could see him doing sums, counting the years of his life against the pile of broken glass above his head, and losing.

"We'll rebuild," she told him those mornings. "Glass is glass. Sand is still sand."

"Mm," he would say. And once, terribly: "Your mother should have seen this from a safer distance."

So at night she thought about Casper, because thinking about Casper required hope, and hope was a fuel she rationed like the oil.

Did he know? Surely not — surely the Republic would not be so stupid as to send the very courier named in the proclamation back to the town he'd sheltered in. Unless the Republic was exactly that stupid, unless the Republic was a machine grinding forward on its own momentum with no one at the levers anymore, which was what her father said, what everyone said, in the last winter when it had become clear that Vessany was not falling to invaders but simply coming apart from the inside, province by province, like a lamp with a cracked reservoir, burning fine and fine and fine and then all at once going out.

And if he did know — if he somehow came —

She would not finish that thought. Finishing it was how you went mad in cellars. She banked it the way she banked the flame.

*Three taps,* she thought, at the edge of sleep, night after night, a rosary of knuckles on glass. *Three taps, then light.*

* * *

It was the twelfth of Deepmonth, by her reckoning, and the oil was into the last jar, and her father had eaten half a slice of bread and pronounced it excellent, which meant it was worse than she feared, when she heard the boots stop directly overhead.

Not the searching pattern. Something else. A single pair, stopping at the mouth of the cellar stairs, which had been sealed behind the flour shelves and painted to match the wall and which no one living in Halven knew about except her, her father, and one boy from another life.

She closed her hand around the knife and stood between the stairs and her father's pallet and did not breathe.

Boots descended three steps — the ones that creaked, deliberately, the way soldiers descend when they want whoever's hiding to hear them and calculate odds. Then a voice, muffled, calling down through the boards: "Clear." And the boots withdrew, and a door slammed, and the patrol moved on up the lane toward the square, and Mira discovered she had dug her fingernails into her own palm hard enough to leave marks for two days.

That was the hour the oil chose to gutter.

She noticed it as a change in the quality of the dark — the amber circle shrinking, the shadows at the cellar walls leaning inward. She went to the lantern and lifted the glass and trimmed the wick with fingers that only shook a little, and while the flame was exposed, while it stood up bright and tall for a moment before settling, she saw her face reflected in the smoked glass, and the reflection looked past her shoulder to the far corner of the cellar, where the old signal shutter sat in the wall beside the coal chute.

Her grandfather had built it. A foot-square iron plate, hinged, that swung open onto the street-level grate — a vent for fumes, officially, though her father had always maintained it was really a mirror-shutter, a way of flashing lamplight to the road in the old days when the workshop had signaled kiln-cooling hours to the bargemen on the river. It had not been opened in Mira's lifetime. It was rusted half-shut, and it faced the lane, and anyone in the lane at night, looking up at just the right angle, would have seen a faint glow through the grate and thought: *someone's cellar, someone's candle,* and thought nothing of it.

She finished trimming the wick. She closed the glass. She sat back down beside her father and took his hand, and he stirred and said, "Costs oil, talking," and slept again.

And it was there, in the smallest hours, with the lantern down to its blue bead and her father breathing his stair-climbing rhythm and the cold coming up through the floor in slow patient waves — it was there that she heard it.

Knuckles on glass. Above her. Faint, unhurried, unmistakable.

Once.

Twice.

Three times.

She did not move. Moving was impossible; her body had become the cellar itself, stone and dust and silence. Somewhere very far away her heart was trying to break out of her ribs, and somewhere very close her father murmured something about kiln temperatures and turned over.

*Anyone can knock,* he had said at the gate, seven years and a lifetime ago. *Three taps is ours.*

But soldiers knocked. Soldiers knocked on doors all over Halven, day and night, and sometimes they knocked politely and sometimes they kicked, and a trick of the ear in a dark cellar could turn any rhythm into the one you were starving for—

Then the light.

It came through the old grate at the head of the chute — not lamplight, not torchlight, something smaller and steadier, shielded by a hand, angled downward into the vent the way you'd angle a flame to reach someone below without blinding yourself. A sliver of warm gold fell through the coal chute's darkness and laid itself across the cellar floor like a ribbon someone had dropped.

And Mira understood, with a violence of feeling that buckled her knees, that whoever stood in the freezing lane above her house was holding a lantern, and showing it into the dark, deliberately, patiently — *I'm alive, and that I remember* — and waiting to see if anyone down in the deep of the ruined world would answer.

She was crying before she knew it, silently, the tears hot on a face gone cold, and she made herself move — made herself cross to the lantern on legs that belonged to a stranger, made herself lift the smoked glass, made herself carry the flame, cupped and trembling, to the foot of the coal chute.

Her father was awake. She saw it in the gleam of his eyes in the dark, and saw that he had heard it too, the taps, the light, and that he was looking at her with an expression she had not seen since her mother died — stripped bare, all the captain's calm gone, just an old man in the dark being offered a miracle he hadn't dared price.

"Answer him," Tomas Veren whispered.

Mira raised her fist to the iron plate of the old signal shutter — the same plate her grandfather had hung, the same iron, the same family, the same stubborn flame — and knocked.

Three taps.

Then she slid the shutter wide and lifted the lantern into the gap, high, letting the light pour up the chute and out through the grate into the night, a beacon the size of a prayer in a town under occupation, rules and caution and seven years of rationed hope all burned in one reckless instant.

For a moment, nothing. Wind. The distant boots in the square.

Then the grate above scraped, softly, and a shape blotted out the stars, and a voice came down through the dark — hoarse, half-broken, and so familiar that her whole childhood answered it at once.

"Mira?" it said. "It's Casper. I've come with news."

And then, lower, urgent, the voice of a man who has ridden through hell carrying something terrible: "God help me — I don't know how to say it, but you have to let me in. There are men behind me, and the dispatches — the dispatches are about your family."
