## 9. Reading Another Man's Mail

The cellar smelled of tallow and wet stone, and Casper sat in it for a long hour doing nothing at all, which was the most dangerous thing a courier could do.

Above him, muffled by floorboards and a century of lantern-makers' dust, Halven's occupiers went about their occupation. Boots on the cobbles of the Lampwrights' Row. The rhythmic hammering of boards being nailed across the Veren workshop's shutters — they had worked all morning at it, methodical as carpenters building a coffin lid. Once, a soldier laughed. The sound came down through the earth strangely softened, almost gentle, like something remembered from childhood.

Casper sat with his back against a shelf of unfinished lamp housings, brass fittings cold through his coat, and tried not to look at the saddlebag.

Mira was above him somewhere in the crawl between the cellar and the old coal chute, sitting with her father, changing the dressing on his leg. Old Veren had been struck by a rifle butt during the arrest that wasn't an arrest — the soldiers had come for the whole family and found only the father, and so they had taken him, held him two days, and thrown him back broken when the paperwork for a proper execution hadn't arrived. *The paperwork hasn't arrived.* Casper knew what was in his saddlebag. He knew exactly which sealed cylinder held that paperwork, wrapped in oilcloth, stamped with the chancery seal he had already proven false once.

He had told Mira he would rest until dark, then move her father to the miller's dry cellar outside town where partisans still ran messages. He had told her this with the steady voice of a man with a plan. It was not entirely a lie. It was mostly omission, and omission was the mother tongue of his profession.

Three dispatches. He turned the thought over again, the way he'd turned it over every night since Vessany, since the Minister's undersecretary had pressed the cylinders into his hands and said *north, and speed*. The first dispatch was administrative — garrison reorganization, probably already obsolete, the garrison it described likely dissolved or dead. He had never been tempted by that one. Sealed orders about troop movements were the mail of a dying animal, twitchings of nerves in a body past saving.

The third dispatch was addressed by name and seal to Captain Ilsa Rehn, and Casper had understood from the moment he took it that it was not meant for his eyes ever, under any circumstance, including his own death. He carried it the way one carries another person's knife.

But the second dispatch —

The second dispatch was addressed to the occupation commandant at Halven. And its paper was older than its seal.

He had known that for six days now, since the relay station at Kolvat, since he'd held the cylinder up to the lamp and seen what no courier was permitted to see and no honest clerk could have produced: wax poured from a matrix cut within the month, laid against paper milled — he checked the watermark twice, three times, hands steady out of long training while his stomach went cold — paper milled the previous spring. Someone had written this order before there was anything to order. Someone had drafted the ending of a story before the story happened.

Couriers did not open dispatches. It was the first oath and the last one, older than the republic itself, older than any government worth betraying: *the seal is sacred because you are trusted with the words of people who will never meet.* A courier who broke a seal was not a thief exactly. He was something worse and stranger — a man who had stepped out of his own life into someone else's, who had read a letter meant for a stranger and could never again be only himself. There were stories told in the courier houses about such men. None of the stories ended well, and all of them began the same way: *he told himself he had good reasons.*

Casper had excellent reasons. That was precisely what frightened him.

* * *

Mira came down the ladder near dusk, carrying a stub of candle and two pieces of black bread, and Casper put the saddlebag behind his heels without deciding to, the way a man hides a letter from his wife without being asked.

She saw it anyway. She saw everything; she always had, even at fourteen, even seven years ago when he'd lain fevered on the workshop floor above this same cellar and she'd brought him broth and stolen glances at the strange dark foreign boy the roads had deposited in her family's keeping. Now she was twenty-one, thin as a wick, with her mother's level gray eyes and a new steadiness around the mouth that hurt him to look at.

"You keep looking at your bag," she said. "Like it's a snake."

"Courier's habit. We're taught to know where our bags are."

"You've looked at it eleven times since you climbed down." She set the bread beside him and sat cross-legged, elbows on knees, the posture she must have used in hiding these three weeks. "Papa says the soldiers were waiting for papers before they shoot us properly. He heard them say it. *When the commission arrives.* So whatever's in there—" she nodded at the bag, and her voice didn't shake, which somehow made it worse— "it might be my family's death warrant, and you're the only person in Halven who knows."

"Mira—"

"I'm not asking you to open it." She said it fast and flat, recited, like a thing rehearsed in the dark. "I'm not stupid. I know what your oath costs. Papa wouldn't ask either. But I wanted you to know that we know. That we're lying down here in the dark knowing, and you're the one who has to decide whether we get to go on not-knowing forever, or—" She stopped. Looked at her hands. "Or whether you'll read it first and spare yourself the trip to the commandant's house just to watch."

Then she climbed back up the ladder, and left him the bread, and did not ask him anything else.

That was the cruelty of it. Not pressure — trust. Pressure he could have resisted. He had spent ten years resisting the clever pressures of captains and ministers. But Mira Veren handing him his own conscience, wrapped and labeled, refusing to touch it — that undid him more thoroughly than any interrogation ever had.

He ate the bread. It tasted of nothing. Above him he could hear her low voice through the floor, speaking to her father in the soft rolling dialect of the border lantern towns, and one phrase surfaced through the boards like a fish rising: *"...the courier came back, Papa. He actually came back."*

* * *

Night came down on Halven, and with it a hard frost that made the cellar walls sweat cold. Casper laid out his tools on a lamp housing with the care of a surgeon: a thin blade, a spool of wax thread, a cake of neutral sealing wax the color of dried blood, a sliver of the chancery-red tinted with iron rust that he had bought years ago in the capital from a locksmith who had asked no questions, because couriers are trained to repair seals — *not to break them*, the instructors had been careful to say, *but to restore them if accident requires.* Every skill is a door that swings both ways.

He took out the second cylinder. It weighed what it had always weighed, roughly four hundred grams of rolled vellum, but tonight it felt heavier, and he understood that the weight was not physical. Some weights are added by knowledge. He had learned that in this very town, seven years ago, when he first understood that the promise he'd made to these people would follow him longer than any assignment.

*If I open it,* he reasoned, *and it is routine — a tax levy, a billeting order, anything — then I reseal it and no one ever knows, and I have only sinned against a principle, which no tribunal punishes.*

*And if it is what Mira thinks it is —*

*Then I will know it anyway, sealed or opened, the moment the commandant reads it aloud before a firing squad.*

The logic was sound. That was what disturbed him most: that treason could be reasoned into, step by clean step, each step defensible, until a man stood where he stood now with a blade under a sacred seal and called it necessity. Somewhere in the capital there was a version of himself — younger, decorated, photographed with the Minister's own hand on his shoulder — who would not have recognized this cellar or this moment. Casper realized he was mourning that other self already, in advance, the way one mourns the living who are certain to die.

*Last chance to be only a courier,* he thought, and slid the blade under the wax.

The seal came up whole. Of course it did; forgery wax was cheap wax, brittle, poorly tempered — even its failure was evidence. Beneath it, the silk retaining cord parted at his touch, and the vellum unrolled with a sound like a small breath let out, and Casper Okonkwo held the second dispatch of his final mission up to the shielded flame of a shuttered lamp, and read.

The hand was fine clerical script, confident, unhurried. He recognized the style — the Chancery of Internal Continuity, formed in the last year of the republic, its very name a confession that continuity required internal work. He had carried their seals a dozen times without wondering what the words beneath them said. He wondered now. He would wonder for the rest of his life.

*By authority of the Provisional Commission for the Security of the Northern Marches:*

*Whereas it has been established by reliable testimony and documentary evidence that the persons named herein did, over a period extending not less than four years, maintain correspondence with agents of foreign powers; did furnish said agents with maps, schedules, and material aid; did harbor and conceal enemy operatives within the town of Halven; and did thereby commit acts of high treason against the Republic of Vessany—*

His eye snagged on the date. It would have snagged on it even if nothing else had, the way a rider's eye finds the loose stone on a road. The commission's decree was dated the third of Frostmonth. He knew the calendar of this war the way he knew his own pulse; the third of Frostmonth was nine days before the incident the charge rested upon — the ambush at the Kessel ford, the one the army's own bulletins blamed on partisan bands from the eastern hills. Nine days. The crime had been prosecuted before it occurred. Whoever wrote this document had not needed to wait for events; events, apparently, took instruction.

*Therefore it is ordered:* the text continued, in the same serene bureaucratic music, *that the following persons be taken into custody and, upon confirmation of the evidence by the occupation commandant, executed by firing squad without further reference to the Commission: Aldous Veren, lantern-maker; Liora Veren, his wife; Mira Veren, his daughter.*

Liora. Casper closed his eyes. Liora Veren had died in the autumn, of the cough, and been buried under the rowan tree behind the workshop with half of Halven's lampwrights walking behind the cart. The republic's machinery, grinding out death warrants from its capital, did not know she was dead. Or — a colder thought, arriving unbidden and precise — the machinery knew exactly what it was doing: names were names, quotas were quotas, and a dead woman listed among traitors was merely one less shot to spend.

There was a fourth paragraph. His hands had gone numb with cold or something colder, and he nearly missed it, a short block appended below the signature line:

*Note to the commandant: the courier bearing this order is to be considered unreliable. He has prior association with the family named. Permit him to complete delivery, then remand him for assessment. Assessment teams are authorized to dispose of the matter quietly.*

Dispose of the matter quietly. Casper read it three times. The words did not change. He, Casper Okonkwo, First Courier of the Northern Circuit, holder of the Minister's commendation, was an entry in the margins of his own mission — a detail to be tidied. They had known, before he ever left the capital, that he might hesitate, might warn, might turn. They had written his hesitation into the document itself, priced it in advance, scheduled its consequence. He was not carrying orders. He was enclosed inside them, like the fly in amber they'd shown him once as a child in the south, perfectly preserved and perfectly dead.

He did not remember sitting down. He was on the floor of the cellar with his back against the lamp housings, and the vellum was shaking in his hands, and the sound he heard was his own breathing gone ragged.

It was not fear, he discovered. Fear he knew intimately; he had dined with fear for ten years and they were old companions. This was something adjacent to grief and shaped like vertigo: the sensation of a floor proving to be painted cloth stretched over a pit. All his professional life he had believed — had needed to believe — that the republic was corrupt at its edges but sane at its center, that the rot was in individual officers, individual ministries, and that the dispatches themselves, the sacred sealed words, flowed from some final clean source. He had staked his honor on the sanctity of the container precisely so he would never have to examine the contents.

The contents were this. A forged atrocity, predated to fit, naming the family that had saved his life, with his own disposal appended as housekeeping.

He thought absurdly of the courier-house oath, recited at his induction with two hundred other young men and women, hands on the codebook: *What I carry, I carry blind; what is written is true; the seal binds me so that the truth may travel safe.* They had made them memorize it in unison, hundreds of voices in the marble hall, and it had seemed the finest sentence ever composed. *The seal binds me so that the truth may travel safe.* Except the seal did not protect truth. The seal was how lies traveled safe — wrapped, honored, escorted through a country of trusting men by couriers too disciplined to look.

His innocence had not died dramatically. That was the terrible thing. It died the way the seal had come up off the vellum: cleanly, in one piece, with barely a sound, leaving the shape of itself behind in the wax.

* * *

How long he sat there he could not afterward have said. Long enough for the candle to burn down to a thumb's width. Long enough for the frost to flower in fern-patterns on the cellar's one high window, silvering the night. He became aware, gradually, of practical things surfacing in him one by one, the trained mind reasserting itself over the broken man the way water reclaims a sunken deck: *reseal the wax — match the impression, warm the matrix — no, you cannot match it perfectly, but the commandant will break it himself, he will never study it — fold along the original creases — count the pages, four, yes, four —*

He was halfway through the folding when he heard the ladder creak.

Not Mira's step. Lighter, quicker, with the particular carelessness of youth descending in the dark. Casper's hands stopped moving, and he watched Tam come down the last rungs into the lamplight — the boy courier, seventeen years old, assigned to him on the road north without explanation, all sharp eyes and borrowed confidence — and Casper saw the exact moment Tam's gaze found the table. The open cylinder. The broken seal lying in two halves beside the neutral wax. The vellum in Casper's hands, folded wrong, caught mid-crease.

Tam froze on the bottom rung. His face, in the shielded light, performed its entire life in the space of three seconds: surprise, understanding, calculation, fear. He was, Casper thought absurdly, so very young. He had been sent to watch a legend, and he had found a man kneeling in a cellar holding the corpse of his country.

"Casper," Tam said carefully, and his voice had the strained formality of a boy reaching for the rules because the rules were all he had. "You've opened—"

"Yes," Casper said.

Neither of them moved. Above them, faint through the earth, the boots of the occupation passed along Lampwrights' Row, and the frost grew on the glass, and the little lamp burned steadily between them, and Casper understood — with the strange clarity that arrives only at genuine precipices — that everything now depended on what was in the face of a seventeen-year-old boy looking at a broken seal.

Tam's mouth opened. Closed. His hand went, by pure reflex, to the breast of his coat — where every courier kept the report book, the little oilcloth notebook in which a shadow recorded the failures of the man he shadowed.

It was empty-handed that it came back down.
