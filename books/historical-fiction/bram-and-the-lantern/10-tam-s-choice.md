## 10. Tam's Choice

Tam had learned to sleep like a soldier before he'd learned to sleep like a boy, and so it was the *sound* that woke him — not a footstep, which he would have known, but the soft rasp of paper against paper, the whisper of a thing being unfolded in the dark.

He kept his eyes closed. That was the first rule of his trade, older than any of Captain Rehn's rules: *hear first, look second, decide third.* He counted Casper's movements the way he'd been taught to count a stranger's coins — by weight alone. The creak of the floorboard by the hearth, where Casper had laid himself down hours ago with his boots on, as he always did, as if sleep were a loan he didn't expect to keep. The strike of no match. No lantern. Which meant Casper was reading by the last grey light of the fire's embers, and there was only one thing on God's cold earth worth reading in secret.

The second dispatch.

Tam breathed slow and even, the way his mother had taught him to breathe through fevers, and let his eyes open to slits. The room above the wayhouse at Kolver's Ford was small enough that he could see everything without turning his head: the sagging beam, the frost-ferned window, and Casper Okonkwo sitting cross-legged by the dead hearth with the dispatch tube across his knees, holding the opened paper up to the ember-light like a man consulting an oracle he already feared.

Casper's face did something Tam had never seen it do.

In the three weeks since Kolver's Bridge, since the relay station where Casper had stood too long over the second dispatch with his thumb pressed against the chancery seal, Tam had catalogued every face the man owned. There was Courier Face, smooth as river stone. There was Road Face, wind-scoured and squinting. There was the rare, brief smile he spent on Mira Veren when he thought no one was watching — no, Tam corrected himself, when he thought *she* wasn't watching; Tam watched everyone, that was the whole point of him. But this face was new. This was the face of a man reading his own death sentence in another man's handwriting, or something worse than death, which was: proof that everything he'd believed had been printed in error.

Casper lowered the paper. Sat very still. Then, with hands that were almost steady, began to fold it along its original creases.

Tam should have coughed then. Should have turned over, sighed, made some small honest noise of waking, given Casper the courtesy of pretending nothing had happened. That was procedure. That was what the boy courier assigned to shadow a suspect was *for* — not to intervene, never to intervene, only to observe and carry word back down the line. *Report within twelve hours by dead drop or verbal relay,* Rehn's instructions ran, written in her own cramped hand on a scrap he had burned in the first week's fire. *You are his apprentice. You are also his witness. Do not confuse the two.*

Twelve hours. The nearest dead drop was half a day behind them, back through the flooded lanes of the bell-less village. If Tam slipped out now, before dawn, he could make it by midday.

He lay in the dark and did the arithmetic of betrayal, and found, to his horror, that the numbers would not come out right anymore.

* * *

He had been sent, originally, because of the lantern charm.

That was the story as Tam had assembled it from fragments — from things Rehn said and things she didn't say, from the particular pause before Casper's name in the assignment orders. Casper Okonkwo was the republic's finest courier, but he carried a brass lantern on his watch chain, and brass lanterns meant Halven, and Halven meant the Verens, and the Verens' names had begun appearing in chancery memoranda with a frequency that made careful men nervous. Someone upstairs wanted to know whether the republic's best courier was also a sentimental man, and sentimental men, in the chancery's arithmetic, were compromised men.

So they had sent Tam, sixteen years old, smallest graduate of his intake class, with orders to be *useful* — to learn the signals, to memorize the routes, to become the sort of apprentice a master couldn't help talking to.

And that was where everything had gone wrong. Because Casper *had* talked. Not secrets — never secrets — but everything else. How to read a road at dusk. How to tell a paid informer from a frightened man by which one asked questions twice. How to tap three times on glass, softly, with the second knuckle, and wait, and tap three times again, and how the world changed when someone answered. Tam had come to spy on a legend and had instead found a teacher who shared his bread cheese-side down, who took the colder bedroll without discussion, who taught him the courier signals as if passing down something inherited rather than tactical.

Somewhere on the frozen road between the capital and Halven, Tam had stopped writing reports in his head and started writing excuses for not writing them.

Now he lay in the dark watching Casper refold a document that could kill a family, and understood that the excuses had run out.

* * *

Casper stood. That was wrong too — couriers didn't stand in the middle of the night unless something had broken inside them. Tam watched him cross to the window, dispatch in hand, and stand looking out at the ford, where black water slid under black ice and the far bank was a rumor of pines. His silhouette was very straight. It was the straightness of a man who has decided something and is waiting only for dawn to make it official.

He means to go back, Tam thought. Or forward. Either way, he is about to stop being a courier, and I am about to stop having a choice.

Unless I make one.

Tam sat up. The bedframe betrayed him with a groan, loud as a shot in the still room, and Casper turned — fast, faster than a tired man should — and for one suspended moment they looked at each other across the dark: master and apprentice, watcher and watched, both of them holding their breath over the same open secret.

"You were awake," Casper said quietly. Not a question.

"Since you unrolled it." Tam swung his feet to the cold floor. His heart was going like a drum in a parade, but his voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect. "I saw your face when you read it. I don't need to know what it says. A man doesn't look like that at good news."

Casper set the dispatch on the sill. For a long moment he seemed to weigh something, and Tam realized — with the strange clarity that comes in the worst moments — that Casper was deciding whether to lie to him, and that whatever he decided would tell Tam more than the document itself.

"It's dated the ninth of Frostmonth," Casper said at last. "It orders the arrest and execution of the family that sheltered me seven years ago. For treason committed — according to the chancery's own chronology — four days after this paper was already sealed and traveling north." He laughed once, a dry sound with no floor under it. "They wrote the ending first, Tam. They wrote the ending before the story happened."

Tam looked at the paper. At the wrapper lying beside it, torn cleanly, irrevocably — the outer cover bearing seal and registration marks, the thing that proved the dispatch had been opened, the thing that would prove *Casper* had opened it.

His oath said: report. His oath said: twelve hours, dead drop, verbal relay. His oath said that Casper Okonkwo was now a compromised courier carrying state intelligence, and that Tam Weller, apprentice, first class, had one duty and one only.

His oath also said, in the oldest unwritten clause of all, the one they never put in the intake manuals: *a courier does not betray the man who shares his road.*

"I was sent to watch you," Tam said.

Casper went very still.

"Captain Rehn's own orders." The words came out of him now like water through a cracked dam, years of it, weeks of it, all at once. "I was to report on the lantern charm. On your route deviations. On anyone you contacted. I burned the orders in our first week because I couldn't — because you taught me the three-tap signal like it mattered, and I couldn't sit by the same fire writing down everything you did. And I know what that makes me. I know exactly what that makes me. But I'm telling you now because—" His voice caught. He pushed through it. "Because if you're going to do what your face says you're going to do, then you shouldn't do it carrying evidence against yourself."

Casper stared at him. In the ember-light his eyes were unreadable, and then, slowly, terribly, they weren't unreadable at all — they were grieving.

"How long?" was all he asked.

"Since Kolver's Bridge. Before. Since the capital." Tam swallowed. "I'm sorry. I chose the wrong side for three weeks and I'm choosing the right one now, and both of those choices are unforgivable, and I'd make them both again."

The silence stretched. Outside, the ford murmured under its ice, patient as everything else that waited for morning.

Then Casper picked up the torn wrapper — the seal, the registration marks, the proof — and held it out.

"You burn it," he said. "I've done enough tonight that needs doing by my hand. This part should be yours. So you know which side you're on, past taking it back."

Tam took the paper. It felt lighter than anything he had ever carried, and heavier than every dispatch combined.

* * *

They fed it to the hearth a piece at a time, because burning a whole sheet makes flare and smoke, and Tam knew fire discipline the way he knew breathing. The chancery seal went first, curling like a live thing touched by flame, the embossed sigil of the Republic of Vessany blistering and going black. Then the registration marks, the columns of numerals that had certified, with all the republic's accumulated authority, that this envelope had traveled sealed and unopened — a certification that was now, itself, a lie told to no one. Tam fed the last corner to the coals and watched it catch, and felt something in his chest come loose and burn with it: the last version of himself that believed the service was clean, that the road ran somewhere honest, that Captain Ilsa Rehn's cold gray gaze contained anything warmer than arithmetic.

"The ash," Casper said.

Tam ground it fine with the poker, worked it into the old char of a hundred earlier fires until no trace remained. When he straightened, Casper was re-sealing the dispatch tube with its false bottom — Tam hadn't even known about the false bottom — the forged orders hidden beneath a layer of innocuous routing papers, one more sealed cylinder among the thousands that had ever crossed Vessany.

"We ride at first light," Casper said. "Not north. South."

"South?" Tam's stomach dropped. "The assembly—"

"The third dispatch is my only leverage and I'm not opening it until I have to. The exoneration can still be filed. If the clerk's still there. If the assembly hasn't run." Casper pulled his boots off at last, an oddly human gesture after everything, and looked at Tam across the darkening room. "Rehn will have figured it out by now. She'll have figured out *you* figured it out. She follows roads the way water follows downhill. Which means from tomorrow morning, we are not couriers. We are fugitives, and you are a traitor to the republic, and I want you to say it aloud so I know you understand what you just bought with that fire."

Tam thought of the intake hall, the oath, the brass-buttoned instructors. He thought of Mira Veren's name in a document dated before her crime. He thought of three taps on glass.

"My name is Tam Weller," he said, "and I am a traitor to the Republic of Vessany, and I'd sign it if they'd give me paper."

Something moved across Casper's face — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would live once there was time for such things.

"Get some sleep, Weller," he said. "First light. South road."

Tam lay back down and did not sleep. He listened to Casper settle by the hearth, boots off now, breathing eventually slow, and he stared at the ceiling and did the arithmetic again, the real arithmetic this time, the one with people in the columns.

Which is why he heard the horses before Casper did. Multiple horses, coming down the ford road at courier pace in the hour before dawn — and under the hoofbeats, faint but unmistakable across the frozen yard, a woman's voice giving quiet orders in a cadence Tam had learned to fear in the capital, a cadence that meant *surround the building first, knock second.*

He was on his feet and shaking Casper's shoulder before the first boot hit the stairs.
