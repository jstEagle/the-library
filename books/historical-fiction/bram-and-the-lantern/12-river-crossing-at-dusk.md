## 12. River Crossing at Dusk

The river announced itself before they saw it — a smell of wet stone and rotting reeds, a widening in the air, the sound of water moving with the lazy confidence of something that had never once been in a hurry. Mira Veren had crossed the Sorel twice in her life, both times as a girl, both times on her father's shoulders while he pointed out the markers: the drowned willow, the split rock, the bend where the current pretended to sleep and then took your oars if you were careless.

She remembered none of it usefully now. What she remembered was the weight of his hands under her knees, steady as a keel.

He leaned on her arm the way a leaning wall leans on its prop, and she adjusted her step so that neither of them stumbled. Behind them, Casper kept Tam moving at a pace just short of running, the boy's face pale under its freckles, his eyes going back down the southern road every thirty strides as though the road might produce Captain Rehn the way a wound produces blood.

"We'll make the crossing before full dark," Casper said, coming up beside them. He said it the way he said most things — evenly, professionally, a courier reporting the weather. But Mira had spent three days learning to read him, the way you learn a new dialect, and she heard the thing underneath: *we have to*.

Her father coughed, deep and wet, and stopped walking.

"Dada—" she began.

"Give me a moment," he said, which from him meant *give me several moments and do not count them where I can hear you counting.* He lowered himself onto a fallen milestone, and Mira crouched beside him and watched the dusk gather in the hollows of his face. The fever had broken two days ago, which the healer in the last village had called a mercy, but the cough remained, and the weakness in his legs, and Mira had begun privately to understand that mercy was not the same as recovery. Mercy only meant the river hadn't taken him yet. It didn't mean it wouldn't.

"The Sorel," he said, looking north through the thinning trees. "You can smell the iron in it. Your grandfather used to say the river kept secrets better than any vault. Said that's why the fords charge so much." A ghost of a smile. "Men always charge for what nature gives free."

"Then we should hope this ferryman charges like nature," Mira murmured, and her father laughed, which turned into coughing, which she endured with her hand flat against his back, counting again, hating herself for counting.

Casper stood a little apart, watching the road behind them, and Mira studied him while no one asked her to. Three days of flight had worn him down to essentials: the lean frame, the road-calloused hands, the eyes that never fully rested. He carried the third dispatch against his chest the way other men carried a crucifix or a knife — close, constant, unspoken about. She knew what it cost him to carry it. She had watched him refuse to open it a dozen times in a dozen small ways, his fingers drifting to his breast pocket and then away, a man refusing to scratch a wound because he suspected what was under the bandage.

He had opened the second dispatch for her family's sake. He would open the third, eventually, for his own — or die not opening it, and Mira could not yet decide which frightened her more.

"Come on," she said softly, helping her father up. "The light's going."

* * *

The ferry landing appeared out of the dusk like something half-remembered rather than half-seen: a plank walkway gone gray and soft with rot, a post with a bell missing its clapper, and beyond it the river itself, wide as a held breath, the far shore a rumor of darker trees under a sky the color of cooling iron.

The ferryman sat on an upturned barrel mending a net he plainly did not expect to use for anything worth catching.

Mira took stock of him the way her father had taught her to take stock of customers — hands first, then eyes, then mouth. The hands were work-worn but clean, a fisherman's hands, patient. The eyes were shrewd and tired. The mouth was set in the particular line of a man who had decided, sometime in the past year, that the world owed him and intended to collect.

"You'll want crossing," he said before any of them spoke. "Everyone wants crossing. That's the whole of my trade these days."

"We do," Casper said, stepping forward, and Mira saw him become the courier — posture straightening, voice finding its official register. "Four fares to the north bank. We're carrying dispatches for the provisional—"

"I don't care what you're carrying." The ferryman did not look up from his net. "Dispatches burn. People drown. The river takes both without asking whose seal is on them."

Tam shifted at Casper's shoulder. Mira's father breathed carefully, husbanding each lungful.

"What's the fare?" Casper asked.

"For four? And at dusk, when the water's worst and every soldier on either bank is watching the crossings?" The ferryman finally raised his head. His gaze went over them one by one, unhurried, itemizing. It snagged on her father's bandaged side, on Tam's youth, on Casper's courier's coat with its insignia half-torn away, and came to rest — Mira felt it happen, a physical thing, like a fishhook setting — on her satchel.

Because her father's finest lantern hung from it, wrapped in oilcloth, but even wrapped, it had a shape the eye found and would not release.

"Ten crowns," the ferryman said. "Each."

Casper did not move, but Mira felt the stillness go through him, the specific stillness of a man doing arithmetic he already knows ends in a number too large.

"That's extortion," Tam said hotly. "That's — the rate posted in Halven was—"

"Halven has soldiers in it," the ferryman said mildly. "Soldiers ask questions at crossings. Questions are bad for trade. My rate reflects the risk." He resumed his mending, pulling the twine through with small, exact motions. "Pay it or stand on the bank and shout across. See who answers."

"We don't have ten crowns," Casper said quietly. "We don't have one crown."

That wasn't quite true. Casper had coins — Mira had seen the flat leather packet — but she understood, watching his face, that those coins were measured out against weeks of road still ahead, against bribes and bread and the price of reaching the assembly, and that spending them here would be spending their future on their present. He was calculating whether the future was worth buying after all.

Mira stepped forward.

"My father," she said, "is Joren Veren. Of the Veren workshop in Halven."

The ferryman's hands paused over the net.

"Lantern-makers," she went on, and heard her own voice take on the cadence her father used with customers, the pride worn smooth by repetition into something almost like prayer. "Three generations of us. If you've ever seen a Veren storm-lantern — the brass ones, with the double wick and the gimbal mount, the kind that burns through a gale when everything else has guttered — then you know our work. And if you haven't," she lifted the satchel from her shoulder, unwound the oilcloth with deliberate care, "you're about to."

The lantern came into the dusk glowing dully, brass catching the last light the way brass does — not brightly, but deeply, holding the sunset inside itself. Her father made it the winter before the war, working alone in the shop through the small hours while Mira slept upstairs, and when she'd asked him why, he'd said: *because a man should build one perfect thing before he dies, and I'm old enough to know which thing mine is.* It had hung, unburned, above his workbench ever since. Too fine to sell. Too true to give away.

She held it out.

"One crossing," she said. "Four fares. The finest lantern made in Vessany in twenty years, and the last one that will ever bear the Veren mark."

The ferryman put down his net. He came off the barrel slowly and took the lantern in both hands, turning it, and Mira watched his shrewdness lose its war with wonder. His thumb found the gimbal mount and rocked it, testing the balance. He opened the glass door and looked at the wick assembly the way her father looked at other men's work — hunting the flaw, failing to find it.

"This is—" He stopped. Started again. "Veren work. I've a cousin in the river trade swears by nothing else." He looked up, and the merchant in him surfaced one last time, weakly. "It's worth more than ten crowns. It's worth forty."

"It's worth more than that," Mira agreed. "And none of it matters if we're standing on this bank tomorrow when the soldiers come. Name your price for the crossing and keep the change. We're buying time, not goods."

Something moved behind the ferryman's eyes — recognition, maybe, of the arithmetic he'd been running all year himself, trading precious things for survivable days.

"The Verens of Halven," he said slowly. "They're saying things about that family. In the towns south of here. Bad things."

"They're saying lies," Mira said. "Lies travel faster than truth. It's why truth needs couriers." She tipped her head toward Casper, who had gone very still. "This man is carrying proof of it to the assembly, at more risk than you or I will ever be asked to bear. All we want is the far bank."

The ferryman was quiet a moment. Then he slung the lantern from its ring onto his own shoulder, and Mira felt the loss go through her like the river's cold — not sharp, but total, entering everywhere at once. Her father's masterpiece. Her family's signature, written in brass and flame. Everything the workshop had been, reduced to barter at a rotten dock in the last light of a dying republic.

"On board," the ferryman said. "All of you. Before the light finishes dying. The Sorel doesn't forgive late starters."

* * *

The crossing was worse than the price.

The river ran high with meltwater, and the ferryman worked the sweep pole alone, grunting, driving them out into a current that took the flat barge sideways immediately, casually, as if picking it up to inspect it. Tam clung to the gunwale, white-knuckled. Casper stood at the bow watching the southern bank, where the road emerged from the trees, empty, then empty again, then — Mira saw Casper's hand drop to his coat — empty a third time, but differently, and she understood that emptiness could change its quality entirely depending on what you expected to fill it.

Her father sat against the cargo rail with his eyes closed, breathing in careful installments, and Mira knelt beside him and put her hand over his.

"You shouldn't have traded it," he said without opening his eyes.

"It bought us the crossing."

"It was the best thing I ever made."

"Yes." She watched the black water slide past, patient and enormous. "And you made it for people to use in storms, Dada. Well. Here's the storm."

He was silent a long moment. The pole knocked against stone; the ferryman swore; spray came up cold and mineral. Then her father's hand turned under hers and gripped it, dry and strong despite everything, the hand that had taught hers to cut tin and seat glass and trim a wick so it burned without smoking.

"There was a night," he said, "the winter we hid your courier friend. You were seventeen. You came down to the cellar where we kept him and you asked me — do you remember what you asked me?"

Mira did remember. She'd asked him whether he was afraid. Whether hiding a stranger, breaking the law of a country sliding toward its own throat, was worth the danger to the two of them. And her father had lit one of his own storm-lanterns there in the cellar dark, and watched it burn steady in the still air, and said: *a light that only shines when it's safe isn't a lantern, Mira. It's decoration.*

"I remember," she said.

"Well." Her father opened his eyes at last, and in the gloom they were bright with something that was not entirely fever. "Tonight you proved you were listening. That's all. That's the whole of what I wanted to say." He squeezed her hand. "The lantern did its work. So are you."

Ahead, the northern bank came up out of the dark — real, solid, mud-slicked, ordinary. The ferryman brought the barge alongside the landing with surprising gentleness and held it there with the pole while they disembarked, Tam first, then her father with Casper's shoulder under his arm, then Mira, whose boots sank into the mud of the far shore and who stood a moment feeling the ground hold her, feeling how much a thing that simple could be worth.

"Ferryman." Casper lingered at the gunwale. When the man glanced up, Casper said, quietly, "If anyone asks — soldiers, anyone — who crossed tonight?"

"No one crossed tonight," the ferryman said. "River's been closed since the week of the floods. Terrible inconvenience." He touched the brim of his hat, an odd, formal little gesture, and added: "Tell Veren the river remembers good work. Even if the republic doesn't."

Then he pushed off, and the barge slid back into the dark it had come from, and somewhere out on the water, faint and brief, Mira saw a small warm point kindle — the lantern, already burning, hanging from the stern like a low star being carried away from her.

She stood until the river swallowed it.

* * *

They walked north along the towpath in the last grey remnant of light, four figures moving through a landscape that had given up pretending to be peaceful. Burned wagons in a field. A shrine to some minor saint toppled face-down in a ditch, its painted face pressed into the mud as if listening for instructions. Once, far off, the flat crack of gunfire, a handful of shots, then nothing — no way to know whose side, or whether sides still meant anything at all.

Tam fell into step beside Mira. The boy had barely spoken in two days, but now he glanced at her satchel — lighter now, the strap pulled short where the lantern's ring had hung.

"I'm sorry," he said. "About your lantern. That was — I saw it, in the workshop, before. It was beautiful."

"It was," Mira agreed. "But beautiful things rot in cellars, Tam. Better they buy something." She managed a smile for him. "Besides. My father can make another."

She believed it as she said it. She wanted very much to keep believing it. Her father walked between the two men ahead of them, leaning hard on Casper's arm, and the set of his shoulders told her things she refused to read.

They made camp after full dark in the lee of a collapsed hay barn, no fire, cold rations shared in silence. Casper divided the watch and took the first himself, sitting against the fallen beam with his face turned south, toward the river, toward the road they'd left, toward whatever followed them up it.

Mira couldn't sleep. She lay listening to her father's uneven breathing and the wind working through the ruined barn and, beneath it all, the river — invisible now, but present, the way grief is present, a vast movement in the dark that never stops.

Near midnight, Tam shook Casper awake for his watch shift, and Casper rose, and in rising, his hand went briefly to his breast pocket — to the third dispatch, the sealed one, the weight he'd carried unopened since the capital. His fingers rested there a moment in the darkness. Rested, and then pressed flat, the way a man presses a wound to judge how deep it runs.

Mira, watching from the shadows, saw him look south one more time.

Far down the black line of the riverbank, so distant it might have been an ember lifted on the wind, a light was moving. Small. Steady. Following the towpath north.
