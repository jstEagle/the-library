## 13. What Rehn Carries

Captain Ilsa Rehn had carried many things for the Republic of Vessany in her thirty-one years of service, and she had long ago made a discipline of knowing exactly what each of them weighed.

As a girl courier, she had carried dispatch cases light as birds' bones and heavy as sins — the weight never in the paper but in what the paper would do once it left her hands. She had carried requisitions that fed a garrison and requisitions that starved a village, and both arrived with the same seal, the same wax, the same indifferent authority. Later, as an officer, she carried verbal orders that could never be written down, which meant they could never be disproven, which was precisely why they were given aloud. Now she carried three things north through the dying country: a pistol with a full chamber, a folded set of instructions she had memorized and burned, and a suspicion she had been trying to shoot out of her own chest for two hundred kilometers.

The suspicion was winning.

She rode at the head of six men — not soldiers anymore, not since the Third Army dissolved itself into rumor and desertion, but men who still answered to a uniform that no longer commanded anything except their own belief in it. Behind them the road unwound through the hill country, brown fields stitched with frost, farmhouses standing empty with their doors swinging like the town had simply exhaled and left. Ahead, somewhere past the river, three fugitives were running south with a dispatch case that should have been delivered by now.

*Should have been delivered by now.* That was the first crack. Casper Okonkwo had been late exactly twice in eleven years of service, and both times because of floodwater. He did not dawdle. He did not defect. He did not open other people's mail.

And yet.

* * *

They stopped at noon in the shadow of a relay station whose flagpole stood bare, the republic's colors cut down so neatly someone had clearly taken them home to keep or to burn. Rehn dismounted, handed her reins to Sergeant Voss, and walked the building's perimeter alone, the way she always did — not because she expected ambush, but because walking let her think, and thinking was the one activity she permitted herself only in motion.

The station master was still there, an old man with a stove and nothing else worth guarding. She bought hot water from him for tea and asked her questions in the flat tone that made civilians answer before they understood they'd been asked.

Three travelers. Yes, they'd come through. A young man, a young woman, and an older courier — the old man at the station had said *courier* with a certain reverence, because even now, even here, the couriers were the last institution anyone believed in. They'd taken the south road toward Ferren's Ferry. The woman had traded a lantern for passage.

"A lantern," Rehn said.

"Fine work," the old man said. "Brass and glass, hand-wrought. Ferryman'll get double for it in Vessa Port, if the Port's still standing by the time he gets there." He chuckled at his own joke, then stopped chuckling when he saw her face.

A lantern. She filed it away with everything else she knew about Casper Okonkwo, which was a longer file than he probably imagined. Handlers knew their couriers the way farriers know horses — not sentimentally, but completely. She knew his stride from a hundred meters. She knew he counted his steps when he was worried, that he slept on his left side with the dispatch case buckled to his wrist, that he kept some small brass thing on a cord around his neck that he touched before every hard ride, superstitious as any soldier, though he'd have denied it under torture.

She knew he had spent a winter in Halven once, seven years ago, convalescing in a lantern-maker's house, and that he had never requested leave to return there, and had never needed to, because Halven sat directly on the northern route he rode every autumn.

That knowledge had been in her file on him for years, annotated, dormant, irrelevant.

Until the third dispatch had named Halven.

* * *

Her orders — the ones she had read once by candlelight and then fed to the flame, word by word, watching the ink curl — were simple. Confirm delivery of the remaining dispatches. Confirm the disposition of the Veren matter. Then retire the courier corps personnel involved. All of them. *Personnel involved* included the apprentice, Tam Wessel, sixteen years old, ninety days in service, whose file she had also memorized: orphaned at nine, workhouse-educated, recruited off a courier exam he'd passed with the second-highest marks in the district.

Rehn had been the highest marks, twenty-nine years earlier. She wondered if Tam knew that, and decided he didn't, because nobody knew that, because it was the kind of fact she carried the way she carried everything — sealed, unopened, hers.

She poured her tea and drank it standing, looking south down the road where her quarry had passed hours ago, and she allowed herself the exercise she permitted perhaps twice a year: she took out her suspicions and examined them in daylight.

The second dispatch, the Veren order, had troubled her from the moment she'd countersigned its transfer in the capital. She had handled execution orders before — too many, catalogued in a memory she kept locked but never empty — and they had a texture. They were drafted fast, stamped fast, dated the day of or the day after the offense, because treason moved quickly and so did the chancery. This one was different. The seal impression was crisp and recent, less than a month old. But the paper — she had held it to the light at the relay in Dorn, casually, the way a handler checks for water damage — the paper was older. Foxed at the edges. Creased along folds that had been folded a long time ago and refolded recently to hide it.

Someone had pre-written history. Someone had drafted the death of the Veren family before the Verens had done whatever it was they were supposed to have done — before, possibly, anyone had bothered to invent what they'd done.

Rehn was fifty-two years old. She had served five Chancellors, two of whom had been removed by the assembly, one of whom had been removed by the other four, and she was not naive. She knew the republic manufactured traitors the way the lantern-makers of Halven manufactured lamps: by process, by craft, season after season, because the product was needed. She had signed off on such processes. She had, on two occasions she could name and could not forget, been the instrument rather than the signature.

But there was a difference, she had always told herself, between a state that lied and a state that forged — between bending the truth of a case and writing the verdict before the crime. The first was government. The second was something older and colder, a machine that consumed its own people to keep its gears wet, and if the machine had begun forging the deaths of a family of lantern-makers in a border town of no strategic value whatsoever, then the machine was not defending the republic anymore.

Then the machine *was* the republic. And there was nothing left to be loyal to except the machine.

She finished her tea.

* * *

"You think they'll run for the Assembly," Voss said when she swung back into the saddle. He was forty, solid, a man who had followed her since the border wars and would follow her into whatever came after the republic, out of habit if nothing else.

"The clerk of records is the only office still functioning between here and the capital," Rehn said. "If Okonkwo has opened what he's carrying — and he has; don't ask me how I know, I know — then the dispatch is worthless as evidence unless he files it somewhere official. He wants exoneration on record. He wants the family cleared. He'll go to the one man who can still stamp paper."

"And we go to the same man."

"We go to the same man."

Voss nodded and said nothing else, because Voss understood her silences the way she understood Casper's strides. But half a kilometer on he glanced sideways at her and said, carefully, "The orders say all personnel involved, Captain."

"I can count, Sergeant."

"Yes, Captain. It's just — Wessel's a boy."

"Wessel is a courier of the Republic of Vessany who knew his obligations when he swore them." She heard her own voice deliver the sentence and recognized it as her father's voice, and her instructors' voices, and every voice that had ever told her that duty was a wall you leaned your whole life against because the alternative was falling. "So are we all."

Voss subsided. Rehn rode, and the road narrowed ahead into the marsh causeway, and she thought about boys.

* * *

She had been younger than Tam Wessel once, which surprised people who saw only the grey at her temples and the set of her jaw. She had been a workhouse girl too — not orphaned, worse, merely unwanted — and the courier corps had been the first institution that had ever looked at her and seen a use. That was all she had ever wanted, she realized now, all those decades later: to be used precisely, efficiently, well. A tool doesn't need love. A tool needs sharpening, maintenance, a purpose. The republic had sharpened her and pointed her and she had flown straight for thirty-one years, and if the hand that held her had begun to shake, that was no fault of the blade.

Except.

Except that in Dorn, holding the second dispatch to the window light, she had felt something shift in her chest like ice cracking on a river — not breaking, not yet, just announcing that it *could*. And except that last night, camped in a burned barn, she had dreamed of the oath ceremony. Nineteen years old, chapped hands over heart, reciting the courier's vow while an officer with a magnificent moustache watched from a dais: *I carry what is given. I do not read. I do not weigh. I deliver.* She had believed, at nineteen, that this was a beautiful vow. She had spent thirty years learning it was a beautiful cage, and another year learning that the door had never once been locked. She had simply never tried it.

She suspected the charges against the Veren family were forged. She suspected it with the certainty of a woman who had handled enough authentic evil to recognize the counterfeit kind. She suspected that Casper Okonkwo — slow, principled, infuriating Casper, the best courier she had ever trained and the worst liar she had ever met — had opened the mail, discovered the forgery, and was running south to undo it. She suspected that the third dispatch ordered her, in advance, pre-written like everything else, to make sure none of them reached the Assembly alive.

She suspected all of this.

And she would deliver the orders anyway.

Not because the orders were just. She had stopped believing the orders were just sometime around the second Chancellor. Not because she feared consequences — the republic was collapsing around her ears; there would soon be no one left to punish anything. Not even because of the vow, though the vow lived in her spine the way vertebrae do, structural, unexamined.

She would deliver them because if she chose this morning to stop — to turn aside, to let the machine's paper rot unread in some ditch — then every order she had ever delivered became retroactively meaningless. The executed, the silenced, the families broken by her neat, correct, punctual deliveries: all of it would resolve into cruelty without structure, violence without meaning. But if she finished — if she carried these final orders to the end of the road, if the republic died with its last courier still obedient — then it had all *been* something. A shape. A service. A life that added up.

It was not a noble calculation. She knew that. It was vanity wearing duty's uniform, pride dressed in oath-grey, a woman so invested in the architecture of her own fidelity that she would hold the roof up with her bare hands while the building burned and called it integrity.

She knew all of this too. Knowing changed nothing. That was the cage: not ignorance but perfect, lucid imprisonment, the prisoner watching her own keys hang on the wall inside arm's reach, hour after hour, and staying seated.

* * *

They found the ferry crossing at dusk, or what was left of it. The ferryman was gone — south, presumably, with a brass lantern and whatever else his treachery had bought him — and the barge rode at anchor on the far bank, taunting. One of the soldiers found tracks: three sets, fresh, heading along the southern towpath toward the chapel at Marsh Hollow, where the map promised a road junction and the Assembly's last relay clerk.

"They're a day ahead," Voss said.

"No." Rehn studied the mud, the churned ground by the mooring post, the angle of the fading light on the water. "Half a day. The woman's father is hurt; they're carrying him or moving at his pace. And they'll stop at the chapel — couriers always stop at chapels; there's a bell code posted in half of them." She remounted. "We ride tonight. Moon's enough."

Voss gathered the men. Rehn sat her horse a moment longer at the water's edge, looking across the black river toward the south, and did the last thing she ever allowed herself: she spoke aloud to no one.

"If you've opened my dispatches, Casper," she said quietly, "then you already know how this ends. You're the best I ever trained. You should have known better than to give me a reason."

The words hung over the water and got no answer, because the honest answer — *you gave yourself the reason years ago, in Dorn, in the light, when you held that paper up and saw what it was* — lived in her chest where the suspicion lived, and she put it back in its compartment, and closed the compartment, and turned her horse south.

Behind her, in her saddlebag, wrapped in oilcloth she would not open until the end of all things, the third dispatch rode as silently as it had ridden in her dreams: her name on the signature line, Casper Okonkwo's name on the sentence, both written by hands that had never met either of them, dated before either of them had ever mattered.

She carried it the way she carried everything.

Perfectly. Punctually. All the way to Marsh Hollow, where the chapel bell stood ready in its tower — and where, when she raised her lantern to signal her men to dismount, she would see, answering faintly from the darkness within, three deliberate taps of light. The old courier signal. The one that meant, in a code only two people alive still knew: *I see you. Come in. Trust me.*

She sat very still in the dark, her hand frozen on the lantern shutter, thirty-one years of obedience warring with one impossible flicker of lamplight — and made no move at all.
