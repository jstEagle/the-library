## 14. The Third Dispatch

The chapel had no name that Casper could find, and no congregation either — not since the war had eaten the parish whole. It stood alone on a rise above the river road, its slate roof patched with mismatched tin, its bell tower empty where the bell had been melted down for shot two winters past. The door hung open at a patient angle, the way doors do when no one has bothered to lock them in years.

Casper led them inside because there was nowhere else.

Mira went first, her father's arm over her shoulders, the old man's breathing a wet rasp in the cold air. Tam came last, walking backward for the first hundred paces, watching the road they'd just abandoned, his hand never far from the signal whistle at his collar. The boy had not slept properly in four days. None of them had. But Tam watched the road the way Casper himself had once watched roads, before exhaustion wore the edges off everything and left only the raw grain of the world beneath.

"Clear," Tam said finally, and the word seemed to cost him something, as if he couldn't vouch for safety anymore, only for the absence of soldiers he could see.

The chapel's interior was stripped but not desecrated. Someone — perhaps the last priest, perhaps farmers with more decency than the times deserved — had carried out the silver and the vestments before the looters came, leaving behind only what was worthless: the stone altar, three benches scarred by knife-points and fire, and the great east window, which depicted nothing anyone would steal. It showed a lantern. A single flame inside a glass case, held aloft by a figure whose face had been worn to anonymity by decades of weather and salt wind. Below it, in letters half-eroded: *I am the light that does not ask permission.*

Mira lowered her father onto a bench near the wall, out of the draft, and began without being asked to unpack the small medical kit they'd carried since Halven — needle, thread, the last of the willow bark, a rag gone stiff with dried blood. Her movements were economical, unhesitating. She had become, in eleven days of flight, someone who did things rather than feared them, and Casper found himself mourning that transformation even as he depended on it.

He should have been watching the road. Instead he stood at the altar with the saddlebag open at his feet, looking down at the third dispatch.

It had traveled with him for nineteen days. Through the capital's falling snow and the mud of the northern passes, through the occupied streets of Halven and the marshes south of it, through river crossings and burned granaries and one night in a hay loft where Rehn's outriders passed so close beneath him he could hear them arguing about tobacco. Through all of it, the third dispatch had ridden against his spine like a second heart — sealed, official, and heavy.

Heavy. That was the thing he kept returning to. Paper did not weigh this much. He had carried dispatches for eleven years; he knew their weight the way a mason knows stone. A chancery packet of this size should have been an afterthought in the bag, a feather among coins. This one pulled. It sat in his hands now with the density of something meant to endure — lead foil layered beneath the wax, the way the republic sealed only its most permanent instruments. Treaties. Sentences of death. Instruments of state that were meant to outlast the men who wrote them.

"You keep looking at it," Mira said. She didn't look up from her father's bandages. Her voice was flat with tiredness, but underneath the flatness ran something sharper. "You've been looking at it since the ferry."

"I'm supposed to deliver it," Casper said. "To the assembly clerk at Corven's Ford. Unopened. That's the oath."

"The assembly may not exist anymore." Old Veren spoke from the bench, his voice a ruin of what it had been, but his mind still cutting clean. "You said yourself the roads south carry refugees, not couriers. If the government has fled, your oath is a letter addressed to a house that has burned down."

"If I open it, I'm the traitor they'll say I always was."

"And if you don't?" Mira tied off the bandage and finally raised her eyes to him. In the gray light through the lantern window she looked older than her twenty-four years, and younger too, both at once — the girl who had signaled him across a frozen courtyard seven years ago and the woman who had smuggled her crippled father through a war zone with nothing but nerve. "Casper. They already named my family traitors on paper dated *before* anything happened. Do you understand what that means? The ink was dry before the crime existed. Whoever wrote that second dispatch wasn't recording history. They were writing it in advance. And they gave *you* the third one. What do you imagine is in it?"

"I don't know."

"You suspect."

He did. God help him, he had suspected since the relay station, since the moment he'd turned the forged seal under a magnifying lamp and felt the first hairline crack run through eleven years of faith. And the suspicion had grown teeth on the road south, fed on small things: the way Captain Rehn's pursuit had felt less like a chase than a schedule, keeping pace with him rather than closing on him, as if she needed him alive until some condition was met. The way Tam had been assigned — a watcher wrapped in the language of apprenticeship, a witness whose testimony would be needed later. The way the mission itself made no sense. Three dispatches, one courier, north into a collapsing front and then all the way back south again? No handler worth her salt routed critical state papers through a thousand miles of civil war twice.

Unless the papers weren't the cargo.

Unless the courier was.

Tam had drifted close during the argument, standing at the end of the bench with his arms crossed, and Casper saw the boy's eyes go to the dispatch too — saw the same arithmetic running behind them. Tam was sixteen and had lied about his age to get his commission and still slept with his boots laced, and he was not stupid. He had carried mail long enough to know what lead foil meant.

"Casper." The boy said it quietly. "If you open it here, nobody ever has to know."

"Somebody always knows," Casper said. "That's how the machine works. That's the whole design of it. There's always a Tam."

The boy flinched, and Casper regretted it immediately, but there was no time to gather the words back; outside, far down the valley, a dog began to bark — not the frantic alarm of a stranger but the rhythmic complaint of an animal disturbed by movement on the road, and every ear in the chapel tuned itself to it like wire drawn tight. The barking faded. Hoofbeats did not follow. But the shape of the silence afterward was different, and they all knew it.

"We rest one hour," Casper said. "No fire. Then we move in the dark, cross-country toward Corven's Ford. If the clerk is there—"

"If the clerk is there," Mira finished, "we file the exoneration, and if he isn't?"

Casper looked down at the third dispatch, and the truth came up in him like water into a well shaft, cold and inevitable, and he heard himself say it before he had decided to say it:

"Then this is the only leverage we have left. And I need to know what it is before I stake our lives on it."

* * *

They let him be alone with it, which was its own kind of mercy. Mira curled beside her father on the bench, and within minutes the old man's breathing deepened into true sleep and hers followed, her hand still resting on his wrist, counting it even in dreams. Tam took the doorway, whittling a stick into nothing, pretending to watch the road while actually watching Casper — and Casper let him watch, because the boy had earned the right to see whatever happened next, and because a part of Casper wanted a witness. Wanted, for once in his careful invisible life, for someone else to carry the knowledge of it too.

He knelt at the altar. Not to pray — the habit had died in him years ago, somewhere between one border and another — but because kneeling put his face close to the stone, and stone was honest, and he wanted something honest near him for what he was about to do.

The seal was black wax over lead, pressed with the Great Chancery die: the tower, the bridge, the seven stars of the founding charter. He knew it intimately. He had kissed it once, absurdly, as a young courier, on the day he received his commission — had pressed his lips to the wax of his first real dispatch like a bridegroom, and old Handler Mosk had laughed at him and said, *Careful, boy. You don't kiss the state. The state kisses you, and you never feel it happen.*

He worked the knife under the edge of the wax slowly, turning it, letting the seal lift intact rather than break. Some superstition in it — leave the seal whole, as though breaking it were the crime and reading it merely the symptom. The wax peeled back like a scab. Beneath it, the lead foil, folded in thirds. He unfolded it.

Inside were two documents.

The first was exactly what the routing docket claimed: a certificate of delivery authority, granting the bearer — himself, by name, his commission number, his physical description down to the scar on his left thumb — full powers to present the enclosed instrument to any officer of the provisional government or its successors. Standard language. He read it in four seconds because he had read a hundred like it.

Then he picked up the second document, and the world went very quiet, the way it does in the instant after a bell stops ringing, when your ears haven't caught up and the silence seems louder than the sound.

It was printed on chancery stock. Watermarked, dated — dated nine days ago, in the capital, on the day after he'd left it. The heading read: *Writ of Execution, Summary Authority, Article Fourteen.* And below the heading, in the formal cadence of a language he had served and believed and bled minor amounts of blood for at three different borders, the republic of Vessany sentenced Casper Okonkwo to death.

Not for opening the dispatch. Not for treason, not yet — those charges would be manufactured later, he understood that now, understood it the way you understand the mechanism of a trap only after the jaw has closed. The writ cited none of the crimes he hadn't committed yet. Instead it invoked Article Fourteen, the emergency provision, the one every courier learned in training and every courier believed would never touch him: the provision allowing the Director of Signals to order the permanent silencing of any intelligence asset whose knowledge constituted a threat to the security of the state, *such determination to be final and not subject to review.*

His knowledge. His knowledge of the second dispatch. Of the forged charges against the Veren family. Of the pre-dated seal. The moment he opened that packet at the relay station — and Rehn had known he would, Rehn had probably counted the hours until he would — he had ceased to be a courier and become a liability. Everything since had been theater. The pursuit, the pace of it, the patience of it: Rehn wasn't chasing him to stop the delivery. She was escorting him to a place where his death could be arranged cleanly, with witnesses, with documentation, with the boy courier as the state's innocent-eyed corroborating witness. Deliver the evidence of the frame, confirm the execution of the Verens, and then be executed yourself by your own student's testimony — that was the architecture of it. Three dispatches. Three deaths. One machine running exactly as designed.

And the signature.

He knew the signature. Every courier knew the signature; it appeared on their commissions, on their codebooks, on the annual renewal of their oaths. *A. Ferrenholz, Director of Signals.* The elegant, slightly ascetic hand that had authorized every order Casper had ever obeyed. It was there at the bottom of the writ, flawless, confident.

Signed nine days ago.

Before he had opened anything. Before he had done anything at all. Before the alleged crime, exactly as with the Verens — because with Article Fourteen there was no crime required, only the *determination* of one man that a courier's knowledge was dangerous, and that determination could be made, and signed, and sealed, and handed to a captain with verbal orders, before the courier in question had taken a single step off his appointed path.

They had signed his death warrant before he had earned it. Perhaps before he had even left the capital. Perhaps the ink had been drying while he packed his saddlebag and weighed his life in portable things and thought, with quiet dread, that this assignment felt different. It had felt different because it was different. He had been carrying his own execution north and south across a dying country like a man transporting a bomb he didn't know was armed.

Casper became aware, distantly, that he was sitting on the chapel floor with his back against the altar, the writ open in his lap. He didn't remember sitting down. The light through the lantern window had shifted; the anonymous saint's face was lit amber now, early evening coming on, and the words of the eroded inscription swam in front of him — *the light that does not ask permission* — and he thought: neither did they. Neither did the republic ever ask permission. It simply wrote, and sealed, and sent men like me to carry the writing, and when the writing was delivered it wrote again, and the new writing erased the carrier, and somewhere in the capital a director with beautiful handwriting signed the erasure in advance and went to dinner.

Eleven years. Eleven years of oaths and codes and cold mornings and the private, secret pride of being trusted — *chosen*, he had always thought of it, chosen among thousands — and the whole time the trust had been a length of rope paid out patiently, coil by coil, until there was enough of it to hang him.

He heard footsteps on stone and didn't reach for his knife. There seemed no point anymore. Everything that could kill him had already been signed.

Tam stood over him. The boy looked down at the writ, and Casper watched him read — watched the sixteen-year-old face perform the same journey Casper's had just made, disbelief, then denial, then the horrible smooth settling of comprehension, like water finding its level. Tam's lips moved over Article Fourteen. His hand came up slowly and touched the signal whistle at his collar, the badge of the service, as if checking whether it had burned him.

"They signed it early," Tam said. His voice cracked in the middle of the sentence and he pushed through it. "They signed it *before*. That means—"

"It means the mission was never a mission. It was a funeral procession, and I was the corpse, and they were kind enough to let me walk myself to the grave."

Below them in the valley, faint but unmistakable in the still air, a horse nickered. Then another. Tam's head snapped toward the window; Casper was already folding the writ, already sliding it inside his shirt against his skin, where paper warmed to the body and became indistinguishable from it.

Down the rise, through the bare trees, lanterns were being lit along the river road. Three of them. Moving unhurried, in formation, the way lanterns moved when the people carrying them had all night and knew exactly where they were going.

"Mira," Casper said softly, waking her with the old signal — three taps on the bench wood. "Wake your father. Captain Rehn has found the chapel."
