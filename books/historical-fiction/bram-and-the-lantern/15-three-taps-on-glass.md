## 15. Three Taps on Glass

The chapel at Vosk Hollow had no roof worth the name and a bell that had been sold to the foundry two wars ago, but it had walls on three sides, and after nine days of running, three walls felt like wealth.

Tam sat with his back against the cold stone and watched Casper Okonkwo do the thing that had first made Tam trust him, back when Tam had still been pretending not to admire anyone: he counted.

Casper counted the way other men prayed. Coins out of the purse and back in. Rounds left in Mira's little pistol. The knots in the rope coiled by the door. Now he counted the distance to the river road on his fingers, murmuring, and Tam knew without asking that the numbers were bad. The numbers had been bad since the ferry. They got worse every hour Rehn's riders stayed behind them like weather rolling east.

Mira slept sitting up, her father's good coat draped over her shoulders, her chin dropped to her chest. Even asleep her hand was closed around the third dispatch — the unopened one — as if sleep itself might loosen her grip and let the state steal it back. Her father dozed against the wall beside her, breath whistling through the splinted ribs, and every fourth or fifth breath caught and hitched and made Tam flinch though the old man never woke.

"Tam."

Casper's voice, low. Not the teacher-voice. The courier-voice, the one that meant: *listen, because I will only say this once.*

Tam crossed the nave, stepping over fallen plaster. Outside, through the empty window arches, the marsh lay flat and silver-grey under a rising moon — miles of reed and black water and the drowned meadows where the river forgot which side it belonged to. Somewhere out there, lantern light moved. Two points of it, maybe three. Patient. Spreading.

"They'll have the ford watched by morning," Casper said. "And they'll have men walking the levee path. Which means by tomorrow we're done."

"We could go north around—"

"North is where she wants us. North is where the warrant gets delivered." He said *the warrant* the way he'd started saying it three days ago, flat and careful, like a man handling glass with someone else's blood on it. "No. She follows the trail. So the trail has to stop being ours."

Tam looked at him for a long moment before the shape of it assembled itself in his chest, cold and complete. "You want someone to be seen."

"I want someone to be *found*," Casper said. "Found carrying this." He touched his breast pocket, where rode the opened death warrant — Casper's own name signed in advance, the paper Tam had held in his hands in the relay station stable while the ink seemed to crawl. "She has to believe the courier she wants is in front of her, going the wrong way, alone. Alone is what makes it true. A decoy with companions is a trap; a decoy alone is just a fool, and fools are believable."

"And then you cross south with Mira and her father."

"With the dispatch. Get to what's left of the assembly. File the exoneration before the clerk packs his chairs onto a barge." Casper rubbed his eyes with thumb and forefinger, and for a moment he looked every one of his years and several he hadn't earned yet. "It has to be someone who knows my signals. Someone who can read the trail signs I leave and counterfeit them well enough to pass inspection by *Ilsa Rehn*, who taught half of them to me."

The silence stretched. In it, Tam heard everything Casper wasn't saying, arranged with a courier's economy: *someone young, someone fast in the reeds, someone whose loss won't end the mission.*

Someone he trained.

"No," Tam said.

"It isn't a request."

"You're right. It isn't." Tam heard his own voice come out steady, and was briefly astonished at himself, the way you're astonished to find your hand doesn't shake when you expected it to. "I'm telling you how it's going to be. You taught me the signals. All of them. Including the ones you didn't teach me on purpose — the ones you thought I hadn't noticed you using. The double-knot on the left rein that means *pursued, don't follow*. The chalk mark under the bridge rail. Three taps on glass." He swallowed. "You think I spent eight months riding behind you learning how to carry mail?"

Something moved in Casper's face — pride and grief fighting for the same ground and both losing to fear.

"Tam—"

"If I say no, you'll do it yourself, or you'll send Mira, or you'll stand in the marsh until they come and then it all dies here and so does everyone I—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "You promised them. Seven years ago. A promise isn't something you keep when it's cheap. I've read enough of your mail by now to know that much."

"That's mutiny," Casper said, and there was almost a smile in it, broken at one corner.

"It's the last lesson," Tam said. "You always said the republic would ask a courier to spend himself someday. That the whole craft was deciding, beforehand, whether the answer would be yes." He held out his hand for the warrant. "So. Decide beforehand. I decided."

* * *

They worked through the small hours by shuttered lantern light, and it was the best work Tam had ever done, because it was the first work he'd ever chosen.

Casper emptied his saddlebag of everything that could survive capture without killing them: the codebook went into Mira's pack, wrapped in oilcloth; the coin purse split, the heavier share sewn into Tam's coat lining because a captured courier with money looked like a courier still carrying something worth buying his silence about. Casper wrote nothing — nothing written could hang anyone — but he talked Tam through the route twice, three times, until Tam could walk it blind: the fisherman's stile at the old eel-weir, the reed channel that ran false toward the flooded quarry, the drainage culvert under the levee where a man could lie in two feet of icy water and watch riders pass close enough to count their buttons.

"When she takes you," Casper said, and didn't soften it, because softening it would have insulted them both, "she'll interrogate you. She's good at it. Better than good. She'll offer you things. She'll be kind. The kindness is the weapon — remember that kindness is the weapon, and it stops working." His jaw worked. "Give her the truth. Only the truth. Tell her you found me at the chapel, that we quarreled, that you took the warrant and ran south on your own because you believed the mission mattered more than the man. Every word true. A lie needs invention, and invention takes time you won't have. Truth under pressure holds its shape."

"And if she asks about you and Mira—"

"She knows already. She's known since Halven. Let her know." Casper gripped Tam's shoulder, hard, the grip saying the rest. "One more thing. If it goes wrong — if there's a moment where it's the warrant or your life — you give up the warrant. You hear me? Paper burns. It's the one thing I know for certain about paper."

"Yes, sir."

"Don't *sir* me. I burned my own orders. I'm the least 'sir' in Vessany." But his voice cracked on it, and he turned away and busied his hands with the saddle girth that needed no touching.

Mira came to Tam last, while the sky was still the grey of old ash. She pressed something into his palm and folded his fingers over it: the brass lantern charm, small and warm from her skin, the one Casper had carried seven years — returned to her at Halven, given by her now to him.

"Lantern-keepers lend," she said. "We don't give. You bring it back. That's the whole religion, right there. Light goes out, light comes home."

"I'm not good at returning things."

"Then get better." She kissed his cheek, quick and fierce, the sister he'd never had and hadn't asked for. "Three taps means safe harbor. Whatever they do to you — however long — somewhere a lamp is lit and we are waiting for the tapping. Don't you dare make us wait past spring."

* * *

He went out with the first true light, when the marsh was all silver fog and the reeds stood like ten thousand sentries holding their breath.

The plan was simple, and simplicity was its armor. He would ride the towpath south along the canal — visible, deliberate, a man in a hurry making no effort to hide — wearing Casper's spare greatcoat, which hung off him like sailcloth but carried the smell of the road and of Casper himself, tobacco and horse and leather. At the second lock he would "discover" that he was followed, cut across the open meadow toward the reed channels, and lead whoever chased him on a long, looping dance westward, away from the southern crossing, deeper into the drowned country, until the pursuit was committed and the day was gone.

At the lock, exactly as scripted, he heard the hoofbeats.

He made himself fumble — made the panic look real, which was easy, because some of it was real — wheeled the horse too sharply, splashed through the shallows, and drove out across the meadow with the fog tearing behind him. Shouts. A whistle, two notes, the signal-repeater Rehn used, and Tam's blood went cold and hot at once: *she's here herself.* Then he was into the reeds and the world narrowed to green walls and black water and the horse's laboring breath, and there was no time left to be afraid, only to ride.

He gave them four hours of it. He counted, because counting was Casper's prayer and today it was his. Four hours of false channels and dead ends, doubling back along the culvert, leaving trail marks copied from memory — a knot here, a chalk dash there, each one a sentence in a language only couriers spoke: *alone, southbound, carrying it, don't follow, pursued.* He left them generous and legible. He wanted them read.

The mistake, when it came, was water.

He'd planned to lose the horse at the quarry flood and take to the footpaths on foot, but the mare foundered in the peat at the wrong moment, screaming, and the splash of it carried further than any bell, and suddenly the reeds were full of men. Not many. Four, moving in a spread, unhurried, professionals closing a net that had already closed. Tam slipped off the drowning mare's back — forgive me, forgive me — and ran low along the dike with his boots sucking at the mud, and for fifty yards, a hundred, he thought he might actually—

A boot caught his ankle. The world rotated. Cold water, reeds in his mouth, a knee between his shoulders pressing him down into the marsh until the water of the ditch and the water from his eyes made one thing.

"Courier," said a voice above him, bored. "Got the coat. Got a packet in the lining. It's the Okonkwo kid, Lieutenant — or it wants to be."

They hauled him upright and searched him with brisk, impersonal hands, and Tam did the hardest thing he had ever done, harder than the riding, harder than the falling: he let his face collapse into terror and guilt and youth all at once, and began, stammering, to tell the truth.

"Casper — Captain Okonkwo — he's at the chapel at Vosk Hollow, he — we quarreled, he wanted to keep going north, I said the mission mattered more than — I took it, I took the warrant, I was taking it south to the assembly, please, I never—"

He watched the words land. Watched one of the soldiers turn and put two fingers to his lips and blow the double note westward, calling the rest of the net away from the crossing Tam had bled four hours to protect. Watched, through swelling eyes, the fog swallow them, heading the wrong way entirely.

*It worked,* he thought, absurdly, kneeling in the marsh with his hands bound. *It worked.* And beneath the triumph, cold and rising like the water in his boots, the other knowledge: *now comes the part Casper couldn't teach.*

* * *

Captain Ilsa Rehn arrived an hour later, riding through the reeds as if the marsh owed her money.

Tam had imagined this meeting a dozen times since the relay station, and every imagining had been wrong. He had pictured hardness. What he got was attention — total, patient, unnerving attention, the kind a master jeweler gives a stone she suspects is worth cutting. She dismounted, crouched to his level, and studied his face in the grey light for a long moment before she said anything at all.

"The Okonkwo boy," she said. "Tam. He called you Tam, I think, before he remembered to call you prisoner." She reached out and straightened his collar, a small domestic gesture that was somehow worse than a blow. "He taught you signals."

"Yes."

"Which ones?"

"All of them. Even the ones he didn't mean to teach."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, more like recognition, like a woman passing her own reflection in a window. "Yes," she said softly. "He would have. He was the same, at your age. Noticed everything, filed it, waited." She rose. "Where is he, Tam?"

"Vosk Hollow. The chapel. We quarreled and I ran." The truth, shaped like surrender. "You'll find the trail marks. He leaves them clean. You taught him half of them yourself — he says so."

For a moment she said nothing, and the marsh ticked and settled around them, and Tam understood that he was looking at the person in all of Vessany best equipped to hear exactly the truth he was telling and refuse it anyway.

"Bind his wrists in front," she told the soldiers. "Get him dry and warm. He rides with me." And then, to Tam, quietly, as they lifted him into the saddle: "You're a bad liar, boy. Fortunately for you, everything you said appears to be true. That's rare. Hold onto it. In the days coming, it will be the only thing you own."

They rode west, away from the crossing, into the fog, and Tam let his head hang and his body shake and saved, in the locked room behind his eyes, three things: the brass charm against his palm, the taste of marsh water, and the image — which he would need, he sensed, very soon — of a lamp lit in a window, and three taps on the glass, meaning *safe harbor*, meaning *hold on*, meaning *we are waiting for you past spring*.

Behind them, far to the south, unseen, three figures crossed the river in a leaking boat with the fate of a family sewn into an oilcloth wrap — and ahead of him, in the saddle ahead, rode the woman who had come to collect a debt the republic had signed in his name.

Tam closed his eyes, and began, silently, to practice being brave.
