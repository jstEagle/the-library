## 16. Interrogation by Lantern Light

The marsh fog came inland at dusk the way a debtor comes to a door—slowly, apologetically, and certain that eventually it would be let in. Captain Ilsa Rehn watched it swallow the tree line beyond the abandoned customs house and thought, not for the first time in forty years of service, that Vessany's borders had always looked like something the republic was ashamed of.

Inside, the customs house smelled of salt rot and mouse droppings and old paper. Her lieutenant, Dravic, had cleared the ground floor an hour before dusk, put two men on the water side, two on the road, and one—a boy barely older than their prisoner—on the stairs, where he now stood trying very hard not to be noticed listening. Rehn let him stand there. A listener learned things, and a learner could occasionally become useful, though she had long ago stopped expecting it.

On the table between herself and the prisoner sat a single lantern. It was not a soldier's lantern; it was a lantern-maker's lantern, brass-framed with glass panes etched faintly with a climbing vine, the kind of object that had a maker's name stamped somewhere on its base because its maker had been proud enough to sign his work. The boy had been carrying it wrapped in sacking when they took him in the reeds. He had not let go of it until Dravic pried it from his fingers, and even then he had watched it across the room like a man watching a hostage.

The boy himself sat bound at the wrists to a chair that had lost two rungs and most of its dignity. Tam, the records said. Tam of the capital orphanage, apprenticed to the courier service at eleven, field-commissioned at fourteen. Sixteen now, maybe seventeen—the war had made everyone's age uncertain, including, Rehn suspected, their own. He had mud to the thighs, a split lip from the taking, and eyes that refused to do what captured eyes were supposed to do. They did not dart. They did not plead. They measured her the way a young surveyor measures a river: respectfully, but with the clear intention of finding the crossing point.

Rehn sat down opposite him and poured tea from the pot Dravic had scavenged. Two cups. She pushed one across the table.

"Drink," she said. "You'll want your voice later."

"I'm not thirsty."

"You are. You've been running through marsh for six hours. Drink."

He considered this with the gravity of a man weighing a contract, then lifted the cup in both bound hands and drank. Rehn noted the technique without meaning to: he drank slowly, watching her over the rim, and set the cup down exactly where it had been, keeping the geometry of the table unchanged. A courier's habit. Know where everything is. Assume anything moved has been moved against you.

She had been that boy once. That was the trouble.

"Do you know why you're here, Tam?"

"Because I ran through your line instead of around it." He said it flatly, an inventory item. "I misjudged the light. Casper taught me better."

"Casper." She let the name sit between them like a coin placed on a counter. "Casper Okonkwo. First courier of the northern route. Decorated twice. Carried dispatch through the Kellin passes when no one else would go. My student before he was yours."

Something flickered in the boy's face—not surprise. Recalculation. He had known, then, or guessed. "He talks about you," Tam said. "Not much. But when he teaches signals, sometimes he stops in the middle and looks at the horizon like it owes him money."

Rehn almost smiled. She kept it off her mouth but felt it reach her eyes, and cursed herself for letting it. *Old woman,* she thought. *Forty years and one homesick apprentice undoes you.*

"He'd know," she said, "that running your line at dusk was a choice, not an error. You wanted to be taken."

The boy held her gaze. Outside, the fog pressed against the shutters and made them tick as the damp swelled the wood.

"Yes," he said finally. "I wanted them looking at me while Casper and Mira went south. That's what a decoy is. I'm not going to insult both of us by pretending otherwise."

*Mira.* So the Veren girl was with him. Rehn filed the name away and hated how carefully she filed it, how neatly her mind still worked in the service's grammar even now, with the service itself dissolving behind her like sugar in rain.

"And what," she said, "did you imagine happens to decoys who are taken?"

"I imagined," Tam said, "that you'd interrogate me by lantern light, because you always interrogate by lantern light—it's in the manual, section four, *conditions favorable to rapport*—and that while we talked, they'd be crossing the ford at Millstone. Which they will have done by now." He glanced at the lantern, and his expression changed by a degree, softened by something private. "It's a good lantern. Veren work. You can tell because the wick seat is brass, not iron—iron taints the flame. Mira's father says—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening, as if he'd caught sight of how much he was giving away and had grabbed it back just in time.

"Mira's father," Rehn repeated quietly. "Tomas Veren. Lantern-maker of Halven. Currently under sentence of death for treason against the Republic of Vessany."

"Currently under sentence of death," Tam said, "on charges dated nine days before the act they describe."

The room went very still. On the stairs, the listening boy stopped pretending to shift his weight.

Rehn kept her hands flat on the table, kept her breathing even, and understood that she had just heard the thing she had been refusing to hear since Halven. She had read those charges. Of course she had—she was Rehn; she read everything—and she had noted, in the cold back room of her mind where she kept the things that could not yet be afforded, that the dates sat wrong against each other like teeth out of alignment. She had told herself it was a clerical error. Clerks made errors. Clerks under bombardment made nothing but errors.

But the boy had said it aloud, and once a thing is said aloud it acquires weight, the way a rumor does, the way a body does when it has to be carried.

"That," she said, "is a serious accusation against the chancery of the republic."

"The republic is three weeks old from dying," Tam said. "Even a serious accusation against it can't hurt it now. But it can still hang a lantern-maker and his daughter." He leaned forward as far as his bonds allowed, and for the first time his voice cracked—not with fear, Rehn realized, but with the effort of holding conviction steady. "You've read the second dispatch. Don't lie to me, Captain; lying is my trade, not yours. You've read it. Tell me the seal was right."

She should end this. She should signal Dravic, have the boy removed, have the record show only what records needed to show. Instead she heard herself say: "The seal was right. Chancery matrix seven, third impression, correct wax compound."

"And the paper?"

"The paper was older than the seal by a month."

The words left her mouth in the flat tone she used for inventory, and only after they were gone did she hear what she had done: she had confirmed it. To a prisoner. On no record but the memory of a boy on a staircase who was not supposed to exist as a witness and now did.

Tam exhaled slowly. Then he did something Rehn would think about for the rest of her life, in whatever life the collapsing century allowed her. He brought his bound hands up onto the table, turned them palm-up, and worked two fingers into the cuff of his left sleeve.

Dravic moved at the edge of her vision. Rehn raised one hand and he stopped.

From the sleeve the boy drew a fold of paper, soft with sweat and handling, folded to the size of a playing card. He laid it on the table between the teapot and the lantern and slid it across with one finger.

"What is it?" Rehn asked, though she already knew. She had known since the moment she saw it, the way one knows a face across twenty years of distance.

"You know what it is," Tam said. "Open it."

She opened it.

The paper was heavy stock, chancery grade, the watermark a standing bear that the republic had used since before Rehn was born. The text was short. Warrants of final measure were always short; the state had never believed in wasting eloquence on killing its own. It named Casper Okonkwo, first courier of the northern route. It authorized his silencing upon confirmation of delivery, by any means, without tribunal, without record. And at the bottom, in the small dry hand of a chancery undersecretary whose penmanship Rehn had seen on a thousand orders over thirty years, was the signature and the date.

The date was four days before Casper Okonkwo had been given the mission at all.

She read it twice. She was thorough; she had always been thorough; thoroughness was the last virtue remaining to her. The signature was authentic—she would have staked her pension, her rank, the few years she had left, on the signature being authentic. The state had signed his death before it had given him his task. Before he had saddled his horse. Before he had carried the sealed dispatches north through a country sliding into ruin, faithfully, obediently, believing all the while that faith and obedience were the same thing.

*Like me,* she thought, with a clarity that felt like cold water finding a crack in a wall and beginning, patiently, to widen it.

"Where," she asked, and her voice was steady, which cost her more than trembling would have, "did you get this?"

"Casper opened the second dispatch at the relay station. Against every oath he had. He opened it because the family it condemned had once saved his life, and because the seal was wrong and he needed to know if the words were wrong too. This was in the same case. Wrapped around the execution order for the Verens like—" Tam searched for it. "Like a knife rolled in a cloth. He didn't find it until later. He kept it because it was proof. Not proof of treason. Proof of what sends treason orders." The boy's eyes were wet, but his voice held. "I took it from him the night before I ran your line. He doesn't know I have it. If you kill me, he'll never know what happened to it, and it won't matter anyway, because I memorized the date and the signature and I'll have told a dozen people by winter that the republic sentenced Casper Okonkwo to death four days before it trusted him with its secrets."

"You're threatening me," Rehn said, "with information."

"I'm offering it," Tam said. "There's a difference. Threats are for enemies. You're not my enemy, Captain. You're the person Casper became despite. I'd like to know what you become."

Outside, the fog had thickened until the lantern's light seemed the only solid thing in Vessany. Somewhere out on the water a bird cried once and was swallowed. Rehn looked at the warrant in her hands—at the standing-bear watermark, at the date sitting wrong against history like a bone broken and badly set—and she thought about her decades with an honesty she had permitted herself perhaps five times before.

She had joined the service at fifteen, in a republic that still believed its own stories. She had carried dispatch through two border wars and one civil unpleasantness that the histories would call something gentler. She had buried couriers, promoted couriers, trained couriers, and signed the transport papers for couriers who had to be silenced, and each time she had told herself the same sentence: *the state knows things I cannot.* It was a good sentence. It had carried her like a ferry across forty years of dark water. But a ferry, she understood now, looking at the boy across the table—young, frightened, unbroken, wearing her own certainty the way he wore his ruined coat—only runs while someone feeds the fire. The sentence had been running on her fuel all along. There had never been another firebox.

And the state those orders served—the state she had fed with her whole life—was already gone. What remained was a machine still executing its last instructions, stamping its seals on dead men's futures, hanging lantern-makers by momentum alone, the way a headless fowl runs.

"You knew," Tam said softly. It wasn't a question. "Before I said it. About the dates. You'd seen it."

"I notice things," Rehn said. "It's my trade too."

"And you were going to deliver the orders anyway."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because orders are the only thing holding the country together, and if men like me start choosing which ones to carry, there is no republic at all. Only armed men with opinions." She heard the words come out and recognized them—they were her own, worn smooth, recited so often they had ceased to mean anything, the way a word repeated a hundred times becomes only sound.

Tam looked at her for a long moment. Then he said, gently, with the terrible tactlessness of the young: "Captain. There's no republic. Look outside."

Rehn folded the warrant along its original creases. Once, twice, a third time, until it was a card again. She became aware that Dravic was watching her from across the room, and that the boy on the stairs had gone entirely silent, and that the decision she made in the next ten seconds would be the last decision of her career no matter which way she made it.

She did not decide. That was the truth she would never write in any report. She simply found the warrant still in her hand as she rose, and her coat still open, and her hand still moving, and by the time conscious thought caught up with the motion the folded paper was in her inner pocket, over her heart, where she had carried sealed orders for forty years.

"Interrogation concluded," she said, loud enough for the stairs. "Prisoner to be held. No one touches him. No one reports tonight's session upward—nothing travels until dawn dispatch, and I will write it myself." She paused at the doorway, her back to the boy, and looked down at the lantern on the table with its etched vines and its proud brass frame, signed inside the base by a maker in a town the republic had decided to bury.

*Tomas Veren says iron taints the flame.* The boy hadn't finished the sentence. Rehn found she wanted to know the rest of it, and understood that wanting to know was itself a kind of treachery, and that she had committed it the moment she pocketed the paper.

"Dawn dispatch," she said again, to no one, and went out into the fog.

Behind her, through the shutter cracks, she could hear the boy ask quietly if he might hold the lantern, and Dravic—foolish, decent Dravic—set it in his bound hands. Through the gap she watched the light steady in the boy's grip, clean and gold, untainted, and she stood in the marsh damp with her collar up and the warrant pressing its folded edges against her chest like a coal that would either warm her or burn through, and she did not yet know which, and morning, coming up the river road with the tide, would make her choose.

At Millstone ford, six hours ahead and moving south, a courier, a lantern-keeper's daughter, and a stolen lever against the state were already past her pickets.

By the time the fog lifted, Rehn was riding after them.
