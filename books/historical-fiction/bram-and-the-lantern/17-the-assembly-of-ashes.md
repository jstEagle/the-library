## 17. The Assembly of Ashes

Casper had imagined the provisional assembly a hundred times on the road south, and in every imagining it was loud. He had pictured queues of petitioners snaking through iron doors, clerks shouting names from ledgers, the desperate machinery of a government trying to outpace its own collapse. What he had not pictured was silence.

The Hall of Registers stood at the head of Vessany's second city the way a jawbone stands in the desert — an outline of what had been, stripped of everything soft. Its windows were dark. Its great doors hung open to the cold, and no guard stood beside them, only a wooden placard nailed crooked to the frame: *BY ORDER OF THE PROVISIONAL ASSEMBLY — SESSIONS SUSPENDED PENDING RESTORATION OF ORDER.*

*Pending restoration of order.* Casper read the words twice and felt something in his chest go very still and very cold. In his experience as a courier, phrases like that were written by men who knew there would be no restoration, who had already chosen their horses and their gold.

Beside him, Mira pulled her father's coat tighter around her shoulders. Seven days they had come — through the river valley, over the ridge road where Tam's tracks had gone cold behind them, down into the low country where every village flew a different flag or none at all. Seven days carrying the third dispatch against Casper's ribs like a coal that would not cool. And here, at the end of it, a locked hall and a placard.

"No," Mira said. It was not a question and not a plea, exactly. It was the word of someone refusing to accept arithmetic.

"The session could have moved." Casper heard how thin his own voice sounded. "There's a secondary chancery. The records office—"

"Casper." She said his name the way you set down something heavy. Then she walked past him, under the crooked placard, through the open doors, into the dark.

He followed. Of course he followed. He had been following the shape of this moment for seven years, since a lantern-maker's daughter had pressed a brass charm into his bandaged hand and told him to come back when the world made sense again.

Inside, the assembly hall was a wreck of abandonment done properly — not looted, but *left*. Benches askew. Papers drifted across the floor like grey snowfall, orders and motions and minutes of a government that had run out of time to matter. Dust hung in the shafts of light falling through high windows. At the far end, above the empty speaker's chair, the seal of the Republic of Vessany — the crossed quill and the mountain — had been prised from the wall. Only its bolt-holes remained, four small mouths in the plaster.

Casper stood among the papers of the dead republic and understood, with a courier's instinct for routes, that they had arrived too late. Not by hours. By history.

But then, from somewhere behind the hall — a scrape. A drawer closing. The ordinary sound of a human being still at work.

Mira heard it too. Her hand found his sleeve.

They followed the sound through a side door, down a corridor lit by one surviving lamp, into a records room stacked floor to ceiling with folios. And there, amid boxes tied with string and trunks stenciled with chancery marks, a small round man in a clerk's long coat was wrapping teacups in old writs of summons and packing them into a crate with the tenderness other men reserved for infants.

He looked up. He saw the strangers. He saw — Casper watched it happen — the courier's satchel, the road-worn state of them both, and he sighed the sigh of a man whose last professional duty had just found him anyway.

"Assembly's dissolved," the clerk said. "If you've come to file, you're eleven days late."

* * *

Mira had spent the whole journey south rehearsing this moment, and now the rehearsal was useless.

In her head she had stood before a tall magistrate in a bright chamber. She had spoken clearly — she had *practiced* speaking clearly, alone by the fire while Casper slept, working the words until they did not shake: *My name is Mira Veren, of Halven, daughter of Aldous Veren, lantern-maker, and I am here to answer charges that were never brought, so that they may be struck down before they are.* She had imagined the weight of the thing being lifted. That was what filing meant, wasn't it? You gave the paper to the proper person, and the proper person made the paper stop being true.

Instead there was this man, this grey little clerk with flour-white hands and teacups, in a room that smelled of dust and lamp oil and surrender.

"Eleven days," she said.

"Twelve, if we count today, which I'd rather not." He set a teacup into the straw. "The assembly voted itself out of existence on the ninth. Some of them went east to Karst. Two, I'm told, went further than east." He almost smiled; it didn't take. "I'm Hobb. Under-clerk of registers. Which makes me, at present, the entire continuing operations of the chancery of Vessany."

"You can't be," Mira said, and hated the crack in it. "There has to be someone with a stamp."

"Oh, I have a stamp." Hobb held up an ink-stained thumb as though it were the stamp itself. "It's in this crate, wrapped in a writ of habeas corpus, which I chose for padding because nobody's used one in months." He looked at them properly then, and Mira saw the moment his clerk's eye caught on details: her father's coat too big for her, the bruise-yellow of exhaustion under her eyes, Casper's satchel worn crosswise the way couriers wore them. His face changed. "You're Veren," he said quietly. "Aren't you. From Halven."

The name landed in the room like a dropped glass. Mira felt Casper go taut beside her.

"How do you know that name?" Casper asked, and his voice had gone flat and careful, the voice she had learned meant danger.

"Because I filed the original complaint," Hobb said. He wiped his hands on his coat, a gesture that seemed to be about wanting to touch nothing, including himself. "Eight months ago. Anonymous deposition, treasonous correspondence with foreign agents, the Veren family of Halven. It came through my desk with a priority seal and went up with a priority seal, and I remember thinking the dates were tidy. Too tidy. Clerks notice things like that. We're not supposed to say so." He looked at the crate, at his teacups, at the door. "Sit down. Both of you. There's tea, if you don't mind drinking it from a packing box."

They sat. Mira's hands would not stay still in her lap; she pressed them flat against her knees.

"Tell me what you have," Hobb said to Casper. "All of it. Quickly, because I leave tonight, and after tonight there is no chancery, no register, and no record. After tonight your family is just a name on a list in a burning country, and lists burn very easily."

* * *

Casper laid it out the way he had been trained to lay out anything: facts first, in order, without ornament.

The first dispatch — codes and route instructions, delivered north as ordered, meaningless now. The second — opened against every oath in his body, and he watched Hobb's face as he described it: the execution order against the Veren family, the fabricated evidence of foreign correspondence, and the date. The date signed and sealed a full month before the crime it answered.

"Pre-written," Hobb murmured. He had stopped packing. His hands rested on the crate's edge, quite still. "History drafted in advance and waiting for events to catch up. That's not forgery, exactly. That's architecture."

"And the third." Casper drew it out of the inner pocket where it had lived against his skin since the chapel. Still sealed. Still bearing the chancery seal — the true one, he believed, though he no longer knew what belief was worth on wax. "I haven't opened it. I was told it contained verification credentials for the northern command. I no longer believe that either."

Hobb looked at the sealed packet for a long moment. "No," he said. "I don't imagine you do."

"There's more." Casper reached past it, into the satchel's deepest fold, and brought out the thing they had risked everything to carry south: the second dispatch itself, the original, smuggled out of Halven in the false bottom beneath the codebook. He unfolded it on the clerk's table and weighted the corners with two teacups, absurdly, and Hobb leaned over it the way a physician leans over a wound.

For a while the only sounds were the lamp's faint hiss and the distant wind worrying the broken windows of the hall.

"This is the master," Hobb said at last. His voice had changed — thinner, careful, the voice of a man handling something that could detonate. "Not a copy. The ink distribution, the strike-throughs, the marginal corrections in a second hand — this came from the office that wrote it. Do you understand what this is worth?"

"I know what it cost," Mira said.

Casper heard it and did not look at her, because if he looked at her he would lose the thread. The workshop boarded. Her father's leg, broken in the arrest attempt, healing crooked in a cellar that smelled of lamp oil and wet stone. Tam somewhere behind them in the marsh country, captured or worse, and Rehn somewhere ahead. Everything they had spent and might yet spend, all of it banked against this one sheet of paper.

"It proves the charges were forged," Casper said. "Filed before the fact, by an office with authority to make the accusation stick. If there is still any institution in this country capable of receiving that proof, the Veren family walks free and the men who wrote this hang. That was the bargain I made with myself on the road. File the truth, and the truth does the rest."

"And if there isn't?" Hobb asked gently. "Such an institution, I mean. Because I have a stamp and a crate of teacups, friend, and between us we constitute the law of Vessany."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them a shutter banged, once, twice, and settled.

"Then I'll make you a different bargain," Casper said, and heard himself speak in a voice he did not recognize — not the courier's flat professional calm, but something rawer, older, the voice of the wounded boy who had once been hidden in a cellar much like Mira's father's. "You're leaving tonight. East, I'd guess — everyone with sense is going east. Take the dispatch with you. The original. Wherever the chancery reconstitutes itself — Karst, across the border, in exile, in ten years or twenty — file it there. Enter it in whatever register survives. Make the exoneration *exist*, even if I never see it done."

"And you?" Hobb asked. "What do you carry then?"

"Nothing," Casper said. "That's the point. A courier delivers and lets go. I've finally learned the lesson."

"No." Mira's voice cut across the room, sharp as flint on steel.

* * *

Mira had listened to them barter her family like two merchants haggling over wool, and something in her had risen up and refused.

She stood. Her knees ached from the road and her hands shook and none of it mattered; she had spent eight months learning that fear was a country you could live in and still plant things.

"No," she said again. "You don't get to carry it away and file it someday, somewhere safe, when someone else has cleaned up the mess. My father is lying in a cellar in Halven with a leg that sets wrong. The soldiers called us spies in front of our neighbors. Our name is on a list." She pointed at the dispatch, at the neat, bureaucratic, murderous handwriting of it. "*That* list. And this man—" she turned on Hobb, and found to her surprise that she was not angry at him, only desperate, and that desperation burned cleaner, "—this man is the last person in this country with a stamp and a conscience, and you want to give him the proof and send him off to safety with our lives in his crate between the teacups?"

"Mira," Casper started.

"They'll kill us anyway," she said. "Don't you see? If the order exists and the proof disappears into an archive, Captain Rehn doesn't need the archive. She needs the order, and she has it memorized by now, I promise you. Filing it later saves our *names*. It doesn't save *us*." Her voice broke and she let it. "I didn't walk two hundred miles so that justice could happen eventually, in another country, to people who used to be us."

Hobb was watching her the way clerks watch documents — reading, entirely, without interruption. Then he looked at Casper.

"She's right," he said. "You know she's right. An exoneration filed in exile is a gravestone with nice lettering." He straightened, and something in his round tired face shifted — the resignation going out of it, replaced by the peculiar fierce alertness Mira had seen on her father's face when a piece of work was going well. "But here is what neither of you knows, because you've been running instead of sitting in rooms where decisions get made. The assembly dissolved. The army is fragmenting. But Captain Rehn's chain of command runs through the Office of Internal Security, and the Office of Internal Security still answers, on paper, to a Director who has not yet fled — because fleeing requires admitting the republic is dead, and that man would rather die than admit anything. He is still in this city. He still holds courts-martial. He still, God help us, signs things."

"You're saying there's someone above Rehn," Casper said slowly.

"I'm saying the machine has a head left, and a head can be shown a document." Hobb tapped the forged dispatch with one white finger. "This, presented openly, publicly, in front of witnesses and soldiers who will all be looking for someone to blame when this country finishes falling apart — this doesn't just exonerate your family, girl. It indicts the office that forged it. It gives every officer under that roof a reason to step back and let the blame fall upward. Men protect themselves faster than they obey orders. Always. I've watched it for thirty years."

"And if it fails?" Casper asked quietly.

"Then you'll be dead," Hobb said, without cruelty, "and I'll still be holding the only copy, and I'll file it wherever the world lets me. That's the bargain I can offer. Both halves of it."

The lamp flickered. Outside, far off, a bell rang the hour — four slow strokes rolling across a city that had stopped keeping appointments with itself.

Mira looked at Casper. He was pale, and there was a day's beard on him, and his thumb kept moving over the brass charm in his pocket the way it did when he was afraid and would not say so. Seven years ago a frightened boy had knocked on her family's door with snow in his wounds, and her father had opened it, and that had been the whole of their fortune ever since: that doors get opened, that strangers get sheltered, that promises get kept past all sense.

"Well?" she said to him. "You heard the man. He can't file it until tomorrow. We hold it tonight."

Casper folded the dispatch along its old creases, slowly, and put it back against his ribs where it belonged.

"Then we need a place to sleep," he said, "and by morning, a way into the Office of Internal Security."

At which point the clerk of the dying republic began, quite unexpectedly, to laugh — and then, just as unexpectedly, to cry, and reached for a third teacup to wrap, his hands no longer steady at all.

"Internal Security," he managed. "Oh, courier. You'll need more than luck for that." He wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand. "You'll need a guide. And the only man who ever walked in that building unwelcome and walked out alive is currently hiding in my cellar, pretending to be my luggage."

Through the floorboards, from below, came the unmistakable sound of someone knocking — three slow taps, patient, asking to come up.
