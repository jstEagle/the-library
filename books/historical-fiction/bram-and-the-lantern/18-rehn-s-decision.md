## 18. Rehn's Decision

The order came at dawn, and it came the way all the worst orders had come in Ilsa Rehn's thirty-one years of service: verbally, from a man who would not meet her eyes.

Lieutenant Dravec stood in the doorway of her billet in the occupied quarter of Halven, his breath steaming, his hand still raised from where he'd knocked — a boyish gesture, apologetic, as though the words he carried could be softened by posture. He was twenty-six. He had been a child when Rehn had already been carrying dispatches through the Vessic passes in winter, and now he stood before her reciting a sentence she had known was coming since the marshes, since Tam, since the moment the trail south went cold and stayed cold until the reports began arriving from Kesselmark.

"Execute the courier Okonkwo on sight," Dravec said. "No arrest. No tribunal. The provisional assembly has been dissolved; there is no authority left to appeal to, and the Directorate considers him compromised."

"Compromised," Rehn repeated.

"Yes, Captain."

"By what?"

Dravec's jaw worked. He was a careful man, careful the way young officers are careful — not out of conscience but out of ambition, and ambition taught a man to hear which questions were traps. "I wasn't given particulars, Captain. Only that the courier opened sealed dispatches and is traveling with persons under charge of treason."

Persons under charge of treason. The Veren family. Rehn turned away from him and looked out the window at the street below, where frost had silvered the cobbles and two of Dravec's soldiers were warming themselves at a brazier, their rifles propped against the wall like tired men leaning on one another. Somewhere down that street, behind boarded windows, a town held its breath. Halven. She had read its name on a dozen manifests over the years — lanterns, mostly, and lamp oil, and once, memorably, an invoice for eleven hundred glass signal-lenses ordered by the Ministry of Ways for the northern relay towers. She remembered thinking, at the time, that it was a town that made light its trade, and wondering idly what that did to a place. Whether a town like that learned to see clearly or only learned to be seen.

She had not wondered long. There had always been another manifest, another road, another winter.

"You have my orders," she said.

"I do, Captain."

"Then go and see to your men. We ride within the hour." She paused, and because she was who she was — because the machine had ground into her, over three decades, a habit of precision that no collapse of states could wear smooth — she added: "On sight, Lieutenant, means on sight. But if the courier surrenders, you will report to me before you fire. That is not an amendment to the order. That is how the order will be carried out."

Dravec hesitated. She saw the calculation move behind his eyes like a fish under ice — the temptation to simply obey, simply shoot, simply be done with a man whose reputation made every officer who served near him feel slightly amateur. Then the calculation finished, and he saluted. "Yes, Captain."

He left. Rehn stood alone in the billet with the dawn coming gray through the window, and she took from inside her coat the folded paper she had been carrying for four days, and she unfolded it again, and read it again, as she had read it every morning and every night since the marshes.

It was not an official document. It was a scrap of cheap paper, water-stained, bearing a child's careful hand. Tam's hand. He had pressed it into the pocket of her greatcoat when she'd found him — half-frozen, fevered, delivered to her by marsh fishermen who wanted paying in coin she didn't have — and he had pressed it there deliberately, she understood now, so that she would find it later, alone, without witnesses. It said:

*Captain — they told me to watch him. They never told me why. Now I know why. Read the second dispatch before you carry out whatever you've been sent to do. Please. You taught me the seals. You'll see it. — T.*

*You taught me the seals.* Four days, and those five words had done more damage to her than any bullet fired by any enemy in thirty-one years.

* * *

She rode south with twelve men and a lieutenant who watched her too closely, through a country coming apart at its seams like wet rope.

Vessany was dying. Everyone knew it; the knowing had stopped being tragic somewhere around midsummer and become merely logistical. On the road to Kesselmark they passed columns of refugees moving the other way, and then columns of deserters moving the same way Rehn was going, and then a village where the church bell had been taken down and buried — she saw the fresh earth in the churchyard, the rope still hanging from the empty tower, swaying — because the villagers had decided that if the war wanted their bell for cannon metal, the war could dig for it. There was a kind of logic in it that Rehn almost admired. A people learning, too late, that everything the state asked them to give, the state intended to spend.

At noon they passed a relay station, abandoned. At dusk they passed a gibbet with nobody on it and a crowd around it anyway, standing in silence, looking up at the empty beam as though waiting. Rehn did not ask what they waited for. She knew. They waited for the republic to finish dying so they could begin arguing about who had killed it.

And through all of it, the paper in her coat, and the words on it, and beneath the words the thing she had not yet permitted herself to think, circling like a hawk she refused to look at directly.

Because here was the truth she had kept from herself since the marshes: she had already read the second dispatch. Not the dispatch itself — Casper Okonkwo had that, or had destroyed it, or had used it to bargain with someone — but the copy. The Directorate kept copies. Copies were how the machine remembered itself, and Rehn had spent enough years inside the machine to know where its memory lived. Two nights ago, in Halven, while Dravec drank himself stupid in the officers' mess, she had sat in the records annex with a lamp and a forged requisition of her own making — oh, the irony of it, the bitter comedy — and she had pulled the file on the Veren case.

She had read the charges. Espionage on behalf of foreign powers. Concealment of enemy couriers. Transmission of military intelligence. The evidence was a ledger, intercepted correspondence, and the sworn statement of a neighbor. And she had read it the way she had been trained to read everything, the way she had trained Tam to read everything — seal first, then paper, then ink, then hand, and only last of all the words, because the words were the easiest thing to lie with and the hardest thing to check.

The seal on the copied warrant was genuine. She verified it against the chancery register twice.

But the paper — the paper bothered her. The warrant was dated the ninth of Frostmoon. The alleged espionage, per the charges, had occurred across the whole of that same month, culminating in acts described on the twenty-third. Yet the sworn statement of the neighbor was itself dated the eleventh, and it referred, in past tense, to acts the warrant claimed were discovered only after the twentieth. The statement knew things before they happened. Someone had written history backward. Someone had built the gallows first and gone looking afterward for a reason to use it.

Or — and this was the thought she had set down like a hot iron, and picked up again, and set down again, four times now — someone in the chancery had prepared the verdict before the crime, because the verdict had been decided higher up, and the evidence was merely the paperwork that verdict required. She had seen that done before. Twice. Once in the western purges, when she was a lieutenant herself, and once — the memory arrived uninvited, cold as well water — once to a man named Aldous Brenner, a customs officer in Ostmere, whom she had personally arrested on charges she had not examined because examining charges had not been her function, and whom she had later learned, years later, from a drunken archivist, had been innocent of every particular except one: he had annoyed a director.

She had carried that knowledge for nineteen years the way you carry a stone in a boot — aware of it, accommodating it, telling yourself the boot still fits.

Tam's note said: *read the second dispatch.* She had not needed to. She had read its shadow. And the shadow was shaped wrong.

* * *

They reached Kesselmark on the third evening, and found the provisional assembly dissolved.

Not stormed, not burned — dissolved. That was somehow worse. The building stood open, its doors swinging in the wind off the river, and inside, clerks moved through the chambers with carts, packing ledgers into crates, stamping boxes, wrapping the seals of office in oilcloth like relics. The republic's last bureaucracy was performing its own autopsy, and doing it efficiently, because efficiency was the one virtue the machine had ever truly possessed.

Rehn left her men in the square under Dravec's command — *no one enters the assembly until I return*, and she put a weight on the words that even Dravec heard — and walked in through the doors alone.

The chamber where the assembly had met was stripped, the benches stacked, the Speaker's dais bare except for a single lamp some clerk had forgotten. Papers lay everywhere, drifted against the walls like snow, most of them blank — forms for proceedings that would never occur, agendas for sessions never called. She stood in the doorway a long moment, looking at all that unused future scattered on the floor.

"Captain Rehn."

The voice came from behind the dais. An old man rose from among the crates, dust on his sleeves, spectacles pushed up into white hair. She recognized him distantly — Clerk-First Emmerich Vasch, keeper of the assembly's record, a name she had seen on a thousand certified documents without ever once imagining the face attached to it. He looked exactly as his documents had always sounded: dry, precise, indestructible.

"Vasch," she said. "You're late leaving."

"The record does not leave itself, Captain. Someone must certify what happened here, or in fifty years no one will agree on anything." He studied her, and his eyes were sharper than his age suggested. "Though I begin to suspect certification is beyond us all. You're here for the courier."

It was not a question. News traveled faster than cavalry in a collapsing country; fear saw to that. "You've seen him, then."

"He was here yesterday." Vasch came around the dais, brushing dust from his hands. "Him and a woman — a Veren woman, lantern-maker's daughter, if the name means anything to you — and a girl carrying their papers. They came to file an exoneration and found nothing left to file it with." He smiled without humor. "The clerk who could have countersigned it was packing, same as me. Same as everyone."

"And?"

"And the courier bargained." Vasch's gaze drifted toward a crate at the edge of the chamber — sealed, stenciled with the chancery mark. "He had the original dispatch. The treason order against the Veren family. He offered it to me — offered to let the record keep it — in exchange for a letter of exoneration under my own seal, which is worth approximately nothing now, but which might be worth something later, when whoever comes next wants to know who was guilty and who was only convenient."

Rehn's mouth had gone dry. "Did you take it?"

"He showed it to me first. Of course he did. What good is evidence no one has read?" The old clerk lowered himself onto a crate, joints cracking, and looked up at her with terrible gentleness. "Captain, I have spent forty years certifying the state's paperwork. Forty years. Do you know what I have learned? That the paperwork is always true and the state is often lying. The document is genuine. The date is genuine. The seal is genuine. And it orders the death of a family for crimes committed after the document was signed." He spread his hands. "I certified it into the record. I gave the courier his letter. And then I sat here all night wondering whether I have spent my life as the state's servant or its accomplice, and whether there was ever a difference worth the word."

Rehn did not answer. Her hand had gone, without her willing it, to the inside of her coat, to the fold of paper there — not Tam's note but the other thing, the thing she had carried since the chapel at Ferren's Ford, where Tam, in custody, fever-bright and unbroken, had pressed it on her the way he'd pressed the first note: *they wrote this before they sent him out. Read your own orders, Captain. They signed his death the day they signed his commission.*

The death warrant for Casper Okonkwo, countersigned and sealed, dated six days before the courier had ever left the capital. Signed in advance. The mission had been designed, from its first hour, to end with its messenger silenced — the dispatches delivered, the family dead, the courier dead, the trail closed like a book. She had read it by lantern light in a marsh village while Tam watched her face across the table, and she had felt something break in her that had held for thirty-one years, and she had not yet allowed herself to learn its name.

Now, standing in the corpse of the assembly, she learned it.

It was not doubt. Doubt she could have managed; doubt was weather, and she had ridden through weather all her life. This was arithmetic. Simple, final arithmetic. A state that pre-signed the deaths of its own faithful couriers. A state that backdated the guilt of a family of lantern-makers. A state that sent a boy to spy on a man and left the boy to drown in marshes when he chose otherwise. Every piece fit. The machine worked perfectly. That was the horror of it — it had never malfunctioned, not once, not in all her years. It had done exactly what it was built to do, and what it was built to do was burn anyone, everyone, whenever burning became efficient. Aldous Brenner. The Verens. Okonkwo. Tam. Herself, eventually, when her own file grew inconvenient — hers was probably drafted already, sitting in some annex drawer, awaiting only the proper date.

She had thought she served the republic. She had thought the republic was a thing that existed apart from the machine, some spirit the machine merely served. But there was no spirit. There had never been a spirit. There was only the machine, and the machine had eaten the republic whole decades ago, and worn its skin, and she had polished that skin every morning of her adult life.

"Captain?" Vasch said quietly.

"They're not here anymore," she said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. Thirty-one years of level. "The courier. Where did they go?"

"The river gate. There's a boat — the ferryman's daughter arranged passage. They'll make the neutral bank by morning if the weather holds." He paused. "Your men are in the square, Captain. Your orders are what they are. I'm an old man with a crate of paper; I can't stop you, and I won't insult you by pretending otherwise. Whatever happens next belongs to you alone."

Rehn stood in the lamplight among the drifts of blank paper, and outside, in the square, she could hear Dravec's voice giving orders, bright and obedient and hollow, and she understood — with the clarity that comes only at the very bottom of a fall — that she was about to choose between two oaths she had spent her life believing were the same oath.

One oath was to the state. The other was to the truth the state had been founded to serve. She had carried both in the same breast for thirty-one years, believing them one chain, and now the links lay broken on the floor of the assembly hall, and she had to pick one up.

She thought of Tam in the cell at Ferren's Ford, offering her the death warrant with shaking hands and steady eyes, saying: *you taught me the seals. You taught me to look.* She thought of the buried bell in the churchyard, the rope still swinging in the empty tower. She thought of a town that made light its trade, and what that must have done to it — whether it had learned to see, or only learned to be seen — and she knew now. It had learned to see. It had seen clearly enough to shelter a wounded courier through a whole winter, knowing what it cost, and it was seeing clearly enough now to hang its bells rather than surrender them.

The machine had taught her everything except how to stop being its instrument. But a courier, she thought, is only ever the instrument of what she chooses to carry.

Rehn buttoned her coat over the two papers — the death warrant and the exoneration letter's twin proof, the forged charges' record — and walked out of the assembly into the freezing square, where twelve men turned at her approach and Lieutenant Dravec straightened, eager, his hand already near his pistol.

"Captain," he said. "The courier's been sighted at the river gate. Orders?"

Ilsa Rehn looked at the river, black under the last gray light, where a small boat was already pushing off from the landing. Then she looked at the young lieutenant, and drew a slow breath, and spoke the first order of her life that belonged entirely to her.

* * *

The boat slid out onto the darkening water, and on the far bank, a lantern appeared in a window — lit, steady, unmistakable. Three taps of light. Safe harbor.

Behind her, Dravec's voice cracked with disbelief: "Captain — what are you—"

"Arresting you, Lieutenant," Rehn said, and stepped between him and the river.
