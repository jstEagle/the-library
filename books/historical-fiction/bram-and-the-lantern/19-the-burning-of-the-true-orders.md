## 19. The Burning of the True Orders

The provisional assembly had been a granary once, and it still smelled like one — old wheat dust and cold stone, the ghost of a more honest trade. Casper stood in the middle of the hall with the forged dispatch in his hands and watched the last light of afternoon fall through broken roof tiles in long amber blades, and he thought: *this is where a republic comes to die. In a building that used to feed people.*

There were perhaps forty souls left in Vessany's government. A clerk with his coat already buttoned for travel. Two deputies arguing over a strongbox. A stenographer who had stopped taking notes an hour ago. And at the far end of the hall, behind a table that had clearly been dragged from a tavern, Undersecretary Voss — the man whose signature appeared on the exoneration template Mira had copied from her father's memory of chancery forms, the man whose authority was the last legal thread between the Veren family and a firing squad.

Mira stood beside him. Her hands were steady, but he could see the effort of that steadiness in the line of her jaw, in the way she kept her eyes fixed on Voss as if looking anywhere else would let her hope escape. Behind them, near the great doors they'd wedged open against being locked in, Tam should have been standing.

Tam was three days gone into the marshes with Rehn's riders on his heels, and every hour since had been an hour Casper spent not knowing whether the boy was alive.

*Three taps on glass,* Casper had told him, the first signal he'd ever taught him, back when Tam was all elbows and hunger and a memory like a steel trap. *It means safe harbor. It means you can come in.*

The boy had tapped twice on the chapel door before he'd run. Half a signal. Half a promise. Casper carried the missing third tap like a stone under his ribs.

"You understand what you're asking," Voss said now. He was a small man made smaller by circumstance, with ink-stained fingers and the haunted look of someone who had spent twenty years filing other people's decisions. "An exoneration filed by this office requires — required — countersignature from two deputies and a sealed warrant of review. There are no deputies to countersign. The seal was sent to the capital for recutting in autumn." He laughed, a dry, terrible sound. "The capital is burning, courier. The seal is probably ash."

"Then stamp it with whatever you have," Casper said.

"That makes it worthless."

"It makes it *true*," Mira said. Her voice carried down the length of the granary and came back off the stone. "Which is more than anyone can say for the orders you're still honoring."

Voss looked at her the way tired men look at fires they cannot put out. "Madam, if I file an improperly sealed exoneration and the military council learns of it—"

"The military council," Casper said quietly, "sent Captain Rehn to kill me. She has my death warrant signed in advance. Do you want to see it?"

He did not wait for an answer. He drew the third dispatch from inside his coat — opened now, these three days past, its wax cracked like old ice — and unfolded it on Voss's table. The undersecretary read it. Casper watched the man's face do what his own face must have done at the chapel: the slow collapse, feature by feature, of a structure built over decades.

"This is dated—" Voss began.

"Before the alleged treason. Yes. The charges against the Veren family were written before the crime existed. Someone in your chancery pre-authored history, Undersecretary. You're not fleeing a republic. You're fleeing an accomplice."

The argument stopped. That was the terrible thing Casper noticed — not that anyone was persuaded, but that everyone simply stopped pretending there was anything left to argue about. The two deputies closed their strongbox and sat down. The stenographer, after a long moment, picked up her pen again and began, for the first time all day, to actually write.

Voss took out a pen. His hand shook so badly he had to steady it with his other hand, and Mira reached across the table and held the paper flat while he wrote, and Casper thought: *this is what the end of a country looks like. Not cannon smoke. A clerk trying to remember how to do his job honestly.*

That was when the doors boomed.

Not knocked. Boomed — the flat concussion of a rifle butt against oak, once, twice, and then the doors themselves swinging inward because Tam had never gotten the chance to wedge them properly, or because wedge or no wedge, forty armed men make their own entrances.

They came in by squads. Casper counted without wanting to — six, twelve, eighteen — gray-coated soldiers of the Northern Command fanning along the walls with the bored efficiency of men who had done this too many times in too many towns. And at their center, walking with the unhurried stride of someone arriving at a meeting she'd scheduled herself, came Captain Ilsa Rehn.

Her coat was buttoned to the throat. Her boots left wet prints on the granary floor — marsh water; she'd come through the fens, which meant she'd come through where Tam had led her, which meant—

He looked for the boy among the soldiers and saw nothing, and the nothing was almost worse than blood would have been.

"Casper Okonkwo," Rehn said, and her voice filled the hall the way her voice had always filled rooms — not loud, just *certain*, a voice that had never once needed to ask whether it would be obeyed. "You've cost me three days, two horses, and one very stubborn boy."

"Is he alive?"

Rehn's eyes flickered. Something moved behind them, quick and buried, like a fish under ice. "Your messenger is in custody. Whether that's the same as alive depends on the next five minutes." She walked forward until she stood ten paces from the table, from Casper, from Mira, and her soldiers spread behind her like a closing hand. "I have orders regarding you. Verbal. From the military council. They're simple enough that even a courier could memorize them."

"I know what they are. I've read my own death warrant, Ilsa. It's a strange feeling, seeing your name signed by a man you've never met."

"It's a familiar feeling," Rehn said. "I read mine eleven years ago. Filed and forgotten, pending circumstances." She said it without self-pity, the way she said everything, and Casper understood suddenly that she was telling him this as a gift — the only kind of intimacy she knew how to offer. *We are the same thing, you and I. Instruments with expiration dates.* "Deliver the dispatches. Confirm receipt. Then stand aside."

"They're forged," Mira said. She stepped forward, putting herself half in front of Casper, and Casper felt something twist in his chest — seven years ago she'd been a girl on a lantern-lit stair, and now she was a woman placing her body between him and thirty rifles, and both of those women were braver than any soldier in this room. "The charges. My family never—"

"Madam Veren." Rehn inclined her head, precise, courteous. "I know."

The words dropped into the hall like stones into a well. Even the soldiers stirred. One of the officers near the door — a lieutenant with a scarred jaw and the hungry eyes of a man promoted by attrition — turned sharply. "Captain. With respect, the orders state—"

"The orders state many things, Lieutenant Brandt." Rehn did not raise her voice. She never had to. "I said I *know*. There's a difference between following orders and believing them, though I grant the distinction has become unfashionable."

Casper's heart was hammering against the dispatch folded inside his coat. He had rehearsed this moment on the road south, rehearsed it in the dark, rehearsed it lying awake listening to Mira breathe and her father cough, and every rehearsal ended differently. Now it was here, unscripted, and Rehn was standing in front of him offering him — what? A loophole? An out? *Confirm delivery and walk away, and no one dies today except the truth.*

"Then don't deliver them," Casper said.

"My duty is not negotiable."

"Neither is mine." He looked at her — really looked, the way he hadn't allowed himself since the relay station, since the seal that shouldn't have been, since the whole rotten architecture of his faith had begun to show its cracks. "Twenty-two years, Ilsa. Twenty-two years I carried whatever they put in the bag and asked nothing, because asking nothing was the price of belonging. I told myself the republic was bigger than its mistakes. I told myself the bag was heavier than my conscience, and that was *good*, that weight meant the work mattered." He drew the forged dispatch out and held it up, and his voice did not shake, and he was grateful for that, at least. "This weighs nothing. Did you know that? Paper and wax, and it weighs less than a lantern charm. But it killed six people in Kellin province last month, and it would have killed this entire family, and it was written before any of them did anything at all."

"Put it down, Casper."

"No." He turned to Voss. "Undersecretary. Is the exoneration finished?"

Voss lifted it with both hands, as though it might ignite prematurely. "Countersigned. Unsealed. Filed under protest, and under duress, which I will also be noting for the record."

"Noted," Casper said. "Now witness."

He crouched and pulled the small iron brazier from beside the table — the clerk's travel brazier, packed with charcoal for a journey that would never happen — and the coals still held a dull red life beneath their ash. And then, moving slowly, deliberately, so that every eye in the hall could follow every motion, he took the second dispatch from his coat. The execution order. The document that had ordered Mira's father shot, Mira hanged, the workshop burned, the neighbors who'd aided them imprisoned — all of it dated, all of it sealed, all of it *real* in every way that mattered except the truth.

"Casper." Rehn's voice, quieter now. Warning. Or plea. He genuinely could not tell, and he suspected she couldn't either.

"You gave me a choice once," he said. "First year. I misdelivered a dispatch to a tax office instead of a garrison, and you could have had me whipped. Instead you sat me down and you said: *a courier's word is the only currency this country mints that can't be counterfeited. Guard it.*" He met her eyes. "You taught me this, Ilsa. Whatever happens next, you taught me this."

He touched the corner of the dispatch to the coals.

The paper caught slowly, the way old documents do — reluctant, curling, the flame crawling up the margin like ivy climbing a wall. The wax bubbled and ran, and the chancery seal that had authorized murder softened into a black tear. Casper held it until the fire licked at his gloves, and then he set it whole into the brazier and watched the execution order of the Veren family become light and heat and a thin ribbon of ash rising toward the broken roof.

Nobody moved. Forty rifles pointed at a man warming his hands on a burning document, and nobody moved.

Then he took out the third dispatch. His own death warrant. He unfolded it and read it aloud — read it plainly, in a clear voice, the way he'd delivered a thousand dispatches to a thousand garrisons, because some things deserved the full ceremony of the service:

*"By order of the Military Council of the Republic of Vessany: upon confirmed delivery of the preceding documents, the bearer, Courier First Class Casper Okonkwo, is to be detained and executed under Article Twelve, provisions of state security. This warrant is issued in advance of the aforementioned delivery and requires no further authorization."*

He lowered the page. "Signed in advance," he said. "Before I ever opened it. Before I ever left the capital. The mission was always going to end here, with me, silenced, and none of you — none of *us* — were ever meant to come home." He fed it to the fire. "So. Delivery confirmed, Captain. Here is your confirmation."

The warrant burned. His own name curled and blackened and went up in sparks, and Casper Okonkwo felt lighter than he had in twenty-two years.

"Arrest him," Lieutenant Brandt said.

No one moved.

"Brandt." Rehn's voice had changed. Casper heard it change, heard the certain note go out of it and something else arrive in its place — something older and wearier and more human. "Stand down your squad."

"Captain, the council's orders—"

"The council's orders are ash, Lieutenant. Both sets of them. Look at the fire if you doubt me." She stepped forward, past Casper, past Mira, until she stood directly in front of Brandt, and the two of them regarded each other across a gulf that had opened in the space of a single minute. "I've served this republic for thirty-one years. I have executed men on thinner evidence than that paper, and slept afterward. But I have never once — not once — executed anyone on an order dated *before the crime*." She extended her hand, palm up. "Your copy of the council's instructions, please."

Brandt's jaw worked. "You can't—"

"I can, and I am. Because there is one more document in this room, and it isn't forged." Rehn drew from her breast pocket a folded, creased, much-handled piece of paper, and Casper recognized it with a jolt that went through him like cold water: the death warrant. Tam's copy — the one the boy had offered her in the marshes, the one she'd pocketed without committing either way. She'd carried it through the fens. She'd carried it here. Doubt planted in stone, and the stone had finally cracked. "Read it, Lieutenant. Read it and tell me whose machine we've been."

Brandt didn't take it. Brandt went for his sidearm instead — a fast, ugly movement, the movement of a man who has just realized the ground is shifting under him and means to be standing on the winning part when it settles.

He never cleared the holster. Rehn's hand closed on his wrist with the speed of thirty-one years of practice, twisted, and the pistol clattered to the stone floor and spun away across the granary tiles. Two of Brandt's own soldiers — after a heartbeat that lasted a year — stepped forward and took his arms.

"Article Twelve cuts both ways," Rehn said, almost gently. "Detain him. Log it under my authority. If the new government wants my head for it, they'll know where to find me — I'm apparently the last officer in Vessany who stays at her post."

The hall exhaled. Casper hadn't realized he'd been holding his breath until his lungs burned. Beside him, Mira made a small sound — not a sob, something before a sob — and her hand found his, and her grip was fierce enough to hurt, and he held on.

Rehn turned back to them. Up close, in the dying light, he could see what the years had done to her — the gray at her temples like frost, the fine web of lines around eyes that had watched too much. She looked at the brazier, where the last flakes of the warrants were guttering out.

"Delivery confirmed," she said. Her mouth did something that was almost a smile and almost grief. "Twenty-two years, Okonkwo, and the finest thing you ever delivered was nothing at all."

"There's one more item," Casper said. His voice came out rough. He took the exoneration letter from Voss's table — unsealed, imperfect, true — and held it out to her. "Filed under protest and duress. Countersigned by a clerk who's already packed. It wouldn't survive a court in the old republic." He paused. "But the old republic is in that brazier. Maybe the new one takes honesty instead of wax."

Rehn took the letter. She read it once, quickly, the way she'd read ten thousand dispatches, and then she did something Casper had never seen her do in all his years of service.

She signed it. Beneath Voss's trembling hand and the deputies' reluctant names, she wrote *Ilsa Rehn, Captain, Retired as of this hour* in a firm, unhesitating script, and handed it back.

"There," she said. "Sealed with a traitor's word. It's worth more than the chancery's ever was." She straightened her coat, squared her shoulders, and became once more the officer the whole road north had learned to fear — but her eyes, when they met Casper's, were the eyes of the woman who had once spared a young courier a whipping and told him to guard his word. "Get the Veren family home, Casper. Get yourself somewhere the new flags don't fly. And when they write about this winter — and they will, badly — make sure someone mentions the boy in the marshes."

"He's alive?"

"Broken wrist, mild frostbite, and a tongue that will be the death of me. He's in the wagon outside." She turned toward the doors, then paused. "He burned your wrapper for you, you know. Day one. I found the ashes in his kit. I've known since Halven what side he'd chosen." A beat. "It's the same side I apparently chose, in the end. Go figure."

She walked out into the blue evening with her soldiers falling in behind her and her lieutenant in irons, and the great doors swung slowly shut behind her, and the granary stood quiet in the ember-light with the smell of burnt paper hanging in the air like incense.

Mira was laughing and crying at once, quietly, helplessly, and Voss was saying something about needing to inventory the remaining files, and the stenographer was still writing, writing, writing, as if she could feel history passing through her pen and wanted to hold every drop of it.

Casper stood over the brazier a moment longer. In the ash, faint and fragile, the last recognizable fragment of the forged seal still glowed — a curl of black wax shaped, if you squinted, like a lantern.

Somewhere outside, a wagon door creaked, and a boy's voice, hoarse and indignant, demanded to know whether anyone had thought to save him any dinner.

* * *

They carried her father out into the evening air because he asked for it — asked with the imperious weakness of a man who had spent three weeks underground and would not spend another hour beneath a roof. They wrapped him in blankets and set the chair by the granary's eastern wall, where the last light came through the broken tiles, and Casper knelt beside him and pressed the exoneration letter into his thin, lantern-scarred hands.

Master Veren read it slowly, his lips moving, and when he finished he looked up at Casper with eyes that had gone pale as paper but were, underneath, exactly the eyes that had once studied a wounded stranger by lamplight and decided he was worth saving.

"You kept it," the old man whispered. "All these years. The promise."

"I kept it late."

"Lanterns are always lit late." He smiled, and closed his eyes, and held the letter against his chest the way a child holds a talisman. "Light it tonight, boy. Hang it in the window. Let the town see what it means again."

And that night, in the ruined workshop with its boarded windows and its smell of scorched wood and old metal, Casper climbed the ladder to the loft and hung the little brass lantern — Mira's lantern, the one traded and ransomed and carried across half a drowning country — in the window facing the square. He lit the wick with a spill from the brazier's surviving coals, and the flame caught, and threw its small gold circle out into the dark.

Below, in the square, people had begun to gather. Word traveled fast in a town with nothing left to lose. Casper watched from the window as faces tilted upward toward the light — soldiers without officers, neighbors without shops, a clerk still holding his packed bag — and one by one, hesitantly, they began to tap on the workshop door below.

Three taps. Then another fist, three taps. Then a dozen, overlapping, arrhythmic, unmistakable.

Safe harbor. Come in.

Mira stood beside him at the window, her shoulder warm against his, watching the lantern burn and the crowd grow. Down in the yard, propped against the doorframe with his wrist splinted and his face bruised purple-yellow, Tam was letting them in, one by one, wearing the expression of a gatekeeper who had decided the job suited him.

"Casper," Mira said softly. "What happens tomorrow?"

He watched the flame bend and steady, bend and steady, the oldest technology of hope there was.

"I don't know," he said. And for the first time in twenty-two years, the not-knowing felt like freedom.
