## 22. Bram and the Lantern

The road into Bram had changed its name twice since Tam last rode it, and both times it had kept its old shape.

That was the thing about border towns, he'd learned in his six years carrying for the Free Towns Compact: governments came and went like weather, but a road remembered where it was going. This one ran along the river's southern bank through alder country, muddy in the shoulders from spring thaw, and at its end — if the letters were right, and Mira Veren's letters were always right — there would be a workshop with smoke rising from its chimney and a lantern burning in the window.

He saw the lantern before he saw the town.

It hung on an iron bracket above a gate of new timber, and even in the flat grey light of late morning it burned. That was the signal. Three taps on glass meant *come in, you're safe*; a lit lantern in the window meant the same thing, older and simpler, a promise made to travelers before anyone had ever written a courier's code. Tam had carried dispatches through three borders and two changes of government since the republic finished dying, and he had learned that most signals were lies told efficiently. This one was not. It never went out. He'd asked once, by letter, whether it was expensive to keep oil in it all winter, and Mira had written back a single line: *We know what it cost to be without one.*

Now, reining up at the gate, Tam understood the line the way he hadn't as a nineteen-year-old apprentice standing in a marsh with soldiers closing in, holding a stranger's treason in his coat pocket. He understood almost everything differently now. That was what years did. They didn't make the old stories smaller; they made the vessel bigger around them.

He knocked. Not three taps — that was for glass, for someone hiding — just the flat knock of a courier announcing himself, and beneath it, out of habit he couldn't kill, the courier's rhythm: two long, one short. *Tam. Alone. Safe.*

Footsteps inside. Then the door opened, and Casper Okonkwo stood in it, and Tam felt the familiar lurch of time misbehaving.

Casper had gone grey at the temples — not the distinguished silver of portraits, but the working grey of a man who spent half his life at a glassblower's bench and the other half arguing with kilns. His hands were different too. They had been couriers' hands once, scarred across the knuckles, always half-curled around a strap or a rein. Now they were makers' hands, burn-spotted, steady, one finger permanently crooked from an old break Tam had heard about but never witnessed. The man himself had thickened slightly through the shoulders, the way men did when their work became lifting molten glass instead of outrunning cavalry, and there was something else Tam took a moment to name: Casper stood in doorways like a man who expected to keep living behind them. In the capital, six years ago, no one had stood like that.

"Two long, one short," Casper said, looking him over. "You're getting slow. I heard your horse before I heard your signal."

"You heard my *horse* because your dog announced me from the tree line like a town crier." Tam swung down and offered his hand, and Casper took it, and held it a moment longer than greeting required, which was its own kind of message. "The road's washed out past Ferren's Mill. Took me two days longer than the letter said."

"Mira guessed as much. She said if you weren't here by Thursday she'd assume the river ate you, and if you weren't here by Sunday she'd assume you'd finally taken a wife to avoid helping with the spring firing."

"And what does she assume now?"

"That you're unmarried and useless, and we're glad of it."

* * *

The workshop had been rebuilt twice, Tam gathered, once after the war and once after a fire four years back that Casper mentioned only in passing and Mira refused to mention at all. The new building was broader than the old Veren shop must have been — Tam had seen sketches of it, in the archive of recovered papers the Compact kept, the ones Casper had smuggled out folded against his chest while the republic's clerks were still packing to flee. The original workshop had been a lantern-maker's pride: tall windows, a drying loft, racks of finished lamps glowing like a shelf of captive suns. The rebuilt one kept the proportions but added a second wing, and when Tam walked in behind Casper he saw why.

They made more than lanterns now. Along the near wall hung signal lamps in the courier pattern — two-lens shutters, the whole vocabulary of light that Tam had learned as a boy and taught, in turn, to eleven apprentices of his own. But the far benches held domestic work: reading lamps, street lanterns for the new towns, a commission of great caged harbor lights stamped with the mark of some port city Tam didn't recognize. And in the center of the floor, dominating everything, stood the piece they were clearly firing for this spring: a chandelier, or something like one, a ring of iron a full arm-span across, fitted with sockets for twenty lamps, each socket crowned with a small brass fitting shaped — Tam leaned closer and felt his throat tighten — shaped like a tiny lantern charm. The same charm Casper had carried in his saddlebag through the last winter of the republic, the same one Mira had pressed back into his hand in the ruins of her father's workshop.

"It's for Halven," Casper said, watching him look. "The town council finally voted on it. Twenty lamps for the twenty families who stayed through the occupation. Mira's doing the glass. I'm doing the iron, which means I'm doing it wrong until she tells me otherwise."

"The charm fittings—"

"Council wanted the town crest." Casper shrugged, elaborately casual, which in Casper had always meant the opposite. "Mira overruled them."

From somewhere deeper in the house came a voice, unhurried and precise: "I can hear you describing me as a tyrant, Casper Okonkwo. Bring our guest in before he dies of politeness in my workshop."

Mira Veren — Mira Okonkwo now, though half the border towns still called her Veren and she had stopped correcting anyone — met them in the kitchen doorway with flour to her elbows and the expression of a woman who had been listening to every word. She was thirty-one and looked, Tam thought, exactly like the person she had been at twenty-four in a cellar under a ruined workshop: someone who had decided what she could survive and then survived considerably more. Her hair was pinned up carelessly; her eyes did the thing they always did, taking inventory of a visitor in one pass — health, exhaustion, news, danger — and filing it away.

"You're thin," she said. "You're always thin. Sit down."

"I brought—" Tam fumbled at his satchel, suddenly aware he'd rehearsed this on the road and lost all of it. "Reports first, I suppose. The Ferren crossing is passable but only for light riders. The Compact's ratified the postal union — all nine towns, Bram included, which means your relay rates drop by a third come summer. And there's a letter from the archivist in Vessany." He drew it out carefully, because he knew what it was. "About the trial records. The ones they found in the chancery cellar."

Mira took it but didn't open it. She looked at him instead, and something passed between them that had nothing to do with records — the old recognition, cellar-dark and lantern-bright, of two people who had each done an unforgivable sensible thing and lived to build something past it. "Later," she said. "You'll eat first. You'll eat first and you'll tell us about your apprentices, and *then* we will ruin a perfectly good evening with dead men's paperwork. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Tam said, and sat down at a table set for three without being told, because of course it was set for three. It had probably been set for three since the day his letter arrived.

* * *

Over supper — river fish, dark bread, preserves from a jar labeled in Mira's blunt hand *PLUMS, DO NOT TOUCH, CASPER* — Tam gave them the account he gave every year, and every year they listened as if it were new.

His eleven apprentices. The girl in Ferren who'd memorized the shutter codes faster than any student he'd had and was, he suspected, going to be better than him within two years. The boy from the marsh country who'd frozen on his first solo run and how Tam had ridden out at midnight, found him sheltering under a bridge with a torn dispatch case, and sat with him until dawn telling him about a winter when Tam himself had hidden in a root cellar with forged orders burning a hole in his conscience. The boy had asked what he'd done with the orders. Tam had told him the truth: he'd fed them to a stove, page by page, and watched a republic's signature curl into ash, and it had been the easiest hard thing he ever did.

"He asked if I was afraid," Tam said. "Of the consequences. And I told him — I don't know why I told him this — I told him fear isn't the thing to check. Loyalty is. Before every run, ask what you're loyal *to*. The route? The paymaster? Or the people at either end of it?" He turned his cup. "Then I realized I was quoting you. Word for word. You said it to me outside a relay station seven years ago and I've been repeating it ever since like it was mine."

Casper laughed — really laughed, head back — and Mira reached over and touched Tam's wrist, briefly, the way family did.

"You improved it," Casper said. "I said it bitterly. You say it like a lesson worth learning. That's the difference between surviving a thing and *teaching* it." He pointed his spoon at Tam. "Eleven apprentices. Gods. When I trained you, I mostly taught you how to lie to customs men and which bridges hold a gallop."

"Both still useful."

"Both still taught. Don't tell the Compact."

After supper Casper went out to bank the kiln — he claimed the night heat needed a particular touch that only he and Mira possessed, and Tam privately believed the particular touch was simply that neither of them trusted the other alone with it — and Mira and Tam cleared the dishes together in a companionable silence broken only once, when she said, without preamble:

"The archivist found the original warrant. Yours. Casper's." She dried a plate slowly. "Signed in advance, dated three days before we were ever accused of anything. It's going in the public record. There's talk of a monument."

"Don't let them."

"I know." A ghost of a smile. "He'll fight it. He fights every memorial. Says the republic built enough monuments to itself while it was alive." She set the plate down and looked out the window toward the glow of the kiln yard, where Casper moved among the shadows with a rake, tending coals. "But the warrant matters to me, Tam. Not as a monument. As a record. Somewhere it should say plainly what happened: that a courier opened mail he swore never to open, because the mail was a murder, and that a boy burned the evidence of his own orders to protect him. History keeps wanting to make you both saints. Saints are easy to dismiss. I'd rather it said you were frightened and tired and did the right thing anyway."

"That's not very heroic."

"No," Mira agreed. "It's true. We've found truth holds up better outdoors."

* * *

Late that evening, Tam walked the workshop alone.

He hadn't meant to snoop. But the drying loft ladder was unguarded, and the loft held the things that didn't fit downstairs — the archive of the rebuilt life, stacked in crates and hanging in oilcloth. Old tools marked with the Veren maker's stamp. A child's slate, puzzling, until he remembered hearing that Mira's younger brother had died in the occupation and felt the floor tilt gently under him. A courier's saddlebag, cracked leather, brass buckle green with age, and stitched inside the flap in faded thread, initials that weren't Casper's — a bag inherited, maybe, from some earlier rider, carried on through every journey since. People in the trade did that. They carried the dead forward.

And on a nail by the loft window, hung with the care reserved for relics: a brass lantern charm, small enough to close in a fist, polished bright by decades of handling.

Tam stood before it a long time.

Six years ago he had been a boy with a handler's whisper in his ear — *watch him, report everything* — and a choice in his hands that felt like it would crack him open. He had chosen wrong by every rule he'd been raised on and right by every rule that mattered, and the price had been a marsh, a cell, Rehn's lantern swinging over his face while she asked questions he answered with silence. He had never regretted it. But he had also never stopped understanding that belonging wasn't something the Compact issued with a commission and a badge. It was something you earned in cellars and stoves and ferry crossings, tap by tap, flame by flame — and once earned, someone had to keep it lit.

Below him, the workshop door opened. Casper came in from the kiln yard shaking rain off his collar, and Mira joined him from the house, and they stood together in the lamplight talking low about firing schedules and council politics and whether the plum trees had survived the frost, ordinary as breathing. Two people the republic had tried to erase, running a lamp shop in a town that had outlived it.

Tam reached out and touched the little brass charm, just barely, with one fingertip. Warm from the day. Bright from use.

Downstairs, Mira glanced up — he'd swear she sensed it, the way she always did — and called up through the floor, "There's a bed made in the east room. And Tam — the lantern stays lit tonight. It always does. But it's nice, sometimes, to have someone home to see it."

He stayed until morning, and then another morning, and helped with the spring firing, and when he finally rode out the lane on the third day, he did what every courier did leaving that house and what no other house on his routes required: he stopped at the gate, turned in his saddle, and looked back at the window.

The lantern burned. It would burn tomorrow and next week and through every winter the border towns could throw at it — a small stubborn light with a vocabulary everyone knew and no one needed to teach. *Safe harbor. Come in. You're kept.*

Tam raised two fingers to it, the old courier salute, and rode south with his satchel light and his heart full, already composing the letter he'd send his own students: *Come see, someday, what a promise looks like when it's kept for twenty years. Come see the lamp that never goes out.*
