## 1. The Weight of Paper

The rain came in off the ridgeline the way it always did at Ypres, sideways and grey, carrying the smell of chalk and burnt something — burnt earth, burnt timber, burnt men; after eight weeks Rafael Yarrow had stopped trying to separate them. It was all one smell. The war had a smell, and it lived in her hair no matter how often she washed with the soft soap that left her hands cracked to the wrist.

She stood in the doorway of Ward B — which had been a carpenter's workshop eleven months ago, before the front ate the village and spat out its beams — and counted what she could see of the world through the doorframe: four stretcher cases under the lean-to tarpaulin, one ambulance bogged to its axles in the lane, a sky the colour of old bandages, and beyond all of it, faint as a pencil line, the ridge where the guns never quite stopped.

Inside, nineteen men lay in two rows on plank beds lashed together from packing crates. Three were sleeping. Two would not see morning, though they did not know it yet and she would not tell them. The rest watched her with that particular patience of the wounded, the patience of men who had learned that nothing good came from asking twice.

"Sister." Private Ellis, his jaw wired, spoke through his teeth in a whistle of sound. "You've been standing there a long time."

"I'm counting," she said.

"Counting what?"

"What I can carry," she said, and went inside before he could ask more.

It was a habit she had fallen into without noticing when it began — this arithmetic of evacuation. Every nurse learned it eventually: the day would come, probably without warning, probably at night, when an order would come down the wire and the dressing station would have forty minutes or sixty to become nothing but memory and mud. In those minutes you took the morphine, the splints, the dressings, the carbolic if there was any. You took the men who could be moved and you did your arithmetic quickly and cruelly about the ones who could not. Everything else stayed for the shells.

Rafael had begun keeping a private tally of it. Not the stores — Matron did that in her ledgers, neat columns in a hand like fence posts — but the other inventory. What a person was, reduced to weight. Thomas's letter from his mother, folded to the size of a stamp. The photograph Corporal Vane kept in his boot until the boot came off with the foot. A button. A rosary gone green with wear. These things could be carried. She had carried them, some of them, in the pockets of her apron, until the pockets sagged and the things had to be posted home or buried in a biscuit tin behind the cookhouse, and even that small ceremony felt like theft from men who had given everything larger.

Her brother's name was stitched into the lining of that thought the way it was stitched into every hour. *Thomas Yarrow, 2nd Battalion.* Missing since the autumn push. Not dead — the regiment said missing, and missing was a country you could live in, a cold country but habitable. She had learned to measure everything against it. If she could carry a man's last words across France to his mother, then perhaps someone, somewhere, might someday carry Thomas's name back to her.

* * *

The deaths began going into her own ledger on a Tuesday, though she did not know yet that it would become a ledger, because on Tuesday it was only a page torn from a ration notebook and a stub of pencil softened by rain.

His name was Whitlock. Gunner, 24th. He had come in at dusk with his abdomen open and the orderly had brought him straight to her table, and there had been nothing — nothing, and she had known there was nothing within the first three seconds, and so she had held his hand instead of cutting, and told him he was safe, which was a lie, and told him she would write to his people, which was a promise.

He died at twenty past nine. She knew the time exactly because the shelling paused, as it sometimes did, and in the sudden enormous quiet she heard the clock in the next room — the one clock in the station still willing to tick.

In the morning she went to ask Captain Aldous for the details. He was the adjutant's man attached to the clearing route, a thin officer with a good manner and ink permanently on his second finger, and it was his job to take down the particulars of the dead for the official record: full name, number, battalion, date, next of kin. She had done it a dozen times. You gave him the facts, he wrote them in his book, and somewhere far behind the lines a form became a telegram became a woman in a parlour learning to stand differently for the rest of her life.

"Gunner Whitlock," she said. "Died Tuesday night, twenty past nine. Abdominal wound. I promised his mother a letter."

Aldous's pencil hesitated. It was only a hesitation, a fraction of a pause, but Rafael had spent years reading the small movements of men's faces across sickbeds, and a hesitation on a clerk's face told you as much as a moan from a patient.

"Whitlock," he said. "Of the 24th?"

"Yes."

"Was he... brought in under his own name, Sister?"

She stared at him. "He was conscious for ten minutes. He gave his name, his number, and his mother's address in Salford, and he asked me to write because his writing hand was gone. Yes, Captain. Under his own name."

"Right." He wetted his pencil. "Right. I'll see what I can do."

*See what I can do* was not an answer a records officer gave. Records officers gave answers; that was the whole of their religion. She stood in the doorway of the office — another requisitioned room, another workshop, the whole war a war of stolen carpentry — and watched him write something, and the something took him less time than it should have.

Three days later, a letter came up the supply chain addressed in a hand she didn't know, and it was not from Aldous. It was from the regimental pay office, and it said, with the courtesy of a closed door, that no Gunner J. Whitlock, 83014, appeared on the current strength of the 24th Battery, and that any correspondence regarding unattached personnel should be directed to the proper channels.

*Unattached.* As if he had drifted loose from himself. As if the man who had died holding her hand had been a spectre, a rumour, a clerical error in a greatcoat.

That night, after the last round of dressings, after the two men who would not see morning had seen morning arrive anyway against all odds, Rafael sat on an ammunition crate behind the cookhouse with a candle-end sheltered in a broken basin, and she tore four clean pages from the ration notebook, folded them, and wrote across the top of the first in her careful schoolroom hand:

*A true account of men deceased in this section whose names are not entered upon the official rolls.*

Then she wrote: *Whitlock, J., Gunner, No. 83014, 24th Battery. Died 9.20 p.m., Tuesday. Mother, Mrs E. Whitlock, 14 Trafalgar St., Salford. Last words: "Tell her the garden will want turning."*

The candle guttered. Somewhere north of them the guns resumed their argument, patient as weather. Rafael looked at what she had written — thirty words, the whole earthly remainder of a man — and understood two things at once, the way you understand things in wartime, both hands around one truth.

The first was that this was dangerous. She did not yet know how dangerous, or why, but Aldous's hesitation had been the visible edge of something larger, the way a shell hole in a road shows you only the top of the cavity.

The second was that she would do it anyway. Not bravely — bravery was a story soldiers told afterward — but simply, the way you bound a wound: because the alternative was to stand by while a man was erased, and she had already stood by too many times in her life watching names go into the ground unmarked. Thomas's name among them, perhaps, wherever he was.

She blew out the candle and slid the pages into the false bottom of her instrument case, beneath the scissors, where the canvas lining bulged just enough.

* * *

By the third week the ledger had eleven names, and it had acquired a system, because systems were how she kept grief at arm's length: each man got a line, a date, a unit, a family, and — where she could get it — a last word or a small fact. *Private Doyle: sang, badly, all through his stretcher journey; asked whether the singing annoyed anyone; nobody said yes.* *Sapper Ndiaye: carried a stone from home in his pocket, smooth, grey; asked that it go back to the river.* Facts were easier than tears. Facts could be filed.

She gathered them the way she gathered everything else, quietly, in the gaps. Orderlies talked to her because she listened. Chaplains talked to her because she made tea properly. Even the wounded talked, in that loosened midnight honesty of the badly hurt, and she caught the names as they fell and carried them to the candle behind the cookhouse.

But it was Marta Kessel who changed the shape of it.

Marta was the Belgian woman who had come with the workshop — the carpenter's daughter, twenty-something or thirty-something, it was impossible to tell, with forearms like a stevedore and a face that gave away nothing except when she laughed, which was rare and total. She had stayed on when the army requisitioned the building, first as a laundress, then as everything: scrubber, carrier, translator of the local farmers, finder of impossible things. She spoke Flemish and French and enough English to swear in, and she treated the British Army as a large, slow animal that occasionally required feeding.

"You are writing again," Marta said one evening, coming on her at the cookhouse wall. She didn't say *what*, because Marta never asked what. Asking questions in this landscape was a habit like drinking from shell holes — you could do it, but you'd regret it.

"An account," Rafael said. "Of the dead."

"The dead keep their own accounts."

"Not these ones. These ones have been..." She searched for the word and found the honest one. "Left out."

Marta crouched beside her, took the candle from the basin, and tilted it so the light fell better across the page. She read the way illiterate people sometimes read — slowly, moving her lips, though Rafael knew she could read three languages. When she reached the bottom of the sheet she set the candle down and said, in her flat way:

"The boy in Ward A. Lefèvre. The one who died Sunday of the gas. Is he in your book?"

"He is."

"Good." Marta stood, brushing ash from her skirts. "Because he is not in theirs. I heard the sergeant telling the paymaster the bed had been taken by a man named Corbier. Lefèvre died in that bed, and the bed has already forgotten him." She handed the candle back. "If you are doing this thing, Sister, do it properly. Get the dates exact. Dates are what hold a family together afterwards. My father keeps the birth registers for three parishes, and he says — he says a man is a date and a place, and everything else is love, and love cannot be filed."

She walked off toward the laundry copper, steam rising around her, and Rafael sat looking at her eleven names and feeling for the first time that they were not a private grief but something with edges and mass — something that could be measured, and therefore something that could be argued for.

That was the night she decided to check.

* * *

The muster rolls came to the station by accident, the way most things came. A runner overshot the road in the dark and dumped two canvas document bags at the wrong dugout, and by the time the error was discovered the bags had been sitting under Rafael's desk for six hours, and the corporal sent to retrieve them was seventeen and terrified of Sister Yarrow for reasons involving a syringe of morphia administered too enthusiastically to his brother in January.

"May I look at these first?" she asked pleasantly. "One of my men came through here last week. I want to confirm his battalion."

The corporal, who would have refused Florence Nightingale herself if ordered, nodded gratefully and waited outside.

The rolls were the standard strength returns — pages ruled into columns, name, rank, number, joined, posted, struck off. She found her man, confirmed his battalion, and should have stopped there. Instead, out of a habit she did not recognize as new, she ran her finger down the battery listings to the 24th.

Gunner J. Whitlock, 83014, was not marked dead. There was no *killed in action*, no date, no cross in the margin. His line ended in a notation she had to read three times before her mind would accept it: *posted to base depot, administrative reasons.*

A man who had died on her table with his abdomen open and his mother's garden on his lips had been posted, administratively, to a depot that did not exist, and so he remained — on paper — alive. Alive meant no casualty telegram. Alive meant his pay continued accumulating in an account no one would claim. Alive meant Mrs Whitlock of Trafalgar Street, Salford, might wait a year, two years, receiving nothing, because officially there was nothing to receive.

Rafael turned the page. Checked Doyle. *Transferred, records incomplete.* Checked Ndiaye. *Struck off strength — clerical.* Eleven names from her candle-lit ledger, eleven lines in the official roll, and not one of them said what had happened. The column marked *casualties* for that fortnight showed a number — a tidy, rounded, impossible number, far smaller than the bodies she had personally counted through the tent flap — and at the foot of the return, in the signature space, a single authoritative name written in a hard downward-slanting hand:

*Hessler, Col., Cdg. 3rd Bde.*

She did not know Colonel Hessler. She knew the handwriting, suddenly and completely, the way you know a face in a crowd that you have seen once at a funeral. The same slanted hand appeared on the fortnight before, and the fortnight before that, each return signed, each return tidy, each return wrong by a margin she could now begin to count.

Outside, the corporal coughed. Rain started again on the canvas roof, soft as footsteps.

Rafael closed the rolls, smoothed her apron, and called him in with a steady voice, and handed over the bags, and answered his stammered apology with reassurance, and watched him jog off down the duckboards into the dark. Then she stood alone in Ward B with nineteen sleeping men and the smell of chalk and burning, and let herself feel the whole size of it — not eleven names now, but whatever number lay behind the tidy columns, multiplied by batteries and battalions and fortnights, mothers unfound, pensions unpaid, graves unmarked, a whole quiet country of the erased governed by one slanting signature.

She measured her world, as she always did, in what could be carried. Her instrument case could carry perhaps forty pages sewn into its false bottom. Her uniform lining, more. Her memory — trained by three years of ward rounds, of remembering who took milk and who could not — could carry hundreds.

And then the fear arrived, punctual as the guns: because if she could count the discrepancy, then others could count it, and the man whose signature sat at the foot of every falsified return had an interest in the counting stopping — and she had just spent an hour alone with the proof, holding it in hands that left prints.

From the ridge, a single gun spoke, once, deliberate, like a man tapping the counter before beginning to deal.

Rafael took out her pencil and, on the flyleaf of her hidden ledger, beneath the title she had written by candlelight, added one more line in her careful schoolroom hand:

*Hessler.*

Then she put it away, deep beneath the scissors, and went in to check the dressings of the living.
