## 2. Muster and Omission

The requisition forms came on Tuesdays. Rafael Yarrow had learned to read the war through its paperwork — how many stretchers the quartermaster expected to need told her more than any briefing about where the line would break next. So when the Tuesday form arrived asking for forty additional cot frames and three times the usual lint, she understood what was coming down the road toward them before she heard a single gun.

She filed the form in the workshop behind the dressing station, a carpenter's shed the army had stripped of its lathes and given over to stores: bandage crates stacked to the rafters, chloroform tins under a workbench still scarred with sawdust that no amount of sweeping would ever fully remove. In one corner, behind a false panel she had built herself from a broken cabinet door, sat her ledger.

It was not a grand thing. A school exercise book, bought in Poperinghe for two francs, its marbled cover already warped by damp. She had ruled the columns herself with a straightedge borrowed from the carpenter before he'd been posted up the line: name, regimental number, date of admission, date of death, ward, bed. And in a fourth column she kept private even from the page's logic — *where they were from*, because a man was not a number, and someone, someday, would need to know that Private Ellis Whitcombe of B Company had a wife in Leeds who signed her letters *your own Nellie*.

Forty-one names now. She added the two from the night before — a Shropshire lad named Cobb who had died with his hands folded as if in prayer, though Rafael suspected he had simply been too far gone to lower them — and slid the ledger back behind the panel.

Then Sister Callaghan put the muster rolls in her way without knowing it.

* * *

They came down from regimental headquarters in a canvas dispatch case, four fat ledgers of foolscap, with a chit requesting that the dressing station verify admissions against the rolls "for purposes of casualty reconciliation." The word *reconciliation* had been underlined twice, in a hand that pressed hard enough to tear the paper.

"Headquarters wants our numbers to match theirs," Callaghan said, dropping the case on the admissions desk with the contempt of a woman who had been a nurse since before this army learned what a field hospital was. "Theirs being wrong, I expect. You're good at sums, Yarrow. You do it."

Rafael did it. It was, on the surface, simple work. Every man admitted to the station appeared on a muster roll unless he had come in as an unidentified casualty; every man discharged or evacuated appeared again; every man who died should have appeared once more, struck through in red ink, his death certified by the medical officer and transmitted up the chain until it reached the clerk who entered it into the permanent record. The system was elegant. The system was designed so that no man could vanish.

By the second evening, Rafael had found eleven men who had vanished anyway.

The first was Cobb himself. She remembered the number because she had written it on his forehead tag in pencil while he still breathed: 24117. She ran her finger down the pages of B Company's roll, past the names of men she had nursed and buried and nursed again, and found 24117 absent entirely. Not struck through in red. Not marked *deceased*, *missing*, *transferred*. Absent, as if he had never answered the roll call at all — except that there he was on the previous month's roll, present and accounted for, drawing pay, standing in a trench within range of German guns.

She assumed clerical error. Errors happened; the war generated paperwork faster than any office could digest it. She set the roll aside and checked the next man on her list. Private Daniel Okafor, 17832, died of wounds on the ninth, gangrene, and Rafael had held his hand while the MO wrote the certificate. On the roll: nothing. Not transferred, not struck. Gone.

A third. A fourth. By midnight, working by the blue flame of a spirit lamp because the generator had failed again, she had eleven names from her ledger that existed nowhere in the regiment's official memory of itself. Eleven men whose mothers would receive nothing, whose pay would stop without explanation, whose deaths had never, officially, occurred.

She sat back on the crate that served as a chair and looked at the exercise book lying open beside the muster rolls, her careful columns beside the army's careful columns, and felt the shape of something forming in her chest that was larger than grief. Grief she knew. This was different. This had edges.

* * *

She did not sleep. At three in the morning the shelling resumed somewhere east of the canal, a low rhythmic knocking like a fist against a door, and she sat in the workshop with both books open and began, methodically, to be afraid.

Because eleven was too many for error. Errors scattered themselves randomly across the alphabet and the companies. These clustered. Nine of the eleven had died in the same fortnight — the fortnight of the push at the beginning of the month, when the dressing station had run out of morphine and Rafael had done arithmetic of her own, deciding which screams got the last tablets. All eleven were other ranks. Not one officer. And every one of them had died during periods when the regiment's returns would have been consolidated for the monthly report.

She took a fresh sheet of foolscap and drew a timeline. Across the top, the months since she had begun keeping her ledger. Down the side, the weeks. Against each week she plotted the deaths she had recorded — small crosses, like the marks on a hospital chart — and then, beneath each cluster of crosses, she noted whether the corresponding men appeared on the rolls.

The pattern emerged the way a photograph emerges in a developing bath: faintly at first, then all at once, undeniable.

In quiet weeks, the dead were recorded. One or two men lost to shellfire on a silent stretch of line, a death from pneumonia, an accident — these went into the rolls properly, red ink and certification and all. But whenever the casualties came in quantity, the recording stopped. Eleven omissions out of fourteen deaths in the first week of the month. Six out of nine in the fighting before that. The worse the slaughter, the emptier the record. As if the pen writing the truth had been ordered, precisely when it mattered most, to lift from the page.

She worked out the figures with a stub of pencil, and the sum made her go cold in a warm room. The regiment's reported casualties for the quarter fell short of her count by thirty-one men — and her count covered only those who had reached her station alive enough to give a name. Add the men who had died at the aid posts up the line, the men brought in unidentified, the men taken to other hospitals, and the true figure could be double hers. Perhaps treble. Somewhere above her, someone was reporting losses a fraction of their real size, and the difference between the real number and the reported one had a use: it made the regiment look like it was holding. It made a colonel's arithmetic balance. It made the staff believe the line could stand where, in fact, it was being quietly emptied of men no one would ever admit had died.

And there was a signature at the bottom of every monthly return. She had seen the chits that accompanied supplies, the requisitions countersigned up the chain. The regimental commander's name recurred like a watermark: *Hessler*. Colonel Edmund Hessler. The hand that pressed hard enough to tear paper.

Rafael put down the pencil. Her fingers had gone stiff with cold and something else.

* * *

Dawn came grey through the workshop's one window, illuminating the floating dust, the stacked crates, the workbench with its ghost of sawdust. Outside, the stretcher-bearers were bringing in the night's harvest, and she could hear Sister Callaghan's voice cutting through it all like wire: *not that table, the other one, and get me hot water, not warm, hot.*

Rafael closed the muster rolls and tied the dispatch case. Then she untied it. Then she stood with her hand flat on the canvas cover for a long moment, listening to her own pulse.

Here was the thing she could not stop turning over: the request for reconciliation had come from headquarters. Which meant that somewhere above her, someone wanted the numbers matched — or wanted to know how badly they didn't match, or wanted a subordinate to certify that they did. If she sent back a clean reconciliation, she became part of it. Her name on the bottom of a sheet of paper that erased Cobb and Okafor and nine others. If she sent back the truth—

If she sent back the truth, she thought, then Colonel Hessler would learn that a nursing sister at a dressing station had counted his lies. And men who built falsifications of this size did not survive boards of inquiry by leaving witnesses standing.

She was twenty-nine years old. She had come to France because her brother Thomas had come to France, and because sitting at home reading casualty lists had felt like a kind of complicity she could not stomach. Thomas had died at Loos the year before — recorded properly, struck through in red ink, a letter received, a grave registered. Even grief, she had learned, could be a mercy when the paperwork was honest. These eleven men's families would wait forever. They would write letters that were never answered. They would watch the lane for a postman who had nothing to bring them, year after year, because a colonel's quarterly report needed to look tidy.

She thought of Nellie Whitcombe in Leeds, signing her letters *your own Nellie* to a man who was already dust, and of the day the letters stopped coming and Nellie would have no way to know why.

Rafael opened the dispatch case one final time, copied the roll numbers of the eleven missing men onto a slip of paper, and burned the slip in the spirit lamp. Then she hid the muster rolls' key discrepancy where no auditor would look for it: inside her own ledger, behind the false panel, written in her own hand, dated, witnessed by no one.

There. Now the lie had a counterweight, however small, however hidden. A grain of sand placed against a wheel.

She washed her face in the basin, pinned her veil, and went in to the ward, where forty cots waited and the supply form had promised more.

* * *

The fear arrived later, in daylight, the way fear always did — after the anger had spent its first heat and left her alone with consequences.

It came during a lull in admissions, while she was folding clean dressings, and it arrived complete: a vision of herself standing before some tribunal in a bare room, the exercise book produced from her kit bag as evidence, a major's voice asking who had authorized her record-keeping. *No one, sir.* And why had she kept records that duplicated official documents? *Because the official documents were false, sir.* And how did she know they were false? *I counted, sir.*

*I counted.* Two words, and behind them the whole machinery of the army turning to look at her — a woman, a volunteer sister, an inconvenient witness with neat handwriting and no rank worth respecting. They would not shoot her. They would do something quieter. They would post her to a base hospital in Rouen, or strike her off the roster, or find some irregularity in her papers, and the ledger would be confiscated and burned with the rest of the discrepancies, and Cobb and Okafor would sink back beneath the surface of the record as if they had never surfaced at all.

Worse: she could be made into the falsifier. A nurse keeping unofficial lists — the phrase had a poison in it already. She could see the accusation assembling itself: *unauthorized documentation, breach of security, possible espionage.* In 1917 a woman who wrote things down outside her orders was halfway to a cell before anyone asked what she had written.

She stood very still among the folded dressings and let the fear finish its argument. It was a good argument. It was, she admitted, almost certainly correct. The sensible course was to burn the ledger tonight, reconcile the rolls honestly or dishonestly as instructed, and keep her head below the parapet where heads belonged. Thousands of people served this army without counting anything. There was no rule of nature requiring her to be the one who counted.

But then a bearer came through the doors with a boy on his stretcher — seventeen perhaps, crying for a mother whose name Rafael didn't yet know and might never be permitted to know — and the argument collapsed under its own weight. Because the boy would die; she could see it in the colour of him, in the bubbles at his lips; and when he died, some clerk with a hard-pressing pen would decide whether his mother ever learned of it, and that decision would be made on grounds that had nothing to do with the boy or his mother at all.

No. She had not come to France to be sensible.

That evening, after lights-out, she took the exercise book from behind the false panel and ruled new columns on a fresh page: not just names now, but *roll status* — present on muster, absent, omitted. Evidence. Corroboration. If the ledger was ever found, let it be found as what it was: not a nurse's sentiment, but an account. Auditable. Damning.

At the bottom of the page she wrote the total, and the total frightened her more than the tribunal had: *forty-one recorded, eleven confirmed omitted, pattern systematic, omissions concentrated in high-casualty periods, returns countersigned E. Hessler, Col., comdg.*

She read the line back twice. It sounded like an indictment. It was an indictment. She was, she realized, no longer merely keeping a record of the dead. She was building a case against a colonel of the British Army, alone, in a carpentry workshop, by lamplight, with a two-franc exercise book.

She blew out the lamp and sat in the dark listening to the guns knock at the eastern door. The ledger lay against her ribs where she'd tucked it inside her jacket, and its small weight felt suddenly enormous — the weight of forty-one men, of forty-one families, of a truth that had no protector but her.

What she needed — what the whole fragile enterprise needed, though she would not let herself think it plainly yet — was another pair of hands. Someone who understood ledgers from the other side of the desk. Someone who knew how the rolls moved, where the copies lived, who touched them.

She did not know yet that such a person existed, three miles down the road at regimental headquarters, a mild census clerk named Bram Odell, who that same evening was frowning at a pay sheet in which dead men went on drawing rations and living men appeared twice.

She only knew that tomorrow was Tuesday, and the requisition forms would come, and the war would ask her again what she was willing to carry.
