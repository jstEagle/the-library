## 3. The Census Taker

Bram Odell had been counting things since he was nine years old, when his father put him at the window of their shop in Lewes and told him to tally the carts that passed, westbound heavy, eastbound light, until supper. It was not a cruel assignment. His father believed numbers were a kind of prayer — that the world could be held steady if only someone kept faithful count of it. Bram had inherited the belief whole, along with the narrow shoulders and the habit of wetting his pencil lead against his tongue before every figure, so that even now, at twenty-six, he tasted graphite when he was concentrating.

The army had discovered this inheritance and, in its efficient way, had made of it exactly what it needed: not a soldier but an enumerator. He had come out to France in the autumn with a second-lieutenant's pay he didn't ask for and no rank insignia he didn't wear, seconded from the Board of Trade census office because somebody in Whitehall had decided the regiment's muster rolls wanted putting in order before the next inspection. *Papers follow the man*, his supervisor in London had said, tapping Bram's chest with two fingers as though locating his heart. *And if the papers are wrong, the man is nowhere. Remember that, Odell. A clerk is the memory of the army. Don't let it have gaps.*

Bram thought about that sentence more than any scripture.

The records office occupied what had been a schoolhouse before the war, in a market town some dozen kilometres behind the line, close enough that on clear nights the horizon carried a flicker like heat lightning that never cooled into storm. The desks had been the children's, and Bram still fit them badly, his knees finding the undersides where generations of boys had carved their initials. The registers lived along the wall in pigeonholes labelled by company, by month, by the particular genius of whichever sergeant had preceded him. The room smelled of cold stove, ink, and the sweetish dust of old paper — a smell Bram loved without shame. It was the smell of things being kept.

His work was simple in description and endless in execution: reconcile the muster rolls against the pay books, against the casualty returns, against the embarkation ledgers, and produce for the colonel's office a clean summary each Friday. Men came and men went. That was the whole of it. Names in columns, columns in ledgers, ledgers in pigeonholes, pigeonholes against the wall of a schoolhouse where children had learned sums and now the sums were men.

He was three weeks into the work before he began, in the way of careful people, to notice what careful people notice first: not the errors themselves, but their pattern.

* * *

It started with Private Ellis Wray, B Company.

Bram found him on the October roll, drew his rations, drew his pay — and then found him again on the casualty return dated the ninth of October, marked *killed*. That happened; the machinery of armies was vast and its gears meshed imperfectly. So Bram did what his training demanded: he crossed the dead man off the active roll, annotated the ledger, closed the file. Clean work. Satisfying, even. Somewhere in England a woman would be informed, or would already have been informed, and the number of living men would be true again.

Except that in November, Wray appeared once more on the pay abstract.

Bram checked twice. Checked the date, checked the company prefix, checked the regimental number — 24117, stamped in that particular violet that meant a duplicate book issued after the original was lost. The signature against the November entry was a corporal's, authorising collection of the dead man's pay, which happened too, sometimes, when a mate forwarded a kit home. But the annotation column was blank where the casualty cross should have been, and when Bram pulled Wray's casualty return to lay beside it, he found the return itself thin, almost new, and initialed not by the medical officer who should have signed it but by someone whose initials — R.H. — he had seen on nothing else in the files except, now that he looked, on a great many other things.

He sat back. The stove ticked. Outside, a lorry changed gear on the cobbled square.

One man was a clerical error. Armies made them by the hundred. Bram copied the discrepancy onto his working sheet, as he'd been trained — *discrepancies noted, referred upward* — and moved on.

By the end of the week his working sheet held eleven names.

* * *

He might have stopped there. He wanted to say, later, that he nearly had. But there was something in the shape of the eleven — they were all infantry, all from the same three companies, all killed in the last week of September or the first of October, and all of their casualty returns bore those same initials, R.H., in place of the proper signatures — that made stopping feel less like prudence and more like lying. And his father's voice was in his ears, though his father was two years buried under the yew at Lewes: *the world can be held steady if only someone keeps faithful count.*

So he widened the net. Evenings, after the other clerks had gone to their estaminet and their cheap wine, Bram stayed with the lamp turned low and worked backward through the summer. He built himself a private table in the back pages of his own notebook — never in the official ledgers — listing each man who appeared on a pay abstract after his recorded death, each man whose casualty return lacked a medical officer's signature, each man whose death seemed to have happened nowhere at all: not in a trench raid report, not in a battalion diary, not in the letters of any officer whose correspondence crossed the desk. Eleven became nineteen. Nineteen became thirty-one.

And here was the thing that turned his stomach, the thing he kept turning over like a stone with something pale underneath: the pay continued. Not always — but often enough. A dead man's separation allowance drawn in June, July, August. A widow's allotment stopped, restarted, stopped again. Once, a man killed in May received his six days' field pay in September, collected by a sergeant-major Bram knew by sight, a large comfortable man named Dole who whistled through his teeth and called everyone *sunshine*.

Bram did the arithmetic he did not want to do. If the men were dead — and they were dead; he had seen the burial parties' rough returns, the grave registration cards that contradicted everything else — then the pay was going somewhere it shouldn't. And if the rolls said fewer men had died than had actually died, then the regiment's casualty figures, the ones wired weekly to division, the ones the colonel signed —

— the ones the colonel signed.

R.H. Reginald Hessler. Bram had never met him. He knew the name only as it appeared at the foot of summaries, a confident descending hand, *Hessler, Lt.-Col., Comdg.*, above figures that were always, always lower than Bram's private totals. Lower by roughly the width of his own list. Thirty-one men, give or take, unaccounted for since May. Dead, buried, paid after death, and absent from the count.

Not chaos. Pattern.

He sat very still in the lamplight, tasting graphite, and felt the floor of his ordinary life tilt one degree — barely, just enough that a glass would slide.

* * *

On Thursday morning the room was fuller than usual. Two clerks from the adjutant's section had come over to chase a disciplinary file, and Sergeant Dole was at the pigeonholes in his whistling way, pulling bundles for the Friday summary. The stove had been fed and the room was almost warm. Bram, feeling the particular courage of a man who has been awake since four with a question, took his working sheet across to Corporal Voss at the far desk. Voss was forty-some, pre-war regular, a neat grey man with neat grey handwriting, and he had been in this office longer than anyone. If the pattern meant anything — or nothing — Voss would know.

"Corporal. When you're free."

Voss finished his line, blotted it, looked up with polite patience. "Odell."

"I'm reconciling the September and October returns." Bram laid his sheet down, squared it to the desk edge the way he squared everything. "I've got thirty-one men marked killed on burial returns and grave registration cards who don't appear on the battalion casualty summaries. And eleven of them drew pay after death. I wondered whether there's a supplementary return I haven't seen. Some list they go on instead."

The words sounded reasonable aloud. He had rehearsed them as reasonable. *Some list they go on instead* — a clerk's question, innocent, administrative, the sort of thing a careful man asks because care is his job.

Voss read the sheet. His eyes moved down the names once, unhurried, and Bram watched the unhurriedness become something else — a stillness, a settling, like water going flat before frost. Voss did not reach for the sheet. He did not push it away. He finished reading, raised his eyes, and looked at Bram for perhaps two full seconds, which in a records office is a very long time.

"Where did you get these?" Not sharp. Quiet. Quieter than the room.

"Burial returns. Grave registration. The pay abstracts." Bram heard his own voice adopt the explanatory tone he used with inspectors. "There should be corresponding entries —"

"There are."

"Then I've missed them. If you could show me which —"

"Leave it, Odell."

It was not said unkindly. That was the worst of it, Bram thought afterwards — it was said the way you warn a man standing somewhere high and unaware of the edge. Voss picked up his pen, dipped it, and returned to his line of figures. Conversation concluded.

But the room had heard. Bram felt it the way you feel weather change — the whistling at the pigeonholes had stopped. Dole stood with a bundle of files half-slotted, his head turned, his small eyes on Bram over the tops of the folders. At the adjutant's table both visiting clerks had gone on copying, pens moving, but moving slowly, and neither of them looked up, which was its own kind of looking. The stove ticked. A lorry passed. Somewhere a door closed softly, and the silence afterward had weight, had texture, the specific textured quiet of a room in which everyone knows something and has agreed, without ever agreeing, that it will not be said.

Bram picked up his working sheet. He made himself walk normally back to his desk, sit normally, uncap his ink normally. His hand, when he set it on the paper, was not normal at all.

*Sunshine,* Dole said to no one in particular, resuming his filing, and began to whistle again.

* * *

He ate nothing at noon. He walked out past the church to the flat fields beyond the town, mud frozen into ridges hard as iron, and stood looking at the eastern horizon where the flicker lived, and tried to make the day's arithmetic come out harmless.

Explanations existed. He listed them like a good clerk. Clerical lag — returns crossing in the post. A lost ledger reconstructed from memory, hence the odd signatures. A colonel smoothing his figures to protect the battalion from being broken up for reinforcements — men said things like that; it was even a kindness of a kind, in a way, from certain angles. And the pay — the pay was slop, everyone knew the pay system was slop, dead men's accounts drifted for months like drowned things.

Every explanation covered part of it. No explanation covered all of it. Thirty-one men, dead and buried and absent, while their pay went on walking to the right pockets and the colonel's wire went out each Friday saying all was well, all was well, all was very well. And the room going quiet when he asked. Voss's face. *Leave it, Odell.*

His father would have said — he could hear him, that mild Lewes voice over the shop counter — that a count is a promise made to the counted. That every man in a column is somebody's boy, and the column is how the news travels home, and a false column is a letter never delivered. Bram had never thought of it in those terms until now, standing in a frozen field with his ears still ringing from a silence. Thirty-one letters. Thirty-one women in terraced houses and farm kitchens, waiting on news that had been, what — filed? Folded into a total that flattered a career?

Or simpler, and worse: waiting on nothing, because the machinery had decided their sons had never died at all, and so could never properly have been mourned, pensioned, grieved, closed over. Men who fell through the arithmetic and kept falling.

He turned back toward the town. As he came up the lane by the churchyard he saw, without meaning to take note — taking note anyway, because he was what he was — Sergeant Dole ahead of him, walking quickly for a large man, and pausing at the door of the adjutant's annex across the square from the records office, and going in without knocking.

Bram stood in the lane a moment longer than a man merely passing through it ought to stand.

* * *

That afternoon nothing happened, which was itself an event. Nobody spoke to him beyond necessary words. Voss corrected one of his entries without comment, pointing rather than speaking. The visiting clerks left early. At four o'clock a runner Bram didn't know came in with the post, sorted it, and paused at Bram's desk long enough to say, tonelessly, "Captain Aldous would see you, Mr. Odell. Tomorrow. Ten o'clock. Adjutant's office," and was gone before Bram could form a question.

Captain Aldous. The adjutant. Hessler's adjutant.

Bram sat with the message slip in his hand and tasted graphite and understood, with the terrible speed of a mind built for patterns, exactly what shape he had drawn himself into. He had spent three weeks finding the edges of a hole the army had dug and filled smooth, and then he had stood on the smooth place in front of witnesses and asked why the grass grew differently there. Tomorrow at ten o'clock he would be invited to explain himself to the man whose job was keeping the surface smooth.

He thought of Voss's quiet voice. *Leave it.* Not a threat — a rope thrown. There had been time to catch it. There had been time all afternoon; the office emptied around five, and Bram stayed at his desk with his notebook open to the private pages, thirty-one names in his small upright hand, and he knew — he knew with certainty that felt physical, a hand between his shoulder blades — that he could close the notebook, feed those pages to the stove, present himself at ten o'clock apologetic and stupid, sign whatever retraction was put in front of him, and be a clerk again by lunchtime. Safe. Small. Counted among the living, doing sums in a warm room.

His father at the window, counting carts. Westbound heavy, eastbound light.

Bram looked at the names. Ellis Wray. Alfred Cobbett. Thomas Nield, whose pay book was still in the drawer behind him, whose mother — Bram had checked the next-of-kin column, unable not to — lived at a street in Lewes ten minutes' walk from the shop where Bram had grown up counting. Mrs. Nield might know his father's grave. They might have nodded to each other outside St. Anne's.

Thirty-one men. Thirty-one letters undelivered.

He closed the notebook, and did not burn it, and put it in his breast pocket where he could feel its small stiff weight against his ribs like a second heart. Then he turned down the lamp, and racked the chair, and let himself out into the blue evening, where the horizon flickered its soundless lightning and the frost took his breath and gave it back.

Tomorrow at ten o'clock, then. He would hear what Captain Aldous wanted him to say.

And whatever answer he gave the captain — whatever he signed, or refused to sign — he understood, walking back through the freezing square past the darkened annex where Dole had gone that noon, that the decision had already been made somewhere below language, in the place where a man keeps count of his own soul: he was going to ask one more question first.

He only needed to find out who else, besides himself, was asking it.
