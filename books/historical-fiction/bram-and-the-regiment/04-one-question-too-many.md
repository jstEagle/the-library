## 4. One Question Too Many

The summons came at half past four in the afternoon, delivered by a lance-corporal who would not meet Bram's eyes.

"Captain Verrall. Adjutant's office. Six o'clock."

Bram Odell stood in the doorway of the records annex with a file of burial returns still in his hand, and for a moment he could not make his legs work. The paper felt suddenly loud against his palm — as though the corporal could hear it rustling, as though everyone in the corridor could. *Private T. Wrenn, killed in action 9th October.* A man who did not exist on any roll Bram had been able to find.

"Yes," Bram said. "Of course. Six o'clock."

The corporal went. Down the hall, two clerks who had shared Bram's table for three weeks — men who lent him pencils and complained about the tea — were suddenly very busy with their own papers. One of them turned his shoulder toward the door.

Bram put the burial return back in its drawer and squared its edges with fingers that wanted to shake. He had asked one question. That was all. He had spent eleven days quietly reconciling muster rolls against casualty returns, because that was what census clerks did: they counted people, they matched names to names, they noticed when arithmetic failed. It was not even his proper work here — he had been seconded from the Board of Trade's enumeration office into this regimental records annex as a kindness, a soft posting for a man with flat feet and a stammer and no talent whatsoever for killing. Counting was the one thing he was good at. He had thought counting was safe.

*Why does Private Thomas Wrenn draw pay if there is no record of his enlistment?*

That was the question. He had asked it across the table to Corporal Finch, mildly, pencil still in hand, the way he might have asked why a column wouldn't sum. And the room had gone quiet the way a wood goes quiet when something moves between the trees.

Six o'clock. He had ninety minutes.

He spent them badly. He walked out to the horse-lines behind the transport park and stood in the cold watching farriers work, and he rehearsed explanations. *A clerical error on my part, sir. I misread the roll. I'm sure the records are perfectly in order.* He practised it until the stammer smoothed out of it, and then he heard how it sounded — like exactly what it was, a man preparing to lie — and he abandoned it. His father had kept the parish registers in a village so small the church and the schoolhouse shared a roof, and his father had a saying: *a number you know to be wrong is heavier than any stone you'll ever carry.* Bram had inherited the saying and, apparently, the inability to put it down.

At ten to six he presented himself at the adjutant's office, a requisitioned farmhouse whose parlour still smelled faintly of apples and gun oil.

Captain Verrall sat behind a desk that had been a kitchen table until quite recently. He was perhaps forty, red-faced in the way of men who drank steadily and slept badly, and he had before him a single sheet of paper laid square to the desk's edge. Beside it, a fountain pen. Beside that, nothing. No file on Bram. No record of his secondment. No evidence at all that Bram Odell existed, which, given what Bram had spent eleven days discovering about how easily men could be made not to exist, was its own kind of message.

"Odell," Verrall said. "Sit down."

Bram sat. The chair was a kitchen chair and his feet did not quite reach the position they wanted.

"You're a census man." Verrall said it the way another man might say *you're a rat-catcher*. "Board of Trade. Counting houses, is that right? Counting people."

"Enumeration, sir. Yes."

"And now you're counting for us. Muster rolls. Casualty returns." Verrall picked up the sheet of paper, looked at it without reading it, set it down again in exactly the same place. "Good work. Very thorough. Perhaps more thorough than the job requires."

Bram said nothing. His heart was going hard enough that he could feel it in his ears.

"On the eleventh of October," Verrall said, "you asked Corporal Finch why a private soldier was drawing pay without an enlistment record. Is that correct?"

"It — yes, sir. It seemed like a discrepancy. In the figures."

"A discrepancy." Verrall smiled without warmth. "You understand, Odell, that a regiment's paperwork is a living thing. It gets corrected. Amended. Men transfer, get re-badged, go missing for a fortnight and turn up again. What looks like a discrepancy to a civilian is very often just — friction. The machine moving."

"Yes, sir."

"The machine moving," Verrall repeated, as though Bram hadn't spoken. He slid the sheet of paper across the desk and turned it so Bram could read it.

It was a statement. It began *I, B. R. Odell, clerk seconded to the regimental records annex, hereby state that on the eleventh day of October I raised a query which, upon reflection, I recognise to have been founded on a misreading of documents...* Below the paragraph was a blank line, and below the blank line, a space for his signature and today's date.

"You sign that," Verrall said, "and the matter ends. You've made an honest mistake, we've cleared it up, everyone's satisfied. Your posting stays comfortable. There's a good fire in the annex and the shells don't reach us. You keep doing your careful work, and nobody ever needs to think about it again." He leaned back. "Or."

"Or," Bram said. His voice came out very small.

"You don't sign it. In which case I'm obliged to conclude that a man who questions the regiment's records and refuses correction is either incompetent or insubordinate, and neither of those is a quality we want in a rear-area billet. There's a draft leaving for the line on Thursday. Infantry. They're always short." Verrall folded his hands. "Flat feet won't save you from that, Odell. Nothing saves a man at the front but luck, and luck doesn't favour clerks."

The stove ticked. Somewhere outside a lorry changed gear. Bram looked at the statement, at the neat bureaucratic language of erasure — *founded on a misreading*, as though Thomas Wrenn were a smudge — and he understood, with a clarity that arrived like cold water, what he was actually being asked. Not to retract a query. To agree that a dead man had never lived. That his name was worth less than a colonel's figures. That the whole apparatus of paper he had spent his life tending was a machine for making things disappear, and his choice was to be its servant or be fed into it.

Thomas Wrenn drew pay. Somebody, somewhere, was receiving that pay — or worse, nobody was, and the money simply vanished into the same ledger that had swallowed the man. And if Wrenn, then how many others? Bram had found four discrepancies in eleven days. He had told himself four was carelessness. Now, sitting in the apple-smelling parlour with his future laid out in one paragraph, he understood that four was only what he'd found before he learned to be afraid of finding them.

"I'd like a little time, sir," Bram said. "To consider."

Verrall's face closed. "Time."

"To be certain. Of my own mind." Bram's hands were clasped in his lap to keep them still. "My father was a parish registrar, sir. He taught me never to sign anything I hadn't read twice. It would be — disrespectful to his memory, to sign in haste."

For a long moment Verrall studied him, and Bram braced for the word *Thursday* to come down like a hammer. Instead the captain almost smiled — the smile of a man who has played this game many times and enjoys watching it play out.

"One night," Verrall said. "Sign it by nine tomorrow morning or your kit goes on the Thursday draft with your name on it. Dismissed."

Bram stood, saluted clumsily, and walked out into the dusk on legs that felt borrowed.

* * *

His billet was a converted hay loft over the records annex, shared with two other seconded civilians who were both, thankfully, at their suppers. Bram lay on his cot in the dark with his boots on and tried to think.

He was not brave. He had known this about himself since he was nine years old, when Tommy Aldous had held his head under the mill stream for a dare and Bram had come up weeping and begging rather than fighting. Every courage he possessed was small and clerical: the courage to check a sum twice, to tell a widow her pension figure honestly, to knock on a door and ask its occupants to be counted. Against Captain Verrall and his Thursday draft, he had nothing but a stammer and a father's saying about the weight of wrong numbers.

And yet.

And yet there was Wrenn. Thomas Wrenn, aged twenty-six according to the pay book entry, killed in action the ninth of October, existing everywhere except where it mattered. Bram had gone back through the rolls after asking his question — carefully, guiltily, after lights-out with a shaded lamp — and found the gap where Wrenn should have been. Enlistment record: absent. Medical inspection: absent. Next of kin: absent. Only the pay continued, month after month, a ghost walking through the accounts, and someone with authority had signed off every page of it. Someone whose initials were *R.H.*

*R.H.* Colonel Hessler. Bram didn't know the man; he'd seen him once at a distance, reviewing a parade, small and neat on a large horse. But the initials were on every discrepancy Bram had found. Four ghosts, four sets of accounts, four signatures of *R.H.*, and each signature attached to a casualty figure reported to division that was lower than the casualties Bram could count himself.

Lower figures meant reinforcement allocations. Lower figures meant honours and commendations and a board's favourable eye. Lower figures meant that somewhere in England a mother was waiting for letters that officialdom had decided, for reasons of arithmetic, she would never receive.

Bram turned onto his side. The loft boards creaked. He thought: *I will sign it. I am not built for this. I will sign the paper and keep my head down and live, and someday, when the war is over, maybe—*

But that was the thing about sums, he thought, in the drifting edge toward sleep. Once you knew a column was false, you could never un-know it. Every total built on it was rotten. Every figure above it was a lie stacked on a lie. His father had said wrong numbers were heavy, but his father had never said the weight got lighter with time. It got heavier. It accumulated interest.

He was still turning this over when he saw the paper.

It had come under the door. The loft door was old, swollen in its frame, with a gap at the bottom wide enough for a draught and, evidently, for a folded sheet. Bram had not heard footsteps. He had not heard anything. But there it lay on the boards between his cot and the wall, a single page, folded once, in the grey light that came through the hay window from the moon on the frost.

He rose. Crossed the floor. Picked it up.

The handwriting was small and precise, a woman's hand or a man trained to fine script, written fast — some words crowded at the line-ends, abbreviations he didn't recognise. At the top, a heading ruled in ink:

*Register of the Unrecorded — Entry 31*

Below it:

*WRENN, THOMAS H., Pte., 2nd Bn. Age 26. KIA 9.x.17, sector E. Died at the dressing station, Yarrow, 10.x.17, 0340 hrs. Spoke at the end of his mother — "Tell her the apples kept." Address unfound: believed Nuneaton area. Pay drawn in his name to present date; enlistment and next-of-kin records suppressed. Initials on suppression order: R.H.*

Bram read it three times. His hands had begun to shake, but it was not entirely terror now, and that frightened him more than terror had.

*Spoke at the end of his mother.* Someone had been there. Someone had knelt in the dark of a dressing station at twenty minutes to four in the morning and caught a dying man's last words and written them down, because no one else would. *"Tell her the apples kept."* Twenty-six years old, and what he chose to carry out of the world was apples.

Bram sat down slowly on the edge of his cot with the page held close. Whoever kept this register knew what he had asked. Knew Verrall had summoned him. Knew the exact shape of the trap, and had chosen this moment — the night of his decision — to slip proof under his door. Proof that he had been right. Proof that Wrenn had existed, had died, had a mother somewhere near Nuneaton who thought the post was slow.

It was also, he understood, a test. Or an invitation. Or a hook. Possibly all three. Someone out there in the dark of this camp was risking — what? A dressing-station nurse, the page had said. *Yarrow.* He didn't know the name. But whoever she was, she had done a braver and more foolish thing than any he could imagine: she had taken the machine's own paper and turned it against the machine, one dying man at a time.

And now she had handed him a piece of it, on the night before he was supposed to agree that none of it was true.

Bram folded the page along its original crease. He thought about where to hide it and realised there was nowhere; a man who slept in a hay loft with two colleagues had no hiding places. So he unpicked the stitching of his uniform lining with a pencil point — his hands steadier than they had any right to be — and sewed the page in against his left side, over his ribs, where it made a small stiff rectangle the size of a hymn book. When he lay back down he could feel it with every breath.

Thursday's draft. Nine o'clock tomorrow. Sign, or vanish into the infantry and let the column stay false forever, with his own name added to its cost.

Outside, far to the east, the guns went on with their low grinding argument, patient as tide. Bram lay in the dark feeling the paper rise and fall against his heart, and found that the fear had not gone — it had only changed shape, condensed, become something he could hold.

He knew now what his answer to Verrall would have to be. It was not the answer on the offered page, and it was not refusal either, not plainly, not yet. It was something craftier and more dangerous: the small, clerical courage of a man who checks a sum twice.

In the morning he would go to Captain Verrall, and he would stall — and while he stalled, he would begin to find out who Rafael Yarrow was, and what else her register held, and whether two people who were no one at all could stand between a colonel and two hundred erased lives.

The frost thickened on the hay window. Bram Odell, census clerk, counter of houses and souls, watched the moon cross the glass and did not sleep at all.
