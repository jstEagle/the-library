## 5. The Workshop

The workshop had been a coffin-maker's before the war, which Rafael had always taken for a joke the universe had made at her expense. Now the coffins were long gone and the smell of pine shavings and glue had faded under carbolic and lamp-oil, but the shape of the trade remained: long trestles where planks had been measured for men who would lie down forever, sawdust worked into the grain of the floor so that no amount of sweeping ever quite removed it. She had requisitioned it in January with a signature she was not entirely entitled to use — *storage of medical records, No. 3 Dressing Station* — and the quartermaster, a tired Flanders man named Verhulst who signed anything that kept his ledger tidy, had not looked inside.

Inside there were no medical records. There were two hundred and six names, and counting.

Rafael worked nights because nights were hers. The ward surrendered her at nine; the sisters' hut claimed her only in body until midnight; and between midnight and the grey hour when the guns resumed their argument, the workshop belonged to her and the lamp. It was an army lamp, dented, its chimney smoked if you trimmed the wick wrong. She had learned to trim it right. She had learned a great many small things since November — how to copy a clerk's hand well enough to fool another clerk, how to dissolve the regimental stamp off a requisition form with steam from a kettle, how to sit still when a flare went up over the ridge and turned the workshop's one window briefly blue as a drowned eye.

Tonight the window stayed dark. Rain came down steadily, the soft Flemish rain that did not fall so much as hang in the air and wait to be walked through, and Rafael counted it a mercy. Rain meant fewer men carried in. Rain meant the war rested, briefly, and so could she — resting being, in her experience, the act of writing names in a book hidden beneath a false drawer in a carpenter's bench while shells fell eight miles off.

The ledger itself was not grand. A stationer's book, cloth-bound, the sort a grocer might keep for accounts. She had bought it in Poperinghe with her own money, and this pleased her in a way she never examined too closely: the record of the erased, purchased at retail, paid for by a woman whose brother was among them.

She dipped the pen. *Private T. Ellery, 2nd Btn., died of wounds 4 April, ward C, buried by Rev. A. Murchison, plot marked.* Her hand was steady now. In the first weeks it had shaken, and the shaking had made the ink blot, and she had thought — absurdly, superstitiously — that the blot would damn her, as though God audited ledgers. Perhaps He did. But if so, Rafael reasoned, He would find her column in better order than the Colonel's.

Ellery. She remembered him: twenty, freckled, a farm boy from somewhere flat who had described his mother's hens to her for an hour while his abdomen did what abdomens did after shell fragments found them. He had asked her twice whether the letters got written. She had said yes both times. The official roll said Ellery had been transferred to a base hospital at the coast. There was no base hospital record. There was only Rafael's page, and the memory of hens, and a mother somewhere in Norfolk who would wait for a letter that no officer had authorized anyone to send.

That was the arithmetic of it. That was what Hessler's figures cost, per man, per line: a woman in Norfolk, waiting.

She heard the footsteps a hundred yards out and put the lamp out before she had decided to.

It was not courage. It was training. You heard a step on gravel at three in the morning near the front, you killed your light first and reasoned after. Rafael slid the ledger into the false drawer with a motion her hands had learned without consulting her, drew the bench drawer shut over it, and stood in the blackness beside the wood-store with her heart going like a telegraph key.

The steps stopped at the door. Someone breathed there — she could hear it through the warped boards, quick and badly controlled, the breathing of a man more frightened than fit. Then a knock. Two knocks, a pause, then one more. Not any signal she knew. Not a military pattern at all. Just — knocking. The way a person knocks when they have come to apologize.

"Miss Yarrow." A voice through the door, pitched low, cracking on the second word. "My name is Odell. Bram Odell. Records office. I—" A breath. "I found a page. Under my door. I believe I've found the rest of it now, and I'd rather freeze out here than go back, but I need someone to tell me I'm not mad."

Rafael stood very still. The rain ticked on the roof. Somewhere eastward the guns cleared their throats and fell silent again.

*Under my door.* So Marta had gone ahead and done it — done the thing they had argued about for two nights, the thing Rafael had forbidden and Marta had promised, eyes flashing, not to do unless the moment demanded. Rafael had told herself the girl had obeyed. She should have known better. Marta Lievens lied the way other people breathed, cheerfully and constantly, about her age, her papers, her opinions, and every lie so far had been correct.

The page under his door had been a test, then. Hers or Marta's or fate's. And here was the result standing outside in the rain, breathing like a rabbit, asking politely to be told he was not mad.

If he had been sent — by the captain whose name she knew but had never spoken aloud, by Hessler, by anyone — he would not knock. Sent men kicked doors in. Sent men arrived with provosts and lanterns and forms already filled out. What came to your door alone, soaked, whispering your name like a prayer it wasn't sure of, was something else entirely.

Or it was a trap built by someone cleverer than she believed Hessler to be. She opened the door anyway. Later she would try to explain this to herself and fail: that after five months of carrying the ledger alone, the sound of another frightened human being asking to be believed had simply outweighed the arithmetic of risk.

She unshuttered the lamp, relit it, and looked at him.

He was not what she expected, though she could not have said what she had expected. Younger than his voice suggested — perhaps twenty-five — narrow-shouldered, spectacles fogged with rain, holding his cap in both hands like a man in a church queue. His greatcoat was civilian-cut underneath, a clerk's coat with buttons added, and the whole of him had the apologetic quality of furniture left in a hallway. Water ran off the brim of his spectacles and dripped from his chin. He looked, she thought, like a man who had been rained on his entire life and had never once complained about it where anyone could hear.

"You're bleeding," she said, because she was a nurse before she was anything, and his knuckles were split.

"I hit the wall," he said. "By the crossroads. There was a sentry and I thought— I got down behind the wall and waited, and then I couldn't feel my hands, so I hit them against the bricks to be sure they were still mine." He said it without self-pity, as a simple report of fact, and something in Rafael's chest shifted half an inch. "I'm sorry to come at this hour. I worked out where the page must have come from by the paper stock. Requisition carbon, third-quality, supplied to carpentry workshops for packing lists. There are four workshops on the sector inventory. Three of them burned scrap in their yards all week. This one doesn't." He stopped. "That sounds mad, doesn't it. Saying it out loud."

"Come in," Rafael said, "before you say anything madder."

* * *

She gave him the stool by the stove, and tea that was mostly warm water wearing the ghost of a leaf, and sat across the bench from him with the lamp between them like a border checkpoint. He wrapped his ruined hands around the mug and looked at the workshop — at the trestles, the stacked forms, the kettle, the smell of ink over old pine — with the expression of a man confirming a diagnosis he has dreaded and hoped for at once.

"The page under my door," he said. "Private Ellery. Died fourth of April. The muster roll has him at the coast. I checked the pay records too — that's my work, mostly, checking. Dead men draw pay here, Miss Yarrow. Did you know that? Eleven of them, by my count last month. Somebody signs for it and it vanishes into an account nobody audits, and I asked Sergeant Pike one question about it, just the one—" His mouth twisted. "And the room went quiet. You've heard a room go quiet? Like all the air has been assigned elsewhere. And two days later a captain offers me a choice between signing a retraction and going up the line, and then that night, under my door—" He looked up. "You didn't send it."

"No," Rafael said. "My assistant did. Against my orders."

"She saved my life," he said simply. "I'd signed nothing. I was going to sign. I want you to know that — I'd been composing the retraction in my head for two days, very reasonable wording, I'm good at wording. And then I read Ellery's page and there was a burial plot in it. A *plot*, Miss Yarrow. Coordinates. Someone knows where he lies, and the army says he's alive at the coast. I can't retract coordinates. They don't retract." He set down the mug. His hands were trembling, but he held them out over the bench, palms up, deliberately, as if presenting evidence. "So. Test me however you like. I've thought about how you'd test me the whole walk here, and I've concluded I can't beat any proper test, so I'll simply answer everything and let you judge."

Rafael regarded him for a long moment. The lamp hissed. Outside, the rain kept up its patient work on the roof.

"Why did you ask Pike?" she said. "One question. Men don't risk their necks over arithmetic. What was the question?"

Bram looked at the table. When he answered, his voice had changed — smaller, careful, the voice of a man handling something breakable.

"A private named Odell," he said. "E. Odell. My brother Edward. He went missing at Langemarck last August, and the family telegram said *missing, presumed*, and then nothing, for seven months. Nothing. And I process pay records all day, so I thought — I'll just look. Civilians look things up; it's all I know how to do. And Edward isn't anywhere. Not dead, not transferred, not drawing pay, not *anything*. Seven months and my brother is an absence in a filing system." He swallowed. "So when I found eleven ghosts drawing wages, I asked Pike whether omissions ran in that regiment. Whether men could be left off a roll on purpose. Because if it happened once by accident, it happens twice by design, and I needed to know whether Edward was an accident or a design."

The stove ticked. Rafael found she had to look away, toward the false drawer, toward the two hundred and six.

"He isn't in the ledger," she said quietly. "I'd have remembered the name. If he'd passed through my ward—"

"He didn't. He's listed nowhere at all. That's rather the point of me." Bram managed something that was almost a smile and mostly grief. "You collect the men the officers pretend lived. Nobody collects the ones they simply dropped. I'm here for the dropped ones."

For a moment neither of them spoke. Rafael understood, dimly and with a kind of vertigo, that she was watching a man decide to hand her his whole life — and that he had done it in the space of ten minutes, in bad tea, in a coffin-shop, on the strength of a stolen page and a fogged pair of spectacles. It was reckless. It was exactly, precisely, recklessly what she had done in November with a grocer's account book, and she recognized the fever of it the way one recognizes one's own handwriting.

* * *

She tested him anyway. She was thorough, because five months of solitude had taught her that trust was a wound and wounds required cleaning before closing.

She asked him the supply figures for the sector's carbon paper — he answered without hesitation, then corrected himself on February's allocation, showing his working aloud like a schoolboy. She asked who else could have traced the page to the workshop; he considered it honestly, enumerated two clerks sharp enough, judged one too lazy and one too scared, and admitted the margin of error in a way no fabricator would have bothered with. She asked what he'd tell the captain tomorrow, and he said, "Nothing yet. Stalling. I'm apparently very good at stalling; it's the one thing tonight's given me talent for."

Then she opened the false drawer, took out the ledger, and laid it on the bench between them, and watched his face while he read.

She watched him arrive at Ellery and stop. Watched him turn pages — carefully, wet fingers wiped dry on his coat first, and she noted that, noted the instinct toward care — watched his lips move slightly over the columns: name, battalion, date, manner of death, burial place, next of kin known or unfound. Watched him reach the running total she kept pencilled lightly at each new page's foot, and watched his finger stop on it.

"Two hundred and six," he said.

"Since November."

"And the rolls show—" He flipped back, checking, checking, the clerk reflex running in him like a heartbeat. "These thirty-one appear as wounded-and-evacuated. These forty-four don't appear anywhere at all, same as Edward. The rest are listed as transferred, desertions, one — dear God — one as *killed in action*, which is at least honest, except the family was never notified because the notification queue is 'backlogged,' and I've seen the backlog file, Miss Yarrow, and it isn't backlogged, it's *signed off*—" He closed the ledger gently, both hands flat on the cover, as if keeping something from escaping. "Hessler's casualty figures. That's what this is. He reports fewer losses than occur, the difference draws pay that goes somewhere, and the board sees a colonel whose battalions hold strength on paper that they haven't held in flesh since autumn. It's not even clever. It's just arithmetic that no one was allowed to check."

"No one was allowed," Rafael agreed. "So I checked."

"Why?" He looked up at her, and the question was not suspicion — it was genuine, hungry curiosity, the same thing that had killed his safety in a records room. "Why carry all this alone? You must have known what it would cost."

Rafael considered lying, found she had no appetite for it, and gave him the truth because it was the first time in five months anyone had asked the question and deserved the answer.

"My brother was on that first page," she said. "Thomas Yarrow, listed as evacuated, actually buried by the road at Boesinghe with eleven others and no marker anyone official recorded. I found the burial party's corporal by chance in December and he showed me the spot. I wrote it down because I couldn't *not* write it down. Then the next week there was Ellery's friend in the next bed, asking why Private Ellery hadn't written, and I knew the answer, and there was nowhere to put the answer. So I made somewhere." She touched the ledger's cover. "A name that exists nowhere is a man who never existed. His mother becomes a woman who never had a son. I have met those mothers in advance, Mr. Odell. Every night, in this book. I write to them without knowing their addresses."

Bram had gone very pale. Under the lamp's smoke-yellow light his face looked like paper itself, blank at the margins, crowded in the middle.

"Mrs. Odell," he said. "Our mother. She sets a plate for Edward. Every Sunday. She knows. She *knows*, women know, and there's nothing to be done because there's no paper. Nothing to appeal to. You can't grieve a form, and you can't fight one either." His voice cracked and he mastered it with visible effort. "Two hundred and six plates."

"Two hundred and six," Rafael said, "that we know of. The true number is larger. The true number is whatever Hessler needs it to be."

* * *

They talked until the lamp oil ran low and the window began to think about grey. It was not planning, at first — it was the raw exchange of two people comparing inventories of damage. He told her about the captain's ultimatum and she felt fear move through her like cold water, because the trap around Bram was also a clock ticking over her head. He told her about Marta's page — how it had been folded twice, in a woman's fashion, and he'd known instantly it was a message and not litter. She found herself telling him things she had told no one: the steam trick for the stamps; the night in February she had nearly burned everything out of terror and instead sat with the ledger on her knees until dawn; the way she measured her whole life now in what could be carried out of a room in ninety seconds, because she rehearsed that evacuation every night the way soldiers rehearse rifle drill.

"Ninety seconds," Bram repeated softly. "Ledger, pen, the evidence pages, the stamp blanks. Is that the order?"

"That's the order."

"Then we should decide," he said, and his voice had steadied into something she hadn't heard in him yet, the clerk's calm rising up through the fright like bedrock through topsoil, "what order it stays in. Because Miss Yarrow — Rafael, if I may — this book in this drawer is a confession waiting for a fire. One search. One informer. One quartermaster who opens a door he's never opened. Everything we are is ninety seconds and luck." He adjusted his spectacles, and she saw his hands had finally stopped trembling, now that the fear had a shape and a task. "I make copies of things. It is, professionally, the only thing I do. Let me help you make copies of this — properly, with cover sheets, dispatch formatting, routing codes, so that a copy leaving this building looks as dull as any other paper in the army. Dull paper travels. This—" he touched the cover "—this is contraband. Its copies could be mail."

Rafael looked at him for a long time. Through the window the sky had gone that colour that is not dawn but only the rumor of it, gunmetal thinning to pewter, and the guns had begun again far off, patient as tide. Five months she had kept this alone, and she had believed solitude was the price and also the armour: no accomplice, no witness, no one to give up. She saw now that the armour had a flaw she had never priced in — that a secret carried entirely alone gets heavy in a way that has nothing to do with weight, and that the heaviness eventually does what the provost cannot.

And here was this damp, brave, absurd little clerk, asking to share the load, and every instinct she owned was shouting *trap* and *fire* and *ninety seconds*, and beneath all of it, quiet and terrifying, was the relief — so strong it was closer to grief — of being believed. Of being, worse, *understood*. He had looked at her ledger the way she looked at her patients: as things that could be saved if someone refused to accept the official prognosis.

"If we do this," she said, "you don't sign anything. You don't ask Pike anything further. You stall that captain until I can get Marta clear and we can plan properly. And you understand—" her voice roughened despite her "—that once you're in this, there is no retraction. Not for either of us. Whatever it costs."

Bram Odell put his split knuckles flat on the ledger, gently, the way one touches a Bible or a sleeping child.

"My mother sets a plate every Sunday," he said. "Cost is a word for people who have something left to lose."

Rafael closed her hand over the ledger's edge, and for the first time since November the weight of it shifted — not lighter, never lighter, but shared, the way a stretcher takes two bearers.

"We'll need more lamps," she said. "And we start tonight—"

But the rest of the sentence never got spoken, because the door swung open without a knock, and Marta Lievens stood in it with the wind and rain behind her and a look on her face that Rafael had seen only twice before — both times over bodies.

"They've come to the dressing station," Marta said. "Officers. Asking for you by name, Rafael. Asking about *records*."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere beyond the ridge, the guns lifted their voices, and Rafael understood that the ninety seconds she had rehearsed all winter had just begun counting down.
