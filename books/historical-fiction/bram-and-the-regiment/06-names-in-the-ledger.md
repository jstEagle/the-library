## 6. Names in the Ledger

Bram Odell had never been inside a carpentry workshop before the war, and now he could not enter one without smelling varnish and feeling like a trespasser. This one sat behind the ration stores, half-hidden by a wall of sandbags that someone had stacked with more care than aesthetics — staggered courses, the joints offset like brickwork. The sign above the door still read *R.E. Joinery — Section II*, though the windows had been painted over with a mixture of lime wash and mud until they gave off no light at all, even at dusk.

He had walked past it four times in two days before he worked up the courage to knock.

The page she'd slipped under his door had carried no signature. Only a name — *Private E. Odell, 2nd Btn., killed at Polygon Wood, 26 September* — written in a small, level hand, and beneath it a second line that had kept him awake for the better part of a week: *If you already knew this, forgive me. If you did not, come to where the dead are counted.*

He hadn't known. That was the thing that would not stop turning over in him, smooth and awful as a river stone. His brother Edward had died fourteen months ago, and Bram had learned it from a printed form, and he had grieved the way one grieves at a distance — properly, quietly, in the manner expected of a clerk whose trade was other people's numbers. He had written to his mother. He had helped her draft the letter to the regimental secretary requesting Edward's effects. He had done everything correctly.

And none of it was true, because according to whatever ledger lived behind these sandbags, his brother's death had never been recorded at all.

Which meant no pension. Which meant his mother had spent fourteen months writing polite letters into a silence that had been manufactured by someone with a pen.

Bram knocked twice, waited, then twice again, the way the note had instructed.

The door opened on a chain. A strip of lamplight fell across his boots, and behind it he saw the nurse from the dressing station — Yarrow, Rafaela or Raphael or whatever her full name was; he only ever heard her called Sister Yarrow — watching him with the flat assessment of someone triaging a stranger.

"You got my page," she said.

"I got your page." His voice came out thinner than he wanted. He made himself stand straighter, put his hands where she could see them, absurdly aware of how guilty an honest man could look standing in the dark holding his cap. "I'm Odell. Bram Odell. Records office."

"I know who you are." She didn't open the door wider. "You asked Corporal Voss why a dead man was still drawing separation allowance. You asked him in front of three other clerks."

The question came back to him now with all its original innocence stripped away: *Doesn't anyone check these against the casualty returns?* He had thought it a good question. He had thought questions were his job.

"Yes," he said.

"Voss reported you within the hour. There's a note about you in Captain Aldridge's out-tray. I've seen it." Her eyes moved over his face, cataloguing something. "You should know that coming here is the second-worst decision available to you. The worst is going back and signing what they've put in front of you."

"Why?"

"Because if you sign, you're safe, and safe men don't ask after dead men's pay." She unhooked the chain. "And because I need someone who can read muster rolls without weeping, and I'm told you have the head for it. Come in before the sentry changes."

* * *

Inside, the workshop smelled of cut pine and lamp oil. Saws and planes hung on the walls like surgical instruments in their racks, but the long trestle table down the center belonged entirely to paper. It was stacked in drifts — requisition forms, ration returns, bundles of field-service postcards tied with butcher's string, and at the far end, under a board weighted with two iron clamps, something bound in grey canvas that Bram's eye went to and refused to leave.

One hurricane lamp burned on the table. Beyond its circle the room fell into brown dark, and he understood suddenly the care of the painted windows, the sandbagging, the chain on the door. This was not a records office. This was a hiding place that happened to contain a person.

"Sister Yarrow," he began. "Rafael—" He stumbled on the name; he had never once said it aloud to her. "I don't understand what any of this has to do with me. I'm a census clerk. I count things. I'm not brave, if that matters. I'd like to be clear about that before anything else happens."

"It matters," she said. "Sit down, Mr. Odell."

She moved the clamps, lifted the board, and put the grey canvas book in front of him. Then she stood with her hands folded — waiting, he realized, letting him come to it rather than pushing him toward it, the way one stands back from a wound to let the patient look at it first.

The cover had no title. Just a stamped regimental number from some quartermaster's stock, repurposed. He opened it.

The first page stopped his breath.

It was ruled like a ledger because of course it was; she had used standard army bookkeeping paper, columns drawn true with a straightedge. But instead of debits and credits, the columns ran: *Name. Number. Battalion. Date. Place. Next of kin. Found?* And the entries were in her small level hand, dozens of them to a page, each one complete, each one dated, each one carrying a small annotation in the final column — *no*, mostly, or *no reply*, or, twice, in red, *mother located*.

"How many?" he asked. He already knew the answer would be large. He asked anyway, because asking was how his mind took hold of things.

"Two hundred and six," she said.

He turned pages. The lamp flame shivered with his movement and the names slid through light and shadow — Whitcombe, A.; Fenn, J. T.; Okafor, D.; boys from Lancashire and London and one whole cluster from a village in Norfolk he'd never heard of, seven names in a row, same date, same wood, the annotations marching down the column like a funeral procession. Some entries had scraps pinned or pasted beside them: a corner of a paybook cover, a copied line from a burial return, a woman's address in a hand different from Rafael's — rounder, quicker. Marta, he would learn. Marta wrote the letters.

"These aren't official," he said stupidly.

"No."

"But the deaths occurred. These men are dead."

"Yes."

"And the rolls show—"

"Some of them show nothing. Some show wounded, evacuated. Four are listed as deserters." Her voice stayed level, but something moved underneath it, the way water moves under ice. "Desertion stops the separation allowance immediately. It also lets the family be written to as criminals rather than mourners, which means most families stop writing. It is very efficient, Mr. Odell. I'll say that for it."

Bram sat with the book open in front of him and felt the room tilt slightly, the way it did when a shell landed far enough away to be sound before fear. He was a man of figures. He knew exactly what two hundred and six meant. Two hundred and six was eleven pages of a census schedule. Two hundred and six was a bad month for one battalion, a footnote in a divisional return. Two hundred and six was also — his mind supplied it unasked, helpfully, cruelly — two hundred and six mothers, roughly; some fathers, some wives; perhaps four hundred people sitting in parlours and kitchens from Accrington to Cardiff, waiting for a letter that had been deliberately not written.

"Why does Hessler do it?" he asked. "The figures. What are they for?"

"That's the wrong order of questions." She pulled a stool around and sat opposite him, and in the lamplight he saw for the first time how tired she was — not dramatically, not romantically, but the deep settled exhaustion of someone who had been doing this since before it had a name. "Ask instead: what does a colonel gain by reporting four hundred effectives when he has lost six hundred and six? He gains a battalion that looks intact. He gains reinforcements calculated against a false strength, which arrive too few and too late. He gains a career through two more battles. And when the arithmetic finally comes due — when the battalion breaks because there were never enough men — it comes due somewhere downstream of him." She touched the ledger's edge. "Paper doesn't bleed, Mr. Odell. It just moves the bleeding somewhere else."

"And the board of inquiry—"

"Is being spoken of. Yes. You've heard." It wasn't a question. "Which is why the audit is coming, and why you shouldn't be here long. But first." She reached across and, before he understood what she was doing, turned the ledger toward herself and began working through it with her finger, fast, sure, counting down the pages. "First, the thing you actually came for. I checked the rolls against the casualty clearing stations for the autumn offensives of last year, cross-sorting by surname, because I was looking for a pattern in the omissions and patterns live in clusters. Your surname appears twice."

His mouth had gone dry. "Edward Odell. Second battalion."

"Third entry from the bottom of this page." She rotated the book back to him. "Read it."

He read it.

*Odell, E. 24107. Pte., 2nd Bn. Killed in action, Polygon Wood, 26 Sept. Next of kin: mother, Mrs. H. Odell, 14 Chandler's Row, Norwich. Allowance stopped 30 Sept — listed missing. No subsequent record. No notification traced. Mother wrote regimental secretary Nov., Feb., May. Replies signed by Capt. Aldridge, records: "No further information at present."*

Three times his mother had written. Three times Aldridge had sent the same courteous nothing. Bram stared at the entry and felt something happen in his chest that was not quite pain and not quite rage but occupied the territory between them, vast and unlit, like a shelled house.

"He answered her," Bram said. His voice sounded like someone else's. "She showed me the letters. I helped her write the third one. He told us there was — he said the file remained under examination. Under examination. I thought it meant the chaos of the offensive. I thought it meant the fog. I sat in that office eight hours a day and I believed the fog."

"The fog is real," Rafael said gently. "That's what makes this work. Nine tenths of every omission could be explained by the fog alone. It's the tenth part that isn't — the signature, the corrected figure, the deserter notation on a man with a witnessed grave — that tenth part hides perfectly inside legitimate confusion. Whoever built this system understood that. They never had to forge anything. They only had to decline to correct."

Bram looked up at her. "You found Edward by accident."

"I found your surname by accident. I found you because of what you did next." She paused. "When I confirmed the death against the burial returns — there is a burial return, Mr. Odell. A chaplain buried your brother with nineteen others and filed it properly, and the filing exists to this day in a box that no one has opened, because opening it would require someone to explain why it was never entered. When I confirmed it, I had a choice about whether the next person who noticed was a friend or an enemy. So I wrote to you."

The lamp hissed softly. Somewhere beyond the sandbags a lorry changed gear, the ordinary sounds of an army going about its evening, and Bram sat very still with his brother's life reduced to nine lines in a hidden book and understood, with the particular clarity of grief, that the nine lines were the most anyone in authority had ever done for Edward since the day he died.

"His watch," he said. "His effects. My mother applied for them. The form came back — insufficient record of status."

"They can't release effects for a man who officially never existed in the theatre," Rafael said. "The form was correct. That's the cruelty of it. Everything they sent her was technically correct."

Bram put his hand flat on the page, over the entry, not reading now, just covering it, the way you'd cover a wound. He became aware that his eyes were wet and did nothing about it. He had cried once at the original telegram and been ashamed of it, and here, in this lamplit forgery of an ordinary workshop, shame seemed like the least relevant emotion available.

"My mother is fifty-eight years old," he said. "She takes in sewing. She has spent fourteen months believing she was forgotten because her letters weren't forceful enough. She apologized to me, in her last one, for not knowing how to write a stronger letter." He swallowed. "There is a captain in the records office who has her handwriting in his file, and he knows exactly what he wrote back to her, and he did it three times."

"Yes."

"And there is a colonel who set the machine running, and he is currently arranging to burn the evidence before a board can read it."

"Yes."

Bram took his hand off the page. Something had finished happening in him — the tilting, the vertigo — and what remained was strangely calm, the calm of a man who has finished counting and gotten his total. He had spent his whole life believing that accuracy was a kind of virtue in itself, that a correct figure was its own justification. Now correctness had a body count, and the count was two hundred and six, and one of them shared his chin and his mother's laugh and was buried under a wooden cross in a wood whose name Bram had only ever seen typed.

"Tell me what you need," he said. "Not what I can safely do. What you need."

Rafael studied him for a long moment. Whatever she was looking for, she seemed to find it, because some of the triage-flatness went out of her face and what came through underneath startled him: relief, raw and unguarded, like a door briefly opened onto a lit room.

"I need the omissions corrected eventually, in a forum where correction sticks," she said. "That means testimony, which means the ledger survives, which means copies — because originals burn, Mr. Odell. Originals always burn; it's practically regulation." She drew toward her a stack of blank ledger paper, identical ruling to her own. "I need someone who can reproduce muster-roll formatting so precisely that a copy could pass through a records office without raising an eyebrow. Someone who knows the reference codes, the stamp positions, the initials conventions. I need a man who asks good questions and, from tonight onward, asks them quietly."

"And in exchange—"

"There is no exchange." She said it without heat, as fact. "I can't protect you. You've seen the note in Aldridge's tray; you understand your position better than I do, probably. If you're caught, I will say you forced your way in and I'll burn your name from my copy myself, and it will likely not save you." She held his gaze. "What I can offer is the truth, which is that your brother is owed a record, and your mother is owed a letter with real information in it, and neither of those things exists yet anywhere in the world except in this book. Help me make more of them exist."

Bram looked down at the ledger, at the ruled columns full of the unfound and the unpaid, at Edward's entry third from the bottom of the page with its three unanswered letters noted in red like accusations. He thought of his mother at her sewing table by the window in Norwich, apologizing for the weakness of her pen. He thought of Captain Aldridge's tidy out-tray.

"I'll need to take some blanks back with me," he heard himself say, in the brisk tone he used for inventory. "Different weights of paper — the dispatch covers are a heavier stock, and if we're forging provenance we may as well forge the feel of it. And I'll want access to the actual return formats for the autumn quarter; I can pull those legitimately, nobody audits a census clerk pulling historical schedules." He was already reaching for the pencil tucked behind the clamp, already squaring a fresh sheet against the edge of the table. "Two hundred and six names. How many have complete kin details?"

"One hundred and seventy-one."

"Then thirty-five mothers are still unfound." He looked up. "Where are the gaps?"

For the first time since he'd entered the workshop, Rafael Yarrow smiled — a small, tired, entirely unsentimental smile, but a smile. "Chaplains' burial returns," she said. "Half the gaps close if we can get to the chaplains' boxes before the audit does."

Bram nodded and bent to work, and it was while he was drawing his first guide-lines, matching her ruling stroke for stroke, that the door chain rattled once and Marta slipped in with the cold air, shaking rain off her shawl, and stopped short at the sight of him.

"Sister," she said, and there was something new in her voice, something urgent, "the quartermaster telephoned the stores. Colonel Hessler's staff have requested a full audit inventory." She unwound her scarf slowly. "*Dated for the end of the month.*"
