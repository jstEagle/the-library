## 7. The Colonel's Arithmetic

The winter that year did not so much fall as accumulate, the way debts do — grey days stacked on greyer ones until the sky itself seemed owed to someone. Rafael Yarrow had stopped noticing weather in any ordinary sense. She noticed it only as it affected her work: damp made ink feather and blot; frost made the fingers clumsy; fog was good, because fog meant fewer wounded coming down the road from the salient, which meant hours at the ledger.

She had learned to measure everything this way now — not in days or weeks but in what each interval allowed her. An hour of quiet was forty names copied. A night without shelling was a page of dates verified against the ward book. A convoy's delay was time enough to sew a false seam into the lining of her apron, deep enough to hold folded foolscap. Her whole life had narrowed to an arithmetic of carrying capacity, as though she were already packing for a journey she refused to name.

On the last morning of February, she was doing sums she hated — counting carbolic, counting lint, counting the eleven men in Ward B who would live if the frost held off another week — when Quartermaster Sergeant Doyle came through the workshop door with a requisition chit in his hand and something wrong in his face.

Doyle was a Ulsterman gone soft around the middle, with hands like slabs of butcher's meat and a voice he kept pitched low as a habit of twenty years' service. He was not, strictly speaking, supposed to have anything to do with her. He issued stores; she received them; their acquaintance should have ended at the signature line. But somewhere in the autumn he had begun lingering over those signatures, and one grey afternoon he had looked at the stacks of coffin-wood she'd requisitioned against the casualty returns posted outside regimental headquarters, and had done the same arithmetic she did, and had said nothing about it except, "Bit short again, Sister," and signed.

That was how sympathy worked out here. It never announced itself. It arrived as a slightly generous count of bandages, a crate of candles marked damaged, a man choosing not to see what he saw.

"Morning, Sister." He set the chit down and didn't leave. That was the first wrong thing. Doyle always left; lingering was dangerous and Doyle knew it better than anyone.

"Morning, Sergeant."

"Requisition for lamp oil. You're short." His eyes went past her shoulder, to the back room where the lamplight burned most nights, and then came back to her face with a weight she felt physically, like a hand laid flat between her shoulder blades. "There's been an order come down. From division."

"Orders come down every day."

"Not like this one." He glanced toward the open doorway, and stepped sideways — casually, a big man shifting his bulk out of the light — so that his body blocked the view from the yard. "Colonel Hessler's put the workshop records under audit. Everything. Stores ledgers, requisitions, carpentry output, burial returns, the lot. A Captain Voss is coming to conduct it. Thinned down, the order says." He pronounced the word carefully, the way a man handles a grenade with the pin half out. "*Thinned.*"

Rafael kept her hands still on the chit. Eleven years of nursing had taught her that much: the body betrays you first in the hands, so you give the hands nothing to do. "Records get audited all the time," she said. "Paper breeds out here faster than lice."

"This isn't breeding control, Sister." Doyle's voice dropped further. "I stack the crates that go into the incinerator detail. I've seen the list of what Voss has flagged for disposal. It's not the old stuff. It's not duplicates. It's—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "It's anything with a figure on it that doesn't match what division was told. Burial returns. Pay ledgers. Muster corrections. Anything where a man's name sits next to a number somebody up the chain doesn't want counted."

*Thinned.* The word sat in the room with them. She thought of pages going into a brazier, curling at the edges, names lifting off them as ash. She had imagined this — she had imagined every version of discovery since the night she'd written the first name in the ledger, Private Edwin Carrick, died of wounds, 14 October, absent from every roll — but imagination was a soft thing, a rehearsal in cotton wool. This was a date forming. This was arithmetic turning into schedule.

"When?" she asked.

"That's the other thing." Doyle took a folded paper from inside his greatcoat — a genuine requisition, she saw, for tent pegs, real and dull and safe — and passed it over as cover while he spoke. "Voss arrives Monday week. The audit runs three days. Incineration detail's scheduled the Thursday after." His eyes came up to hers. "Twelve days, Sister."

Twelve days. She heard the number land in her chest and settle there like a swallowed stone. Twelve days of lamplight. Twelve nights in which two hundred and six names either became copies that could survive fire, or became smoke.

"I don't know what you're talking about, Sergeant," she said, because the words were required, a ritual both of them understood. "But I'm short of lamp oil, and I'll thank you to note it."

He almost smiled. It was a terrible smile, worn thin. "Noted. Two tins extra, marked spoiled." He turned to go, then paused at the threshold, his broad back filling the doorway, and said, without looking round, "My brother's boy went missing at Langemarck in August. The family got a form saying *missing, presumed*. Eight months now, nothing else." A beat. "A woman could do worse than keep good books."

Then he was gone across the yard, a big slow man moving too fast, and Rafael stood alone in the workshop with a requisition for tent pegs and twelve days of light.

* * *

She spent the afternoon doing what she always did when fear arrived, which was to make herself useful until usefulness wore the fear down to something she could lift. There were dressings to boil. Ward B's frostbitten needed feet rubbed dry and raised. A gunner with a perforated eardrum wanted someone to shout at him kindly, from the correct side. She did all of it with the stone still lodged under her sternum, and all the while the workshop stood behind her in her mind like a lit window seen from a dark road — warm, exposed, finite.

By the time full dark came and the lamps could be lit without drawing attention, she had composed herself into something functional. She found Bram Odell already there, hunched at the workbench they'd cleared for him, squinting at muster sheets through a jeweller's loupe he'd bought off a dead sapper. He looked up when she came in, and whatever he saw in her face made him set down his pen.

"What is it?"

"Doyle came today." She told him. All of it — Voss, the audit, the word *thinned*, the incineration detail, Thursday after next. She kept her voice level, the voice she used for reporting deaths to men who couldn't afford to hear them shouted. She watched the colour leave Bram's face in stages, like water draining from a sink.

"Monday week," he repeated. "That's — that's nine days before they burn anything. Nine days to copy two hundred and six entries by hand." He did the arithmetic instantly; she'd noticed that about him, the clerk's reflex, numbers arriving before feelings. "Even working every hour we're not needed elsewhere, even with Marta helping before she — " He stopped. Marta's name had become a thing they handled carefully since her detention, a bruise neither of them pressed. "It's not enough. Not for the full ledger with dates and supporting references."

"No," Rafael agreed. "So we stop thinking about the full ledger."

Bram stared at her. In the lamplight his mild census-clerk's face looked suddenly very young, and very frightened, and underneath the fright something harder was setting like concrete. "You mean choose."

"I mean triage." She pulled the ledger from its hiding place beneath the loose floorboard where the old carpenter had once kept his chisels, and laid it open on the bench between them. Two hundred and six names. She knew them the way a mother knows children — not the pages but the individuals: Carrick, whose officer had reported him *wounded, evacuated* when he had died on her table; the twins from the Dorsets, entered twice by clerical error and once, correctly, by her; Lieutenant Fairweather, whose death would have cost his colonel a court-martial and instead had cost nothing at all. "If the workshop burns, everything here burns. So we decide what walks out of here sewn into my uniform, and what we grieve."

"It feels like doing Hessler's work for him," Bram said quietly. "Choosing what he gets to erase."

"He doesn't get to erase anything we've copied. And what we can't carry—" She touched the edge of the page. "What we can't carry, we memorise. I've been memorising since October. Names, ranks, dates, units. It's slower than ink but it doesn't burn."

They worked until the small hours. She read aloud; he wrote; she checked; they swapped. Around three in the morning, copying the entry for a lance-corporal called Hobbes, killed in a trench raid no report ever mentioned, Bram's pen stopped.

"Rafael." He hadn't called her Rafael before; it was always Nurse Yarrow, or nothing, the way people out here protected themselves with titles. "My brother Thomas is in here. Page thirty-one. I've known since you showed me the ledger, and I've copied his entry four times now, and I still — every time I reach his name, my hand does this." He held up the pen. It was shaking. "I wanted you to know that I'm not doing this work because I'm brave. I'm doing it because I can't do anything else. There's a difference, and I think it matters which one you believe you're getting."

She looked at him across the ledger, this small precise man whom a single question had dragged out of a warm office in Étaples and into a conspiracy that would, if it went wrong, end against a wall at dawn. "I don't want brave," she said. "Brave men sign retraction statements. I want stubborn. You're the stubborndest man I've met in this entire war."

Something moved through his face — not quite a smile, but the ground a smile would grow from. He bent back over the page. His hand, she noticed, had steadied.

* * *

The next three days passed in a state she could only describe as double exposure. By daylight she was Sister Yarrow, efficient and unremarkable, changing dressings and signing store receipts with a hand that did not tremble. After dark she was something else — a woman committing treason in careful copperplate, one lamp trimmed low, the floorboard lifted, the pages multiplying in a biscuit tin under Marta's old bunk.

On the fourth evening, Doyle came again. No chit this time; he simply appeared in the doorway as she was banking the stove, and the look on his face told her before he spoke that the arithmetic had changed.

"They've moved it up," he said.

Her stomach dropped as if the floor beneath her had been removed. "Moved it how far?"

"Voss arrives tomorrow. First light. And the incineration detail—" He wet his lips. "Thursday's cancelled. They're burning the flagged material Friday. Day after tomorrow. Hessler's signed it himself. My corporal showed me the order." He shook his head slowly. "Man doesn't wait for a board of inquiry to finish asking questions before he starts answering them, does he? That's not tidiness, Sister. That's a man clearing ground."

Day after tomorrow. The stone in her chest turned over. Twelve days had been a wall to climb; two days was a door closing. She thought of the half-finished copies, sixty-three entries duplicated, a hundred and forty-three still living only in the original and in the increasingly crowded attic of her memory.

"Thank you, Sergeant," she said. "For the lamp oil."

"You'll want more than lamp oil." Doyle's eyes were steady on hers, and for the first time he let her see the whole of what he'd been carrying since Langemarck — not suspicion, not deniability, but choice. "Friday morning, there's a stores convoy running to the coast depot. My wagon's on it. Crates of salvage paper, bound for pulping." He let that sit. "Nobody counts crates of paper going to be destroyed, Sister. Nobody's ever counted them. That's rather the point of this whole war, isn't it."

She understood him then, fully, and it frightened her more than Hessler had. "If anyone opens those crates—"

"Then I'm a quartermaster sergeant who misrouted salvage, and you're a nurse who never saw me." He straightened his cap with those butcher's-hands. "Get your figures right by Thursday night. Whatever you're keeping — and God forgive me, I don't want to know what it is — have it packed by midnight Thursday, because Voss's men will be walking that floor by six."

He left. Through the workshop's small, cracked window Rafael watched him cross the yard, and beyond him, beyond the wire and the flooded fields, she could see the distant stutter of the guns along the salient, patient as a pulse. Somewhere south of them the Germans were massing for the spring everyone whispered about; somewhere behind them Hessler was sharpening his arithmetic into flame.

Two days. Sixty-three entries copied, a hundred and forty-three to go, and a convoy that would not wait.

She went back inside, lifted the floorboard, and reached for her pen.

* * *

She woke Bram by throwing gravel at his billet window, which felt appropriately criminal for the hour. He came down in his greatcoat over his pyjamas, hair flattened on one side, and listened to the new arithmetic without interrupting once.

"Two days," he said at last. "Then we need more hands, and there's exactly one person I trust who can forge a dispatch header fast enough to matter." He was already pulling on his boots. "And there's exactly one place she might be."

"Marta's under watch."

"She's under watch by two bored privates who owe me favours from the census office." Bram stood, and in the dark of the doorway he looked, briefly, nothing like a mild little clerk at all. "Wake the others. Start reading aloud. I'll be back before the lamps need trimming."

She watched him go down the frozen lane, a small hunched figure moving quickly through the blue pre-dawn, and then she shut the door, trimmed the lamp, opened the ledger to entry sixty-four, and began to write as fast as her hand could move.
