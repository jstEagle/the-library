## 8. Copying by Lamplight

*Rafael*

The workshop smelled of sawdust and lamp oil and, after the third night, of ink. Rafael had learned to measure time in cartridges of light — how long a wick would burn before the paraffin ran low, how many names she could set down in that space before her hand began to betray her with tremors she could not afford.

Bram Odell sat across the workbench from her, a carpenter's bench worn smooth at its edges by decades of hands that were not theirs, copying in a clerk's hand so neat it looked printed. He worked the way he spoke: carefully, completely, as though each name were a small door he was afraid of rattling.

"Odell," she said. "You've written *Whitmore* twice."

He looked down. His pen hovered. "So I have." He struck through the second one, blotted it, wrote the correct name beneath in the margin — *Hobbes, P., L/Cpl.* — and went on without complaint or excuse.

That was the thing about Bram Odell that she was only beginning to understand. Other men would have apologized. Apology was a way of asking to be comforted, and comfort took time, and time was the one commodity the workshop did not stock. Bram simply corrected himself and continued, as if error were weather.

They had been at it four nights now. The ledger lay open between them under the hooded lamp, its two hundred and six names arranged in Rafael's cramped columns — name, rank, regimental number, date of death, place, the officer who had signed against the entry, and where the official roll said nothing at all. The copy existed now in three parts, because a single document was a single death; three documents scattered among three plausible destinations might survive any one of them being found.

The cover sheets were Bram's invention. She watched him make them now, on a second sheet of paper, his pen shifting into another personality entirely: block capitals, service abbreviations, the bored bureaucratic cadence of forms that no one ever read because they were designed not to be read.

*Duplicate extract, muster verification, 3rd Bn. Forwarded for record concurrence per standing instruction. Not for distribution.*

"It's beautiful," Rafael said, meaning it the way one means it about a weapon.

"It's boring," Bram said. "That's better. Beautiful things get read. Boring things get filed." He dusted the sheet with sand, tapped it clean, and laid it atop the copied pages. "A dispatch that says nothing important, carrying pages that say everything. If a provost opens it, he'll read the cover, sigh, stamp it, and pass it along. Men are paid to be incurious. I know. I was one of them until last month."

"And if they're not paid to be incurious? If someone opens it properly?"

He considered this honestly, which was another thing she was learning about him. He did not flatter their chances. "Then we've lost those pages," he said. "But not all three. And not the original." He glanced at her, then away, at the ledger. "Not while you're carrying it."

She let that sit. There had been a moment, on the first night, when he had understood without her explaining that the original stayed with her — sewn eventually into her uniform, against her body, wherever her body went. He hadn't argued. He'd simply begun cutting paper to size.

Outside, the guns kept their distant argument. Nearer, an owl called from the shattered poplars along the canal road, and Rafael thought absurdly of the wards at night, the men who moaned in their sleep calling for mothers, for water, for England, in that order usually, sometimes all three at once.

"Bram." She had started using his Christian name somewhere around the second night, without deciding to. It had simply become necessary, the way a splint becomes necessary. "Your brother."

His pen stopped.

"Thomas Odell," she said. "Private, 2nd Bn., killed at St. Eloi, April the ninth. Your mother received a letter?"

"A form," he said quietly. "A printed form with his number filled in. No date of burial. No place. Just—" He made a small gesture, a clerk's gesture, indicating a blank field. "I've been copying other women's sons for four nights and I haven't reached him yet. We're going alphabetically by company. He's in the third section." He picked up the pen again. "I'll get there."

"You don't have to copy him yourself. I can do that page."

"I want to do it myself." A pause. "If that's all right."

"It's right," she said. "That's different."

He bent back over the work. The lamp hissed. Somewhere down the corridor of the requisitioned workshop, wind moved through broken glass in the high window, making a sound like a man whistling very softly to keep his courage up.

At half past two by Rafael's wristwatch — a dead man's watch, she never told Bram whose — they finished the section. She checked Bram's columns against the original, name by name, running her finger down both pages like a woman counting stitches. Two errors total. Both corrected. She initialed the corner of each completed sheet with a mark that was not her initials, a small anchor-and-bar symbol she had invented for the purpose, meaningless to anyone but the two of them.

"We should sleep," Bram said.

"In a moment." She sat back. Her spine crackled like a fire taking hold. Across the bench, Bram's face was grey with fatigue, shadowed under the eyes, and still he sat straight, hands flat on the table, waiting — not asking — to see whether she needed anything else from him tonight.

There was something in that patience that frightened her more than Hessler did. Trust, she had learned, was not a feeling. It was a structural decision, like load-bearing timber, and once you put weight on it you could not take the weight off again without bringing the roof down on your head.

"Go on, then," she said. "Cot's behind the timber rack. Marta keeps it made up."

"Marta," he repeated. The name landed strangely between them, and Rafael felt it land. They had not spoken of Marta in two days. The quartermaster's warnings had grown more specific — an audit, Hessler's signature on the requisition, a date — and every hour spent here was an hour stolen from someone who might pay for it. Marta had gone back to the dressing station, ostensibly reassigned, actually hidden, and Rafael carried the arrangement like a stone in her boot.

"She's safe," Rafael said, too quickly.

"I didn't ask."

"You wanted to."

"Yes," Bram admitted. "But wanting isn't asking. My father used to say a man can want all day and it costs nothing till he speaks." He rose, joints cracking, and gathered his coat. At the doorway he paused. "Goodnight, Rafael."

"Goodnight, Bram."

The lamp burned on. She did not blow it out. She had discovered that she could not bear to end the day in darkness in this room, with all these names around her, so she let it gutter down to a blue bead of flame and sat with the ledger open on her knees, reading names aloud in a whisper, the old nurse's habit, checking the living by voice.

When she finally slept, she dreamed of ink.

*Bram*

Bram Odell had spent eleven years learning to disappear inside offices, and now, perversely, the skill was keeping him alive. By day he sat in the regimental records annexe with forty other clerks, copying casualty returns in his small precise hand, invisible as furniture. By night he forged documents in a carpenter's workshop with a woman whose eyes missed nothing, and he had never in his life felt so fully awake.

It should have frightened him more than it did. He examined himself for fear the way one probes a bruise, and found some, certainly — a flinch when boots sounded in the annexe corridor, a cold wash when Captain Ellery's voice passed within earshot — but underneath the fear was something steadier, almost pleasant, like the warmth of a stove after years outdoors. He was doing work that counted. Every line he copied was a line that could not be erased twice.

The forgery was the part he loved, and loving it worried him.

He had discovered a talent. Give him a specimen of genuine army paperwork and twenty minutes, and he could produce a twin — the right paper stock, the right rubber-stamp ghosting, even the right smudges, because he had learned that authentic bureaucracy is never clean. Real forms are dropped in mud, stained with tea, annotated by three different hands in three different inks. So Bram aged his cover sheets deliberately: a thumbprint of grease, a coffee ring applied with the dregs of his canteen mug, a marginal note in a fictitious staff officer's hand — *seen, no action reqd, R.W.* — initialed with letters that matched no one living.

"You enjoy this," Rafael had said the second night, watching him blot a forged tea stain with something close to pride.

"I'm good at it," he'd answered. "Those aren't the same thing. But they're related."

Now it was Thursday, and Thursday meant the annexe released him at noon for the weekly run of duplicate returns to the base depot, and Thursday meant his legitimate courier's satchel could carry illegitimate cargo in its false bottom — a compartment Marta had shown him how to build from a biscuit tin and canvas, before she disappeared back into the dressing station's shadows.

He walked the canal road with the satchel knocking gently at his hip and rehearsed his innocence. He was good at innocence. It was nearly native.

The drop point was a ruined estaminet near the crossroads, its sign hanging by one chain — *À LA BELLE VUE*, the paint flaked to ghosts of letters. The arrangement was Rafael's: papers sealed in oilcloth, left in the bread oven behind the collapsed bar, collected on alternate days by a chaplain attached to the Lines of Communication who owed Rafael a debt no one spoke about. Bram had met the chaplain once, briefly, a tired man with shell-shocked hands who looked at the satchel and then at Bram and said only, "God sees the ledgers, son. Someone has to see the rest."

Bram knelt in the cold oven's brick mouth, wedging the oilcloth packet deep into the flue where even a fire wouldn't easily reach it, and heard the bicycle bell.

Not the chaplain. The chaplain came on foot and coughed before speaking, always, a habit of courtesy or gas.

Bram drew back against the cold brick. Through the estaminet's broken front window he saw a military policeman dismounting, unhurried, folding the bicycle's stand. Red caps. Provost.

The MP stood in the road for a long moment, looking at the ruined building with the professional boredom of a man checking a place off a list. Then, instead of leaving, he walked slowly around the side — toward the rear. Toward the kitchen. Toward the oven.

Bram's mind performed the clerk's trick it knew best: it indexed. Exit through the far wall — no wall, just a gap where a door had been, opening onto the orchard behind. Thirty yards of open ground, then the hedge, then the sunken lane. The packet was in the flue; if the MP searched the oven, the oilcloth might pass unnoticed among sixty years of soot, or might not.

Boots crunched on glass in the next room.

Bram moved. Not fast — fast was fatal — but with the smooth economy of a man who had learned, in the annexe, exactly how much motion attracted attention. Through the gap, into the orchard, down behind the hedgerow into the lane, walking, always walking, a clerk out for his health in a landscape with no health in it. Behind him, faintly, he heard the MP kick something metallic, mutter, and remount. The bicycle bell rang once, absurdly cheerful, receding toward the crossroads.

Bram leaned against a pollarded willow and discovered his heart going like a telegraph key. He laughed — a short, appalled sound — and pressed his hand flat against the bark until the shaking stopped.

Then he thought: *he wasn't searching randomly. He stopped here. This was on a list.*

Someone had given the provost a list of places, and the estaminet was on it.

*Rafael*

She knew something was wrong before he said a word. He came up the workshop steps at dusk with his cap wet from the drizzle and his face doing what faces did in the wards when men were trying to report their own wounds as someone else's.

"They visited the drop," Bram said. "An MP. Alone, on a bicycle. He searched the building."

"The packet?"

"Still in the flue. He didn't find it — I don't think he was looking hard enough. But Rafael, he had a list. You don't cycle to one ruin in Flanders for exercise." He set the satchel down on the bench, and she noticed his hands were steady, which meant the fear was being held somewhere expensive. "The quartermaster said audit. Audits come with lists of premises. Records annexes, store sheds—"

"—workshops." She finished it for him. The word dropped between them and neither picked it up.

For a long moment the rain ticked against the skylight, and the lamp hissed, and the ledger lay open on the bench with its patient hundreds of names, oblivious, the way the dead are oblivious, having delegated everything to the living.

"All right," Rafael said.

"All right?"

"We accelerate. Three sections become six. We stop being careful about hours and start being careful about exits." She was already moving, pulling the copied stacks from their hiding places behind the timber rack, laying them out, assessing weight and bulk the way she assessed a wounded man — what could walk, what must be carried, what could not be saved. "You said the courier run goes out Tuesday. We put two full sets on it instead of one. The third set goes with the chaplain Sunday. The original stays with me."

"And if the workshop is audited before Tuesday?" Bram asked. "If soldiers walk in tomorrow morning?"

"Then you are a records clerk conducting an authorized spot-verification, alone, unsupervised, and I am the nursing sister who came to collect bandage crates, and the ledger is in the flour barrel under the oats, where it has been since Monday, and neither of us has anything to declare because neither of us was asked anything." She looked up at him. "Can you do that? Stand in a room full of soldiers and be nobody?"

Something moved through his face — not quite a smile. "Rafael, I've been nobody for thirty-one years. Tomorrow I'll be the best nobody in Flanders."

She laughed. It startled her, the sound coming out of her own chest, rusty as a gate in winter. When had she last laughed in this room? The workshop had been a crypt for four months, and now it held two people and a joke and a plan, and the difference was larger than the room.

Bram hung his coat on the nail Marta had hammered there, rolled his sleeves, and sat down at the bench. "Where were we?"

"*Verelst,*" she said. "Corporal, C Company. Killed at Hill 60, August the fifteenth. Left off the October return entirely."

"Left off entirely," he murmured, dipping his pen. "As if the ground declined to mention it."

They worked. The lamp burned. Outside, the rain thickened toward the coast and the guns muttered their slow arithmetic, and somewhere south of them, beyond the dark fields, a colonel was signing papers that would bring soldiers with a list to this door. Neither of them said so. Both of them knew.

Near midnight, Bram set down his pen and flexed his writing hand, and said, without looking up:

"When this is over — if we're over — I should like to see the sea. Properly. Not a port. A beach, with nothing on it."

"Why a beach?"

"Because everything we've touched for two years has been either filed or buried. I want to stand in front of something that does neither. That just comes in and goes out and doesn't pretend to be permanent."

Rafael looked at him across the lamplight, at the ink on his fingers and the exhaustion carved under his eyes and the stubborn gentleness that had survived a census office and a war and her.

"Then we'll see it," she said. "After Tuesday."

He nodded, and picked up his pen, and turned to Corporal Verelst's page.

And it was then, in the quiet that followed, that they both heard it — footsteps on the gravel path outside, unhurried, several pairs, and the deliberate scrape of a lantern-slide being opened.

Bram's eyes found hers across the bench. The lamp flame stood perfectly still between them, as if it too had been ordered to attention.

"Not the audit," Rafael whispered, reaching for the flour barrel. "Audits come in daylight."

The knock, when it came, was soft. Almost apologetic. That was somehow worse.
