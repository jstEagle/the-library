## 9. A Visit from the Provost

The tip came to us disguised as weather.

Marta mentioned it first, the way she mentioned everything—sideways, with her hands busy, as if words spoken while mending could do less damage than words spoken still. *They're searching carpenters' shops on the Poperinghe road,* she said, pulling a thread tight with her teeth. *A joiner I know lost his stock of glue to two provost sergeants who didn't so much as wipe their boots.* She spat the thread free and looked at me across the lamplight, her face carefully empty. *Looking for hoarded timber, they said.*

Hoarded timber. In a war that ate wood the way fire ate straw—duckboards, stretchers, crosses for the graves they were digging faster than the joiners could build them—no one searched for hoarded timber. They searched for paper.

I had known this day would come the way one knows a wound will turn septic: not the hour or the minute, but the certainty of it, sitting in the blood. For three weeks I had felt the workshop's safety thinning like light in October, each morning a little more precarious, each knock at the door a little harder to answer without my heart climbing into my throat. We had been careful. Careful meant the ledger lived under the false bottom of a coffin crate, that the copying was done after midnight with shutters blacked and lamp turned low, that only three people alive knew what the workshop truly was. But careful, I had learned at the dressing station, was a currency that bought less every day. Somewhere out there a man had decided to sell his conscience, or spend his fear, and we were the price.

*How long?* I asked Marta.

She shrugged, threading her needle again. *Could be days. Could be never. Sergeants get bored.* Then, because she was Marta and kindness embarrassed her: *You should move some of it anyway. The obvious things.*

The obvious things. As if there were anything obvious about two hundred and six names copied in a dead woman's careful hand.

* * *

I spent that evening doing what I always did when fear arrived: I worked. There is a particular clarity that comes of terror when you refuse to let it paralyze you—a coldness, almost pleasant, in which the hands know what to do before the mind consents. I sorted. That was all. I sorted the way I had once sorted wounded men at Ypres, in those hours when the shells came faster than the stretcher-bearers could carry: by likelihood of survival. Papers that could be burned without loss went into one pile. Papers whose loss would be survivable went into another. And the third pile—the names, the dates, the arithmetic of men who had died and been unwritten—I wrapped in oilcloth and lowered into the coffin crate's false bottom, and then I sat on the crate lid and thought about how strange it was that I had come to measure my life's work against the inside dimensions of a coffin.

Bram arrived after dark, coming through the yard the back way, whistling the three-note pattern we'd agreed on—something half-remembered from a music hall tune, absurd and unmistakable. He came in with rain on his shoulders and an expression he tried to arrange into calm and failed at.

*You've heard,* he said.

*Marta heard.*

*It's worse than a rumor now.* He sat down across from me without being asked, which by then was his privilege and mine both. His hands wouldn't stay still; he kept flattening them on his knees like a man smoothing wrinkles from cloth that wouldn't lie flat. *Sergeant Okafor tipped me. Provost detail drew rations for twelve at the caserne this morning. Twelve men, two vehicles. That's not a timber inspection, Rafael. That's a search party.*

Twelve men. I looked at him and saw my own fear doubled in his face, and something else besides—the census taker's mind already working, turning over numbers the way other men turned over cards. Twelve men could tear a workshop apart in an hour. Two vehicles meant transport for whatever they confiscated, and possibly for whoever they detained.

*When?*

*Nobody knows. That's the cruelty of it. Okafor thinks within the week.*

Within the week. I nodded, and found my voice was steadier than I had any right to expect. *Then we have within the week to make certain they find nothing worth finding.*

We talked until nearly midnight—the disposition of papers, Bram's plan to forge audit covers that would make our copies look like routine dispatch abstracts, the routes out of the yard if anyone ever came over the wall instead of through the gate. It was good talk, useful talk, the kind of talk that builds a wall between you and panic brick by brick. And underneath the talk ran the other thing, unspoken but present as the lamplight: that we had become, without ceremony or confession, two people who planned their lives around a shared secret, and that neither of us any longer pretended otherwise.

He left at one. Marta was already asleep in the loft she kept above the storeroom, having refused weeks ago to sleep anywhere else—*someone honest ought to live here,* she'd said, which I understood later was her way of telling me she knew everything and had chosen me anyway.

I lay awake in my cot behind the office partition and listened to the rain, and told myself the usual lies about probability and boredom and sergeants who lose interest. Sleep took me sometime before dawn, shallow and dreamless, the sleep of a woman keeping one ear open.

* * *

They came on the fourth day, at ten minutes past nine in the morning, while I was bandaging the hand of a Belgian boy who'd caught it in a planing machine.

I heard the vehicles first—that heavy, institutional sound of army lorries changing gear in a narrow street, so unlike civilian traffic that my body knew it before my mind did. My hands finished tying the bandage. Some part of me noted, distantly, that my fingers were steady, and filed the observation away the way one files away the color of the sky before a storm.

Then the gate rattled, and Marta called up the yard in her flat voice—*Miss Yarrow. Visitors*—and the morning changed shape forever.

There were eleven of them, not twelve; perhaps boredom had claimed a man somewhere along the road. A captain led them, young through the cheeks and old through the eyes, the particular agelessness the army manufactures in its officers by the dozen. Two sergeants flanked him. The rest spread through the yard and workshop with the unhurried competence of men who had done this many times and would do it many more, and who felt about it precisely nothing.

*Captain Renwick,* the officer said, showing papers I barely glanced at. *Military police. We're conducting an audit of requisitioned premises in the sector. You are?*

*Rafael Yarrow. Nursing sister, seconded to casualty clearing. This workshop processes splints and burial fittings for the sector.*

*Then you won't mind if we look around.*

*I don't suppose it matters whether I mind.*

He almost smiled at that. Almost. Then his face closed again and he gestured, and the search began.

I will not pretend I watched it with detachment. I stood in the middle of my own workshop with a boy's half-bandaged hand forgotten under mine and watched eleven strangers dismantle the small world I had built, and every muscle in my body sang the same useless song: *run, fight, lie, confess.* I did none of them. I stood still and let my face go into the expression I had worn over too many dying men—the smooth, attentive nothing that nurses learn because grief is a luxury the ward cannot afford—and inside that expression I screamed.

They were thorough. God, they were thorough. They opened every crate and ran bayonets through the wood shavings. They pulled the tools from their racks and weighed each one as if a hammer might be a coded message. They climbed into the loft where Marta slept and shook out her blankets, held her spare dress to the light, read her letters from her sister in Lille—read them, I saw, with real attention, one sergeant's lips moving over the French. They found the false bottom in the coffin crate within twenty minutes.

I had moved everything, of course. Three nights earlier I had transferred the ledger to its final hiding place, one I had designed for exactly this moment and never believed I would use: beneath the floor of the drying shed, under a flagstone, in a cavity lined with tar paper, accessible only by levering up a stone that weighed as much as a man. The false bottom held nothing now but a nurse's textbook and six months of legitimate supply requisitions. Sergeant Okafor—or Captain Renwick, rather—leafed through them with mounting dissatisfaction, and I watched him fail to find me guilty, and hated myself for the relief.

But searches are not only about what is hidden. Searches are about people. And it was Marta they circled back to, again and again, in that way investigators have when the evidence disappoints them and they reach instead for the softest thing in the room.

She was working through it all, you understand. That was the thing I couldn't stop seeing afterward, the thing that woke me at night for months. While they tore her blankets apart and read her letters, she went on sanding stretcher handles, because stopping would have been an admission of something, and Marta did not admit things. When the sergeant asked her name she gave it. When he asked how long she'd worked here she said since March. When he asked why a woman of forty-one was doing carpenter's labor in a war zone she said, *Because the alternative was nursing, and I can't stand the sight of blood,* and two of the younger military policemen laughed despite themselves.

It was the letters that did it. Her sister in Lille wrote in French; Lille was occupied territory; correspondence with occupied territory passed through German lines somehow, or was smuggled, or was simply suspect, and suspicion does not require logic, only appetite. I watched the captain read the fourth letter through twice, and I watched him arrive at a conclusion he had probably carried through the gate with him and was now pleased to find confirmation for.

*Mme Vasseur.* He pronounced it carefully, wrongly. *Your assistant. Where was she born?*

*Poperinghe,* I said. *She's a local woman. Her family has been here three generations.*

*Her sister lives in Lille.*

*Her sister married a Lille man before the war and got trapped there when it fell. Half of Flanders has family across the lines, Captain. Do you propose to detain all of them?*

The silence that followed had a texture. The workshop went quiet around it—even the boy with the bandaged hand stopped breathing loudly—and I understood that I had made an error, the oldest error, the error of defending someone too well. Suspicion is a dog; argue with it and it bites.

Captain Renwick folded Marta's letter along its original creases, very precisely, and put it in his breast pocket.

*Mme Vasseur will accompany us,* he said. *For questioning. Routine.*

*She sweeps floors and sands stretchers,* I said. *What questions can you possibly—*

*Sister Yarrow.* He turned to me then and I saw that behind the ageless officer's face was a tired man doing sums, and that I was not worth much in his arithmetic, but that I might be worth something if I made myself difficult. *You may lodge a protest through your matron. In writing. Now—is there anything on these premises you wish to declare before my men conclude their search?*

There was a floor under a flagstone under a floor, and two hundred and six names wrapped in oilcloth, and I looked this tired young captain in the eye and said, *Nothing.*

* * *

They took her out through the gate at half past eleven, between two military policemen, her shawl pinned crooked because they hadn't given her time to pin it straight. She did not look back at me. I have thought about that often since—whether it was pride, or mercy, or simple economy, a woman refusing to spend her last look on someone who had brought this down on her. She walked to the lorry with her spine straight and her chin level, and the neighbors came to their doors to watch, because that is what neighbors do, and by nightfall the street would know that the widow Vasseur had been taken away in a police lorry, and by morning the story would have grown whatever details gossip needed it to grow.

I stood in the gateway until the lorry turned the corner. Then I went back inside and finished bandaging the Belgian boy's hand, because he was still sitting there with his mouth open and his palm out, and work is the only prayer I know.

Afterward, alone, I let myself feel it—not the fear, I was used to fear, but the other thing, the newer thing. Guilt. It came up through me slowly, like water rising in a trench, and it found every seam I had.

Here is what I had told myself since the beginning: that I was careful, and that care was a wall, and that the wall would keep the danger on my side of it. That whatever risk existed belonged to me, because the work was mine, the ledger was mine, the rebellion was mine. Bram had chosen in with his eyes open—but Marta. Marta had been hired to sweep floors and keep the stove lit and manage the accounts of a splint workshop. I had never told her what the ledger contained. I had never needed to; she had known anyway, known and said nothing and slept above it every night, and I had let her, because her presence made my cover stronger and my loneliness smaller, and because I wanted, selfishly, not to be alone in it.

And now she was in a cell somewhere answering questions about letters from her sister, and the wall I had built had proven to be exactly as solid as everything else built in wartime, which is to say not at all.

I sat on the coffin crate until the light went long and gray. The workshop around me bore the search like a field bears a battle—things upended, drawers emptied onto benches, a fine snow of sawdust over everything, the smell of other men's tobacco soaked into the shavings. Eleven strangers had spent two hours in this room looking for the truth, and the truth had lain four feet below their boots under a stone none of them thought to weigh, and I had won, and winning felt like this: like ash in the mouth, like a crooked-pinned shawl going out through a gate.

Bram came at dusk, whistling his three notes softly, and found me in the dark without the lamp lit. He heard the whole of it standing by the bench, and I watched his face go through the same sequence mine must have gone through—fear, calculation, and then the guilt arriving, heavier than either, because he had located Marta's cell through his records office access within two hours of her detention and knew already what I did not: that questioning meant the interrogation cells at the caserne, and that the innocent did not come out of those quickly, or unchanged.

*They'll hold her a few days,* he said quietly. *Routine cases run five, six days. Then either they charge her or they release her.*

*And if they charge her?*

He didn't answer, which was an answer.

I looked at my hands in the darkness—at the hands that had written two hundred and six names, that had sewn and bandaged and buried and copied, that had done so much small good and could not now undo one medium-sized harm. *She swept my floors,* I said, and heard my voice crack on it, which embarrassed me. *She slept above a coffin crate full of treason because I asked her to trust me, and I never even paid her properly.*

*You'll get her out.* Bram said it with the flat certainty of a man deciding something, and I realized with a jolt that it was not comfort he was offering—it was a plan, forming even as he spoke, behind the census clerk's eyes that could find any name in any roll. *I can find where they've put her. I can find who signed the detention order and what queue it sits in and who owes whom a favor in that building. It's only paper, Rafael. Everything in this army is only paper. Paper can be found, and paper can be moved, and paper can be—* He stopped himself, hearing what he was saying, understanding what it would cost him to move other people's paper, and I watched him decide to say it anyway. *I'll get her out.*

That night, after he left, I pried up the flagstone in the drying shed and unwrapped the oilcloth just to touch the pages, the way a doubter touches a relic. Two hundred and six names. I had always known the ledger would cost something—I had written the first name with that knowledge already settled in me like lead shot. But I had imagined the cost as mine. Arrest, perhaps. A tribunal. The comfortable arithmetic of martyrdom, in which I alone paid.

The candle guttered. Below my feet, under tar paper and clay, the dead waited in the dark for me to carry them somewhere safe, and upstairs in the loft Marta's bed stood empty with her blanket still thrown back where the provost's sergeant had shaken it out, and I understood at last that no one carries a secret alone, however hard they dig the hiding place. The secret reaches outward. It spends the people around you like currency, whether you consent or no.

I wrapped the pages back up and pressed the stone down into its bed of clay, and I made myself a promise there in the freezing shed, kneeling on cold earth: that Marta's freedom would come before any further copying, before Bram's clever forged covers, before my own safety, before everything. That I would spend whatever the spending cost.

Three days later, Bram came through the yard at dusk, and he was not whistling, and I knew before he reached the door that the price had gone up again.

*They've moved her,* he said. *And Rafael—there's something else. You need to sit down.*
