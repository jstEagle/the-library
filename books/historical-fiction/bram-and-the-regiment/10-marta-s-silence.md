## 10. Marta's Silence

They took Marta Kessel at half past nine in the morning, and by noon the records office had already stopped speaking her name.

Bram learned of it the way he learned most things now — through paper. A routing slip crossed his desk marked *detainee transfer — provisional*, and there she was: K., M., civilian auxiliary, workshop section, held under Section 4 pending inquiry. Fourteen words on a buff-coloured card. No charge. No destination beyond a number that meant the corps detention shed behind the provost's compound, where the army kept what it could not yet decide to keep or throw away.

He sat with the slip for a long time. Around him the office went on breathing its dry, clerical breath — the tick of the date stamp, the rasp of Sergeant Coyle's pencil sharpener, someone laughing two desks over about a letter from home. The world had not paused for Marta Kessel. The world did not pause for anyone; that was the whole machinery of it, Bram thought. Men vanished and the ledgers kept their rhythm. He had noticed it months ago as an oddity of arithmetic. Now he understood it as a moral fact.

Coyle glanced over. "Bad news?"

"Duplicate entry," Bram said. "Nothing."

He filed the slip where it was meant to go, then took it out again and put it in his tunic pocket instead, against his chest, where it lay all morning like a coal.

* * *

The census access was the one thing he still owned.

It had come to him absurdly — seconded clerk, mild little Bram Odell, trusted because no one had ever imagined he was worth distrusting. The population returns, the billeting registers, the movement cards for every civilian worker attached to the lines of communication: all of it passed through his hands, or the hands of men too busy to care that they did. He knew how to find people. It was the one talent the war had sharpened in him, and now he would use it to find a woman who had been taken for the crime of working near him.

He told himself, walking across the compound in the grey drizzle of early afternoon, that he was going only to look. To establish where she was. To think clearly before doing anything foolish. This was a lie of the same species as the ones he'd been telling since the day he'd asked his question about Private Enfield's pay record — the polite lie, the lie that let a man walk into a room while pretending he hadn't already decided why.

The provost's compound occupied a converted estaminet, all its lace curtains gone and its windows wired over. A military policeman with a face like a shut gate checked his papers twice and let him into the outer office, because a census clerk with a requisition form was weather, not news. Bram asked for the detainee log with the flat confidence he had practised in mirrors of the mind, and the corporal behind the desk — young, chilblained, drowning in forms — slid it over without asking why.

Marta Kessel. Admitted 09:40. Transferred 11:15 to holding room three. Interrogation scheduled: tomorrow, 08:00, Captain Voss presiding.

Voss. Bram knew the name the way you know the name of a dog that has bitten someone on your street. Voss was the captain who had offered him his choice — sign the retraction or the trenches — six weeks and a lifetime ago. Voss did not conduct interrogations; he conducted erasures, and the men who sat across from him came out smaller, agreeing to whatever made the inconvenience stop.

Tomorrow at eight. Which meant tonight.

"Anything else?" the corporal asked.

"Yes," Bram said, and heard himself add, lightly, "I'll need a movement authorisation countersigned. Civilian escort matter." He produced the form he had filled in that morning with hands steadier than his heart — a routine transfer request, a woman named as *auxiliary labour, reallocated*, needing only a provost stamp to make her removal look like paperwork rather than rescue.

The corporal read it. Bram watched him read it, watched the chilblained fingers move down the columns, and understood with terrible clarity that he was gambling everything on the fact that nobody, anywhere in this machine, read anything properly anymore. The war had made them all illiterate in the things that mattered.

"Escort detail's short," the corporal said. "You're saying the records office provides the escort?"

"I'm saying it's been approved at my end," Bram said. "Your end just needs the stamp."

A pause. Rain ticked against the wired glass. Then the stamp came down, and the red ink bloomed like a small wound, and Bram Odell walked out of the provost's compound carrying the freedom of another human being inside a folder under his arm.

He was halfway across the parade ground before his legs began to shake.

* * *

There was more wrong to do. That was the thing no one warned you about — that the first compromise was never the last, that each one reached back for your sleeve and said, *just this once more, just enough to finish what you started.*

To move Marta he needed a vehicle, and to get a vehicle he needed Sergeant Coyle, and to use Coyle he needed to lie to a good man. So at four o'clock Bram stood in the records annex with his hat in his hands and told Coyle that there was a clerical irregularity — a civilian detainee wrongly processed, mixed files, an embarrassment for the office if it wasn't quietly corrected before the audit — and wouldn't Coyle, who had the lorry allocation for Thursday, see his way to a run into Poperinghe with a passenger?

Coyle looked at him for a long moment. He was forty years old, Coyle, with a photograph of three daughters taped inside his ledger lid and a face built for disappointment, and Bram watched him weigh the request against everything unspoken between them. Coyle knew something was off about Odell lately. Everyone knew something was off about Odell lately. But Coyle had lost a son at Loos and had never once been told officially that it had happened, and perhaps that was in the weighing too.

"Thursday," Coyle said finally. "And I don't want to know what's in the back of my own lorry. Understood?"

"Understood."

"And Odell." Coyle capped his pen. "Whatever it is you've stepped in — clean your boots after. There's men here who'd walk you to the front gate themselves if they smelled it on you."

"There are," Bram agreed quietly. "I know."

* * *

Thursday came grey and cold, with a wind off the flats that carried the far-off sound of the guns — louder now, everyone said, closer every week, though the maps in the office had not yet admitted it. At seven in the evening Bram walked into holding room three with his folder and his forged authorisation and his heart going like a man running for a train.

Marta sat on the plank bed with her coat folded over her knees. She had been there four days, and four days showed on her — not beaten, nothing so simple, but worn down the way stone is worn down, by repetition, by questions asked in circles, by the particular cruelty of being given nothing to push against. When the door opened she looked up with an expression Bram had seen on soldiers' faces at the dressing station window: braced for worse.

"Census office," Bram said to the guard, showing the folder. "Transfer order. Reallocation of civilian labour."

The guard barely glanced at it. The stamped page did its work. And then Marta was standing, and her coat was on her shoulders, and they were walking down a corridor lit by one guttering bulb, and neither of them spoke until the yard door opened and the cold hit them and Coyle's lorry coughed in the dark with its engine running low.

"In the back," Bram murmured. "Under the sacking. Please."

She climbed up without a word. But at the tailboard she turned, and in the light spilling from the guardroom he saw her face clearly, and it was not grateful and it was not afraid. It was level, and tired, and it looked at him the way Rafael Yarrow looked at things — measuring.

"You're the clerk," she said. "Hers."

It was not a question. He felt it land somewhere beneath his ribs.

"The lorry goes to Poperinghe," he said. "Sergeant Coyle drives. He'll set you down at the convent on the Rue de—"

"I know where the convent is." She swung up into the dark. Her voice drifted back down, low and even. "Tell her I kept quiet. Tell her I'd do it again. And tell her—" a pause, the sacking rustling "—tell her the copying isn't careful enough. They found carbon dust on my cuffs. That's how they started on me. Tell her to burn her gloves."

Then Coyle engaged the gears and the lorry rolled out of the yard, and Bram stood alone in the wet gravel listening to the engine fade toward the road, feeling the strangest thing: relief with a hole in the middle of it, like a shell casing with the charge removed. She was free. She was free, and she had spent her last minutes of captivity giving instructions for the work, and he understood suddenly that Marta Kessel had known exactly who was taking her and why, and had chosen to be taken quietly so that no thread ran back to the workshop.

She had paid for his secret with four days of her life and offered, as thanks, better tradecraft.

* * *

The cost arrived on Monday, as costs do, wearing an ordinary face.

It came as a memorandum: *reorganisation of secondary access, census liaison duties consolidated, effective immediately.* In plain language, Bram's pass to the movement registers was withdrawn, his counter-signature rights revoked, and his desk moved from the corner by the window — where he had sat for eleven months, where the light came in in the afternoon — to a table in the corridor outside the strong room, where there was no light worth the name and no register within reach.

Coyle brought him the memo personally. The sergeant's face was carefully empty, which was itself an answer.

"It wasn't me," Coyle said quietly. "I want that understood. Whatever you did, whoever pulled your teeth for it — that didn't come through me."

"I know it didn't."

"Somebody audited Thursday's vehicle log." Coyle looked out the corridor's end, where the grey rectangle of the courtyard showed rain coming on. "Somebody asked why a lorry ran to Poperinghe with no manifest cargo. Somebody asked who signed the escort authorisation, and somebody found a stamp on a form that shouldn't have carried one. You want my advice, Odell?"

"No," Bram said honestly. "But I expect you'll give it anyway."

"Get out." Coyle's voice stayed low, almost gentle. "Not eventually. Soon. Whatever you're carrying around in that coat of yours, it's heavier than you. I've watched men carry things heavier than themselves, and I've buried most of them." He set the memo on Bram's new corridor table, squared its corners precisely, and walked away, and that was the last kindness the records office ever did him.

Bram sat down at the corridor table. Through the strong-room door, left open a hand's width by a careless clerk, he could see the muster rolls racked in their bays — the rolls he no longer had the right to open, the rolls whose falseness had started everything, waiting in their green boxes like ammunition stored against some future need.

He had traded his access, his reputation, his one clean corner of the machine, for a woman's freedom and four days' time. It was, he thought, the first trade of his life he did not regret. But regret was not the same as safety, and he could feel it now, distinctly, the way you feel weather change: the office's attention settling on him, slow and cold and patient. Not Voss yet. Not arrest yet. Just the machine noticing, the way it notices everything eventually, that a part was moving out of true.

That evening he walked out past the wire to the ruined orchard where the ground rose enough to show the country to the north. The guns were talking again, longer conversations than last week, nearer. The evacuation timetables were being drafted in offices he used to have access to; he could read the war's direction now without a map, in the lengthening queues at the transport pool, in the way the quartermaster's men had begun inventorying things that were supposed to be immovable.

Three days ago he had believed they had until spring. Tonight, watching the horizon flicker like a bad lamp, he gave them a month.

He turned his collar up and walked back through the mud to write it all down for Rafael — the audit, the vehicle log, the attention of the machine, the carbon dust on Marta's cuffs and her instruction delivered like a soldier's last gift: *burn your gloves.* And underneath it, because he was honest with her and could not stop being honest with himself, he wrote the other thing too, the thing Coyle's warning had carved into him:

*Sooner than we thought. Whatever we're going to do, we must begin deciding now what can be saved and what cannot.*

He sealed the note, and slipped it into the channel of his greatcoat lining where the pages would later live, and started the long walk toward the workshop and the lamplight and the woman measuring the world in what could be carried.

Behind him, at the crossroads by the transport pool, a staff car with colonel's stars stood idling in the rain, its driver watching the records office door.
