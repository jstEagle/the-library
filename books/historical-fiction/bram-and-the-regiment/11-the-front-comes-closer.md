## 11. The Front Comes Closer

The guns changed their voices on the fourth of April.

Rafael had learned to read them the way a farmer reads sky. For months the artillery to the south had spoken in the flat, patient cadence of routine — registration shoots, harassment barrages, the small spiteful conversations of a line that was not going anywhere. Now there was a new note underneath, a deep rolling thunder that did not pause for dawn or dusk, that went on for hours until it stopped being sound and became weather. She stood at the doorway of the dressing station with her hands still wet from someone else's blood and listened, and knew what it meant before anyone told her.

"They've broken through at the river," Sergeant Quill said, coming up the duckboards at a jog, his stretcher-bearers behind him caked in mud to the eyebrows. "Portuguese sector. Whole division's gone. They're saying ten miles, maybe more, and nothing between here and the coast but what we can put there."

"Bring them in," Rafael said. "All of them."

The first wounded came within the hour, and they did not stop.

She worked, because working was the one prayer she trusted. The ward she had built from a gutted farmhouse filled past its capacity by nightfall, then past its decency by morning. Stretcher cases lined the walls two deep; men who should have been evacuated lay on straw in the barn, in the yard, in the lee of the shattered wall where the wind was kindest. The operating table never cooled. She tied arteries, packed wounds, wrote tags with a pencil stub worn down to a sliver, and between patients she counted — not shells, not hours, but faces. Names she knew. Names that would go into no ledger, because these were honest deaths, witnessed and recorded, the ordinary arithmetic of the front.

It was the dishonest arithmetic that kept surfacing in her mind, unbidden, like a stone worked loose by traffic.

By the third day of the flood, an orderly named Petch told her the thing she already felt in her spine. "They're saying the whole salient's to be shortened. Voluntary evacuation of non-essential personnel from this sector by month's end." He said it lightly, mopping blood off the floor, as if discussing weather. "Essential stays, of course."

"And which are we?"

He grinned without humor. "We're essential, Sister. We're always essential. It's everyone else that gets carried out."

* * *

She walked to the workshop that evening under a sky the color of a healing bruise. The road was busier than she had ever seen it — lorries going the wrong way, toward the guns, loaded with wire and stakes; staff cars going the other way, toward the coast, carrying the people who decided such things. A battery of field guns had appeared in the beet fields south of the track, camouflaged under fish-nets, and their crews watched her pass with the flat incurious eyes of men who had been told something she had not.

Inside the workshop, Marta had lit the lamp and set out the evening's work: fresh paper, ink, the original ledger open beneath its board. Bram was already there, sleeves rolled, copying casualty summaries in the clerk's hand that could make any document look born official. The smell of wood shavings and glue still hung in the rafters, the ghost of the carpentry that had lived here before the war requisitioned everything, even the making of coffins.

"You've heard," she said.

They both looked up. Bram set down his pen. "Evacuation orders. End of the month." He tried to say it as though it were administrative news. His face refused the effort. "Twenty-six days."

"Nineteen," Rafael said. "I asked the quartermaster this afternoon. The transport is scheduled for the twenty-third, whatever the paper says. Paper slips, Bram. Paper always slips when someone wants it to."

Marta stopped grinding ink. In three months Rafael had learned every silence the woman owned, and this one was new: not fear exactly, but the particular stillness of someone calculating what home means. Marta was from Lille originally, had family behind lines that moved, and every rumor of advance or retreat rearranged the map of her heart.

"My cousin writes from Poperinghe," Marta said carefully. "She says the roads are already full. Refugees going west. If the evacuation starts properly—"

"It won't come to that," Bram began.

"It will come to exactly that," Rafael said, and heard how harsh her own voice sounded in the warm little room, and made herself soften it. "That's why we're here at all. That's why we started. Because when the front moves, the first thing it buries isn't the dead. It's the record of the dead."

She took the ledger out from its board and laid it open, and they gathered around it the way people gather around a fire. Two hundred and six names in her careful hand, each with its date, its wound, its ward, and — where she had been able to find them — the small human details that officers' reports never carried: *Private E. Dunn, age 19, played concertina, died asking after his horse.* The pages had softened with handling. She thought of them the way she thought of certain patients: too long in her care to be anything but hers now.

"Hessler knows the inquiry is coming," she said. "Quill heard it from a staff captain. Third week of the month, they think, maybe sooner if the front stabilizes enough for anyone to bother with paperwork. When it comes, Hessler will want his arithmetic clean. And we—" she touched the ledger's edge, "—we are the error in his sums."

"So we go faster," Bram said. "We've been doing four, five copies a night—"

"We do thirty a day."

The lamplight made his face look very young. "Thirty. Rafael, my hand won't—"

"Marta types. You forge cover sheets. I copy names, dates, regiments — that part doesn't need beauty, only accuracy. We work in shifts around the clock. We sleep in shifts. We finish before the transport leaves or we don't leave with anything worth having carried." She looked at each of them in turn, and hated herself a little for what she saw in their faces — the trust, the willingness, the terrible faith that she knew what she was doing. "I know what I'm asking. I'm asking it anyway."

Marta reached over and turned the ledger to a fresh page, squaring it precisely, and said nothing at all, which from Marta was agreement given form.

* * *

The next six days blurred into a rhythm that had no hours in it, only tasks.

Rafael ran the dressing station through the mornings and afternoons — the wounded flood had crested but not receded; trains of them still came up the road, and twice she operated by hurricane-lamp because the generators were reserved for the clearing station further back. Then she walked to the workshop and copied until her vision doubled, slept two hours in the chair by the stove while Bram worked, woke, and copied again. She ate standing. She forgot the taste of tea. Her handwriting, always precise, grew a slight forward lean, as if the letters themselves were hurrying westward.

The war came closer in ways large and small. The horizon to the south glowed at night now, a dull orange smear like a city burning beyond hills. Refugees appeared on the cross-roads, a family with a handcart and a goat, an old man pushing a wheelbarrow containing all of his wife's belongings wrapped in a carpet. The soldiers stopped calling it a push or a withdrawal and started calling it *the show*, in the tone men use for things too big to be spoken plainly.

And the light — she found herself measuring time in light, in the shrinking coin of the days. Dawn came at six; the workshop could risk shutters-open work until seven in the evening. Between those hours lay the whole of their possible future. Every cloudy day was a gift, every bright one a debt. She caught herself standing in the yard at noon, face tilted up, thinking: *this much daylight left. This much.*

On the ninth day, the shelling arrived within earshot of a new kind — heavy naval guns, somewhere north, firing shells so large the ground shivered a full second before the sound arrived, like grief preceding news. A shell fell close enough that evening to shake dust from the workshop rafters onto the drying pages. Marta swept it up without comment, but Rafael watched her hands shake, and understood that the dust was the argument she'd lost.

"We should talk about what happens if we don't finish," Bram said that night. He had ink to his elbow and exhaustion in the corners of his mouth. "If the copies aren't done. What do we take?"

"The ledger itself."

"And the rest? The muster books, the ration returns, Hessler's signed requisitions — the evidence that proves the ledger isn't one woman's invention? Rafael, the ledger alone is a claim. The ledger with the paperwork is a case."

"I know what it is." She pressed her palms against her eyes until the darkness sparked. "And I know what we can carry. Two people, moving fast, through collapsing lines, hunted if anyone looks at us twice. We can carry perhaps ten pounds of paper between us, and every ounce is a decision about some mother who will or will not learn where her son lies."

Bram was quiet a moment. Then, gently, in the voice he used for figures: "Then we decide early. Not on the road, in panic, with shells falling. We sit down with everything and we choose, coldly, beforehand. Triage. You taught me that word."

She wanted to argue. She recognized the wanting — the animal refusal to rank human beings by weight of paper — and she recognized also that he was right, and that being right about this was a kind of wound he would carry too.

"After the last page," she said. "Not before."

* * *

On the twelfth day the offensive reached them in person.

A column of stragglers came up the track at dusk — not wounded, not deserters, just men emptied out, faces like unsent letters, drifting toward the coast on some current none of them could name. An officer followed them, shouting himself hoarse, and the shouting accomplished nothing. Among them Rafael found a lance-corporal of her own regiment, a boy she had bandaged in January, sitting in the ditch holding his rifle like something he'd forgotten to return.

"Where's your section?" she asked him.

"Gone," he said, with perfect calm. "All of it. There's a gap, Sister. Miles of nothing. They're coming through it."

She brought him tea he didn't drink and sat with him until the military police collected him, and then she walked to the workshop faster than she had ever walked it, and worked until dawn with her coat still on.

The word spread through the dressing station the next day like dye through water: *the line is being rebuilt further back. This sector's to be thinned. Everything non-essential moves by the twenty-third.* Non-essential. She wore the word like a tag pinned to her own chest, and found, absurdly, that it comforted her — for once the army's categories served her. She would be carried out. The question was what she would be carrying.

That evening the sympathetic quartermaster — Captain Voss, who had never asked what she wanted with extra paper and carbon, and whose silence she had long since stopped pretending was ignorance — appeared at the workshop door after dark. He came in stamping cold off his boots, accepted tea he also didn't drink, and set a single typed sheet on the bench beside the ledger.

"Burning detail," he said. "Scheduled for the twenty-fifth. Records audit first, twenty-fourth, my section handles it. Workshop's slated for clearance — everything on the premises to be inventoried and destroyed as redundant. Officially it's housekeeping before the move." He met her eyes, and let the sentence sit until its real shape showed. "Unofficially, Sister, I've seen audits before. Things get found in them. Or don't. Depending."

Rafael kept her voice level. "Who ordered it?"

The look he gave her was answer enough.

"When does the inventory list reach his desk?"

"Evening of the twenty-fourth. Which gives you—" Voss stood, buttoning his coat, and paused at the door, and for a moment the professional mask slipped and she saw the man beneath, tired and complicit and afraid. "Ten days. Whatever you're doing in this building that needs doing, Sister — do it by the twenty-third. On the twenty-fourth, be elsewhere. I was never here. Neither was this." He tapped the doorframe twice, some old habit, and was gone into the dark.

Marta began, very quietly, to cry. Not sobbing — crying the way women cry who have learned to do it silently and keep working, her hands still sorting pages, tears dropping onto the bench and her sleeve taking them away. Bram put his hand over hers and left it there.

"Ten days," he said. "Thirty-one names a day. We can do thirty-one names a day."

"No," Rafael said. She looked at the ledger, all two hundred and six of it, and at the stacked evidence around it — the muster books, the requisitions bearing Hessler's signature, the pension claims returned unanswered, the whole paper continent they had spent months assembling against a colonel's arithmetic. "Ten days is enough for the names. It is not enough for everything. So we start deciding tonight, Bram. Triage, like you said." She drew the lamp closer and opened the ledger, and her voice, when she spoke again, was the flat competent voice she used over the operating table, because the alternative was not usable. "Which mothers do we save?"

* * *

Outside, far to the south, the guns rolled on without pause, patient as tide. Above the workshop the clouds had cleared, and the stars came out over a countryside full of movement — troops, lorries, refugees, all of it flowing westward like iron filings drawn by a magnet buried somewhere under the Channel. Rafael bent over the page and began to write, and measured the remaining world in days of light, and found there were fewer than she needed.

Three days later, a note reached her at the dressing station, folded into a bundle of clean bandages in a hand she did not recognize. She read it standing in the linen store with the door shut.

*Colonel Hessler has requested a full audit of auxiliary records, effective immediately. Your workshop is first on the schedule.*

*The inspection is tomorrow morning.*
