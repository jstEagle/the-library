## 12. Hessler's Offer

The summons came on a Tuesday, which Bram would remember afterward as the detail that frightened him most. Not the note itself — a plain card, regimental crest, *Capt. Odell will call upon Col. Hessler at his convenience* — but the day of it. Tuesdays were pay-parade days at the records office, the one morning when every clerk in the annex was accounted for and every discrepancy between the rolls and the paymaster's books sat exposed like a wound under lamplight. If a man were going to be caught doing something wrong, he would be caught on a Tuesday.

He had known this was coming for eleven days. He had counted them the way he counted everything now, in columns, in margins, in the small obsessive arithmetic that had made him useful to the census and dangerous to the regiment. Eleven days since the quartermaster-sergeant had looked at him across a crate of field dressings and said, too casually, *You've been asking after stores that went through Poperinghe, sir? Funny thing, asking.* Eleven days since Marta's release papers had come through with a speed that could only have been purchased, since Bram had burned his last reserves of goodwill with Major Fenwick and understood, signing the falsified transfer order, that the ash of that bridge would follow him for the rest of his life.

Eleven days of waiting for the knock, and here it was, delivered by a lance-corporal who wouldn't meet his eyes.

"Colonel says now, sir."

"Of course he does," Bram said, and heard his own voice come out steady, which surprised him. Fear, he was learning, did not always sound like fear. Sometimes it sounded like a clerk being punctual.

* * *

Hessler's billet occupied the ground floor of a farmhouse that had belonged, until eight months ago, to a family named Verhaeghe. Bram knew this because census men knew things like that; because he had walked past the house a dozen times and noted the way the curtains had been replaced with army blanket, the way the orchard out back stood untended, its fruit dropping into the mud unpicked. The war ate houses the way it ate men — officially, in ledgers, without anyone having to say the word *taken*.

A batman took his cap and greatcoat. Another officer's voice came faintly from the study, laughing, and then boots on boards, and then Colonel Hessler himself in the doorway, filling it, a big man gone soft in the middle the way garrison men go soft, with a face like a closed ledger — red, ruled, nothing written on it that you could read at a distance.

"Odell." He said the name the way he might have said a figure he was checking against another figure. "You'll take a drink."

It was not a question. Bram followed him into the study, where a fire burned against the April damp and two glasses already stood on the desk beside a bottle of something amber and civilian. Two glasses. Bram looked at them and felt the floor tilt slightly under his understanding. This meeting had been dressed before he arrived. He was expected. He had been expected for some time.

"Whisky," Hessler said, pouring. "Real stuff, not the chemical they issue. A man should taste what he's fighting for, don't you think?" He handed the glass over and settled behind the desk, and for a moment he simply looked at Bram with the patient attention of a man reading a document he had already memorized.

Bram drank. The whisky was very good. It occurred to him, distantly, that this was how it would be done — not with threats, not yet, but with good whisky and a warm room and the terrible courtesy of powerful men.

"You're from Dorset," Hessler said. "Odell. There are Odells in the census returns for Dorset going back to the first enumeration. Your father kept the parish accounts at Shillingstone, I'm told. A careful man."

"He was," Bram said. "Is."

"A careful family." Hessler smiled. It did not reach anywhere near his eyes; it stayed down at his mouth like a animal that hadn't been fed. "That's why I wanted you. Do you know how rare a careful man is, Odell? The Army is full of clever men and brave men, God help us both, but careful men — men who can look at ten thousand entries and see the eleven that don't fit — those are worth more than staff officers. Those are worth their weight in shells." He turned a paper on his desk ninety degrees, so it faced Bram. "Your work on the duplicate-payments question. I've read your memorandum. Before it was suppressed, naturally."

Bram's glass stopped halfway to his mouth.

"Yes," Hessler said pleasantly. "We read everything. That's rather my function. You found forty-one men drawing double rations and separation allowance, and you traced it to clerical error at three levels, and you wrote it up in language so dry a bishop could have signed it. Very fine work. And then—" he let the pause stretch, savoring it— "you asked Sergeant Quill why Private Ellery, killed at Langemarck in August, was still carried on the effective strength in October. And Quill asked me. And I began to wonder about you, Odell. I've been wondering ever since."

The fire ticked. Somewhere outside, a lorry changed gear on the road to Ypres, the sound enormous and ordinary all at once.

"I asked a question," Bram said carefully. "Census work is questions. It's all it is."

"It is indeed." Hessler leaned back. "And questions are like bullets, Odell. Perfectly harmless in the box. It's only a question of where you point them." He picked up the paper again — Bram could see now it was a service record, a real one, blank in the posting column — and held it up between two fingers. "Captain. Temporary rank, made permanent within the year if there's any justice, and there is justice, Odell, there's mine. G.H.Q. liaison, records division. A desk in a heated room. Your own clerk. Authority to requisition whatever the records require, which is to say anything at all. You'd wear pips instead of stripes. Men would stand when you came into a room."

He set the paper down and pushed it across the desk with one finger, slowly, the way a man pushes the last inch of a rope across a chasm.

"And all it costs," Hessler said, "is the answer to one question."

Bram looked at the whisky in his glass. He thought: *I am twenty-nine years old and I have never been offered anything in my life except more work.* It was true, and the truth of it shamed him, and the shame was the point. Hessler had done his homework not just on the leaks but on the man. A small man, the colonel would have written somewhere, in some file Bram would never see. *Ambitious beyond his station. Father a parish clerk. Brother killed. No prospects. Will want to matter.* Every line of it accurate. That was the horror of it — that Bram could read his own indictment in the shape of the offer, and could not find a single word in it to dispute.

"Which question?" he said.

"The name," Hessler said. "Or names. Whoever has been copying restricted material out of the workshop on the Menin road and passing it where it shouldn't go. You've been seen, Odell. Tuesday evenings, mostly, leaving by the yard gate. Don't trouble yourself denying it — denial is for courts, and I'm offering you a way to avoid ever seeing one." He rose, crossed to the window, hands clasped behind him, a man surveying an estate. "Here is what you must understand about the situation, because I think you genuinely don't. There is a war on. Everything — everything — is subordinate to winning it. A colonel who reports his battalion at six hundred men keeps it supplied, reinforced, and alive. A colonel who reports four hundred gets it broken up for drafts and its survivors scattered to plug holes in other men's lines. My figures kept regiments whole. My figures kept men *fed*, Odell. Whatever your nurse friend tells you about names, ask her how many living soldiers owe their continued existence to my arithmetic."

"She isn't my—" Bram began, and stopped, because it was a confession, and they both knew it, and Hessler turned from the window with the smile again, the fed-animal smile.

"There," he said softly. "You see? You've given her away already and you haven't said a word. That's what I mean about careful men. You can't help being honest; it's in the bones, like your father's ledgers." He came back to the desk. "Nurse Yarrow. Yes. We know. We've known since February. What we don't have is the ledger itself — the master copy, the evidence, the thing that could embarrass the Army in front of politicians who don't understand the first thing about maintaining strength. Deliver that to me, give your testimony quietly, and none of this touches you. You'd be a hero of the filing system, Odell. Invisible medals are the only kind worth having."

"And if she refuses to hand it over?"

"Then she's a thief of official documents in a war zone," Hessler said, without heat, without cruelty, which was worse. "The penalty is what it is. But it won't come to that, because you're going to persuade her. That's what you're *for*, Odell. You're a reasonable man and she trusts you — I watched her trust you, through a telescope of paperwork, and it was quite touching. Reasonable men persuade people. Unreasonable men get shot, and reasonable men write the reports afterward." He tapped the service record once, lightly. "Think of it as a census, if it helps. You're simply counting what's actually in the room."

Bram stood. His legs worked, which seemed like information from a stranger's body. "May I have until tomorrow?"

"You may have until Thursday," Hessler said. "Pay parade is Friday. Things happen at pay parades — errors surface, men are posted. I'd hate for an error to surface in *your* file." He extended his hand, and Bram took it, because refusing to take it was not among the options the room contained. The colonel's grip was warm and dry and strong. "One more thing. Your brother."

The room went very still around Bram, the fire and the lorry and his own pulse all suspended.

"Thomas Odell," Hessler said. "Killed in action, or so recorded, the ninth of August, 1916. Guillemont." He let it sit. "You'd want to know I checked. Personally. His record is clean, Odell — no omission, no fiddle. He died the way the report says he died. Whatever else you decide, don't decide it thinking I owe you a lie about Thomas. I deal in figures, not comfort, but I don't deal in false ones either. Not about the dead." Something moved behind the red ruled face, and was gone — regret, or its excellent imitation. "Thursday. The batman will show you out."

* * *

Bram walked for two hours. He walked past the dressing station without going in, past the carpentry workshop with its boarded windows and its smell of cut pine and lamp oil, out along the canal road where the poplars had been shot to splinters and the water lay flat and grey as slate. Rain came and went without conviction. His mind ran the offer over and over the way a counting-house machine runs a column, looking for the error, and the terror was that there wasn't one. That was the seduction of it — not the whisky, not the pips, but the *rightness*. Hessler's logic was airtight. Regiments did break up when their numbers fell. Figures did keep men fed. And Rafael's ledger, beautiful and damning, was still just ink on paper next to the fact of two hundred and six mothers who didn't know — no. He corrected himself, walking, rain running off his nose. Next to the fact of two hundred and six families who would never know, unless someone carried the truth out of this mud and put it in front of people with the power to act on it. That was the whole of the argument. Everything Hessler had built — the commission, the future, the warm room, even the strange mercy about Thomas — was scaffolding around one load-bearing lie: that the war could not afford the truth. When the war ended, and it would end, the only thing left standing would be what was written down.

He thought of Marta, three weeks released, working again with her sleeves rolled, saying nothing about the nine days they had questioned her, and what her silence cost her face each night.

He thought of Rafael by lamplight, ruling her lines straight, writing dead men's names in a book she sewed into her coat like a heartbeat.

He thought of Thomas. *His record is clean.* Bram stopped at the canal edge and pressed his fist against his mouth. Clean. As if that settled anything. As if the point had ever been whether Thomas was on a list. The point was that lists were being edited, and someone had to keep the unedited version, or the dead would die twice — once in the mud and once in the record, and the second death was the one that erased the mothers' pensions and the brothers' graves and the whole plain human fact of them.

By the time the light failed he had stopped shaking. That was how he knew what he'd decided; the shaking always stopped when the decision was real.

There was one thing left to do, and it had to be done tonight, and it had to be done well, because Thursday was not a deadline. It was a fuse.

He went to the workshop. Rafael opened the door before he knocked twice — she had taken to knowing him by his step, which frightened him and steadied him in equal measure. Lamplight behind her, the long table with its stacks of copied pages, Marta's chair empty.

"They came to me today," Bram said quietly. "Hessler. He offered me a captaincy for the ledger and your name." He watched her face do what faces did — the flicker, the closing — and he stepped closer, voice low and fast. "I'm going to take it. Partly. I'm going to give him a ledger."

Her eyes narrowed. "*Give* him—"

"A forgery. Tonight, while the light holds. Real names — half of them, the earliest entries, the ones whose families we've already located, the ones it can't hurt anymore. Right format, right dates, enough of Hessler's own signature trail to make it convincing. He'll burn it and believe he's won, and while he believes it, he'll stop hunting." Bram pulled off his gloves, fingers finally steady, and reached for the lamp. "Because he told me something he thinks I don't understand the value of. He said things happen at pay parades. Friday. Rafael — the workshop doesn't have weeks. We have until Friday morning."

Rafael stood very still in the doorway, lamplight catching the grey at her temples, and Bram saw the calculation move through her like weather. Then she turned and took down the good ink.

"Then we'd better begin," she said, "while there's light to copy by."
