## 13. What Can Be Carried

The order came at dusk, folded into a requisition slip as if the paper itself were ashamed of what it carried: *Workshop 4 to be cleared of all stores by month's end. Priority to surgical and transport materials. No records to be retained.*

No records to be retained.

Rafael read it twice by the light of the storm lamp, then set it on Marta's empty stool, where it sat like something dead. Outside, the guns had found a rhythm again — not the near thunder of the last week, but a steady grinding away at some sector south of them, patient as tide. The workshop smelled of pine shavings and lamp oil and, beneath both, the antiseptic ghost of the crates that had passed through on their way to the front. Everything in this room could be counted. Everything in this room was now scheduled for disappearance.

She pulled the ledger from its hiding place beneath the false bottom of the tool chest — Bram's invention, a carpenter's trick he'd described with the shy pride of a man offering flowers — and set it on the workbench under the lamp.

Two hundred and six names.

She had written every one of them herself, or copied them from Marta's notes, or taken them from Bram's cross-referenced slips in his small, careful clerk's hand. Two hundred and six men who had died at her station or passed through it into death, whose names existed nowhere in the official record except as arithmetic errors absorbed into Colonel Hessler's tidy casualty figures. She had spent eleven months building this book. She had one month — less, if the offensive kept its appetite — and no way to carry it all.

So she did what she had trained herself to do since Passchendaele's first spring. She triaged.

* * *

The ledger weighed more than she remembered. That was absurd — paper didn't gain weight — but when she lifted it from the chest it seemed heavier than it had in autumn, as though each name added mass, as though grief were a measurable quantity like morphine or blood. She laid it flat and opened it to the first page and made herself look at what she had built before she decided what to destroy.

The early entries were sparse. *Pvt. E. Callum, 2nd Btn., died 14 March, abdominal wound. Not entered on roll.* Her handwriting had been cramped then, frightened, the letters pressed deep into the paper as if she could bury the evidence by force. By summer the entries had grown methodical: name, rank, battalion, date of death, wound, next of kin where she knew it, and — after Bram came — a reference number keyed to the muster rolls he'd copied, proof that the man existed in the regiment's pay books and nowhere else.

That was the first cut, and it was the cruelest. The stories would have to go.

Marta had collected those. In the months before her detention, while Rafael worked the ward, Marta had sat with the dying men and written down what they said — the farm in Suffolk, the girl in Rouen, the brother who owed him two pounds and would carry the debt like an heirloom. Marta wrote in French and English both, sometimes in neither, capturing accents phonetically because she refused to let a man's voice be flattened into standard spelling even on the page that preserved him. Those pages were loose, interleaved through the ledger like pressed flowers. Rafael touched them now and felt her throat close.

If she took everything, she would be caught. A nurse crossing lines does not carry a foot of bound paper sewn into her coat without someone noticing; she had watched searches happen, had seen what suspicion did to soldiers' faces when the provost's hands went through their packs. Whatever she saved had to pass as nothing. Had to be invisible. Had to weigh almost nothing at all.

Names first. Always names. A family that learned their son had died — truly died, on a date, in a place, with a witness — could survive the absence of everything else. A mother did not need the story of the Suffolk farm. She needed the date. She needed to know it was real.

* * *

She worked until the lamp burned low, sorting pages into three piles that were really three futures.

The first pile was the spine of the thing: the names themselves, the dates, the battalions. One hundred and six pages once she stripped out the narrative material, compressed the entries into columns. She could copy these tighter still — Bram's hand was smaller than hers, and he had offered to take the copying himself, but there wasn't time to wait for him to come up from the records office, and there was a part of this work she would not delegate. She had written the names in secret and by lamplight while the ward slept; she would write them again the same way. Some debts you pay personally.

The second pile was the evidence against Hessler, and it was smaller and sharper than any blade in the workshop. Three documents. The first was the photostat Bram had lifted of the colonel's quarterly return for June — the page where the arithmetic bent, where two hundred and six men dissolved into "wounded, returned to duty" and the signature block at the bottom bore *A. R. Hessler, Col.* in ink so confident it looked carved. The second was the quartermaster Sergeant Pike's chit, signed and dated, confirming receipt of personal effects from men officially listed as unwounded — effects that had gone home to mothers who had been told their sons were alive. The third was a single line from the audit trail Bram had reconstructed: the date on which the muster rolls had been altered, matched to the date of Hessler's promotion board. Motive, means, and a signature. If the ledger reached anyone with the power to act, those three pages were the reason.

Everything else — the duplicate rolls, the correspondence drafts, the pay-book extracts, four months of Bram's meticulous reconciliation tables — went to the third pile.

She sat looking at it for a long time. It was nearly as thick as the first pile. Months of proof, corroboration, redundancy — the scaffolding that made the ledger unimpeachable. Any board of inquiry would want all of it. Any board of inquiry would also never see it, because it would be ash in a fortnight, and she knew that the way she knew which of her patients would die: not as a hope she could argue with but as a fact she was already grieving.

*Leave the rest,* she thought, and the words felt like triage always felt — like choosing, with full knowledge, whom not to save.

There was no alternative worth pretending toward. She had run the arithmetic a dozen ways. A nurse's kit, a haversack, one uniform. Even if she abandoned her own kit entirely — even if she walked out carrying nothing but paper — she could not take the whole ledger and move quickly, and moving quickly might soon be the only kind of movement left. The offensive had broken the Fifth Army's front forty miles south. Refugees clogged the roads she would need. And Hessler's audit was coming regardless of shells; the quartermaster had confirmed the date, the twenty-ninth, though everyone understood dates near a collapsing front were suggestions.

One book. One copy. The names, the dates, the signature.

She began tearing the third pile apart.

It sounded obscene, the sound of it — paper ripping in an empty room, each sheet protesting longer than she expected. She tore methodically, feeding the pieces into the cold stove because burning them tonight would put light in the windows and the windows had blackout discipline, and because she could not yet bear to watch them go. Let them sit in pieces in the dark stove. Tomorrow, or the day after, she would strike the match and stand there while they became smoke over Flanders, and she would think of Marta's phonetic transcriptions of dying men's voices, and she would not stop tearing until it was done.

Halfway through the pile her hands stopped. Not by decision — they simply quit, mid-sheet, as if the body had filed its own objection. She stood in the lamplight with half a letter in each hand, one of Marta's, the account of a lance-corporal named Dewar who had talked for six hours about his father's boat before he stopped talking altogether, and Rafael found she could not tear it.

She set it aside instead. Then another. Then a handful more — not many, maybe thirty pages, chosen by no logic she could defend, only by the pressure of her thumb pausing on certain sheets. Dewar's boat. The Suffolk farm. A boy called Tam who had recited railway timetables because timetables meant going home. She told herself she was being sentimental and that sentiment got people killed, and then she folded the pages flat and slid them into the stove with the rest, because she could not carry them and she could not watch them torn, and the stove at least was quick.

She closed the stove door on them and stood with her palm flat against the cold iron, the way she had once stood against a dying man's chest, counting what was left.

* * *

Dawn found her with scissors, a needle, waxed thread, and the uniform she wore on march days — the plain grey jacket, unremarkable, the kind that passed through checkpoints without a glance. She had chosen it over her dress blues for exactly the reasons that now served her: nobody searched a nurse's marching jacket. Nobody imagined a nurse carrying anything but lint and morphia.

The problem was geometry.

She had tried the haversack first and rejected it within minutes — packs were opened, always, at every checkpoint, and rolled bandages could not explain away a hundred pages. She had tried splitting the load between kit and person and rejected that too, because split loads got separated in evacuations, and she had seen enough men lose their packs between dressing station and barge to know that whatever left her body might as well already be burning.

So: the lining. It was the oldest trick in smuggling and therefore either the worst or the best, depending on how bored the searcher was, and she would bet a hundred and six pages' worth of lives on boredom.

She turned the jacket inside out on the workbench and studied its architecture the way Bram studied muster rolls — looking for the seams, the places where structure concealed space. The lining was linen, worn soft at the elbows. Between lining and shell ran channels at the side seams and across the back panel, wide enough for a finger, narrow enough to escape notice. If she flattened the pages thin — thinner than comfortable, thin enough to bend without cracking — she could build flat packets and slide them into the channels, then close the seams with stitches fine enough to pass for original work.

She cut the pages free of their binding one last time. This was the moment the ledger stopped being a book and became cargo, and her hands shook doing it, and she let them shake because no one was watching and shaking cost nothing but time. The cover she kept separate — the dark boards, the title she had burned into the leather herself with a heated nail one reckless night in April: *REGIMENTAL RECORDS, SUPPLEMENTARY*. It would burn with the rest. Books could be replaced, Bram had said once; it was people who couldn't, and then he had gone quiet, because we both know that isn't quite true, he'd added, and neither of them had corrected it.

She built the packets on the bench like a surgeon laying out instruments. Names and dates, compressed into columns, folded in eighths. The Hessler photostat, folded around a scrap of oiled canvas to keep damp from the signature. Pike's chit. The audit line. Fourteen packets in all, each one wrapped in a single outer leaf marked with a cipher of her own devising — a numbering system that looked, to any casual eye, like laundry marks.

Then she threaded the needle and began to sew them into herself.

Not metaphorically, though it felt that way. Into the right side seam went the names, packet by packet, her stitches small and even, the linen closing over them like skin. Into the left seam went the evidence, the Hessler pages lying flat against her ribs. Across the small of the back, where a greatcoat would hide them and searchers rarely looked, went the thickest packet — the master index, the key to everything else, without which the names were just a list and with which they were a case.

It took four hours. The light changed twice outside the boarded windows — grey to white to grey again — and the guns never stopped, and twice she heard shells land close enough to make the shavings on the floor jump, and both times she bent lower over her stitching and continued. There is a kind of calm that comes from work done under fire; she had learned it over operating tables and it transferred without modification to a workbench. Your hands do the thing. The rest of you can scream later.

By late afternoon the jacket hung on her frame differently. Heavier by perhaps three pounds — she weighed it against her kit on the bench scales, out of a nurse's habit of quantifying everything — stiffer down the sides, warm where it lay against her. When she buttoned it and moved, the pages whispered faintly against her shift, a sound like distant applause or distant rain. She practiced the movements she would need: lifting a stretcher, running, doubling over, raising her arms. The packets held. One corner of the back index pressed into her kidney when she bent fully, a small persistent ache, and she decided she would live with it. She had lived with worse and called it duty.

She stood before the shard of mirror Marta had left propped on a shelf and looked at herself. A nurse. Tired, thin, ordinary. Nothing in the face admitted that beneath the grey wool, against the cage of her ribs, rode two hundred and six men.

"Right," she said aloud, to the mirror, to the empty workshop, to whoever was listening on the other side of whatever distance separates the living from the listed. "You're coming with me."

* * *

She spent the evening destroying the rest.

The stove was lit now, properly lit, because darkness had fallen and the flames showed through the shielded vent as nothing but a dull glow, indistinguishable from any of ten thousand braziers along this stretch of front. She fed the remaining papers in slowly, not from ceremony but from sense — a sudden column of black smoke from Workshop 4 would bring questions, and questions brought provosts, and she had buried enough friends this year.

But she allowed herself the reading. Each document, before it went in, she read once — a last visitation, a final act of witness. The pay-book extract of a sergeant from Winnipeg whose effects had shipped to a wife already notified of his death by a chaplain's letter that officially should not exist. The draft memo in which some staff captain proposed, in flawless administrative prose, that "discrepancies of the present character be resolved by consolidation of returns," as if men could be consolidated. Bram's tables, page after page, each figure double-checked in two colors of ink, the work of a mind that believed — had believed, once, before all this — that records were a form of honesty.

The last item was the ledger's cover. She held it a moment, thumb over the burned letters, remembering the night she'd made them and the fear that had tasted like copper in her mouth, the sense that she was crossing a line from which uniforms and oaths could not retrieve her. She had been right about that. She had also been right to cross.

She put it on the coals and watched the leather curl and blacken and go to flakes, and the burned-in letters glowed briefly brighter than the wood around them, as if resisting, and then they were gone.

When the stove held nothing but ash, she raked it cold, mixed the ash with the stove's old cinders so that even the residue of the record would be illegible, and swept the floor. The workshop stood empty around her: benches bare, tool chest open and hollow, Marta's stool vacant under the window. Eleven months of work reduced to fourteen packets of stitched paper and a smell of burnt ink.

Tomorrow Bram would come up from the records office with news, and she would tell him what she had done and what she had left, and he would grieve the lost corroborations more loudly than she had — he was the archivist, the believer in completeness, and it would hurt him to learn how much of the proof was already smoke. She rehearsed the argument she expected and found she was too tired to fear it. If he wanted to hate her for it, he could. They had bigger enemies.

She banked the lamp, put on the jacket, and felt the pages settle against her like a hand pressed flat between her shoulder blades — steadying, insistent, unignorable. *Carry us,* they said. Or perhaps that was only her own pulse, knocking against the Hessian photostat, keeping its stubborn count.

Outside, the southern sky pulsed with the offensive's light, and somewhere on those roads refugees were learning the same lesson she had just taught herself in an empty room: that the whole world cannot be saved, and that survival is measured in what you can carry, and that carrying is not the same as saving, and you do it anyway.

She locked the workshop door behind her and turned toward the dressing station, and had gone perhaps fifty paces when she saw Bram coming up the lane at a run — actually running, capless, which he never was — and knew before he spoke that the calendar she had been working to had just collapsed.

"They're not waiting for the twenty-ninth," he gasped. "Rafael — it's three days."
