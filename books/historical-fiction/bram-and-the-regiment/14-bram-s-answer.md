## 14. Bram's Answer

The commission sat on Hessler's desk between them like a thing that had been laid out for dissection: a folded paper, a pen, a small brass inkwell with a chip in its lip.

"Second lieutenant," the colonel said. "Records section, but attached to my own staff. A future, Odell. Clean boots, clean hands, and when this war ends — and it will end, sooner than the optimists think — a reference that opens any ministry door in London." He turned the paper so it faced Bram, the way a man turns a plate toward a guest. "All you have to do is bring me what you've already seen."

Bram's hands were in his lap. He kept them there because he did not trust them not to shake, and shaking would tell Hessler everything he needed to know.

"And the name," Hessler said. "Of whoever has been feeding you."

"The ledger isn't being fed to me, sir. I found the discrepancies myself. I told you that."

"You told me what you thought was safe to tell me. That's different." Hessler rose and went to the window of his office at divisional headquarters, where the light came through glass gone grey with mud-spatter. Below, a column of lorries ground past toward the salient, their canvas backs dark with rain. "I don't think you're a bad man, Odell. I think you're a small man who has stumbled into something larger than he understands, and you're frightened. Fear is honest. It's the one virtue conscripts have left." He looked back over his shoulder. "So let me make the arithmetic simple. Bring me the ledger and the name by the twenty-ninth, and you leave this room a lieutenant. Don't, and you leave it for the front. Third Battalion is short of signallers. Signallers stand up in the open, Odell. They hold wires while the shells come down."

*The twenty-ninth.* Three days from now.

Bram heard himself say, "May I have until evening to consider it? I'm a clerk, sir. We're taught to check our columns before we total them."

Something flickered in Hessler's face — amusement, perhaps, or contempt wearing amusement's uniform. "Evening tomorrow. Not the twenty-ninth. Tomorrow evening." He tapped the commission once with one finger, as if testing whether it rang true. "You'll find, Odell, that everyone chooses eventually. The only question is whether they choose while they still have something to choose *with*."

* * *

He walked back through Poperinghe with his collar up and his mind running cold and clear, the way water runs over stones.

Tomorrow evening. Not three days. Hessler had moved the date forward and hadn't bothered to disguise the lie, because he didn't think Bram would live long enough or matter long enough for the discrepancy to surface. Which meant one thing, if Bram was willing to read it honestly: the workshop would burn ahead of schedule too. You did not give a man three days' grace on his own life and extend courtesy to his accomplice's papers. The audit, the thinning of records, the tidy little fire that Colonel Hessler had planned as an administrative necessity — all of it had been pulled forward, compressed into the same window. The twenty-sixth, perhaps. The twenty-seventh. Before Rafael could finish. Before Marta could come back. Before anyone could carry anything out.

Unless Hessler believed he already had what he wanted.

Bram stopped at the corner by the estaminet, where a girl was chalking the day's prices on a slate board, and made himself look at the thought squarely, because a man who could not look at his own thoughts was no use to anyone.

Hessler wanted a ledger. If Bram delivered a ledger — a real-looking ledger, with real names, real dates, Hessler's own signature reproduced where the evidence lived — then the colonel would burn it, satisfied, and sleep soundly, and the true pages would be sewn into a nurse's uniform lining, riding out of Flanders against her ribs.

It was a forgery. Worse than a forgery — it was a *gift*. It would hand Hessler the very evidence he feared, except false, and let him destroy it believing it true. And if it were ever discovered — if Hessler ever learned he'd burned a decoy — Bram's name would be first on the list of men to be shot at dawn. Not court-martialled. There wouldn't be time for courts where this was going. The front was coming apart to the south; everyone knew it; the whole sector smelled the way a house smells before the chimney catches.

And there was the other cost, the one with no arithmetic at all: the decoy would contain names. Names of men who had died. He would be writing the dead into a lie, handing them over to be burned a second time, even symbolically, even falsely. He thought of his brother's name in Rafael's ledger — *Odell, T., private* — the ink small and level, the date beside it, the note in her hand: *no next of kin notified per regimental return.* Tom had been erased once by an officer's pencil. Bram would be erasing him again with his own, in service of saving him. It was a wrong thing done for a right reason, and he had learned enough in the last months to know that the wrongness did not wash off. You carried it. You carried everything.

He went into the estaminet, ordered coffee he didn't want, and asked the girl for paper. She brought him the back of a delivery slip, and he thanked her, and began, right there among the smell of chicory and woodsmoke, to plan a forgery.

* * *

He spent the afternoon becoming a clerk again, which was the one disguise he'd never had to practice.

The records office kept old ledgers by the crate — superseded muster books, damaged in transit, water-stained, their bindings cracked, awaiting pulping. No one guarded them because no one imagined anyone would want them. Bram signed them out under a routine requisition, the kind of paper-shuffle that made clerks' eyes glaze, and carried four of them back to his billet in a sandbag.

Then he sat on the floor with a candle stub and studied Rafael's work from memory.

He had seen her ledger twice now, whole, under the lamp in the workshop: the narrow ruled columns, her handwriting upright and unornamented, each entry stripped to what mattered — name, rank, number, battalion, date of death, place, cause, and then the short damning line about what the official return said instead. Two hundred and six entries as of the last count. He could not reproduce two hundred and six from memory. But he didn't need to.

Hessler wanted proof of scope. Proof that the ledger existed, that it named names, that it implicated him. He would read the first thirty pages closely and the rest with growing satisfaction. So the first thirty pages must be perfect, and the rest need only be *plausible*, filled out with the names Bram knew best: the men from his own company lists, the ones whose pay books he'd processed, the dead who still drew rations in the system because no one had entered the terminal date. He knew dozens of them intimately — their numbers, their next of kin, the towns they'd come from. He had been the last clerk to touch most of their files.

He copied onto the aged pages in ink he'd thinned with water to match the faded brown-black of Rafael's entries. He blotted carefully. He tea-stained the outer leaves and dried them by the stove. He forged her neatness badly at first, then better, then well enough that by the second night his hand ached and the letters came without thinking, and he understood that he was learning her handwriting the way a man learns a prayer — not by deciding to, but by repetition in the dark.

On the third page he wrote his brother's name. His hand stopped above it.

*I'm sorry, Tom,* he thought. *This is the only way I know to keep you.* And then, because sentiment was a luxury the schedule didn't allow, he finished the entry: rank, number, date, the false line about the falsified return, everything correct except its purpose. Somewhere in the burning, when the fire took the decoy, some part of him hoped the flames would hesitate over that page. They wouldn't. Fire was fire. It was Bram who hesitated, and only briefly, and then kept writing until dawn.

* * *

The cover sheets took longer than the ledger itself.

A document moved through the army's veins on paperwork — docket slips, dispatch covers, routing stamps — and a naked ledger, however convincing, would raise questions a dressed one wouldn't. So Bram built it a wardrobe. He forged a routing stamp from a rubber heel and a knife. He created a chain of custody: the ledger ostensibly seized during a routine records audit at a casualty clearing station, forwarded under escort, annotated by two fictitious warrant officers whose signatures he invented and practiced until they flowed. He dated everything backward, spread across six weeks, so that the document's history felt worn, used, ordinary. When Hessler opened it, he should feel not discovery but confirmation — the paper equivalent of hearing your own opinion read back to you.

By the middle of the second night he caught himself smiling at the work, and the smile frightened him more than the risk did. This was what Hessler's world did to people. It taught them to love the forging.

* * *

He went to the workshop on the second evening, taking the long way through the duckboards and the flooded tram cutting, watching his backtrail the way Rafael had taught him without ever formally teaching it — *vary your route; never arrive at the same minute twice; if a man is standing at a corner doing nothing, walk past and come round again.*

Rafael met him inside, lamplight low, the copying table cleared except for the true ledger and a stack of finished duplicate sheets tied in oilcloth bundles. She looked at his face and knew before he spoke. He saw her know it. It was one of the things about her that still undid him: nothing got past her eyes, and yet she waited, always, for people to say their own truth in their own time.

"Three days," he said. "That's what he gave me. The twenty-ninth, he said at first, and then tomorrow evening for my answer. Nobody moves a deadline forward unless they mean to move everything forward with it. The burning won't wait for the end of the month, Rafael. It'll be the twenty-seventh. Maybe the twenty-sixth."

She stood very still. Behind her, the lamp flame bent slightly in a draft from under the door, and the shadows of the stacked lumber leaned and straightened like men shifting their weight.

"He offered you a commission."

"He offered me everything. That's how I knew how frightened he is." Bram set the sandbag down and untied it, laying the forged ledger on the table between them, next to the true one. For a moment the two books lay side by side under the lamp, twins, indistinguishable. "It's good," he said quietly. "I made it good. Thirty pages near-perfect, the rest filled from files I know by heart. Cover sheets, routing stamps, a seizure history. He'll believe it because he wants to believe it, and because believing it lets him stop being afraid." He looked up at her. "There are names in it that shouldn't be in any book, even a false one. I put Tom in. Page three. I couldn't — I don't know why. It felt like leaving him out would be another kind of lying."

Rafael touched the forged cover but didn't open it. Her fingers rested on the stained cloth as if feeling for a pulse.

"It's a good lie," she said at last. "Better than anything I could have made. I can copy records, Bram. I can't invent them." She raised her eyes to his. "You understand what this makes you. Not a clerk anymore. Not even a witness. If he finds out —"

"He won't find out. Because after tomorrow evening I'm either a lieutenant in his pocket or a signaller in the line, and either way he stops watching me and starts watching you." He drew a breath. "Which brings us to the part I hate. You have to move the copies out early. Not the twenty-eighth. Now. Tonight, tomorrow night at the latest. Whatever isn't sewn into your uniform by the twenty-sixth doesn't exist."

"And you?"

"I deliver his book tomorrow evening. I take his oath. I let him promote me." Bram heard the words come out of his mouth and marveled at them, at how steady they sounded, when underneath them his heart was going like a man running on duckboards. "A lieutenant attached to the colonel's staff has access to everything, Rafael. Movement orders. The burning detail, when it comes — who's assigned, what hour, which entrance they use. I can warn you with proper notice instead of guesswork. I can slow the audit. Misfile things. A clerk's sabotage is invisible; nobody suspects the furniture." He almost smiled. "I've been furniture for twenty-nine years. It's the first time it's been useful."

"No." Rafael said it flatly, immediately, the way you'd refuse a man permission to walk into shellfire. "Once you're inside his staff he'll have your file open every day. One inconsistency —"

"One inconsistency is exactly what I'm for." He stepped closer, and the lamp threw their shadows together on the plank wall. "Listen to me. Marta is still detained because of this work. The workshop burns in days whether we're ready or not. Two hundred and six names, and counting, and mothers who think their sons are alive because an officer rounded down his losses. I am not going to stand outside the fire warming my hands while you carry all of it against your skin." His voice dropped. "I asked one question too many, and it nearly destroyed me, and then you slipped a page under my door and gave the question a purpose. I'm done being careful. Careful is just fear with good posture."

She looked at him for a long moment. Outside, far to the south, the guns had begun their evening argument — a deep irregular rolling, like furniture being moved in a giant's house, closer than it had been a week ago. The lamp flame shivered.

"If this goes wrong," Rafael said finally, "they won't court-martial you. They'll shoot you behind the aid post and enter it as killed in action, and I'll spend the rest of my life unable to prove otherwise." Her voice stayed level; only her hands, folding the oilcloth over the true ledger's duplicates, betrayed her. "Do you understand that's what you're choosing?"

"I've understood it since the captain's office." He picked up the forged ledger and bound it shut with the string he'd aged with tea and ash. "Fear doesn't go away, does it. I kept waiting for it to. Everyone talks about courage like it's the absence." He shook his head. "It's not absence. It's just fear that's finally been given somewhere to go."

Rafael came around the table and stood close, and for a moment neither of them spoke, and the guns rolled, and the lamp burned, and the two ledgers lay side by side — one to be saved, one to be sacrificed, both written in the same tired ink, both full of the same dead.

"Twenty-sixth," she said. "If I haven't heard from you by noon on the twenty-sixth, I burn nothing and save nothing and assume you've been taken, and I go out the back with whatever's sewn in."

"Fair."

"And Bram." She put her hand flat against his chest, over the pocket where he'd carry the decoy into Hessler's office. "Whatever he offers you tomorrow — eat first, sleep first. Men sign things hungry."

He laughed — a short, startled sound, the first laugh in that room in weeks — and she smiled, and then the smile faded into something steadier, and she squeezed his lapel once and let him go.

* * *

He walked back through the dark with the decoy ledger inside his coat, against his ribs — the mirror image of the weight Rafael carried, though his burden was a lie and hers was the truth, and he wondered which was heavier. The shelling to the south had settled into a sustained mutter. Flares went up over the distant ridge, pale and slow, like lanterns carried by drowned men.

At the crossroads by the military police post a sentry challenged him, and he showed his pass, and the sentry waved him through without looking at his face, and Bram understood that from tonight onward he would never again be entirely sure which side of any doorway he was walking through. Double agent. The phrase sounded grand in novels. In fact it meant your stomach hurt at every meal and every friendly face might be the last one that believed you.

In his billet he laid out his best tunic, brushed it, set the forged ledger on the chair beside the commission papers he would sign in the morning. Tomorrow evening he would stand in front of Colonel Hessler and swear loyalty with a forged book under his arm and treason in his heart, and the terrible thing, the thing he would carry as long as he carried anything, was that part of him — the clerk part, the part that loved a clean column and a completed record — would enjoy it.

He blew out the candle. To the south the guns went on talking, closer now, patient, certain.

Three days. He fell asleep counting them, the way Rafael counted everything: in what could still be carried, and in what was about to burn.
