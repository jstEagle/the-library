## 15. Three Days of Light

*Rafael*

Marta came back on the morning of the first day, standing in the doorway of the workshop with a canvas bag over her shoulder and a bruise still yellowing along her jaw from the provost's cells, and Rafael did not know whether to embrace her or fall to her knees.

"You're supposed to be in Rouen," Rafael said. "I arranged the transfer. You were meant to be on yesterday's convoy."

"You arranged it badly." Marta set her bag down on the workbench among the ink pots and the stolen requisition forms as though she had never left, as though ten days of detention had been nothing more than an inconvenient errand. "The matron at the clearing station read my file. She saw where I'd been seconded from. She said—" and here Marta's mouth did something that was almost a smile, sharp as a bayonet— "she said anyone the military police found interesting enough to lock up was probably doing something worth doing. She gave me three days' leave and told me not to come back until it was spent."

"Marta—"

"Don't." Marta held up a hand. She crossed the workshop, took Rafael's face in both hands, and looked at her the way she had looked at her across two years of wards and dying men. "You didn't put me in that cell, Rafael. A colonel who counts dead men as living ones put me there. If you want to make it up to me, hand me a pen."

Rafael handed her a pen.

By noon they had reorganized the whole operation. Bram had worked out the arithmetic the night before: two hundred and six names in the ledger proper, plus the muster sheets, the pay records, the pages bearing Hessler's signature with its small spiteful downward slash—and only three days before the workshop was due for evacuation. One copyist working alone could not do it. Three might. Marta took the ward records, because she knew hospital shorthand better than either of them; Bram took the clerical cover sheets, forging dispatch headers in the round bureaucratic hand of a man who had spent years making fiction look like fact; Rafael copied the ledger itself, name by name, date by date, because she would not trust those pages to any hand but her own.

They worked at three tables pushed end to end beneath the workshop's high dusty window, and the light through the glass moved across their papers like something alive, like something they were trying to catch and keep. When it faded toward evening Rafael lit the lamps—three of them now, hoarded paraffin—and the shadows swung against the rafters where shavings still clung from the workshop's first life, curls of pine and oak that no one had ever swept away.

Outside, artillery muttered to the east. It had been muttering for days, but today it had changed its voice. Closer. Lower. The windowpanes ticked in their frames at each report, faintly, like teeth.

"Third battery this week that's shifted south," Marta said, not looking up from her page. "My brother drives ammunition lorries. He says the roads past Poperinghe are solid with guns moving night and day."

"Then we work faster," Rafael said, and dipped her pen.

*Bram*

Bram had forged perhaps four hundred documents in his life before this month, and all of them had been true in their way—a census clerk learns early that every record is a kind of story, and his job had always been to tell the stories neatly. These were different. The cover sheets he built now were elaborate fictions: regimental dispatches, casualty returns en route to base records, quartermaster audits, each one stamped with a number he invented from thin air and a signature he had practiced forty times until Colonel Hessler's adjutant's looping *W* flowed off his nib without thought.

He hated how good he had become at it. He hated more that he did not hate it at all.

His brother's name sat folded in his breast pocket—copied out fair in Rafael's hand weeks ago, when he had first seen it in the ledger and the floor of the world had tilted. *Odell, T. J., Pte.* Killed in action, 9 August 1916. Not reported. Not recorded. His mother still set Tom's place at the table on Sundays because officially, in the eyes of the army and of God's registry office in Canterbury, Tom Odell had never died at all. He had simply stopped existing, swallowed by a colonel's arithmetic, so that a line on a graph at headquarters could stay pleasingly flat.

Bram wrote another false header. His pen did not shake. He was surprised to find that somewhere in the last weeks his fear had stopped being a wall and become a floor he stood on.

At some hour he could not name—midnight or after—Rafael fell asleep over her table. She did not slump; she simply went still, chin resting on her laced fingers, eyes closed, the lamp throwing her shadow long across the names she had not yet finished copying. Marta caught Bram's eye across the room and tipped her head toward the door.

Outside, the night smelled of cold smoke and turned earth. The eastern sky pulsed without sound, a dull orange flicker like a fire seen through a closed door.

"She hasn't slept properly since the audit order came," Marta said. "I've watched her. She lies down and her eyes stay open."

"I know."

"Do you?" Marta studied him with the frank, unhurried gaze of a woman who bandaged men for a living and had therefore lost all patience with evasion. "Then here is what I know, Mr. Odell. That woman built all of this out of grief and stubbornness, alone, for a year, and it was killing her by inches. Then you walked in, and now she sleeps sometimes. Whatever the two of you are or aren't—don't you dare let this war take it from you half-finished."

She went back inside before he could answer, which he suspected was deliberate.

Bram stood a while longer in the yard. Somewhere beyond the ridge, star shells rose and drifted, silvering the ruined tree line, and he thought about Tom, who had once told him that the worst thing a man could lose was not his life but his name—that a man could survive almost anything if somebody, somewhere, still knew exactly who he was.

*Rafael*

The second day, the shells found their voices.

They had been thunder all week, general and distant; now, in the afternoon, a particular gun began speaking to a particular place, and the place was nearer than any place ought to be. The workshop windows flexed inward with each concussions' breath. Dust sifted from the rafters onto the fresh-inked pages, and Rafael swept it off with the side of her hand and kept writing, because the alternative was to stop, and stopping was unthinkable.

*Whitcombe, E., Cpl. 14 March 1917.* A farm labourer from Suffolk who had shown her a photograph of his bees, carried in his pay book behind a cellophane window. He had talked about them for twenty minutes while she dressed his thigh, about the queen and the winter cluster and how the hive hummed differently before a storm, and then he had gone back out and a shell fragment had done in six seconds what the war's whole machinery could not manage in his two years at the front—and no one had written his name anywhere but here.

*Ferrand, L., Pte. 22 April 1917.* A Belgian volunteer, sixteen years old, who had lied about his age twice.

Name after name. Her wrist ached. Her eyes burned in the lamplight that had begun before noon, because the sky outside had darkened with smoke from the direction of the offensive, a brown-grey wall sitting on the horizon like a lid pressing down.

Bram brought her tea at four o'clock—the real tea, from the bottom of Marta's canvas bag—and stood behind her reading over her shoulder, which he had earned the right to do and which still made her chest tighten, being read by someone.

"You've done sixty-one since yesterday," he said.

"Ninety-three remain. Plus the signature pages."

"We'll finish."

"You said that last night."

"And I was right last night." He set the tea down within reach of her hand, an act of such ordinary care that she nearly wept over it, and touched her shoulder once, briefly, before returning to his own table. That brief weight of his hand stayed with her for an hour. She copied four more names with it still warm in her memory.

At dusk the gun found its range on something two kilometres off, and the workshop shuddered, and a shelf of paint tins came down in the corner with a crash that put everyone's heart into their throats. Marta laughed—high, unsteady, real laughter, the kind people use to keep from screaming—and righted the tins, and they all went back to work, and Rafael understood that this was what she had been starving for without knowing it: not safety, there was no such thing anymore, but company in the work. Three lamps burning against the dark. Three sets of hands moving for the same dead.

That night, between shifts, she and Bram walked the perimeter of the yard because Marta ordered them to walk it, ordered them to breathe air that wasn't full of ink, ordered them with the absolute authority of a senior nurse, which neither of them dared question.

The sky to the east burned continuously now, a low flicker without pause, and the ground carried a tremor like a heartbeat.

"If we get these pages out," Bram said, "what happens after? To us, I mean. Have you thought?"

"Every day," Rafael said. "Usually at three in the morning."

"And?"

"And I think that whoever I am after this war, she will be someone who was made here, in this workshop, by lamplight, alongside you." She kept her eyes on the burning horizon because looking at him directly would have undone her. "That is the most honest answer I have. I can't promise anything shaped like a future. But I can promise that I know exactly who you are, Bram Odell. That has to count for something. You taught me it does."

He reached over and took her hand, ink-stained both of them, chapped and cold, and they stood in the yard holding on to each other while the horizon flickered, and it was not much, it was almost nothing, five stolen minutes—but it was everything, and they both knew it, and neither wasted a word saying so.

*Bram*

The third day broke grey and close, and Bram woke at the table with his cheek on a finished cover sheet and the taste of paraffin in his mouth, and knew before he opened his eyes that something was wrong.

Not the shelling—though that too, the rhythm had changed again overnight, longer salvos, wider spacing, a gun re-laying itself onto new targets. Something smaller. Marta was standing at the window with field glasses she had produced from nowhere, and her posture was all wrong.

"What?" he said.

"A staff car came up the Poperinghe road twenty minutes ago and stopped at the crossroads. Two officers got out. They walked the boundary of the next property and then drove on toward the transport depot." She lowered the glasses. "Surveying. That's what it looked like. Surveying."

Rafael rose from her cot in the back room, already dressed, already awake—he wondered if she ever truly slept—and joined them at the window. For a moment none of them spoke. The lamps still burned on the tables, guttering low; the last thirty-one names waited in the open ledger under Rafael's hand.

"Evacuation isn't until the day after tomorrow," Rafael said quietly. "And Hessler's audit is scheduled for the same week. Both after we're meant to be gone. That was the timetable. Quartermaster Verhaeghe confirmed it himself."

"Timetables," Bram said, "are things men change."

He spent the morning doing what he was best at and most ashamed of: walking into the records office at the transport depot with a census clerk's harmless face and pulling strings he had no right to pull, trading the last of his goodwill like a man spending his final coins. He bought a lorry driver two bottles of vin blanc and learned that a requisition order had come through overnight for demolition materials—petrol, incendiary paste, timber for burning—assigned to a work party scheduled for the old carpentry workshop.

No date on the order. Which meant the date was whatever Hessler wanted it to be.

Bram walked back along the road with the bottles untouched in his hands, giving them to the driver instead as thanks, and did not run, because running attracted attention, though every nerve in his body screamed at him to run. Two kilometres at a pace just short of conspicuous. Behind him the guns spoke, and ahead of him three lamps were burning over thirty-one unfinished names, and he counted his steps and did the arithmetic he had fled his old life to escape: petrol, incendiary paste, a work party, no date.

When he came through the workshop door, Rafael looked up from the ledger, read his face in half a second the way she read a wounded man's, and set down her pen.

"How long do we have?" she asked.

"I don't know," Bram said. "But it isn't three days."
