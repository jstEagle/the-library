## 16. The Burning

The smoke came before the trucks.

Rafael smelled it at half past four in the afternoon—a wrongness in the wind off the ridgeline, char and petrol where there should have been only mud and rain and the distant iron stink of the guns. She was standing at the workshop's long bench with a pen still wet in her hand, copying Private Ellery Cobb's name for the third time because her eyes had blurred on the second, when the smell reached her and every nerve in her body went quiet the way water goes quiet just before it freezes.

Marta noticed it too. She had been sorting the finished dispatch folders into their false regimental wrappers, humming something Lithuanian under her breath, and she stopped mid-note. Her head came up like a deer's.

"That's not our chimney," she said.

It wasn't. Their stove burned coal, and coal had a patient smell, domestic, almost kind. This was petrol and green wood and something else beneath it—something that did not belong to any hearth. Rafael set down her pen with exaggerated care, the way one does when running would be fatal, and crossed to the window.

The lane outside ran grey with drizzle. Beyond it, the poplars along the canal road stood black and dripping, and beyond those—where the road bent toward the crossroads at Veldhoek—there was dust. Dust in the rain. Wheels.

"They're early," she said. "They're a day early."

Bram was not there. He had gone to the records office at dawn to pull the last of the cover sheets, carrying his forged requisitions folded inside his greatcoat like love letters, and he was not due back until dark. The plan—the plan they had built over three weeks of stolen lamplight and cold tea—had been simple enough to survive exhaustion: burn nothing until the copies were out; move at night; let Hessler find an empty room and a stove full of ash and no witnesses but the rats. They had one more day. Bram had counted the hours aloud the night before, tapping them off on his fingers like a schoolmaster: *one more day of light, Yara—one more day.*

But Hessler's arithmetic had never once matched hers.

"Marta." Rafael heard her own voice come out level, and marveled distantly at it. "The tin box. The north wall."

Marta was already moving. She went to the loose board behind the timber rack and lifted out the tin biscuit box that held what could not be replaced if this hour went badly: the evidence of Hessler's signatures, the quartermaster's sworn note about the audit order, the photograph of the muster page with its two hundred and six ghosts. She pressed it into Rafael's hands without a word, and then she did the other thing they had agreed on, the thing they had rehearsed twice with candles and once with a real lamp, and began feeding the nearest ledgers into the cold stove—not lighting them, there was no time to light anything, simply destroying them by wet and tearing, pages ripped and drowned in the water bucket so that no legible word would survive whatever came through that door.

Rafael took off her uniform jacket.

She had sewn the lining three nights ago, sitting on her cot with a sailmaker's needle borrowed from the barge men, while Bram read names aloud to her in his flat census-clerk voice—*Cobb, Ellery A.; Danner, Wilfred; Okafor—*—and she stitched each page of the true ledger into the fabric between wool and cotton, flat as pressed flowers, close as ribs. Two hundred and six names. Forty-one pages. It made the jacket hang slightly wrong on the left side, and no one who saw her wear it would ever know why she kept touching her left arm as though it ached.

She put it on now. The pages settled against her heart, warm from her body already, and she thought—absurdly, in the last calm moment she would have for years—that this was what armor felt like. Not steel. Paper. The weight of paper, which she had measured since the first night at the dressing station, when grief had taught her that everything in this war came down to what a person could carry when the evacuation order came.

Boots in the lane. Not one pair. Many.

"Back door," Marta said. "Go now. Don't run till the hedge—you'll be seen if you run."

"You come with me."

"I'm the laundress," Marta said, and smiled, and it was the worst smile Rafael had ever seen, bright and finished, a smile with an ending already written into it. "I've got papers. I'm a Belgian washerwoman with a permit and a bad temper and no English. You're a nurse with two hundred and six dead men sewn into your coat. One of us can talk her way out of a search, and we both know which." She shoved the water bucket across the floor with her foot, slopping it over the ruined pages. "Go *now*, Rafael. Before I lose my courage. I'm not brave. I'm just better at pretending than you are."

The door at the front shuddered under the first blow.

* * *

Rafael went through the back into the yard where the carpenters' offcuts rotted under sacking, and crouched behind the timber stack with her heart slamming against the sewn pages, and did not run, because Marta had told her not to run, and obeying Marta was the last thing she would ever be able to do for her.

Through the gap between boards she saw them come in: six men in provost armbands and two in officer's greatcoats, and a seventh figure at the rear who did not enter at all but stood by the truck with his hands clasped behind him, watching the windows the way a man watches a fire he has ordered. She knew the silhouette even at forty yards. Colonel Hessler had a stillness about him that she had learned to dread in the ward, in officers deciding which men were worth the morphine. He did not move. He did not need to. The door broke inward on the third blow and his men poured through, and the sound that followed was the sound Rafael would hear for the rest of her life: boots on floorboards, a table going over, Marta's voice rising—not frightened, furious, a washerwoman's torrent of Flemish and French—and then the crack of something struck hard, and silence, and then Marta again, lower, steadier, saying words Rafael couldn't make out except for the tone, which said: *I am nobody. You have made a mistake. I am nobody.*

A drawer wrenched open. Paper torn. The stove lid clanged.

"Where is the woman who works here?" An officer's voice, bored and loud, performing authority for the empty lane. "The English nurse. Where does she sleep?"

"I work alone," Marta said. "I wash. I press. I go home to Poperinghe on Thursdays. There is no nurse. You have frightened an old woman for nothing."

Something else crashed. Rafael's hands found her mouth and held it shut. Through the gap in the boards she watched Hessler by the truck tilt his head slightly, listening, and saw him say something to the sergeant, and understood with a cold that started in her teeth and spread downward: he didn't believe it either. He had come early precisely because the trail was going cold, and he would take apart this room plank by plank, and the woman standing in the middle of it with her chin up and her hands red from drowning pages was not going to be permitted to be nobody.

They were going to take her.

And Rafael—this was the wound she would carry beside the others, the one that would wake her at four in the morning for thirty years—Rafael stayed behind the woodpile. Because the ledger weighed against her ribs. Because two hundred and six names could not survive an arrest. Because every argument she had ever made to herself about the arithmetic of sacrifice assembled itself in that moment, orderly and terrible, and told her to stay down.

She stayed down.

The sergeant's voice: "Take her. The colonel wants everyone who's touched these rooms."

Marta did not scream. That was the thing. When they took her arms she twisted once, hard, professional, a woman who had wrestled wet sheets all her life, and nearly freed herself—and then she stopped. Deliberately. She looked at the back wall. At the timber stack. For half a second her eyes found the gap in the boards, found Rafael behind it, and Rafael would swear until her dying day that Marta looked straight at her and shook her head once, small, almost gentle.

Then she spat at the sergeant's boots and was dragged through the door, and her voice went on a long time after she was gone, cursing them in two languages, growing fainter down the lane toward the trucks, until even that was taken.

Hessler stood another minute in the yard. Rain dripped off the poplars. Then he said, quietly, in German-accented English that carried strangely far in the wet air:

"Burn it."

* * *

They were efficient. Of course they were. Petrol from the truck, splashed across the bench where Rafael had copied Cobb's name three times; a lantern hurled through the window of the little office at the back where Bram had forged his cover sheets, where the two of them had sat shoulder to shoulder through the small hours with ink on their fingers and their knees touching under the table and neither of them ever once acknowledging it, because acknowledging things was dangerous and they were already carrying enough.

The fire went up with a soft whump, almost gentle, and then greedily.

Rafael ran.

Not until the hedge—she remembered that, she honored that—but the moment the bend of the lane took her out of sight of the yard she ran as she had not run since training, along the drainage ditch with the water sucking at her boots, past the shattered barn, into the beet fields where the mud pulled and grabbed, and behind her the workshop caught fully alight and the smoke stood up into the grey sky in a column visible, she knew, from the dressing station, from the canal, from everywhere, and men at the guns would glance at it and think: *ammunition dump hit,* and go on with their dying.

She reached the copse at the field's edge and turned, chest heaving, sides knifing, and looked back.

The roof was going. Flames stood out of the windows like things escaping. Sparks rose and wheeled in the rain and drifted east over the fields, and ash came with them, settling on the beet leaves, on the ditches, on her sleeves—soft grey flakes that she caught on her palm and stared at, because she knew what they had been an hour ago. Ledgers. Muster rolls. Letters home that officers had never sent, drafts and fragments and the quartermaster's careful inventories, three months of Bram's beautiful fraudulent clerksmanship, everything they had not yet copied, everything they had judged too heavy to carry.

Ash fell on the fields like grey snow, and the pages of the true ledger lay warm and flat against her heart, and she pressed her hand over them and felt them there, forty-one sheets of paper, two hundred and six men, and understood that this was the whole of it now. This was everything left. What she could carry.

She said Marta's name once, out loud, to the burning, because someone should, because a name spoken aloud was the only monument that could be raised in an afternoon in a beet field in Flanders, and then she turned south toward Poperinghe where the roads to the coast began, and walked.

* * *

She found Bram at dusk, or he found her—it was impossible afterward to say which—at the ruined mill two miles down the canal where they had agreed, in the old plan, to meet if the night movement went ahead. He came up the towpath at a stumbling half-run, coatless, ink still on his fingers, his face white as paper in the last light, and when he saw her standing under the broken arch he stopped as though he'd walked into glass.

"The smoke," he said. "I saw it from the crossroads. I thought—" His voice failed. He tried again. "Marta?"

"She's taken. Hessler's provosts. She gave them nothing." Rafael heard her voice as if it belonged to a stranger, flat and precise, a nurse delivering a report. "They'll question her at the corps cage. Bram, she looked at me. Through the boards. She knew I was there and she shook her head so I wouldn't—" The precision cracked. She put her hand against the mill wall and stood very still until it passed, and Bram came and stood beside her without touching her, because some things could not be touched, only witnessed.

"They burned it," he said finally. "All of it? Everything?"

"Everything we hadn't carried out." She opened her jacket, right there in the ruin with the rain coming through the broken roof, and showed him the lining, the flat stitched weight of it, the corner of a page just visible where the thread had strained. His breath went out of him slowly. He reached toward it and stopped himself, fingers curling back, as if the pages were a flame or a relic.

"It's here," he whispered. "Two hundred and six."

"And Hessler's signature. And the audit order. Enough." She closed the jacket. Under her palm the pages were warm. "Enough to hang him, if anyone ever has the courage. Enough to send the letters. It has to be enough, because it's all there is."

He nodded, and then his composure went all at once, the mild census clerk dissolving into the exhausted young man underneath, and he pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes and said, "I was one day late. One day. If I'd been there—"

"If you'd been there you'd be in the cage beside her, and the ledger would be ash, and she'd have shaken her head at both of us." Rafael took his wrists and lowered his hands and made him look at her. "Listen to me. Grieve later. We can't afford it yet. The coast is thirty miles and every road between here and Calais will have checkpoints on it by morning, because Hessler knows what survived his fire—he knows, Bram, that's why the trucks moved early, he's not hunting paper anymore, he's hunting *us*. We are the discrepancy now."

Down the canal, far off toward the front, the guns opened their evening conversation, flashes stuttering along the horizon like heat lightning, closer than yesterday, closer than the week before. The offensive was coming. Everyone in Flanders could feel it in the ground.

"We walk tonight," Rafael said. "Canal paths, then the secondary road past Reninghelst. We're a nurse escorting a wounded clerk to the base hospital—I'll write you a chit, I still look the part. We don't stop. We don't speak unless we must. And Bram—" She hesitated, and the hesitation cost her more than the running had. "When we get there. When it's done. We find out where they've taken her, and we get her back. Whatever it costs. She bought this with her life's peace, and I will not let the price be paid and the goods abandoned."

Bram looked at her a long moment. Rain ran off the broken mill beam above them. When he spoke, his voice had changed—something hardened in it, something she had never heard there before, quiet and absolute.

"No," he said. "We do it first."

"What?"

"We find where they've taken her *first*. Before the coast. There's a divisional holding depot at Proven—they process detainees through it, I've seen the routing slips, it's in the files I was pulling today." He was already turning the idea over, and she could see the clerk in him waking, the man who found patterns in other people's lists. "If she's anywhere, she's there tonight, before transfer. One night, Rafael. One night while the roads are chaos and every provost in the sector is watching the front, not the rear." His jaw set. "I lost her once today because I was counting days. I won't count them again."

The guns flashed. Ash was still drifting down through the rain, fine and grey, settling on the towpath, on their shoulders, on everything the workshop had been. Rafael touched the pages over her heart—warm, flat, alive—and looked down the dark canal toward Proven, toward the coast, toward the long night coming, and made the choice she would carry with the rest.

"All right," she said. "One night. Then we run."
