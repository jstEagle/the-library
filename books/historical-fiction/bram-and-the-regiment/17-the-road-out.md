## 17. The Road Out

*Rafael*

The road to Poperinghe had become a river of everything an army could not carry.

Rafael walked in the ditch because the ditches were slower and slower meant alive. Above her, lorries ground past loaded with crates of brandy for officers' messes while ambulances went empty. Men sat on the tailboards with their boots dangling, staring at nothing, and one of them called down to her — *Sister, is it true they're giving up the sector?* — and she said she didn't know, which was true, and he laughed as though she'd told him a fine joke.

The pages against her ribs were warm from her body. She was conscious of them the way a man is conscious of a broken tooth: not always, but never far, a small constant pressure that sharpened whenever she forgot it. Marta had sewn them there three days before the fire, kneeling on the workshop floor with a sailmaker's needle and waxed thread, laughing shakily at the absurdity of it — *you'll set off the metal detectors at Heaven's gate, Rafa* — and now Marta was ash somewhere behind her, and the joke had curdled into something Rafael carried along with the pages themselves: two hundred and six names, forty-one sheets of paper, one dead friend, all pressed flat against her sternum by a uniform jacket buttoned to the throat.

Bram limped beside her. His wound was in the thigh — a splinter of brick from the burning workshop that had gone deep and come out uglier than it went in — and every half mile or so his stride would hitch, a tiny stutter, and then correct itself, and neither of them would say anything about it. They had developed this economy of silence on the first night out. There was so much that could not be said that they had agreed, without agreeing, to say almost none of it aloud.

They looked like deserters because that was what they were, in the eyes of any provost who stopped them. Bram still wore his records-office tunic; Rafael had traded her nurse's apron for a greatcoat two sizes too large, taken from a pile outside a casualty clearing station that no longer existed. But deserters moved furtively, hugged hedges, avoided roads. So Rafael made herself walk in the open, chin up, eyes forward, the walk of a woman with somewhere official to be. It cost more strength than she could account for. Endurance, she was learning, was mostly performance.

At a crossroads clogged with refugees — old men with carts, women pushing prams full of bedding, children carrying the family cat like a sack of meal — a military policeman was checking papers. Rafael watched him work his way up the line of carts, bored, thorough, and felt the pages go cold against her skin.

"Papers," the MP said when they reached him.

Bram produced his regimental pass, the one Hessler's own office had issued, and Rafael watched him hold it steady and watch the MP hold it too long.

"Records section," the MP read. "What's a records man doing on foot?"

"Evacuation orders," Bram said. "Workshop's being cleared ahead of the withdrawal."

"You'd know better than me." The MP handed it back, then looked at Rafael. "And you?"

She had prepared a lie. She had prepared it carefully, in the hours of walking, and it dissolved now like sugar in rain. "I'm a nurse," she said. "My station was evacuated this morning. I'm making for Calais for reassignment." And then, because the truth was the only thing she had left that worked: "Check my hands if you like."

He did look at her hands — the carbolic-cracked skin, the crescents of dirt under nails she'd stopped bothering with weeks ago — and something in his face shifted, some memory of his own, of a dressing station perhaps, of someone. He waved them through.

They walked another mile before either spoke.

"You should have let me talk," Bram said.

"He wasn't going to believe you either. He was going to believe my hands." She glanced sideways at him. He was grey around the mouth, sweating though the day was cold, and each hitch in his stride came a little sooner than the last. "How bad?"

"It's fine."

"Bram."

"It needs cleaning," he admitted. "It needs cleaning and I need to stop walking for a day, and we don't have a day."

She looked back down the road, at the smoke standing over Poperinghe in columns like organ pipes, and did the arithmetic she had been doing since the workshop burned. Three days to Calais if they were lucky. Five if the roads stayed choked. A wound like his, untended, would take the choice out of their hands long before the coast decided it.

"There's a convent at Oostvleteren," she said. "We ran wounded through it in '15. The sisters kept a cellar ward. If it's still standing—"

"And if they ask questions?"

"Sisters don't ask questions. Sisters have heard everything." She took his arm, partly for the appearance of it, mostly because he was swaying. "Come on. We can be carried together or fall down separately."

* * *

*Bram*

He dreamed of the workshop on fire and woke in the dark of the convent cellar with Sister Bernadette's lamp swinging above him and the smell of carbolic where he'd expected smoke.

"The fever is nothing," the sister said, in the tone of a woman who had pronounced worse things nothing and buried them anyway. "The leg is something. You will stay until tomorrow night, no longer, because tomorrow night there is a lorry going west and I have already spoken to the driver about two passengers who do not exist."

Rafael was asleep sitting up, her back against the cellar wall, her head tipped forward, and even asleep her hand had found the edge of her jacket where the pages lay, fingers resting there like a guard dog that dozed but did not leave its post. Bram watched her for a while. In the lamplight she looked younger than she ever allowed herself to look awake, and older too, worn in a way that had nothing to do with years.

"You love her," Sister Bernadette observed, changing his dressing with hands that were quick and impersonal as weather. "Or you will. It makes no difference to me which."

"I barely know her."

"I buried a husband in 1871," the sister said. "I knew him four months. People say *barely* as though knowing were measured in clocks." She tied off the bandage. "The wound is clean enough. The walking will reopen it. That is not my business. What is your business, Mr. Odell, is deciding what you are carrying her toward. The coast is full of military police, and the ports are worse, and whatever you two have done—" she held up a hand before he could speak, "—do not tell me, I have not asked and will not — whatever it is, it is written on both of you the way guilt is written on boys after their first battle. Decide whether it is worth what it costs. Then stop deciding and carry it."

In the morning Rafael brought him bread and chicory coffee and sat on the edge of the cot, and he saw that she had been crying, briefly and efficiently, sometime before dawn, and that she would deny it if asked, so he didn't ask.

"Marta's file," she said. "I've been thinking. When this is over — the names restored, all of it — someone has to write to her mother. Someone has to write the letter the army won't write, saying what she actually died of. Holding a door." Her voice held level until the last word and then didn't quite. "I've started it eleven times in my head. Every version sounds like a report."

"Then write it like a letter," Bram said. "Like she's reading it."

Rafael looked at him for a moment, and he saw the thought land, and saw her fold it away to keep. "Yes," she said. "All right. Yes."

The lorry came at dusk, a requisitioned civilian bedford with straw in the back and a driver named Verstraete who asked no questions because Sister Bernadette had paid him in candles and cigarettes and the currency of having once hidden his brother from the gendarmes in another war. They rode through the night wedged among crates marked FLOUR, and Bram slept badly against Rafael's shoulder, and woke each time the lorry slowed, certain of checkpoints, hearing instead only wind and the creak of the canvas and once, far off, the flat rolling thunder of the front falling further east behind them.

* * *

*Rafael*

Calais announced itself as a smell — coal smoke, fish, tar, and under it the sea, vast and indifferent — before it resolved into a skyline of cranes and masts beyond a ridge of dunes. By then they had left the lorry and were walking again, because Verstraete would go no closer than the outskirts, and because Bram's wound had done exactly what Sister Bernadette predicted, opened and seeped, so that he walked with his weight thrown oddly to one side like a man leaning into a permanent headwind.

They came down through streets crowded with soldiers on leave and soldiers waiting to embark and soldiers too broken to be either, past cafés roaring with English voices, and Rafael understood suddenly how exposed they were. In Flanders everyone had been fleeing in the same direction; here the crowd flowed both ways, and authority pooled in doorways and street corners, red-capped MPs in threes, an officer with a clipboard outside the quay gate, and everywhere the casual machinery of checking, counting, stamping.

"We need papers that get us on a boat," Bram murmured. "Not across a border. Just onto a ship. Hospital transport, troopship, anything."

"You can forge dispatch covers. Can you forge travel passes?"

"Dispatch covers are easy — nobody reads them, everybody files them. Travel passes are the opposite. Everybody reads them, nobody keeps them." He was quiet a moment, thinking, and she watched the clerk wake up behind his eyes, the mild man who had once noticed dead men drawing pay, finding in himself a talent he must have suspected and never named. "But there's another way. The base records depot is here — I saw the sign coming in, Rue de la République. If I can get inside, I can enter us into the movement ledger myself. Two lines of ink and we're officially en route to Étaples for reassignment. Nobody stops a man moving between depots. It's the one direction nobody questions."

"Inside a records depot. With your leg."

"With my leg," he agreed. "And with you standing outside looking like a woman waiting for her husband, which you are very good at."

"I am *not*—" she began, and stopped, because arguing cost time, and because some traitorous part of her wanted to hear the sentence finished. "One hour. If you're not out in one hour I'm coming in after you, and I won't be subtle."

"One hour," he said, and touched her elbow, briefly, the way you touch a railing in the dark to confirm it's solid, and limped off toward the Rue de la République.

She waited in the lee of a warehouse wall, counting minutes by the bells of a church whose tower had lost its top to a bomb the year before. Twenty minutes. Thirty-five. At fifty minutes a column of troops came down the street singing, and she lost sight of the depot entrance in the press of them, and at sixty-two minutes she pushed off the wall to go in after him —

— and the door of the depot opened and Bram came out of it at speed, white-faced, not limping now but hurrying, hurrying in a way that had nothing to do with urgency and everything to do with flight, and behind him in the doorway stood two men in the blue-grey of military intelligence, and one of them raised his voice across the street, unhurried, terribly courteous:

"Sir. Madam. If you'd step inside — just a few questions. It won't take a moment."

Rafael felt the pages shift against her ribs as she turned, forty-one sheets of paper, two hundred and six names, everything, pressing cold against her heart, and she took Bram's arm and smiled at the intelligence officers the way you smile at the edge of a cliff you're about to be driven over.

"Of course," she said. "We'd be happy to help."

* * *

The room they were taken to had a table, two chairs, a map of the Channel ports pinned to one wall, and a window high and barred. The intelligence officer — a major, his cuff tabs crisp, his eyes tired in the specific way of a man who had spent two years listening to liars and had begun to find the truth exotic — set down a folder that was thin, which frightened her more than a thick one would have.

"Your name," he said to Rafael, not unkindly.

"Give him the truth," Bram said quietly, beside her. Not a question. An answer to the question she hadn't asked yet.

The major waited, pen poised, and outside, faintly, like the first spatter of rain on a far roof, came the sound of heavy guns to the south.
