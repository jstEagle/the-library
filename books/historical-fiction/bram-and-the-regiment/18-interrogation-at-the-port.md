## 18. Interrogation at the Port

The room at the port had once been a customs office, and it still smelled of ink and damp wood and the sea that came through the boarded window in thin salted drafts. A desk stood in the middle of the floor with nothing on it but a lamp and a single sheet of paper laid square to the desk's edge, as if whoever used the room believed that order on a surface might produce order in the world. Rafael Yarrow sat opposite the desk on a chair too low for her, so that she had to look up at the two military policemen flanking the door, and further up at the map of the French coast pinned behind them, dotted with pins whose coloured heads meant things she was not cleared to know.

Her wrists ached where the corporal had bound them with signal wire, not cruelly but thoroughly, the way men did things when they had done them many times. The wire had been cut an hour ago, but the ache stayed, printing itself into her memory of the journey from the quay: Bram limping between the gendarmes, his face grey under its stubble, both of them shouting explanations that no one wrote down.

The lamp hissed. Somewhere beyond the wall a ship's horn sounded, long and mournful, and Rafael thought: *that is the sound of everything I have not yet done.*

They had taken her bag at the gate. Not the uniform — they had not dared undress a woman at a checkpoint with a queue behind her — and the pages lay against her ribs where Marta's stitches held them, thirty-one sheets sewn into the lining of her tunic like a second skin. She could feel them when she breathed. For four days on the road she had told herself that feeling them was comfort. Now, sitting in this tidy room with its squared paper and its low chair designed to make people feel small, she understood that feeling them was also risk. If anyone searched her properly — a matron, an orderly, any woman with quick fingers and orders — the seams would give up their secret in ten seconds flat.

The door opened.

The man who came in was perhaps fifty, unremarkable in every particular except his eyes, which were the pale washed-out blue of a winter sky over the dunes and which moved, when he entered, first to Rafael's hands, then to her boots, then to the cut signal wire still coiled on the corner of the desk where someone had left it. He wore the insignia of a lieutenant colonel in the Intelligence Corps, but his uniform was travel-worn and he carried no cane, no gloves, none of the small theatre of rank. He closed the door himself and did not lock it.

"Nurse Yarrow." He said it as though reading it off a list, though there was no list on the desk. "I am Lieutenant Colonel Vasseur. You'll forgive the accommodations. Calais is not built for guests."

"You've made my friend a prisoner as well," Rafael said. "He needs a doctor. His leg."

"Captain Odell is being seen to." He said *Captain* deliberately, watching her face, and Rafael kept hers still even as something turned over inside her. They had promoted Bram somewhere between the quay and this room, or invented the rank to make him talk, or simply misspoken — three possibilities, each one a different kind of trap. "Sit, please. Or continue sitting. You have the advantage of me; you're already arranged."

She did not smile. He crossed to the desk, took the single sheet of paper, and read it as if for the first time.

"It says here," he remarked, "that you were detained attempting to board a hospital transport without papers of movement. That you gave your unit as a dressing station near Ypres which, I can confirm, was evacuated nine days ago. That the man with you claims to be a records clerk attached to the same sector, carrying no records. And that when searched—" he set the paper down and looked at her— "nothing was found on either of you. No paybook. No identity disc. No letters. Two people crossing a war zone in uniform with empty pockets."

"The supply lines collapsed," Rafael said. "Our kit went forward on a lorry that never reached us. I've been wearing the same clothes for eleven days; you're welcome to smell the proof."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite amusement, more the acknowledgment that she had declined to be frightened in the approved manner. He pulled the other chair around to her side of the desk and sat, closer than protocol wanted, so that the lamp light fell on both their faces equally. She noticed his hands then: a surgeon's hands, or a clerk's, clean and precise, one fingernail ragged where it had been bitten.

"I am going to tell you how these interviews usually go," Vasseur said. "I ask where you are going. You say England. I ask why you have no papers. You say lost. I ask why your companion cannot keep his story straight about his own rank. You say shock. Then I send you to the holding shed at the end of the quay to await transfer to Boulogne, and in Boulogne there is a man whose methods I do not supervise, and in four days you will tell him anything he wishes to hear whether it is true or not, because everyone does. Including innocent people. Especially innocent people." He folded his bitten fingernail against his thumb. "I would rather not send you to Boulogne. But you should understand that I will, if you leave me no alternative. That is not a threat. It is arithmetic."

Rafael looked at the pale eyes for a long moment and thought of Colonel Hessler's office, the cologne and the polished brass, the way he had smiled at her across his desk six months ago while signing away forty men with a flourish that looked almost like affection. An institution was an institution. Trust was the thing she had learned last to give and first to regret.

But arithmetic ran both ways. If Vasseur was Hessler's man, honesty would deliver the ledger straight into the fire it had been carried through ash to save. And if he was honest — if there was one honest man left between here and London — then silence would accomplish what the burning workshop had failed to do: bury the names for good.

There was no third option. She had counted the possibilities all night on the floor of a cattle truck, and there were only two.

*Trust,* she thought, *is just a gamble you make after the odds stop mattering.*

"My name is Rafael Yarrow," she said. "Sister, Queen Alexandra's Imperial Military Nursing Service, currently absent without leave. The man you have in the other room is Bram Odell. He is not a captain. Until eleven days ago he was a census clerk seconded to the regimental records office of the 118th Brigade. He is twenty-six years old. His left leg has shrapnel in it from Passchendaele that was never properly removed, and he walks on it because he is stubborn." Her voice stayed level; she was surprised by that. "And I am carrying, sewn into the lining of this tunic, the true casualty ledger of the 118th Brigade for the period March 1916 to April 1918. Two hundred and six names of soldiers killed in action whose deaths were never reported. Their families were never notified. Their pensions were never paid. Some of their mothers are still writing letters to addresses where no one will ever answer them."

Vasseur had gone very still. It was the stillness of a man who had been listening for exactly this and was now afraid he'd heard it.

"Colonel Hessler signed the falsified returns," Rafael went on. "His signature is on these pages, dated, alongside the figures he sent to corps — figures that showed his brigade at strength when it was at half. That is why the workshops burned. That is why Marta Kessel is dead. She was a carpenter's widow who mended our shelves, and she stood in a doorway so I could get out the back, and they shot her for it." She heard her own voice rise and forced it back down. "You can send me to Boulogne. You can have this tunic taken off me and these pages put in an evidence locker where one clerical error will lose them forever. Or you can help me get them to London, where there is a board of inquiry convening in June that has already requested independent verification of the 118th's returns. Those are your alternatives, Lieutenant Colonel. I've done my arithmetic too."

For a while the only sound was the lamp and the sea.

"Two hundred and six," Vasseur said at last. It was not a question. He was testing whether the number held weight in his mouth.

"Two hundred and six. Plus eleven more whose surnames we never confirmed — marked by battalion and date only. We did what we could. It wasn't enough."

"Show me."

"Not here." She shook her head before the word finished leaving her mouth, and the refusal felt enormous, absurd — refusing a lieutenant colonel in a locked room — but she held it. "Not in a building with a signals section and a typing pool. If you're his man, you'll take them from me regardless, and I'd rather you had to work for it. If you're honest, then you'll understand why I don't show them to anyone until they're in front of the board. Read them here, and within an hour three people know they exist. Carry them to London yourself, tonight, and nobody knows anything until it's too late to burn them."

Vasseur regarded her. Outside, the ship's horn sounded again, and beneath their feet the harbour water knocked against pilings like a patient fist.

"You realize," he said quietly, "that you have just described, accurately, the tradecraft of a spy."

"I've described the tradecraft of a woman who has already been robbed once," Rafael said. "Everything I had is ash. Everything that survived is against my ribs. I'm sorry. I don't have any trust left to perform."

To her astonishment, he nodded — slowly, the nod of a man conceding a point in an argument he had been having with himself.

"In April," he said, "I was asked to look into a discrepancy. A chaplain at Hazebrouck wrote to GHQ because a woman in Leeds kept receiving letters about her son — cheerful letters — from comrades who did not know he was dead, while the War Office insisted he was alive and drawing separation allowance. Someone at GHQ decided the chaplain was confused. The file was passed to my section because no one else wanted it." He rubbed the ragged thumbnail. "I found three more like it before I was instructed to close the file and return to routine duties. The instruction came endorsed by Colonel Hessler, whom I had never met, recommending my section be commended for efficiency." The pale eyes came up to hers. "So you will understand when I say that I believe you, Nurse Yarrow, and that believing you has cost me something already."

"Then help us," she whispered.

"I am going to do something worse than help you. I am going to believe you officially." He stood, took the single sheet of paper from the desk, and tore it in half, and in half again, letting the pieces fall. "As far as this office is concerned, Sister Rafael Yarrow and civilian Bram Odell were detained at the port in consequence of a documentation error arising from the evacuation, identified and released same day. No record of search. No record of interview." He took his cap from the desk corner. "There is a mail boat leaving on the morning tide, carrying wounded priority personnel. Captain Odell's shrapnel has been noted by the medical officer, who is an old patient of mine and asks few questions. You will travel as his escort. In Folkestone there will be a car. The driver's name is Hobbes; he will ask you nothing and remember nothing."

Rafael rose on legs that did not entirely feel like hers. "Why?"

Vasseur paused at the door, hand on the latch.

"Because in nineteen months, that file in Leeds has kept me awake more nights than the mines at Vimy," he said. "Because a mother in Leeds deserves better than a cheerful letter. And because if Hessler's friends find out what I've done tonight — and they will, eventually — then you'll want to reach London faster than I reached it, and warn the board before the warning reaches them instead." He opened the door onto the noise of the harbour, gulls and engines and men shouting in two languages. "Get some sleep if you can. The tide doesn't wait for heroes. It barely waits for the living."

* * *

They let her see Bram in the medical hut, a canvas shelter smelling of carbolic and wet wool, where he sat with his trouser leg rolled down over a fresh dressing and a mug of tea he hadn't touched. When she came through the flap he tried to stand, thought better of it, and settled for gripping her hand hard enough to hurt.

"They believed you," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"One of them did. One was enough." She lowered herself onto the stool beside his cot and, very carefully, let herself lean until her shoulder rested against his shoulder. Neither of them said anything for a moment. Through the canvas they could hear the tide turning, the whole vast indifferent machinery of the port grinding toward morning. "We sail at dawn. There'll be a car at Folkestone. After that I don't know."

Bram turned the mug slowly on his knee, watching the tea go cold.

"When they questioned me," he said at last, "the sergeant kept asking the same thing over and over. Who am I, why no papers, why the wrong rank. And I kept thinking — the whole time he was shouting — I kept thinking about Tom's name in the ledger. Page ninety-four. Your handwriting under mine." His voice cracked on the last word and he pressed on through it. "Eleven days ago I couldn't even say my brother's name out loud. Tonight a sergeant was screaming at me and all I could think was: you don't know who I am. You don't know what I'm carrying. None of you know what we carried through the fire." He looked up at her, and his eyes were wet and fierce. "Is that what being brave is? Just being too angry to be frightened anymore?"

"No," Rafael said softly. "Being brave is doing it frightened. The anger comes later, if you're lucky." She squeezed his hand. "Sleep, Bram. Tomorrow we give them the names."

Beyond the hut, out on the black water, the morning boat sounded its horn — long, low, and this time it sounded less like mourning than like a summons. Against Rafael's ribs, thirty-one pages rose and fell with her breath, keeping time, keeping faith, waiting for London.
