## 19. Hessler's Fall

The room they put us in at Calais had once been a customs office and still smelled of it—tar, damp paper, the ghost of a hundred declarations of goods that had never mattered to anyone. A single window, barred, looked out on the inner harbor where lighters sat low with their loads of shell cases going back out to feed the guns. Every few minutes the glass shivered. The bombardment inland had been audible since dawn, a long rolling argument of thunder that never quite resolved.

Rafael sat beside me with her hands folded in her lap like a woman waiting in a church porch. She had not slept. Neither of us had, not properly, not since the road—the ditches, the dead mule we'd walked around for half a mile because there was no other way past, the military policeman at the checkpoint who had looked at our papers and then at us for one second too long before waving us through into the arms of the next man down the line. Her uniform lining hung wrong where she'd cut the stitches out and given the pages up. I kept thinking about that: her fingers working the thread loose in some barn or cellar while I stood watch, the ledger coming out of the cloth the way a child comes into the world, wet and wrinkled and screamingly alive.

The pages lay on the table between us now, squared up by an intelligence officer named Major Calloway who handled them as though they might go off. Which, in a sense, was true.

"Two hundred and six names," Calloway said. He said it without inflection, which frightened me more than shouting would have. Shouting I could read. This flatness could mean anything—a man convinced, or a man composing his report to Hessler's friends. "You'll understand my difficulty, Odell. Any two clerks with a typewriter can produce a document like this. The war has produced forgers of every grade. What I require is corroboration from sources you did not touch."

"Then verify against the pay records," Rafael said. Her voice was steady. I marveled at that even now, remembering it. "Bureau records at Rouen, the pension ledgers, the chaplain's burial returns. If a man is dead and drawing pay, someone is pocketing the difference or hiding the loss. Either way, the arithmetic will show it."

"I know how arithmetic works, Sister Yarrow."

"Do you?" And there it was, the crack in her steadiness, the thing underneath. "Because Colonel Hessler's arithmetic says two hundred and six men are alive who are not, and the board of inquiry believed him, and the mothers of those men are still waiting for letters that were never written. Forgive me if I've lost faith in what people know."

Calloway regarded her for a long moment. Then he did something unexpected: he pulled a chair over and sat down across from us instead of standing over us, and the room changed shape around that small act. Interrogation became something else. Conversation, maybe. Or confession.

"My brother died at Loos," he said. "1915. We got a telegram in nine days. My mother got a letter in six weeks, written by his commanding officer, describing a death so gentle it sounded invented. It was invented. I found the battalion diary later. He took four hours to die of wounds that no one dressed." He turned a page of the ledger with one finger. "So you will forgive me if I don't dismiss this. But you must also forgive me if I don't act on it until it's iron. There are men above me whose careers are built on Colonel Hessler's casualty figures. If I move too soon, this—" he tapped the table— "goes into a drawer, and you two go into a cell, and the drawer stays shut for fifty years."

"And if the bombardment reaches the port?" I asked. "Those records at Rouen won't survive being needed twice."

"The bombardment," Calloway said, "is precisely why I have authority to act quickly. Half the staff officers have already run for Boulogne. Nobody left here to protect." He stood, gathered three of the ledger's pages—carefully, I noticed, leaving us most of them—and paused at the door. "Stay here. Don't leave the building. If this goes well, you'll be bored. If it goes badly, you'll wish you'd run when you could."

He went out. The door closed. The window shivered again, farther off this time, or nearer—it had become impossible to tell.

* * *

We waited six hours. I want to record that honestly, because in memory it compresses to nothing, but at the time it was six hours of sitting in a customs office while the war came toward us, and Rafael teaching me a game she'd played in the ward with fevered men—naming rivers, naming hills, anything alphabetical, anything to keep the mind moving forward when the body couldn't. Somme. Scheldt. Scarpe. I gave her the census districts of Lincolnshire in return; I had carried them in my head for three years and had never once thought them useful for anything but tedium. She laughed at Kesteven. Actually laughed, short and cracked, and then pressed her hand flat against the table as though the laugh had cost her something and she needed it back.

"Marta should be laughing," she said, after. "Not me."

I had no answer for that. There wasn't one. Marta had held the door and the door had burned and there was no arithmetic in any ledger anywhere that made that come out level. All I could say was what I'd been saying since the workshop: "She knew what she was doing. She chose it."

"That's worse, not better." Rafael looked at the barred window. "If she hadn't chosen it, it would just be waste. Because she chose it, it's a debt. I keep accounts, Bram. You know that better than anyone. And this account doesn't close."

The light moved across the floorboards. A corporal brought tea once, lukewarm, in enamel mugs, and would not meet our eyes. Twice runners went past the door at a jog. Once the whole building shook—not the distant bombardment but something closer, a magazine somewhere along the quay, and the tea in my mug rang like a struck bell.

It was nearly dark when Calloway came back.

I knew before he spoke. Not from his face—he'd arranged it into professional blankness—but from his hands. They weren't steady. A man carrying bad news has steady hands; he's rehearsed. A man carrying news that has knocked him sideways does not.

"Sit down," he said, and then realized it was his own office and we were already sitting, and for a moment the absurdity of it hung in the air, and I think that small human fumble did more to convince me of him than everything that followed.

He laid documents on the table beside our ledger pages. Pay ledgers. Muster abstracts. A typed summary in several hands, initialed and dated.

"Bureau records at Rouen confirm forty-one of the names immediately. Men deceased per burial returns, carried on strength for periods ranging from three weeks to seven months. Pay drawn against them throughout. The signature authorizing continued carriage on the rolls is consistent across all forty-one files." He paused. "Colonel A. Hessler."

The name landed in the room like a dropped instrument.

"There's more. A clerk at Rouen—an acting sergeant, name of Finch—had flagged eleven of these himself last autumn. His queries were returned unanswered with instructions to cease correspondence on the matter. He kept copies." Calloway allowed himself, briefly, the ghost of something that was not quite a smile. "Your colonel has been careless with quiet people. Quiet people keep records too."

"He'll say forgery," I heard myself say. "Ours, yours, Finch's. He'll say the whole trail was manufactured."

"He already has." Calloway sat down heavily. "He arrived at GHQ annex two hours ago, ahead of the bombardment, and filed a complaint naming you both as enemy agents attempting to destabilize the Fifth Army's command through fabricated documentation. It was a reasonable move. It would have worked a year ago." He looked up at us, and now I saw what his hands had told me: the man was angry, deeply and coldly angry, and the anger had somewhere to go. "But he made two errors. He demanded your immediate arrest and execution of sentence under field regulations—execution, before verification, in writing. And he signed the order with the same hand that signs his casualty returns. I've spent the afternoon comparing them under a glass. He writes his H's like a man crossing out something he regrets. Every time."

Rafael reached across the table and touched the edge of the ledger, not picking it up, just resting her fingers on it, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal.

"So it's done?"

"It's done. Provost officers took him twenty minutes ago at the annex. He didn't resist. That's the worst part, apparently—he watched them come up the steps as if he'd been expecting them for years, and asked whether they'd mind if he finished his tea." Calloway rubbed his face. "There's a board convening at Étaples within the week. Restoration of the omitted names to the regimental rolls has been ordered tonight—my signature, countersigned by the DAAAG, effective immediately. Two hundred and six. Next of kin notification through the usual channels, with priority given to pension claims." He hesitated, and then, more quietly: "The letters will be late. Some of them very late. I can't fix that."

"No," Rafael said. "You can't. But the families will know their sons died, and how, and where they're buried. After eighteen months of silence, that's not nothing. That's everything. That's the whole war, to some of them."

The bombardment chose that moment to speak again, close enough now that dust sifted from the ceiling beams, and outside a whistle blew and boots ran along the quay. The port was filling, I realized. Filling with whatever could be carried out ahead of the German guns—guns, ambulances, crates of records, men. Always men.

"You should know," Calloway added, gathering his papers, "that your part in this will be stated in the board's report. Formally. Both of you." He almost smiled. "Hessler complained you were agents of chaos. The report will describe you rather differently. I'm told Sister Yarrow may even be mentioned in dispatches, if the phrase 'mentioned in dispatches' can stretch to cover what you actually did."

"What about Bram?" Rafael asked.

"Odell acted under duress and beyond his station and displayed initiative unbecoming a census clerk." Now Calloway did smile, briefly, tiredly. "Which is to say, they'll probably offer him a commission. They always do. It's cheaper than thanking people properly."

After he left, neither of us spoke for a long time. The lamp hissed. Outside, the sky over the harbor glowed orange where something burned inland, and the water in the basin caught the color and held it, so that it seemed the whole port floated on fire.

"It's over," I said finally. And then, hearing how wrong it sounded: "That part of it."

Rafael nodded slowly. She was looking at the ledger, all its pages returned to the table, two hundred and six names lying there in her careful hand, and her expression was not triumph. It was not relief either, exactly—or it was relief the way rain is relief in a drought, arriving after everything it came too late to save.

"I keep thinking about Private Ellery," she said. "Number sixty-one. He used to sing while I changed his dressings. Badly. Music-hall songs, all the verses, including the ones the sergeant major hated. When I wrote his name in the ledger I couldn't remember his first name. Only Ellery. I still can't." Her voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "Somewhere in England there's a woman who knows his first name, and his favorite song, and which verses scandalized the sergeant major. And she's been writing to the War Office for a year, asking where her son is, and getting answers that aren't answers. Tomorrow she gets a letter with the truth in it. And the truth is that he died in March of last year and nobody told her, and the letter will be signed by some staff officer she's never met, expressing regret."

"That's vindication?" I asked.

"No," she said. "Vindication would be him walking through the door singing. This is just... the record being set straight. It matters. God help me, it matters, I've bled for it. But don't ever mistake it for victory, Bram. Victory would have been doing this before they died. Everything after is only accounting."

I understood her. I think I understood her better in that moment than I ever had—better than in the workshop, better than on the road. She had not fought to win anything. She had fought so that the losing, at least, would be written down truly.

The door opened again. A runner, breathless, saluting Calloway through the doorway behind us: shells falling on the Étaples road, the annex being evacuated, movement orders for everyone in the port by morning. The war, closing in on the paperwork, on the truth, on the two of us sitting in a customs office with a ledger full of the dead.

And beneath the runner's voice, beneath the thunder and the whistles, I heard Rafael begin—very quietly, half to herself, the way she must have done a hundred times alone in that lamplit workshop—to read the names. Ellery. And the next one. And the next. Not mourning them now. Delivering them. Carrying each one across the threshold of the world's attention, two hundred and six times, until the bombardment swallowed even her voice—

—and even then I saw her lips still moving, keeping count, because the account, as she had said, was not yet closed.
