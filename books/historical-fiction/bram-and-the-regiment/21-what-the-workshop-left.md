## 21. What the Workshop Left

The convalescent home at Étaples had once been a casino, and the sea still showed through the tall windows where the roulette tables had stood. Bram Odell had been given a bed by the glass, and for six weeks his whole world was the width of a blanket, the rhythm of bandage changes, and the grey water that came in and went out and came in again, indifferent to armistices.

The wound was in his left side — a splinter of shell casing from the bombardment at Calais that had missed his heart by the width of a paybook. The surgeons said he was fortunate. Bram, who had spent three years among ledgers and knew precisely what fortune weighed, thought: *fortunate men do not get to lie still for six weeks with nothing to do but count the names they failed to save.*

Two hundred and six names. That was the number when the workshop burned. Some of those pages were ash now. Some were sewn into the lining of Rafael Yarrow's uniform, riding against her ribs through checkpoints and interrogations and a winter that would not end. And some — forty-one pages, as it turned out, plus three loose sheets Marta had slipped into a biscuit tin — survived because Rafael never trusted any single hiding place, even her own body.

He learned this on a Tuesday in March, when she walked into the ward carrying a parcel wrapped in brown paper and string, and the light behind her made him think, absurdly, of lamplight in a carpentry workshop.

"You're thinner," she said.

"You're not."

"Matron feeds me." She set the parcel on his blanket like it might go off. "I need your hands."

"My hands are fine."

"I know." She sat down on the chair beside the bed, and only then did he see how tired she was — tired in the way of someone who has been carrying something heavy for so long that setting it down feels more dangerous than holding it. "It's your handwriting I need. A clerk's hand. Legible, unremarkable, boring enough to pass an inspection by anyone." She smiled, and it was the old smile from the workshop, wry and guarded and a little sad. "I'm going to build a book, Bram. Out of what we have left. And I can't do it alone."

* * *

They started in April, when he could sit up for hours without the wound weeping, in a corner of the casino's old card room that Matron — a brisk Yorkshirewoman who had lost two sons at Passchendaele and asked no questions she didn't want answered — let them use in exchange for Bram reading the daily papers aloud to the ward each evening.

The surviving material lay before them on a card table like the ruins of a cathedral: forty-one ledger pages, water-stained where the workshop's roof had leaked before it burned; three loose sheets; the copied pages Rafael had smuggled out separately during the autumn, hidden in the lining of a medical text; and one folder of Hessler's own requisitions, verified by the intelligence officer at Calais and stamped with the board of inquiry's seal. Evidence, all of it. But evidence was not what Rafael wanted.

"Evidence tells you what happened," she said, squaring the pages against the table's edge with the precision of a woman dressing wounds. "It doesn't tell you who. Private Thomas Ellery, died 9 October 1917, omitted from muster." She tapped the line. "That is what the army wrote, when it wrote anything at all. It is not what his mother needs."

"What does his mother need?"

"To know that he laughed. That he owed someone half a franc. That he talked about his father's orchard while you stitched his arm. That he existed, in a place no dispatch ever reached." She looked up. "We met them, Bram. All of them, most of them. You held their service books. I held their hands. Between us there must be something worth writing down."

And there was. Not everything — never everything, the gaps were terrible and permanent — but fragments. Bram discovered he remembered more than he had thought. Three years in the records office meant three years of handling the regiment's paper ghosts: letters home that passed under censor's stamps, next-of-kin forms filled out in pencil, the small confessions men made to a clerk because a clerk was safe. He remembered Ellery now — not the ledger entry, the man. A Kentish boy who'd grown cherries and complained that army jam was an insult to fruit.

"He told me the cherries near Sittingbourne were better than anywhere in England," Bram said slowly, "and that when he got home he was going to eat so many his mother would bar the pantry door. He made me write it down as his official statement of intent upon demobilisation." His throat closed. "I stamped it. Routine correspondence. I don't even remember which box I put it in."

Rafael wrote it down anyway. *Private Thomas Ellery. Grew cherries. Meant to go home.*

"That," she said quietly, "is the book."

* * *

They worked through spring. The method evolved the way methods do in wartime and after: rough and then refined, improvised and then ritual. For every name in the surviving ledger, they searched the fragments for a story. Where they had none — where the fire had taken the page and memory offered only a number and a date — Rafael insisted they leave the space honest and blank rather than invent.

"We will not fabricate," she said, the night he suggested filling a gap with plausible detail drawn from another soldier's file. "These men were erased once by arithmetic. If we guess at them, we erase them again — we replace a person with a convenient fiction. Blank is true. Blank says: *there was a man here, and the record failed him, and we refuse to pretend otherwise.*"

So the blanks stayed, ruled off cleanly, each one a small accusation. But the filled entries grew, page by page, and it was strange — Bram felt it happening to him — how much a name could summon if you let it. He wrote in his best clerk's hand, the unremarkable hand Rafael had wanted, and found that the act of writing slowed his mind to the speed of grief, and that grief at that speed was bearable, almost companionable, the way a long march becomes bearable after the tenth mile.

Rafael handled the medical details. She had been there for the deaths the ledger recorded — she had been the last hand on nearly every one of them — and she gave each account the same unsentimental mercy she had given the men. *Died quietly, holding his mate's hand. Died badly, and deserved better. Died calling for his brother, who was already gone and listed nowhere.* She never flinched writing it, but Bram noticed she slept badly on the nights they worked through the worst entries, and he began, without discussion, to arrange the order of the pages so that no day ended on a death without a story of life following it. A joke. A cherry orchard. A girl named Violet in Boulogne. She noticed what he was doing around the third week, looked at the sequence of pages, looked at him, and said nothing — which was its own acknowledgment, and the closest thing to tenderness either of them had words for.

Marta's page they wrote together, though her name was not among the two hundred and six. She had died holding the workshop door, and no ledger anywhere had claimed her. Bram argued she belonged at the front of the book, not the back.

"She wasn't omitted by Hessler's arithmetic," he said. "She chose—"

"She was erased by it all the same." Rafael's voice was flat, the voice she used when flatness was all that stood between her and falling. "She died for paper, Bram. For other people's names. If the book doesn't hold her, then everything she did was for nothing." She took a fresh sheet. "Front of the book. First entry. Marta Kowalczyk, who kept our secrets and paid for mine."

He did not correct her — *paid for ours* — because some debts are not worth arguing over, only carrying.

* * *

In May the families began to write.

The board of inquiry had done its slow, shabby work through the winter; the restored names had gone onto the official rolls in February, corrected in red ink by clerks in London who had never seen Ypres. The War Office sent form letters. The pension office sent smaller, stingier ones. But word travels strangely along the lines of grief, and somewhere — Bram never learned how — mothers and widows heard that a nurse and a census clerk were making a different kind of record, and letters started arriving at Étaples addressed to *Miss R. Yarrow, c/o the Convalescent Home*, in handwriting that shook or didn't shake, on paper that smelled of kitchens.

The first one Bram opened because Rafael's hands were full of pages. It was from an Mrs. Ellery, of Sittingbourne.

*Dear Miss,* she had written. *They tell me now my Tom died in October '17 and it was not put in the proper place, and I am to be angry, they say, and entitled to what is owed. But nobody can tell me if he went quick. Nobody can tell me if he was buried proper or just somewhere. If you knew him — the paper says you may have known him — I would be grateful for anything at all. Even small things. Especially small things.*

Bram read it twice and then carried it to the card room and set it in front of Rafael without a word. She read it, and closed her eyes, and for a long moment the room was very quiet.

"The cherries," she said finally.

"The cherries." He pulled the page toward himself. *Private Thomas Ellery. Grew cherries near Sittingbourne, the best in England — his word, sworn to a clerk and stamped as official correspondence. Meant to eat so many his mother would bar the pantry door.*

"Copy it," Rafael said. "Copy it exactly. And send it to her tonight."

The reply Mrs. Ellery sent a fortnight later Bram kept folded in his paybook for the rest of his life, though he never spoke of it. *You cannot know,* she wrote, *what it is to be told a thing like that. He was seven years old the last time he stole from my pantry. I had forgot. Thank God you remembered for me.*

After that the letters came faster, and the work changed shape. They were no longer building a book out of what remained; they were building it outward, entry by entry, each returned letter adding a line, a correction, a photograph sometimes — small stiff portraits of men in uniforms too big for them, sent on loan and always with instructions for return. Rafael treated every photograph like the original ledger itself, wrapped in tissue, catalogued, returned within the month with a copy enclosed and a paragraph of thanks in Bram's hand.

"It's not a ledger anymore," he said one evening in June, looking at the growing stack. "A ledger is a list. This is—" He stopped, because the word that came was *memorial*, and memorial meant stone, and stone meant finished, and nothing about this felt finished.

"A book," Rafael said simply. "Books aren't finished. They're read."

* * *

His own brother's page he left until last.

Wilfred Odell, omitted, muster of March 1917. Bram had found the name in Rafael's ledger a year before, in the workshop, in the hour when private grief became shared purpose, and he had not been able to write a single word about Wil since. The blank ruled space waited at the bottom of the penultimate page like an open grave.

Rafel never rushed him. She simply left that page on top of the stack each morning, face up, and said nothing.

In July, on an evening when the sea through the tall windows had gone gold and the ward behind them smelled of carbolic and cut grass, Bram picked up his pen.

"I was eleven when he taught me to swear," he said. He hadn't meant to speak; the words came anyway, the way they came to the men who'd talked to a clerk because a clerk was safe. "Behind the tithe barn, with diagrams. He was very thorough. Father thrashed him for it and Wil said it was worth it on educational grounds." The pen hovered. "He joined up because I was too young and somebody in the family ought to. That's the whole of it. No grand cause. He just decided it was his turn, because I was his, and then the paperwork lost him."

"And now?"

"Now—" Bram wrote, slowly, in his unremarkable clerk's hand: *Corporal Wilfred Odell. Taught his younger brother everything worth knowing, including several things their father disapproved of. Joined up so his brother would not have to. The record lost him for eight years. This book is where he is found.*

Rafael read it when the ink dried. She did not say it was good. She put her hand flat on the page for a moment, the way she used to check a pulse, and turned to the final blank, and said, "Now we decide what happens to it."

* * *

They argued about that for a week — gently, in the way of two people long past the arguing-that-damages stage. Rafael wanted copies in three places, no originals together, redundancy learned the hard way. Bram wanted the book accessible, public, *read*. What they settled on, in the end, was both: a press copy to be made in London by a printer Rafael had found through the inquiry board, the original pages bound but housed where families could come and sit with them, and a second fair copy — Bram's whole hand, four months of evenings — to travel wherever it was needed, to boards and archives and, if it came to it, courtrooms.

"There will be questions," Rafael warned, on their last working night at Étaples. Matron had found Bram a discharge posting in London, a records clerkship with a veterans' bureau, and Rafael had accepted a position preparing testimony files for the pensions tribunal. The book would go with them, but the easy days were over. "Hessler's friends are not all arrested. Some men signed the same arithmetic he did and were never questioned. Every family we contact, every letter we answer, makes us easier to find."

"I've been easy to find since Calais," Bram said. "I find I mind less than I expected."

She looked at him across the card table, at the stacks of pages between them, at the year of it — the workshop, the burning, the road, the port, the winter — and something in her face eased that he had not seen ease since he'd first tracked her by lamplight.

"Do you regret it?" she asked. "Any of it? You were a census taker. You counted people who were alive and filed the numbers. It was safe."

Bram considered the question honestly, because she had earned honesty and he had little else of value to give. He thought of the ledger, of the two hundred and six, of Marta at the door, of the chafed seam against Rafael's ribs, of Mrs. Ellery's shaking hand writing *especially small things*.

"A census counts the living," he said at last. "This—" he touched the bound pages, the whole improbable weight of them, "—this is the rest of the count. Somebody had to finish it." He managed the smile she'd first seen in the workshop, the one that arrived only at the edge of disaster. "Besides. You needed a boring hand."

"I needed more than that."

The words hung in the gold light between them, unadorned, unretreated-from, and Bram felt his heart do something the shell fragment had failed to accomplish. Before either of them could decide whether to be brave about it, a knock came at the card room door, and a hospital runner stood there with the evening post and an expression of professional neutrality.

"Parcel for Miss Yarrow. Signed-for."

Rafael untied the string with steady fingers. Inside, wrapped in a plain linen cloth, was a single sheet of old paper — not one of theirs, older, foxed at the edges — and a covering note in a solicitor's hand. Bram read over her shoulder.

*Madam, — I am instructed by the estate of the late Quartermaster-Sergeant Duval to forward the enclosed, which he directed should reach you upon settling of his affairs. He left no further message except the words written on the reverse of the document itself.*

Rafael turned the old paper over. In a quartermaster's careful script, the hand of a man who had spent a war counting what others wasted:

*Found this among Hessler's overflow stores when clearing the annex. It is a second casualty schedule, earlier than yours, from 1916. There are more names on it than your book holds. I did not burn it. Perhaps you will know why.*

Rafael spread the page on the card table. Column after column, dates from a winter before the workshop, before the ledger — names, ranks, omissions, and at the foot, in a signature Bram recognized with a cold that had nothing to do with the sea wind, Hessler's initials approving every falsified line.

One hundred and twelve additional names. None of them in the book. None of them yet found.

Rafael looked up at him, and Bram saw it in her face before she said a word: the work was not ending. It had only been waiting.

"Get your coat," she said. "We'll need more pages."
