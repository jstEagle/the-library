## 22. Carried Forward

The reading room of the Imperial War Museum smelled of dust and floor wax and the particular sweetness of old paper left too long in sunlight. Rafael Yarrow sat at a long oak table beneath a green-shaded lamp, and in front of her, wrapped in brown paper and string, lay the ledger.

She had carried it for three years now — first against her heart, sewn into her uniform lining, then across the Channel in a kit bag, then through two years of hearings and waiting rooms and corridors where men with kind eyes asked her to repeat herself. The string was new. She had tied it that morning at her lodgings in Pimlico, and her hands had been steady, which surprised her. She had expected to shake.

The archivist, a thin man named Prewitt with ink stains on his cuffs like old bruises, stood waiting beside her chair. "Whenever you're ready, Miss Halloran," he said. She had given them the name Yarrow still — it was how the testimony read, how the newspapers had printed it, how the families knew her — but on the deed of gift she had written her true name, because the ledger deserved to be filed under something honest. "Take whatever time you need."

"I've taken three years," she said. "I don't think another ten minutes will hurt it."

She untied the string.

The cover came away soft as cloth, worn to suede at the corners where her thumbs had held it. Two hundred and six names in her own hand, entered in the order they had come to her: Private Albert Fenwick, 14 October 1917. Corporal Thomas Merrick. Lance-Sergeant Wilfred Odell.

Wilfred Odell. Bram had found his brother's name in this book before he had ever found her face, and she remembered the sound he made — not a cry, nothing so dramatic, just a small exhalation, like a man setting down something heavy after carrying it farther than he should have. He had traced the letters once with one finger and then taken off his spectacles and cleaned them very thoroughly, and neither of them had said anything at all, and it had been the most complete conversation of her life.

"Miss Halloran?" Prewitt said gently.

"I'm ready."

* * *

They processed the deposit with forms in triplicate, because even grief required bureaucracy, and perhaps especially grief did. Rafael signed where the crosses indicated. Prewitt read out the catalogue entry for her approval: *Ledger of casualties omitted from official regimental returns, 1917–1918, compiled by R. Yarrow, Queen Alexandra's Imperial Military Nursing Service, with supplementary material by B. Odell. Deposited by the compiler, March 1920.*

"With supplementary material," she repeated.

"The board felt the provenance should reflect both hands." Prewitt's voice went carefully neutral. "Mr. Odell's copies of the dispatch covers were quite instrumental to the inquiry."

Bram's copies. Forty-three forged cover sheets, each one a small crime, each one the reason the ledger had been believed when it mattered — because an intelligence officer in Calais had turned them over and seen regimental stamps and clerical neatness and thought, *this is real, someone official kept this*, before understanding that the most official thing about it was its truth. Bram had always been modest about the forgery. *Anyone could have done it,* he said. *It was only knowing which boxes to tick.* But Rafael had watched him work by lamplight with shells falling close enough to sift ash onto his paper, ticking his boxes, and she knew that anyone could not have done it. Anyone would have run.

"He'll want to see the catalogue card," she said. "He collects them now. Little bits of paper that prove things happened."

"I'll have a duplicate made."

She thanked him and gathered her gloves, and then, because there was no way to say it plainly and she had learned that plain things sometimes needed saying anyway, she added: "There were mothers who waited four years because a colonel rounded his figures down. When they come here — the sisters, the sons, whoever is left — will they be able to see it? All of it?"

Prewitt looked at her over his spectacles. "Reading room hours are nine until five, madam, six on Saturdays. There is no fee. There never will be."

"No fee," she said. "Good." She had spent enough of her life watching institutions charge people for what was owed them.

* * *

Outside, London was doing its loud, unfinished spring. Scaffolding everywhere, hoardings painted with cheerful lies about the future, buses grinding past a shopfront whose windows still bore the ghost-words of a pre-war glazier. Men sold matches on corners; other men hurried past them with the particular gait of those who had learned not to look. Rafael walked from the museum toward the river without any destination in mind, which was itself a luxury she was still growing accustomed to — walking somewhere for no reason, with no list of names in her head, no ward awaiting her, no clock except the ordinary ones.

For years she had measured everything by weight. It began at the dressing station near Ypres, in the autumn the omissions started, when the evacuation order came down and every nurse packed her kit and faced the same arithmetic: what can be carried. A dressing station could burn in eleven minutes; she had timed it once, professionally, in her head, the way some people counted sheep. So she weighed. Morphine tablets against bandages. Her mother's photograph against a second pair of stockings. And then, folded into the back of her Bible, forty pages of names that weighed almost nothing at all and more than everything else combined.

That arithmetic had governed her ever since. What can be carried. She had carried Marta's death out of a burning workshop along with the ledger, though Marta's name was not in it — Marta had died holding a door so a stranger could slip into the dark with a bundle of paper against her ribs, and there was no line in any register for that either, not properly, not the way it deserved. Rafael had written it in herself, on the last blank leaf, in the margin where the formal entries ended: *Marta Kessler, friend, who held the door.* Prewitt had raised his eyebrows at that page today and said nothing, and she loved him a little for it.

What can be carried. She had carried the chafed skin where the sewn pages rubbed her raw from Ypres to the coast. She had carried Bram, half-conscious, through the last mile to Calais with his blood soaking through her skirt, and he had carried her through the interrogation afterward, when her voice failed and his did not. They had carried two hundred and six names into a hearing room in January 1919 and read them aloud, all of them, taking turns when their voices cracked, until the chairman stopped checking his watch and the stenographer stopped writing and simply listened, and Colonel Hessler sat very straight and very grey at the far table with his arithmetic finally, irrevocably wrong.

And now she had carried it here, and set it down, and the strange thing — the thing she would try to explain to Bram tonight and probably fail — was that setting it down did not feel like loss. It felt like the moment at the end of a long march when you finally lower the pack and discover your spine still works.

Some things you carry forever. But a record wants to be public. That was the whole point of a record; she understood it now, after three years. Grief kept private curdles. Grief given a shelf, a catalogue number, a reading-room lamp — grief given *witnesses* — becomes something else. Becomes memory with a door open.

She reached the Embankment and stood a while watching the water move. Somewhere behind her a church bell counted five, and she thought: *in the reading room right now, the ledger is lying under its lamp, and tomorrow morning at nine o'clock the doors open, and anyone may come.*

* * *

Bram was waiting at the lodgings, sitting in the window chair with his stick hooked over the armrest, correcting proofs. He corrected proofs now for a small publisher that specialized in local histories — parish registers, regimental associations, the humble paperwork of remembrance — and he was good at it, meticulous and unshowy, a man who had discovered late in life that his talent was precisely the one the world had mocked him for: noticing what others missed.

"You've done it, then," he said, looking up. His eyes went to her empty hands, and something moved through his face — relief and bereavement in equal measure, the twins that attended every ending worth having.

"It's catalogued. *With supplementary material by B. Odell.*"

"Good Lord." He took off his spectacles and cleaned them, the old ritual, buying time. "They put my name on it."

"They put your name on it because your name belongs on it." She hung her coat. "Forty-three forged documents, Mr. Odell. You're lucky they didn't put your name on a warrant instead."

"A warrant would have been tidier. Less paperwork for everyone." He set the proofs aside and looked at her properly, and she saw him arrive at the question underneath the day. "How was it? Actually?"

"Quiet," she said, and found that it was true. "Quieter than I'd feared. I kept waiting for the room to argue with me, and it didn't. It was only a room, and the book was only a book, and both of them were perfectly willing to hold what I put down." She sat across from him. "I keep thinking I should feel emptied out. I don't. I feel — delivered."

"Like a parcel."

"Don't be flippant."

"I'm not." He leaned forward, wincing slightly at his hip — the wound at Calais still spoke to him in cold weather, and would forever. "I mean it exactly. You were a parcel marked fragile and urgent, and you've been in transit for three years, and today somebody signed for you. That's not being emptied. That's arriving."

Rafael laughed — a short, surprised sound — and then, because the laugh had opened something, covered her mouth with her hand. Bram watched her with the mild patience he brought to everything, the patience of a man who had once asked one question too many in a records office and refused, ever afterward, to ask fewer.

"There's something else," she said. "Before I handed it over, I took one page out."

His eyebrows rose. "Stolen evidence, Miss Halloran?"

"Reproduced evidence. Copied in fair hand, witnessed by the archivist, noted in the file." She reached into her pocket and drew out a single sheet, laid flat these three years inside the cover of her Bible, where her mother's photograph used to live. "But it isn't my hand."

Bram went still.

It was one of his copying pages from the workshop, from the last desperate sprint — the three days of light, Marta returned and running the press, shells falling closer each night, all of them writing until their fingers cramped. His copy of the ledger's opening entries. And there, third from the top, in his careful clerk's script, slightly blurred where a drop of lamp oil had fallen and been blotted:

*Lance-Sergeant Wilfred Odell, aged 29, of Trowbridge. Killed 3 June 1917. Mother notified: no. Pension: unpaid.*

"I couldn't give them that one," Rafael said. "Everything else is theirs. The archive, the inquiry, the historians who'll come poking about in fifty years. But that name — you wrote it, Bram. You found your own brother in my book and then you sat down and copied his name out letter by letter so that no fire could ever take it twice. I've carried it since the workshop. I'm going to carry it the rest of my life. I hope that's—" She stopped. Started again, plainer. "I hope you don't mind that I kept it."

Bram looked at the page for a long time. The lamp caught the wetness in his eyes before it caught the ink.

"When we build the memorial book," he said at last — the project they had begun in the winter, pairing each of the two hundred and six names with whatever story could be gathered: a letter home, a comrade's recollection, a photograph borrowed from a mantelpiece — "when it's finished, I thought we might print this page as the frontispiece. Wilfred first. Alphabetical would put Ackroyd ahead of him, but alphabetical has already had its say, hasn't it. It's had rather too much to say." He touched the edge of the paper, not the name. "Keep it. Carry it. And when we're both — much later, I mean, decades, when we're tiresome old people boring the neighbors — then it goes in the front of the book, and after us it goes wherever the book goes."

"Together, then," Rafael said. "Not archived. Carried."

"Together," he agreed. "Not archived."

She rose and put the page back inside her Bible, back where her mother's photograph had kept watch through the war, and noticed that her hands were steady still, and understood at last why. For years she had measured her world in what could be carried when evacuation orders came — morphine against bandages, photograph against stockings, names against fire. She had thought the measuring was a wound. It was not. It was a discipline, and it had taught her the only inventory that ever mattered: not what a person owns, but what a person refuses to let burn.

The lamplight fell across the table where the proofs lay waiting, and outside a bus rattled past toward some corner of the city that was rebuilding itself brick by brick. Somewhere across town, in a quiet room with green-shaded lamps, two hundred and six names slept in the open air, free to any soul who walked in off the street at nine o'clock in the morning. And here, against the paper of her Bible, one name traveled on in the hand of the man who loved its bearer's brother, and would travel on after them both, and after everyone who had known why it mattered.

Rafael sat down again across from Bram, and he pushed the proofs aside to make room for the tea things, and the evening settled around them the way evenings do when nothing is being lost.

"Tell me about Ackroyd," she said. "For the memorial book. We start at the beginning."

Bram smiled, took up his pencil, and opened the folder of stories they had gathered — and the first page, as it happened, already bore a name.
