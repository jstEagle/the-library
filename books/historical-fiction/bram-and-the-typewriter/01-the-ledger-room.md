## 1. The Ledger Room

The ledgers smelled of salt rot and lamp oil, and by the end of each day Tamsin Eriksen could no longer tell which smell came from the books and which from his own hands.

He sat at the third desk from the window in the Pearlline Trading Company's ledger room, a position chosen not by himself but by the room's own quiet arithmetic: close enough to the light to be useful, far enough from the supervisor's door to be forgotten. Sixteen years old, second-year indenture, five more to run. The paper that owned him lived upstairs in the company archive, in a drawer marked with his father's name, because a boy's debt was always filed under the man who'd made it.

*Eriksen, T. — diver's son, bond of 40 pounds, term of seven years, renewable at company discretion.*

Renewable at company discretion. He had read those words so many times they had stopped being language and become weather.

"Manifests," Mr. Quill had said that morning, dropping the stack on the desk without breaking stride. "The *Selene*, the *Cormorant*, and the *Gullwing*. Clean copies only. I won't look at your blots."

Quill was the second clerk, a thin man with ink-stained cuffs and a talent for making cruelty sound like bookkeeping. He never shouted. Shouting was for men who lacked systems.

Tamsin dipped his pen and began.

Copy work was supposed to be beneath thinking, and that was precisely its danger. The manifests told stories if you let them. He was three lines into the *Selene*'s cargo when he noticed the weights didn't add. Shell listed at one figure, shell paid out at another, and the difference — eleven pounds' worth of pearl shell — simply vanished between columns, the way a diver vanished between swells. Nobody above stairs would have missed it. Nobody below stairs was permitted to see it.

He copied the figures exactly as written. That was the first rule of the ledger room: you did not correct, you did not question, and above all you did not *notice*. Noticing was a disease. It started small — an arithmetic error here, a name entered twice there — and if the supervisors ever caught it in your eyes, they transferred you dockside, where men who noticed things got their fingers caught in winches.

So he kept his head down and his eyes soft and let his mind work behind them like a man rowing under a tarpaulin.

It was a good arrangement, or had been for two years. His hands were quick and his memory quicker; he could hold a page of figures in his head long enough to recopy it after Quill spilled tea on the original, which had happened twice, both times blamed on Tamsin. He had learned to write in the company's style — narrow, unlovely, uniform — so that nothing he produced looked like anything. Invisibility was a skill like any other. He practiced it the way other boys practiced diving.

The trouble with being invisible was that you saw everything.

* * *

At midmorning the door banged open and the room's temperature dropped by degrees.

Dane Vos did not work in the ledger room. Vos worked wherever the company needed him, which mostly meant wherever someone was behind on payments or ahead on questions. He wore a good coat and bad boots, and he moved through rooms the way tide moves through a wreck — patient, total, indifferent to what he displaced.

"Tamsin Eriksen." He said the whole name, the way you'd read it off a document. "Your father's boat came in short yesterday. Third short trip this quarter."

Tamsin kept his pen moving. "I don't set the catch, sir."

"No." Vos smiled at something internal. "You don't. But you're on his bond, aren't you? Family debt accrues to family. That's just paper." He leaned on the desk, and up close he smelled of tobacco and something faintly chemical, like the stuff they used to preserve specimens. "Tell your father the company is patient. Tell him patience has limits, and he knows where they're marked."

"Yes, sir."

Vos straightened, surveyed the room as if pricing it, and left. No one spoke until his footsteps reached the stair. Then the scratch of pens resumed, a little faster than before, the way birds go back to feeding after a hawk passes.

Quill drifted over and stood reading over Tamsin's shoulder — not the numbers, the *hand*. He did that sometimes. Checking.

"Neat enough," he said finally. "You know, boy, there are clerks in this building who'd give a year's wages for a hand like yours, and here it sits copying manifests." He said it almost kindly, which from Quill was the most dangerous register of all. "Mind you keep it boring."

"Yes, Mr. Quill."

"Boring hands stay attached." He went back to his desk.

Tamsin looked down at his fingers, grey to the second knuckle with cheap ink, and thought of the drawer upstairs where his life was folded into four pages. Forty pounds. Seven years. His father had signed it in a hospital corridor with a broken hand, the season after the storm took the family boat, and Tamsin had been too young then to understand that a signature was a kind of drowning too — slower, but just as certain, and no one dove down after you.

* * *

The drowning happened at half past two, though nobody in the ledger room learned of it until nearly five.

Tamsin heard the boats first — the irregular slap-slap of oars when men row hard without caring who hears. Then voices at the quay, rising, and a woman's cry that cut through the office walls like the smell of smoke. Pens slowed all around him. Quill stood, went to the window, and looked down at the harbor with an expression Tamsin would remember for years: mild distaste, as if something had been spilled.

"Which boat?" asked old Hessel from the corner desk.

"The *Cormorant*." Quill turned away from the window. "Boy named Anders. Seventeen, I think. Or nineteen. They'll want the entry amended."

They did want the entry amended. At ten past six a runner brought up a slate from the quay, and Quill handed it across to Tamsin with the air of a man delegating a chore.

"Diver lost, *Cormorant*, twenty-second. Cause: cramp. Enter it in the incident ledger, initial it, date it."

Tamsin took the slate. There was a name on it: **Joren Anders**. And there was a line item beneath it that made his stomach turn slowly over — *gear recovered, value 3 lb., charged against diver's account.*

The dead boy's family owed the company for the air hose he drowned in.

"He didn't cramp," said Hessel quietly, not looking up from his column. The old clerk had worked Pearlline for thirty years and had perfected the art of speaking to pages rather than people. "Cramp doesn't take a boy who's been in the water since he was six. Rope fouled. They've been running the *Cormorant*'s lines past safe length since spring — you can see it right there in the maintenance costs. Cut and cut and cut."

"Mr. Hessel," Quill said mildly, "the slate says cramp."

"The slate says cramp," Hessel agreed, and returned to his figures, and that was that. Thirty years of survival distilled into five words.

Tamsin sat with the incident ledger open before him. Around him the room scratched on, calm as a field of insects. Outside, the light was going gold over the harbor, and somewhere down there a mother was being handed her son's hose and an invoice in the same breath.

He wrote: *22nd — Joren Anders, diver, s.v. Cormorant. Lost by cramp.* His handwriting came out perfectly uniform. He despised it.

Then, because the form required it, he initialed the entry — a small *T.E.* in the corner, tidy and blameless, his name pressed against a lie the way a seal presses into wax. When he was done he understood something new about the paper that owned him. It wasn't just that he was owned. It was that ownership wanted his hands. It wanted every hand it touched to leave its mark on the record, so that when the day came to ask who had done all this, the answer would be a chorus: *we wrote what we were given.*

He closed the ledger and blew out his desk lamp, though the room still had light. It felt necessary to sit in the dark for exactly one moment, unseen.

* * *

He walked home along the seawall, and the town did what the town always did: smelled of fish and tar and the sweet rotten richness of the oyster beds at low tide. Divers' families lined the quay near the *Cormorant*'s berth, quiet in the way of people who had learned that grief held a price and crowds raised it. He found his sister Maren at the door of their house, arms crossed, watching the water.

"They're saying rope," she said, without greeting.

"They're writing cramp."

She looked at him then, sharply, the way she'd looked at him since they were children — Maren, two years older, who read people the way he read ledgers. "And which one did you write?"

His silence answered her, and she looked away, jaw tight, and let them both inside.

Their father was already home, sitting at the table with his hands around an empty cup, staring at nothing. Debt-short again. Vos's message had traveled faster than Tamsin had. Their father's knuckles were white around the cup, and there was something else in his face Tamsin had never quite seen before — not defeat, defeat he knew — something more like a man doing sums he doesn't want to finish.

"You heard about the Anders boy," their father said. It wasn't a question.

"Everyone heard."

"Aye." He turned the cup a quarter-turn, then another. "Best not to hear too much, Tam. In this town, hearing is expensive."

That night Tamsin lay in his bunk listening to the tide work the pilings and rehearsed the entry in his head. *Lost by cramp.* Four words, in his own hand, forever.

Five days later, Quill called him off the manifests at noon, and his voice had a new smoothness to it, the tone of a man about to spend money he'd found.

"Clear your desk, boy. Special work. Upstairs." He set a single envelope on the blotter, sealed, addressed in a hand Tamsin didn't recognize. "Not that. You'll type the copy. Mr. Harrow wants it in duplicate, on the machine, before the evening packet boat. And Tamsin—" Quill paused at the door, and his kindness returned, thin as paper. "What you read up there stays up there."

The typewriter. The company's Remington, kept in Harrow's office, the one machine in the building reserved for letters that mattered. Tamsin had cleaned its keys often enough to know every worn face of them, but he had never once been asked to feed it paper.

He picked up the envelope. It was heavy in a way paper shouldn't be, the way a secret is heavy, and somewhere below his ribs his heart began working like a man rowing against the tide.
