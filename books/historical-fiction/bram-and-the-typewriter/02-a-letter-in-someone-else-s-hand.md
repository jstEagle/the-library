## 2. A Letter in Someone Else's Hand

The summons came on a Thursday, when the rain had been falling for three days and the harbor smelled like a drowned thing.

"Tamsin." Mr. Quill's voice, from the doorway of the ledger room. Not loud. It never needed to be loud. "Upstairs. Bring your hands."

That was how he said it. *Bring your hands.* As if Tamsin might leave them at his desk among the manifests, as if they were company property — which, in a way, they were. Sixteen years old, indentured for seven more, and every finger accounted for on a contract his father had signed with an X because no one had bothered to teach him letters.

Tamsin rose. Around him, the other ledger boys kept their heads down over their columns. That was the first rule of the room: nothing happens here, no one sees anything, and if you are called upstairs, you go quietly and you come back the same.

He followed Quill through the counting hall, past the tall windows where the rain made grey rivers of the glass, up two flights to the office at the top of the company house. The office of Aldous Crane himself, factor of the Meridian Pearl & Trading Company, master of forty boats and six hundred souls.

Crane sat behind a desk that was always too clean. No papers on it except one folded sheet. No ink but one pot, stoppered. He was a small man who made you feel large and clumsy, which was its own kind of trick.

"Sit."

Tamsin sat. There was no chair offered; he stood by it instead, hands at his sides, the way he'd learned. His palms were sweating. He pressed them flat against his trousers.

"You can work a machine," Crane said. Not a question. "Quill tells me the boy can work a machine."

"I can copy," Tamsin said carefully. "Fair hand, both scripts."

"A typewriter." Crane's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "The Remington in the back room. Quill says you've been practicing after hours. On your own initiative."

The floor tilted slightly under Tamsin. Practicing after hours had been a transgression, then. He waited for the punishment.

Instead Crane slid the folded sheet across the desk.

"There is a letter to be typed. It will be typed tonight, on that machine, by you, and it will be sealed before it leaves this room. You will not read it."

Tamsin looked at the paper. Crane looked at Tamsin looking at the paper, and something moved behind his eyes — the particular patience of a man who has watched many boys learn what they are worth.

"You will not read it," Crane repeated, "because boys who read things they should not have read become men who talk about things they should not have talked about. Do you understand me?"

"Yes, sir."

"The letter goes out tomorrow morning. The recipient is Mr. Halloran, of Halloran & Sons, Salvage. He'll know what it means. Seal it with the black wax. Then go home and sleep well, because tomorrow you're just a boy who typed a letter."

*Just a boy.* Tamsin took the sheet. It was warm from the desk, or his hands were cold enough that everything felt warm.

"Go on, then. Quill will show you the machine."

* * *

The Remington lived in a windowless back room that smelled of machine oil and old sealing wax. It was older than Tamsin — older than his father, maybe — a battered upright with a carriage that stuck at the letter M unless you coaxed it, keys worn nearly blank from years of other people's words. Quill set it on the table, laid out a fresh ribbon, a box of black wax sticks, the company seal, and left without a word.

Tamsin unfolded Crane's draft.

He told himself he would not read it. He told himself the words would stay marks on paper, meaningless, safe. But reading was the one thing the company could not unteach him, and his eyes moved over the lines the way water moves downhill, without permission, without stopping.

It was addressed to Halloran. It concerned debts.

No. Not debts exactly. Debts were the company's weather; everyone in the port lived under them. This was something else. This was a list of divers' accounts — accounts held by Halloran's salvage concern as surety against loans — with figures beside each name, and instructions in Crane's neat, economical hand: *these balances to be called due within the fortnight. All at once. None may be refinanced.*

All at once. No diver on the coast could pay a debt all at once. That was why the debts existed — slow tides, never a wave. Called due together, they weren't debts anymore. They were a drowning. Boats seized as collateral, licenses voided, men who had worked the oyster beds for twenty years suddenly owning nothing, not even the right to own nothing.

And there — halfway down the second page — the names.

Tamsin read them the way you read your own name in a stranger's mouth: first not understanding, then understanding completely.

Eriksen, Bram. Eriksen, Bram (jr.). Vosseling, Pieter. Okafor, Daniel. Marsh, Tomas. Eleven names in all. Eleven men marked for ruin — and threaded through the language around them, careful as netting, phrases that meant more than they said. *Those parties previously approached regarding fleet matters.* *Their cooperation having been secured through financial pressure.* *Once distressed assets are consolidated—*

His father. His father's name, typed into a plot to strip him bare. And beneath it, his brother's — sixteen-year-old Bram junior, licensed last spring, proud as a rooster, already owing the company eleven pounds for his gear.

They were being sold, Tamsin understood. Sold out. Someone — Halloran? someone above Halloran? — was going to buy the fleet cheap off the backs of these eleven men, and Crane's letter was the invoice. *Cooperation secured through financial pressure.* His father hadn't signed anything. His father had done worse: he'd owed, and owing was enough. Owing made you cargo.

Tamsin stood very still in the windowless room. Somewhere below, the counting hall murmured with rain-day business. Somewhere out in the harbor, his father was at sea, hauling wet shells in the dark, working to service the very debt this letter was about to call due.

He thought about refusing. He was sixteen; he imagined it vividly for perhaps four seconds — walking upstairs, saying *no sir, I won't*, and what happened after. Indenture contracts had clauses. He'd seen the clause once, in a boy's file he wasn't supposed to be reading: *breach of reasonable orders, forfeiture of term, remand to company custody.* There were no jobs outside the company. There was no port outside the company. Refusal didn't free you. Refusal just changed whose hands you were in.

He thought about warning his father. A note, slipped into a pocket, a whisper on the dock tonight when the boats came in. But even as he imagined it he saw the flaw: any warning named the plot, and naming the plot named Tamsin. Boys who read things become men who talk. Crane had said it plainly, and Crane did not say things plainly unless they were already true.

So Tamsin Eriksen did the arithmetic of survival, which is the only arithmetic an owned boy learns young: keep the name, lose the family; or keep the family, lose everything including the name. There was no third column. He rolled the draft into the platen.

The keys were stiff and familiar at once, like his own hands answering him. He typed slowly, correctly, the M requiring its little coax. *Eriksen, Bram.* The hammers struck. The ribbon inked. Somewhere in the works of the machine, in the shape of each steel letter, the words were becoming permanent — becoming evidence, though he did not yet know of what, or for whom. Every impression pressed through the paper with that faint bruised relief typing leaves, and Tamsin found himself looking at the backs of the pages, at the ghost-letters pushing through, as if the document were writing itself twice, once for Crane and once for whatever came after.

When it was done he read it once more, all the way through, because now it didn't matter. Now he was already guilty.

Then he folded it, dripped the black wax, and pressed the company seal into it — a ship's wheel inside a circle — and watched the wax cool dull and final. Sealed. Irreversible. The word arrived in him like cold water: *irreversible*. There were moments you crossed and could not cross back, and he had just typed his way across one at a borrowed machine for eleven pence a day and his board.

Quill collected the envelope without looking at him. "Done?"

"Sealed."

"Go home, then."

Tamsin went home through the rain, past the drying sheds, past the chandler's, past the tavern where his mother sometimes drank away what his father brought back. He ate supper. He lay awake listening to the harbor working in the dark — winches, gulls, the slap of water against hulls — and somewhere in the smallest hours he slept without meaning to.

* * *

They pulled Mr. Halloran out of the channel just after dawn.

Tamsin heard it before he understood it — the bells, the running on the dock, the crowd gathering at the seawall in the flat grey light. He went with the others because everyone went, and stood on his toes at the back, and saw them carry the body up the ladder steps: a heavy man, grey-faced, dripping, someone's coat thrown over him that did not cover his shoes. Halloran & Sons. The recipient. The man the letter had been sealed for with black wax not fourteen hours ago.

Drowned, they said. Taken cramp, they said, or a slip on his own wet stairs — a big man, poor balance, terrible luck. The company men said it loudest and fastest, which was how Tamsin knew none of it was true. He watched Aldous Crane stand at the edge of the crowd, dry under an umbrella held by Quill, watching the body rather than the water, and Crane's face was not grief. It was inventory.

Tamsin's stomach turned over slowly, thoroughly, like something coming loose on the seabed.

Because he had written the last thing that man was ever sent. Because his fingerprints of ink were on it — no, better than fingerprints: impressions. The exact bite of that specific battered machine, the coax of the M, the worn keys. Anyone who put the letter beside the Remington would know the hands that made it, and only three people alive knew which hands those were, and one of them was floating in the channel.

The letter would still arrive. Letters do. Somewhere in the dead man's office it would sit on a desk among other papers, and whoever inherited Halloran's affairs would find an unsigned letter calling eleven families to ruin, typed on a machine that still sat in a windowless room upstairs.

Tamsin walked to work because not walking would be worse. In the ledger room, the other boys whispered. Quill was absent. Upstairs, doors opened and closed all morning, and at noon a company cart went past the window carrying the Remington itself, wrapped in sacking like a body, headed — he was almost sure — not toward the repair shop but toward the company warehouse by the drying sheds, where things the company wished to keep were kept, and things it wished to forget were forgotten.

That night his father came home late, smelling of brine and fear he wouldn't name, and wouldn't say why. Bram junior said the whole fleet was talking: debts being called, notices posted, eleven names read out at the company office like a death roll. Their father sat with his hands open on the table, staring at them as if they belonged to another man, and said only, "It will be seen to."

But Tamsin had read the letter. He knew what *it* was, and he knew it would not be seen to. He knew the machine that matched the pages was rolling away in a cart, and that a sealed envelope had outlived its recipient, and that the only proof of anything — the only record, the only witness — was a battered typewriter whose keys remembered every word he'd ever struck.

He lay in the dark doing the arithmetic again. It came out different this time.
