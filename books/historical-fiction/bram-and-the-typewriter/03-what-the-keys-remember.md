## 3. What the Keys Remember

The letter came back to Tamsin the way a drowned man comes back—feet first, wrong way round.

He was three pages into a manifest of copra when he stopped seeing the numbers. He saw instead the small grey ghost of each keystroke on the carbon sheet he'd been ordered to burn and hadn't. Every machine writes itself into what it touches. The typebars of the company's Underwood had struck through that unsigned letter with their particular wounds: the *e* with its chipped crown, the *r* that printed fat at the shoulder because old Halversen in repairs had never bothered to true it, the *4* that landed low like a drunk leaning on a wall. Twelve years of hard service had given the machine a signature as distinct as a thumbprint.

And Tamsin had fed it the paper. He had rolled the platen, set the margin, typed names he knew—*Eriksen* among them, his father's name coming off his own fingertips—and sealed the envelope with wax pressed by Mr. Quill's signet ring while the clerk watched him with the flat satisfaction of a man watching a dog fetch something dead.

Now the recipient of that letter was face-down in the harbor, and everyone in Port Callow pretended it was drink.

Tamsin sat very still in the ledger room after hours, lamplight pooling on his desk, and made himself think it through the way he thought through sums: coldly, one column at a time.

If the letter mattered enough to kill over—if it named conspirators, if it promised to rig debts and drown rival divers in obligations they could never pay—then the letter itself was now the most dangerous piece of paper in the port. Whoever held it could prove nothing without proving where it came from. And where it came from was a battered Underwood No. 7 in a room full of indentured boys who were all, conveniently, bound to silence by papers of indenture they hadn't signed so much as inherited.

Vos would understand this. Vos understood everything about paper, which was why the company kept him. Not a clerk, not quite a manager—a fixer, a broad quiet man who smelled faintly of lamp oil and appeared in doorways without arriving through them. When the drowned man had been pulled from the basin on Tuesday, Tamsin had seen Vos across the crowd, not looking at the body but at the faces around it, reading them like manifests.

So: the machine's impressions would be compared against whatever copies existed. If any page survived, the keys would testify. They could convict the men who dictated the plot—or, more usefully to the company, they could convict whichever boy had touched the keys.

Tamsin looked down at his hands. Quick fingers, his mother used to say, before she died. Quick fingers on a slow life.

He thought about his father out past the shoals with the other debt-divers, breathing from a line, bringing up shell for men who owned his lungs until 1919. He thought about the letter's careful phrasing—the plot wasn't violence, it was arithmetic. Debts arranged so a man couldn't surface. Divers sold to themselves. His father's name on a list of men who'd agreed to help make it happen, or who'd agreed to look away, Tamsin still didn't know which, and that not-knowing was a splinter working deeper every day.

There was a carbon copy. There was always a carbon copy; Quill was too careful to trust originals to the mails. Tamsin had been ordered to feed it to the stove and had palmed it instead—not from courage, he told himself, only from the same instinct that made him copy manifests faster than necessary and hide the fact: a refusal to let anything be lost that might later matter.

It was folded now inside his shirt, soft as cloth from four days against his skin.

The plan assembled itself the way plans do for people who have never been allowed one: badly, quickly, all at once.

Tomorrow, Saturday, the ledger room would empty by noon. Old Marshlock locked the cabinet drawers but never the typewriter; a machine, in his view, was furniture. Furniture did not need guarding. Furniture could not run away.

Tamsin meant to prove him half right.

* * *

He took the typewriter at dusk, wrapped in sacking, carried openly because openly is the last thing anyone questions. A ledger boy hauling the office machine to the repair shop on a Saturday evening—two clerks saw him and neither asked. One said something about the *r* key sticking and laughed at his own joke. Tamsin smiled with the bottom half of his face and walked.

The drying sheds stood on stilts at the harbor's edge, past the fish-curing yards, where the divers' families hung shell racks and stretched nets and nobody official came after dark except to collect rents already collected. The sheds smelled of brine and kelp and the sweet rot of discarded meat, and the boards rang hollow underfoot with the tide slapping beneath.

The third shed from the end belonged, in the loose way such things belonged, to no one. Its floor had a gap behind the salt barrel wide enough for a machine in sacking, and beneath a loose plank Tamsin buried the carbon letter in an oilcloth packet with his spare shirt wrapped round it, so that digging hands would find laundry before evidence.

He knelt there in the dark doing the arithmetic again, because arithmetic was the only prayer he knew. If the original letter still existed somewhere—in the dead man's effects, in Quill's files—it would damn whoever's fingers matched its impressions. If it didn't exist, then the only testimony left was memory, and memory could be bought, frightened, or drowned. Either way the machine was a witness. Witnesses got killed. Machines got burned.

Let them try to burn it where he'd put it. Let them come asking at the sheds among people who owed the company nothing but grief.

He was rebolting the door when he heard the footsteps on the boardwalk and flattened himself against the wall between two shell racks, heart going like a fish in a bucket.

Two lanterns. Voices. The taller light-bearer moved the way Vos moved, unhurried, as though the night had been scheduled for him personally.

"—asking at every house," said the second man, younger, breathless. "Started at the basin end. Half the divers' row done."

"And?"

"A deaf grandmother, a woman who spat at me, and a boy of maybe ten who says he saw a ledger boy go past with a bundle Thursday night."

A pause long enough to fall into.

"Ledger boy," Vos repeated. He said it the way a man reads a figure twice to check a sum.

"Could've been washing. They haul bundles everywhere—"

"Could have." The lantern swung, and light crawled across the gap under the shed doors, across the fresh-scuffed boards where Tamsin had dragged forty pounds of iron. Vos stopped walking. Tamsin could hear him not moving, which was worse than hearing him move.

"The machine's in the shop since Saturday," the younger man offered. "Halversen has it."

"Halversen received it Monday morning and swears it hasn't left the office since Friday." Vos's voice stayed level, conversational, terrible. "I checked. That's why I'm walking the shore at night, Jem, instead of sleeping. Because a machine can't leave a room, and yet every impression on that dead man's desk matches a machine that was supposedly elsewhere."

Tamsin's mouth went dry. On the dead man's desk. There was a copy in the dead man's effects after all—Quill must have sent a second, or the man had made his own—and Vos had already laid the pages beside the typebar tray and matched wound to wound. Which meant Vos knew exactly what the keys remembered. Which meant Vos was hunting not the crime but the courier.

"Boys from the ledger room?" Jem asked.

"Boys don't steal typewriters. Boys steal coins and bread." Another pause. "But a boy might steal a witness, if someone explained to him what a witness was worth. Or if he read the letter himself." The lantern turned, and Vos's shadow fell long and thin across the boardwalk toward the shed where Tamsin stood. "Find out which boys worked late this week. Find out whose family name appears in correspondence. And do it gently. We are not looking for a thief, Jem. We're looking for a reader."

They moved off toward the curing yards, the lanterns shrinking to two sparks, then none.

Tamsin stayed against the wall until his legs shook, then longer. Above him the shell racks creaked. Beneath the boards the tide turned and began to run out, whispering over the oyster beds, patient, keeping its own records in sand.

A reader. Well. He had been called worse in his own head.

He thought of Maren at home, fourteen and sharp-eyed, counting coins on the table while their father slept before the dawn boat. He thought of Vos standing in their doorway with his lamp and his gentle questions, and his father—his father whose name sat in a stolen letter hidden under a plank—being asked where his son spent his evenings.

Fear had gotten him this far. Fear had carried the machine down the boardwalk and buried the letter under salt. But fear was a current, and currents only take you places; they don't tell you why you're going.

Kneeling again in the dark, Tamsin made a second decision, quieter than the first and heavier. The letter named conspirators. Fine. But conspirators lie, and dead men can't correct the record, and his father might be guilty of nothing worse than being named. Somebody had to find out the truth of it—diver by diver, clerk by clerk—and write it down where fire couldn't reach.

The machine would type it. The same chipped *e*, the same drunken *4*. Let the keys remember everything, then. Both letters. Both truths.

He pressed his palm flat against the sacking one more time, like taking an oath off cold iron, and went home the long way, along the tideline, where his footprints filled slowly with water behind him as if the sea were already trying to erase him.

At the top of divers' row, a lantern waited outside his own front door.

Vos was sitting on their step, hat in his hands, patient as arithmetic.
