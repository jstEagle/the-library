## 4. Salt and Silence

The sea did not care whose name was on paper.

Torsten Eriksen had learned that at fourteen, when he first went over the side of a pearling lugger with a stone in his hand and the sky shrinking above him to a coin of light. Thirty years on, he still went down the same way — rope in his fist, breath measured, the weight of the water pressing on his chest like a patient hand. The sea took what it wanted and gave back shells. It did not read ledgers. It did not know that Torsten Eriksen owed the Pearl Coast Trading Company two hundred and forty pounds, eleven shillings, and a debt of a different kind that no clerk had yet found a column for.

He broke the surface, spat salt, and hauled himself onto the gunwale of the *Merrow's Gift*. The tender was pitching gently in the long swell off Cormorant Bank, and the other divers bobbed like seals in the grey morning water. His brother-in-law Halvard reached down and gripped his forearm, pulling him aboard.

"Bottom's thinning," Torsten said. "We've stripped this bank near bare."

"Company says dive it another season."

"The company says a lot of things from shore."

Halvard laughed, but without much in it. They had been having this conversation for three years, and each year it ended the same way: with the debt growing instead of shrinking, with the shell coming up thinner, with the company's tally-clerks writing numbers that never quite matched the numbers Torsten counted in his own head.

He knew arithmetic. He'd had four winters of school in the old country before the sea swallowed the family whole, and he could add a column faster than any clerk in the port — which was precisely the problem. A man who couldn't count could believe he was paying off his debt. Torsten counted every season, and every season the sum came out wrong by exactly as much as a man could bear and no more. Interest compounding. Gear charged at double its worth. Water and biscuits entered against his account as though breathing were a service rendered.

They were not trying to be repaid. They were trying to keep him.

* * *

It was Halvard who had brought him to Ansel Rook.

Not brought exactly — led, one night in the back room of the Siren's Rest, where the lamplight was low and the rum was company-issue despite being forbidden to company men, and Rook sat waiting with his soft voice and his clean hands. Rook was a tally-master then. He was something more now; nobody said exactly what. Men who worked for the company learned to leave those questions alone.

"You've got standing," Rook had told Torsten that night. "Twelve luggers out of this port, and the divers' council listens when you talk. We don't want much. Sign here, stand there, say nothing anywhere. In return—" and here he'd slid a paper across the table, and Torsten had seen the figure at the bottom, "—your account is settled. Cleared. Your indenture torn up. You sail free."

Two hundred and forty pounds, eleven shillings. Gone like foam off a rock.

He should have asked then what the silence was for. He didn't ask. That was the thing about drowning men, Rook must have known it better than anyone: they'll take a rope from any hand, and they won't look up to see who's holding the other end.

So Torsten signed. And for two years he kept the bargain — stood in the divers' council meetings with his mouth shut, steered grumbling men away from the company office when they wanted their accounts audited, passed along the word that grievances would be handled, that patience would be rewarded. He told himself it was nothing. Paper work. Committee work. What harm could a man do by sitting quiet?

Then the fleet transfers started. Then the debt-rigging — he saw the shape of it even if he couldn't prove the details, new contracts written so that half the independent divers would owe the company everything by season's end, their boats forfeit, their boats becoming the company's boats. And then, last month, Rook's plan had shown its teeth: the rival operation at Greywater, the one undercutting the company's shell prices, its divers were to be ruined the same way. Ruined, and if the rough work fell to hired men from up the coast, well, paper could not be blamed for wet work.

Torsten understood, finally, what he had been holding shut with his silence. It was not a door. It was a drain plug, and under it was the whole harbour.

* * *

The letter had gone out ten days ago. He hadn't written it. He'd been told only that it was written — that arrangements were sealed, that names were on record now, his among them, and that the record lived in a safe in the ledger room where all records lived. He had signed enough papers by then to know how his name looked in ink. Eriksen, T. Lugger *Merrow's Gift*, account number 411.

His son worked in that ledger room.

That thought arrived some nights like a fish hook set behind his eye. Tamsin, sixteen now, quick-fingered, quick-minded, the boy who at eight could mend a net faster than grown men and at twelve had taught himself letters off cargo manifests because nobody stopped him. Torsten had gotten the boy the indenture — pulled what strings a bonded man could pull, called in favours from men who pretended to owe him — because the water kills divers, and paper doesn't. Clerks die old in beds. That was the whole of his ambition for his son: that he would die somewhere dry.

And now the boy sat six days a week in the room where the conspiracy kept its paperwork, copying manifests for the men who owned his father, and Torsten could not tell him anything, could not warn him off, could not so much as look at him too long at the Sunday table without wondering what the company might already have decided a clerk's son was worth.

Silence is protection. He built the sentence again each morning like a breakwater against the guilt. The boy knows nothing. The boy is beneath their notice. If I speak, if I flinch, if I come home with questions in my face, then he becomes interesting — and interesting men in this port end up in the shipping lists under *lost overboard*.

He tested the belief the way he tested his air underwater, counting what he had before the blackness came.

What he had: the boy was small in the company's eyes, a copying-hand, replaceable. What he had: Rook needed the divers' council calm until the scheme closed, and Torsten's usefulness lay in staying unremarkable. What he had: two more signatures, one more season, and then the account cleared and the family free and all of it behind them like weather.

What he did not have: any way to know whether Rook's partners intended to let loose ends remain loose.

* * *

News came out to the banks on the third day, riding the supply tender, and it came in the shape of Halvard's face.

"Kestrel's dead," Halvard said, crouching beside him on the deck, voice pitched under the wind. "Joren Kestrel. Found in the harbour basin yesterday morning, under the coal stage. They're calling it drowning."

Joren Kestrel. Torsten kept his face still, which took work, because inside him something went down fast and cold, like a stone dropped through dark water. Kestrel was the Greywater man — the rival operator's broker, the one the whole scheme bent around, the one whose ruin was to have been managed on paper. And he had received a letter. Torsten didn't know that for certain, but he knew the way the plan ran, and a man doesn't drown quietly under a coal stage ten days after conspirators seal their arrangements unless somebody decided the arrangement needed adjusting.

"They dragged him," Halvard went on. "No marks anyone will speak of. Company's saying bad luck, cramp, the usual." He paused. "There's a fixer going door to door in the town. Vos. Asking about paper. Asking who's touched company correspondence lately."

The wind moved across the water and everything it touched was grey.

"Why would a fixer ask about correspondence?" Torsten said, and heard how flat his own voice came out.

"Don't know. But Torsten — your boy. He's in the ledger room. If something's gone missing off a desk somewhere—"

"He copies manifests. Nothing else."

"Sure." Halvard looked at him a moment too long. "Sure. Nothing else."

* * *

That night, anchored in the lee of Cormorant Bank, Torsten sat on deck where the others slept and did the arithmetic he had been refusing for two years.

Item: Joren Kestrel, drowned under a coal stage. Item: a fixer walking the town with questions. Item: a letter typed and sealed in the ledger room by hands he could not stop imagining — small, quick hands, the hands that mended nets at eight. Item: his own name, in ink, in a safe in that same room.

If the letter named conspirators, and the recipient was dead, then the letter was evidence now, and evidence gets hunted. And when the hunt turned inward, it would turn toward the ledger room first, because that was where the company's paper lived — and toward whoever had touched it last.

Rook's promises meant nothing anymore. Rook's promises had been made when the plan was paper; the plan had killed a man, and plans that kill men do not honour debts, they bury them. Two hundred and forty pounds, eleven shillings. He had sold his silence cheap, and now his silence had a body under it, and the price was climbing by the day, and the currency was looking more and more like his son.

He could go to Rook. Say: my part is done, tear the page, take the loss. But men who tried to step out of Rook's arrangements became items in other men's arithmetic, and Torsten had just finished learning what happened to inconvenient names.

He could go to the magistrate. And hand the company proof that Torsten Eriksen's signature sat in the middle of it, and hang his whole family alongside himself.

He could go home at the end of the run, sit across the table from Tamsin, and say nothing, and keep saying nothing, and hope the sea between them held.

He looked east, where the port's lights would be, a smudge of gold on the horizon under the early stars. Somewhere in that smudge was a boy who trusted him, or had once, before the indenture and the silences and the Sundays where Torsten watched his son's face for signs of knowledge and gave back a face with nothing in it. Sixteen years old. Dry all his life. That had been the gift. That had been the point.

*Keep him boring,* Torsten told himself. *Keep him small. A fixer asking questions is not a fixer finding answers. Let it pass over. Weather passes.*

But under the words, settling the way silt settles in a diver's lungs, slow and fine and final, was the knowledge he could not cough up: that the letter had been typed in the ledger room, and the machine that typed it stayed in the ledger room, and machines remember what fingers do — and somewhere ashore, his son had put his hands on that machine, and Torsten had not been there to stop him, and would not be there tomorrow either, because the tide was turning and the *Merrow's Gift* was needed on the banks, and the company owned his mornings, his breath, and his silence, and had not yet decided what else it owned.

He went below and slept badly, and dreamed of a coin of light on the surface, far above, shrinking.
