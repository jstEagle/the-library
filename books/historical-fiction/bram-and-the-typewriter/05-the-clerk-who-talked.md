## 5. The Clerk Who Talked

The man I'd been told to find was drinking gin out of a teacup in the back room of the Anchor & Chain, and when I pushed through the door he looked at me the way a man looks at weather he's already decided to ignore.

"You're one of Hollis's boys," he said. "Ledger room. I can tell. You've got ink on your cuff and you walk like you're sorry to take up floor."

My name is Tamsin Eriksen. I was sixteen years old and owned on paper down to my teeth, and I had spent four nights working up the nerve to walk into this room, so I didn't much care what he thought of my cuffs.

"Are you Perrin Quill?"

"I'm whoever you need me to be, lad." He raised the teacup in salute. "Tonight I'm mostly gin."

Perrin Quill had been second clerk in the manifest office until fourteen months ago, when he'd been dismissed for "irregularities" that nobody would name. The divers' mothers called him the only honest man the company ever fired, which told you everything about how he'd left. He was forty if he was anything, with a clerk's soft hands gone rough from drink, and he lived now in the back room of a tavern that smelled of tar and old fish because nowhere else would have him.

I sat down across from him. He watched me do it with the flat patience of a man who had all night and no reason to spend it well.

"I want to ask about a debt," I said.

He laughed. It wasn't a kind sound. "Everyone wants to ask about a debt. That's all this port *is*, boy — a debt wearing a harbor."

"Not a diver's debt. A fleet's." I kept my voice low. The door to the taproom was shut but not thick. "A debt big enough to sink the *Meridian* line. Big enough that somebody needed it typed on a machine nobody could trace."

The laugh went out of him like a lamp turned down.

For a long moment he just looked at me. Then he set the teacup down very carefully, the way drunk men do when they're trying to prove they're not drunk, and said, "Who sent you?"

"Nobody. That's the trouble."

"And why does a ledger boy come asking about fleets at night?"

"Because I typed the letter," I said. "And because the man it was sent to is dead in the channel, and because my father's name was on it, and because I haven't slept since."

Something moved behind his eyes — not pity exactly. Recognition. The look of a man seeing an earlier version of himself walk in wearing younger skin.

"Shut the door," he said. "Properly. And sit where I can see your face while you lie to me or don't."

* * *

What he told me took an hour and three more teacups of gin, and it matched the letter word for word, which was somehow worse than if it hadn't.

"The company's been lending against its own ships for two years," he said. "Paper debts, real interest. Hollis set it up so the *Meridian* partners — Brandt, Okafor, the Voss brothers — each hold notes on vessels they don't own yet. On paper the fleet's mortgaged past its worth. In truth the mortgages are smoke. But smoke burns, boy, and there are men holding matches."

"The rival divers," I said. "The ones who broke from the company last spring."

"They're the match-holders' insurance. If Meridian tries to call the debts, the independents get drowned first — in paper, in court costs, in actual water if it comes to that. Divers die cheap here. You know that better than I do." His voice had gone careful, deliberate, the drink burned off by something stronger than sobriety. "Your father's name is on the list because the conspirators needed divers' signatures. Debt-bonded men sign what they're told to sign. It doesn't mean he wanted any of it. It means he couldn't refuse."

"He could have told me."

"Could yours have told you?" Quill looked at me steadily. "Mine never did either. That's what love looks like in a place like this. It looks like nothing at all."

I made myself ask the question I'd carried through four sleepless nights. "The letter I typed. The man who received it — Captain Reyes. Did they—"

"They drowned him before the ink dried," Quill said flatly. "Reyes was going to sell the scheme to the magistrate. Everyone knew it except everyone who needed to. So they wrote him a letter naming conspirators, knowing it would pass through hands, knowing it would be found on his body or traced to his rooms, and let it hang him posthumously as one of them instead of a witness against them." He smiled without any humor in it. "It's elegant. I'd almost admire it if I weren't the man they ruined for noticing."

"Why were you ruined?"

"Because I kept books, and the books didn't balance, and I was stupid enough to say so out loud at a meeting where Hollis was drinking." He spread his soft-rough hands. "They took my position, my references, my lodging. They left me my life, which I've been spending ever since, badly."

Then he stood, swayed once, and crossed to the iron cot in the corner. From beneath it he dragged a sea chest, and from beneath the chest's false bottom — a trick I recognized, because every diver's family keeps one — he lifted a bundle wrapped in oilcloth.

"I tore it myself," he said. "The night before they dismissed me. Half the fleet ledger. The pages with the mortgage entries, the interest schedules, the initials. The other half lives in the company archive under seal, which is why they haven't come for me — half a ledger proves nothing, they think. But half a ledger in the right hands—" He pressed it into mine. It was heavier than paper should be, or maybe that was what it meant. "Get it to someone who can subpoena the rest. A magistrate. A newspaper. Anyone with a stamp and a spine."

"Why give it to me? You don't know me."

"I know you came here at night with no one behind you," he said. "That's rarer than honesty in this town. Besides—" and here his grin surfaced again, brief and genuine, "—you've got ink on your cuff. A bookkeeper recognizes his own kind."

I asked him to testify. I asked him plainly, sitting on the edge of my chair with the oilcloth bundle burning cold against my ribs: come forward, swear it, put his name under the record.

"In time," he said. "When you've got the magistrate's ear, not before. I'm no use to anyone dead, and right now I'm a drunk in a tavern back room — easy to dismiss, easier to drown. Let me stay useless a little longer." He poured the last of the gin. "Come back Thursday. I'll be sober enough to write my statement by then. Probably."

I believed him. God help me, I believed him, because I needed to.

* * *

Thursday, the back room of the Anchor & Chain held an empty cot, a cold stove, and a smell of gin that was already fading.

The publican shrugged when I asked. "Left Tuesday night, owed me a week's rent, took nothing. Not even his cup." He nodded at the teacup on the table, still sitting where Quill had set it down the night we talked. "Odd sort. Polite, though."

Tuesday. Two days after I'd sat where I was standing. Two days after he'd handed a stranger half a ledger and promised to testify.

I walked the length of the waterfront looking for him anyway, telling myself he'd moved lodgings, gone to visit a cousin, done anything other than the thing the port did to inconvenient witnesses. At the customs house a clerk mentioned a body pulled from the channel Wednesday morning, unclaimed, middle-aged. At the harbormaster's office they said it had been released for burial already, paid for by an anonymous subscription, quick as closing an account.

Drowned men don't get funerals that fast unless someone is paying for speed.

I stood at the harbor rail with the gulls screaming and the tide going out and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, what I was holding. Quill was gone. The half-ledger was all that remained of him — his testimony compressed into paper, mute, waiting for a voice it would never hear. Every page I'd carry now would carry his silence too.

And if they'd found him, they might find the trail to me. He'd been seen talking to people; drunks talk; the publican remembered faces. A ledger boy, ink on his cuff, asking after debts.

* * *

Vos came to the drying sheds on Friday.

I wasn't there — I'd taken to keeping away from my own hiding places, circling them like a gull circles a fish — but Maren saw it happen, and Maren told me that night in the space between our house and the fence line where we sometimes spoke without seeming to speak at all.

"A broad man," she whispered, eyes fixed on the dark. "Company coat, though he wears it like it embarrasses him. Went shed to shed along the row. Opened yours."

My stomach dropped through the boards of the world. "Opened it how?"

"Pried the hasp. Didn't force it — had a key or patience. Looked through the net racks, the drying frames, the oil drums." Her hand found my sleeve in the dark, gripped hard. "Tamsin. He crouched at the floor seam. The loose board. He *knocked on it*. Like he knew what loose boards are for."

The typewriter was under that board. Wrapped in sacking, wedged between the joists, with the original letter folded inside its carriage case. My whole case against them — the impressions, the pages, the machine that could match them — six inches of salt-rotted timber away from a man whose job was finding things.

"Did he lift it?"

"No. He knocked, waited, and moved on. Maybe it sounded solid. Maybe he was interrupted." She turned to face me then, and in the thin light from the harbor lamps her eyes were fierce. "But he talked to the shed foreman after. I heard the foreman tell Da. He asked about the ledger room boys. Names. Hours. Which ones came this way evenings."

The ledger room boys. There were five of us. Three had families too frightened to breathe wrong, and one had left the port in autumn. Which left a sixteen-year-old indentured copyist with quick fingers and a sister standing in the dark beside him, both of us now counted somewhere in a broad man's patient arithmetic.

"You have to move it," Maren said.

"I know."

"Tonight. And Tamsin—" her grip tightened, "—if they come for you, you run to me first. Whatever else happens. Whatever you're carrying. Promise."

I promised. It was the easiest promise I'd ever made and I suspected it would be the most expensive.

That night I lay awake listening to the tide, doing sums I hated. Quill dead before testifying. The sheds searched. Vos narrowing his circle from a whole port to a handful of boys with ink on their cuffs. Hope and loss arriving in the same hour, packed together like ballast, and only one of them buoyant.

Somewhere across the water my father was diving in waters the conspirators intended to make red, bound by a signature I'd read by lamplight, silent the way fathers in this port were silent. Somewhere Vos was writing names in a book of his own.

And somewhere under a loose board in a drying shed, a typewriter sat in the dark holding every word I'd ever feared, waiting for me to decide what it would say next.
