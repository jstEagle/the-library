## 6. Ink Under the Floorboards

Maren found the loose board by accident, the way you find anything in that house — by nearly breaking your neck on it.

She was hauling the washbasket past the back wall of the lean-to, where the floor never dried and the boards had gone soft as old bread, when her heel caught a corner of one that should not have moved. It tipped. Beneath it there was dark, and in the dark something rectangular, wrapped in oilcloth.

She stood very still for a long moment, listening to the house. Father was at sea, three days into a ten-day run. Tamsin hadn't come home since Tuesday; she'd told herself he was sleeping in the loft at Halloran's, dodging their mother's questions, dodging hers. The lamp guttered. Her heart did something strange and slow, like a fist closing.

She put down the basket. She knelt. She lifted the board the rest of the way.

The oilcloth bundle was heavier than it looked. She unwrapped it with the care of someone defusing something, and by the light of the lamp on its nail she saw: a typewriter, an ugly dented thing with keys worn to gray nubs. Underneath it, folded flat, a letter. And beneath that, a stack of paper — some blank, some filled with typing so faint it looked like breath on glass.

She read the letter twice. She read her father's name in it once, and that once was enough.

* * *

She didn't cry. Crying cost heat, and heat cost wood, and wood cost money, and money was the whole reason any of them were in this position — that was the first lesson of the Eriksen household, learned before letters, before prayers. She sat back on her heels and looked at the machine and understood several things at once, the way you understand a wave only when it's already broken over you.

Tamsin had taken this from the company. Tamsin could hang for taking this from the company. And whatever was in these pages — debts rigged, divers drowned on paper before they ever drowned in water, her father's name signed to it like a man signing his own death warrant — it was either the thing that would kill them all or the only weapon they'd ever had.

Maren was seventeen. She had been keeping the family's books since she was eleven — the real ones, kept in her head, because the company's numbers were lies and somebody in the family had to know the truth of what they owed. She knew arithmetic and she knew fear, and she knew that fear without arithmetic was just drowning with extra steps.

She rewrapped the letter. She left the machine under the floor, exactly as found, exactly angled. Then she went inside, banked the stove, and waited up for her brother with a needlework frame in her lap and her ears open like doors.

He came at half past midnight, smelling of salt and fish-oil and terror. He stopped dead in the doorway when he saw her awake, and for a heartbeat they simply looked at each other across the room like two strangers who'd been told they were related.

"You went through my things," he said.

"There are no your things," Maren said. "There's this house and everything in it, and we hold it together or we don't." She set the needlework down. "Sit down, Tamsin. I know what the machine says."

His face did something complicated — guilt and relief fighting over the same ground — and then he sat, heavily, the way boys sit when they've been carrying weight in places weight isn't meant to go.

"I didn't want you in it," he said.

"Too late. We share a name with a man whose name is in that letter. I've been in it since the day I could read."

* * *

They talked until the candle burned down to the collar of its dish. He told her about the ledger room, about being ordered to type the letter, about reading his own father among the conspirators and sealing it anyway because refusing would have been the same as confessing. He told her about the drowned recipient, about Vos going door to door with his soft voice and his notebook. He told her about the ex-clerk, Halloran's cousin or somebody's cousin, who'd given him half a ledger and then evaporated like spit on a stove.

"And now?" Maren said. "You have a stolen machine, a stolen letter, half a book of numbers nobody can read alone, and no witness alive who'll say any of it out loud. What's the plan, brother?"

"The plan is—" He stopped. She watched him discover, right there in front of her, that he didn't have one.

"The plan," Maren said, "is that testimony is worth nothing unless it's written down before the people in it die or run. You said it yourself — the machine remembers. So make it remember everything. Every diver who's been cheated, every clerk who saw, every name. Type it all. Copies. More than one. Put them places Vos can't burn down all at once."

"That's months of pages."

"Then we'd better start tonight."

He stared at her. "You'd help? Knowing what Father—"

"Knowing exactly what Father is." Her voice came out flatter than she intended. "He's a man in a barrel going over the falls, Tamsin. Somebody signed him into that barrel. I'm not saying he's innocent. I'm saying the men who put his name on that letter wanted him there, and I want to know why, and I want it in writing, from him, before anyone else writes it for him."

For a moment something passed between them — not forgiveness, nothing so finished as that. Recognition. They had spent years orbiting each other, two survivors in one small hull, each privately convinced the other didn't see how hard the surviving was. Now, in candlelight, over a stolen typewriter, they finally looked up at the same time.

"All right," Tamsin said. "But we do it here, and we do it quiet, and if Vos comes to the door you've never seen me in your life."

"I've seen you every day for seventeen years," Maren said. "I'll say you're stupid. Nobody will question it."

And that — absurdly, terribly — made them both laugh, low and shocked at themselves, the first laugh either of them had managed in weeks, quickly smothered in case the walls were paid by the word.

* * *

They worked by night after that. The machine lived under the floorboards by day and came up after the house slept, set on the kitchen table with a folded blanket beneath it to eat the clatter. Maren learned the rhythm of the keys the way she'd learned the rhythm of the tides — fast enough to matter, slow enough not to wake their mother in the next room.

Tamsin dictated from memory. Names of divers shorted on their shares. Dates. Boat registrations. The clerk's drunken account, written out first in pencil and typed fair. Half-ledger figures, checked against the real accounts Maren kept in her head, and when the two agreed — and they agreed, page after page, to the shilling — something in the room changed. It stopped being a boy's frightened scribbling and became a record.

"He skimmed the death benefits," Maren said one night, tapping the ledger column with a grim kind of satisfaction. "Every diver lost, there's a payment owed to the widow, and look — paid, paid, paid. Except Widower Chen got eight of twelve. Widow Okafor got nothing. It's all here. Someone's been collecting the difference."

"That's motive," Tamsin said, fingers moving. "That's a reason for a drowned man."

"It's a reason for a hanged man," Maren corrected quietly. "We just have to be sure it's the right one who hangs."

Some nights their mother stirred and Maren killed the lamp and they sat in absolute blackness, listening to the house breathe, hands finding each other across the table in the dark — not holding, just touching, wrist to wrist, a pulse check between conspirators.

Once, footsteps stopped outside the shutter. A man's weight on the step, unhurried. Vos's weight — she'd learned it from a single visit, the particular patience of it. Neither of them moved. The typewriter sat in the middle of the table like a confession with a ribbon.

The footsteps went away. When they breathed again, Tamsin whispered, "We need more copies. If they take the machine and us, the pages still have to get out."

"I know a girl at the shipping office," Maren said. "And I know the night mail leaves at six."

* * *

It was near the end of the third week, pages stacked in threes now — one to keep, two to move — that Maren noticed the change in her brother. He stood straighter. He spoke less apologetically. He had begun, she realized, to think of himself as a witness instead of a suspect, and it suited him the way new boots suit a man who's walked barefoot: painfully, but better.

"You know what I keep thinking?" he said, feeding in a fresh sheet. "The company thinks the only true copy of anything is the one in their files. They burn the file, the truth dies. That's their whole power. Paper that can be burned."

"Then we become paper they can't find all of," Maren said.

"Exactly." He began to type. In the small hours, with the blanket muffling the keys and his sister checking his figures line by line, the pages came off the platen clean and damning: *the undersigned record, taken from persons having direct knowledge, to wit—*

It was close to dawn when the knock came. Not Vos's patient knock — a hammering, urgent, fists gone frantic against the door, and through it a voice Maren knew from the docks, one of her father's crew, cracked wide open:

"Mister Eriksen's boat is in. There's been trouble. There's blood. Get your brother — get him *now.*"

The candle flame leaned in the draft. Down the lane, somewhere toward the water, a bell had started ringing.

Tamsin and Maren looked at each other across the machine, across the stacked pages, across everything they'd built in the dark.

"The floorboards," she said.

But he was already pulling the cache up, already reaching — not for the machine, but for the letter, the original, folding it into his shirt. "They'll search the house first," he said. "Not me. I'm going to the boat."

"Tamsin—"

"If Father dies out there, he dies the man that letter says he is." His jaw was set, and underneath the fear in his eyes was something she had never seen there before: purpose, hard as a keel. "I'm going to bring back the rest of the story."

Then he was out the door and running toward the bells, and Maren stood alone in the candlelight with a stolen typewriter, a stack of testimony, and the sudden, terrible understanding that whatever happened next, the record was no longer finished — and neither were they.
