## 7. The Dying Confession

Old Halloran's house sat at the end of the shingle road like a tooth gone loose in the jaw of the town — leaning, dark, waiting to fall. Tamsin had passed it a hundred times on deliveries and never once seen a light in the upper windows. Tonight there were two lights: one candle behind the shutters upstairs, and one lantern bobbing at the far end of the shingle road, where the company's men were asking their slow way through the fishing huts.

He carried the typewriter against his chest under his oilskin, wrapped in sacking, and every step on the wet stones sent a small iron heart beating against his ribs. Maren had wanted to come. He'd told her no, and she had looked at him the way she looked at Father when he lied about the state of his lungs, and he had almost broken. But if Vos took him tonight, someone with clean hands had to carry the pages.

The door was unlocked. That frightened him more than a locked door would have.

Inside, the smell hit him first — camphor and something sweeter underneath, the smell he'd learned in the ledger room the winter the second clerk died: a body turning its own poison inward. A lamp burned low on the hall table. The housekeeper was gone. The good furniture was covered in sheets, as though the house were already mourning itself.

"Halloran," Tamsin called softly. "It's the Eriksen boy."

A voice came down from above, cracked and thin but still carrying the old command in it, the voice of a man who had signed papers that ended lives without ever raising his own hand. "Up, then. Before I change my mind about dying alone."

* * *

Ambrose Halloran lay in a bed too large for him, propped on pillows like cargo lashed down for rough weather. He had been the biggest man in the port once — Tamsin remembered him from the fleet blessings, broad and gold-chained, throwing coins to the divers' children while his clerks calculated what those children's fathers owed. Now the chains were gone and the flesh had fallen off him and his skin had a gray-green cast, like old copper. Poison, the fishwives said. Slow arsenic in his nightly brandy, administered by partners who no longer needed an architect now that the building stood.

"You brought it." His eyes found the lump under the oilskin. "The machine."

"You said you'd talk. I said I'd write. That was the bargain."

"Then we're both men of our word, which makes us rare in this town." He coughed, wet and long, and when he wiped his mouth there was rust on the cloth. "Set it on the chair. Paper?"

Tamsin set the typewriter down and unwrapped it, and the old Remington sat in the lamplight with its chipped keys and its crooked R, looking less like a murder weapon than anything he knew — and being exactly that. He fed in a fresh sheet. His fingers found home row the way they found the rungs of a ladder in the dark.

"Say your name first," he said. "Full name. For the record."

Halloran laughed, and the laugh turned into another cough. "For the record. God help me, a pearl diver's whelp is going to teach me how a confession works." He drew breath. "Ambrose Nathaniel Halloran. Director of the Meridian Pearl and Trading Company. Formerly of Bristol. Formerly honest, some said."

Tamsin typed. The keys struck hard and sure. *Ambrose Nathaniel Halloran.*

"Now say the rest."

* * *

It took two hours, and Halloran gave it the way he must once have given dictation to clerks — measured, precise, pausing only for Tamsin's fingers to catch up, correcting himself with a lawyer's care: *no, strike that, put 'in the spring of 1909,' not 'two years past' — dates, boy, dates convict men.* The scheme unspooled across six pages. The rigged advances to the diver families, the debts calculated to be unpayable by design. The buying of the fleet's mortgages at fractions of their worth. The plan to seize the boats whole when the debtors defaulted en masse — and the deliberate over-issuing of diving insurance so that a drowned man paid better than a living one.

"The drownings," Tamsin said, when Halloran reached the part about the rival company's crews. "You ordered them?"

"I ordered the debts. Men do the rest themselves when they're drowning in paper." Halloran's eyes were fever-bright. "Write it plain, boy. Don't soften me. I didn't order any man into the water. I built the arithmetic that put them there, and I slept well, and that's worse. Put that in."

Tamsin put it in. His hands were steady and that surprised him; he had thought they would shake. There was something in the work itself that held him up — the click and carriage-return, the discipline of getting a man's ruin down accurately before the man was gone. It was the ledger room turned inside out. Same keys. Different master.

"Your father," Halloran said, during one of the pauses.

Tamsin's fingers stopped. "What about him."

"He never signed the main instrument. He refused the fleet seizure — did you know that? Refused it twice. They kept him on the debt rolls anyway, used his name on the lists to bring the others along. 'Even Eriksen's in,' they told them. Even Eriksen." Halloran closed his eyes. "His mark isn't on anything. I made certain of that much before they started feeding me my own brandy. Write it. He'll not say it himself, and you should have it from someone."

Tamsin typed it. Something in his chest that had been clenched since the night of the letter came loose all at once, quietly, like a knot giving way underwater. He did not trust himself to speak for a moment. When he trusted himself again, he said only: "Why are you doing this?"

Halloran was quiet so long that Tamsin thought he'd drifted off. Then: "Because they poisoned me instead of paying me out, and I intend to cost them more than my silence was worth. And because—" he gestured vaguely at the window, the harbor beyond, "—I counted those children same as anyone. Coins in one hand, columns in the other. You get to an age, boy, where you want the columns to lose for once."

Down the hill, faintly, a dog began to bark. Then another. Then the sound Tamsin had been listening toward all night without admitting it: boots on shingle, several pairs, unhurried. Men who believed the tide was on their side.

* * *

"They're here," Tamsin whispered.

"I heard." Halloran's voice had gone thin. The fever was climbing; sweat stood on his forehead. "Finish the last page. Quickly."

Boots on gravel. The lantern light swung across the shuttered windows and slid away. Tamsin typed faster, missing a letter, backspacing, striking it again. *I do solemnly attest that the foregoing is a true account, dictated freely…*

"Sign it," he said, and turned the page toward the bed, pen already in his hand.

Halloran took the pen. His signature, once a thing taught in commercial academies, came out as a scrawl that wandered off the line and stopped halfway through the final *n*. He looked at it without shame. "Dying men sign badly. Magistrates know the difference between a bad hand and a false one. Get the date right."

Tamsin dated it. From below came the knock — not a knock really, a blow, the flat impact of a fist or a shoulder against a door that was never meant to keep out the company's own men. The housekeeper's door splintered somewhere far off in the house.

"Halloran!" Vos's voice, smooth as always, pitched for politeness even now. "Director. We've come about your correspondence."

"The back stair," Halloran gasped. "Goes down to the garden wall. The wall drops to the oyster beds. Tide's half out — mud to the knee, shells underfoot, but a man can cross it if he doesn't stop. They'll take the shingle road. Nobody crosses the beds at night unless they're mad."

"Are they mad?" Tamsin asked, hauling the typewriter into the sacking, into his arms.

"You are," said Halloran, "so go."

At the door Tamsin stopped. He couldn't have said why. The old man lay in his great bed with his scrawled name drying beside him, and everything he had owned was sheeted and boxed, and no one was coming to sit with him.

"Thank you," Tamsin said.

"Don't thank me." Halloran turned his face to the ceiling. "Get it printed. That's the thanks."

Tamsin went down the back stair in the dark, one hand flat on the wall, the typewriter hugged to his chest like a child he would not drop. Behind him he heard Vos's boots on the landing above, unhurried, and Halloran's voice raised for the first time all night, strong and contemptuous, saying words Tamsin could not make out and did not need to. A door slammed. The voice stopped.

Tamsin did not let himself think about that. Thinking was for later, if there was a later.

* * *

The garden wall was shoulder-high and slick with spray. Beyond it the oyster beds spread black under a half-clouded moon, a mile of mudflat stitched with the pale rows of cultch racks, and past them the harbor channel glimmering, and past that the warehouse roofs of the port where Maren waited with the pages. The tide was out; the mud sucked at the world.

He went over the wall and dropped the last three feet and the mud took him to mid-shin, cold as a grave, smelling of sulfur and salt rot. He got the typewriter above the muck, braced it against his hip, and moved. One foot, pull free, next foot. The shell hash underfoot bit through his boot soles at every step. Somewhere out on the flats a curlew cried and was answered, and he knew — the way you know a typebar is about to jam — that the answering cry was a man's whistle. Vos had not all gone to the front door.

He did not run. Running in oyster mud is how men die out there, stuck fast with the tide coming in; the divers' children learn that before they learn letters. He walked, fast and low, rack to rack, using the rows of stakes for cover, keeping the channel lights fixed over his left shoulder. Behind him, lanterns came down the garden wall and split into three lights fanning across the flats. One swept within forty yards of him and passed on. His breath tore. The sacking weighed a hundredweight. His legs were lead to the knee and he loved the mud for slowing the men behind him just as much as it slowed him.

Halfway across, stuck to the thigh, he allowed himself one look back. The nearest lantern had stopped at a rack thirty yards off, and a figure stood beside it, very still, head tilted — listening. Even at that distance, in that light, Tamsin knew the shape of the hat.

Vos said, conversationally, to the darkness: "You can't type in the mud, boy."

Tamsin pulled his leg free with a sound like a cork leaving a bottle and went on — because the man was right, and wrong, and it didn't matter, because the confession was dry under his coat and the morning mail coach left at six, and between him and the post office stood a mile of mud, a hunted father, a sister waiting in a warehouse loft, and whatever Vos had left to spend.

The tide, far out in the channel, had already begun to turn.
