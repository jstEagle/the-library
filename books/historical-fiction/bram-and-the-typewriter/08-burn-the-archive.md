## 8. Burn the Archive

Vos arrived at the company offices at four in the morning, before the gulls, before the clerks, before the light could see what he was doing. He carried a key ring of nine keys and a tin of lamp oil, and he had already decided which papers would die.

He did not think of it as burning evidence. He thought of it as correcting an error. Somewhere in the past eleven days the company's history had been written down wrong — in the wrong hand, on the wrong machine, with names attached to acts that had no witnesses living who mattered. His job was simply to bring the record back into agreement with the facts as the company required them. The facts were flexible. The fire was not.

The ledger room smelled of cold ink and dust. Vos set his lantern on the copying table — the same table where a boy named Tamsin Eriksen had sat for three years with his head down, copying manifests in a hand so neat it had once drawn a compliment from the harbor master himself. Vos remembered that. He remembered everything. It was the whole of his method: he kept accounts the way the company kept accounts, obsessively, in duplicate, and he had never once needed a ledger to do it. He carried his in his skull.

He began with the debt files.

The scheme had been elegant, as schemes went. Rig the divers' advances so they owed more each season than they earned. Let the debts pile until a man's boat, his gear, his sons' labor — all of it belonged to the company on paper. Then buy the rival fleet's divers the same way, drown them in obligations they could never service, and take their boats for pennies without firing a shot or cutting a throat. No blood in it anywhere. Only arithmetic. Arithmetic was legal. Arithmetic was respectable. A man could hang for murder and sleep sound after a morning of compound interest.

But arithmetic left paper. Paper was the flaw in every beautiful system, and now somewhere out there was a torn half-ledger and a letter typed on a machine whose keys had opinions of their own.

Vos fed the first file into the iron stove and watched the corners curl. Debt schedules for the eastern divers, 1908 through 1910. The second file followed: correspondence between Mr. Aldous Renwick — the architect, the clever one, now dying of something his partners had poured down his throat — and two directors whose names would look very fine on an indictment if anyone ever read them. Nobody would. That was the point of tonight.

He worked steadily, file by file, feeding the stove until the room glowed orange and sweat ran down his back under his coat. He burned manifests that showed altered tonnage. He burned wage sheets with the withholding columns rewritten. He burned the original indenture rolls' working copies — not the sealed masters, those were registered with the port authority and untouchable, but the drafts, the ones with corrections in margins, the ones where a clerk's pencil had noted *advance raised without authorization* beside certain names.

One name appeared in the margins more than any other. Eriksen. The father. A diver twenty years indentured, debt compounding like mold, marked in one draft as *cooperative — see R.*

Vos paused over that page before consigning it. *Cooperative.* The father had signed on to the scheme to save his own skin — agreed to lend his name among the conspirators, to be listed as a signatory when the fleet changed hands, in exchange for the erasure of his debt. And then the letter naming him had gone out from this very room, typed by his own son's fingers, and the man meant to receive it had turned up drowned under the pier with his boots full of harbor water.

Vos did not believe in coincidence. Coincidence was what lazy men called patterns they hadn't bothered to finish reading.

He fed the page to the flames and watched Eriksen's name blacken, curl, and go.

* * *

By seven the stove was ash and the ledger room looked untouched except for a smell of scorched paper that would fade by noon. Vos washed his hands in the basin, dried them on a clean cloth, folded the cloth into precise quarters, and went to find the family.

He knew where they lived. He had known since the second day, though he had not yet used the knowledge. There was an order to these things. You gathered everything first; you moved only when moving could accomplish something. Premature motion wasted leverage, and leverage was the only currency Vos had ever respected.

The Eriksen house stood on Rope Walk, third from the corner, leaning slightly seaward like everything else in the poor quarter. Vos knocked the way he did everything — evenly, without haste, a knock that said *I have all day and you do not.*

The father opened the door. Harl Eriksen was a big man gone lean, shoulders still broad but flesh melted off them by years of diving and short rations, hands scarred white at the fingertips from cold water. He saw Vos and Vos watched him understand, immediately, completely, what was standing on his step. Some men needed a speech. This one only needed a face.

"Mr. Vos," Eriksen said. "Company business?"

"May I come in."

It was not a question and they both let it be one anyway. Inside, the house smelled of salt and boiled fish and lamp oil. Two rooms downstairs. A girl — Maren, fourteen, sharp-eyed, standing too still by the stove, watching Vos the way small animals watch hawks — and an empty chair by the window with a jacket over its back. No son.

"Your boy isn't home," Vos observed.

"He works early tides sometimes."

"He works in my ledger room. I know his hours better than you do." Vos took the chair opposite the empty one, uninvited, and arranged himself. "I'll be plain with you, diver, because plainness saves time and time is the one thing running short. Eleven days ago a letter was typed in the company offices. Unsigned. It named certain men as parties to a plan concerning the fleets. Your name was in it. The letter has vanished. So has a typewriter. So, intermittently, has your son."

Eriksen said nothing. Maren had stopped breathing visibly, the way children do when they hope stillness will make them invisible.

"The man the letter was sent to is dead. Drowned. I don't believe in drowned men who can swim." Vos laced his fingers. "I also don't believe a sixteen-year-old clerk planned anything. He's a tool. Tools get picked up, tools get put away. What I want is simple: the machine, the pages, and the boy, delivered quietly, and this matter closes itself."

"And if we don't have them."

Vos smiled. It was not a warm operation. "Then I begin collecting debts. Yours stands at four hundred and twelve dollars against your indenture, which has nine years remaining. Your daughter is unregistered labor — the port authority takes such children into company service at thirteen, did you know? She's late for her own paperwork. And your son—" he paused, savoring the precision, "—your son is guilty of theft of company property, theft of correspondence, and obstruction of lawful investigation. In this port, diver, those words are whatever I say they are. I could make him a thief in the morning and a felon by supper."

"You'd burn us," Eriksen said quietly.

"I burn paper," Vos said. "This morning I burned a great deal of it. Files, records, drafts. Everything that ever tied certain names to certain arrangements is smoke now. Do you understand what that means?" He leaned forward. "It means the company no longer needs you cooperative. It means your name in that scheme protects you no longer — there's no document left worth protecting. You are not a conspirator anymore, Eriksen. You're a loose end. And loose ends, in my experience, end up like the man under the pier."

The stove ticked. Outside, a cart rattled past on Rope Walk, and for a moment the ordinary world seemed to insist on itself — fish being sold, nets mended, the tide coming in on schedule regardless of what happened in this room.

Then Eriksen said, "You have until dark. If my boy comes home, I'll speak to him."

"You'll speak to him now." Vos rose, straightened his coat. "Because I'm going to tell you exactly how this ends either way, so you can weigh it honestly. Give me the machine and the pages and the boy walks back into that ledger room with a caution on his file and his indenture intact, and your daughter stays off the registry, and your debt — I'll have the figure amended. Reduced. Considerably. Refuse, and I take all three of you apart piece by piece, starting with the easiest, and I start tomorrow."

At the door he stopped. He did not turn fully around; he gave them his profile, which was more mercy than most got.

"One more thing. Your son has been asking questions of divers and clerks. Taking testimony, I'm told. Typing it up somewhere." He let that sit in the air like weather coming. "A record, Eriksen. He's building a record. Think about what happens to a family that helps him finish it — and think about what happens to the family of a magistrate or an editor who receives one."

The door closed behind him with the soft click of a well-made latch.

* * *

Inside, neither Eriksen spoke for a long time. The stove ticked. The jacket stayed on the chair by the window, and the chair stayed empty, because Tamsin had not slept at home in six nights and both of them knew it.

Maren spoke first, in a whisper, though the street was empty. "He knows about the sheds. Or he will by dark."

"He knows enough." Her father stood at the window, watching Vos's gray coat recede down Rope Walk, unhurried, inevitable. "And he knows I'm in it. Was in it. Whatever's left of it now that the paper's burnt."

"Papa—"

"He offered me money to sell my own son." He said it flatly, testing the weight of it aloud. Behind his eyes was the arithmetic every indentured man lived by — debt against years, years against flesh — and for one long moment Maren watched her father actually calculate, actually stand there weighing his boy against four hundred and twelve dollars and nine years of his life, and hate himself for the weighing, and do it anyway, because that was what the company had made of him.

Then his fist came down on the windowsill hard enough to crack the wood.

"No," he said. "No. God forgive me — no."

He turned from the window, and something had settled in his face, the way silt settles after a storm, into a shape she had never seen there before. Resolve. Terror underneath it, but resolve on top, packed down hard.

"Maren. Listen. Tonight, before the tide turns, you find your brother. You tell him the fixer gave me until dark and I mean to spend every hour of it making sure dark comes too late for him. Tell him—" Harl Eriksen stopped, jaw working. Tell him his father is sorry. Tell him his father is done being silent. None of it would survive the trip.

"Tell him," he said instead, "his old man is finally going to be some use. And tell him to mail it. Whatever he's written — tell him to mail it before anyone can stop him."

Outside, far down the harbor road, a gray coat turned the corner toward the drying sheds, and the afternoon began spending itself toward dark.
