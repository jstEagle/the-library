## 9. The Night Mail

The tide turned at half past four, and the mail boat left with it. That gave us the dark hours to cross a town that had become, in the space of one burning afternoon, Vos's hunting ground.

Maren carried the satchel. I carried the typewriter.

It was heavier than I remembered — or my arms were weaker than they should have been after three nights without proper sleep — and it knocked against my hip with every step like a second heartbeat. Inside its case, wrapped in oilcloth, were the confession pages Halvard Brandt had dictated with his last breath, the original letter in my own typed hand, and Maren's copies made by candlelight under the floorboards: three sets, one for Magistrate Corren, one each for the *Coastal Argus* and the *Northern Standard*, and the third for a newspaper so far away it seemed almost mythical — the *Meridian Gazette*, whose editor had once printed something true about the pearl trade and paid for it with his position. If he was still there, he'd print this. If he wasn't, someone would find the letter on his desk eventually and be curious enough to read it.

And the half-ledger, torn down the spine, its columns showing the debt-rigging in figures no magistrate could ignore.

"You're limping," Maren whispered as we came off the sea wall onto Cobbler's Lane.

"I'm walking fast."

"You're limping *and* walking fast."

She took my elbow and steered me into the shadow between two drying sheds. Behind us, at the top of Harbour Street, a lantern swung. Not a watchman's rhythm — too quick, too deliberate. A man looking for something specific.

"Vos?"

"Or his dogs." She peered around the shed's edge. "He's got men on the quay already. I counted four when we crossed the fish market."

Four on the quay. The mail boat docked at the north pier, past all of them.

We stood in the dark breathing salt air and doing arithmetic neither of us wanted to finish.

"There's the other way," I said. "The warehouse district. The lane behind Kessler's rope works comes out at the pier stairs. It's longer, but it's roofed most of the way."

"It's also where he'll expect you to go."

"He expects me to run for the boat. He doesn't know about the pier stairs. Nobody uses them since the new customs house opened."

Maren looked at me for a long moment. In the dark I could just make out her face, sharp-boned and stubborn, our mother's face worn young by worry. "If we're caught," she said, "you give me the satchel and you run. You hear me? The record matters more than either of us."

"The record is *ours*."

"The record is *true*, Tamsin. That's better than ours. Truth can be picked up by anyone." She adjusted the strap across her chest. "Now come on. We're losing the tide."

* * *

Kessler's rope works smelled of tar and hemp, and the covered lane behind it smelled of rats. We moved through it in the blue-black hour before dawn, when even the company's fixers grow slow, and for twenty minutes I let myself believe we might actually do this.

Then the lamps came on ahead of us.

Three of them, spaced along the warehouse row, lit in sequence like someone opening a hand. And in the middle distance, framed in lamplight, a broad-shouldered figure standing very still, hands folded behind his back, waiting the way patient men wait.

Vos.

"Tamsin." His voice carried down the lane without effort, unhurried, almost kind. "I know it's you. The machine's impressions match your fingers. They've always matched. Come out and we'll discuss what your father's debt is worth."

Maren's grip tightened on my arm.

"That's a lie," she breathed. "Father's debt was settled years ago — that's what the ledger shows—"

"He doesn't care whether it's true. He cares whether Father believes it."

I did the only thing I could think of. I set the typewriter case down, lifted the satchel from Maren's shoulder, and pressed both into her arms.

"What are you—"

"The stairwell behind the cooperage. It drops to the water line. There's a gap in the pilings — you can wade under the pier and come up on the north side, past the customs house. He won't have men there."

"And you?"

"I'm going to make noise." My mouth had gone dry but my voice, oddly, held steady. "He wants me, not you. A girl running through the dark is an accident. A boy carrying a typewriter is evidence. Give him evidence."

"Tamsin—"

"Maren." I touched her face once, quickly, the way we hadn't touched since we were children. "Pick it up by anyone. Print it by anyone. Go."

She went. I heard her boots fade down the cooperage stairwell and then nothing, and then I picked up a loose bollard-weight from beside the rope works door and hurled it through the nearest warehouse window.

Glass exploded across the lane. Every lamp swung toward the sound.

I ran the opposite way, toward the harbour street end of the lane, and heard Vos shout — not angry, not surprised, just *directing*, the way a man directs cargo — and boots hammering behind me. Two sets. Maybe three.

I cut through the fish market, knocking over crates as I went, making myself loud, making myself worth chasing. The market stalls streamed past, tarpaulins and hanging scales and the reek of low tide, and I thought absurdly: *I have copied ten thousand manifests in that building and never once been thanked, and now I am running through it for my life.*

They caught me at the harbour steps.

A hand closed on my collar and yanked me backward so hard my feet left the ground. I twisted, struck out, connected with something soft — a grunt — and then another arm locked around my chest from behind and the world became lamplight and shouting and the smell of wet wool.

Vos walked toward me through it, unhurried, folding back his cuffs.

"Well," he said. "You're lighter than I expected."

* * *

What happened next, I saw from the ground, cheek pressed to cold stone, arms pinned.

Vos's men dragged me toward the customs house cellar — I knew the cellar; every indentured child in the port knew the cellar — and I was cataloguing exits, counting seconds, doing the desperate arithmetic of a boy who has mailed nothing yet, when a figure stepped out of the alley by the chandler's shop.

My father.

He was still in his diving clothes, still crusted with salt, and he moved like a man who had spent twenty years hauling his body up from the bottom of the sea and had decided, tonight, he would not go back down. In his right hand he held a boat hook.

"Let the boy go, Vos."

"Mr. Eriksen." Vos didn't even raise his voice. "Your debt—"

"My debt was *paid*." And here my father did something I had never seen him do: he smiled, and it was terrible. "You think I never checked? You think I spent nineteen years underwater and never once learned to count? I've known what you people were doing since before my son could write. I said nothing because I thought silence would keep them safe." He looked down at me, pinned and bleeding at the lip, and something in his face broke open. "Silence kept nobody safe. So tonight the silence ends."

He swung the boat hook into the nearest man's knee. Bone cracked. The man screamed and went down and my father was already turning, using the hook's length to keep the others at bay, backing toward me step by step, herding them away from my body the way he'd once herded me away from a moray hole on the reef.

"Run," he said, not looking at me. "North pier. Now."

"Father—"

"*Run.*"

I ran.

Behind me I heard the scuffle break apart and reform, heard Vos's voice go flat and procedural — *don't kill him, we need him talking* — and then a sound I will carry to my grave: the wet crack of something heavy against flesh, and my father's breath leaving him in a long, astonished rush, and then Maren screaming from somewhere near the water, high and furious, and the mail boat's bell ringing out across the black harbour like a judgment.

I reached the north pier with my lungs on fire. The gangway was being hauled in. A sailor — a stranger, grey-bearded, unimpressed by anything — looked down at me as I clawed at the rail.

"Passage?"

"I'm posting mail." I thrust the satchel up at him, all four packets, tied and addressed in Maren's careful hand. "It *has* to go. Magistrate Corren, and these—" the newspapers. My voice cracked. "Please."

He looked at the addresses a long moment. Somewhere behind us, in the warehouse district, the shouting had stopped, which was somehow worse than the shouting.

"Corren, is it." He took the satchel. "Old Anders Corren?"

"Yes."

"Hm. He hanged my brother's employer in 'ninety-eight." The satchel disappeared inside his coat. "Consider it posted."

The gangway dropped again, just wide enough, and I went through it and fell to my knees on deck as the lines came off and the tide took us.

Maren found me there, soaked to the waist from the pilings, shaking. We clung to each other at the rail while the port shrank behind us — the ledger room windows dark, the drying sheds dark, the whole town going dark except for one light still burning in the customs house, and beside it, on the stones of the quay, a shape lying very still that two sailors had to carry aboard at the last possible moment, laid out on the deck planks, breathing shallowly, alive.

Barely. But alive.

"They have him," Maren whispered. "They have Father."

"No." I watched the harbour lights sink below the horizon, my hand resting on the typewriter case between us. "They had him. Now the record does."

The mail boat ran east with the morning tide, and somewhere behind us the sun came up on a town that thought it had caught us — and did not yet know what we'd set loose.
