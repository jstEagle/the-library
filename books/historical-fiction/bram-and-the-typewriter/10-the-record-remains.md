## 10. The Record Remains

The magistrate's ruling came down on a Tuesday, read aloud in the courthouse square because the courtroom couldn't hold everyone who wanted to hear it. Tamsin stood at the back, hat in his hands, and listened to a man in gray robes say the word *indicted* nine times — once for each director of the Meridian Pearl & Trading Company.

*Voided.* That was the word for the indentures. He counted that one too. It came four times, once for each contract the court struck from existence, and when it came for the Eriksen name the crowd near him shifted, some of them looking around to see who it was, and Tamsin kept his eyes on the rostrum like a man watching weather.

Four years. His signature at the bottom of a page he'd been too young to read properly, his father's mark beside it because his father couldn't write. Four years of his labor owned by men who had spent the same years arranging drownings on paper. The judge called it *involuntary servitude under fraudulent pretense*, and the phrase went through the crowd like wind through wheat, and somewhere in it Tamsin felt something tear loose that had been sewn into him so long he'd mistaken it for skin.

His father wasn't there. He was still in the charity ward with the wound Vos's knife had left him, though Vos himself was three provinces gone by then — fled the night the indictments were announced, according to the papers, taking nothing but a carpetbag and whatever he'd skimmed over fifteen years of making other men disappear. The newspapers had a sketch of him that looked like forty different men. Tamsin studied it anyway, every morning, out of a habit he didn't trust himself to break.

Maren found him after the reading. She didn't say anything about the ruling. She took his elbow and steered him through the crowd the way she'd been steering him since they were children, and only when they were clear of the square did she say, "Well," in the flat voice she used when feeling would cost too much.

"Well," he said.

They walked down to the water because there was nowhere else in this town that didn't belong to somebody the company had owned or bought, and stood on the shingle where the drying sheds still leaned together like old drunkards. One of them was theirs now, in the way of things nobody wanted. Inside, under the floorboard where he'd hidden it through the worst months of his life, the typewriter waited in its crate.

"You could leave it here," Maren said. She said it gently, which meant she'd thought about saying it cruelly and decided against. "Burn it. Sink it. Let the sea have it."

He crouched and lifted the board. The machine sat in its packing straw, dull with salt haze, the ribbon long since dried to a black crust. He lifted it out and set it on the upturned barrel they'd been using as a table, and his fingers found the keys without his asking them to.

They were worn. That was the thing he noticed every time and noticed again now: the *e* polished silver, the *t* and *n* and *a* gone pale as bone where ten thousand strikes had worn the lacquer through. His keys. His impressions. The company clerk who'd ordered the letter typed had chosen him precisely because his touch was light and fast and left no smear — and the machine remembered every word he'd ever given it, including the ones that had damned him, including the ones that had saved him.

"It convicted eleven men," he said. "It exonerated six divers whose debts weren't real." He ran his thumb along the space bar, warped slightly where he'd gripped it running through the warehouse district with the case against his ribs and Vos's footsteps behind him. "It's the truest thing I own."

"It's also the reason we can't stay."

That was the truth under everything, and they both knew it. Justice had come, but it had come loud. Every paper in three counties carried the Eriksen name now — the boy diver who typed the record. There were people who shook his hand in the street with wet eyes, and there were others, quieter, whose brothers or fathers had taken company coin to keep the ledgers dirty, and those ones looked at him the way men look at a door they're not sure is locked. The company's creditors circled what remained. Its remaining clerks needed work and blamed the testimony for the absence of it. And Vos was out there somewhere with a carpetbag and a long memory and no more need for procedure.

Their father could dive again, barely. But no boat in the harbor would sign a man whose name appeared in the trial transcripts, not as conspirator — he'd been cleared, turned crown's evidence in the end, bled for it — but as *Eriksen*. The name itself had become a kind of debt.

"We go east," Tamsin said. "Inland. Where nobody dives and nobody types."

"Where will you type, then?"

"I don't know yet."

She looked at the machine a long moment. Then she reached into her coat pocket and put a railway timetable on the barrel between them, folded to the right page, because she had known the answer before she asked the question and had already done the work of it. That was Maren. Sixteen months of typing testimony by candlelight had taught him that his sister's love arrived dressed as logistics.

"The Thursday train," she said. "Third class. Father's fit to travel by then if the doctor says so, and I've told the doctor he'll say so."

*

They left on a morning of low fog, the harbor dissolving above the waterline like a pencil drawing someone had breathed on. Their father said nothing until the town was gone. Then, facing the window, his hands — scarred, shortened at two fingers by rope and reef — resting on his knees, he said:

"I should have told you. That night. About the letter, about what I'd let them bind me into. You were a child carrying my sin in your satchel and I let you carry it because telling you frightened me more than the drowning did."

Tamsin watched the coast unspool backward. "You bought us time," he said. "In the warehouses. With your blood."

"I bought minutes. You bought the truth. Don't you dare make us even."

The old man's voice cracked on the last word, and Tamsin looked away, out at the fog, and let him have the privacy of it. Somewhere behind them the port went on existing — the fleet reflagged, the oyster beds leased to new men with new ledgers, the graves on the headland keeping their count. He would remember it the way the typewriter remembered: exactly, mechanically, whether he wished to or not.

They changed trains twice and rode a third line into country where the hills had no shells in them. On the second evening they came down off the ridge into a river town called Marrow Bend — a mill town, timber town, nothing to do with the sea at all, which was its whole recommendation. A main street of false-fronted shops. A church with a bell gone green. Boarding house with rooms to let, run by a widow who looked at their travel-worn faces and asked no questions beyond the rate.

That first night Tamsin couldn't sleep. He lay in the narrow bed listening to a river instead of tides, an entirely different sound, softer, less patient, and after an hour he got up and brought the typewriter to the table under the window and uncovered it in the moonlight.

He hadn't planned what he meant to do. He rolled in a fresh sheet — he'd bought paper in the city during the changeover, good rag stock — and sat with his fingers on the home row, and for a long time he typed nothing at all.

Then he began.

Not a letter. Not testimony. Not a manifest or a confession or any document with a seal at the bottom and a man's freedom hanging from it. Just this:

*Marrow Bend, the first night.*

*My name is Tamsin Eriksen. I was born in a port town you will never see, and for sixteen years I belonged to a piece of paper. This is what happened after the paper burned.*

He stopped. Read it back. The letters stood on the page in his own particular impressions, the worn *e*, the bone-pale *a*, the small crooked *k* that no other machine in the world would make quite the same way — the mark of him, unmistakable, the thing that had nearly hanged him and had finally, at the last, set him free.

There was a life in it yet, he understood suddenly. The machine. Not just as evidence, not just as weapon, but as the place where he thought best, the way some men thought best walking or mending nets. Whatever he had witnessed — the drownings, the ledger room, the dying architect dictating with Vos's boots on the stairs below — none of it was finished with him. Testimony ended when the gavel fell. Witness didn't.

Down the hall, his father coughed once and settled. The river said its soft endless word against the pilings. In the morning there would be a town to learn, work to find, a family to build out of what the fire had left. Vos was still somewhere under the sky, and the story was still somewhere ahead of them both, unfinished as the sheet in the carriage.

Tamsin set his fingers on the keys and, in the dark, in a town where no one knew his name, wrote the next line.
