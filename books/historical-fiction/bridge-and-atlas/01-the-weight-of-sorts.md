## 1. The Weight of Sorts

My father taught me that a letter weighs nothing and everything. A single sort — a lowercase *e* in ten-point Baskerville, say — sits in your palm like a beetle's husk. Nothing. But set forty thousand of them into a forme, ink it, pull it through the press, and you can topple a minister or bury a man so deep his own mother won't recognize him at the graveside. Everything.

I was thinking about this particular piece of inherited wisdom on a Tuesday morning in March, because I had just hidden three plates of seditious pamphletry inside my father's funeral type, and I wanted very much to believe he'd have approved.

He would have. That was the trouble with him. Dead eleven months now, and still he kept approving of things I did, which is either the mark of a good education or a sign I should get out more.

The funeral type was his own indulgence, if you want to know. Most printers keep their black-letter faces for obituaries and proclamations, but Father had commissioned an entire case of them — heavy, gothic, funereal sorts with serifs like clenched teeth — back when he was young and morbid and flush from printing hymnals. "For the day they print my name," he told me once, laughing. He said it the way men say things they don't mean to happen soon. The regime obliged him within the year, though not with a death notice. With a knock at the door, a fever that went to his lungs, and an invoice from the undertaker that I am still paying off in quarterly installments, which strikes me as the regime's most efficient cruelty: they kill you and let your daughter fund it.

So the black-letter case sat in the corner of the shop, mostly empty, mostly mourned over by nobody but me. And now it held, beneath a false bottom of broken sorts and a layer of dust I applied by hand every morning with a squirrel-hair brush, three zinc plates. Each plate bore eight pages of a pamphlet titled *The Bread We Are Owed*, which argued — politely, in the way that makes censors nervous — that the grain levy imposed last autumn was theft, that the ministers who ordered it had names and addresses, and that printers were under no obligation whatsoever to pretend otherwise.

The plates came to me wrapped in oilcloth, left under a sack of rags by a boy who claimed to be delivering tallow. I never saw his face twice. That's how it works in Vellmora now: everything arrives by accident, everything leaves by night, and everyone is innocent until approximately four o'clock.

I pressed the false bottom down, scattered the broken sorts across it — a shattered ligature here, a cracked asterisk there, the debris of thirty years of honest work — and brushed my dust over all of it. Then I stood back and admired my father's coffin type doing the one thing he never intended: keeping something alive.

"You're talking to the furniture again."

Doss. Of course Doss. Our foreman had a genius for arriving precisely when a person wanted to be alone, and a genius for standing in doorways as though the door owed him money. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with forearms like hams and eyes like two wet stones — the kind of eyes that never blinked when you expected them to. Forty years in the trade. He'd set type beside my father since before I was born, and he liked to remind me of it the way other men remind you of debts.

"I was inventorying," I said.

"At seven in the morning."

"Early inventorying catches the moths asleep."

He grunted. Doss communicated in grunts the way monks communicate in silence — extensively, and always about himself. He came into the shop proper, boots loud on boards worn pale by three generations of Oyelaran feet, and looked at the black-letter case with an expression I couldn't read, which frightened me, because I could usually read anyone who touched this shop. It was a small talent, reading people, and I'd honed it out of necessity: when your family prints pamphlets the government calls sedition and you call Tuesday, you learn to watch faces the way sailors watch weather.

Doss's face was weather I didn't know.

"The levy protest is Saturday," he said. "They'll be printing handbills somewhere. Not here, I hope."

"Here we print wedding invitations," I said. "And a memorial program for the Harrow Street choral society. Very dull. You'd sleep through it."

"I sleep fine." He picked up a composing stick from the stone — mine, set with the morning's job, a christening notice for the Vassek twins — and turned it over as if checking my measure. My measure was correct. My measure was always correct; it was the first thing Father ever beat into me, gently, with words instead of hands: *Casper, a bad em is a lie, and lies accumulate interest.* Doss set the stick down a half-inch out of alignment anyway, just to remind me he could.

"Doss," I said. "Is there a reason you're haunting my stone at dawn?"

"Just came for the quoins." He took a fistful of steel quoin keys from the drawer, jangling them like a miser counting coins, and paused at the door on his way out. He did that too — paused. "Your father would be proud of how you run this place. Mostly."

"Mostly," I repeated, but he was already gone, and his boots went down the stairs slower than they'd come up, which meant he'd been thinking. Doss thinking was a sound I was learning to fear more than any boot on our stairs at midnight.

* * *

After he left I did what I always do when the world turns strange: I measured something.

This sounds mad. I know it sounds mad. But hear me out, because it isn't madness, it's grammar. Every printer lives inside a system of measurement older than most kingdoms — points and picas, ems and ens — and inside that system everything has its place. An em quad fills the gap after a sentence; an en quad fills the space between words; and if you're setting justified type, you learn to distribute your spaces so evenly that no line looks hungry. Hunger shows. Hunger is what gets a page rejected, a reader suspicious, a censor interested.

So when Doss paused in my doorway with wet-stone eyes, I went to the case and set a line just to feel my hands remember order: *THE QUICK BROWN FOX JUMPS OVER THE LAZY DOG*, the pangram every apprentice cuts their teeth on, and then, because nobody was watching, I broke it apart and reset it as: *THE DEAD DOG OUTLIVES THE QUICK FOX*. Which is nonsense, typographically speaking, but it made my chest hurt in a way that felt honest.

Because that was Father's real catechism, the one under the one about ems. He used to stand behind me at the case — I can still feel where his breath landed on my neck, warm and smelling of lampblack — and he'd say: *Letters outlive men, little Casper. Kings die, ministers fall, and somewhere in some case a sort they commissioned is still sitting there waiting. Be careful whose letters you set, because you'll be dead and they'll still be talking.*

He meant it as a warning against vanity commissions. Set no tyrant's name in gold. But warnings are like type: turn them upside down and they read differently. I'd spent eleven months turning his warning over, and what it said to me, upside down, was this: if letters outlive men, then letters are how you murder-proof the truth. You put a thing in type, you cast it, you scatter ten thousand copies into a city of ninety thousand hands, and no minister alive can recall them all. The regime burns pamphlets. Fine. Paper burns in minutes. But the memory of a sentence, once read, is printed on something the censors haven't found a solvent for.

That was why the plates slept in the funeral case. Not because I was brave. Because I was arithmetic. Three plates, eight pages each, times whatever press time I could steal between wedding invitations — it wasn't courage, it was a distribution problem, and I have never met a distribution problem I didn't want to solve.

I finished the morning's legitimate work — the choral society memorial, which required me to set *IN LOVING REMEMBRANCE* in a face so sentimental I felt I needed a bath afterward — and ate lunch standing at the window, watching the paper district do its slow gray business. Rag carts. Apprentices with drying racks. Across the canal, the Customs House clock ticking toward whatever the regime had scheduled next. Somewhere past the river, past the Bridge of Atlas with its soldiers and its scales for weighing every cart that crossed, there were men called the printers' council who were supposed to be organizing all of us quiet criminals into something louder. I'd never met one. In Vellmora, the council was less an organization than a rumor with excellent posture.

The afternoon brought actual customers — Mrs. Vassek wanting the christening notice re-inked darker because her husband was going blind and she refused to read it aloud twice — and ordinary work has this wonderful property of making the extraordinary seem survivable. By dusk I'd forgotten Doss's pause. Almost.

Then he stayed late.

Doss never stayed late. Doss had a wife in Tallow Lane, a dog with opinions, and a tavern schedule regulated like the Customs House clock. But that evening he remained at the proof press, ostensibly cleaning it, and I watched him in the long mirror we keep by the delivery door — a mirror for checking composed pages in reverse, and, apparently, for checking foremen — and I watched him look at the black-letter case.

He looked at it the way a man looks at a safe he's decided he can crack.

Not now, his posture said. Not tonight. But soon, and I know exactly what's inside, or I suspect exactly what's inside, or I'm being paid by someone who suspects.

I blew out the lamp by the stone, wished him a good night in a voice I kept pleasant and level, and listened to his boots descend. At the bottom, they stopped. For a count of five. Then they continued, out the door, into the street, away.

Five seconds of silence at the foot of my stairs. That's what I carried up to bed that night, along with the day's worries: five seconds of a man deciding something.

* * *

I couldn't sleep, so I went down.

This is a habit Father would have scolded and secretly shared. There's a lamp behind the third case that doesn't show from the street, and there is a particular peace in a print shop at midnight that I cannot explain to civilians. All the day's noise settles into the wood. The presses sit like sleeping animals. And the cases — hundreds of thousands of sorts, lead and antimony and tin, each letter cast in its own image, each one patient — the cases hum. Not audibly. But if you've grown up among them you can feel it, the way you feel weather coming in an old scar.

I lit the hidden lamp and took out the funeral case's false bottom and looked at the plates, because looking at contraband at midnight is either madness or prayer, and I've stopped distinguishing.

Eight pages. Somebody had written these — some hand I'd never meet, some voice arguing for bread and dignity in sentences tight enough to survive a censor's shears — and somebody had engraved the plates, and somebody had smuggled them to a woman who inherited her father's shop and his habits, and now the whole chain of somebodies hung on whether I had the nerve to pull proofs and distribute them before Saturday's protest. Before the city marched about bread while knowing nothing of the names behind the levy.

I was re-sealing the case when I heard the knock.

Not the street door — the water door. Every shop on Canal Row has one, a loading door opening on the water, and ours hadn't been used since Father died, since the barges stopped calling. The knock came again: soft, deliberate, twice, then a pause too long to be accidental.

Midnight knocks at water doors are never good news. They are either the constabulary, in which case I should blow out the lamp and pretend to be furniture, or they are trouble of the unofficial kind, in which case I should also blow out the lamp, but for different reasons.

Instead — and I blame the hour, and the plates, and the five seconds of Doss's silence, and my father's voice saying *be careful whose letters you set* — I blew out the lamp, crossed the dark shop, and opened the door.

The river gave me a woman. Soaked to the waist, gray-lipped with cold, gripping the doorframe with both hands like the frame might testify on her behalf. Behind her, the canal lay flat and black and indifferent, already erasing the wake of whatever she'd crossed it in.

She looked up at me, and even half-drowned and shivering she managed the expression of someone apologizing in advance for what she was about to say.

"Casper Oyelaran?" she said. Her voice had rust in it. "I need a printer. And I think—" She stopped, swallowed, started again. "I think you knew my brother."

Which is how, at half past midnight on an ordinary Tuesday in March, with three plates of sedition sleeping under my father's coffin type and my foreman's silence ringing in my ears, I learned that Aldous Vey — hanged by the state six months ago, his execution printed in every gazette in Vellmora, his name already passing into the martyrs' litany — had sent me a letter.

But I'm ahead of myself. First I had to decide whether to let a drowned stranger into the only place in the world where I kept everything I wasn't supposed to have.

I stepped aside. God forgive me, or thank me — the verdict's still out. I stepped aside.
