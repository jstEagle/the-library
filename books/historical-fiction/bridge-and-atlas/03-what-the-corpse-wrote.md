## 3. What the Corpse Wrote

There is a particular skill to stealing from a room full of dead men's papers, and it is not the skill you'd imagine. It isn't nerve. It isn't sleight of hand. It is patience — the patience to sit in a records office for eleven days copying depositions nobody reads, in a hand so deliberately dull that the clerks stopped looking at me by the third morning, and then to wait, quill drying in the air like any other tired scrivener, while the man whose job it was to fetch the execution files went to his supper.

His name was Pell. I want that recorded somewhere: Pell, who had ink on his fingers and a widow's cravat knotted wrong and a cough he kept meaning to have seen to. He was not a wicked man. He was a tired one, and tired men leave keys in locks.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. The woman across the table from me had just asked how my brother could have written anything at all, given that her city had watched him hanged in Printer's Square eight months ago, and she deserved an answer before she deserved a chronology.

"My brother," I said, "is either alive or he is the most industrious ghost in Vellmora. Either way, the letter is real. I watched the hanging from the second balcony of the Salt Exchange. Everyone did. They put him on the scaffold and they read the sentence and they dropped him and the crowd made that sound — you know the sound, everyone makes it, you can't help it." Casper Oyelaran didn't nod, but something behind her eyes moved, so I continued. "And then the body came down very quickly, quicker than custom allows, and it went into a covered cart instead of a common pit, which is also against custom, because the regime wanted him buried quietly. You don't bury a martyr quietly unless you're afraid of where the digging leads."

The shop smelled of ink and tallow and, underneath both, of dust settling on ten thousand pieces of lead. It was past midnight. Rain still dripped off me onto her floorboards and I confess I watched each drop darken the wood with the mild vindictiveness of a woman who has walked a long way wet and intends to be forgiven for it.

"You said you copied documents," Casper said. "For eleven days."

"I'm a clerk by trade. Or I was. Records office on Candlemaker's Row, the annex where they keep everything too sensitive to burn but too boring to read." I turned the letter toward her again, face down on the table between us like a losing card. "Three months ago a deposition came through that wasn't boring. A turnkey's statement, taken after the fact, sealed under the ministerial cipher. Clerks aren't supposed to read sealed things. We do anyway; it's the only entertainment the job affords. I read it twice, put it back exactly as found, and began planning."

"Planning what?"

"How to get a copy out of a building that searches every clerk at the gate."

* * *

Here is what I learned in eleven days in that office, and what I told Casper Oyelaran while the candle burned down between us:

The hanging was staged. Aldous Vey stood on that scaffold, and the crowd saw a man drop, but the rope was rigged and the body in the cart — if there ever was a body — was never shown, never photographed by the new machines, never laid out for the customary public viewing. The turnkey's deposition said so plainly. Said that Vey was taken down within the minute, breathing, drugged to limpness, carried out through the prison yard before the crowd finished its horrified noise. Said that the order came from above, and named the names.

Three names. Minister of Interior Corvane. Minister of Justice Hallam — no, forgive me, the pronunciation and the spelling diverge in this city and I had copied it letter by letter — *Halle*. And the third, the one that surprised even me, the Deputy Provost of the Garrison, Serle. Small men, all three. That's the thing nobody tells you about conspiracies: they are rarely run by monsters. They are run by administrators. Men who decided that killing Aldous Vey publicly would break the movement, and that keeping him secretly alive was cheaper than the risk of a corpse being exhumed and examined by someone persistent.

"They didn't kill him because they hated him," I said. "They killed him because it was efficient. And then they didn't kill him because it turned out a living Vey in a cell was useful leverage against the council — proof they could produce, or threaten to produce, or let rot. He's alive, Casper. Somewhere in the garrison cells or some estate upriver, writing letters in a hand I've known since I was six years old, and the whole city lit candles for a corpse."

Casper had gone very still. She had been still since I said the word *staged*, actually, still in the way I've since learned is characteristic of printers: the stillness of a person checking something character by character.

"And your copy," she said. "How?"

"The gate search looks for paper in bags, in linings, in boot heels. It does not look at the work product of the clerks themselves, because clerks carry documents in and out constantly and stopping them would collapse the entire apparatus of government inside a week. So I didn't smuggle the letter out. I smuggled the letter *past*. I copied it in the office hand — the bland, looping secretariat script every clerk is drilled in — on office paper, with an office heading, and I walked out at end of shift carrying it openly in a folder of genuine depositions, yawning, hoping my yawn looked genuine." I smiled without much humor. "It did. Tiredness is the best disguise in the world. Nobody interrogates a woman who looks like sleep itself would be an improvement."

"And then the original hand?"

I slid the letter across to her, face up at last. "That I wrote at a safehouse over four nights, copying my own copy, matching the original deposition's script as closely as these old fingers manage. But the *body* of it — the confession, the names, the account of the scaffold — that's Aldous's words, transcribed. And the last page—" I tapped it. "The last page is in his true hand. Pell left the files unattended for his supper on the twelfth day, and I went back for the original itself. One page only. The page I judged most dangerous and least likely to be missed immediately. His own covering note, written in the cell, meant for whoever finally received the deposition. It took me nine more days to arrange the exit. I resigned. Gave notice like an honest woman. Walked out the gate for the last time with my reference letter in my pocket and my brother's handwriting folded into the false bottom of a hatbox, along with two hats I genuinely owned, because nothing allays suspicion quite like millinery."

Casper looked at the page. Her hands hovered over it the way I'd later see her hover over everything in that shop — with respect, as though paper could bruise.

"This is his hand," she said quietly. "I set proofs for *The Commonweal* two years ago. Compositor's holiday — the regular man had the fever. I set eleven pages of Aldous Vey's corrections in his own margin notes." She touched the edge of the page, not the writing. "He crossed his sevens. Look — here, and here. Most people don't bother. And the descender on the *y*, always looped left, like it's trying to get away from the word."

Something in my chest unlocked at that. I hadn't realized I'd been holding it. For four months I had carried certainty alone — certainty about the letter, about the staging, about the names — and certainty carried alone goes stale, curdles into doubt, and doubt into a quiet conviction that you are mad. This ink-stained stranger with rain in her hair had known my brother only through his typography, and she knew him more intimately in one glance than the ministers who signed his death knew him in their lives.

"It matches?" I asked. Foolish question. I could see it matched.

"Wait." She rose, took a lamp, and went to the far wall where shallow wooden trays hung in rows — her type cases, dozens of them, labeled in a small precise hand. She pulled down one marked in faded chalk: *V — specimens.* From a drawer beneath she brought a broad sheet, foxed with age, printed in several sizes: a founder's specimen book, or part of one, and pinned to it older proofs gone brown at the edges.

She spread them on the table beside the letter. "These are pull-proofs. Authors' corrections, fair copies, the scraps that survive when editions die. Printers keep them the way families keep portraits." Her finger moved from specimen to letter and back, unhurried, thorough, and I understood I was watching not a comparison but a cross-examination, and that my brother's handwriting was the witness.

"The sevens cross," she murmured. "The *y* flees left. Here — look at the *th*. He doesn't lift the pen between them; most writers don't, but look how the crossbar dips, here and here and here. Same dip. Same slight drag where the nib catches on the upstroke — he pressed hard, always did, Vey wrote like a man carving." She straightened. "And this word. *Efficient.* He uses it twice in your letter. In the margins of the 1841 proofs he used it three times in one paragraph. It's his word. People have vocabularies like fingerprints."

She sat back. The candle guttered, steadied.

"It's him," she said. "Or it's the finest forgery I've ever seen, forged by someone who knew his hand better than I do." Her eyes came up to mine, and they were not unkind, but they were not soft either. "So give me the rest. All of it. Why bring it to a half-dead print shop in the Paper District instead of to the council directly? Why me?"

"Because the council is being watched, and I couldn't reach them without walking into whatever net caught the others. Because your father printed for the movement before the crackdown, and died doing it, and his shop still stands, which means it's overlooked, which means it's *useful*." I paused. "And because I needed someone who could verify the hand. If I'm carrying a forgery — even a forgery made in good faith — the whole thing collapses and takes the movement with it. You were the only person in Vellmora I could think of who'd know."

"And if it's true?"

"Then three ministers conspired to fake an execution of the most famous man in the city, and are holding him alive as leverage, and the letter proves it, and the movement has spent eight months mourning on command. When the printers' council reads those names—" I heard my own voice go flat and hard, and I let it. "Corvane. Halle. Serle. When those names are read aloud in a square full of people, this city will come apart at the seams. I've thought about that every night for four months. I want it anyway. I want it *more*."

* * *

What I did not say, and what sat in me like swallowed glass: that I had rehearsed doubt on the river barge all the way down from the capital district. That in the dark of the hold, wedged between crates of tallow, I had taken the letter out and looked at the crossing of the sevens until my eyes watered, asking it to tell me it was false, because a false letter was survivable. A false letter got me hanged alone and left the world intact. A true letter meant my brother had been in a cell for eight months while I taught children their letters in a border town and told myself I'd done all a sister could.

A true letter meant the sound the crowd made in Printer's Square — that sound I hear at the top of every stairwell now — had been made over nothing. Made over a trick. My brother heard it too, wherever they took him. I'd bet my life he heard it.

Casper was reading the letter again, lips moving slightly. I watched her arrive at the passage I knew by heart, the one where Aldous — dry even in chains, dry as bone — observed that the state had at last achieved the perfect censorship: not banning the press but *executing* it, since a dead editor required no license. She laughed. Actually laughed, one short breath through her nose, and then pressed her hand over her mouth like the laugh had escaped custody.

"He was like that at dinner too," I said. "You never knew whether to feed him or arrest him."

"Both," she said. "My father said you always did both with Vey. Feed him and watch him."

She fell quiet then, and I watched grief move through her — grief for my brother, whom she'd never met, and beneath it the older grief for her father, which she wore the way printers wear ink, worked into the grain. And I watched the grief do what grief does in certain people: harden. I could nearly see it set, like plaster, like type metal cooling in the mould, taking an impression.

"We can't just carry it," she said at last. "A letter is one object. Objects get seized. Objects get lost, burned, buried in evidence vaults for twenty years while lawyers argue." She looked up, and her voice dropped into something steadier and more frightening than fear: certainty. "If we take this to the council, we don't deliver a letter. We deliver a *text*. Something that can be set, proofed, and running off a hundred presses before the garrison finishes its breakfast. It has to exist in ten thousand hands by noon or it exists in none."

"You're saying print it."

"I'm saying that's the only way a letter from a dead man matters." She gathered the pages with both hands, squared their edges against the tabletop — an unconscious gesture, a compositor's gesture, making type of them already. "And printing it means moving it to the council presses across the river, past three checkpoints, in a city where the man who ordered this runs the garrison. Do you understand what you've brought into my shop tonight, Ilse Vey?"

"A reason," I said.

"A death warrant with elegant descenders." But she was already standing, already turning toward the cases on the wall, and there was something almost like hunger in the way she looked at them. "We'll need passes. Cover. And we'll need to move before the records office notices what's missing from Vey's file."

I did not tell her then that the records office would notice sooner than either of us liked — that Pell's cough had been getting worse, that seals had a way of being checked after holidays, that I had already seen, from the barge, smoke over Candlemaker's Row that might have been a chimney and might not. There is such a thing as telling someone bad news in the correct order, and I am my brother's sister: I believe in sequence, in setting the type before inking the plate.

"Tomorrow," I said. "Tonight we sleep, if sleeping's on offer. Tomorrow you can show me what a forge and a customs pass have in common."

She showed me to a cot in the back room, among the paper stock, and I lay in the dark listening to the building settle and the river muttering somewhere below the hill, and I thought: *she'll do it.* I had watched her test the hand for a quarter hour without once touching the ink, and I knew the breed. Give a printer the truth and she won't admire it. She'll want to multiply it.

Somewhere across the city, in whatever room they kept him, Aldous was perhaps asleep, perhaps awake, not knowing that his words had crossed the river in a hatbox and were lying in a print shop smelling of tallow and defiance, about to be broken into individual letters and reassembled ten thousand times over.

They had killed him to stop his voice. By tomorrow night, God willing, it would be the loudest thing in Vellmora.

I slept. It was the last good sleep for some weeks, though of course I didn't know that yet — and when Casper woke me near dawn, there was a man's silhouette in the doorway behind her, and she was wearing the particular smile of a woman about to lie to someone she distrusted, and she said, "Ilse, this is Doss, our foreman. Doss, this is a cousin of mine from upriver."

And I understood that the next performance had already begun.
