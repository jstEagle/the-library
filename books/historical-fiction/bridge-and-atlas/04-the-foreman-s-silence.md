## 4. The Foreman's Silence

My father used to say that a print shop has two kinds of silence: the good kind, when the press is running and the whole building hums like a hive that's made its peace with the beekeeper, and the bad kind, which is any silence Doss is standing in.

The morning after Ilse Vey climbed out of the river and into my life, I woke to the bad kind.

I came down the back stairs at half past five, the letter folded flat against my ribs where my stays should have been — I'd decided some years ago that a corset was a poor investment compared to a hiding place — and Doss was already at the composing stone, sorting type he had no order to sort. That was my first clue. Doss never touched the cases before seven unless something was wrong. He claimed it was devotion to the craft. A man who loved the craft would not hold each sort up to the window light the way he did, angling it like a jeweler checking for flaws in a stone he intended to swap.

"Casper." He didn't look up. "You were late coming down."

"I slept badly."

"Rats again?"

"Something like that."

He set the sort down with a click that was far too precise for a man pretending indifference. Doss had been my father's foreman for nineteen years, which in Vellmora meant he'd known me since I was tall enough to reach the lower cases, and it meant — or I had always believed it meant — that his loyalty ran along the same channels as the shop's ink supply: old, settled, taken for granted. My father trusted him with the ledger keys. My father trusted him with the names of customers who paid in nothing but gratitude. When the fever took Papa last spring, it was Doss who carried the coffin's front corner, and Doss who wept, or produced something that performed weeping convincingly enough that nobody asked follow-up questions.

"You've got a visitor," he said. "Slept in the storeroom loft, I take it. Your river woman."

"She's not my river woman. She's a client."

"At half past ten at night? Through the ink door?" He picked up another sort, held it to the light. "We're very devoted to our clients these days."

There it was, laid out between us like a proof sheet: he knew. Of course he knew. You cannot smuggle a soaked stranger past a man who sleeps one floor below the storeroom, and I hadn't even tried, which in hindsight was either trust or sloppiness, and I no longer felt qualified to tell the difference.

"Doss," I said, "how long have you been at this shop?"

"Nineteen years. You know that."

"And in nineteen years, how many times have I asked you to forget something you saw?"

He smiled, which he rarely did with his whole face; usually just one corner, like a door left ajar. "Never. Which is why I'm asking now what needs forgetting."

* * *

Here is what I knew about the letter, as of that morning, standing in my own shop with my own foreman watching me the way a customs clerk watches a cart:

It was written by a dead man. I had set Aldous Vey's proofs twice, three years apart, and a compositor's hands remember handwriting the way a singer's throat remembers a melody — the forward lean of the capitals, the way he crossed his t's like a man slashing through an argument he intended to win. Ilse had put that page on my stone and my stomach had dropped through three floors of the building. Dead men do not write letters. Dead men especially do not write letters naming the three ministers who signed the order for their staged execution, with dates, amounts paid to two prison officials, and a sentence — I could still see it, would see it every time I closed my eyes for the rest of my life — reading *I write this in the hand they believe burned, so let it burn them instead.*

And here is what I knew about Doss: he had offered to help before I asked.

In nineteen years, Doss had never once volunteered assistance for anything outside his wage. He grumbled about overtime. He billed my father's ghost for candles. And now, unprompted, over a case of sorts he had no business touching, he was offering to forget things. In my experience, people offer to keep secrets in exactly two circumstances: when they want something, or when they already know everything and are enjoying the view from inside.

So I lied to him. It was easier than I expected, which frightened me more than the lie itself.

"It's a debt paper," I said. "Her family's. There are men in this city who'd forge anything to get out from under the levy collectors, and if she's carrying something that looks official, she needs it to look unofficial fast. That's all."

"A debt paper." He turned the word over like a coin of doubtful metal. "And she came through the river to deliver it?"

"Some people can't afford the bridges."

"No," said Doss mildly. "Some people can't."

He helped me distribute the morning's forme after that — the funeral invitations for Alderman Creve's mother, God rest her, set in the heavy black type we called the mourning face, though everyone in Vellmora knew it doubled as our pamphlet type because grief prints the same as sedition if you don't look too closely at the words. We worked in something adjacent to the good silence. It fooled neither of us.

Twice, while we worked, he mentioned — casually, the way you'd mention weather — that the Garrison Arch had new procedures this week. Twice, that soldiers on the Bridge of Atlas were weighing carts now, actual scales brought out onto the span. He didn't ask me if I was planning to cross the river. He didn't need to. A man reporting to the ministers couldn't have arranged the information more neatly if I'd handed him the questions first, and a man genuinely trying to help couldn't have either, and that was the whole problem with Doss, that morning and forever: both readings fit his face, and only one fit his wages.

"I'll be taking the afternoon delivery run myself," he said, shrugging into his coat. "Creve's invitations won't carry themselves. You'll lock up?"

"I always do."

"Yes," he said, from the doorway. "You always do. Lock it well, Casper. There are things in this shop worth locking."

Then the door clicked, and the silence he left behind was a third kind entirely, one my father never named because he never had the misfortune to hear it.

* * *

I gave myself one minute of standing still, which I charged against my sleep debt, and then I got to work.

The letter could not stay in my stays. If I was searched anywhere — checkpoint, street, my own doorstep — it died there, and Ilse with it, and possibly the city's last chance at believing Aldous Vey had outlived his own hanging. But it also could not go anywhere obvious. Not under the floorboards; soldiers find floorboards the way gulls find fish. Not in the press bed; every raider in Vellmora knows to check the press. Not sewn into furniture, not slipped behind a loose brick, not in any of the clever places, because clever places are the first places a clever man looks, and this morning I had looked at a clever man and seen how well he wore his cleverness.

So I chose the stupid place. The best hiding places are stupid, if you commit to them properly.

At the back of the type racks, third row, sat the case my father called the widow — the font of 12-point that had come to us damaged decades ago, dropped off a cart by a wholesaler who denied all responsibility, a fifth of its sorts cracked or chipped across the shoulder. No compositor ever used it. No thief would ever steal it. It sat in the dark being worthless with tremendous conviction. And broken type, I will tell you, hides paper beautifully: a sheet folded to the width of a sort, slid down among a hundred genuine sorts, reads to any searching finger as exactly what it appears to be — a case of ruined metal nobody has opened since the old king died.

I hid it in eleven minutes, taking nine of those minutes to fold the letter properly, because folding a dead revolutionary's testimony small enough to pass for type is a task that deserves patience, and because my hands were shaking and lying to them about that seemed rude.

Ilse found me there, kneeling at the widow case with dust on my knuckles.

"You've hidden it," she said. Not a question.

"In the least valuable object in this building. If Doss searches — when Doss searches, because he will — he'll find a broken font and a woman with an honest face."

"Do you think he'll search?"

I stood, brushed off my skirt, and considered how much truth to spend. Trust, I have learned, is rationed like ink in this trade: you never pour out the whole pot, because the day you need some for yourself, the pot is empty. "Doss offered to help me this morning," I said. "Before I asked him for anything."

Ilse went very still. She had a way of going still that most people reserve for near-missed carriages. "That's bad."

"It might be bad. Or it might be a lonely old foreman who watched my father die and wants to feel useful to the daughter." I heard my own voice do the thing it does when it's defending someone — light, reasonable, generous — and I hated it a little. "He carried Papa's coffin."

"He also told you about the Arch and the bridge scales without asking why you'd care."

Yes. He had. And that fact sat in my chest like a sort set upside down — everything about it correct except the one thing that mattered, printing backwards until you looked at it in a mirror. "We leave tomorrow night anyway," I said. "Whatever Doss is, we outrun him by a day."

"And if we don't? If he writes tonight?"

"Then we're caught tomorrow instead of today, and honestly, Ilse, I can't plan around every possible betrayal in Vellmora or I'd never finish setting Creve's invitations." I meant it as a joke. It landed somewhere in the neighborhood of a joke, close enough that Ilse's mouth twitched, and then we were both laughing, quietly, hysterically, two women giggling over a case of broken type in a seditious print shop at dawn, and I understood suddenly why conspiracies throughout history must have been such fun right up until the firing squads. Fear does that. It boils off and leaves something that feels almost exactly like joy.

When it passed, Ilse touched the edge of the widow case, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "Your father taught you this? Hiding things in type?"

"My father taught me everything about type except how to hide in it. That part I learned from the pogrom." I hadn't meant to say that aloud. It came out anyway, the way family history does around strangers who remind you of family. "We left Karst with one case between us. Papa carried it on his back the whole way. Everyone else carried silver, paintings, whatever fits in a wagon. He carried forty pounds of lead and said — I can hear him — *letters weigh less than gold and are worth more, because letters agree to mean the same thing tomorrow.*"

Ilse looked at me for a long moment. Her brother's lean was in her face when she did that, that same forward tilt toward whatever interested her, as if the world were an argument she intended to win. "Is that true?" she said. "That they mean the same thing tomorrow?"

"It's the only true thing in this shop."

* * *

Doss came back at dusk, earlier than promised, smelling faintly of the tavern on Cordwainer's Row and not at all of Creve's house, which told me the delivery had gone somewhere other than Creve's house, or had gone nowhere at all, or had gone precisely where he'd wanted it to go, and I had stopped pretending those were different possibilities.

He hung his coat. He checked the press. He looked at the type racks the way a man looks at a room he is memorizing, and then he said, cheerfully, terribly: "Word at the tavern. They raided Quill & Sons tonight. Took the plates, took old Quill. Something about pamphlets." A pause, measured to the hairline. "Good thing we're only doing funerals these days."

"Very good," I said.

"Isn't it." He poured himself water from the jug, drank it standing, looking at the third-row rack. Looking at it. Not searching it — searching takes an hour and a warrant, and he had neither, yet — just noting it, the way you note which shelf holds the good brandy. Then he wished me goodnight and went upstairs to his rooms, and I sat down on the composing stone in the dark and did arithmetic.

Quill & Sons was two streets over. The raid had happened within hours of Doss's morning performance. Either the ministry was sweeping the Paper District on general principle, which was possible, or someone had given them a reason to start with the shops nearest ours, which was also possible, and both possibilities led through the same tavern and the same foreman's coat. I thought about waking Ilse. I thought about getting the letter out of the widow case and finding somewhere better, though there was nowhere better, that was rather the point of the widow case. I thought about my father saying *letters agree to mean the same thing tomorrow,* and how he had never once added the caveat that the men reading them might change their meaning overnight.

Trust, I decided, sitting there in the smell of ink and lamp oil, is not a pot you ration after all. It's a forme you lock up: you set your words carefully, you judge the spacing, and then you tighten the quoins and hope to God the whole thing doesn't spill when the press comes down.

I locked the shop. I locked it well. There are things in this shop worth locking.

Upstairs, through the floor, I heard Doss's boots cross his room once, stop at the window, and stay there a long time — facing the river, I'd have bet the shop on it, facing the Bridge of Atlas and the dark water beyond.

Tomorrow night we would cross it ourselves, past the Customs House, past the Garrison Arch, past the scales on the bridge. And somewhere between here and there, I would have to decide whether Doss was a traitor, a coward, or simply a man who had learned — as I was learning — that in Vellmora that season, the three categories overlapped almost completely.

I blew out the lamp. In the dark, the widow case sat in its row among the living fonts, holding a dead man's voice in among its broken letters, patient as lead.

Let Doss listen at doors. Let him count my footsteps if he liked. My father was right about one thing: letters weigh less than gold. What he never mentioned was how much heavier they get when you're the one carrying them.
