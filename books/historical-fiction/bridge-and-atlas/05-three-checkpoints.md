## 5. Three Checkpoints

Casper drew the city on the back of a spoiled proof sheet — a misprint of a funeral announcement for a man who had not died, which struck me as the most honest thing Vellmora had produced all year. She laid it flat on the composing stone and weighted the corners with quoins, and then she stood over it with a pencil worn down to a stub, and I understood that in this shop, a map was just another kind of type: something set carefully, meant to be read once, and distributed at your own peril.

"Three ways across," she said. "One road worth taking."

I had been in Vellmora four days. In that time I had slept in a cellar that smelled of lampblack and vinegar, eaten two meals I could identify and several I couldn't, and learned that printers measure everything — grief included — in units called ems. It was, I was discovering, a family trait of Aldous's too. My brother used to say you could weigh a government by what it forbade. By that arithmetic, Vellmora's Ministry was the heaviest thing in the world.

"Show me," I said.

* * *

Casper's pencil touched the river first, a long wavering stroke she made without looking at the paper, the way you'd sign your own name. The Vell ran through the middle of the city like a sentence through the middle of an argument, dividing the print quarter and the markets from the council halls and barracks on the far bank. Everything we needed was on the other side. Everything that wanted us dead was on this one.

"The Customs House." She tapped the near quay. "It isn't a way across so much as a way to be counted. Every barge, every ferry, every cart that goes within a hundred yards of the water passes the customs clerks. They don't search much. They don't need to. They know faces, and they know what faces look like when they're carrying something."

"And ours will be."

"Ours will be carrying exactly what we say we're carrying, which is the only trick that works twice." She drew a small square at the quay's edge. "They'll want papers. Manifest, pass, a name they can write down in a ledger that outlives everyone who writes in it."

"You sound like my brother."

"He sounds like a printer. We got there first." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile and decided against it. "Second is the Garrison Arch. Here — where the old triumphal gate straddles the processional road. The Arch is where they put soldiers who've annoyed their officers, which means the men there are either lazy or furious, and you can't always tell which until it's expensive."

"What do they look for at the Arch?"

"People. Not goods — people. The Customs House counts cargo; the Arch counts bodies. They're watching for couriers, organizers, anyone moving with purpose toward the council district." She looked up at me, and her gaze rested a half-second too long on my face before sliding back to the paper. She had done that since the night I arrived, that flicker of recognition she never named. I knew why. I'd seen it in every mirror since I was fourteen: Aldous looked back at me from the glass, thinner, hungrier, wearing my hair like a borrowed coat.

"They'll see it," I said, because saying it first hurt less than waiting for her to say it.

"They might."

"My brother's face is on three hundred posters across this city. Dead-eyed engravings. REWARD. They'll see it."

Casper was quiet for a moment. Then she said, in the flat practical voice of someone handing you a rope and hoping you grab the right end: "Then we make sure they see a woman, not a resemblance. Hats are good. Bad posture is better. Nobody suspects a sloucher."

"That's your plan? Slouching?"

"It's a beginning. Plans are beginnings with pencil on them." She drew a triangle at the Arch and then crossed it out immediately, as if even marking it gave the soldiers there more power. "Third is the Bridge of Atlas. The only crossing wide enough for carts, which means it's the only crossing where they stop and weigh them. There's a scale house at the midpoint. Soldiers, a weighmaster, and a queue long enough that you stand still for twenty minutes while a man decides whether your life is interesting."

"Weighing carts," I repeated. "Why weigh a cart?"

"Because weight tells truth. A cart full of grain weighs like grain. A cart full of grain with pamphlets under the grain weighs like grain plus pamphlets. They've got the tolerances memorized, the clever devils. It's the one checkpoint run by arithmeticians instead of bullies." She said it with something close to professional admiration. Printers, I was learning, respected anyone who took measurement seriously, including the people measuring them for prison.

"So we don't take a cart."

"We take something that has no expected weight. Something whose heaviness can't be argued with." She set the pencil down. "We'll come to that."

* * *

That evening we walked the route. Not all of it, and not openly — Casper had a horror of walking anywhere twice, which she said came from her father, who believed repetition was how the Watch learned you. But we went along the quay at dusk, when the Customs House windows threw long yellow bars across the cobbles, and we stood in the shadow of a salt merchant's awning and watched the clerks work.

They were not what I expected. I had imagined the customs service as fat men with rings and sneers, the villains of the pamphlets Aldous used to write before he graduated to villains with armies. Instead there were perhaps six clerks, thin, ink-cuffed, moving with the weary diligence of men doing sums forever. And at the counter nearest the door sat one apart from the others — older, gray, with a stack of books beside him that he was not inspecting so much as handling. He turned each volume over. He opened it to the title page. He ran his thumb down the spine with a tenderness that had nothing official in it.

"There," Casper murmured. "That one's Quill. Senior examiner. He's the dangerous one."

"He looks half-asleep."

"He confiscated a book of poems last spring and read the whole thing before burning it. Made a report of eleven pages on its dangers." She paused. "My father knew him, before. Says Quill wanted to be a publisher when he was young. Didn't have the money, so he joined the ministry instead, and now he touches books all day under a license to destroy them."

I watched the man's hands move over a slim octavo and thought: there but for a legacy. There but for a loan refused. The regime was not staffed by monsters; it was staffed by disappointed readers, which was worse, because monsters don't know what they're burning.

"He'll be our problem," I said.

"He'll be your problem. I'm going to be a type merchant standing behind you looking bored." Casper pulled her cap lower. "Come on. The Arch before full dark."

* * *

The Garrison Arch was visible from three streets away because nothing else on the processional road was allowed to be taller than a shopfront, and the Arch rose above them all like a fist raised mid-oath. Black stone, old empire masonry, carved with names of generals nobody living could recite. Underneath it, the road narrowed to a single lane, and the narrowing did the work a wall would do: everyone funneled, everyone slowed, everyone lifted their chin or dropped their eyes according to temperament and guilt.

Two lanterns burned under the vault. A squad of maybe eight soldiers lounged against the pillars with the studied idleness of men who would rather be drinking. And one officer — a captain, by his cuffs — stood in the center of the lane actually paying attention, turning pedestrians by the shoulder now and again, asking short questions, waving most through.

"That's Captain Renn," Casper said. We had stopped a street short, in the doorway of a shuttered chandlery, and she spoke with the low speed of a woman reciting from memory. "He commanded the escort when they moved Vey from the record prison to the execution ground. He signed the transfer ledger."

Something went cold in me, starting at the hands. "You know that how?"

"Everyone in the print quarter knows it. It was in one of the last pamphlets before the presses went quiet." She glanced at me. "He stood next to the scaffold. If the execution was staged, he's either part of the lie or one of the few honest men alive who believes it happened."

"Which is worse for us?"

"Honest men are worse. Liars can be bought; believers have to be persuaded, and I haven't got the time or the rhetoric." She was quiet a moment, watching the lantern-light slide across the wet stones under the Arch. Then, carefully, not looking at me: "There's another thing about Renn."

"Say it."

"He met Aldous. Twice, they say — once to arrest him, once before that, years ago, when Vey wrote something Renn's mother admired and the captain asked for a signature instead of an arrest." Casper's jaw tightened. "He'll know the face. He's the reason the posters matter less than the blood in yours."

I stood in that dark doorway and felt the shape of the thing we were attempting settle onto my shoulders like a loaded pack. It is one thing to carry a letter. It is another to walk beneath a soldier who has shaken the hand of the man whose face you wear, and hope that the light is bad and his memory is worse.

"Well," I said, when I trusted my voice, "at least the bridge will be easy."

Casper laughed — one short syllable, entirely without humor. "Oh, Ilse. Sit down somewhere. I haven't shown you the bridge yet."

* * *

She wouldn't take me near it that night. "The Atlas gets patrols on the approaches after dusk," she said, "and there's nowhere to stand and look at it that doesn't look like standing and looking at it." So instead she took me to a pie stall two streets off, bought us both something hot and unidentifiable, and drew the bridge on the greasy paper with the same doomed pencil.

The Bridge of Atlas, rendered in tallow grease: a long spine across the river, wide enough for two carts abreast, lined with parapets. At the midpoint, the scale house, squat as a toad, with a beam balance mounted outside it under a hooded lantern, big enough to weigh a wagon and horse together. A queue marked in dashes stretching back along the span.

"At dawn and at dusk, they weigh everything," Casper said. "Midday they weigh carts only, and foot traffic walks through with a glance. But midday is also when the queue is worst, because the market carts all cross at midday, and the queue gives the soldiers time to be curious."

"Then dawn."

"Dawn. First light, when the weighmaster's coffee is stronger than his attention, and the levy crowds are already gathering on the far bank for the protest. Ten thousand angry people make excellent cover — nobody searches hard when the crowd itself is the suspect." She dotted the scale house with the pencil, pressed too hard, and the point snapped. She stared at the broken stub for a moment as if it had betrayed her personally.

"Casper."

"Mm."

"Why do you keep breaking the lead and not sharpening it?"

She turned the stub over in her fingers. "My father sharpened it this morning," she said. "The morning he died, two years ago. I keep intending to sharpen it and I keep not doing it." She put it away in her waistcoat pocket, briskly, closing the subject the way you close a case lid. "Anyway. Pencils erase. That's why we plan in pencil. Anything drawn in ink starts believing it's true."

"And this plan — do you believe it's true?"

"I believe it's the best lie we can afford." She folded the greasy map along its creases and handed it to me. "Here. You carry it tonight. If we're stopped, eat it — it's a meat pie wrapper, no one burns a meat pie wrapper."

I took it. It was still warm.

* * *

Back at the shop, past midnight, we rehearsed.

This was Casper's idea, and at first I thought it was madness — two women alone in a locked printing house, acting out our own arrest at two in the morning. But she insisted, and I learned quickly that Casper Oyelaran rehearses the way other people pray: repetitively, seriously, in fear of the alternative.

"You're Marthe Kessel," she said. "Widow. Type merchant out of Lowmarsh, delivering a consignment to the Gildenhall pressworks across the river. You inherited the business from your husband. You know nothing about type except what it weighs and what it sells for."

"And you?"

"I'm your hired carter. I know type the way a fish knows water, and I'm too bored to hide it. Between us we cover everything: if they ask you something technical, you look at me; if they ask me something official, I shrug and point at you." She paced the length of the shop, hands jammed in her apron. "Now. Quill asks to see your manifest. Go."

I cleared my throat. "Good morning. Consignment of mixed sorts, forty pounds, bound for—"

"Wrong." She stopped pacing. "Never open with the consignment. Open with yourself. Men like Quill aren't listening for cargo, they're listening for nerves, and nerves live in the first five words. Say your name first. Names are boring. Boring is armor."

"Marthe Kessel," I said. "Type merchant, out of Lowmarsh."

"Better. Again."

We did it a dozen times. Quill and his hunger for books. Renn and his memory of a dead man's face. The weighmaster's scales and their merciless arithmetic. Each time Casper played the officials she became them — she had a gift for it, a small cruel ear for bureaucratic voice, the clipped impatience, the pleasure of small power — and each time she cut my answers down shorter, shorter, shorter, until I was mostly silence and a nod, which she said was the safest dialect in any occupied city.

Somewhere in the third hour, between iterations, she paused by the type cases and looked at me with an expression I hadn't seen on her before — not suspicion, not calculation. Almost wonder.

"You're good at this," she said. "You lie like someone who's practiced."

"I lived four years in a city where being my brother's sister was a hanging offense," I said. "You learn to introduce yourself slowly."

"And the letter. What do we do if—" She stopped herself, but I heard the rest of the sentence: what do we do if it's taken from us. What do we do if everything Aldous wrote, the names of the ministers, the proof that his death was theater — what then.

"Then we carry it somewhere they can't take it," I said.

"Where?"

"In here." I tapped my own temple, and then, because it seemed required, I tapped one of her type cases. "You told me yourself, printer. Letters outlive men. You just haven't decided yet whether that's a comfort or a threat."

Casper didn't answer. She took out the broken pencil, turned it over once, and put it back. But before she rolled out her pallet among the empty delivery crates, she unpinned a page from the proof wall — a specimen sheet, rows of letters descending from grand to minuscule — and pinned it up again in the darkest corner, facing outward, where anyone who burst through the door would see it first.

A decoy. A thing that looked like contraband to draw the eye from the real thing sewn into my coat lining, three folded pages in a dead man's hand.

"Sleep," she said. "Dawn comes early, and the Atlas doesn't care whether you're ready."

But sleep was a long time finding me, and when it did, I dreamed of scales — a great beam balance swinging over black water, one pan heaped with everything we carried, and the other empty, waiting to be filled with whatever the soldiers decided we were.

In the morning, Casper spread a fresh sheet on the composing stone, took out her father's knife, and began grinding a discarded official font down to forge us a customs pass — and that, though neither of us knew it yet, was the smallest of the lies we would need before the day was done.
