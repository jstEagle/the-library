## 6. The Type That Lies

The official font of the Vellmoran Customs Bureau lived in a crate under the stairs, behind my father's funeral type and three decades of things he couldn't bring himself to throw away. I knew it was there because I had catalogued it myself, two winters ago, in one of those fits of grief that masquerade as tidying. The label on the crate read, in his handwriting: *Bought at the Harrow Street auction — never use — bad metal.*

He'd written *never use* with the particular emphasis of a man who intended to use it.

The story, as Doss once told it over cheap wine — back when Doss still told stories instead of watching me like a cat watches a birdcage — was that the Bureau had commissioned a private foundry to cut them an exclusive face for their seals and passes, something no counterfeiter could match because no other shop owned it. Then the foundry went bankrupt, and the whole font came up at auction, and every printer in the city understood that owning it was either a windfall or a death sentence depending on which way the political wind blew. My father bought it for less than the price of its lead weight and buried it under the stairs, where it sat through eleven years of quiet like a corpse that hadn't been informed it was dead.

Bad metal, he'd said. Bad luck, more like. But tonight, bad luck was exactly what we needed.

I hauled the crate into the composing room at half past ten, when the lamps across the lane had gone out and even the rats had clocked off. Ilse sat where I told her to sit — on the high stool by the stone, hands folded, wearing one of my father's old aprons, which hung off her like a sail off a rowboat. She'd slept four hours in the loft above the ink room. It showed. But there's a kind of exhaustion that sharpens people instead of blunting them, the way a blade is sharpest just before it snaps, and she had it.

"That's it?" she asked, nodding at the crate. "The famous unforgeable type?"

"The famously unforgeable type that has been sitting in a cellar since before you could read." I pried the lid. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, the sorts lay in their compartments like teeth pulled from some enormous jaw. I lifted a handful and let them catch the lamplight. "You see the trick of it? Look at the serifs. Bracketed, but thin — thinner than any commercial face. And look here." I held one letter close to her eye. "The crossbar of the e. Slightly angled. Not level. Every clerk in this city handles these letters daily and never notices, because you don't notice the face on your own money."

"But a guard who sees it will feel it's right without knowing why."

"That," I said, "is the entire philosophy of typography, and also of lying."

She smiled. It was the first smile I'd seen from her that wasn't a weapon or a shield, and I looked away from it faster than was strictly polite, because there are things a person can't afford to look at directly at midnight when everything depends on steadiness.

I set the pass while she watched. A customs transit permit, Form 11-C, authorizing the bearers to move one consignment of printer's type across the Bridge of Atlas, exempt from levy as industrial material. The genuine article was a gray-green slip with a stamped seal, and the stamp was the hard part, because the Bureau used an embossed die and all we had was type-high metal and a press that could only kiss, not crush. So we would do what printers have always done when the truth won't fit the form: we would change what people expected to see. The seal would be printed, not embossed, but printed in that unmistakable face, in that slightly angled e, and the clerk at the Customs House would run his thumb over it, find it flat, and hesitate — and in that hesitation Ilse would say the words we were going to spend tomorrow rehearsing, about how the new forms came flat-printed since the winter reorganization, surely he'd seen the circular, everyone complains about it, the reorganization nobody can be bothered to remember except the people it inconvenienced.

A lie works best when it flatters the liar's laziness. My father taught me that too, though he taught it about jobbing work: *never make the customer think, Casper. Thinking is where complaints come from.*

"You're smiling," Ilse said.

"I'm remembering my father." I locked the form into the chase. "He'd have hated this. Or loved it. He contained multitudes, most of them contradictory."

"Tell me about him."

People ask that question expecting an anecdote. What they get from me is usually inventory. "He was a small man with ink permanently in the creases of his knuckles, so his hands looked older than his face. He believed a letter was a promise made by someone dead to someone unborn. He said a compositor's job is to stand in the middle and keep both ends of the bargain." I pulled the proof. The impression came up clean and gray-green and beautifully false. "And he believed you should never set anything you wouldn't want read aloud at your funeral, which is why the pamphlets go in the funeral type. He'd have found that funny. Eventually. After the shouting."

Ilse took the proof sheet and held it at arm's length, then close, the way people do when they're pretending to examine a thing so they don't have to speak. Her brother had been a publisher. She knew what a clean pull meant. When she finally handed it back she said, quietly, "Aldous used to say almost the same thing. About promises. He said the regime's real crime wasn't the censorship, it was the forgery — printing lies in the people's own voice until we forgot which words were ours."

"Well," I said, "tonight we're returning the favor."

* * *

We rehearsed in the composing room with the shutters drawn and the lamp turned low, because rehearsing by daylight felt like tempting something.

The cover was simple, which is to say it was complicated in the way that only simple things are. Two type merchants — sisters, because sisters explain resemblance better than strangers ever could, and because Ilse's face and mine had already begun doing that disquieting thing faces do after three days of shared fear, settling into the same weather — delivering a consignment of broken sorts to the council district beyond the river. Broken type travels constantly between shops; it's melted down, recast, traded. Nobody weighs it carefully because nobody wants it. The letter would ride inside a hollowed case of nonpareil, sealed under a false bottom I built from an old furniture board, and the case would sit in plain view on the cart among forty honest cases of genuinely worthless metal.

Hiding a thing in plain sight costs more effort than hiding it well. But well-hidden things invite searching, and boring things invite yawns, and we were betting two lives on the yawns.

"Again," I said. "From the top. The clerk asks what's in the cases."

"Broken sorts. We're clearing our late father's stock." She said it smoothly. Too smoothly. "Our late father's stock," she repeated, and then stopped, and her jaw did something small and terrible. "Sorry. I keep — saying it makes it true, and then I remember it isn't, and then I remember the other thing that is true, and—"

"Say it again."

"What?"

"Say it again. Our late father's stock. Say it until it stops costing anything."

She said it again. And again. On the fourth repetition her voice flattened out, and on the sixth it went somewhere past flattening into a strange calm, and I recognized that place, because I live there. Grief doesn't leave a room when you rehearse in it. It just learns its lines.

"The clerk asks your name," I said.

"Hazel Oyelaran." She stumbled on the surname. "Oyelaran. The accent falls wrong when I say it."

"It falls wrong when anyone says it who didn't grow up being teased with it. That's fine — clerks don't check accents, they check papers. But if he asks where the shop is, don't name this street. Name Cordwainer Lane. There's a shuttered bindery at number nine; the sign's still up; nobody's rented it since the flood year."

"Why Cordwainer Lane?"

"Because it's fifteen minutes' walk from here, and if he sends a runner to verify, we'll hear the door before the runner reaches the corner." I turned the lamp down another notch. "Now the hard part. He asks to inspect the consignment. You open the nonpareil case."

"And he sees broken type."

"He sees broken type, yes. But you have to let him see it *bored*. You lift a handful, you let a few sorts spill — not many, six, seven — and you complain about the price of lead alloy. Complaints are the best camouflage in the world. No one searches a woman mid-complaint; it feels rude even to soldiers, and Customs men are ex-soldiers with better posture."

Ilse practiced spilling the sorts. First she spilled too few, delicately, like a lady scattering crumbs for birds. Then she spilled too many, a whole cascade, and we both stared at the clatter in the silent room like it had fired a shot.

"Six," I said, retrieving them. "Count them out beforehand. Six sorts in your left palm, and throw them like you're angry at them, not offering them."

"Angry at the sorts."

"At the price of lead. At the levy. At the whole rotten arrangement of the world. You've been angry at cheap things your whole life — everyone has — this is just the first time you get paid for it."

She tried again. Six sorts hit the stone and scattered, and she glared down at them with such genuine, sudden fury — not performed, I think, but borrowed from a deep account she kept elsewhere — that I forgot my next note entirely.

"There," I managed. "That. Whatever that was."

"My mother throwing dishes," Ilse said, not looking at me. "When the censors came for Aldous the first time. She threw every dish in the house so there'd be nothing left for them to confiscate that she loved." She gathered the sorts up, one by one, deliberately calm again. "I was nine. I thought it was madness. Now I think it might be the sanest thing anyone in my family ever did."

We ran the scene eleven more times. By the ninth, she was complaining about lead prices with the weary fluency of a woman who had personally ruined herself buying them, and by the eleventh I caught myself answering as the clerk, huffing and stamping, and realized we had built something that no longer needed either of us to push it. The lie had learned to walk on its own. That's the moment in any forgery — of paper or of personality — when you stop crafting and start believing, and it's the moment you should be most afraid of, because a lie you believe is a lie you can't fix.

I killed the lamp and stood in the dark listening to my own heartbeat slow down.

"Casper." Her voice came from the direction of the stool. "Why are you doing this? Truly. You have a shop. You could burn the letter and no one would ever know it existed."

I considered several answers — duty, justice, my father's memory, the usual currency — and discarded them all, because she had spent the evening learning to hear falsehood in a human voice, and I wasn't going to hand her counterfeit coin.

"Because I set proofs of his columns once," I said. "Years ago, before the ban, when his publisher farmed out composition. I set maybe forty pages of Aldous Vey, and I know what you're thinking — typesetters forget everything, the words pour through us like water — but it isn't true. Some writers you forget by the time you distribute the case. And some you set, and your hands learn their rhythm, their long sentences and their short knives, and years later you find yourself thinking in their cadences without knowing whose voice it is in your head." I heard the stool creak; she'd gone still. "Your brother has been living in my hands for six years, Ilse. Tonight is the first time he's asked me for anything."

The dark held its breath. Then, very quietly: "What did he ask?"

"For a good line spacing," I said. "He always wanted more leading. Said the truth needs room to breathe."

She laughed — one short, cracked note, surprised out of her — and in the dark it sounded like something mending rather than breaking.

* * *

Doss arrived at dawn, which was itself a message; the foreman of a print shop has no honest reason to appear before the day's first pull unless he wants the shop to know he's watching it wake.

He stood in the doorway of the composing room with his coat still buttoned and looked at the open crate under the stairs, because I'd been careless — or careful, depending on how you read it — and left the lid off. His eyes went to the crate, then to the press, then to me, with the unhurried thoroughness of a man taking inventory.

"Bureau font," he said. "Haven't seen that out of its box since your father buried it."

"Auction lot came with the crate," I lied, pleasantly. "Thought I'd finally sort the good sorts from the dross."

"Mm." He turned the lid over in his hands, reading the old label — *never use* — with an expression I couldn't parse, which was becoming the only expression he owned. "Your father was a careful man."

"He was a terrified man with excellent habits."

"Same thing, in this city." He set the lid down precisely where it had been, squared to the grain of the floorboards, and I understood that the precision was the message. "There's talk of a bread levy protest at the bridge Saturday. Big one. The garrison's doubling the checkpoint rotations." He straightened his coat. "Be careful what you sort, Miss Oyelaran. Lead's heavy. Things break under it."

He left. Ilse emerged from the ink room, pale. "He knows."

"He knows *something*. With Doss that's a profession." But my hands weren't steady as I closed the crate, and we both watched them shake. Saturday. Two days. The pass was cut, the cover was drilled, the lie could walk — and now the ground beneath it was scheduled to catch fire.

Ilse looked at the gray-green proof drying on the stone, the beautiful false seal with its angled e, and said, "Then we go tomorrow. Before the crowds. Before the doubled guards have learned each other's names."

"Tomorrow," I said, and began locking the form into the chase, and neither of us mentioned the other thing we were both thinking: that a plan moved up a day is a plan with its rehearsal still wet, and that the Bridge of Atlas on a Friday morning would be quiet in exactly the way that gives a lone clerk nothing to do but read papers closely.

Outside, the first carts rattled toward the river, carrying ordinary freight through ordinary checkpoints, under a sky going the color of fresh newsprint.
