## 7. Customs House

The Customs House of Vellmora had been built by men who believed that intimidation was best expressed in stone. Everything about it was oversized and slightly wrong — doorways tall enough for a giant, windows narrow enough for a prison, a facade of gray granite furred with lichen so that the whole building looked like something dredged up from the river and stood upright before it finished dripping. Even the queue outside was monumental: forty people deep in the morning damp, merchants with ledgers strapped to their backs like shields, fishwives with baskets they held slightly away from their bodies as if the fish might be interrogated too.

I stood among them with a canvas satchel on my shoulder and a lie sitting behind my teeth where my tongue could warm to it.

The satchel weighed nothing. That was the horror of it. Casper had spent half the night building our story — forged pass, consignment docket, the careful grubbiness of two type merchants who resented every minute spent away from their shop — and all of it rested on one sheet of paper that weighed less than a sparrow's feather and was worth more, at that moment, than every cart in the queue combined. Aldous Vey's letter. The confession of a dead man, naming three ministers who had arranged his living grave. It sat folded inside a folio of printer's proofs, third sheet from the top, disguised by its own plainness.

*Hide it in plain sight*, Casper had said, inking her fingers as she talked, as though she could not think without her hands being black. *Guards search for what's concealed. Nobody searches twice at something shown to them openly.*

She'd been right, of course. She was usually right about paper. It was people I worried about — specifically, one person, whose description Casper had extracted from a smuggler friend over cheap wine while I'd sat with my stomach in knots listening.

*Customs clerk, second floor, west window*, the smuggler had said. *Name of Pellam Harrow. Fat as a cathedral collection plate, spectacles mended with wire, and — here's your luck — mad for books. Confiscated books. They bring him contraband and he's supposed to destroy it, and instead he reads it. Keeps a private collection in the drawer under his counter like a dragon on a very small hoard. If you get him talking about books, you could walk out with the customs seal itself.*

So our plan, refined to its final elegance, was this: make friends with a government clerk by appealing to his secret criminality, and pray he never once asked to see what a type merchant carries openly.

Casper had wanted to come through with me. I'd refused. One woman and one parcel is a transaction; two women and a parcel is a conspiracy. She'd argued, in her flat way, staring at her ink-stained fingers rather than at me — she does that when she cares about something, looks anywhere but at it — and finally said only: "Don't let him touch the proofs."

"I thought we were showing him the proofs."

"Showing isn't touching. There's a difference in the trade." And then, quieter, the closest thing to tenderness she permitted herself before dawn: "Oil from fingers lifts old ink. His letter has survived a prison office and a river. Don't let it die of a clerk."

Now I was three places from the window, and my hands were cold, and I discovered that fear, for me, had stopped being an animal thing somewhere in the past weeks and become instead a kind of stagecraft. I could feel it happening — the dread gathering itself up, shaking out its skirts, and stepping forward onto the boards of my face as poise. My brother would have recognized it. Aldous had done the same thing his whole life: terrified underneath, serene above, the performance so complete that even the performer forgot there was anything beneath until three o'clock in the morning. We were alike in that. We were alike in the face, too, which had nearly killed me at the Garrison Arch and which I did not intend to think about now, standing in a queue, because thinking about faces made me want to check my reflection in every dark window to see if he was looking back.

"Next."

* * *

Pellam Harrow was worse than described and better than hoped. He was fat in the way of men who have gone soft from sitting and bitter from boredom, with jowls that quivered when he stamped, and the promised wire-mended spectacles perched on a nose reddened either by drink or by the perpetual grievance of a clever man buried in stupid work. His counter was scarred oak. His stamp was cracked. Behind him, on shelves meant for tax rolls, sat books — dozens of them, stacked spine-out with the careless reverence of a man who has run out of proper shelf space and refuses to admit it.

He took my consignment docket without greeting. He read it the way he presumably read everything, lips moving faintly, wire spectacles sliding down the greasy slope of his nose. I watched him arrive at the words *type specimens* and saw something move behind his eyes — not suspicion. Appetite.

"You're a printer," he said.

"A type merchant." I gave him the smile I had rehearsed, the one belonging to a woman tired, competent, and mildly aggrieved by bureaucracy. "We cut and cast. The letters, not the books. Everyone always assumes the books."

"Everyone assumes wrong," Harrow said, and set down the docket, and leaned back in his chair with the creak of a man settling into his favorite subject. "Books are where the trouble is. Paper travels. Type stays put. You can burn a book but a font of type just sits in a shop being metal." He squinted at me. "You'd know. What have you got, then? Specimens, is it?"

"Proofs," I said. "A specimen book we're compiling. Faces we've cut or inherited. My partner insists on carrying proofs of everything — she says a specimen book without proofs is like a menu without food." I opened the satchel as I spoke, unhurried, and drew out the folio, and laid it on the counter between us, and opened it to the first page.

Here I must confess something, since this account will otherwise make me seem braver than I am: my heart was going like a drum corps. The letter was four sheets down. Between it and Harrow's eyes lay three pages of innocent typography — a bold roman, a slanted italic, a decorative border of acanthus leaves that Casper had pulled from her father's stock and which she had handled, while setting the page, with more gentleness than she ever showed living people. All I had to do was turn leaves slowly, keep my voice level, and never, ever let the man's hands cross the counter.

"This is a garalde," I said, turning the first proof toward him. "Cut by my father. Old school. You'll notice the ascenders —"

"I know what an ascender is," Harrow snapped, and then, mortified by his own eagerness, softened. "My mother was a binder," he added, as if confessing to a disease. "Before the levy ruined her trade. I grew up with books under everything. Slept on them." He bent close over the proof, spectacles fogging with the effort. "That's fine cutting. Who grounds your italics?"

"We do. In-house." Leaf two. The italic swam up, elegant and harmless, and behind it, invisible, a dead man's hand waited.

And here is what I learned at the Customs House of Vellmora, at the first of three doors between us and the printers' council: terror, properly harnessed, is indistinguishable from performance. Every instinct in my body screamed to rush — to show him the required minimum, snatch the docket's stamp, and flee. But rushing is what guilty people do, and so I did the opposite. I lingered. I turned the pages like a woman proud of her stock. I answered his questions about casting matrices with details borrowed from Casper's shop talk, and when I stumbled, I covered it with fatigue — "my partner handles the foundry end, I handle the roads" — and Harrow nodded along, hungry, drinking it in.

Because that was the truth under all his bluster: this man was starved. Vellmora's censors had starved him. Every interesting book that crossed his counter was confiscated, and every confiscation should have ended in fire, and instead he kept them, secretly, shamefully, in a drawer, reading by lamplight the very texts his office condemned. He was a censor who loved what he burned. There are few creatures on earth so lonely.

"You keep confiscated volumes here," I said lightly, on leaf three, letting my gaze drift to the shelves behind him. "Tax rolls, are they? Those spines look suspiciously well-read."

Harrow went the color of ham. For one long moment I thought I had overplayed it — that the performance would collapse and the stamp would stay in its cradle and a whistle would sound. His hand moved toward the counter drawer and stopped. His eyes flicked to the door. To the soldier posted by the stairs, bored, facing away. Back to me.

"Confiscations are destroyed," he recited, in the voice of a boy reciting catechism. "Per regulation."

"Naturally," I said. "Though a man in your position must occasionally be obliged to examine a text thoroughly before deciding its disposition. For accuracy of report."

The silence stretched. Then something happened in Harrow's face — a small tectonic shift, the settling of a mask he had worn so long he'd forgotten it was removable. He looked at me, and I looked back, and between us passed the oldest agreement in Vellmora: *I know what you are, and I am not your enemy.*

"You've sharp eyes for a type merchant," he said hoarsely.

"My family reads," I said, which was truer than he could know.

* * *

It went wrong, of course. Things go wrong precisely at the moment you believe they won't; I have since built a philosophy on this principle.

Harrow stamped the docket. I saw the thump of it, the dull red bloom of the customs seal on the paper, and felt the first loosening in my chest — we were through, it was done, four sheets of paper had crossed the spine of the regime and been found merely paper. He slid the docket across. I reached for the folio to close it.

And Pellam Harrow put his hand flat on the open proof.

"One more," he said. His voice had changed — gone quiet, wheedling, the voice of a man leaning over a tavern table to propose theft. "The next one. Turn the next one. I haven't seen a new face in —" he stopped himself, recalculated, lied — "in some time. Cuts are all the same now. State faces. State sizes. Everything stamped and dead. What's under the italic?"

"The border designs," I said. "Leaves and scrollwork. Nothing you'd —"

"Turn it."

There is a particular quality to a moment when it turns against you. Time doesn't speed up; it thickens, like ink left open overnight. I heard the soldier by the stairs shift his weight. I heard the fishwife queue shuffling outside, someone laughing. I watched Harrow's fat fingers, nail-bitten, trembling faintly with longing, resting six inches from the letter that named three ministers and one staged death and could, if read aloud in this room, hang us both from the Customs House rafters by lunchtime.

And I understood, in that thickened instant, exactly what Casper had meant. *Showing isn't touching.* Because a man may look at a page and see only what he expects — proofs, specimens, the dull commerce of metal and ink. But the moment his fingers lift that sheet, he becomes a reader. And a reader will find a letter where a stranger sees only type.

So I performed.

I laughed — actually laughed, a short, weary, complicit sound — and turned the page myself, fast, my thumb covering the fold-line where the letter's edge might show, presenting him with the decorative border: acanthus leaves, curling, ornate, utterly blank of any meaning whatsoever.

"There," I said. "The pride of the consignment. Cut by my late father. Do you know how long a border like that takes? Fourteen punches for one repeat unit. He went blind doing the corner pieces." I kept my hand flat on the page, my palm over it like a woman guarding a photograph, and I watched Pellam Harrow's face as he gazed at those leaves, and I gave him what he was starving for: I talked about the book he couldn't have. "He used to say a border was a promise — that whatever the page said, somebody cared enough to frame it. Even a tax roll deserves a decent border. Don't you think?"

Harrow stared at the acanthus leaves for a long time. His fingers curled once against the counter — I watched the hunger fight the propriety, watched propriety win by a nose — and he withdrew his hand, and pushed the spectacles up his nose, and said, in a voice roughened at the edges:

"My mother bound a border like that once. Gilt. On a psalter." He cleared his throat. "State faces," he muttered, almost to himself. "Everything stamped and dead." Then, louder, the clerk restored, the mask re-settled: "Your docket. Consignment's cleared. Move along — there's a queue."

I closed the folio. I folded the letter's hiding place shut with fingers that, I noted with distant academic interest, had gone entirely numb. I tucked the folio into the satchel, took the docket, and wished Pellam Harrow a good morning with the unremarkable courtesy of one tradeswoman among thousands.

He did not answer immediately. As I turned to go, he said, quietly, to my back:

"The border. Fourteen punches, you said?"

"Fourteen," I said. "Fifteen, if you count the corner piece he cut twice, because he dropped the first one in the river."

Harrow made a sound that might once, years ago, have been a laugh. "Tell your partner," he said, "that a specimen book wants better paper than most shops use. If a man's going to look at a page all day, the page ought to be worth looking at." A pause. "Some things are worth keeping. Whatever the regulations say."

I walked down the granite stairs of the Customs House into the gray morning light, past the bored soldier, past the queue, out through the oversized door into a city that still believed its ministers were ministers and its dead men were dead. Casper was waiting by the fountain three streets over, pretending to read a broadsheet, and when she saw my face she stood up so quickly the paper dropped.

"Well?" she said.

I held up the docket with its red seal. Then, because terror transmuted fully into performance deserves a curtain call, I swept her a small bow.

"Madam," I said, "your borders are a triumph. Now let us go and lie to a captain of the garrison, and afterward I am going to sleep for a week."

But Casper wasn't listening anymore. She had picked up the fallen broadsheet from the pavement, and I watched her read it, and watched the color leave her face by degrees, like water through sand.

"Bread levy," she said quietly. "They've posted the rates. It doubles tomorrow." She looked up at me, and behind her eyes I saw the city already beginning to turn over in its sleep. "Ilse. It starts today."
