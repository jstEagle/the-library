## 8. The Pamphlet Riots

The bread levy came down on a Tuesday, which felt fitting. Tuesdays were the day my father had always reserved for paying bills, on the theory that bad news should arrive on schedule and leave the rest of the week alone.

By Wednesday morning the levy was all anyone could talk about in the Paper District, and by Wednesday afternoon it was all anyone could do about anything else. The bakers' guild had posted the new prices in their windows — chalked up neat and official, as if arithmetic committed in chalk were somehow less of an insult — and by noon someone had rubbed out every figure with a wet thumb, and by two o'clock someone else had rubbed out the windows themselves, and by four the first barricade of flour barrels went up at the corner of Millwright's Row with a crowd around it that had stopped being a crowd and started being a weather system.

I watched it from the shop door with Ilse beside me, both of us still carrying the road dust of three checkpoints in our clothes and the particular exhaustion that comes from having lied well to armed men.

"So that's a riot," Ilse said, in the tone of a woman taking notes.

"That's a *prelude* to a riot," I said. "A riot has loaves in it somewhere. That's just hunger standing around deciding what shape to be."

I knew more about this than I wanted to. My father had lived through two bread riots in Vellmora and printed broadsides about neither of them — "a press is not a drum, Casper, and a man who beats it like one will break it" — though he'd kept the type set for a third, locked in the case, the way you keep a will in a drawer. He believed cities were like compositors: patient for years, then all at once they reach for the wrong letter and there is nothing to be done but wait for the line to be redistributed.

Merrick's warning was still folded into my bodice, along with everything else we hadn't managed to deliver. *The council will not act on paper carried quietly. They will act on paper thrown loudly.* The council was paralyzed; the letter sat in a safehouse across the river like a loaded thing nobody would aim; and now the city itself had decided to stop waiting for permission. There is a particular frustration in watching history happen without you, especially when you are carrying its rough draft.

"We should go back," Ilse said. "If the quarter's burning—"

"The shop isn't burning. The shop is mine." I said it with more confidence than I felt, which is the family method. "And if the levy riots have taught me anything about my neighbors, it's that printers are considered essential infrastructure. Nobody burns the place that tells them what they're angry about."

Which is why, an hour later, we were crossing back over the Bridge of Atlas in the wrong direction — against the current of fleeing respectable people, into the smoke-smudged quarter — with a letter's worth of treason hidden in my hatband and no plan beyond *be there before the soldiers are*.

I want it noted that I have since revised this plan considerably.

* * *

You could hear the district before you saw it: not screaming, exactly, but the low continuous roar of a large number of people agreeing with each other, which is louder than any disagreement ever invented. Windows had gone dark along the print quarter — shutters pulled, presses covered, the whole street holding its breath the way shops do when the mob hasn't yet decided whether you're a neighbor or a symbol.

Our shutter was open.

Not broken. Not forced. Open, at half-mast, in the way of a shop that expects to be visited. And standing in the doorway, arms crossed, watching the street with the expression of a man minding someone else's fire, was Doss.

"You," he said.

"Me," I said. "It being my shop."

"It was my shop for six hours." He stepped aside to let us in, and I noticed he didn't move quite quickly enough for it to be courtesy. "Garrison patrol came through at midday. Two sergeants and a lieutenant with a list."

My stomach performed a small mechanical operation. "A list."

"Names and premises. Pamphleteers, agitators, distributors of sedition — the usual vocabulary. They took Anselm's press apart on Coriander Street and confiscated four hundred handbills from the chandlery." He said it flatly, the way he read proofs aloud, no emphasis wasted. "They came here third. Searched for an hour."

"And found?"

"Type." He gestured at the shop, and I made myself look at it properly. The cases were all in order. Too much in order — the funeral type drawers squared with a precision no one achieves twice in one day. "A working press, a lawful inventory, and a foreman with a very boring manner. The lieutenant asked whether I'd seen seditious material. I said I'd seen a great deal of type and very little of anything worth reading, and he agreed with me about the reading."

Ilse, beside me, had gone very still in the way she did before checkpoints — the stillness of a woman redistributing her weight onto her lies.

"You covered for us," I said slowly.

"I kept the shop," Doss corrected. "The shop keeps itself. I merely stood in front of it."

There was something in the room besides relief. I want to be honest about that, because at the time I mistook it for gratitude and spent a full minute feeling ashamed of myself for having left him here. The shame was real. It may even have been earned. But it was also, I understand now, exactly the instrument he intended to pick up and play.

"Doss," I said. "Thank you."

"Mm." He began squaring the sticks on the stone, a habit of his when he wanted his hands to have an opinion. "Your father used to say a foreman's loyalty is like ink. Cheap in quantity, precious in quality, and it stains whatever it touches."

"He never said that."

"No. I said it. He said worse things about me, I expect, when I wasn't in the room." A small smile, gone as fast as it came. "The point is that ink costs something, Casper. Even the ink you already own."

And there it was, laid out on the stone between the type sticks, priced but unpriced.

"What do you want?" Ilse asked. She has no talent for indirection, Ilse; it's one of the few things about her I find restful.

Doss looked at her for a long moment — the first time he had really looked at her since she arrived soaked at our ink door — and then back at me, and whatever calculation he ran, he ran it visibly, like a press working a slow sheet.

"Nothing today," he said. "Today I want you both upstairs, doors barred, away from windows, until the garrison finishes making examples of Coriander Street. What I want later, I'll tell you later. When you're calmer. And when you owe me more precisely."

He turned back to the stone. Conversation, apparently, concluded.

Upstairs, in the little room where my father had died within earshot of his own presses, Ilse sat on the bed and I stood at the shutter crack and we watched the district go up in the ordinary way that districts do — a baker's cart overturned, a bonfire of levy notices, the garrison forming squares at intersections like pieces on a board someone else was moving.

"They searched for an hour," Ilse said quietly. "Where was the letter?"

"In the font case. Where I put it." Broken sorts, the drawer of damaged type no compositor touches except in grief or necessity. Anyone who knew presses would look there eventually. Anyone who didn't would take apart everything else first.

"And he told them nothing."

"He told them nothing."

"Then either he's loyal," Ilse said, "or he's saving us."

I didn't answer, because I had been composing the same sentence in my head for the past hour and had been unable to decide which setting to use. That is the trouble with a lifetime in letters: you learn early that the same characters can spell opposite words depending entirely on where you place the space.

* * *

The riots lasted three days, which is long enough for a city to acquire new habits and short enough for the garrison to pretend they'd been temporary. By the second day the pamphlets started — not ours, not yet, but others'. Handbills against the levy, crude woodcuts of bakers weeping over scales, verses about the Lord Treasurer's waistcoat that were funnier than they were legal. Every wall in Vellmora wore them by Thursday morning. Every wall wore their torn ghosts by Thursday night, because the garrison scraped them off with painters' knives, and by Friday morning the walls wore fresh ones, and I realized I was watching the argument conducted entirely in our medium. Ink versus knife. Impressions versus scraping. We had done this to the city, we printers. We had taught it that an opinion could be reproduced.

On the second night a stone came through the window of the chandlery next door, and on the third night one came through ours.

It missed the press. That's the whole of the miracle and I've decided not to inflate it: a paving stone, thrown by somebody who probably couldn't have named a single thing we'd printed, came through the display glass, crossed the shop, and stopped against the frame of the iron hand-press my father bought the year I was born, leaving a dent in the wood and a spiderweb in the glass and not one broken sort anywhere.

Doss stood over it the next morning while Ilse boarded the window, and he said, conversationally, to the press rather than to me:

"Loyalists, most likely. Or just hungry. Doesn't matter — the garrison will log it as damage to a suspected seditious premise. Which puts this shop on a list now. A different list than Tuesday's. Worse lists come after this one."

"Is there something you're recommending?" I asked.

"I'm recommending that you tell me what you're carrying." He straightened up and looked at me directly, and for once there was no boring manner anywhere about him. "You crossed the river during a riot to fetch something from a safehouse. You came back with it hidden in your clothes — don't bother, I've dressed your father's proofs for twenty years and I know what a woman looks like when she's wearing paper. There's a council over there that won't act, a city over here that's acting without them, and you in the middle with the one document that might aim all of this at the men who deserve it. I can stand in front of doors, Casper. I'm very good at doors. But I'd like to know what the doors are for."

Every instinct my father installed in me said: give him a lie, a good one, the kind with true things in it. And I opened my mouth to do it, and what came out instead was:

"No."

Just that. Doss blinked, actually blinked, and I understood I had surprised him, which frightened me more than anything the lieutenant's list could have done, because men like Doss — careful men, patient men, men who square type sticks when their hands want opinions — do not ask questions they cannot afford to have refused.

"No," I said again, and found the ground under it as I stood. "You've earned my thanks and my wages and my silence about whatever it is you're going to ask me for later. You haven't earned the letter. When you tell me what you want — the precise price, Doss, not the hint of it — then I'll decide what I know that's worth that much. Not before."

The silence went on long enough that I heard Ilse stop hammering outside.

"All right," Doss said at last, softly, and picked up his apron. "Later, then."

But as he turned away I saw him glance — quickly, habitually, the way a man checks a pocket watch he has owned for years — at the bank of type cases along the north wall. At the whole bank, not any single case. Memorizing the arrangement, perhaps. Or confirming it.

Or pricing it.

That night I moved the letter out of the broken sorts and into a place so stupid it has never once been searched: inside the specimen book of my father's funeral type, shelved among forty other specimen books exactly like it, in a shop where nobody reads. Then I sat up with the lamp until Ilse woke and took the chair from me without comment, and I lay in the dark listening to the last of the riots gutter out in the streets below, and I thought about guilt, which I had always assumed was a heavy thing, and discovered it is actually a light thing, a paper thing, easily carried and impossible to burn.

Because here is what I could not stop composing, in the dark, in ems and picas: the raiders had come to the shop and found nothing, and Doss had sent them away satisfied, and I had thanked him. And a man who can make you grateful for a debt is halfway to collecting it. My father taught me that letters outlive men. He neglected to teach me that some men live like letters — patient in the case, undamaged-looking, waiting years for the one composition that gives them a word.

Three days after the riots ended, Doss knocked on the upstairs door and asked, politely, if we could discuss the price of ink.

I knew before I opened it that he didn't mean ink.
