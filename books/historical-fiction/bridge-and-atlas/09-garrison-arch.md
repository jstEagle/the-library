## 9. Garrison Arch

There is a particular cruelty in the way Vellmora builds its checkpoints: always at the mouth of something beautiful. The Customs House squatted beside the fish market, so that the smell of honest trade covered the smell of dishonest government. The Bridge of Atlas spans the river at its widest, most silver stretch, where on a clear evening you can see the lamplighters working both banks at once, like two hands closing a book. And the Garrison Arch — the second of our three doors, the one I had privately been calling *the bad one* since Casper first sketched our route in pencil — stood astride the Processional Way exactly where the road crests the hill and gives you your first full view of the river city, all its spires and cranes and smoke, laid out like a gift you are about to be charged for.

The arch itself had been built to celebrate some victory or other three hundred years dead. Now it celebrated nothing but the garrison's talent for inconvenience. They had bricked up the side passages, leaving a single throat of a gate, twelve feet wide, through which everything the city swallowed had to pass: carts, coffins, wedding parties, fish, hay, and us.

Casper walked beside me with the consignment cart's shafts in her fists, playing the hauler, which suited her. She has what I have come to think of as a workman's face — not plain, exactly, but *occupied*, a face busy with internal arithmetic about leading and kerning even when she is being interrogated. Nobody looks twice at a face that isn't looking back at them.

I played the merchant's factor. This required me to be slightly stupid, slightly bored, and slightly better dressed than my employer — a combination I found insultingly easy.

The letter rode between us, in plain sight, folded into a printer's proof of an order form for mourning stationery. That was Casper's idea, and I still maintain it was either the cleverest thing anyone has ever done or the maddest. Hide a document in bureaucracy, she said, and no one sees it. Everyone in Vellmora has learned to look *past* paperwork the way you look past a beggar. The eye slides off it. The eye is trained to slide.

We joined the queue at midmorning, which was a mistake I will own. Midmorning is when the garrison changes its sergeant of the gate, and a change of sergeants means a change of mood, and a change of mood means they search things they were ignoring an hour ago. We sat in that queue for the better part of two hours while carts ahead of us were emptied, prodded, and repacked by soldiers with the brisk resentment of men doing work beneath their rank. A woman selling live geese ahead of us lost one to the mud and nearly started a riot over it. The goose won.

I used the time badly. I used it looking at the arch.

Carved into its keystone, worn nearly smooth now, there is a figure carrying another figure on his shoulders — Atlas, holding up not the sky but a man, which the sculptor had apparently thought more moving. Some student had chalked beneath it, in letters half scrubbed away by rain: *WE ALL CARRY OR WE ARE CARRIED.* Someone else had chalked underneath that: *OR WE ARE WEIGHED.* The garrison hadn't bothered to scrub that one yet. Perhaps they liked it. Perhaps they thought it was theirs.

Casper saw me reading it. "Don't," she said, without moving her lips much. Haulers don't chat with their factors. "Save your wit."

"I wasn't going to be witty. I was going to be terrified."

"Be terrified quietly. It weighs less."

That is Casper Oyelaran entire, if you want her in one sentence: terror, weighed, and found lighter than expected.

* * *

The trouble began — as trouble at checkpoints reliably does — not with the letter, not with the forged pass, not with anything we had prepared for, but with a face.

The new captain came down from the gatehouse at a walk that said he owned the hill, the river, and possibly the concept of morning. He was perhaps fifty, gray at the temples in the deliberate way of a man who trims it rather than fights it, with a cavalry sword he was too old to be wearing and too vain to stop. He stopped at our cart because ours was next, but before he looked at the cart, he looked at me.

And stopped.

You know the feeling. You have felt it, everyone has felt it, the cold drop when a stranger's gaze snags on you and stays, not with desire, not with suspicion, but with *recognition* — the worst of the three, because desire passes and suspicion can be answered, but recognition cannot be argued with. It simply is. He knew my face. He did not know from where, and that gap — that little bridge of confusion his mind was trying to cross — was going to burn us down.

"Aldous Vey," the captain said.

I laughed. I want it noted for history that I laughed, quickly and easily, the way a person laughs at a joke made at a party by someone slightly drunk. "Everyone says that lately," I said. "Since they hanged him. Half the pamphlets in this city have his face on them; I suppose some of it washes off onto the rest of us."

It was a good answer. I want that recorded too, because what happened next was not my fault in any fair accounting. The captain didn't hear the answer. He was still looking. And the problem — the whole ruinous problem — was that Aldous and I did not merely resemble each other the way siblings do, sharing a nose here, a jaw there. We shared the whole architecture. Our father used to dress us in each other's coats as a joke. Our mother claimed she could tell us apart only by our hands, and even then she'd been lying to spare one of us the knowledge of how interchangeable we were. When Aldous smiled with just the left side of his mouth, which he did when he thought he was being charming, we smiled identically, because it was never his smile. It was the family's.

The captain had known him. I could see the knowing settle into him, layer by layer: the tilt of the head, the narrowing, the small unconscious straightening that men do when a ghost walks past their checkpoint in daylight.

"You knew him," I said, before he could speak again. Take the ground first. It is the oldest rule of crossing borders, older than borders: whoever names the thing first owns the shape of it. "You must have. You said his name like a man greeting someone."

"I reviewed his case." His voice had gone flat, official, which meant it had gone frightened, though he didn't know that about himself yet. "Before the execution. Standard procedure. I read his confession."

"Did you?" I said. "What was he like?"

That wrong-footed him beautifully. Captains are asked questions by superiors, by clerks, by their own consciences at three in the morning. They are not asked questions by factors standing next to geese. "What was he—"

"On paper, I mean." I gestured at the cart, at the world, at nothing. "My employer prints. Mourning cards, ledgers, the usual respectable drivel. There was talk of commissioning a commemorative — the regime does love a martyr it manufactured — and someone suggested copying his hand for the heading. So I've been studying specimens." I held up the proof, let it catch the light, let him see the columns of type and the sample lines of script without letting him read a word. "But paper tells you nothing, does it? Paper is a liar. What was he like?"

"He was," the captain said slowly, "not what his confession said he was."

Somewhere behind us a soldier barked at a carter to move along. Neither of us moved.

"That's not an answer a man gives casually," I said, softer now. "Captain. May I ask your name? For my report. My employer will want to know who spoke so frankly."

He told me. Captain Erren Halvath. I gave it the respect of repetition, and I watched him decide — visibly, like watching a man decide whether to step off a ledge — that I was harmless. That I was a merchant's creature with ink under her fingernails and no politics at all. People decide that about me remarkably fast. I have spent thirty years making sure of it, and it has cost me, as I keep discovering, rather more than it has earned me.

Then he ruined everything by being human.

"Your face," he said. "Forgive me. But you have his face. Not the pamphlet face — the pamphlets flattered him, gave him cheekbones he rented for the occasion. I mean the living one. I sat across a table from that man for six hours while he confessed to crimes he committed with more style than they credited him for, and he had a habit—" Halvath touched the corner of his own mouth, left side. "When he thought he'd gotten the better of me. Just the one side. You did it just now."

* * *

I want to tell you what it is like to erase yourself in front of a witness.

It is not dramatic. That's the first thing. No thunder, no pulse of fear you can point to afterward. It is administrative. You feel yourself becoming a clerk of your own erasure, filing away pieces: the smile goes first — kill the smile, kill the left corner specifically — then the posture, because Aldous stood like a fencer and I stand like a tired woman, so become the tired woman, drop the shoulder, let the hands hang. Then the voice. My voice and his were close enough that our mother used to answer both of us from the kitchen without checking which child had called. Flatten it. Add the market rasp. Become someone whose mother would never have recognized her.

And the terrible part, the part I did not understand until that morning under the arch, is that you do it *gladly*. That is the trap of survival. In the moment, erasing yourself feels like relief. The self is the dangerous cargo. The self is the thing that gets people hanged. Strip it off, hand it over, walk through the gate as nobody, and nobody gets hurt.

You only find out later what you handed across the counter, and whether you get it back.

"I'm sorry," I said to Captain Halvath, and I made my voice bored, faintly offended, the voice of a woman who has heard this comparison too many times and profited by it not at all. "You're the fourth man this month. When they put his face on every wall in the district, they put it in everyone's eye. My employer says I should charge for the resemblance — he thinks there's money in it. Widow's-mourning commissions, mostly. Grief makes people sentimental about faces." I turned to Casper. "We're holding up the line. Tell the man about the consignment so we can all go home."

Casper produced our forged pass with the flawless dullness of a woman who has done this nine hundred times, which, thanks to her father's discarded official font and one long criminal night of composition, she technically had. Halvath barely glanced at it. That was the other lesson of the morning, and it chilled me worse than the recognition had: he didn't care about our papers. Papers were furniture. He cared about the ghost, and the ghost was walking away from him, and some buried, decent part of him wanted very badly to call out after it and didn't dare.

"Wait," he said anyway.

Casper's knuckles went white on the cart shafts. I felt it more than saw it; we had spent a week learning each other's weather.

"The commemorative," Halvath said. "If it's ever printed. If your employer ever prints anything about Vey — I'd want to see it. Before publication. As a professional courtesy." And then, in a voice pitched precisely for the two of us and no one else, a voice that had clearly rehearsed nothing and surprised its owner completely: "His confession named three ministers. The record now names none. Someone should remember what the paper used to say."

I looked at him. He looked at the keystone, at the chalked words — *OR WE ARE WEIGHED* — with the expression of a man who has just realized which side of the scale he is standing on.

"Someone should," I agreed, in my flat market voice, in my erased and borrowed life. "Though I couldn't say who. I'm a stranger to all of it. Good day, Captain."

* * *

We cleared the arch. I want that sentence to sit alone for a moment, because neither of us believed it while it was happening. We rolled through that stone throat with our contraband in plain sight, past soldiers who had already lost interest, out onto the long descending road toward the river, and for a hundred yards neither Casper nor I spoke. The cart wheels talked instead, that wooden clatter that fills silence the way sawdust fills a crack.

Then Casper said, "You did it again."

"Did what?"

"The thing with your mouth. When you got the better of him. Left corner." She didn't look at me. Her eyes stayed on the horse's ears, on the road. "He saw it. I saw him see it. And then you killed it so fast that I almost doubted I'd seen it myself."

"It seemed advisable."

"It was advisable." A pause, the length of four wheel-turns. "It was also the first time I've seen you look like him. Really like him. Everyone talks about the resemblance and I never — you're Ilse. You're Ilse, you're soaked-river-water-and-nerve, you're the woman who lied to a customs clerk about a specimen book with her brother's handwriting in it. You're not the pamphlet face." Another pause. Longer. "And then under that arch you weren't you, and it worked, and I hated how well it worked."

"There's a compliment buried in there somewhere," I said. "I'll dig it out later, when my hands have stopped shaking."

"They're shaking?"

"Only since the keystone."

She reached across without stopping the cart and gripped my wrist once, hard, and let go, and that was the whole conversation, because we were Casper and Ilse and we rationed everything — trust, ink, comfort — against the journey ahead.

But I will tell you what I carried away from the Garrison Arch, down that long hill, with the letter riding in plain sight among the mourning orders. Not relief. Relief is for people who have finished something. What I carried was the bill for what I'd done. Because Halvath had looked at me and seen Aldous, and I had stood there and made myself into a stranger to my own name, my own face, my own family — and the awful efficiency of it, the ease, the *relief* of it — that was the true price of the checkpoint. Not the risk. The risk I could pay all day. The price was this: I was getting good at disappearing. And a woman who gets good at disappearing should occasionally check whether anyone is still holding the other end of her.

Ahead of us the river showed itself at last, wide and brown and patient, and beyond it, somewhere in the print quarter's crooked lanes, waited the council that Casper believed would act, and Merrick the blind compositor, and the third checkpoint, the worst one, the Bridge of Atlas, where soldiers weigh every cart.

I had erased myself once that morning and been paid for it in passage.

I did not yet know how many more times I would have to do it — or that the last time, on the bridge, I would be erased entirely, taken off the face of the city with my name still in my mouth, unfinished.
