## 10. What Can Be Carried

The safehouse was a candlemaker's storeroom behind a shuttered tannery, which meant the whole place smelled of tallow and old hides and the particular sourness of a river city trying very hard to pretend it wasn't afraid. There were two chairs, one table, a cot that had been built for someone considerably smaller than either Ilse or me, and a window the size of a prayer book, set so high in the wall that you could see nothing but sky and, if you stood on the table, the top of the Garrison Arch two streets off.

I did not stand on the table. I was not interested in admiring the architecture of the thing we'd have to pass through at dawn.

Ilse was asleep on the cot — actually asleep, which I envied with a purity I usually reserved for other people's money. She slept like a soldier, flat on her back, hands folded, as though she might be inspected at any moment even by God. Her boots stood beside her, upright, laces tucked in. Everything about Ilse Vey was packed and ready to move. It occurred to me that she had been living out of her own hands for months now, maybe years, and that I had only been doing it for three days.

I couldn't sleep, so I did what my father taught me to do instead of sleeping. I opened his kit.

* * *

Every compositor has a kit. Not the shop cases, not the great banks of type that live in their frames like furniture — the *kit*. The case you take when everything else stays behind. My father's was a shallow wooden box, walnut, its corners rubbed to the color of dark honey from thirty years of being carried, and inside it he had arranged his life according to a logic that made perfect sense to him and none whatsoever to anyone else.

A bodkin with a cracked handle. Two composing sticks, one brass and good, one iron and crooked, which he kept anyway because, he said, "the crooked one tells the truth." A lead mallet no bigger than my fist. A burnisher worn concave where his thumb had ridden it for decades. A lump of printer's wax wrapped in oilcloth. And type — always type — a few pounds of sorts in paper twists, sorted by hand into little bundles tied with string, each bundle labeled in his cramped backward script: *10pt Baskerville, worn; 12pt Caslon, good; long-s, do not use unless desperate.*

He had written *do not use unless desperate* on the long esses. I remember laughing at that when I was nine. I am not laughing now. Now it reads like scripture.

I untied the bundles one by one and counted them, because counting is what I do when the world refuses to hold still. The letters held still. That is the entire secret of my profession and possibly my personality: letters hold still. You can trust a lowercase g the way you can trust almost nothing else in Vellmora this week.

And while my hands sorted, my head went where it goes when the shop is quiet and the grief gets loud. Back to Ostrava. Back to the winter I was seven.

* * *

You have to understand something about my father, and about me, and about why we measure things the way we do.

We came to Vellmora the way most people come to Vellmora — running. There was trouble in Ostrava that year, the kind of trouble that has a name nobody will say out loud and a schedule everyone knows by heart. One night the windows started going in. I remember the sound more than anything else: glass breaking all down the street in a rhythm, almost musical, like someone playing a tune on a very bad instrument.

My father woke me by lifting me out of bed already dressed. He didn't pack clothes. I want that understood, because people always assume you pack clothes. He went to the shop and he stood in front of the great wall of type cases — forty years of his life, every font he'd ever bought or inherited or traded a coat for — and he stood there for perhaps ten seconds, which felt like an hour, and then he took down one case.

One. Out of hundreds.

He dumped the loose sort into a drawer without sorting them — an act that would have horrified him in any other season — and filled the empty compartments with the things he chose. His best stick. The burnisher. The wax. A handful of sorts in twists. And then, in the compartment where the quads should go, he put our papers. And in the compartment where the em-quads lived, he put his wedding ring and my mother's, because my mother had died two years before and the ring had nowhere else to be.

Then he strapped the case to my back.

I want to be honest about this part, because it's the part I've never told anyone, including myself until quite recently: I screamed. I was seven years old, and my father put a box full of metal letters on my back and told me I could carry it or he would carry me, and I could not carry both, and I screamed at him that he cared more about his letters than about me.

And he knelt down in the doorway of the burning quarter of Ostrava, with the glass music playing, and he said — and I can recite this the way other people recite prayers —

"Casper. Clothes can be replaced in any town we reach. Bread can be begged. But these letters are ours. No one gave them to us. No one can take them back. Whatever else they burn tonight, they cannot burn what you carry in your hands."

So I carried it. Four miles to the river road, then eleven days to Vellmora, and I complained the entire way, and the case weighed approximately the weight of a small sinning child's entire capacity for grievance, and I hated those letters with a hatred so pure it was nearly love.

And when we arrived — when we stood in the foreign mud of the Vellmoran customs yard, stateless, penniless, smelling of smoke and each other's fear — a clerk looked us up and down and said, "What's your trade?"

My father said, "Printer," and set the case on the clerk's desk, and pulled out the stick, and composed a line right there, standing, in under a minute: *THE OYELARANS ARE HERE AND WILL WORK.*

The clerk read it. Something in his face changed — softened, or hardened, I've thought about it my whole life and I still don't know which. He stamped our papers. He said, "Gutter Lane. Tell them Marta sent you." And that was how we got a shop.

Not with money. Not with names. With a line of type, composed standing up, in a stranger's office, in under a minute.

That is what my father meant when he said letters outlive men. Not that they're immortal — ink fades, lead wears, fires happen. He meant that a letter is the smallest possible unit of survival. A man can lose everything except what fits in his hands, and if what fits in his hands is language, he has not lost everything. He has lost his house and kept his voice. And a man with his voice can rebuild the house.

I learned that at seven years old with a bruised spine and a mouth full of grievances. I am learning it again at twenty-six in a candlemaker's storeroom with a forged pass in my boot and a dead man's letter sewn into my coat lining, and this time there is no screaming. This time I understand the arithmetic.

* * *

Here is the arithmetic, since I'm confessing things:

Everything I own that matters currently fits in a space smaller than this table. Father's kit. The letter. The forged customs pass, which I made myself and which I check roughly every four minutes the way some people check a wound. A spare shirt. Half a loaf. Ilse.

Ilse, who is asleep, and whose breathing is the only clock in this room I trust.

I keep looking at her and doing sums I don't want to do. Sums like: *she is her brother's sister.* Sums like: *the captain at the Garrison Arch looked at her face for six seconds too long, and I counted, because counting is what I do.* Sums like: *if they take her tomorrow on the bridge, what exactly am I carrying across it, and for whom, and does it matter if the person who wrote it is gone?*

Father would say yes, it matters. Father would say the words are the cargo and the body is merely the cart, and carts can be replaced. But father also ran back into a smoking street once — I found this out years later, from old Sef Brandt, of all people, back when Brandt and Papa were rivals the way brothers are rivals — he ran back for a single drawer of type, and he'd have burned alive for it if a neighbor hadn't dragged him out by the collar.

Carts are not so easily replaced as all that. I know it. He knew it too. He just preferred the version of the truth that fit in a case.

* * *

Somewhere past the middle of the night I heard the cot creak and found Ilse sitting up, watching me sort type by moonlight like a madwoman at her devotions.

"You're counting," she said.

"I'm resting."

"You're counting. Your lips move. You count the way my brother used to proof-read in his sleep — silently, furiously, as though the errors might escape if he stopped."

"Aldous proofed in his sleep?"

"He proofed everywhere. He read the prison ration lists for typographical errors." She smiled, and it was the first smile I'd seen on her that had any of the letter's warmth in it — the handwriting come briefly alive. "He found three."

I laughed before I could stop myself, and the laugh came out strange and wet, and we both pretended it hadn't.

"Come here," I said. "Look at this."

She came and sat on the floor beside me, which told me something about how far she'd fallen in the world, or risen — with Ilse I genuinely could not tell anymore — and I put my father's kit open between us like a map.

"Every compositor has one of these," I said. "The case you take when you leave in the night. Most of them are the same. Stick, bodkin, wax, type. But look—" I turned the case so the moon caught the compartments. "He packed it like a sentence. Tools first, the verbs. Then the sorts, the nouns. Papers here, where the quads go — the spaces, the things that hold the words apart. Do you see? He arranged our lives in case form. Because if you can carry the alphabet, you can carry anything written in it. A country. A family. A testimony."

Ilse was quiet a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had gone careful, the way voices go when they're stepping around something sharp.

"My brother wrote his last letter knowing it might weigh more than he did," she said. "He wrote it knowing it would have to be carried by strangers across a river. He spent his whole life telling the council that words were heavier than soldiers, and they nodded politely, and then he let himself be 'executed' to prove it." Her jaw tightened. "He always did prefer a demonstration to an argument."

"Family trait," I said.

"Family trait," she agreed.

We sat with the kit between us, two women and a dead-alive man's handwriting, and outside somewhere a patrol went past — boots on cobbles, unhurried, four men at least — and neither of us moved, and neither of us breathed much, and the boots passed, and the silence came back like water filling a footprint.

When they were gone, Ilse said, "What weighs the most? In there."

I considered honestly. "The wax, pound for pound. But the papers." I touched the compartment where the quads went, where the rings had gone, where the spaces lived. "The papers. They're the lightest thing in the case and they've decided every heavy thing in my life."

"That's not an answer," she said.

"It's the only kind I have."

* * *

Near dawn I repacked the kit, because packing is praying, in my family. And as I closed the lid I noticed something I had somehow never noticed in nineteen years of owning this box — or rather, something my father had hidden so well that noticing it felt like being handed a letter addressed to me specifically, postmarked from the grave.

Under the false bottom, beneath the wax and the long esses marked *do not use unless desperate*, there was a thin packet of oilcloth, and inside the oilcloth, a second stick — a travel stick, folding, the kind smugglers use — and wrapped around it, a slip of paper in his backward script.

It said: *If you are reading this, you are carrying the case somewhere you shouldn't be. Good girl. Remember: they can search your pockets, your cart, your coat seams. They cannot search a line of type. Memorize the words and set them on the far side of any wall they build. Lead is patient. So are we.*

I sat very still for a while.

Then I woke Ilse, because dawn was coming, and the Garrison Arch with it, and because I had just learned that my father — cautious, careful, rule-keeping Papa Oyelaran, who paid his taxes and hid his pamphlets behind his funeral type and never once said the word *revolution* above a whisper — had built this exact moment into a wooden box fifteen years ago and left instructions.

"Get your boots," I said. "Before we cross that bridge, I need you to memorize a letter. All of it. Word for word."

Ilse looked at me, and understood instantly, the way printers understand each other — without a wasted character.

"And if we lose the paper?" she asked.

"Then we haven't lost it," I said, and slung my father's case onto my back, seven years old again, four miles to the river, everything I own in my hands. "Paper burns. Sorts wear. But a line of type set on the far side of their wall—"

"—is a wall they can't tear down," she finished.

Outside, the bells began. Not the alarm bells. The levy bells — the ones the ministers had ordered rung to summon the crowds they thought they could manage. They sounded, to my ear, like a compose stick being rapped twice on the stone: *get to work.*

Downriver, on the Bridge of Atlas, a blind compositor named Merrick was waiting for a letter. What he was going to get instead, I was beginning to suspect, was a lesson in what can be carried.
