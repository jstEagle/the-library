## 11. The Council That Waits

Merrick's workshop smelled of lead and lamp oil and, beneath both, of a man who had stopped believing anyone would come. Casper had told me to knock three times, pause, then twice more — "like a compositor setting a bad line and correcting it" — but the door was already unlatched when I tried it. It swung inward on a room so dark I thought for one foolish moment we had walked into a cupboard.

Then the lamps came up — all four of them at once, which was the first sign that our host knew exactly where everything in that room stood, down to the match tin — and I saw him.

He was small. That was my first impression and it stayed with me: a small man in a large chair, hands folded on his knees like two sleeping animals he'd been keeping quiet. His eyes were the color of milk gone slightly off, and they did not track us as we entered. They pointed at a fixed spot somewhere over my left shoulder and stayed there with an obedience I found, in that moment, almost holy.

"You're late," Merrick said.

"We were careful," Casper said behind me. Her voice had changed since we'd crossed the river. On our side of Vellmora she spoke like a woman apologizing for taking up space. Over here, among her own kind, she spoke like someone reading a proof aloud: level, certain, daring you to find the error.

"Careful," Merrick repeated, tasting the word. "Careful is what they said about Aldous Vey. Careful got him a rope and a stage and six hundred witnesses." He turned his face toward us — not toward us, precisely, but toward the sound of us, which amounted to the same thing with this man. "Sit. You'll want to sit when you hear it."

I sat. Casper didn't. She stood by the door with her arms crossed, the way she'd stood through every hour of our journey across the river, as though standing were a form of vigilance and vigilance were a form of prayer.

"Merrick," she said, "this is Ilse Vey."

The old man's hands came apart. One of them rose, palm out, feeling the air between himself and me the way another man might reach into darkness for a candlestick. I let him take my hand. His fingers were hard as type metal and just as cold.

"So," he said. "The sister. The face." He turned my hand over and read my knuckles like a page. "You have his chin. Everyone says so. I wouldn't know. I only ever knew him by his voice, and yours is nothing like it. Yours is a woman's voice that has been shouting into wind."

"It has been."

"Hm." He released me. "Show me the letter."

I took it out from where it lived now, against my ribs, inside the false lining of my coat — a pocket Casper had sewn herself, badly, at three in the morning in a safehouse that smelled of boiled cabbage, swearing under her breath at the needle the whole while. I had come to love that crooked seam the way other people love saints' relics. It was evidence of care. In a city that wanted Aldous Vey dead twice over, someone had cared enough to sew badly for me.

Merrick could not read it. I knew that before I crossed the room; Casper had told me. But there is a difference between knowing a thing and watching a man hold the thing your brother died — or did not die — to write, turning it over in his blind hands, weighing it like a coin he suspects is clipped.

"How many pages?" he asked.

"Six," I said. "In his hand. Sealed with the prison records office stamp, because that's how it left the building and I never dared break it open again after the first time."

"The first time?"

"On the night I took it." I heard myself speaking faster and made myself stop. This was the part I had rehearsed on the boat, in the customs queue, in the dark of the Garrison Arch's shadow: say it plainly, Ilse. Plainness is armor. "Aldous wrote it in the last weeks of what they called his imprisonment. He knew they meant to fake his death — he'd worked it out from the rotation of the guards, which is such a printerly way to discover your own murder that I laughed when I first read it, and then I didn't laugh for two days. He names them. Three ministers. Corvel. Hask. And the Lord Treasurer's man, Anselm Roke. Names, dates, the sum paid to the executioner's guild to provide a body. It is all there. It has been against my ribs for nine days."

Merrick held the letter flat on his knees. His fingers moved across the seal without pressing it, tracing its ridges, and I understood he was reading it the only way left to him: as an object. As weight. As craft.

"Casper Oyelaran vouches for the hand?" he asked.

"I set proofs for Vey for eleven years," Casper said from the door. "I know his letterforms the way you know your staircases. It's his. Every ascender, every quirk. He crossed his t's left to right like a man in a hurry and always has."

"Always," Merrick said softly. "Yes. Always." He was silent a moment, and in that silence I let myself do a dangerous thing. I let myself hope. Hope is a sort of ink, I've since learned; it goes into everything it touches and never comes fully out.

"Then take it to the council," I said. "Tonight if you can. Tomorrow at the latest. Once they've read it—"

"They can't read it," Merrick said.

The sentence went past me the first time, the way a cart goes past whose danger you only feel in your teeth afterward.

"What?"

"Half of them are functionally illiterate, girl — no, don't look offended, they're printers' councilmen, not scholars; they know ink, not letters, and half of them can't parse a sentence longer than their own names. And the other half—" He set the letter down on the table beside him with terrible gentleness. "The other half can read perfectly well. That's the problem."

* * *

He poured wine — found the bottles, the cups, my elbow, all without a fumble, and I thought: forty years in the dark, and this man has never once needed to be afraid of his own room. What must it be to have that kind of certainty? I had crossed a river, forged passes I couldn't forge, lied to soldiers with my brother's face hanging off my own skull, and I still flinched when doors opened suddenly.

"Council met Tuesday," Merrick said. "Nine men. I'll give you the count, because counts matter. Two are ready to rise on any scrap of paper that smells of smoke. Four want to wait for the levy protests — bread's the spark, they say, not martyrdom, let the crowd gather first. And three—" he drank, set the cup down with a click that sounded like a full stop "—three sent word to the Garrison last week. Quietly. Asking whether the amnesty for informants would extend to masters who merely *looked away*."

Casper made a sound low in her throat. Not a word. A word would have been too much for her.

"They haven't sold you yet," Merrick went on. "They're pricing you. There's a difference, in this city, and it buys you perhaps a week. Perhaps less. The riots started tonight, you know. Bread levy. They burned the tollhouse on Tanner's Row." He tilted his head. "You passed through it. I can smell the smoke on your coats."

"We saw it," Casper said.

"Saw it or lit it?"

"Saw it. Others lit it. We had cargo."

"The cargo is sitting on my table," Merrick said dryly, "and the council won't touch it. So let me tell you what they will touch, because I've known these men thirty years and I can tell you exactly how each of their spines bends." He raised one finger. "They will act on the letter if it is delivered publicly. Publicly. Where ten witnesses become a hundred become a thousand, and backing out means confessing cowardice before the whole trade. Printers are vain creatures, Mistress Vey. We will die for our reputations long before we die for our principles. Give them a stage and they'll stand on it."

"And if it's delivered privately?" I asked, though I already knew. I'd known since he said *can't*.

"Privately, it's a liability. Six pages naming three ministers, carried by a woman with the martyr's chin? Privately, it's evidence against whoever holds it. They'd vote to burn it and thank themselves for their prudence." He smiled without warmth. "Fear doesn't make men cautious. It makes them precise about their cowardice."

* * *

I stood up because sitting still had become impossible, and walked the length of the workshop, touching things. A case of sorts — I knew the shape even blindfolded now; Casper had drilled me until I could field a California job case in the pitch dark, which she claimed was training and I suspected was grief wearing work clothes. A stack of dampened paper. A composing stone worn into a shallow valley by decades of the same hands.

"Aldous believed in this council," I said, to the stone, to nobody. "He wrote to them every month for fifteen years. When they jailed him he said — in the letter — he said *the council will know what to do with this.*" My voice did something I hated. It cracked. "He trusted them."

"He trusted typesetters," Merrick said. "It's different. Typesetters are trustworthy; councils are committees, and God preserves us from committees." He listened to me breathe, I think, the way you listen for weather. Then: "Come here. Both of you. Sit, and I'll tell you what the bridge has to do with it."

We sat.

"The Bridge of Atlas," he said. "Third checkpoint. Weighs every cart that crosses, yes? And on Thursday the levy protests converge there — everyone angry about bread meets everyone angry about everything, and the garrison doubles the guard and triples the searches, and every printer in Vellmora knows that the one place you cannot move seditious paper on Thursday is across the Bridge of Atlas." He spread his hands. "Which is why it must happen there."

"That's madness," Casper said flatly.

"That's *theater*," Merrick corrected, "and theater is the only language fear speaks fluently. Listen. Thursday, noon. The protest crowds will jam the bridge approaches — thousands of bodies, merchants and dyers and fishwives, everyone furious and no one organized. If the letter is read aloud from the bridge steps — if Vey's own voice comes off those pages into a thousand ears at once — no councilman can deny hearing it. No informant can un-hear it. The council will be forced to move with the city instead of behind it, which is the only direction they've ever been brave enough to go." He paused. "And better than that. Read aloud, the letter stops being evidence. Evidence can be seized. Six pages can be confiscated at a checkpoint by a bored sergeant — you've seen it done; don't tell me you haven't planned around it. But a thousand people who have *heard* it?" He tapped the table once, sharply, a compositor's rap. "You cannot confiscate a memory. Words in ten thousand heads weigh nothing and cannot be weighed. That's the trick of it, girl. That's the whole trade. Paper burns. Type melts. But a line once set in living minds stays justified forever."

I looked at Casper. Casper was looking at Merrick with an expression I had learned to read by then — her jaw set slightly forward, her thumb running along the edge of her sleeve, which meant she was doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"The bridge steps," she said. "Under double guard. During a riot."

"During a *protest*," Merrick said serenely. "History will sort out the vocabulary later."

"And who reads it?" I asked. "Because I'll tell you now, if you're imagining me standing up there with my brother's chin and my brother's name—"

"I'm imagining exactly that," Merrick said, "because it's exactly right. A stranger reads six pages, the crowd shrugs. A Vey reads them, the crowd kneels. Your face isn't a burden, Mistress Vey. It's the largest font in the city, and Thursday you get to print with it." He turned that ruined gaze on me, and I had the strange sensation of being seen more thoroughly than any sighted man had ever managed. "Unless you'd rather go home."

Home. The word landed strangely. I had spent nine days learning that home was a crooked seam against my ribs and a woman who swore at needles.

* * *

Afterward — after the plan had been sketched in pencil on the back of a duty roster Merrick kept for exactly this purpose ("pencil," he said, "because plans should be erasable; it's the only mercy I allow myself") — Casper and I stood outside in the alley while Merrick barred his door behind us, four locks, each one sounding like a syllable.

The sky over the river was the orange of a bruise healing wrong. Smoke from Tanner's Row. Somewhere east, a crowd was singing something with the word *bread* in every verse.

"Well," I said.

"Don't," Casper said.

"I haven't said anything."

"You were going to say it's impossible."

"I was going to say," I said, "that I crossed a river, a customs house, and a garrison arch for a council that needs a riot as a delivery mechanism, and I'm trying very hard not to find that funny."

Something happened at the corner of her mouth. In Casper Oyelaran, that constituted laughter.

"He's right, though," she said quietly. "About memory. My father used to say a locked shop keeps nothing safe except the lock. What's carried in the head—" She stopped. She looked at the river, black and patient under the burning sky, and I watched her decide something, though I didn't yet know what.

Thursday. Noon. The Bridge of Atlas, with my brother's face and my brother's words and a thousand strangers between me and the garrison's muskets. I put my hand flat against my ribs, over the crooked seam, over the six pages that had become suddenly not a deliverable but a performance, and found that my heart was doing something I refused to call fear.

Hope curdles, they say. Standing in that alley, I learned the truth of it: hope doesn't curdle. It *hardens*. Into something with edges. Something you can be cut by.

"Thursday," I said.

"Thursday," Casper said, and then, in the voice of a woman reading a proof she already knows contains an error she can't yet locate: "We'll need a bigger press."
