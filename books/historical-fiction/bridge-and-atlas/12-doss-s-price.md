## 12. Doss's Price

Merrick arrived at the shop an hour past midnight, guided through the paper district by Ilse on his arm and by some older navigation that lived in a blind man's feet. He came in sideways, like a rumour, and stood in the middle of the composing room with his head tilted as though he were reading the ceiling.

"Oak beams," he said. "Iron stove to your left, cold. And someone has left a galley of type standing loose on the stone. Twelve-point, no spacing material. It'll be pieing itself by morning."

He was right. I had left it standing because I'd been too tired to distribute it, and I felt the specific shame of a compositor caught in bad housekeeping by a stranger who couldn't even see it. That is how trust begins sometimes — not with oaths but with a man correcting your sloppiness before he's been offered a chair.

"You're Merrick," I said, unnecessarily.

"And you're Oyelaran's daughter," he said. "I set two seasons beside him at Kestrel & Vane, before you were born. He used to say a case should be so clean a man could find a comma in the dark." He smiled, and the smile was aimed slightly wrong — over my left shoulder, where the window was. "I found one once. Proved him right. He never forgave me for it."

We put him in the back room, the one where Father had slept the last winter of his life, and Ilse sat with him while I banked the stove, and for about four hours I let myself believe we had accomplished something. We had carried the letter across three checkpoints. We had delivered it into the hands of the printers' council, or at least into the hands of its ears. The council would act. The letter would be read aloud. Aldous Vey's voice, which the state had hanged in effigy and declared extinguished, would come off a press by week's end and the ministers named in it would have to answer for the staging of his death.

That was the story I told myself while I banked the coals. I have since learned that the stories you tell yourself while doing small domestic tasks are the ones most likely to be lies, because your hands are busy and your judgment is tired.

* * *

Doss knocked at six.

Nobody knocks at their own shop door at six in the morning unless they want something or know something, and Doss had been our foreman for nine years, long enough that knocking was a formality he usually ignored. So I opened the bolt with my pulse already climbing, and there he was in the grey light with snow in his collar and his face arranged into what he believed was an expression of concern.

He had a big, careful face, Doss. A face built for committees. He'd come to us from the Kestrel & Vane bankruptcy like a fixture included in the sale, and Father had kept him because he was good with the apprentices and better with suppliers, and because — this is the part I keep returning to — Father distrusted him mildly, comfortably, the way you distrust weather. Mild distrust is a luxury. It assumes the thing you distrust will stay outside.

"Casper." He stamped the snow off his boots without coming in, which was unlike him; Doss always came in, took the good chair, helped himself to the coffee as if the shop's inventory were partly his salary. "You'll want to hear this inside."

"Then say it inside."

He looked past me down the corridor, and I watched his eyes do it — the small, professional sweep, taking in the wet footprints, the two sets, one of them favouring the wall where a blind man had trailed his fingers. Nine years of watching my hands at the case, and I had never once thought about what all that watching accumulated into.

"There's a warrant being drawn," he said quietly. "For a woman answering Ilse Vey's description, and for whoever's sheltering her. It reaches this street tomorrow. Perhaps tonight, if the levy riots give them an excuse to search early."

The cold came up through the floor stones and into my shins. "Who told you about warrants?"

"Casper." He said my name the way you'd steady a ladder. "Let me come in."

I let him. That was my first error, and I want it recorded that I knew it was an error while I was making it, and made it anyway, because nine years is nine years, and because he had carried Father's coffin on his shoulder along with Sef Brandt and two men from the typefounders', and you do not easily convert a coffin-bearer into an enemy. The mind resists the arithmetic. It keeps trying to round the numbers down.

He took the good chair. Of course he did. He looked at the cold stove, at the standing galley — which someone had since distributed, and I saw him register that too — and then he laid his hands flat on his knees like a man settling accounts.

"Merrick can't stay here," he said.

"He's already here."

"That's what I'm saying can't continue." He rubbed his jaw. "Listen to me now, girl, because I'm going to say things once. The council won't move on the letter. You've seen that already — they gave you Merrick instead of action, a listening man, a safehouse man. They're frightened, and frightened councils eat couriers. But Merrick here, under your roof, with soldiers sweeping the quarter — that's not sanctuary, that's bait in a trap with you inside it. If they take him here, they take you, Ilse, the plates, the pamphlet stock, everything. Your father's shop ends in a confiscation ledger."

Everything he said was true. That's what I clung to, in those first minutes. Everything he said was true, and truth spoken by a traitor still sounds like truth, and I wish someone had warned me — I wish Father had warned me — that the voice is no test at all.

"So what do you propose?" I asked. "Carry him to the river in a barrow?"

"I propose a trade." And there it was. I heard the floor of the conversation change under us, heard the boards go from shelter to scaffold, though nothing in his voice had shifted yet. "There's a cellar on Tanner's Row. Dry, double-entranced, owned by a woman who owes me. Merrick goes there today. I'll move him myself — nobody watches Doss hauling beer kegs. In exchange—"

"In exchange."

"The shop comes to me. Signed over. Quietly, soon, before any warrant touches it. You keep your hands and your freedom and your father's type — the personal cases, the funeral font, take them, I wouldn't touch those. But the premises, the press, the stock, the trade connections." He said it the way he'd quote a price for paper. "And your silence. No more pamphlets, no more letters, no more councils. You go be a young woman somewhere the revolution isn't."

The stove ticked as it cooled. Somewhere out in the street a cart went by loaded with something that clinked, glass probably, and a voice swore at the horse, and the ordinary city went on being ordinary, which suddenly seemed like the most obscene thing imaginable.

"You're asking me to sell the shop to buy a blind man a cellar."

"I'm asking you to save three lives and a legacy with one signature. Yes."

"And if I don't sign?"

He looked at me then, fully, and dropped the foreman, and let me see whatever it was underneath, and it wasn't cruelty. That was the terrible part. It was patience. It was a man looking at a problem he had already solved, waiting for the problem to finish pretending otherwise.

"Then tomorrow there's a warrant, and I stand aside, and I grieve at the trial, sincerely, because I did genuinely respect your father." He stood, brushed his coat down. "Think until noon. Not longer. The woman on Tanner's Row needs to know today if she's hosting a guest."

* * *

I did not think until noon. I thought for perhaps forty minutes, and then I stopped thinking and started working, because that's what Father taught me — when the mind won't move, move the hands, and the mind follows like a apprentice chasing a dropped sort.

I distributed the twelve-point galley properly. I cleaned the ink plate. I inventoried, in my head, the way you do when you might be about to lose things: the hand press bought secondhand from a synagogue that had closed, the paper stock in the loft, Father's personal cases in the vault, the proof press, the composing stones, the little brass reglet he used as a bookmark. I measured my whole life in ems and picas and asked myself what it weighed and what it could be bought for.

And somewhere in that inventory my hands stopped, over the font case where I had hidden the letter two nights ago — Ilse's letter, Vey's letter, the original, which we had carried to the council as a copied proof and then brought home again because Merrick said originals get people killed and copies get people hanged, and better the hanging than the killing, or something council-shaped and cowardly like that — and a very small, very cold fact walked into the room and sat down beside me.

Doss had known, within hours, that Ilse was in the city.

Not just that she was here — that she was *mine*. Her description in a warrant, matched to my address, matched to my sheltering of her. Warrants are drawn from reports, and reports are written by people positioned to observe, and there was exactly one person positioned to observe the comings and goings at the Oyelaran shop, who had watched me hide pamphlet plates among the funeral type for two years and never once mentioned it, whose silence I had read as loyalty when it was in fact *craft* — the silence of a man keeping an asset intact, feeding it line by line to whoever paid him, letting the little seditions run so the large one could be caught in the net.

I thought about the raid after the bread riots. The soldiers had come to our shop with a list and found almost nothing, and Doss had covered for us — sent them to the cellar, showed them the legitimate jobbing work, laughed with the sergeant about the price of ink — and I had wept with gratitude that night and promised him first month's wages of anything I ever earned. At a price, I'd thought at the time, guiltily. But what price? He had taken wages and gratitude and my unquestioning trust, all of it cheap, all of it *payment*, because a source who doesn't know he's a source works free.

And the checkpoints. God, the checkpoints. The Customs House clerk who wanted to touch our specimen book. The captain at Garrison Arch who stared at Ilse's face — her brother's face — and let her pass. I had thought we were clever. I had thought we were smugglers, Ilse and I, outwitting the state with forged passes and hidden type. But every gate had opened too easily, every search had passed over exactly the right places, and I understood now that we had moved through the city the way a piece moves across a board — legally, smoothly, and entirely according to someone else's plan. They hadn't failed to catch us. They had *escorted* us. Let the letter travel, let it lead them to the council's contact, to Merrick, to every thread worth cutting — and then cut them all at once, tomorrow, with a warrant and a search and a foreman standing aside to grieve sincerely at the trial.

The trap wasn't at the bridge. The trap was the shop. It had been the shop for nine years.

I sat down on the composing stool and laughed, briefly, horribly, the laugh of a person who has just found the flaw in a proof after ten thousand copies are printed. Ilse came to the doorway with two cups of chicory and stopped when she saw my face.

"He's been reporting since before Father died," I said. "Maybe since before I took over. Maybe — " and here my mind did the last, worst sum — "maybe the reason Father's shop survived every purge that ate its neighbours is that it was *worth more alive.* A known nest, left standing, to catch every bird that flew to it."

Ilse set the cups down very carefully, the way she does when she's frightened, making her hands useful. "What did he offer you?"

"The shop. My silence. Merrick moved to a safe cellar." I picked up the chicory and drank it scalding, because I wanted to feel something honest. "And the hell of it is he'll deliver, Ilse. He'll move Merrick today, exactly as promised, because that's the trade — he gives us the illusion of rescue, we give him the shop, and then the warrant falls on schedule anyway, only now it falls on people who signed away their assets first. Confiscation ledgers need owners of record. He's not buying a print shop. He's laundering one."

"So we don't sign."

"We don't sign." I set the cup down. My hands were steadier than I deserved. "But we don't refuse him either, because a refusal tells him we've guessed, and a man like Doss who suspects he's guessed-at changes the game board before you've finished reading it. No. We say yes. We tell him yes, gladly, gratefully — and then we spend the hours between now and noon doing what a print shop does best."

Ilse looked at me, and I watched her arrive at it, her face doing what faces do when a stranger becomes a confederate in earnest: the small widening, the settling.

"Distribution," she said.

"Distribution," I agreed. "If the council won't carry Vey's words, and Doss means to make sure no one ever carries them, then we stop carrying and start *scattering*. Every press in this quarter owes my father a favour or owes me paper. Before that warrant lands, the letter stops being a letter — one page, one original, one precious secret that can be seized — and becomes ten thousand sheets that can't be gathered faster than they're handed out. You can't arrest a pamphlet, Ilse. Believe me. Men have tried. My whole family tree is men who tried."

"And Merrick?"

"Merrick stays put until Doss collects him, because Doss moving Merrick is the one thing that proves my theory, and if I'm wrong about that—" I stopped. "No. There's no version where I'm wrong. I watched his eyes count the wet footprints. But Merrick deserves better than being the proof of it, so we warn him first, quietly, and let him choose whether to be rescued by his hunter."

Ilse was quiet a moment. Then: "You loved him. The foreman."

"Nine years is love, in a shop. It's not the passionate kind, it's the kind that knows how you take your coffee and covers your mistakes at month's end." I looked at the good chair, still holding the shape of him. "And he loved Father, or something he called love, and none of it stopped him pricing us. That's the lesson, if I take one: betrayal doesn't look like hatred. It looks like a fair offer delivered kindly at dawn."

At half past eleven I went out to where Doss waited by the stove, and I smiled at him — a compositor's smile, even, well-set, justified to both margins — and I said, "You'll have the shop, Doss. Draw the papers."

Relief moved through his big careful face like water under ice, and for one heartbeat I hated how much of my childhood was stored behind that face.

"But the signatures take a day or two, and the vault key stays with me until they're done — you understand, sentiment, my father's sorts." His eyes flicked to the vault door and away. Counted it. Filed it. "And Doss — thank you. For covering for us. All these years."

"It's been an honour, girl," he said, meaning every word, which was the most frightening thing he'd said all day.

He left at noon sharp. By twenty past, Ilse and I were in the loft pulling pamphlet stock, and I was writing in pencil on butcher's paper the list of every pressmaster in the paper district who owed the name Oyelaran, and I had just reached the B's — Brandt, Sef, god help me, Brandt first — when the church bells started ringing wrong.

Not the hour. Not the levy. The alarm pattern — the one the guilds used when a press had been raided, passed tower to tower, quarter to quarter, faster than any rider.

Ilse came to the loft door with an armful of stock pressed against her chest like a shield.

"That's St. Ferrow," she said. "And that's the river parishes answering. Casper — they're ringing *everywhere*."

Somewhere in the city, a press was running that shouldn't be, or a pressmaster had been taken, or the rising had begun without us — and below us, in the yard, I heard the yard gate open, and boots, more boots than one man makes, and Doss's voice, pleasant as a price quote, saying, "In there. The vault's the iron door. She keeps it locked, but she's sentimental."
