## 13. The Broken Font

I had been a smuggler of documents long enough to know the first rule: never go back for seconds. The second rule — never trust an office that keeps records of its own crimes — I learned from my brother, which is proof that Aldous was capable of being right about large things and catastrophically wrong about small ones, such as whether ministers who stage executions bother to file the paperwork.

He did. That was the horror of it. The letter pinned inside my coat named three ministers and was signed in my brother's living hand; somewhere in the prison records office on Candlemaker's Row there was supposed to be more — his full testimony, forty pages he'd written under the pretense of a deathbed confession, dictated to a clerk sympathetic enough to copy it twice before filing it once. The clerk had died of a fever in the spring, inconveniently for everyone except the fever. But paper outlives clerks. My brother taught me that too, though he taught most things by dying badly and then not staying dead.

So on the third night after we'd delivered the first letter to Merrick — after the council had done precisely nothing with it, because fear is a heavier cargo than any I've carried — I went back.

* * *

Candlemaker's Row at two in the morning smells of tallow gone rancid and river damp, and beneath that, that night, something sweeter and wrong. Smoke, but not hearth-smoke. Woodsmoke has a temper; this smoke was patient. It took me another street to understand what patience like that meant: they weren't burning rubbish. They were burning archives, and you don't rush an archive.

The records office sat where it always had, a fat grey building with the posture of a man who has never once been asked to hurry. Except now its windows glowed orange from the inside, and there were soldiers on the steps, and — this was the detail that stopped me flat in a doorway, my heart doing something unsporting — there were clerks going *in*. Not out. In, with armloads of files, feeding them through the doors to men inside who fed them onward to the fire.

They were not burning the office down. They were burning it *up*, floor by floor, in good order, the way you'd strike a tent. The regime was destroying its own evidence with the same meticulousness it had used to create it, and somewhere in that orange throat were forty pages of my brother's voice.

I want to tell you I hesitated. A wiser woman would have. But wisdom, in my experience, is mostly the story people tell afterward about why they didn't act, and I had already spent four months believing my brother was ash. I was not going to stand in a doorway and let someone finish the job on his only remaining words.

* * *

There are three ways into a records office: the door, the window, and the way no one thought about because it's beneath dignity. In Vellmora, beneath dignity is the coal chute. Every building on the Row was heated by the same arrangement of cellars and grates, laid down when some duchess's grandfather decided servants should enter houses the way fuel did. I had spent two years as a courier learning exactly which buildings could swallow me, and the records office had a coal grate behind a low wall in the alley, barred with iron so rusted it flaked at a touch.

I went down into the dark with my coat buttoned and my heart in my teeth, and I will confess here what I confessed to no one: part of me hoped the testimony wasn't there. Because if it wasn't there, then I hadn't failed yet. Hope is very flexible that way. It will do acrobatics to keep you moving toward a thing that will break you.

The cellar was hot. That was the first wrongness — cellars should be cold, and this one breathed warmth down through the ceiling like the belly of some animal digesting. Above me, floorboards creaked under the shuffling of feet and the thump of dropped ledgers, and ash came down through the gaps in fine grey snow that settled on my shoulders and in my hair, so that by the time I found the stairs I looked prematurely aged by exactly the number of years this errand would cost me if I survived it.

I climbed one flight and came up through a service door into a corridor lined with shelving, and the shelves were emptying.

Not burning — emptying. The fire roared somewhere ahead, but between me and it, men worked with the unhurried competence of dockhands, pulling files and sorting them into two piles. One pile went toward the fire. One went into crates stenciled with a customs mark I recognized, because I had forged it once in Casper Oyelaran's shop with her steady hands guiding mine.

They weren't destroying everything. They were keeping some of it. And the thought that arrived cold and complete in my head was: *the kept pile is the evidence against their rivals.* Fire for their own crimes; filing for everyone else's. A regime, I have since concluded, can be judged entirely by what it chooses to burn versus what it chooses to keep, and Vellmora's ministers were keeping receipts.

I pressed myself behind a shelf of tax rolls — nobody has ever voluntarily approached tax rolls, which makes them excellent cover — and watched, and listened, and did arithmetic.

Forty pages. Filed under confessions, or executions, or V, or under whatever private system a dead clerk with a fever had favored. Somewhere in a building that was eating itself.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about watching your history burn: it sounds ordinary. Paper doesn't scream. It goes with a soft rushing sound, like wind through wheat, thousands of pages an hour, each one a deposition or a sentence or a widow's petition, and the men feeding the fire chat while they work. I stood behind the tax rolls and heard a soldier complain about his boots and another laugh, and between the two of them a decade of somebody's trial record went up as kindling.

My brother believed words were immortal. He said it often enough that I memorized it against my will: *they can kill the writer but the letters outlive him.* He was wrong in every particular that mattered that night. Letters live exactly as long as the paper they're set on and the people willing to carry them, and both were in short supply, and the supply was shrinking by the sackful.

I found the registry. Every office has one — the ledger of ledgers, telling you what lives where. It was open on a stand near the fire, being consulted even now, because even arson requires indexing. I waited until the clerk turned to heave a crate, and I read backward down the columns with my finger hovering over the page, never touching it, until I found the entry in the dead man's tidy hand:

*Vey, Aldous — confession, transcript, 40 ff. — Annex B, Case 114.*

Annex B. Which was, according to the layout of the building as I'd known it two years ago, across the yard. A separate building. Not on fire.

Yet.

* * *

Getting across the yard was the easy part, which I note only because everything after it was not. The yard lay between the burning office and the annex in a grey wash of ember-light, and I crossed it bent double behind a cart of wet sacking, and if anyone saw me they decided I was a cat, because cats were the only creatures with sanctioned business abroad that night.

Annex B was locked, of course. Records offices lock the past away and then lose the key, but this one had lost its window instead — the sash frame was rotten along the bottom rail, and I got my knife in and my shoulder after it, and the sound it made was swallowed whole by the roar from the main house.

Inside, the annex was cold and smelled of dust and mouse, the smell of unvisited truth. Case 114 lived on the fourth shelf of the fourth case, because bureaucracy is the only religion with honest numerology, and there it was: a folder, tied with the red tape that in Vellmora means *never to be read again.*

I untied it with fingers that would not stop shaking, and I read the opening line by the light leaking through the yard-facing window, and it was him. It was Aldous — not the pamphlet Aldous, not the granite monument Aldous, but my brother with his ink-stained middle finger and his habit of starting sentences he intended to finish later. *I, Aldous Vey, being instructed to make my confession, will instead make my accounting,* it began, which was so perfectly him that I laughed, once, alone, in a freezing room, and the laugh came out closer to a sob than I will admit in any court.

Forty pages. Names, dates, the machinery of his own murder laid out in his dry, precise hand. He had known, writing it, that it might never leave the shelf. He wrote it anyway. That is either faith or madness and I have never successfully told them apart.

I folded the pages flat against my body, inside the lining Casper had sewn for the first letter, and I turned to go, and that is when the light changed.

Firelight, coming through the yard window. Not spilling — *aimed.* Men with lanterns crossing toward the annex. And above the crackle behind me I heard a voice, bored and terrible: "Annex next. Minister's orders — everything."

Everything. They were going to burn the annex with the records *in* it, and possibly with me in it, and the distinction would not appear in anyone's ledger.

* * *

I went out the way I'd come in, which the window made difficult and my cargo made worse — forty pages don't bend, and neither, it turns out, do ribs around forty pages. I got through with a tearing sound I chose not to investigate and dropped into the yard behind the sacking cart as the annex door splashed with lamplight.

Then across the yard, then down, and here my luck performed its one kindness of the night: the coal grate gaped open exactly as I'd left it, and I went into the dark belly of the building with my brother's testimony crushed against my sternum like a second heartbeat, and above me the soldiers began carrying shelves.

But the cellar route exits through the Row, and the Row was now fully awake with the business of destruction. Carts were being loaded at the office's side door — crates, the customs-stenciled crates, the *kept* pile going somewhere. And standing by the carts with a lantern held at chest height, checking manifests, was a face I knew.

Doss. The foreman from the Oyelaran shop, in a minister's livery, at two in the morning, at the burning of a nation's memory.

I stood in the coal-dark of the grate and understood several things at once, the way you understand things when understanding is least useful. Doss had not been watching Casper out of suspicion of pamphlets. Doss was the ministers' man inside the last honest press in Vellmora, and he was here tonight taking inventory of what survived, and every hour I delayed brought morning closer, and morning would bring him back to the shop where Casper was sleeping, trusting him.

Behind me, upstairs, the annex caught. I felt it rather than heard it — a change in the building's breathing, heat beginning its long descent through the floors, hunting the cellar routes just as I had. The regime had learned from smugglers too.

I went down the coal passage with the fire following somewhere above and my brother's forty pages against my heart, and I made myself two promises in the dark. The first was that I would get these pages to Casper, because she was the only person I knew who could make the words multiply faster than they could be burned.

The second promise I made to the city itself, which was asleep and did not hear: *you cannot erase it all.* You can burn the office and the annex and the clerk and the tape, but every page that gets carried is a font you cannot break, and I would carry these if I had to recite them barefoot through the checkpoints with soldiers' hands inside my coat.

I came up out of a grate three streets east, into air the color of dishwater, and the glow over Candlemaker's Row was visible from the rooftops, and people were already standing in doorways pointing, already inventing the story they would tell. By noon the official version would be everywhere: an accidental fire, a tragedy of old wiring, nothing of value lost.

By noon, if the presses held, the city would have a different story. It depended on me, and on pages that smelled of smoke at the edges — scorched at one corner where a spark had kissed them in the annex, the top line of the first page half-eaten, my brother's opening sentence amputated mid-word.

It didn't matter. I had read it. It was in me now, and I am, whatever else may be said of me, very hard to burn.

I ran for the river with the dawn coming up grey behind me and the fire's glow ahead, and I did not yet know — how could I — that the bridge between me and Casper had become, in the space of a single night, the most dangerous span of stone in Vellmora.
