## 14. Hunted Through the Paper District

They came for us at the hour my father called the dead press hour — that gray sliver before dawn when the ink is still tacky and the whole street smells like a wet crow. I know because Merrick woke us, which is itself a miracle I intend to mention in whatever memoir I am too busy to write. A blind man hearing six pairs of boots three streets off, while Ilse and I slept the sleep of the righteous and the profoundly stupid.

"Boots," he said, from the doorway of the safehouse, in the tone other people use to remark on weather. "Six. Pausing at corners. That's a sweep, not a patrol."

Ilse was awake before he finished the sentence. She has a soldier's trick of surfacing without transition, no grogginess, no blinking — one moment asleep, the next fully aimed. I, by contrast, sat up with my hair doing something architectural and asked, with real feeling, "Are you certain?"

"They stop where patrols walk," Merrick said. "Somebody's told them where to look."

I didn't need him to finish that thought. Somebody had told them exactly where to look, and somebody had a foreman's knowledge of our habits, and somebody had spent years accepting my tea each morning while quietly measuring me for a cage. Doss had never seen this safehouse — of that I had been almost sure, right up until the moment I heard boots pausing at corners.

"Back way," Ilse said. She was already dressed. Some people are simply unbearable.

* * *

The Paper District earns its name honestly. It's the quarter behind the great printing houses where the rag-boilers and the driers work, and where every roof between the dye works and the canal is strung with lines — not laundry lines, though God knows the city's underlinen hangs there too some desperate weeks — but paper lines. Sheets of freshly couched paper, hung by the corners to dry in the river wind, hundreds upon hundreds of them, floor above floor, building after building, so that from the street it looks as though the whole quarter is breathing through white gills.

From above, it looks like something else entirely. From above, it looks like a field of snow that hasn't decided yet whether to fall.

The back way put us out into a courtyard reeking of size and wet rags, with a ladder against the wall and no better ideas available. Ilse went up first. I went up second, slower, with the specific dignity of a woman who has spent her life lifting type cases and not her own body weight, and who resents the difference.

Below us, the boots reached the safehouse door. We heard knocking. Then not-knocking, which is worse — the splintering kind of silence that means a door has stopped being a door.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about fleeing across rooftops: it is mostly walking. Urgent, crouched, blasphemous walking, but walking. The romance lasts precisely as long as your first look down. After that it's all grip and arithmetic — that gap is two feet, that gutter will hold, that line of sheets is anchored to something or it isn't and we're about to find out at speed.

It was full light coming up, the dirty apricot kind, and the city below us was already wrong. Not quiet-wrong, which I'd have understood; loud-wrong. The bread levy had curdled something overnight. From the height of the driers' roofs I could see baker's streets filling like drains, see a cart overturned near the corn exchange, see smoke rising from at least three places in thin, undecided columns, the smoke of things burning because they were nearest rather than because they mattered.

"It's starting without us," Ilse said. She said it flatly, but I heard the thing underneath, which was: we are carrying the match and the city has begun striking itself against its own walls.

"Revolutions are like proofs," I said. "You can pull one early. It just comes out blotchy."

"You are not comforting."

"I wasn't attempting comfort. I was demonstrating expertise."

That earned me half a laugh, bitten off, because below us a whistle blew — the two-note garrison whistle, sharp as a paper cut — and then another, answering, from the direction we'd meant to go.

We moved along the ridge of the driers' long roof, bent double, past lines hung so low with wet sheets that we had to duck through them like curtains. And that is how I came to push my face directly through forty pounds of drying pamphlet stock, tearing a sheet clean down the middle, and to spend the following thirty seconds watching half of someone else's broadside flap away toward the canal like an enormous wounded bird.

I read what was left in my hand as it tore. It was a levy protest bill. *BREAD FOR BELLIES, NOT FOR BANKS.* Set badly — the kerning was criminal, the leading looked chosen by dice — but set, and printed, and being handed out somewhere below me by hands I would never meet.

Somewhere out there, typesetters I'd never known were setting fire with their sticks. The thought arrived with equal parts pride and vertigo. All winter Doss and I had believed ourselves the secret heart of the sedition trade, the quiet engine under Vellmora. We were not the engine. We were one cylinder among dozens, and now every cylinder was firing at once, un-timed, unsynchronized, and no hand on earth could tell you what the machine would do.

"The descriptions," Ilse said, pulling me flat behind a chimney stack. Below, in the street between the driers and the rag-works, a squad was going shop to shop, and the sergeant was reading aloud from a paper. Sound carries strangely up there; I caught fragments. *Woman, printer's stains, mid-height... her sister, foreign-born, gaunt, dark-eyed... may travel together... reward.*

"Mid-height," I repeated. "He couldn't even be accurate about my cruelty. Mid-height."

"He described your hands," Ilse said quietly. "Printer's stains. He knew you by your hands, Casper."

She was right, and it landed harder than any list of my supposed features. Doss had watched my hands for eleven years — over the composing stone, inking the rollers, sorting sorts — and had written them down for soldiers the way you'd describe a tool that had gone missing. My father used to say you can read a compositor's whole biography in their hands. Apparently Doss had done exactly that, then sold the edition.

"Come on," I said, because grief is a luxury of people who aren't currently worth forty crowns apiece.

* * *

We crossed the rag-works by way of a plank some drier had left spanning two roofs, presumably for his own lawful purposes, and which became, for roughly four seconds, the most important bridge in Vellmora. I mention it because later, when the histories get written — and they will get written, if I have anything to say about it, and I do, professionally — someone will describe the flight across the Paper District in terms of strategy and daring. Let the record show it was mostly a plank, a prayer, and two women apologizing to their knees.

But the roofs themselves — those I'll grant were extraordinary.

By mid-morning the wind had come up off the canal the way it does, and the whole district began to move. Every loose sheet, every mis-hung corner, every broadside pulled too soon off the frames — the wind took them. They lifted off the lines in spirals, dozens at a time, hundreds, white pages wheeling up over the chimneys and out over the city, and from the roofline it looked exactly like the moment a flock decides. Not birds leaving. Birds arriving at the same decision simultaneously.

And below, in the streets, people were catching them. I saw it plainly. A woman on a crate snatched a drifting sheet out of the air, read it, shouted something, held it over her head. A boy chased one down an alley like a kite string had broken. Papers that had been hung by honest tradesmen to dry were becoming, in transit, literature. Or incitement. In Vellmora that week the distinction had stopped existing.

"That's the trouble," I said to Ilse, watching a sheet the size of a bedsheet cartwheel majestically into the canal, unread, unsung. "Everyone thinks a revolution is a letter delivered. One document, one council, one signature, and history signs for the parcel." I nodded at the sky full of escaping paper. "It's not a letter. It's weather. You don't deliver weather. You just find out you're standing in it."

Ilse was watching the crowd, not the sky, and her face had gone to that hard still place it visits. "Aldous used to say nearly the same thing. He said you can't steer a flood, you can only make sure it flows around the things worth keeping." She paused. "He also said it the week before he let himself be arrested, so I've revised my opinion of the aphorism."

"We'll do both," I said. "Keep the things worth keeping and be arrested by no one. It's called learning."

* * *

We were halfway along the dye works roof when the sweep found its voice again, and this time it was aimed at us.

Not at our description — at our location. A whistle from the rag-works yard, answered from the canal towpath, and then the particular ugly sound of a cordon deciding to exist. They had worked out, as any competent hunter works out eventually, that prey on the roofs must have gone up somewhere and must come down somewhere, and that the somewheres are finite.

"There," Ilse said, pointing with her chin. Beyond the dye works the roofs stepped down in a staircase of warehouses toward the Paper Market, and past the market lay the last stretch of the district, and past that — visible between chimneys, a gray stone stripe crowded with fugitives and bayonets — the Bridge of Atlas.

Everything, always, that bridge. Every road in this story runs across it.

"If we go down through the market—" I began.

"They'll be waiting at the bottom of the market."

"If we stay up here—"

"They'll come up." She glanced back along our trail, where a helmet was already negotiating the plank I had prayed on. "Casper. The lines."

I followed her gaze and understood, and hated it. The great anchor-lines of the driers ran from the high roofs clear across the lane between buildings to iron rings on the dye works wall — thick manila, sagging under the weight of a hundred drying sheets, sloped like the world's worst rope bridge and the world's best idea, depending on your relationship with gravity.

"That line is holding a hundredweight of wet paper," I said.

"So don't weigh more than paper."

"Ilse. Sweetly. I weigh as much as two hundred pamphlets and I know because I've carried them."

"Then carry yourself the same way. Hands first. Like a compose-stick. Even weight." She was already swinging out over the lane before she finished explaining, which I consider a character flaw, and went hand-over-hand down that swaying line through a blizzard of torn sheets, quick and sure and utterly without ceremony, and dropped to the dye works roof rolling like a sack of rags.

My crossing was less cinematic. I will describe it only as involving the loss of one shoe, the acquisition of a rope burn I shall treasure, and a moment in which I hung above a lane full of soldiers looking up at me like a congregation at a very surprising sermon, while the wind filled my coat and the papers streamed past me in both directions, going wherever papers go when no one can hold them.

I did not look like a wanted woman in that moment. I looked, I suspect, like part of the weather. Nobody fired. Half of them cheered, which tells you precisely which way the garrison's heart had already tilted, and which Doss — clever, patient, doomed Doss — had somehow failed to report.

* * *

The far side of the district caught us in its crowd, and here the story stops being ours alone, because by noon there was no such thing as a private story in Vellmora. The Paper Market had become a single animal — not angry, exactly, though anger was in it, but *full*, packed to the skin with people who had spent a year being told to wait and had collectively, wordlessly, finished waiting. Bread levy, staged execution, vanished publishers, missing sisters — none of it needed saying anymore. It had all soaked through, the way ink soaks through a sheet, showing its shape on the other side.

We were carried. There's no better verb. The crowd picked us up and moved us the way a river moves a leaf, and I confess I stopped steering entirely, pressed against Ilse's shoulder, one shoeless, both of us with our faces down, anonymous at last — not because we hid but because everyone, finally, was suspicious-looking. A sergeant not ten feet from us was reading Doss's description aloud and nobody listened, including the sergeant, who kept losing his place when the crowd shoved him.

And through the middle of it all, the pamphlets flew. Off roofs, out windows, from hand to hand — old stock, new stock, badly set, beautifully set, the levy bills and the older seditious ones we'd hidden under funeral type for a year, and others I'd never seen, from shops whose names I didn't know. The whole city was publishing. Every window was a press.

"We're too late," I said, and felt it leave me like blood, "and we're too early, and we're exactly on time, and I cannot tell which."

"Welcome to my year," Ilse said.

Then, over the heads of the crowd, past the market's last stalls, I saw it again: the Bridge of Atlas, and the checkpoint at its foot, and the soldiers there no longer checking anyone — just holding, barely, a line against a tide that was learning, visibly, in real time, that it did not have to stop.

And I saw something else. At the bridge's edge, a knot of garrison men around a prisoner — a woman, gaunt, dark-eyed, being turned by the shoulders so an officer could compare her face to a paper in his fist.

I grabbed Ilse's arm. "They have someone at the bridge. Dark-haired. Your build. Ilse — they have your description and they're holding someone who—"

She had already gone pale, already pushing forward. "It's not me," she said, in a voice that meant she feared it wasn't, and that was worse. "Casper, if they took her with the letter—"

The letter. The letter that named three ministers, the letter the whole rising was supposedly about, possibly at this moment in an officer's saddlebag on the Bridge of Atlas, one order away from a fire.

The crowd surged toward the bridge, and we surged with it, straight into the third and last checkpoint, where everything we had carried was about to be either delivered or destroyed.
