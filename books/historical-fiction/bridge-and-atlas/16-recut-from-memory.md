## 16. Recut from Memory

My father used to say there were two kinds of grief: the kind you carry and the kind that sets. He meant it about type, of course — everything with the man came back to type eventually, the way other men's conversations came back to drink or God. Loose grief sloshes around in you like unsorted sorts, rattling at every step. Set grief sits in a form, locked tight, ready to print. He never told me which kind he'd rather leave me with.

By the time I reached Sef Brandt's door on Candlemaker's Row, I had both, and neither would hold still.

I knocked the way my father taught me to knock at a rival's door — three slow, insolent raps, the knock of a man confident his work speaks for itself. It took so long for footsteps to answer that I had time to compose an apology and abandon it twice. Then the door opened a hand's width on a chain, and one eye appeared in the gap, red-rimmed and suspicious as a customs clerk's.

"The shop's closed," said the eye.

"Your shop has been closed since your last apprentice ran off to the garrison, Sef. Your shutters haven't been open since the levy riots started. Everyone on this street thinks you're dead."

"Everyone on this street is right to think what keeps them safe." But the chain rattled, and the door opened, and there he was: Sef Brandt, seventy if he was a day, in a nightshirt that had once been white and a cardigan that had never been anything but grey. He looked smaller than the monument my father had built him in my imagination. Rivals always do, when you finally see them by lamplight instead of legend.

He looked at me a long moment. "Oyelaran's girl."

"Casper."

"I know your name. I set it once, in obituary type." He stepped back to let me in. "Come in before someone sees you and makes it true early."

* * *

His shop was a museum of a dying trade, and he was its only living exhibit. Composing sticks hung on pegs like surgical instruments. A hand press sat under dust sheets in the corner like furniture in a dead relative's house. The smell hit me before anything else — ink and oil and metal polish and the particular dry sweetness of old paper — and I will confess something shameful: after everything, after the bridge and the soldiers and Ilse being taken not twenty minutes before, that smell undid me worse than any of it. I stood in Sef Brandt's doorway and cried like a child, silently, the way you cry when you've learned nobody is coming.

Sef did not comfort me. He lit a lamp, put a kettle on a spirit stove, and waited with the patience of a man who had spent fifty years waiting for ink to dry. When I had finished, he handed me tea in a cup with a chip in it and asked, "What have you lost?"

"A letter."

"What did it say?"

And there it was — the question underneath all questions, the one I'd been carrying since the Bridge of Atlas. Because here is the thing about letters, real ones, the kind that matter: they exist in exactly one place, or they exist nowhere. The regime understood this better than we did. They took the paper off Ilse at the checkpoint because paper can be taken. What they could not take — what no search could find and no soldier could seize — was sitting behind my eyes, and had been sitting there since the night I tested Aldous Vey's hand against my father's specimen books and felt the truth of it click into place like a quoined form.

"It said," I began, and then stopped, and then made myself go on, "that Minister Corvane ordered his execution staged. That Minister Halle provided the prison records. That Minister Detch signed the warrant for a corpse that never died. It named dates. It named the officer who fired the blank. It was four hundred words in Vey's own hand, and I read it eleven times while I was setting a forgery of the customs pass, because I am my father's daughter and I cannot stop proofing text that passes through my hands."

Sef drank his tea. The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, a patrol went past, boots keeping their idiot time.

"You memorized it," he said.

"I didn't try to. That's worse, somehow. It just... stayed. The way bad verse stays."

"No." He set down the cup. "The way good type stays. There's a difference, girl, and the difference is the whole trade." He was already moving toward the back of the shop, throwing dust sheets off things, waking the room up section by section. "How much of it do you trust?"

"All of it."

"How much of it can you swear to?"

I thought about that honestly, because Sef Brandt deserved honesty and because lying to a compositor about text is like lying to a priest about prayer. "Three hundred and sixty words I could recite in my sleep. The rest I'd be reconstructing from sense."

"Then we print the three hundred and sixty and let sense go hang." He pulled down a job case, blew the dust out of its compartments, and set it on the stone with a click that sounded, in that quiet, like a decision being made. "We'll need matrices. My punches are old but true. How are your cutting skills?"

"My father wouldn't let me touch a graver until I was fourteen. Said I'd ruin my temperament before I ruined the steel."

"And was he right?"

"He was right about the steel."

Something happened to his face then — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the expression of a man remembering that he had liked my father even while hating him, which is the only way rivals ever love each other properly. "Sit down," he said. "Tell me the first line."

* * *

We worked through the night, and I want to tell you what that night actually was, because history — when it finally gets around to writing this, if it bothers — will call it something grander than it felt. It felt like the worst kind of job: rush work, bad light, a partner who argued punctuation.

That's what I'll remember longest, I think. The arguments. We fought over commas the way other men fight over borders. Sef wanted to regularize Vey's phrasing, smooth the rough edges of a letter written fast in hiding, and I refused, because the roughness *was* the evidence — a forged-smooth letter would read like what the regime would produce, all polished and official, and the whole point was that this was a living man's crooked, urgent, human voice. "A dead man writes formally," I told him. "Only a live man leaves his participles dangling."

"Your father dangled participles like a man baiting hooks," Sef said, and there was grief in it too, his own, forty years' worth, and we both heard it, and we both kept working.

Here is how you rebuild a dead man's testimony from memory, in case anyone ever asks you to. You start with the words you're surest of, the ones burned deepest, and you set them first — not because order matters in the case, but because your hands need warming on certainty before they can face doubt. Then you go hunting through the doubtful parts with a compositor's trick my father taught me: you don't remember sentences, you remember *shapes*. The look of a paragraph block. Where the line broke short. Which word sat at the end of a line like a man leaning in a doorway. I closed my eyes and saw the proof sheet as I'd seen it in the lamplight of our shop, Ilse watching me, the rain still drying on her shoulders, and I read it again from memory the way you'd read a photograph of a room you'd fled.

Line by line. Word by word. Letter by letter.

For the words I couldn't recover whole, we cut new sorts. Sef's gravers were older than I was, but steel doesn't forget its business. I cut the letters I could see in my mind's eye; he corrected my angles without mercy; and somewhere around the third hour I stopped feeling like a fugitive and started feeling like what I had been before any of this — a woman at a bench, doing the only thing she had ever been any good at. The grief didn't leave. It changed states. It set. Every stroke of the graver was a word I owed a hanged-alive man; every sort dropped into the stick was a small refusal of the ministers who had decided that truth was theirs to burn. My father said letters outlive men. He never mentioned that sometimes you have to build them a body first, with your own hands, in a rival's shop, by lamplight, while the city outside tears itself apart over bread.

Near dawn, Ilse's face arrived in my head without warning — the way they turned her toward the soldiers, her chin up, her brother's jaw set on her brother's courage — and my hand slipped and gouged a channel across a half-cut *n*. I sat back and pressed the heel of my hand against my eyes until the world went orange.

"She's alive," Sef said quietly. He hadn't asked who I was seeing. He didn't need to; the whole quarter knew about the arrest at the bridge by now, the way a street knows about weather.

"They'll question her."

"They'll get nothing. She carried that letter through three checkpoints. Do you imagine she carries it less carefully now that it lives in her skull?" He took the damaged *n* from my hand, examined it, tossed it in the scrap box. "Grief makes bad cuts. Save it for the pressroom."

"That's cold, Brandt."

"That's craft. Cold is what keeps the metal true." He slid a fresh blank slug across the stone. "Again."

* * *

The sky was going grey at the shutters when we finished. Four hundred words, give or take the thirty-eight we'd agreed to sacrifice to honest doubt. Locked in the chase, quoin tight, it didn't look like much — a slab of reversed type, mirror-world writing, meaningless unless you knew the trick of reading it. I put my palm flat on it anyway. Warm metal, warm from the room and from us. It hummed, or I imagined it hummed. At that point the distinction had stopped mattering.

"There's a problem," Sef said, wiping his hands on a rag gone stiff with old ink. "One press. One forme. Even running day and night, we're talking days to move the city. And the garrison will be moving faster than that."

"I know."

"Do you? Because I'm an old man with one press and a reputation for being dead, and you're—" he waved the rag at all of me, the ink, the exhaustion, the wanted-ness, "—considerable trouble."

"So we don't print it alone." I looked at the forme, and then at the dusty windows, beyond which Candlemaker's Row was beginning to wake into a city on the edge of something. "There are a dozen shops in this quarter with presses, Sef. Printers who've been sitting on their hands for months because they had nothing worth risking their necks for. Merrick said the council was paralyzed. Well. Paralyzed men can still pull a lever, if you put the forme under their hands."

"You want to distribute it."

"I want to *cast* it. Matrices off this forme, one set for every shop that'll take one. Let every printer set his own copy, run his own press, sign his own risk. The ministers can raid one shop." I touched the chase again. "They can't raid a dozen. Not before dawn tomorrow."

Sef was quiet a long time. Through the wall, a neighbor's kettle whistled; the ordinary sounds of a morning that didn't yet know what it was carrying.

"Your father," he said finally, "would have hated this plan."

"Why?"

"Because it means admitting I'm useful." But he was already pulling his coat off its hook, already blowing the lamp out, already reaching for the shutter bar. Outside, the grey was turning to gold, and somewhere across the river, faint on the wind, we could hear the crowds gathering at the levy protests — hundreds of voices, then thousands, a sound like a river deciding where it wants to go.

Sef paused at the door, matrices wrapped in cloth under his arm, and looked back at me.

"Well, Oyelaran's girl. Last chance to stay dead with me."

I picked up my share of the type. "My father also said a forme isn't finished until it's printed."

The first shop on our list belonged to a widow named Ansel who owed my father a debt and had a press she hadn't oiled since winter. As Sef hammered on her door, the crowd-sound swelled again, closer now, and beneath it — unmistakable, impossible, threading through the noise of the rising city like a needle through cloth — I heard a bell. Not a church bell. A pressman's bell, the signal every printer in Vellmora knew: *forme ready, come and get it.*

Someone else, it seemed, had spent the night cutting too.
