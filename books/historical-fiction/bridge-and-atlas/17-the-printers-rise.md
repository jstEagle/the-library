## 17. The Printers Rise

*Casper*

There is a moment in every print run — my father taught me this before he taught me anything else — when the forme is locked, the ink is rolled, and the machine has not yet spoken. The press stands over you like a held breath. Everything that will happen in the world because of what you are about to make has already happened in miniature on the stone. You have only to pull.

At half past three in the morning, in Sef Brandt's shop on Candlemaker's Row, I pulled.

The press was older than both of us put together, a groaning iron beast Brandt had inherited from his own father along with the feud, and it took the sheet the way old men take bad news: slowly, and then all at once. The cylinder rolled. The gears caught. And Aldous Vey's voice came up out of the platen black and wet and whole.

*To the printers of Vellmora, and through them to every hand that holds a page:*

I had set those words eleven hours ago from memory. Not from the letter itself — the letter was gone, seized at the Bridge of Atlas with everything else, probably by now in an incinerator or a minister's drawer — but from the proofs. From the hours I'd spent with that handwriting spread across my father's composing stone while Ilse slept in the back room and Doss pretended not to watch. I had read that letter so many times, testing the hand against old specimens, that it had stopped being paper to me and become type. Every loop of Vey's pen had a letter underneath it. Every letter had its place in a case I knew blind.

So when they took the original, they took paper. They did not take the words. Words live in cases. That was my father's whole theology, and last night it became mine.

Brandt leaned over the sheet as it came off, squinting through the spectacles he refused to admit he needed. He had worked beside me since sundown without once saying what we both knew: that we were rebuilding the testimony of a murdered man letter by letter, that my memory was the only archive left, and that if I got a word wrong there was no one alive who could correct me.

"Line forty," he said. "Read it back."

"*We were condemned in the third week of Frostmonth, by order of Minister Corvane, countersigned by Ministers Halle and Deuze,*" I recited. "*The execution announced to the city was theatre. The grave is empty. I am alive, and I am evidence.*"

"That's the fourth time you've read it."

"You keep asking."

"I keep asking," Brandt said, "because you haven't changed a word yet, and it's beginning to frighten me." He lifted the sheet, held it to the lamp, and something moved across his face that I had never seen there in twenty years of rivalry between our fathers' shops. It took me a moment to recognize it. Respect, maybe. Or grief wearing respect's coat.

"My father said yours wasted his talent on pamphlets," he said.

"My father said yours wasted his talent on wedding invitations."

"He wasn't wrong about the weddings." Brandt laid the proof down with the tenderness other men reserve for infants. "Pull another. We have four shops waiting on this stereotype and the sun doesn't care about our sentiment."

* * *

*Ilse*

I came over the prison wall on a rope that burned my hands and dropped into a courtyard that smelled of ash, because the records office was still burning behind me. I had gone back for the second letter — Aldous's full testimony, forty pages in his careful clerk's hand — and found instead a fire with three soldiers feeding it, feeding *pages* into it, the regime destroying its own evidence faster than any courier could carry it.

I got out with six scorched sheets clutched inside my coat. I got out with burns on my wrists. I got out because a mob surging toward the granary broke the cordon at the same moment I needed breaking, and because in the crush nobody looks twice at a woman running in the same direction as everyone else. What can be carried, Casper says. What can be carried is whoever carries it.

I walked the last mile to Candlemaker's Row through streets that had come loose from their moorings. Bread riots, they were calling it — the levy, the levy — as if bread had ever been the whole of it. A city does not rise for bread. A city rises for the accumulated weight of small insults, and then someone hands it a piece of paper that tells it the insult was deliberate, and named, and signed.

By the time I reached Brandt's door the sky in the east had gone the grey of uninked paper.

I knocked the pattern — two, pause, three — and heard the press stop, which in a shop working against dawn is the sound of a heart skipping.

Casper opened the door herself. She was ink to the elbows, ink on her jaw where she'd pushed back her hair with the back of her wrist, ink in a long smear across her collarbone like a wound in the wrong color. Her hands were shaking. I saw it plainly, the fine tremor of a body that has been running on craft and fear for thirty hours, and she saw me see it, and she said:

"Don't. If you say something kind I'll sit down and I'm not sitting down until ten thousand copies exist."

"The records office is ash," I told her. "He burned everything else himself. Whatever we hold now is all of him there is."

"Then it's enough." She stood aside to let me in. "It has to be. Pull your weight; Brandt's boy delivers to Harrow Street within the hour."

* * *

*Casper*

Here is how a city wakes to revolution, if the revolution is done properly: it wakes slowly, and then all at once, exactly like a sheet coming off a press.

Harrow Street went first, then Quaymakers', then the little shop behind the tannery where old Wenna still ran a handpress her grandmother had bought smuggled. Twelve shops, twelve presses, one stereotype plate passed hand to hand through back yards and cellar doors in the dark before dawn, each printer casting their own copy so no single seizure could end it. Merrick's plan, delivered blind through a trusted apprentice. The council that would not act on a private letter would act on ten thousand public ones — or rather, the council discovered that it didn't matter anymore whether they acted. The city had read.

I know what happened on Harrow Street because Brandt's boy came back breathless to report it: the printer there, a widow named Ostrey, hung her first wet sheets straight in her window, fifty of them, and by full light a crowd three deep stood reading in silence. Then a man at the back read the names aloud — Corvane, Halle, Deuze — the way you call out a debt in a market, and the crowd took it up like a litany, and the widow Ostrey unlocked her door and started handing pages out until she ran out of pages.

By seven, there were copies in fish carts and flour sacks and prayer books. By eight, a soldier at the Garrison Arch was seen reading one, which should have been impossible, since his orders were to confiscate them, and the look on his face — so the story went, and stories were traveling faster than soldiers now — was the look of a man reading his own orders' death warrant.

Ilse worked the press beside me without being asked, feeding sheets with the economy of someone who grew up in her brother's shop before his brother grew famous. We did not talk much. There is a conversation that happens between two people working one machine, conducted entirely in glances and the rhythm of the crank, and it is more honest than most conversations conducted in words. Hers said: *you're shaking*. Mine said: *so are you*. Both were true and neither required an answer.

Halfway through the second hundred, she stopped and looked at the sheet under the lamp and said, quietly, "That's his hand. On the specimen line. You even set his signature."

"I couldn't recut his face," I said. "His name was easier."

"It's better than his face. Faces lie. Names don't." She fed another sheet. "He used to say the only honest thing a man leaves is his typesetting, because you can hear a voice lie but you can feel a compositor's conscience in the spacing."

"Your brother," I said, "sounds like my father."

"No," Ilse said. "Your father sounds like my brother. One of them learned it from the other, or neither learned it from anybody, and that's the sadder and better story." She cranked. The press spoke. Somewhere across the river, eleven other presses were speaking the same sentence at the same minute, and I confess the thought made the hair stand up on my arms: for the first time in my life I could *hear*, faintly, under the noise of the city, the sound of typesetting happening everywhere at once, and it sounded like rain on a roof.

* * *

*Ilse*

I left the shop at midmorning to find out whether the garrison would fire on the crowds, because someone had to know, and Casper's hands had finally stopped shaking and I was not going to be the reason they started again.

What I found instead, coming down Cooper's Cut, was a wall of people moving the way water moves when the thing holding it back stops pretending to be a wall. The levy protest and the pamphlet crowds had met and married somewhere near the granary, and the child of that marriage was filling the street corner to corner. I am not a small woman and I am not a timid one, but for a quarter hour I was not walking so much as being poured, and during that quarter hour I passed a barricade being built out of — I checked twice, because I did not believe it the first time — confiscated pamphlets, baled and stacked like sandbags, the regime's own seizures turned to masonry.

A boy on top of it, perhaps fourteen, was nailing up a copy of the testimony as if it were a proclamation. He had set it crooked. Some ancient instinct in me — Aldous's instinct, drilled into me at his workbench before I was ten — made me reach up and straighten it, and the boy looked down at me and said, "You want to check my spelling too?" and the whole barricade laughed, and I laughed, and that was the moment I understood we had already won whatever winning was going to look like, though nobody had thought yet to write the terms.

Because here is the thing about ten thousand copies: you cannot arrest them. You can seize a letter. You can burn a records office. You can stage a hanging and call it justice. But you cannot unpublish a morning. Every handbill was a seed off the same plate, and the plate existed in twelve shops and in the memory of one exhausted compositor, and the ministers' power had always rested on the fact that their crimes lived in exactly one place — their own signatures — and signatures can be forged, but ten thousand witnesses cannot.

I was still standing in the street doing arithmetic with this thought when the crowd surged, the cordon at the far end gave, and I was carried — gently, absurdly, like a leaf in a friendly flood — three streets east, directly past the Garrison Arch.

Directly past Captain Renn, who had looked at my face nine days ago a moment too long.

He was not looking at the crowd. He was looking at the papers in it, snatching them out of the air like a man catching flies, and he had one in his fist, and I watched him read it — really read it, lips moving — and I watched his face do what faces do when a man discovers his superiors have been lying to him in a way that makes his own oath a joke. He lowered the handbill. He looked up. His eyes went down the crowd, past me, past me—

Past me.

He did not know me. He had known a woman whose face mirrored her brother's, nine days ago, at a checkpoint. But the woman he'd examined had been *claiming to be a stranger to her own name*, and somewhere between then and now I had stopped claiming anything, and it turns out that is a different face. Survival had erased me, and the erasure had saved me, and I stood in that flood feeling the cost of it arrive all at once, delayed like thunder after lightning, and I let myself feel it for exactly as long as it took the crowd to carry me past the Arch, and not one second longer.

Then I ran for Candlemaker's Row, because I had news worth carrying, and carrying things is the whole of what I am for.

*Casper*

She came through the door at a dead run, stopped, and stood getting her breath back while Brandt and I froze over the forme with the quoins half-tightened.

"The Arch is ours," she said. "Not taken. Ours. Renn read the testimony in the middle of his own post and didn't arrest anyone. Didn't move. Just stood there reading." She put her hands flat on the composing stone to steady herself, and I noticed her knuckles were wrapped in strips torn from her petticoat, burned, from the records office fire I hadn't yet heard the whole of. "And Casper — the crowd carried me past him and he looked *through* me. I've spent a month terrified of my own face. Turns out I left it somewhere along the way and never missed it."

"There's a sentence," Brandt muttered, "that no good can come of."

But he was tightening the quoins as he said it, and I was already reaching for the ink roller, because there was one more run to pull. The final sheet. The last of the paper Brandt had, good rag stock he'd been hoarding for wedding invitations, and I intend to note in some future account of this business that the definitive text of Aldous Vey's testimony was printed on stock intended for the marriage of a merchant's daughter to a customs official, and that no irony in the subsequent literature has improved upon it.

I pulled the lever. The press spoke its last word for the morning.

And then, from the doorway behind Ilse, a knock — not our pattern, no pattern at all, just fists, urgent — and Merrick's apprentice tumbled in, a boy of sixteen with ink on his chin and his eyes enormous.

"They're fleeing," he said. "Corvane's carriage went out the south gate at dawn with the blinds drawn. Halle didn't even pack. And the garrison at the Bridge of Atlas just grounded arms — the captain read them the testimony himself, my master says, stood on the tollhouse steps and read it to his own company." The boy gulped air. "And there's a rumor going through the quarter — nobody knows from who — that Vey isn't dead. That the empty grave is real. That he's been in hiding this whole time and he's coming out today, coming to the bridge, and the whole city's turning toward it like—"

He stopped, out of breath, and the shop went very quiet, the particular quiet of a room where a press has just stopped and everyone can suddenly hear themselves.

Brandt removed his spectacles and polished them on his apron, which is what he does instead of expressing hope.

"If your brother walks onto that bridge," he said to Ilse, without opening his eyes, "in front of that crowd, on a morning when every wall in this city is papered with his own words — then either he's alive, or someone very clever is about to hang a stranger. And I find I need to know which. Before noon."

Ilse looked at me. Her face, the one she'd lost and found again, had gone very still.

"Aldous always said," she said slowly, "that the letter would reach the council or it wouldn't, and either way the truth would find its level. He never once said *he* would." Her hand went to the scorched pages inside her coat. "But there was a seventh sheet I couldn't read. The edges were burned. And at the bottom, under the char, there was one line I could just make out, and I've been telling myself all morning it meant nothing."

"Read it," I said.

She unfolded the sheet with burnt fingers, and laid it on the stone next to the fresh proof, and read aloud:

"*If you are setting this by hand, then I am already walking toward you.*"
