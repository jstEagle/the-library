## 18. Doss at the Door

The city had been shouting all night, but by the hour before dawn it went quiet in the specific way a feverish child goes quiet — not better, just gathered. I stood at the composing stone with my hands still black from the night's work and listened to that silence, and I thought: *here it comes*. Not because I heard anything. Because I had spent twenty-nine years learning the rhythms of this shop, and I knew the sound a building makes when something is about to be asked of it that it cannot give.

Merrick sat near the stove with his face turned toward the door he could not see, which was his habit and, I suspect, his method. Blind men listen the rest of us out of a job. The apprentices — Tam and the Brasky girls and little Ondo, who was fourteen and had lied about it to get on the press — were stacked around the room like sacks of grain someone had finally decided not to abandon.

"He's coming," Merrick said.

"You can't know that."

"I can smell lamp oil through a closed door, Casper." He smiled without much humor in it. "I could smell it before you did. It came up the alley about an hour ago. Someone's been standing there working a stopper."

I want to say I was surprised. I have promised myself honesty in these pages, or whatever passes for it in a woman who measures her life in picas, so: no. What I felt was closer to embarrassment. Doss had stood at my father's funeral with his hat in his hands like a man who had loved the deceased, and I had let him stay on as foreman because grief makes bad bookkeepers and worse judges of character. For eleven years I had read his too-long glances at the pamphlet plates as loyalty straining against caution. Caution straining against appetite, more likely. A man watching a drawer full of money is not wondering whether to take it. He is wondering when.

The knock, when it came, was almost polite. Three raps, evenly spaced. Doss had always knocked evenly. Even his betrayals kept to house style.

I opened the door.

He stood in the alley mouth with two men I didn't know and one I did — Sergeant Vahl, whom the paper district called the Ledger because he wrote down everything and forgave nothing. Doss held a storm lantern in one hand and a can of lamp oil in the other, and he had arranged himself into the posture of a man performing an unpleasant duty rather than enjoying one, which was the last kindness available to him and he took it.

"Casper," he said.

"Doss." I stepped back from the doorway, which he would read as surrender and which was, in fact, arithmetic. Behind me, four apprentices rose from their grain-sack positions. "You've brought oil."

"And a writ." He produced a folded paper with the care of a man handling a proof sheet. "Order of seizure. Seditious press, seditious stock, seditious persons found therein. I'm to see it burned and everyone inside taken to the Garrison for questioning."

"Questioning," said the Ledger, from the shadows. His voice was exactly the voice you'd expect. Dry. Indexed.

"Whose writ?" I asked.

Doss's eyes flicked — left, quick, involuntary — toward the letterhead of the paper in his hand, and I understood that he had not been sent by any minister at all. Ministers were fleeing their own mansions tonight. This was a man seizing his moment while the scale hung unweighted, before anyone upstream could decide who had been loyal to what. Doss had spent eleven years reporting to whoever paid, and now the paymasters were running, and he wanted the shop, the plates, the name Oyelaran struck off or absorbed — whichever the morning required.

"You," I said. "This is yours. You wrote it yourself."

"The authority is real enough."

"The authority is you, holding oil." I leaned against the doorframe. My heart was going like a press run, but I have learned that the body lies loudest when you let it speak first. "Eleven years, Doss."

"Nine of them good," he said, and there it was — the crack in the typeface, the flaw that lets you identify a counterfeit. He believed some part of this. That he'd earned something. That the years had been a currency deposited and never drawn upon. "Your father took me off the garrison labor rolls when every other master printer wouldn't touch a man with my record. I set his memorial edition myself. Every line. You think I don't know what this shop is?"

"I think you know exactly what it is. That's why you're here with a can of it."

The two strange men shifted. The Ledger made a small note, probably of my insolence, possibly of the weather. Somewhere across the river a bell began ringing and stopped, as if it had changed its mind.

"Step aside, Casper."

"No."

It is a small word for the largest thing I have ever said. Let me be precise about what I meant by it, because precision is the whole trade: I did not mean *I am brave*. I meant that behind me stood a blind compositor, three children under twenty, a girl who had crossed a river twice with her brother's testimony sewn into her coat, and ten thousand sheets of Aldous Vey's voice, still wet. Everything I valued fit inside these walls. Power is what can be seized. Truth is what can be carried. And Doss was carrying oil, which carries nothing, which only takes away.

"Then we do it the hard way," Doss said, and he tipped the can.

He never finished the pour. It is important to me, somehow, that the record show he never finished the pour — that even at the end his treachery was incomplete, a broken sort, a letter that prints its ghost impression and no more. Ondo hit him from the left, which no one expected, least of all Ondo, whose eyes went wide with terror at his own courage. The can went spinning into the gutter, glugging oil across the cobbles like spilled ink. One of the strangers grabbed for Tam and got a knee to the thigh for his trouble, because Tam's mother had raised printers' children the old way, which is to say with fists. The Ledger drew a baton and discovered Merrick standing in front of him.

I will never forget the geometry of it. A blind man, arms spread, occupying the entire doorway of a burning-to-be print shop, saying nothing at all. The Ledger looked at him the way you look at a locked case of type: here is an obstacle that is also a message.

"Gentlemen," Merrick said pleasantly, "you are outnumbered by people with nothing left to lose, which history assures us is the worst arithmetic in the world. And the bells you hear are not on your side."

They weren't, quite. The mob from the levy protests was coming down along the river road — we could hear it now, that deep braided sound of many feet and one intention. Whether the strangers heard it too, or simply decided that Doss's wages had been paid in a currency that was ceasing to exist, I couldn't tell you. They melted backward down the alley the way hired men do, leaving their employer alone with his empty can.

Doss stood in the spreading oil with the lantern still lit in his hand.

And here is where I must confess the thing I have confessed to no one but the type. Because in that moment — lantern, oil, alley, man — I saw very clearly how easy it would be. He was between me and everything that had been taken: my father's reputation sold to informers, Ilse dragged off the bridge, my shop listed in some sergeant's ledger as evidence. The lantern was in his hand and my father's iron poker was in mine, and I don't remember picking it up, which frightens me more than anything else that happened that year. There is a version of that alley where I swing. In that version I am never haunted, because Doss has no friends to haunt me with, and the city is burning anyway, and everyone would have said *she had no choice*.

Power is what can be seized. He was offering it to me, lying in the gutter oil, waiting for whichever woman I decided to be.

"Inside," I said. "All of you. Now."

The apprentices went, dragging Tam, arguing, alive. Merrick stayed a moment longer. He couldn't see the lantern, but he could hear it — flame has a sound, small and greedy — and he could certainly smell the oil.

"Casper," he said quietly. "Whatever you're deciding, decide it fast. Fire travels faster than regret."

I took the lantern from Doss. He let me. I think his arm had simply stopped taking orders; I have seen it happen to compositors at the end of a long forme, the hand refusing the next letter. Then I took his collar, because a man should be moved by something stronger than suggestion, and I walked him through the shop — past the press my father bought secondhand and trued with his own shims, past the stone where I had set my first word at six years old (*cat*, since you ask, and I spelled it wrong), down the stairs to the type vault where the founders' matrices slept in their drawers like reliquary.

There is a strong room at the back. Father built it to keep the valuable fonts safe from fire and theft, a brick box with a barred door and a lock worth more than the door. I had never once put a person in it. It seemed suddenly the only honest use for it.

"In," I said.

"Casper—"

"In. The city will decide the rest tomorrow. Tonight you don't get to decide anything."

He went. Through the bars he looked at me with an expression I have spent some weeks decoding and have concluded was relief. That is the terrible thing about mercy: sometimes the man receiving it is more ashamed of it than the man giving it would have been of withholding it. He gripped the bars like a communicant.

"They'll come for me by morning," he said. "Either side. Doesn't matter which."

"Probably."

"You could leave me the lantern."

"So I could hear you burn? No."

"That's not—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Your father would have done this. Exactly this. Locked me in with the letters and gone back to work."

"Yes," I said. "That's rather the point of me."

I locked the vault and pocketed the key — my father's key, brass worn to the shape of his thumb, now learning mine — and I stood a moment in the dark listening to a man breathe on the other side of three inches of brick, and I felt none of the triumph the ballads will eventually assign to such moments. I felt like a compositor who has just distributed a difficult line: the words put away, the case orderly, the meaning still sitting somewhere in the chest where the metal can't reach it.

Upstairs, Merrick had already swept the oil-soaked cobbles with sand. Of course he had. The shop was lit like a festival; the presses were cold but ready; the apprentices were pressing their faces to the windows watching the river road glow with torches.

"What happens now?" Tam asked.

"Now," Merrick said, tilting his head toward the window and the growing roar beyond it, "we find out whether the words took."

Because here is what I had not yet told them, what I had been carrying since sundown like a stone in my shoe: the garrison had been ordered to clear the Bridge of Atlas at dawn, and half the garrison had refused, and the other half was marching on the ministries. Ilse was somewhere in that current. She had slipped her cell during the surge — a fisherman's boy told me, grinning gap-toothed, that a woman matching her description had walked out of the Garrison lockup between two arguing sergeants, bold as a dropped capital. But walked out *toward what*, the boy couldn't say. Toward her brother, maybe, if Aldous Vey truly lived. Toward the ministers' houses, if she wanted blood. Toward me, if I mattered as much as I had begun, dangerously, to hope.

Through the window, downstream, the sky over the ministry quarter began to change color — and not with sunrise.

"Merrick," I said. "How fast can we set a headline?"

He smiled the way only a blind man can smile at a printer. "Casper. I've been composing it in my head since midnight."

* * *

By the time the first light came up gray over Vellmora, the presses were running again — all four of ours, and I could hear, faint and impossible, the stutter of other presses waking across the district, one, then two, then a ragged chorus of them, shops I had never set foot in answering like bells. The apprentices worked in shifts of pure joy. Ondo fed the press with the solemnity of an acolyte. Merrick read our proofs with his fingers and pronounced them clean.

And then, as the sun finally cleared the river mist and the bells began properly this time — all of them, unruly, overlapping, glad — the street door opened without a knock.

She stood in the ink-light, soot to her knees, one sleeve scorched to the elbow, her brother's eyes scanning the room until they found me. Ilse looked at the running presses, the flying sheets, the apprentices black to the wrists, and then at me, ink-stained and shaking and grinning like a fool, and she said:

"I lost the letter."

I pulled a fresh copy off the press and put it in her hands.

"No," I said. "You didn't."
