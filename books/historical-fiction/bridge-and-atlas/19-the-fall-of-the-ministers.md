## 19. The Fall of the Ministers

My brother has always had a genius for entrances.

When we were children in Corvel Bay, he once arrived at our mother's funeral an hour late, soaked to the knees, having walked the last six miles from the mail coach because — he said — it seemed wrong to arrive at a burial on wheels. He was fourteen. He cried through the whole service and then ate three plates of food at the wake, because grief, Aldous always insisted, is a kind of hunger. I have spent my whole life loving a man who turns his own legend into theater without ever quite admitting that's what he's doing.

So when the garrison refused its orders at half past two in the morning, and the ministers' carriages came rattling out of the Ministry Quarter like beetles scattering from a lifted stone, I should not have been surprised that Aldous chose that precise hour to walk back into Vellmora on foot, hatless, gaunt as a fence rail, looking for all the world like a man arriving late at his own funeral.

Which, in a sense, he was.

* * *

I did not see him emerge. I want to be honest about that, because everything else people will tell you about this night is embellished, including by me. What I saw was smaller and stranger: I was standing in the doorway of Sef Brandt's shop with Casper, both of us inked to the elbows, watching ten thousand copies of our brother's testimony dry on every flat surface in the room, when a knock came at the door — three raps, then a pause, then two more. A rhythm I hadn't heard since I was nine years old, since the game we invented on the wharf at Corvel Bay to signal each other across the fish market crowds. *Here. Here am I. Where are you?*

Casper looked at me. "You know that knock."

"I've known it since before I knew my letters," I said.

She unbolted the door, and there he was. My brother. Alive. Thinner than the proofs I remembered setting, grayer in the beard, with a scar along his jaw that hadn't been in any specimen book. He smelled of river mud and woodsmoke and some safehouse's cheap tallow candles. Behind him, Merrick stood with his white eyes turned toward the harbor wind, wearing the small satisfied smile of a man whose plan has worked exactly as convolutedly as he intended.

"You're supposed to be dead," I said. It seemed important to say it out loud, to establish the terms.

"I was," Aldous said. "It didn't take."

And then, because he is who he is, he looked past me at the drying handbills stacked in fathoms on Brandt's stone floor, and picked one up, and read his own testimony — the letter I had carried through three checkpoints sewn into my coat lining, the words Casper had recut from memory through a sleepless night, letter by letter, grief transmuted into lead — and his eyes filled, and he said, in a voice gone hoarse:

"The kerning's a little tight on 'ministers.'"

Casper hit him. Open-palmed, across the shoulder, hard enough to make the handbill jump from his fingers. "The kerning is *fine*. I cut those sorts at four in the morning."

"It's very fine," he said, rubbing his arm. "It's the finest thing I've ever read." And then he hugged her, and then me, and for a long moment none of us managed any wit at all.

* * *

By four in the morning, Vellmora belonged to no one and everyone.

You could feel the change in the sound of it. Cities have a texture under their noise — the cobbles hum differently when soldiers march on them than when carts do — and that morning the hum was all wrong for the Ministry. The garrison had refused the order to clear the Bridge of Atlas at bayonet point; a captain — not the same captain who had stared too long at my face at the Arch, but word travels fast in a garrison — had reportedly torn his orders in half in front of his colonel and walked back into the barracks. The barricades that had risen overnight out of paper carts, printing stones, and the furniture of the Paper District were being reinforced rather than stormed. Somewhere a church bell that had been silent for years was ringing, badly, an amateur hand on the rope.

Aldous wanted to go up onto the bridge. Of course he did.

"No," Merrick said, before I could say it. The blind compositor sat with his hands flat on a case of type like a man resting them on a sleeping animal. "Not yet. Not until you understand what you're walking into. They don't believe you're alive yet, Aldous. They believe the *letter*. That's different, and it matters."

"How does it matter?" Aldous asked. He had a way of asking questions he knew the answers to, which I suspect he learned from years of editing other people's pamphlets.

"Because they believe the letter, they'll follow it anywhere," Merrick said. "Including after you. If you appear now, in this light, half of them will decide you're a ghost, and the other half will decide the letter was forged, and both halves will be harder to reach tomorrow. Let them read you first. Then let them look at you."

"That's obscene," I said. "Making him wait while the city reads him."

"It's printing," Merrick said mildly. "You let the ink dry before you fold the sheet. Otherwise everything smudges."

Aldous laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him — and I watched something pass between him and Merrick, some recognition between two men who understood that words were instruments and instruments required discipline. It stung, a little, that quick intimacy. I had crossed a city with his letter against my ribs. I had talked a captain into erasing my own name. And here was my brother, back from the dead by way of a staged execution I still hadn't heard explained, taking direction from a stranger in under a minute.

Family, I find, is mostly jealousy with better manners.

* * *

He explained the execution eventually, in the grey hours, sitting on a crate of Casper's recut sorts with his hands wrapped around tea nobody remembered making. The story was less romantic than the city would come to believe, which is the fate of all stories that survive their own telling.

They never meant to kill him. That was the banality of it. Minister Corvane — the first name on the letter, the one with the most to lose — had ordered him *removed*, and the word *removed* had traveled down three layers of bureaucracy until it arrived at a prison yard as *executed*, and the officer charged with carrying it out had discovered at the last moment that he couldn't do it, so he'd substituted a corpse from the fever ward, a man already dead of no one's intention, and reported success up the chain. Then everyone above him had found themselves committed to a fiction. Aldous had spent eight months in a cellar in the Tanners' Row district, moved twice, fed by a jailer's widow who believed she was hiding a priest.

"They announced my death before they'd decided what to do with me," he said. "That's the whole regime in one sentence, Ilse. They announce first and govern second. I lay in the dark listening to the bells ring for my funeral and thought: they've finally printed something true by accident."

"And you stayed hidden," Casper said. "For eight months. While they burned your press and jailed your compositors."

"I stayed hidden because the moment I surfaced, I became the story," Aldous said, and there was something raw under the wit, the first unguarded thing he'd said all night. "A living martyr is worth nothing if he's caught. Dead, I was useful. Every pamphlet they banned proved I was dangerous; every rumor that I lived made them flinch. I was more powerful as a ghost than I have ever been as a man. Don't look at me like that, either of you. You've read enough of my prose to know I can dress up cowardice as strategy better than anyone in Vellmora."

"It wasn't cowardice," I said.

"Of course it wasn't," he said. "That's what makes it so easy to defend."

* * *

The ministers fled in ones, the way cowards always do — together only in the sense that plague fleas travel together.

Corvane went first, before dawn, his carriage so overloaded with strongboxes that it threw a wheel on the Corvel road and had to be abandoned; a fishwife told us later, with immense relish, that she'd seen the Minister of the Interior standing in the mud in his nightshirt directing servants to carry ledgers like a general commanding a retreat. Minister Hale tried the river, which would have been clever any week but that one; the ferrymen had heard the handbills read aloud, all three names, and simply rowed him back to his own dock and left him sitting there among his luggage like forgotten freight. He was taken by the crowd before noon, though not harmed — the printers' council had been explicit, in a leaflet set by Casper's hands before sunup, that there would be no hangings, that the punishment for these men was to live in a city that had read their correspondence. I have never been sure whether that mercy was principle or a subtler cruelty. With printers, it is usually both.

The third name on the letter — Undersecretary Brann, the small fish, the man who had actually signed the removal order — did not flee at all. He locked himself in his office with the ministry's records and set fire to them, which tells you everything about where such men believe their safety lives: in paper. But Vellmora was done with that lesson. The fire brigade arrived, and instead of water they brought compositors, and by evening Brann's own clerks were sorting his files into evidence and handing them over in ordered bundles, catalogued, collated, *set in sequence*. You cannot outrun people who have spent their lives putting things in the right order.

Watching it from Brandt's upstairs window, Casper said quietly: "Doss is still in the vault."

"Yes."

"What happens to men like him, when the fighting stops?"

"Men like him," I said, "become the argument everyone pretends not to be having." She didn't answer, and I understood I had said something truer than kind, and that Casper Oyelaran was going to spend the next part of her life deciding what to do with it. That was hers to carry. I had learned something about what can be carried, and part of it is: other people's choices are not among the things that fit in your hands.

* * *

At noon, Aldous went to the bridge.

Merrick had been right to make him wait. The city had read him all morning — sheets passed hand to hand down the line of the barricades, read aloud at corners by anyone with a voice and the lungs for it, and by afternoon the words had done their work. When Aldous walked up onto the Bridge of Atlas, hatless, in the same mud-stained coat he'd knocked in our door with, the crowd did not gasp or cheer. There was a silence, a full five seconds of it, ten thousand people checking a face against a memory and a memory against a letter. And then a woman near the bridgehead — I never learned her name — simply began to laugh, a huge helpless laughter, and said, "Well. He's uglier than his statue," and the bridge came apart with noise.

He spoke for perhaps four minutes. I barely remember what he said, which I confess with a historian's shame and a sister's relief. I was watching the crowd instead. I saw grown printers weeping without embarrassment. I saw apprentices holding copies of the handbill overhead like relics. I saw an old woman touch his sleeve as he passed and then look at her own hand as if it had touched history, and I thought: *he's theirs now.* Whatever he had been to me — the boy on the wharf, the signal knock, the infuriating genius of my childhood — he had finished becoming public property, and there is no deaccessioning from that collection. You can resign from a ministry. You cannot resign from a legend.

Afterward, in the back room of Brandt's shop, he offered me the thing I knew was coming. He'd been composing it all day; I could see the sentences assembling behind his eyes the way they used to when he was drafting pamphlets at our mother's table, lips moving slightly.

"There will have to be a new press authority," he said. "Something between the council and the ministries. Someone the printers trust, someone who bled for the letter, someone with my name who isn't only my name." He took my hand. His was shaking; the bridge had cost him more than the crowd saw. "Stand beside me, Ils. Publicly. We finish it the way we started everything — together, arguing."

And here is the part I will be misquoted about for the rest of my life, so let me set it straight myself, in my own hand, since I have apparently become a person whose hand matters.

I said no.

Not because I didn't love him. Because I had just spent a week learning exactly what a name costs. At the Garrison Arch I had erased myself to survive — claimed to be a stranger to my own name, felt it peel away from me like wet paper — and I had sworn, in the cold under my fear, that if I ever got my name back, I would not immediately hand it to a government. Aldous needed a partner who wanted the podium. I wanted the *work*: the cases, the proofs, the small honest labor of getting a sentence right. Power is what can be seized. Truth is what can be carried. I had carried it through three checkpoints, and I had no intention of setting it down on a ministerial desk.

Besides — and this I did say aloud, because he deserved the wry version too — "You've never once in your life accepted editorial correction, Aldous. You want me standing beside you correcting your speeches in real time? You'd have me hanged within the year. Properly, this time."

He laughed until he had to sit down, and then he stopped laughing and held my face in his hands the way he did when we were children and one of us had skinned a knee, and he said, "Then stay a printer. Stay the hardest critic in Vellmora. God knows the republic will need critics more than it needs ministers. Ministers we seem able to grow ourselves."

* * *

Evening came down over the city like a slow wash of clean water. From the shop roof we could see fires — celebratory ones, cooking ones, the ordinary fires of a population suddenly allowed to be hungry in public — and the bridge crowded still, and somewhere a press running, and another, and another, a heartbeat of iron under the whole town. Casper stood beside me with her arms folded, ink to the elbow even after washing, that permanent stain her father's trade had written into her skin.

"He'll be different now," she said. "Your brother."

"He was always different. Now it's just official."

"And you?"

I thought about it honestly, which is more than most people get to do on the best day of their city's life. "Lighter," I said finally. "Which is its own kind of loss. I've been heavy for so long I'll have to learn how to walk."

Below us in the shop, Merrick's knock sounded at the door — three raps, pause, two — and Casper went down to answer it, and I heard her stop halfway down the stairs, and call up to me in a voice pitched strangely flat:

"Ilse. Come look at this. Someone's left something at the door."

I went down. On the threshold, wrapped in oilcloth against the evening damp, weighted with a stone, was a package — and pinned to it a note in a foreman's careful, servile, unmistakable hand. The handwriting I had hoped never to see again.

Casper read it once, and her face did something complicated, and she handed it to me without a word.
