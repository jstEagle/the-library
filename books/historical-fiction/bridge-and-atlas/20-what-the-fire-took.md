## 20. What the Fire Took

The fire had eaten the front of the shop and left the back standing out of what I can only assume was professional courtesy. Fire is a compositor too, in its way — it sets lines and breaks them, decides which letters survive a page. It had chosen my father's corner.

I stood in the doorway at dawn with the soot still warm underfoot and did what any sensible woman does after catastrophe: I took inventory.

The windows were gone. The door was gone. The great iron press — Father's press, bought secondhand from a failed Bible house in his thirties, older than me by a decade and better maintained than either of us — lay on its side with its platen cracked like an egg. The paper stock had burned to a gray drift two inches deep across the floorboards, and when I moved through it, it rose around my ankles in soft clouds and settled on everything, including, eventually, inside me. I would be coughing gray for a week. Grief, I have found, comes in many textures. This one got into the lungs.

"Measuring?" Ilse said from behind me.

"Accounting." I held up a hand before she could cross the threshold. "Mind the third step. It's not there anymore."

She came in anyway, because of course she did, stepping where I'd told her not to and finding the gap with her boot. She had a way of entering rooms as if they owed her something. Three weeks ago that quality had terrified me. Now it was nearly comforting, in the manner of a roof that leaks in a familiar pattern.

"How much is gone?" she asked.

"That's not how you count it." I turned a slow circle. "You count what's left. Otherwise the numbers eat you."

* * *

Here is what was left.

The stone sink in the back room, black with old ink, unbothered by anything as vulgar as fire. My cot, smelling now of smoke instead of mildew — an upgrade, technically. The drying line with its last four sheets of proof paper, curled and browned at the edges but whole. And in the far corner, behind a wall that had held while everything around it gave, the type vault.

The vault was Father's stubbornness made architectural. Other printers kept their sorts in cabinets, in racks, in the open air where any inspector or arsonist could count them. He built a room within a room, brick-lined, with a door that weighed as much as a sinner's conscience, and he kept in it only the things that mattered. When I was small I asked him why he didn't keep money in there instead, since money mattered more, and he set down his bodkin and looked at me over his spectacles with the particular patience he reserved for questions that would embarrass me later.

"Money is a rumor," he said. "Type is a promise. You can print a rumor about money. You cannot print money about itself."

I was seven. I thought this was nonsense. I have since spent thirty years learning it was the entire architecture of my life.

The vault door was swollen shut with heat, and I had no key anyway — the key had been in Doss's pocket for eleven years, along with everything else of ours he'd quietly pocketed. But hinges are honest workers; they only need persuading. Ilse found the maul. I found the wedge. Between the two of us and a fury neither of us named aloud, we had the door open within the hour, breathing out air that smelled of lead and linseed and my father's coat, which had hung on the inside hook since before I could reach the hook.

His case was there. Not just *a* case — *the* case. The one he'd carried out of the Vellmora riots of his own youth, the one I'd carried out of the Ashwater quarter when I was six and the soldiers came, the one that had crossed two borders strapped to a woman's back while I rode on top of it, a child ballasted with forty pounds of other people's alphabet. Walnut case, brass corners worn to gold, every compartment labeled in his cramped upright hand: *long s*, *swash caps*, *flourishes — use sparingly, Hazel*.

He had written my name on the flourishes. Not Casper — Hazel, the name he called me when the work was done, the name that belonged to evenings, to supper, to being a daughter instead of an apprentice. I stood holding that label longer than any grown woman should stand holding a slip of paper, and Ilse, to her enormous credit, went outside to smoke and let me do it without an audience.

Some griefs are private property. Trespassers should be prosecuted.

* * *

By noon we had carried the case into the yard behind the shop, where the light was honest, and I began the work of triage. A type case after a fire is a census of survivors. Some compartments had cooked — the lead doesn't melt at ordinary fire temperatures unless the fire means it, but the wood warps and the sorts shift and mix, and mixed type is worse than lost type, because you must sort it all back again by hand, letter against letter, an act of patience I did not currently possess.

The italic had survived. The small pica had survived. The swash caps, God forgive them, had survived intact and smug, every useless ornament accounted for. My father's oldest sorts — the ones he'd cut himself, before I was born, with a graver and bad light and no teacher — were fine. Of course they were. They'd already survived worse than a fire. They'd survived him dying.

"You're smiling," Ilse observed from the doorstep.

"I'm inventorying."

"Your face does something else when it inventories."

"My face," I said, "has been through a great deal lately and is entitled to its inconsistencies."

* * *

Doss's plates were also in the vault. That deserves explaining, though I will explain it badly, because betrayal resists tidy narration.

In the weeks before everything, before the letter and the checkpoints and the bridge, Doss had taken possession of certain confiscated plates — pamphlet matrices seized in the raids, official plates, government work he'd brought home like a cat bringing birds to the door. I'd assumed then that it was ambition. It was, but the ambition ran the other direction: he wasn't collecting trophies for himself. He was curating evidence for the ministers, cataloguing exactly whose words existed in metal, so that when the fires came, the fires would know where to look.

Except the fire had come for *him* instead, in a sense. Or rather, the mob had. And the vault, that fat silent brick heart of the shop, had kept his collection safe from everyone including him.

Eleven plates. Heavy things, stereotype and electrotype, faces I recognized — the bread levy proclamation, the sedition act, a commemorative plate struck for the anniversary of some minister's elevation, cast in the smug official roman that every schoolchild in Vellmora could read in their sleep. Evidence of what had been done to us, cast in the very metal of the doing.

Ilse wanted them for the council. Burn them, she said, or archive them, or hang them in the square with placards. Merrick, when I mentioned it to him, went quiet in the way blind men go quiet, listening to some inner ledger, and said only: "Ask yourself who benefits from keeping them."

I asked myself. I asked myself for three days, sorting type by day and thinking about metal by night, and here is the arithmetic I arrived at.

Those plates were Doss's. His confiscations, his betrayals, his currency — the coin he'd minted out of other people's silenced voices. Every sort on them had been taken from a printer who lost his shop or his hands or, in two cases I knew of, his life. They were not evidence. Evidence is what you carry forward. These were weight. And I had spent my whole life learning the difference between what can be carried and what cannot.

My father taught me the trade, but my mother taught me the funerals. She grew up on the coast, where they buried their dead in sand above the tide line so the sea could take its time. "Nothing is finished until it's given back," she used to say, and she meant fish, mostly, but a philosophy will stretch if you pull it.

So on the fourth morning, before the city was fully awake, I took the plates down to the river in a market basket, with a hand spade and a length of rope, and Ilse came along because she had decided somewhere in the past weeks that she was responsible for me, a delusion I permitted because it made her easier to manage.

The river at that hour was doing its best impression of indifference. Fog on the water, barges asleep, the Bridge of Atlas upstream with its new barricade stones still mortared crooked from the fighting. I chose a spot below the tannery steps, where the mud is deep and nobody fishes, because even the fish deserve better than Doss's legacy.

"You're sure about this," Ilse said. Not quite a question.

"No."

"Good. Certainty is for magistrates."

I tied the rope around the bundle of plates — all eleven, bound face to face so their letters kissed each other in the dark, every proclamation pressed against every proclamation like conspirators — and I weighted it with a broken quoin stone, and I lowered it off the steps into the green-black water, and I let the river take it the way my mother's sea took its dead. Slowly. With interest.

I won't pretend it was solemn. The rope slipped twice, the second time soaking me to the knee, and Ilse laughed — actually laughed, that dry bark of hers — and the laugh broke something loose in my chest that had been sitting there since the bridge, since the search, since the moment the letter left my hands in a soldier's fist and I chose, in half a heartbeat, to run rather than die beside it. I had been carrying that choice like a stone in my shoe. Somewhere between the second slip and the splash, it worked its way out.

"For what it's worth," Ilse said, watching the bubbles rise, "he'd have sold those plates to buy his way out of the city by now, if he hadn't been locked in your own vault waiting on the mob."

"I know."

"He isn't worth a funeral."

"Nobody's worth a funeral," I said. "That's what makes them generous."

* * *

We sat on the tannery steps afterward and let the fog decide whether to be morning. My stockings steamed. Downriver, a barge crew was arguing about cargo in the universal language of barge crews, which is to say at volume and without content.

"Do you ever count it?" Ilse asked eventually. "What it cost?"

"Constantly. I get different totals every time."

"What's today's?"

I thought about it properly, because she had earned a proper answer. "The press. The paper stock. Four years of Doss, which I'll deduct from his wages if I ever pay out his estate, which I won't. Mr. Pellucci on the corner, who died on his own doorstep during the raids for the crime of sweeping. Ten thousand copies of a letter I can recite but never hold." I turned my hands over. Ink in every crease, burned into the whorls now, I suspect, permanently. "And a version of myself who believed the shop was permanent. She's under that rubble somewhere. I find I don't miss her as much as expected."

"And what survived?"

"The type." I said it simply, because it was simple. "The craft. One rival I haven't spoken to in fifteen years who turned out to keep his promises. One sister I never planned on." Ilse went very still at *sister*. I pretended not to notice, which is the family art of both our families. "And a memory good enough to rebuild a dead man's testimony letter by letter. Which, as it turns out, was the only asset in the whole inventory anyone with power couldn't seize, burn, or buy."

"Your father's thesis," she said.

"My father's thesis. Power is what can be seized. Truth is what can be carried." I stood, and brushed off nothing, because ash brushes off onto further ash. "Though I'd amend it, these days. Truth is what can be carried *and put down again*. Carrying alone is just martyrdom with better posture."

* * *

Back at the shop, I opened the vault one more time and stood among the surviving cases and did the final sum. It came to this: enough sound type to set eight pages. One battered press needing a new platen, which Sef Brandt could machine if his hands allowed, and Sef Brandt's hands always allowed, the old goat simply billed extra for the suffering. One walnut case with my name hidden in a drawer of flourishes. Two compositors' worth of knowledge — mine and, increasingly, whether she liked it or not, Ilse's. And an apprentice or two from the wrecked quarters, Merrick had said, young hands with nothing left to lose, which is the traditional recruitment pool for both armies and presses, and historically the presses come out ahead.

I took the walnut case inside and set it on the bench where Father's bench had been, in the corner the fire had spared, and unlabeled as it was, unlabeled as I am, I felt the shop stop being a ruin and start being a site. There is a distinction. Ruins are endings. Sites are arguments about beginnings.

Ilse appeared in the burnt doorway with two cups of something the color of regret and the temperature of vengeance.

"So," she said. "What does a woman print, the morning after she buries a foreman's sins in the river?"

I drank the coffee. It was terrible. I had earned terrible coffee; I intended to enjoy it thoroughly.

"Not a pamphlet," I said, and watched her face do surprise, that rare and delicious event. "Pamphlets are for wars. Wars end." I looked at the type cases stacked against the surviving wall, at the light coming through the empty window frames like the building had decided to think bigger about windows. "We're going to need a new face of type. Something cut fresh. Something that hasn't lied for anybody yet."

Ilse set down her cup. "And what would we call it?"

I had, as it happens, been carrying that answer for days. Everything I own, I carry.
