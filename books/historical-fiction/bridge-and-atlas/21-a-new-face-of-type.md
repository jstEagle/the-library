## 21. A New Face of Type

Spring came to Vellmora the way ink comes to paper — slowly, then all at once, and never quite where you intended it.

I stood in what remained of my father's shop on the first warm morning of the year, and I will confess that my first act was not noble. I did not weep over the scorched lintel or salute the memory of the barricades. I swept. Two hours with a broom, moving ash from one corner of the floor to another, because grief, in my experience, is mostly housekeeping you keep postponing.

The fire had taken the press. It had taken the roof above the press, which is how fires usually manage it, and half the wall, and every case of type except the one case that mattered — my father's original, the fourteen-point he'd carried out of Marrow's Landing on his back when he was younger than I am now, the one I had once carried out of a pogrom myself, though I hadn't known yet that I would carry it twice.

Ilse found me at noon, still sweeping.

"You missed a spot," she said, leaning in the doorway where Doss used to stand when he was watching me too closely. The doorway suited her better. She'd put weight back on since the bridge; her face no longer looked like a rumor of her brother's.

"I'm being thorough."

"You're hiding." She picked up the second broom propped against the wall — she'd brought her own, which told me something about how long she'd expected this conversation to take — and started on the far corner. "Merrick says the council wants to talk to us about the shop."

"The council can want things. Wanting is free."

"They want to give it back to you. Formally. With a deed and everything."

I leaned on the broom. "And?"

"And they want it to be a cooperative press. Six shops, pooled type, pooled profits. They're calling it the Commonwealth of Vellmora Printers, which means a committee named it, because no one person would choose six words when two would do."

I looked around at the ash and the broken sky where my ceiling used to be. My father had built this shop with his hands and filled it with letters that outlived him, and I had spent one long winter learning that the letters could also outlive their uses — that a font cut for pamphlets could set a primer just as easily, and that the same hands that forged customs passes could teach a child her alphabet if you gave them a different afternoon.

"Tell them yes," I said.

Ilse stopped sweeping. "That was fast."

"Don't sound disappointed. You had a speech prepared."

"I had three speeches. One of them involved crying."

"Save it. We'll need it for the deed signing."

* * *

The trouble with cooperatives, as any compositor will tell you, is that everyone owns the em quad and nobody owns the decision. Our first full meeting ran four hours and settled nothing except that Merrick, blind as he was, had the best sense of the room of anyone present — he could hear who was about to say something foolish and interrupt them before they got there, a gift I intend to study under laboratory conditions.

But the second meeting produced something better than procedure. Ilse brought out a sheet of proofs — specimens, really, pulled from the surviving fonts of the six shops, laid side by side on the long table — and asked the question that changed the year:

"Why are we setting the city's future in dead men's faces?"

She didn't mean it cruelly. Half those fonts *were* dead men's — founders gone, foundries burned, punches sold off piecemeal during the occupation. But the room went quiet the way rooms do when someone has said the true thing by accident.

"We cut a new face," said old Sef Brandt, who had come down from his own shop across the lane, ostensibly to complain about our chimney smoke and actually because he couldn't bear to be excluded. He was seventy-three and had spent forty years as my father's rival and one very long night as his daughter's partner, recutting a murdered man's testimony letter by letter while the city burned outside. Rivalry, in the print trade, turns out to be a slow form of love. "A new face. Cut here. Cut by us."

"By whom?" someone asked. "Type cutting takes years."

"It takes patience," Sef said. "Which we have in surplus, since none of us can afford to die."

* * *

We called the face Atlas before we'd cut a single letter, which is backwards, but printers have never let sequence dictate sentiment. The name was Merrick's. "It held up the bridge," he said, meaning the Bridge of Atlas, meaning the morning the presses ran and ten thousand handbills carried Aldous Vey's living voice into a city that woke up already decided. "Let it hold up something else now."

Cutting type is an education in humility, which I recommend to everyone and have personally endured enough of for one lifetime. You begin with steel punches, each letter graven larger than life so its impression can be struck into copper, so the copper can be cast from, so the cast can be dressed and fitted — and every stage of this is an opportunity to ruin everything, which is the trade's way of keeping you honest.

Sef taught the cutting. He held the graver like a surgeon holds a blade and talked the whole time, which I learned was how he kept his hands steady. "The counter," he'd say, carving the white inside an o, "is where the letter breathes. Get the counter wrong and the whole word suffocates." He made me cut fifty o's before he'd let me touch anything with corners. Fifty o's, and he rejected forty-nine, and the fiftieth he held to the light for a long moment and said nothing at all, which from Sef Brandt was a knighthood.

Ilse turned out to have the steadier hand. I try not to resent this. She'd spent months forging prison records under a regime's nose, copying a dead-seeming man's handwriting until she could produce his g's in her sleep, and it transpired that a woman who can counterfeit one hand can invent another. Her lowercase had a warmth mine lacked. Mine had the discipline. Between us — her counters, my fit — the face began to exist.

There were evenings, that spring, when the two of us sat at the cutting bench until the lamps guttered, arguing about the terminal of an r, and I want to be honest about what those evenings were, because it took me longer than it should have to name it. They were the first time in my adult life that I was building something instead of hiding something. No seditious plates under the funeral type. No lies rehearsed in a safehouse. Just steel and copper and the slow, stubborn manufacture of a thing that would outlast both of us, made for no purpose more urgent than teaching children to read.

It felt, absurdly, like luxury. It took me weeks to understand it was actually peace.

* * *

The first commission came in June, and the committee nearly strangled it in its cradle.

"A school primer," Merrick reported, turning his unseeing eyes toward each of us in turn, a habit that always made me feel briefly audited. "The new municipal schools. Four hundred copies by autumn."

"Four hundred," said one of the Bridge Street men, "in a face that isn't finished."

"In a face that will be finished," I said, "because it has to be."

"There are older fonts. Proven fonts. This is children we're talking about."

"Yes," I said. "Children. Which is exactly why."

I didn't have the argument fully assembled yet, but it assembled itself as I spoke, the way arguments do when they've been waiting in you for years without your knowledge. "Every pamphlet in this city was set in borrowed type. Everything the old regime printed, everything we printed over it — all of it worn, secondhand faces, letters that belonged to other people's purposes. These children will learn their alphabet from the first face this city ever cut for itself. Their a will be ours. When they write, they'll write in shapes we made. That's not vanity, that's—" I stopped, because the end of the sentence was *that's legacy*, and legacy was a word I associated with my father's grave and wasn't ready to say aloud in a committee.

"That's foundation," said Merrick quietly, saving me as he always did.

* * *

The primer itself was my father's ghost in more ways than one. Among the salvage from the fire, water-stained but legible, we'd found a draft he'd been working on in his last year — pages in his own cramped hand: *A is for Atlas*, apparently, because even twenty years ago the man had been unable to resist a pun, and beyond that alphabets of trades, a compositor's verse about the em quad, a little story about a boy who carries a type case on his back across a border at night.

He'd never finished it. There were gaps where his attention had wandered, pages that trailed off mid-sentence. Ilse and I spent a week of evenings filling them, and I will tell you the strangest part: finishing my dead father's sentences in a typeface I had helped invent, with the sister of a man the old world tried to kill sitting close enough that our elbows touched at the bench — the strangest part was how ordinary it felt. How much like Tuesday.

"This stanza," Ilse said one evening, tapping his verse about the compositor's trade. *"What fits in your hands is what you keep."* Did he know? Did he know that line would be you someday?

"He knew everything," I said. "That was the exhausting thing about him."

She laughed, and I caught myself laughing, and somewhere in that laugh the shop stopped being a ruin we were restoring and became a place we simply worked, the way you stop noticing a scar.

* * *

The first proof came off the rebuilt press — a loaner from Sef, delivered with the observation that he wanted it back "when you've finished ruining it" — on a gray morning in late July. Ilse pulled the lever; I fed the sheets. Four hundred copies of *a b c d*, in a face that existed because two women had argued about the curve of an r for a season, cut from steel neither of us owned alone.

I held the first damp sheet up to the window light, and the letters breathed — Sef's word, Sef's test — every counter open, every word standing easy on the page like it had lived there all its life.

"Well?" Ilse said.

I thought about measuring my answer in ems, because that's how I measure most things, and I found I didn't need to. Some things don't fit a gauge.

"It'll do," I said, which is Oyelaran for *this is the best thing I have ever made and I am not going to cry in front of the help*, and Ilse, who had learned to read me the way I read proof, grinned and said nothing, and outside the window the presses of six shops began to run.

The primer went out to the schools in September. In October, a girl of perhaps eight came to the door with her teacher, wanting to see "where our letters live," and stood in the middle of my shop staring at the type cases like a pilgrim at a shrine, and when I showed her the steel punch of the lowercase a — Ilse's a, with its warm round belly — she touched it with one finger, the way you touch something alive.

"Who made this?" she asked.

"We did," I said. "All of us. You'll make things too."

She nodded solemnly, unconvinced, the way children are, and left with her class, and I went back to the bench, and that should have been the end of the chapter, the tidy ending, the curtain.

But that evening a letter arrived at the ink door, hand-delivered, sealed in black wax, and the hand on the address was not one I knew — and I have known every hand in this city since I was tall enough to sort a case.

It was addressed not to the Commonwealth of Vellmora Printers, nor to me, nor to Ilse.

It was addressed to *the compositor of Atlas*, in a script I did not recognize, from a city I had never heard of — and inside, when I broke the seal that night with the shop empty and the lamps low, was a single line in that stranger's hand:

*They are burning the print shops in Corvane. We need a face of our own.*
