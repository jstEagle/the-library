## 22. The Letters Outlive Us

The apprentice's name was Tam, and he had the particular talent of the young for asking questions at exactly the wrong moment. He asked this one while I was standing on a ladder with a composing stick in one hand and half my weight committed to nothing at all.

"Casper," he said, "why do you measure everything in ems? Why not just use inches like a normal person?"

I considered my answer from the top of the ladder, where I had a good view of the shop — the long banks of cases, the two presses running already despite the hour, the smell of ink and oil and the river coming through the open doors. Ten years ago I would have told him it was because inches were the measure of carpenters and tailors, and we were none of those things. Five years ago I might have made a joke about it and climbed down.

But Tam was seventeen, which was the age I'd been when my father first put a type case in my hands and told me that letters outlive men. And it was the anniversary of the rising, and the city outside was hanging bunting off the Bridge of Atlas as if a bridge could be festive, and some days the truth wants telling more than a joke does.

"Come here," I said, and climbed down, because a lesson delivered from a ladder sounds like scolding and one delivered at eye level sounds like inheritance.

He came. They always do, when you don't make them.

"An em," I said, holding up the quad I'd been carrying, "is the width of the letter M in whatever face you're setting. That's all it is. Not a fixed thing. It changes with every font. Set in our Atlas face, an em is a generous, well-fed letter. Set in something cramped and mean, an em shrinks to fit."

"So it's — different everywhere?"

"Everywhere. Every face, every founder, every shop. An em in Vellmora isn't an em in Corvassa. There is no authority anywhere that can tell you what an em is. You have to know your own type." I set the quad in his palm and closed his fingers over it. "Inches are handed down by kings. Ems are agreed upon by people who work together. That's why we measure in them."

Tam turned the little block of metal over, frowning at it the way he frowned at everything, which I had decided was a virtue and not a nuisance. "That seems like a lot of philosophy for a unit of measurement."

"Everything worth measuring carries philosophy in it somewhere. You just have to weigh it properly." I took the quad back. "Besides. A man once taught me that power is whatever can be seized, but truth is whatever can be carried. And an em is small enough to carry. An inch never fit in anyone's pocket in their life."

He laughed at that, and went back to his case, and I stood a moment longer watching him sort type by feel the way I'd taught him, the way my father taught me, the way someone had surely taught someone since before there were cities to print in. Then Ilse came through the door with flour on her sleeves and a look on her face that meant she'd heard at least the last part of it, and she raised one eyebrow at me in the old way, and I went to meet her before she could say anything embarrassing in front of the apprentices.

"You're lecturing again," she said.

"I'm answering a question."

"You're lecturing. You get that tone. Your father had it too. Sef used to complain about it for forty years."

"Sef earned the right to complain about anything he liked. He's dead." I brushed flour off her sleeve, mostly to be doing it. "Is the bread done?"

"The bread is done, the bread has been done since dawn, and if you ask me about the bread one more time I'm going to start charging you rent on the oven. Come on. Merrick will want to hear the presses."

* * *

Merrick sat in his usual chair by the south window, where the light came in even though he couldn't use it, because he said he liked to know the sun was working. He was older now, whiter at the temples, but his hands hadn't aged a day — they moved over the proofs we brought him with the certainty of water finding a channel.

"Page four," he said, before I'd even handed it to him. "You've got a widow on page four. Line eleven of the second column."

"There is not a widow on page four."

"There is, and it's the word 'Vellmora,' which is the worst possible word to orphan, because it's the only word on the whole page anybody actually cares about." His fingers walked the column, patient as a blind man reading a face, and stopped. Smiled. "There. Told you."

Ilse leaned over my shoulder. "How does he do that?"

"Ten years of practice at being insufferable," Merrick said. "Fix the widow, Oyelaran. On a day like today, of all days, the city should read whole."

So I fixed the widow. I stood at the stone and nudged the spacing until Vellmora sat snug against its neighbors, and I thought, not for the first time, how strange it was that the largest things in a life turn on such small movements — the width of a hair, the space between words, the difference between a line that holds together and one that comes apart. The whole rising had turned on less. It had turned on whether one woman could remember the shape of another woman's grief accurately enough to cut it into metal in a single night, with a rival's grudging help and her own hands shaking.

They still asked me about that night, sometimes. The apprentices had heard the story so many times they mouthed along with the good parts. *Recut from memory, letter by letter, while the barricades went up.* Tam liked to imagine it dramatic — candlelight, storm, me striking the punches like a smith at a forge. The truth was duller and stranger: an old man who hated my father's ghost grinding punches in silence, me reciting Aldous Vey's letter under my breath like a prayer I didn't believe in yet, and both of us too tired and too frightened to say anything kind to each other until dawn, when Sef looked at the first proof and said, in the flattest voice I ever heard out of him, "Your father set a better M. But this one will do."

It did do. Ten thousand copies did. The whole city woke up reading a dead man's living words, and by noon the dead man wasn't dead anymore, and by evening the ministers whose names he'd written were running for the coast like men late for a ferry, and the garrison — the garrison simply put down its weapons on the bridge and started directing traffic, which remains to this day the most Vellmoran act of cowardice or wisdom ever recorded, depending on who tells it.

Sef lived four more years after that. Long enough to see the cooperative press take the shop over, long enough to quarrel with me about kerning at least weekly, long enough, I think, to stop being my father's rival and become something else, though neither of us ever found the word for it. When he died, we buried him next to Papa, and I set the memorial card myself, in Atlas face, and I checked the spacing three times, and it was perfect, and I cried anyway. Letters outlive men. But somebody has to carry them across the gap.

* * *

At midday we locked the shop — a formality; half the printers' guild was marching past our door — and went up to watch the anniversary procession from the roof, which was the traditional place, and also the place where Ilse and I had eaten lunch every anniversary since the third year, when we discovered that watching a parade from above was the only way to enjoy one.

Below us, the paper district had dressed itself in everything it owned. Banners of printed cotton hung from the drying lines. Apprentices marched with composing sticks held up like halberds. Someone had strung a great sheet from the Customs House — the same Customs House where a clerk had once nearly undone us with his hunger for a specimen book — bearing the first line of Vey's testimony in letters six feet tall, and beneath it, smaller, the words SET BY THE COOPERATIVE PRESSES OF VELLMORA, which Ilse claimed was vanity and I claimed was provenance, and we had never settled the argument and never intended to.

"There he is," Ilse said quietly.

Aldous Vey walked near the front, as he had every year, refusing the head of the parade with the stubbornness of a man who had learned exactly what pedestals cost. He was greyer. He walked with a stick now. The crowd loved him in the hungry, careless way crowds love their miracles, and he bore it the way he bore everything — as a debt to be paid off in work. He had never taken the ministry they offered him. He ran a small press in the old records district instead, printing school primers and municipal accounts and, once a year, a plain unadorned account of the three days in prison that had produced the letter, because he said a man who lets others tell his story deserves whatever story they tell.

"He should be up here eating our bread," I said.

"He'd hate it."

"He'd hate it thoroughly."

We ate the bread ourselves, then, sitting on the warm slates with our legs hanging over the street, watching ten thousand people celebrate the day the words got loose. Somewhere in the crowd, no doubt, Doss was remembered too — there was a ballad about him, of all things, a grim little song about the foreman who sold the shop and got locked in the type vault, and the ballad got the ending wrong, the way ballads do. In the ballad he dies in there among the sorts. In life he lived, and served his sentence, and left the city, and last anyone heard he was setting wedding invitations in a town upriver, which I choose to think of as a punishment rather than a redemption, because it keeps the ledger balanced in a way I can live with.

"Do you ever miss it?" Ilse asked. She meant the old days — the checkpoints, the fear, the letter warm against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

"Never," I said. Then, honestly: "Only the version of me that existed during it. She was very certain of things. This version measures everything in ems and worries about widows."

"This version fed a city on primers for ten years. Every child in Vellmora learned to read off your Atlas face."

"Our Atlas face. You cut half the lowercase."

"I cut the letters nobody notices. Which is most of them." She passed me the last of the bread. "That was the point, wasn't it? Your father's point. The ones nobody notices hold the rest together."

Below us the presses were running — not just ours. All of them. Every shop in the district had thrown its doors open for the anniversary, the way they did every year: free printing for anyone who came with words worth carrying. By tonight the walls would be papered with poems and grievances and recipes and love letters, the whole unruly census of a city talking to itself in ink. The soldiers' children would come with drawings. The clerks would come with jokes about their superiors. Somebody always printed something seditious, and nobody arrested them anymore, because there was no longer any authority in Vellmora that believed a piece of paper was dangerous enough to burn — and no one foolish enough to test whether the printers had forgotten how to fight.

Merrick appeared at the roof door, feeling his way along the rail with the ease of a man who had memorized this building down to its nails. "Oyelaran. Your apprentice is ruining my forme."

"Tam?"

"Tam. He's re-spacing page four. Says there's still a widow."

I sighed and got up, and Ilse laughed at me, and I went down the stairs two at a time into the noise and the smell and the light of the shop, where the presses were running and the boy was bending over the stone with my stick in his hand and my habits in his fingers, checking the space between the words the way you check the pulse of something alive.

"Leave it," I told him gently. "Some widows stay."

"But you said—"

"I know what I said. Today you learn the other lesson. The one that takes twenty years." I put my hand over his on the stick, and felt the type waiting under it, patient, portable, ready to be carried wherever the world needed it next. "Power is what can be seized, Tam. Truth is what can be carried. So we make the truth small enough to lift, and we keep lifting it, and one day some apprentice of yours will stand on this spot and ask you why you measure in ems, and you'll tell him it's because no king ever decided how wide an M should be."

Outside, the presses of Vellmora ran on, all of them at once, like a heartbeat that had finally learned its own name.
