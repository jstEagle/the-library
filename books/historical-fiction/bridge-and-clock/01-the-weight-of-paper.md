## 1. The Weight of Paper

There was a particular sound a well-folded signature made when you creased it properly—the kind of soft, satisfied crack, like a knuckle or a green twig, that told you the paper had accepted its fate. Odile Nystrom had been listening for that sound since she was nine years old, and she still found it the most honest noise in the world. Paper did not lie about whether it wanted to fold. People did.

"Again," said Ambrose from his bench by the window. "You're rushing the third fold."

"I am not rushing."

"You are rushing. The grain can feel it." He did not look up from the spine he was paring, but his knife paused in that way it did when he was about to say something true. "A book knows when it has been made with patience. It holds together longer. Same as people."

Odile smoothed the sheet against the bone folder and made the third crease again, slower this time, letting her wrist do the work instead of her temper. The crack came—soft, satisfied—and Ambrose grunted approval without turning around, which from him amounted to applause.

The Vellbruck bindery smelled the way it had smelled for sixty years: paste and leather and the faint sweetness of old glue warming in the kettle, underlaid with the river-damp that got into everything in this town and gave up fighting only in August. Morning light came through the north windows in long grey panes and fell across four workbenches, three of them Odile's now, though once they had belonged to apprentices who had gone off to the war, or to the capital, or simply to other lives. She kept their benches clean anyway. It seemed rude to let them gather dust on purpose.

She folded signatures until her shoulders ached pleasantly, then sewed them onto tapes at the sewing frame, the needle going down through the kettle-cuts and back up in the rhythm Ambrose had drilled into her: *down, across, up, pull; down, across, up, pull*. A book took shape one thread at a time. You could not see it happening, most days. Then one evening you looked up and there was a whole Testament sitting square and tight on the bench, and you thought, *well, I suppose I did that.*

It occurred to her sometimes—not often, and never out loud—that this was exactly how she preferred to think of herself. Someone things got done through. Not someone who decided anything.

* *

At noon the church bells rang, and with them came Herr Fenn, the chancery clerk, wearing his grey overcoat even though the stove made the shop warm enough to soften wax. He came twice a week, always at noon, always carrying nothing visible, which was the point of him.

"How is the Kessler account book coming along?" he asked loudly, so that anyone on the street could hear him ask it.

"Slow," Odile said, equally loudly. "The old owner used iron-gall ink like it was free. Every page wants mending."

"Terrible thing, careless ink." He set a slim package wrapped in brown paper on the counter, between the ledger of ordinary commissions and the tin of brass paper fasteners. "From the magistrate's office. For rebinding. Master Ambrose will know what to charge."

"Master Ambrose always does."

They had been having this conversation, in one form or another, for two years. Fenn would leave a package; Odile would carry it that night to one of six addresses—a fish smokehouse, a widow's house with blue shutters, the back room of the Lutheran reading society—and bring back another package, or nothing at all. She did not open them. That was the arrangement, and she had insisted on it herself, standing in Fenn's office with her chin up, explaining that she was a binder and a courier of objects, not a reader of secrets. What she didn't know couldn't be sewn out of her under a lamp somewhere.

Fenn lingered at the counter a moment longer than usual. His gloves were worn through at the thumb, she noticed. In the old days they hadn't been.

"And how is—" he began, and stopped himself, because the answer was obvious to everyone in Vellbruck and he knew it. The republic was losing. Had lost, probably, weeks ago, somewhere far to the east where the maps went vague. The government had left the capital by train in November. Nobody in Vellbruck used the word *fleeing*. They used the word *relocating*, the way you'd say a family had relocated after the house burned down.

"The chancery is very grateful for your discretion," Fenn finished instead.

"The chancery pays on time," Odile said. "That's rarer than discretion these days."

He almost smiled at that—a small, tired cracking at one corner of the mouth—and then he was gone into the noon crowd, grey coat among grey coats, forgettable as intended. Odile looked down at the brown-paper package. It weighed almost nothing. It always weighed almost nothing. She had decided long ago that this was the trick of the trade: whatever was inside, it never weighed more than a book.

She put it under the counter with the others and went back to her sewing frame. Down, across, up, pull.

* *

Ambrose taught her the rest of the craft in the afternoons: how to pare leather thin enough to turn a corner without buckling, how to raise the bands on a spine so gilt would catch light like water catching sun, how to match a marbled endpaper to a book's temper—"this one is a stormy fellow, give him stormy marble"—and how to judge, by smell alone, whether glue had gone past saving.

His hands had begun to shake. He hid it badly, or perhaps he had stopped trying to hide it. When he reached for the paring knife some mornings it danced an inch before his fingers settled on it, and Odile learned to have the knife already lying near his hand, angled correctly, without being asked. Neither of them mentioned this either. Their household ran on a mutual policy of not mentioning things, and it had served them both reasonably well since Marta died.

Marta. There was a name Odile did not allow herself at the bench. Names were like loose threads—you pulled one and the whole spine came apart in your hands.

"You're rushing again," Ambrose said mildly.

"I am not."

"You are. And you've folded that same signature twice, which means you're not here." He set down his knife and looked at her properly for the first time all day, his pale eyes doing that dissection thing that made her feel like a book opened to its worst page. "Girl. Whatever it is you're not thinking about, you're doing it very loudly."

"It's nothing. It's the weather."

"It's December. The weather is permitted to be nothing." But he let it go, the way he let most things go, and returned to his paring, and the shop filled up again with the small honest noises of work.

That evening they ate soup together in the room behind the workshop, bread from the baker who no longer asked why Odile's coin was sometimes French, sometimes something else entirely. Ambrose said grace, though neither of them believed much in anything anymore; it was a habit he performed for the benefit of the dead, he'd once explained, who were the only audience whose opinions he still worried about.

Afterward Odile washed the bowls and checked the package from Fenn a final time—string tight, seals intact, corners square—and put on her dark coat and her plainest scarf, the one that made her look like every other young woman hurrying home through the cold with her chin tucked down.

"Forgettable," Ambrose said from his chair, not looking up from his book. It was his version of *God keep you*.

"Always," she said, and went out into the dark.

* *

Vellbruck at night was a town pretending to sleep. Lamplight leaked through shutters in thin orange lines. Somewhere upstream the mill turned even at this hour, its wheel turning the river into a long low mutter. Odile moved the way she'd taught herself to move over two years: not quickly, not slowly, at the pace of a woman with a legitimate errand and no interest whatsoever in anything else. She kept to the side of the street that had the deepest doorways. She counted steps between corners without meaning to—she had tried not to and given up; the counting happened below the floor of her mind, like a mouse in a wall.

Forty steps to the fountain square. Sixty to the cooper's yard. Turn left at the shrine with the chipped saint, cross the lane where the cobbles were loose—she could find that spot blind, in ice, in fog—and then the widow's house with the blue shutters, and three knocks, pause, two knocks.

The widow answered tonight herself, which was unusual; usually it was a man Odile pretended not to recognize. The widow was small and round and smelled of camphor, and she took the package with hands steadier than her age should have allowed.

"Anything back?" Odile asked.

"Nothing tonight." The widow studied her a moment. "You're the binder's girl."

"I'm the binder's assistant."

"Mm." The widow's eyes were sharp as a bone folder themselves. "You know, in the old country my mother carried messages too. In bread. She never read a word of them. Lived to be ninety-four." A pause. "Her sister read one. Shot at forty-one."

"That's a grim bedtime story, madam."

"I'm not a bedtime sort of woman. Go home straight, girl. Don't take the bridge road."

"Why not?"

"Because I said so, and I'm old enough to be obeyed." The door closed, gently, decisively.

Odile stood a moment in the cold, breath smoking, and considered. The bridge road was the fastest way home. It was also, she realized, the road a person would watch if that person wanted to see who came and went from the chancery quarter—and lately there had been soldiers on the roads east of town, refugees coming west through it, and rumors stacking up like uncollected snow. She took the long way, along the river path, and told herself the widow was superstitious, and counted steps the whole way anyway.

* *

Three nights later the second package came, and this time the route led her past the fish smokehouse, and this time there was a packet waiting for return delivery, and this time—it being Thursday, and Thursdays being the day she also collected paste ingredients from the chandler—her errands braided together into one long loop through the sleeping town, ending near midnight at the top of the hill where she could see all of Vellbruck laid out below her: the roofs furred with frost, the clock tower black against the stars, the bridge crossing the gorge in a single pale arc, lamplit at both ends and dark in the middle, the way it had been her whole life.

The republic's flag still hung from the chancery. Somebody climbed up every few days to make sure it did. Odile wondered idly, leaning on the cold rail, who that somebody was, and whether he ever paused at the top of the pole and looked at the eastern roads, where the horizon had lately taken on at night a faint wrong glow that might have been furnaces, or army encampments, or nothing at all. Probably it was nothing. Probably it was always going to be nothing, right up until the morning it wasn't.

She memorized routes, she reminded herself, descending the hill. Not contents. She knew the smokehouse, the widow's shutters, the reading society's back door, the six safe ways between them. She did not know what was in the packages. She did not know names. She was a pair of hands and a forgettable face, and when the war came—if it came—it would pass over her the way weather passes over a stone, and she would still be at her bench the next day, folding signatures, making that small honest crack.

This was what she told herself, and she told it to herself so regularly that it had begun to sound like grace at meals: automatic, comforting, and addressed to nobody.

What she did not count, walking home along the frozen river path with her collar up, was that hands remember shapes. That she could identify Fenn's packages by weight and string-tension alone. That she knew, without ever having been told, which addresses expected sealed diplomatic correspondence and which expected money, purely from how the string was tied—and that a mind which catalogued such things without permission was not, whatever she called it, ignorant. It was merely patient. It was a ledger that hadn't been opened yet.

She let herself in the back door of the bindery at half past midnight. The banked stove breathed its low warmth into the dark. Ambrose's chair sat empty by the window—he'd gone to bed early, complaining of his chest, which he did now most nights—and the shop lay around her in familiar shadow: presses, type cases, rolls of leather sleeping on their shelves like animals.

On her workbench, where she had left nothing, sat a package.

Brown paper. Water-stained—one whole corner of it darkened and warped, the string retied in a knot that was not Fenn's careful hitch but a stranger's clumsy bow. Whoever had brought it had come late, wet, and in a hurry, and had known exactly which bench was hers.

Odile stood very still in the doorway, her heart ticking oddly, and looked at the stain spreading across the paper like a map of some drowned country.

Then, because the paper was already damaged and the seal beneath it was broken anyway—she told herself this later, and told herself she believed it—she picked the package up. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed, she thought, considerably less than it should have.

Inside the ruined wrapping, the water had gotten into the folio itself. Ink had run at the edges of the first page, letters bleeding into grey ghosts of themselves—but not all of them. Not the middle of the page. She bent toward the banked stove's glow, and the light moved, and three-quarters down the sheet, in the chancery's meticulous hand, dry and untouched and perfectly legible, was a name she knew.

Not her own name.

Her sister's.
