## 2. What the River Carries

The pouch came up from the landing on a Tuesday, which Odile would remember later, because Tuesdays were the days she boiled paste and the whole bindery smelled of flour and misery, and because it was the last ordinary Tuesday she would ever have.

It arrived the way all her pouches arrived: wrapped in oilcloth, tied with the flat gray cord that meant *chancery business, ask nothing*, and left on the bench beside the nipping press by whoever had carried it up the hill before her. She was not supposed to open them. That was the arrangement, and it was an arrangement she had defended to herself for three years with the seriousness of a woman defending a religion. Odile Nystrom carried things. She did not read them. She sewed signatures in the daylight hours and ferried sealed pouches along the river roads in the dark ones, and if she knew the schedules by heart—Tuesdays downriver, Fridays across the bridge, the long wet loop through the marsh villages when the moon was generous—that was only route-knowledge, the same knowledge any carrier has, the same as knowing where the ruts were bad on the Millen Road. Routes were not contents. A person could know every road in the province and still be ignorant.

She told herself this while she untied the cord, because the oilcloth was wrong.

It was wrong in a way only a binder's hands would notice. The cloth was soaked through at one corner, stiff with dried river water, and the wax seal beneath had gone soft and half-melted, so that instead of breaking cleanly it tore like old skin, and the flap of the inner wallet gaped open before she had properly decided whether to look or not.

She looked.

Inside lay perhaps twenty sheets of thin chancery paper, written close in the small upright hand the clerks used for anything that mattered, and the water had gotten in at the corner and bloomed there, feathering the ink outward until the letters at the edge of each page dissolved into little gray ferns. She saw dates. She saw sums—money moved somewhere, a good deal of it, in the careful code of amounts that meant *payments to persons unnamed*. And at the top of the third sheet, where the water had been cruelest, one line stood out darker than the rest, the ink having run *toward* her finger rather than away, as though the page wanted to be read—

and the line began with a word she knew better than her own name, because she had watched her sister write it a hundred times at the kitchen table, tongue between her teeth, back when Marta signed everything twice just to be sure:

*Marta.*

Not *Marta Nystrom*. The surname after it was another one—an alias, though Odile didn't have the word for it yet, only the cold sliding feeling of seeing her sister wearing someone else's name like a borrowed coat. *Marta Kesselring.* Dead eleven months, buried under the alder tree behind the church at Sennholt, and here she was, flowing out of a stranger's ledger in wet gray ink, active, accounted, *paid*.

Odile set the sheets down on the bench very carefully, the way you set down something that might be asleep.

Then she went and stood at the glue kettle for a while, stirring paste that did not need stirring, and let the steam get into her eyes so that if anyone asked, and no one would, she could say the redness was the flour.

* * *

"You opened it."

Ambrose said it without looking up. He was at his bench working a spine with the backing hammer, tap-tap-tap, the rhythm he'd kept for fifty years and would keep, Odile privately believed, for some time after he died, out of sheer habit.

"The seal was ruined," she said. "Water damage. I thought—" She stopped. She was good at stopping sentences; it was most of what she'd learned in three years of courier work. "I thought it might be damaged past reading. It isn't."

"No." He set the hammer down. "And now you've read it."

"I read a name."

"Names are the whole cargo, child. Everything else is wrapping."

He came around the bench slowly. Ambrose Fenn was seventy-four and shaped like the question mark at the end of a long sentence, and he moved these days the way old bridges move, with creaks that might mean nothing and might mean everything. He took the wallet from the bench but didn't look inside. He held it closed in both hands, as if warming it.

"Which name?" he asked, and his voice was gentle in the particular way that meant he already knew.

Odile looked at the kettle. At the presses. At the window, where the light was doing its gray December best. Anywhere but at him.

"Marta," she said finally. "There's an entry. Marta Kesselring. Payments, quarterly, going back—" Her voice did something she hadn't authorized. She pressed her lips together and waited it out. "Going back two years. She died in March, Master. The payments stop in April. Someone wrote her down and someone paid her and nobody—" 

"Nobody told you," Ambrose finished.

"She told me she mended lace for the widow at Brenn." Odile laughed, once, a short ugly sound she didn't recognize as hers. "Lace. I used to tease her about it. Eleven months dead and I'm finding out she was—" She gestured at the wallet, at the whole invisible architecture it implied, the network of names and quarters and quiet money that she herself served every week without ever letting the word *network* form in her mouth. "Whatever she was."

Ambrose put the wallet down and took her by both shoulders. His hands were spotted and strong and they smelled, permanently, of hide glue and beeswax, and being held by them was the closest thing to being held by her father that remained anywhere in the world.

"Listen to me," he said. "I've carried secrets since before your mother was born, and I'll tell you the one thing I know about them. Carrying is a trade. You learn it. You wrap the weight well, you balance it, you walk. But *knowing*—knowing is not a weight at all. Knowing is a debt. And debts collect interest, Odile, day and night, whether you work or sleep, and they collect fastest off the young."

"Then un-know it for me."

"I can't. Neither can you. That's the cruelty of it." He released her shoulders and turned back toward the wallet, and for a moment his own hands shook—not the honest tremor of age, but something with feeling in it—and Odile noticed, and filed it away, because noticing things was the other half of her trade. "You'll carry this now alongside everything else. That's all anyone can do. Carry it square, keep it dry, and don't let it change your face."

"My face is forgettable."

"Your face is excellent," he agreed. "That's why I hired you. Now fetch the press boards. If the chancery wants this re-wrapped and re-sealed, we'll do it properly, and neither of us will read another word."

They worked in silence for an hour. He showed her how to flatten a water-buckled page under blotting paper and weight, patiently, over days—the same patience you'd give a book, because paper was paper—but Odile noticed he positioned himself so that his body blocked her view of the text, casually, the way a man shifts in front of a fire, and she understood that the lesson about not reading had been meant for both of them.

* * *

The refugees had begun coming through in earnest that same week.

Vellbruck sat on the river where the north road met the bridge road, and in the years since the war started elsewhere—always elsewhere, always a week's travel away, always *nearly over*—the town had grown expert at the small commerce of other people's disasters. The innkeeper sold soup at prices that climbed with each bad report from the front. The ferrymen ran a side business in forged transit papers of truly appalling quality. Old Mrs. Halloran rented out her barn to families who slept in rows like books shelved spine-to-spine, and in the morning Odile would pass them on her way to the bindery, whole households standing in the frost stamping their feet, children holding shoes.

They thickened through November like sediment in a flooded river. By the first week of December you couldn't cross the square without stepping around bundles, cart shafts, a man sharpening knives for coppers, twin sisters selling matches who were clearly not twins and whose matches were clearly not matches.

"They're saying the imperial army crossed the Lornet yesterday," the knife-sharpener told her, apropos of nothing, as she passed. "Whole division. They're saying there's nothing between it and us but mud."

"People say things," Odile said.

"They do. It's mostly rubbish. But they're saying it faster than usual." He spat and went back to his wheel, and the sparks flew orange in the gray air, and Odile walked on with her collar up and her face arranged into its usual expression of a woman thinking about bread.

* * *

The chancery clerk found her at the end of the week, on Friday, which was bridge-day, which meant she was already dressed for the dark and had her delivery satchel packed with the innocent top layer—a customer's Psalter, overdue, genuinely bound for a farmhouse across the water—and he came into the bindery smelling of ink and brandy and apologizing before he'd gotten the door fully shut.

"Fraulein Nystrom. Good. Good. Is—Master Fenn about?"

"Asleep. He works mornings now. His hands are bad in the evening." She kept her tone mild and her eyes down, the double courtesy she extended to everyone official. "Can I help you, Herr Quist?"

Herr Quist was maybe forty and looked sixty, the way chancery men did, worn smooth and gray by paperwork the way stones are worn by patient water. Tonight he looked worse than usual. Tonight he had the look of a man who had spent the day counting things he wished didn't exist.

"It's the pouches," he said. "The Tuesday pouch came up damaged—we know, we know, the ferryman reported it, and the replacement went out Thursday, all correct, all in order." He said *all in order* the way other men said prayers. "But there's one more run needed before the month turns. One more. Downriver, to the marsh stations. It can't go by the regular carrier because—" he waved a hand that contained an entire sentence he wasn't permitted to speak, "—because. And the others won't take it. Pell says his mother's ill. Lorenz has simply vanished, which Lorenz does. And you—" He looked at her with naked, miserable hope. "You never refuse."

"I never refuse," Odile agreed pleasantly, "because I'm never asked to do anything dangerous. That's the arrangement. I carry parcels. Books, mostly. Sometimes letters. If this run is the kind that makes grown carriers invent sick mothers, then I think you should find someone else, and I think you should do it quickly, and I think we should both pretend this conversation was about bookbinding."

"Fraulein—"

"Herr Quist." She softened it, because he was frightened, and frightened men deserved some kindness even when they were trying to drown you. "Why one more? Why now? If the roads are getting worse—"

"Because the archive has to disperse," he said, too fast, and then visibly wished the sentence unborn. He pressed his knuckles against his mouth. Behind him the glue kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere out in the square the match-sellers were calling their wares in voices too cheerful for December.

"What's coming, Herr Quist?" Odile asked quietly. "Tell me that much. Not contents. You taught me that much yourself—you used to lecture me about it, my first year. *Routes, not contents.* So tell me the route. What's on the road behind those people in the square?"

Quist looked at her for a long moment, and whatever he saw in her forgettable face made him decide she was safe, which was either the compliment of her life or the beginning of her ruin.

"Everything," he said simply. "Everything is on the road. And the army doesn't stop at towns like ours, Fraulein. It requisitions them. Buildings. Records." His eyes flicked, involuntary, toward the back wall of the bindery—the wall that adjoined, on its far side, the chancery archive room, where forty years of the republic's memory sat shelved and waiting. "Paper especially. Armies love paper. They take it, and they read it, and they act on it. So the less of ours there is when they arrive, the better—for everyone whose names appear anywhere in it. Do you understand me?"

"I understand that you want me to walk into the marshes in December carrying something that makes carriers vanish."

"I want you to walk into the marshes carrying *nothing*," Quist said, with sudden fierce precision. "That's the beauty of it. Empty hands, empty satchel. You'll come to the drop at Tarnow Mill, and you'll leave the satchel empty there, and you'll bring back—" he hesitated, "—you'll bring back a receipt. That's all. An hour's work at the mill, and then home."

"An empty satchel."

"Completely empty."

"And the danger?"

"The danger," Quist said, "is that the roads are full of soldiers now, and empty satchels interest them more than full ones. But you're a bookbinder, Fraulein. Binders travel for work. Nobody interrogates a girl carrying a Psalter to a farmhouse." He attempted a smile. It came out crooked and human. "Nobody ever has."

* * *

She told Ambrose that night, or rather she laid it out at the bench the way she laid out leather for cutting, piece by piece, and let him see the whole shape.

"Empty satchel," she said. "Downriver. Before the month turns. He's terrified, Master. He drank half a bottle standing in our doorway and his hands shook so badly he couldn't light his pipe."

"Ambrose was silent a while. Outside, the wind came off the river and rattled the loose shutter on the north wall, the one neither of them had fixed in three years, because fixing it would require admitting the building was falling down around their trade.

"There was a time," he said at last, "when I'd have told you to go. When the work mattered enough that I'd have sent my own daughter, God rest her, without a second thought." He rubbed his ruined right hand with his left, absently, the gesture of a man touching an old scar. "But my hands shake, Odile. And yours are the steadiest I've trained in thirty years. And there's a difference between courage and arithmetic, and I'm too old to confuse them anymore."

"So don't send me. Advise me. That's all I'm asking. You're the master; I'm the apprentice; the arrangement is that when I can't see the shape of a thing, you tell me what it's made of." She turned the Psalter over in her hands—her cover story, her harmless freight, its new endpapers still faintly sweet with paste. "What is this run made of, Master? Truly?"

Ambrose looked at her for a long time. In the lamplight his face had the quality of a map of somewhere mountainous—all ridges and weather, all of it earned.

"It's made of the fact," he said slowly, "that the republic is dying, and dying things scatter their papers, and somebody has to gather them in before the wolves arrive. And it's made of the fact that you are the best pair of legs and the best blank face in this district, and that Quist knows it, and that he wouldn't ask unless the alternative was losing something that cannot be replaced." He sighed. "And it's made, child, of the fact that you asked me anyway, when you had already decided. Which means you're your sister's kin after all, whatever name she wore."

Odile's throat tightened. She bent lower over the Psalter and turned a page that didn't need turning.

"I'll go Sunday night," she said. "After the market wagons clear the bridge road."

"Wear the brown cloak. Not the gray—the gray reads military at a distance." He was already moving, gathering thread and awl and the bone folder, the tools of a trade that had nothing to do with any of this and everything to do with all of it. "And Odile."

"Master?"

"When you come back—" He paused, and the lamplight caught the shake in his hands as he set the awl down, deliberately, precisely, point toward himself the way he'd taught her on her first day. "When you come back, we're going to talk about what happens to a bindery when an army takes a town. Because armies requisition buildings, and ours stands next to the most important room in Vellbruck, and I have been lying awake doing that arithmetic I told you about."

Outside, the wind rose off the river, and somewhere down in the square the match-sellers had gone quiet, and the clock tower began to strike nine, each stroke falling into the cold air like a stone into still water—one, two, three—counting down, though to what, no one in Vellbruck yet knew.
