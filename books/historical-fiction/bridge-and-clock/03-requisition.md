## 3. Requisition

The soldiers came on a Tuesday, which Odile thought was characteristic of them. Wars, in her experience of other people's wars, preferred Tuesdays. Sundays were for churches and funerals; Mondays were for recovering from both. Tuesday was the day nothing expected.

She was at the pressing bench when the first column came over the bridge, and she knew before she looked up, because Ambrose stopped mid-sentence. He had been lecturing her about the moral superiority of linen thread over silk—a lecture she had heard eleven times and could now recite herself—and then he simply wasn't talking anymore, which in eleven years had happened perhaps twice, once when the lightning struck the clock tower and once when a customer's dog died on the doorstep.

"Odile," he said. "Come look at this."

"I'm gluing."

"The glue will wait. The empire will not."

That was Ambrose's way of joking, which was to say it wasn't a joke at all, but you laughed anyway, because he was old and his hands shook and a man who had survived three governments deserved to make his own weather.

She wiped her fingers and went to the window. Below, in the square, the imperial troops were entering Vellbruck without a shot being fired anywhere, which was somehow worse than if there had been shots. There is a particular silence that falls on a town being taken quietly. It isn't fear exactly. It's more like the hush in the workshop when a book is opened for the first time after rebinding—everyone holding their breath to see if the spine holds.

The column crossed the bridge two abreast, gray coats, gray faces, breath steaming. Their boots made a sound Odile felt in her teeth, a sound like a very large book being closed. An officer rode at the front on a horse too fine for the weather, and behind him came the wagons, and behind the wagons came the paperwork—she could see it already in the way the sergeant at the rear carried a leather satchel against his chest like a hymnal.

"They don't look hungry," Ambrose observed.

"They look tired."

"All armies are tired by December. The question is what tired armies do." He adjusted his spectacles with the hand that shook least. "They requisition. It's in all the manuals. Chapter one: find the granaries. Chapter two: find the records. Chapter three—" he tapped his own chest, "—find somewhere warm to sleep."

Odile's stomach performed a small, professional calculation independent of her feelings. The bindery was warm. The bindery adjoined the chancery archive through a door that had been bricked over in '74 and reopened in '81 because the chancery clerk kept losing his key and Ambrose kept letting him through. The bindery had presses, benches, a stove, and two apprentices' cots in the loft that hadn't been slept in since Marta—

Since Marta.

"We should bank the fire," she said. "Make the place look cold."

"And freeze my glue pot? Girl, I am seventy-one years old. I have been requisitioned by a kingdom, a republic, and a flood. I know how this goes. They take the front room. You keep the back room. Everyone pretends the back room doesn't exist, and civilization limps along."

* * *

By evening it was official, which is to say it was written down.

A corporal arrived at the door with a clipboard—notepad, actually, but Odile thought of it as a clipboard, because the man held it the way a priest holds scripture—and read aloud from a form. The town square was placed under military administration. The chancery building was seized for the custody of government records pending transport. And the premises adjoining the chancery, being the bookbindery of A. Halloran & Apprentice, were designated as billets for one officer and twelve men, "in consideration of the premises' stoves, water access, and structural suitability."

"Structural suitability," Ambrose repeated afterward, in the tone he reserved for bad marbling. "Eleven years I've begged the town council to fix that roof. One afternoon of occupation and suddenly we're structurally suitable."

"You could appeal."

"To whom? The council's fled to Corvess. The mayor's fled to Corvess. Half the choir's fled to Corvess." He sighed and began covering the finished bindings with canvas, an act that protected them from nothing but made him feel better, and Odile understood this completely, because she was doing the same thing with her face.

The officer arrived at dusk. Captain Aldric Verren—that was on the paper too, everything was on the paper, the empire believed in paper the way the church believed in God—with a neat hand and a list of questions. How many rooms. How many presses. Was the stove shared or separate. Did the establishment employ anyone besides the master and the assistant. Where did the assistant sleep.

"The loft," Odile said, when Ambrose nodded at her to answer. She kept her eyes at the level of the captain's collar button, which was brass, and slightly tarnished, and gave her something to look at that was not a face.

"The loft has its own stair?"

"Yes, sir."

"Good. My men will take the workroom floor. You'll hear noise. Boots, mostly. Some singing, early, until I stop it." He said this not as a threat but as a weather report, and Odile, who had spent six years making herself unremarkable to men carrying pouches and four years making herself invisible to men carrying rifles, recognized the type immediately: a man who apologized to furniture he bumped into. Dangerous in a completely different way from the usual kind.

He signed the requisition in duplicate, left one copy pinned to the door with a tack, thanked Ambrose for the tea he had not been offered, and went back out into the snow to count things.

Ambrose waited until the door shut. Then he said, quietly, "Well."

"Well," said Odile.

"He asked where you sleep."

"He asked about the premises."

"He asked where *you* sleep, Odile." The old man took off his spectacles and polished them on his apron, which needed polishing far less than the situation did. "I've watched officers ask questions for fifty years. That one wasn't reading his list. He'd memorized it. Men memorize lists when the answers matter to them."

Odile said nothing, and went upstairs, and counted.

This was her discipline, the one thing Marta had taught her that she had never told anyone she was taught: when fear arrives, count. Not sheep, not prayers. Exits. The loft window opened onto the chancery roof—one exit, shallow pitch, easy run, tiles slick with ice so mind the third course. The loft ladder—no, they'd post a man at its foot by tomorrow, count it gone. The press-room window stuck in winter but the frame was rotten on the left side—one good shove, one exit, loud. The cellar coal chute—too narrow for her shoulders since she was sixteen, but a boy could fit, remember that, remember it for someone. Four exits, minus one, plus a roof. Three and a half exits. In Vellbruck in December, under occupation, that was wealth.

She lay on the cot under Marta's old quilt and listened to the boots below, twelve pairs of them arranging themselves around her presses, her bench, the kettle where Ambrose cooked the glue. They were polite about it, which was the strangest part. She heard a soldier say *mind the papers, mind the papers, captain says nothing gets touched*, and another one laugh and say something about how he'd rather guard paper than dig latrines in frozen ground, and a third voice, young, saying nothing at all, just humming, badly, some marching song slowed down to almost a lullaby.

Dread, she decided, smelling the strangers' tobacco seeping up through the floorboards, arriving not as violence but as inventory. As billeting forms. As a corporal measuring the distance between her cot and the nearest window with a piece of string, for reasons the form would explain and she never would.

* * *

The next three days established the new arithmetic of the house.

Twelve soldiers ate at the big bench where editions were trimmed; they moved the lying press to make room and put a cloth over it, someone had told them the machine was valuable, which it was, though not in the way they imagined. The tall soldier with the burned eyebrow claimed the corner by the stove and read confiscated novels there every evening, holding them upside down sometimes when he dozed, and Odile, passing with glue buckets, corrected none of them. The chancery clerks came and went through the connecting door with armloads of registers, sealing them into crates with nails driven by soldiers who hit their thumbs with the regularity of clockwork. The archive, room by room, shelf by shelf, was becoming cargo.

And the captain came each morning at nine, precisely, drank precisely one cup of the tea Ambrose insisted on making—it seemed to puzzle him, and he accepted it the way one accepts weather—and asked his harmless questions.

How long had the shop stood here. Since before the annexation? Before even that? And the master had bound for the chancery all those years, must have seen every document in the republic pass under his knife. Ambrose answered these with the cheerful vagueness of the genuinely old, offering opinions about paper stock that were true and useless, and Verren wrote down the useless truths in his notebook, and Odile stood at the bench sewing signatures and lowered her eyes and listened to everything.

Because that was the other half of the discipline, the half under the counting. When fear arrives, count—but while counting, listen. Boots on the stairs meant nothing. Two sets of boots meant a patrol. The captain's step she learned in a single morning: measured, unhurried, with the faint hesitation at the threshold that a man has when he enters a place he half-remembers. She noticed that. She filed it away in the drawer where she kept things she told herself she didn't know.

On the fourth morning the crate for the chancery ledger came down into the workroom to be fitted with straw, because it was the largest crate and the bindery had the largest table, and Odile, sewing at her bench with her eyes down, watched over the top of her needle as the soldiers stenciled it.

ARCHIVE — CHANCERY OF VELLBRUCK
FOR TRANSPORT — PRIORITY
CROSSING: NEW YEAR'S EVE, 1899–1900

New Year's Eve. Six weeks. The whole memory of the republic, nailed into pine, scheduled to cross the bridge like everyone else's luggage.

Ambrose looked at the stencil for a long moment. Then he looked at Odile. Then he looked at the kettle, briefly, the way a man looks at a tool he hasn't used in years and hopes he still remembers how.

"More tea, Captain?" Ambrose called across the room, bright as a Sunday bell. "It's a cold job, guarding paper."

Verren, at the table, glanced up from his notebook. For just a moment his eyes passed over Odile—over her bent head, her busy hands, her forgettable face—and something in the glance paused, the way a reader pauses at a word he's sure he's seen before in another book entirely.

"No thank you, Master Halloran," he said. "I drink tea slowly. I find it keeps me in a room longer than I mean to be."

Odile sewed her signature, pulled the thread tight, and counted again: three and a half exits, six weeks, one kettle, one clock tower, one bridge.

She did not yet know why the captain's pause mattered. She only knew, with the certainty of a woman who had carried enough sealed things to recognize the weight of one, that something in this house had just begun to be carried—and that this time, she might be the seal.
