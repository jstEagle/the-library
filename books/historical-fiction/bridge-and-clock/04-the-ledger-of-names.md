## 4. The Ledger of Names

Captain Aldric Verren had been in Vellbruck for six days before he was permitted to open a single drawer.

The delay was not the town's fault, strictly speaking. The requisition order had come down from the provincial command in triplicate, and triplicate, Verren had learned over eleven years of service, was the empire's true native language—more so than any tongue spoken in its provinces. The first copy named the chancery as a target of seizure. The second authorized an inventory. The third, arriving two days late and smelling faintly of someone's lunch, authorized him to *act* on the inventory, which meant that until all three documents were filed together in his field desk, the entire archive of the Republic of Vellbruck sat locked behind a green door while he drank tea in the square and pretended this was strategy rather than bureaucracy.

"You could break it," said Lieutenant Sasse, who was twenty-three and believed that most problems yielded to enthusiasm.

"I could," Verren agreed. "And then I would spend a month explaining to a colonel why I lacked form 11-C."

"What is form 11-C?"

"Exactly."

Sasse went away shaking his head, and Verren returned to the window of the commandeered customs house and looked out at the town he had not seen in nineteen years.

It was smaller than memory made it. Most places were; memory was an inveterate embroiderer. But the clock tower still stood at the river end of the bridge, black with age, and the bridge itself still ran its long gray back across the gorge like something patient and half-asleep. He remembered crossing it as a boy with herring barrels on a cart, his father swearing at the mule, the whole family bound for market on the far bank. He remembered the sound the planks made under wheels—a deep wooden drumbeat you felt in your teeth.

He did not remember the bindery. That troubled him later, though at the time he noticed nothing at all, which was its own kind of failure, and he would have cause to count it among many.

* * *

On the seventh day the third document arrived, and Verren unlocked the green door of the chancery archive with a key that turned more smoothly than he expected, as if the lock had been oiled recently, as if someone had expected him.

Inside: order. That was his first impression, and it stayed with him longer than anything else he found there. He had entered occupied archives before—in Kesselstadt, in Marrow—and found chaos dressed up as administration, papers stuffed into hatboxes, tax rolls used to wedge windows. The republicans of Vellbruck had done none of this. Every shelf was labeled. Every box was numbered in a small, disciplined hand. Folders hung from their spines like well-fed books, and the air smelled not of mildew but of clean paper and, faintly, of glue.

"They kept better records than we do," said Sergeant Ilma, who had come along to carry things and had appointed himself commentator instead.

"Revolutions are paperwork," Verren said. "The fighting is just what happens when the filing fails."

He began at shelf one and worked methodically, because method was the only prayer he had that anyone above him would ever read what he produced. Treaties, mostly dead. Customs agreements with neighbors who no longer existed as neighbors. A correspondence file concerning drainage on the north road that he read twice out of sheer relief at its dullness. The republic had been, by all appearances, governed by people who genuinely believed that if they wrote everything down correctly, the world would behave.

There was a poignancy in that which Verren did not examine closely. It was easier to note it, file it mentally under *local character*, and move on.

He found the ledger on the fourth afternoon.

* * *

It was not hidden. That was the thing he would return to, later, when sleep declined to come: it was not hidden at all. It sat on a shelf at eye level in a section labeled, in the same tidy hand as everything else, *NETWORK — ACTIVE*, between a box of postal receipts and a folio of grain tariffs. As if it were simply another instrument of government. As if names were only ever names.

It was a thick volume, bound in dark blue goatskin—not the republic's official binding, which was buff calfskin with a stamped seal, but something older, hand-tooled, the work of a craftsman rather than a ministry. The spine bore no title. When he opened it, the hinges gave a soft sigh, the sound of a book that was opened often and cared for always.

The pages inside were ruled in columns. Name, district, function, status. Couriers marked with a small wheel symbol. Safehouses with a roofline. Sympathizers—clerks, ferrymen, a physician, a woman who apparently owned a bakery and had opinions about artillery movements—with a simple dot. Marginal ticks in three colors of ink: black for dormant, red for active, and a green so faded it took him a page to realize it must mean *deceased*. Each entry cross-referenced others by number, so that following one courier through the ledger was like following thread through cloth—you pulled, and a whole garment came with it.

Verren counted. He was good at counting; it was the least glamorous of his skills and the one that had advanced him furthest. Four hundred and eleven entries. Perhaps ninety still active. Names, addresses, meeting rhythms, drop points coded as dates and bridge tolls. Somewhere in those columns was every person in Vellbruck who had spent the last decade passing paper against the empire, written down in the republic's own immaculate hand, preserved for him like a gift.

He sat down on the step stool provided—he noted, distantly, that they had even left a stool—and read for two hours without moving.

"Captain?" Ilma, from the doorway. "Begging pardon. You've gone a funny color."

"It's the dust," Verren said, and closed the ledger, and heard again that soft sigh of the hinges, and felt something in his chest arrange itself uncomfortably, like a coat buttoned wrong from the start.

Four hundred and eleven names. He knew what happened to four hundred and eleven names once they traveled east to provincial command. They became quotas. They became a list pinned to a courthouse door and men with rope who worked quickly because the cold made them impatient. He had watched it in Marrow. He had signed the transport order himself, because the order was correct in every particular, and correctness had been drilled into him since he was fourteen years old as the highest form of morality available to a soldier.

Order is mercy, he told himself, in the formula he kept for such moments. Order is mercy. Without the ledger loose in the world, both sides go on guessing, and guessing is where the cruelty lives. One list, secured, means no house-to-house searches, no denunciations traded for bread, no neighbor selling neighbor. Take the names off the street and the war here is already over, truly over, and nobody else has to hang.

It was a good argument. He had constructed it himself and he knew its load-bearing walls intimately. It held weight the way a bridge holds weight—impressively, right up until you looked at what was underneath.

* * *

That evening he dined on requisitioned pork with Sasse and Ilma in the customs house, and the conversation, as it always did among junior officers far from the front, circled toward speculation about promotion.

"They'll want it in the capital by spring," Sasse said. "The whole archive. There'll be commendations. Trials, probably. Big ones."

"There won't be trials if the network's already broken," Ilma said. "You don't try ghosts."

"They're not ghosts yet." Sasse grinned. "But they will be. Captain, you should see your face. You look like a man eating soup and finding a key at the bottom of the bowl."

Verren looked down at his soup. "I was thinking about the bridge."

"The bridge?"

"The troop crossing. New Year's Eve. The engineers say the span needs reinforcement before we can move a full column across—the ice load, the age of the timbers. I inspected it today. It's older than my father. If the century turns and we haven't moved the archive east by then—" He shrugged. "Paper keeps. Men don't. But the schedule is the schedule."

"You worry too much," Sasse said comfortably. "We'll have the crate across by midnight and toast the new century on the far bank. First act of the empire's next hundred years: carrying some clerk's homework to the capital."

Ilma laughed. Verren smiled the way he'd been taught to smile at mess dinners, and thought: *homework.* Four hundred and eleven names, and the boy called it homework, and the terrible thing was not that Sasse was cruel but that he wasn't. He was simply a man who had never had to read a ledger slowly, entry by entry, and feel each name arrive in his chest with its own small weight, the way stones settle into a sack.

After dinner Verren walked the square alone. The curfew bell had rung; the town was shuttered and quiet, snow beginning again, fine as flour. Lamplight leaked gold through the shutters of a building adjoining the chancery—some workshop, a tradesman's place, the smell of paste and leather drifting faintly even in the cold. A light burned late there. Working past curfew, or unable to stop working, which amounted to the same thing in a town that had lost its government and gained an army in the space of a week.

He stood in the falling snow and let himself think the thought he had been declining all day.

He could lose the ledger.

Not destroy it—he was not built for destruction, and besides, the empire would simply re-collect the names through informers, at triple the cost in blood. But mislay it. Report water damage. A crate dropped from a cart on the mountain road; the river taking the rest. The network would survive scattered, blind, harmless. The province would stay quiet. No lists on courthouse doors. No rope in the cold.

And then the counter-thought arrived, punctual as a bugle: *and if it surfaces anyway—if one page survives, one name, and it traces back to Captain Aldric Verren, who filed a report of accidental loss that any competent auditor would pull apart in an afternoon—then it is not one man who hangs. It is the captain first, and then, as a matter of course and example, everyone the captain has ever spoken to kindly.*

He had seen that arithmetic performed too, in Marrow. It was always performed correctly.

Order is mercy, he told himself again, and the words felt thinner tonight, like a coin rubbed smooth by too many hands. He told himself the alternative reading—that mercy was sometimes disorder, that a well-kept list was just violence with good posture—but the thought frightened him, and he was a man who had been rewarded his whole life for not being frightened, and so he put it away.

Tomorrow he would draft the transport order. Crate reinforced, escort doubled, crossing scheduled for the turn of the century, when the whole empire would be looking backward and forward at once and no one would watch a cart crossing a bridge. He would write *the archive constitutes the complete pacification of the region* in his careful officer's hand, and every word would be true, and he would sign it, and the signature would look exactly like all his other signatures.

He paused at the corner of the square. The workshop light had gone out. In the dark he could just make out the painted sign above its door, flaking gilt on old wood: a binder's mark, a bridge and a wheel.

For no reason he could have given in a report, Aldric Verren stood in the snow a moment longer, looking at a door he did not remember, feeling that he had been expected there.

Then he turned back toward the customs house, and behind him Vellbruck breathed under its snow, four hundred and eleven names deep.
