## 5. Ink Under the Fingernails

The soldiers snored.

That was the first thing Odile learned about occupation: it was mostly noise. Boots on the stairs at all hours, the clatter of mess tins, a harmonica played badly by someone who would not be discouraged—and beneath it all, from the room that had been the map-flat until three days ago, the deep, untroubled breathing of six men asleep among other people's secrets.

She lay in her cot in the bindery's back alcove and counted the breaths through the wall. Six. The same as last night. She had begun to think of them the way she thought of the clock tower—fixed features of the world, indifferent to her, ticking away at something that had nothing to do with her.

*Nothing to do with me.* She turned the phrase over the way she'd turn a folio looking for a watermark, and found it thin.

The archive room—the chancery's overflow vault, which the army had claimed along with everything else—shared a wall with the workshop. On the other side of that wall, stacked in crates not yet nailed shut, lay the republic's paper memory: manifests, correspondence, requisition records, and somewhere among it, she now allowed herself to admit, the ledger. *The* ledger. Ambrose had never said its name aloud; he only ever said *the book*, in the tone a man uses for a debt he cannot pay. But she had carried pouches for four years, and couriers know what they carry even when they don't read it. A ledger of names travels differently than a ledger of flour prices. It sits differently in the satchel. Heavier, though the scale would disagree.

And it was sleeping twelve feet from her head, guarded by men who snored.

Odile got up, dressed without lighting the lamp—her hands knew the buttons—and went into the workshop, because sleep was not going to have her and neither was anything else tonight. She banked the stove coals and sat at the bench with a customer's prayer book that wanted new corners, and worked, and did not think about the wall.

She was lying to herself again, of course. It was becoming a specialty.

* *

The trouble with being invisible, Odile reflected over breakfast two mornings later, was that nobody bothered to keep things from you.

The soldiers billeted in the bindery had adopted her the way garrisons adopt cats. They called her *the mute one*, having decided within an hour that she couldn't talk, because she didn't, and they spoke freely around her as one does around furniture. Corporal Hasek, round and cheerful, told her his mother kept goats. Private Lommel showed her a photograph of a girl in a town whose name Odile made herself forget. And this morning, over the army's terrible coffee, Hasek complained about inventory duty.

"Twelve crates so far and the clerk wants *lists*, cross-referenced against their own confounded records. Their clerks wrote neater than ours, if you can believe it." He shook his head with genuine grievance. "There's a register in there must have every soul in the province in it. Couriers, postmasters, innkeepers—half the town, I shouldn't wonder. Old fellow who runs the ferry's in it twice."

The prayer book corner slipped under Odile's blade. She made the cut anyway, steady, and only then noticed her hand had gone cold.

"Every soul," she said. Her voice, unused for days, came out rusty. Hasek looked delighted—the mute one spoke!

"Well, everyone worth recording. Why d'you think they kept such books?" He drained his coffee and grimaced at it fondly. "A empire runs on knowing who's who. That's half the army's work right there, the knowing."

He went off whistling to his inventory, and Odile sat very still among the shavings, doing arithmetic she did not want to do.

Every courier. Every sympathizer. Every safehouse. The network that had carried her sister's letters, funded her sister's passage, buried her sister's name—that network, written down in the republic's own tidy hand, crated on the far side of a wall she could touch with her knuckles. And if every courier was in it, then so was the courier who had run Marta's routes after Marta died. So was the girl who had inherited a dead woman's alias because a live woman's name would have put a rope around her master's neck.

They had never recorded Odile Nystrom. They had recorded *Frau Anselma Voss*, and Odile had answered to it in dark doorways for four years, and somewhere in that crate of paper, Anselma Voss was still alive, still active, still marked in whatever way the chancery had marked its living assets—and Anselma Voss was her, and she had just learned it from a corporal complaining about coffee.

She put down the knife. Her fingers were ink-stained to the second knuckle; they always were. She looked at them as if they belonged to someone else, someone who had made a career of carrying things without opening them, and she understood, finally and completely, what Ambrose had tried to warn her of on the night the water-damaged pouch arrived.

Knowing was heavier than carrying. Knowing could not be handed off at a doorway and forgotten by morning.

* *

Ambrose was in the cellar, which was where he went now when the shaking in his hands embarrassed him. He sat on the paper chest—the good rag stock, imported, hoarded against some future that had probably just expired—and he was sorting type sorts by feel with his eyes shut, an old trick, except that twice he dropped one and had to grope for it on the flagstones, and the third time he swore, quietly, in the trade dialect of apprentices.

"You dropped an em quad in 1834 and blamed me," Odile said from the stairs.

"I stand by it. You were a clumsy child." He didn't open his eyes. "You've been standing there forty seconds deciding whether to speak. In my day apprentices decided faster."

"The ledger has my name in it."

His hands stopped. For a long moment the cellar was quiet enough to hear the river through the foundation stones. Then he said, "Anselma?"

"Anselma."

"Yes." He opened his eyes. They were clear this morning, which meant the pain was manageable, which meant this was as good as any hour for catastrophe. "Yes, it does."

"How long have you known?"

"Since before you did. Since I helped write it." He said it plainly, the way he stated prices. "When Marta died we needed the alias to stay live—the routes she ran were still running, and a route with no courier is a road with no bridge. So I gave them Anselma Voss, deceased, survived by a colleague. And then the colleague needed papers of her own, and so on, and the book grew the way books do." He rubbed his face with a hand that trembled only slightly. "I'm sorry, child. I built your invisibility out of the same pages that will hang us."

Odile came down the rest of the stairs and sat beside him on the paper chest. It was, she thought absurdly, the first time in years they had simply sat together, master and apprentice, without a deadline between them.

"They're going to ship it across the bridge," she said. "Hasek says the crate seals when inventory finishes. Then it goes wherever empires send their evidence, and every name in it gets read by people paid to act on names."

"I know."

"And we can't stop it. There are thirty soldiers in the square and the war's over and we're a bindery." She heard her own voice climb and forced it down. "We're a bindery, Ambrose. We make books. We don't unmake them."

Ambrose was quiet for a while. Then he stood—slowly, using the shelf—and crossed to the corner where he kept his private stock, and came back with something wrapped in oilcloth that she had never seen him touch in nine years.

"We make books," he agreed. "So let's consider what that means."

He unwrapped it on the paper chest. Inside lay a ledger—not *the* ledger but one like it, same format, same foolscap proportion, the chancery's standard issue from some earlier decade. The chancery had ordered its blank registers from this bindery for forty years. Of course they had. Where else in Vellbruck?

"Paper," Ambrose said, touching it. "We have matching stock in the cellar—same mill, same year, bought in the same lot; I always over-ordered because their clerk always under-counted. Thread—" he lifted a spool from the bench above, the linen thread in the particular gray-green the chancery specified, because Ambrose had dyed it himself for decades and knew the formula in his bones. "Sewing pattern. Two-on, raised bands, kettle stitches at head and tail. Marginal ticks in iron-gall mixed with their particular sand—chancery sand, they dusted everything, God knows why. The seal." His finger rested on the blind-stamped device at the foot of the spine. "Their stamp is in the cellar. It has been in the cellar since the annexation, because I am a sentimental old man who steals from clients."

Odile stared at the array of him: paper, thread, sand, stamp, forty years of exact knowledge laid out on a paper chest like instruments before surgery.

"No," she said.

"It would take three weeks done properly."

"There isn't three weeks. There may not be two."

"Then we'll do it improperly and pray the inspector is nearsighted." His eyes were bright and terrible. "It can be done, Odile. It can be done here, behind this wall, while they inventory on the other side of it. A false ledger, identical in every visible particular, sewn by the same hands that sewed the true ones. The crate closes. The empire carries a book full of nothing across the bridge, and every living name walks free, and no one ever knows."

"No *one ever knows*," Odile repeated, and heard how the words landed wrong, how they rang like a dropped weight. "You want me to forge a state document. You want me to counterfeit the republic itself. Ambrose, I carry things. That's what I do. I take a sealed pouch from one door and deliver it to another door and I do not open it and I do not ask and that is why I'm alive and Marta is—"

She stopped. The cellar held the sentence for her, finished it without her.

"That is why I'm alive," she said more quietly, "and it has to be enough. I only carry things. I've never made anything but book covers, and those are honest."

Ambrose sat down again beside her. When he spoke, it was gently, which was worse than if he'd argued.

"Child, listen to what you just said. You carry things. Very well. In eleven days, a cart will carry a ledger across that bridge, and inside its covers will be the name of every person who ever trusted this shop—including yours, including mine, including the ferryman and the widow Pechl and the boy who runs messages for the doctor. You will have carried it. That is what carrying *is*. You don't get to choose the contents by refusing to look at them. Refusing to look is a choice too. It's the choice the empire is counting on."

"The empire is counting on people not forging its ledgers, I should hope."

"The empire," said Ambrose, "is counting on honest people staying in their lanes. It builds whole bridges for that purpose." He picked up the old sample ledger and set it in her lap, and its weight was nothing, a few ounces of rag and thread, and it crushed the air out of her all the same. "I'm not asking you to decide tonight. I'm telling you it's possible, which I suspect you already believed, and that I'll teach you whatever you don't know, which is less than you fear. The rest is yours."

He gathered up his oilcloth and his stolen stamp and climbed the stairs, one step at a time, pausing on each like a man ascending a gallows politely. At the top he stopped.

"For what it's worth," he said, not turning around, "I refused too. Forty years ago, when they first asked this shop to serve the chancery. I said I only bind books, I don't write them." A dry sound that might once have been a laugh. "Look how far refusing carried me."

* *

Odile worked through the afternoon because her hands insisted on it and because hands that are busy cannot shake.

She rebacked the hymnal. She pared leather for the alderman's account book, which was overdue and would remain so. Around her the workshop performed its ordinary liturgy—the press, the plough, the smell of paste and calf-skin—and through the wall came the small sounds of the archive: Hasek calling numbers, the clerk's pencil, a crate lid fitted and nailed. Inventory proceeding. The future being packed in straw.

She tried to hold her line. *I only carry things.* It had been a good line. It had served for four years, through checkpoints and burned districts and the winter Marta died, when Odile had run her sister's routes with her sister's name in her throat and had gotten through on exactly this principle: *I am not deciding anything. I am delivering.*

But Ambrose had put his finger on the flaw, the little wormhole in the spine of her whole philosophy. Carrying was choosing. She had chosen every time she took a pouch without asking. She had chosen blindness, over and over, deliberately, the way you choose a binding style—for safety, for speed, because it hid the cords. And now the bill for all that tidy blindness had come due, itemized in a dead woman's alias, and paying it meant learning a craft she had sworn off: making, not carrying.

Because here was the truth she circled all afternoon and finally caught at dusk, standing at the shared wall with her palm flat against the plaster:

If the false ledger existed—if it lay finished and perfect in a crate on the far bank while the true one fed the stove—then Odile would not be a forger. She would be a courier. She would be running the longest route of her life: from this wall, across the bridge, into the next century, carrying every name in Vellbruck safely past the empire. The decoy wasn't a forgery. It was a pouch. It was a sealed envelope with the most important contents she had ever been trusted with, and the only difference from every other delivery was that this time she would know what she carried.

She had always known, really. That was the joke of it. That was the gentle, awful joke.

The stove had burned low. In its grate the day's waste lay in soft gray drifts—clippings, parings, a few spoiled leaves of practice stock—and Odile watched the ash settle and thought, without at first understanding the thought: *ash keeps.* Kettle ash, hearth ash, ash in a bindery hearth anyone might inspect. Ash could be testimony, if there were ever anyone left to testify to.

Her hands had gone still on the hymnal. Ink under the fingernails, in the creases, in the whorls—she examined them by stove-light like a fortune-teller reading her own palm, and the reading was short and clear.

"All right," she said aloud, to the empty workshop, to the wall, to the snoring men beyond it. "Teach me."

Behind her, from the doorway where he had evidently been standing for some time with two cups of tea going cold, Ambrose said, "First lesson: never announce a conspiracy within earshot of a wall."

"Then the walls of this shop have known worse."

"They have," he agreed, handing her a cup. "Sit down, Anselma. We begin with endpapers."

Outside, the clock struck eight, and across the square, in the archive room, a crate lid came down—only provisionally, only for the night—but Odile did not hear it. She was already counting signatures.
