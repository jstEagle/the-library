## 6. The Officer Who Drinks Tea

The soldiers had been in the bindery for six days before the captain came to tea.

Odile knew it was coming because Corporal Fess, who read confiscated novels behind the press of sermons and had thus far proven too incurious to be dangerous, mentioned it that morning while she stitched headbands. "Captain Verren takes his tea at four," he said, in the tone of a man reporting weather. "He'll want it in the front room. He doesn't like the smell of paste."

"Then he came to the wrong building," Odile said, and Fess laughed, which was a small victory, and victories were to be hoarded like candle-ends.

She spent the interval doing what she always did, which was nothing at all, conspicuously. She trimmed boards. She fed the glue kettle just enough coals to keep it warm but not enough to make it interesting. She swept. Sweeping was the best work in the world for a person who wished to be furniture; no one interrogates a broom.

At four o'clock precisely—she noted this, and filed it under *habits, his*—the captain came down the stairs from the requisitioned archive room and stood a moment in the doorway of the workshop as though he'd remembered it suddenly.

Odile did not look up. She looked at everything else. Tall, not particularly broad, in a gray-blue greatcoat brushed clean of snow. He held his cap in one hand, which was unusual. Occupying officers generally wore their caps indoors the way other men wore their opinions.

"You're the binder," he said. Not a question.

"Ambrose Kell is the master," Odile said, eyes on her thread. "I'm the assistant."

"And you're called—"

"Nystrom."

"Nystrom." He said it twice, the way a man tests a floorboard he suspects of being loose. "Fess tells me you do good work."

"Fess tells me you don't like the smell of paste," Odile said. "Between us, we've established very little."

The corporal, who had been pretending to inventory a crate of calfskin, made a noise that might have been a cough or might have been terror.

But the captain laughed—a short, surprised sound, quickly folded away—and said, "Tea, then. In the front room. If your master permits."

Ambrose permitted. Ambrose permitted everything these days from his chair by the stove, wrapped in two shawls and a temper, his hands too shaky now for a knife but perfectly steady when they wanted to be, which Odile had noticed was true only when he was lying about how bad the shaking was. He received the captain the way he received customers whose books he disapproved of: courteously, and with an air of already knowing how it would end.

The tea was poor. Everyone's tea was poor now; the blockade had seen to that years ago, and the war had finished what the blockade started. The captain drank it anyway, all of it, without sugar, without complaint, and this told Odile three things at once: he had been poor recently himself, he was not fastidious, and he intended to stay a while.

*Habits, his,* she thought. *Item one.*

* * *

Verren had known the bindery the moment he saw it, which was the problem, and he had known the girl the moment she spoke, which was worse.

Sixteen years ago this shop had been Kell & Voss, Binders, and a boy of eleven could stand at the window on a market day and watch through the glass while two girls—one tall, one small, unmistakably sisters despite the difference—played some game of their own invention among the paper stacks, moving books around a table like armies. The small one had always won. He knew this because the tall one would throw her hands up theatrically every time, and the small one would laugh, and the sound carried through winter glass better than church bells.

Marta. And the little one, whose name he never learned, whom everyone in town simply called *Marta's sister*, as if she were a feature of the landscape, a fountain or a bridge.

She wasn't little anymore. She was a woman sweeping a floor with the deliberate industry of someone who does not wish to be asked questions, and she had answered his first question with a joke and her eyes had never once come up to meet his, and Captain Aldric Verren, who had requisitioned three towns and parts of a fourth without losing a night's sleep, found that his hands wanted to fidget.

So he drank the bad tea and began, harmlessly, harmlessly, to talk.

"Your master has been here long?"

"Since before me."

"Before the annexation?"

"Longer than the bridge," Ambrose said, which was either an answer or a border, depending on how you took it.

Verren took it as an invitation, which was perhaps his first mistake. "I lived here. As a boy. Before my father's posting took us north." He watched them both. Neither face changed. "Kell & Voss. There were two daughters of the house across the lane—I used to watch them play at the window. Books as battlefields, I think it was."

Silence. The kettle ticked over its coals.

"The tall one threw her hands up," Verren said. "Every time. I used to set my watch by it."

And there—the smallest thing, so small he nearly missed it, and would have missed it in any officer in the empire but could not miss it here, because he had been watching for it: the sweep of the broom paused, half a second, the length of a swallowed breath. Then resumed.

"Marta Nystrom died four years ago," Ambrose said mildly, into his tea. "Winter fever. Her sister doesn't care to speak of it. If you'll forgive an old man's bluntness, Captain, we bind books here. We don't open them."

It was beautifully done. It was so beautifully done that Verren felt something almost like professional admiration, and beneath the admiration something colder assembling itself, nameless yet, patient.

"I beg your pardon," he said, and meant it, and rose, and thanked them for the tea, and at the door he turned and said, "The work on the bench by the window—is that a hymnal repair? Small octavo, brown calf?"

"Yes," Odile said.

"It belongs to Frau Ostler, I think. On Chandler's Row. She's lent it to half the street; half the street has learned the psalms whether they wished to or not." He settled his cap on. "Good evening, Mistress Nystrom."

In the stairwell he stopped and stood very still and let himself admit what he had been refusing to admit since the requisition papers crossed his desk: *that ledger is full of names, and this town is full of graves, and I am the man holding the shovel.*

* * *

"He knows," Odile said, the moment the stairs stopped creaking.

"He suspects," Ambrose said. "Knowing is expensive. Most men stop paying before they get there."

"Don't philosophize at me, Ambrose, I'm working." She was working; she was standing in the middle of the workshop reconstructing the entire conversation from memory the way she reconstructed routes—left at the chancery steps, pause at the window, cap in hand at four o'clock, off at five past five. "He remembers Marta. He watched us through the glass. He was the boy on market days—the one you used to chase off for smudging the pane."

"I remember no such thing."

"You chased him with a finishing iron."

"I chase everyone with a finishing iron. It builds character." But the old man's eyes were distant, looking at some middle distance where a boy stood at a winter window. "He drank the tea," he said finally. "All of it. Without sugar."

"I noticed."

"That's not a soldier of the occupation, girl. That's a homesick boy drinking bad tea in a room he used to love. Which is either our great fortune or our funeral, and I genuinely cannot tell you which."

Odile went back to the hymnal—Frau Ostler's hymnal, which now had hidden inside its resewn spine four false signatures of aged stock, and which would go home to Chandler's Row tomorrow smelling innocently of paste. Her hands did the work. Her mind did the arithmetic.

*Habits, his.* Tea at four. Cap in hand. No sugar. Upstairs to the archive by six, lights until ten. He walked the perimeter at dusk alone, unescorted, which no regulation required and no sensible officer did. He had returned Frau Ostler's borrowed book personally last week instead of sending a corporal, and had stood in the workshop doorway a moment too long, and had asked after the hymnal.

Harmless questions. Every single one harmless. That was what frightened her. A man who asks dangerous questions can be managed—you know what he wants. A man asking harmless ones wants something he hasn't admitted to himself yet, and there is no ledger anywhere with that entry in it.

* * *

On the ninth day Verren brought his own tin of tea.

It was, he told himself, simple efficiency. The bindery's tea was dust swept off a warehouse floor; a man could not think on it. He had the tin; he had the kettle's cooperation; he had, apparently, a standing appointment he had never once had to request, because at four o'clock the small table by the front window was cleared and a cup set out, and he sat, and the girl worked at the bench within earshot, and old Kell dozed by the stove in a way that Verren had begun to suspect was entirely fraudulent.

He asked harmless things. What papers came through the shop. Whether the trade was dead since the university closed. Whether the river flooded the cellars in spring. She answered each one in three words or fewer, flat as a ledger line, and then—this was the thing—he began to notice the pattern underneath the flatness, because patterns were his whole cursed profession.

She always knew how long he'd stay. Not approximately. Exactly. Twice he rose to leave and found his coat already brought from the peg by the door—not fetched eagerly, just *there*, as if the coat had concluded independently that he was going. Once, when the archive clerk shouted down the stairs for him at half past four, she said, "You'll be twenty minutes; the kettle will hold," without having looked at a clock.

She was mapping him. He was certain of it the way he was certain of forged signatures: not by any single feature but by the accumulation of small rights. And the absurd, humiliating truth was that he kept coming back anyway, day after day, bringing better tea into an occupied workshop like a tribute, sitting in the window where a boy had once stood outside it, performing the innocent officer for a woman performing the mute artisan, both of them aware, both of them aware that the other was aware, and neither of them willing to be the first to say a single true thing.

Except once.

Once—on the twelfth day, when the light was going blue over the rooftops and the kettle smelled of hide glue and the century felt old in its bones—she was sewing endpapers and humming. Three notes of something. She stopped herself instantly, the way one stops on ice hearing it crack.

"What is that?" he asked. "The song."

"It isn't a song. It's a counting rhythm for sewing stations." Flat. Ledger-flat.

"It's 'The Miller's Daughter.'"

The needle did not pause this time. She was better than that today. "Is it," she said.

"My mother sang it. Here. Before we left." He looked into his cup. "You'd have been a child. You wouldn't know it."

"No," Odile said. "I wouldn't."

And they sat there in the smell of paste and coal smoke, two liars of considerable talent, drinking tea.

* * *

That night Odile added to her private inventory, scratched mentally beside the others: *He hums when he reads inventory lists. Left-handed, mostly, except with keys. He stands where he can see both doors. He takes the archive stairs slowly, third step from the bottom, every time—as if listening.*

Because the third step from the bottom creaked directly beside the wall of the archive room. And against the far side of that wall, stacked in crates and waiting for the bridge, sat the ledger with every name in it, hers among them, wearing her dead sister's alias like a borrowed coat.

She lay awake doing curfew arithmetic and arrived at an answer she did not like at all: the officer who drinks tea was not gathering intelligence. A gatherer would have searched by now. A loyal man would have reported the hymnal, the pauses, the counting rhythm that was a song. He reported nothing. She had watched the couriers come and go from his office; she knew the rhythm of reports the way she knew the rhythm of sewing stations.

Whatever Captain Aldric Verren was doing at four o'clock every afternoon, he was not doing it for the empire.

Which meant he was doing it for himself. And a conflicted man, Ambrose had once said, over wine they could not afford, is more dangerous than a loyal one, because you cannot predict him—*but,* he had added, refilling her cup, *you can sometimes aim him.*

The next morning, Ambrose did not get up from his chair by the stove. His hands, when Odile took them, were steady as stones, and his eyes were clear, and he said, quietly, so that Fess in the next room would not hear:

"Girl. Sit down. It's time I taught you how a book lies."
