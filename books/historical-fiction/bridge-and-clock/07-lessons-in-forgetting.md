## 7. Lessons in Forgetting

Ambrose Halloran had spent fifty-two years teaching apprentices that a book was a lie agreed upon by its parts. The boards lied about being sturdy; the spine lied about having been born folded; the gilt lied about being gold. A binder's whole trade, he liked to say, was the honest manufacture of dishonesty, and the reader forgave all of it the moment the pages opened flat.

He had never expected to teach the corollary: that a book could lie so well it saved lives.

The lesson began, as his lessons always had, with tea. He insisted on it even now, even with soldiers snoring two rooms away and the kettle reserved—though Odile did not yet know for what—for darker purposes. He measured out the leaves with hands that would not hold still. That was the indignity of his seventies: not the pain, which he had expected, nor the fog of the mornings, but the shake. A binder's hands were his signature. His had signed half the fine bindings in Vellbruck, and now they trembled like a drunkard's over a spoon.

"You're staring," he said.

"I'm watching you spill the tea you complain we can't spare."

"Watching is how you learned everything else." He got the spoon to the pot without catastrophe and counted that a triumph no one would ever record. "Sit. We have perhaps an hour before Corporal What's-His-Name wakes from his nap and comes to be bored at us."

Odile sat across the workbench, and he saw her arrange herself the way she arranged herself for everyone—shoulders easy, face pleasant, eyes on nothing in particular. He had spent a year believing that blankness was her nature. Only lately had he understood it was her craft, practiced as diligently as any sewing frame technique, and that beneath it she was keeping accounts with the rigor of a banker.

"Lesson one," Ambrose said. "Fetch me the ledger."

Her jaw tightened. She had hidden the true ledger three days ago, wrapped in oilcloth behind the false backs of a shelf of sermons, and she fetched it now the way one fetches a snake—by the corner, at arm's length, glad of the wrapping.

"Good," he said. "You hate it. Hold on to that. Respect it, don't worship it. The moment a book becomes sacred, you stop seeing it, and then you can't copy it or kill it. It's paper, Odile. Paper is the most obedient material God made. It does whatever it's told, forever."

"It remembers everything it's told."

"That too." He unwrapped it himself, because his hands wanted the familiarity. The ledger was a thick quarto, sewn on five raised cords, bound in calf the color of weak coffee, blind-tooled with a double fillet border. The chancery's seal sat in gilt at the center of the front board, slightly off-center, because the young Ambrose—who had bound it nineteen years ago—had been distracted that day by a girl in the bookshop across the lane whom he'd intended to marry and never did. He had never told anyone the flaw was a love story. Perhaps tonight he would.

"Tell me what you see."

"A ledger," said Odile. "Nineteen years old. Calf binding, five cords, gilt seal. Sewn all-along, no recessed grooves—the chancery was cheap even when the republic still had money."

"Sewn *two-on*," Ambrose corrected, pleased despite himself. "Look at the spine. Two signatures per pass, except—" he turned the book to catch the lamplight— "here, and here, and here. Single passes. Three of them. Why?"

She leaned in. This was the part he loved, had always loved, the student's face going quiet and hungry. Even billeted soldiers, even the war, even the grave-shaped tiredness under her eyes could not spoil it.

"Repairs?" she guessed. "Signatures replaced?"

"No repairs. No replacements. Those single passes were sewn that way from birth, deliberately, at my own bench, by my own hand, on the chancery secretary's instruction." He tapped each one. "A two-on sewing pattern is ordinary. Nobody counts it. But if you *do* count it—and now you'll never stop—you find the irregularities fall at gatherings four, eleven, and seventeen."

"Gatherings of what?"

"Names." He let that sit. "Each gathering holds entries for one district of the network. Vellbruck town, the river villages, the mountain posts. The single-pass gatherings are the ones that mattered most. The live ones. The ones the chancery would want to find quickly without appearing to look. A clerk flipping through for a name would seem to flip at random. But the men who knew the code opened first at four, eleven, seventeen."

Odile was silent a moment. Then, dryly: "You hid a cipher in the *sewing*."

"Hid is generous. I put it where only another binder would think to look. That is the whole art of hiding anything, my girl—in plain sight, inside someone else's expertise. Soldiers read words. They do not read thread."

* * *

They worked until the candle had burned a thumb's width, and he taught her the marginal ticks next, which was a crueller lesson.

"The tick in the left margin—a short stroke, like a flyspeck—means the entry is live. The person named can still be reached, will answer a letter, will act if asked. The stroke through it means retired, dead, or burned. Now." He opened to an entry near the front and turned the book toward her. "Read this one."

She read. He watched her lips move around the name and saw the exact moment it landed—not this one, not yet, but close enough that her breath changed.

"This woman," Odile said slowly. "This address. That's the dressmaker's on Tanner's Row. Marta bought buttons there."

"Marta knew everyone on Tanner's Row," Ambrose said gently. "That was rather the point of Marta."

The tick beside the entry was crossed. Deceased. Odile's finger hovered over the little stroke-through, not quite touching the ink.

"And this one means she's finished. Out of it. Dead means dead in this book."

"Yes," said Ambrose, and the guilt arrived on schedule, punctual as pain. "Yes, mostly. Lesson two, then, and it's the hard one, so make tea of it if you need to. Ticks can lie."

Her finger came down flat on the page. "Explain that."

"An entry marked dead cannot be acted upon by an enemy who reads the book. It's the safest state a name can occupy. So sometimes, when a contact had to go quiet but the network still needed them—when the money had to keep moving, when letters had to keep flowing—we did not retire the entry. We killed it. On paper." He met her eyes. "Your sister is marked deceased because your sister died. But there are names in this book marked deceased who receive letters every month and cash them at the post office in their neighbors' names."

"How many?"

"I don't know precisely. Eleven or twelve. I stopped counting years ago because counting felt like something a coward does."

Odile pulled back from the table. He watched her assemble the thought, watched her arrive where he needed her to arrive: *if ticks can lie, then any copy of this book is a weapon aimed at living people, and only someone who knows the lies can disarm it.*

"So a perfect forgery," she said, "a perfect copy, tick for tick, would hang people."

"It would. Which is why the forgery we build won't be perfect in that way." He drew a scrap of wastepaper close and sketched with a shaking pencil: the margin, the ticks, and then new marks. "Every deceased tick gets copied as deceased. Every retired entry stays retired. But the live ones—" the pencil wavered and he steadied it against his knuckle— "the live ones get copied dead. All of them. The false ledger will tell the empire that the network died with the republic. Let them open it on the far bank and read a graveyard."

"And anyone still receiving letters?"

"Will have stopped, by then. I'll see to it. There are letters I can write—one last round—that will close every channel cleanly. Under a name I've used before."

There it was. He had meant to save that confession for later, for a braver night, and instead his mouth simply went ahead without him, the way old mouths do.

Odile caught it instantly—she missed nothing, that was her gift and her torment. "'A name you've used before,'" she repeated. "Whose name, Ambrose?"

* * *

He should have lied to her. Fifty-two years of discretion argued for the lie. But his hands shook, and his chest was a bellows with a split seam, and he had buried her sister's secret for six years already, and he found he was tired in a way sleep does not fix.

"Get the brandy," he said. "The bottle behind the false backs. If I'm to be hanged for honesty I'd rather be warm."

She fetched it and poured, and did not drink herself, and waited. That was the worst of Odile: she could wait like stone waits.

"When Marta died," he began, "the network didn't die with her, though half the chancery believed it had. She was the hub, girl. Everything came through her—money, routes, the mountain couriers, the man in the customs house. And the funding came from abroad, from people who had never met her and would not trust a stranger's word. When she stopped writing, the money would have stopped within the quarter. The safehouses would have emptied. People would have scattered into the snow with nothing."

"So someone kept writing as her."

"I kept writing as her." He said it plainly, because ornament would have been unbearable. "I had her hand—I'd taught her letterforms myself when she was fourteen, she practiced on my spoiled endpapers. I had her phrases, her little jokes, her way of signing off. I wrote to her contacts as her for two years. Then, when that grew dangerous, I invented reasons for the correspondence to pass to 'a colleague of the late Mademoiselle'—and I invented the colleague too. An alias built out of her alias, a room inside a locked room. The funding has moved through that fiction ever since. It pays for coal in three safehouses and a doctor in the mountain posts and the silence of a customs man whose name is also in this book."

Odile stood up. She walked to the window, where the shutter was closed against the curfew dark, and stood with her back to him, and he counted her breaths. Four. Five. Six.

"You wrote letters in my dead sister's name," she said, "and you didn't tell me."

"Yes."

"For six years."

"Yes."

"Why?" And then, before he could answer, with a bitterness he had earned: "No. Don't give me the noble version. Give me the true one."

"The true one," Ambrose said, "is that telling you made it real. As long as no one knew, the fiction stayed clean—a mechanism, nothing more. But you loved her, and I loved her, and to sit across from you at this bench, week after week, knowing I was wearing her voice like a coat—" his own voice betrayed him, and he was grateful for the shake, since it gave the pause an excuse— "I couldn't do it. It seemed kinder to steal from you than to grieve with you. That was vanity, not kindness. I've known it was vanity for six years. Old men are permitted to know these things late."

She turned around. Her eyes were wet and furious, and both were directed somewhere past him, at six years of letters she had never known existed, at a sister corresponded-with after death.

"Does the alias have a name?" she asked. "The colleague. The room inside the locked room."

"It does."

"Is it in the ledger?"

"It is. Marked deceased, like all my best lies."

"Then teach it to me last," Odile said. "When you teach me the rest. Because if the empire ever reads that name and pulls its thread, I want to be the one holding the other end."

Something unclenched in his chest that had been clenched since the troops marched in. Not forgiveness—she had not offered that, and he hadn't earned it. But enlistment. Conspiracy. The terrible, warming fact of not carrying it alone anymore.

"Then we begin properly tomorrow," he said. "Watermarks first. You'll learn to match the chancery stock by feel in the dark, and to age paper with smoke and tea until a colonel's clerk can't tell 1899 from 1889. Your hands are better than mine now, anyway." He lifted his trembling fingers, and made himself smile. "Fifty-two years of craft, and the sum of it is that I must pour everything I know through a girl who once glued her own sleeve to a bookbinder's table."

"That was *once*."

"Twice. The second time doesn't count less because we didn't speak of it."

Despite everything—despite the soldiers down the hall, the crate waiting in the archive, the names in the kettle she hadn't told him about yet (he had seen the ash; he had smelled the burnt ink; he was old, not blind)—she laughed. Short and unwilling, but a laugh. He held onto it the way a cold man holds a coal.

* * *

After she went up to bed, he sat alone at the bench with the ledger and the brandy, and did the arithmetic he avoided in daylight.

His hands had perhaps weeks left of usefulness. Perhaps less. The sewing pattern she had learned in an hour; the ticks in a night; the watermarks would take days. Then the aging, the tooling, the pressing of the false seal—and the seal required strength, a clean hard pull of the press arm that his shoulder no longer owned. It would be her hands. It would all be her hands soon, and his whole remaining job was to pour out fast enough.

He turned to the entry he had avoided all evening. Her sister's name, in his own hand—he had written it into the ledger himself, the month after the funeral, with the crossed tick and the note *deceased, all contacts reassigned*. Beneath it, in the tiny marginal shorthand only three people alive could read, the continuation: *letters continue under secondary alias, funding active.*

Guilt was a glue he knew well. It set slowly and held forever, and you could not solvent it out again; you could only bind over it and hope the board stayed shut.

"Forgive me the method, Marta," he murmured to the page. "Not the outcome. The outcome kept people warm."

The house creaked. Somewhere below, in the workshop, the glue kettle sat cold on its hook, full of ash he was pretending not to have identified, and upstairs his apprentice slept—or didn't—on top of secrets multiplying between them like mold in damp paper.

Tomorrow, watermarks. Tomorrow, forgery. Tomorrow he would teach her everything, including the parts that would shame them both, because the crate would seal soon and the bridge would close at midnight on the last day of the year, and the empire does not wait for grief to finish its lessons.

He pinched out the candle and sat in the dark a while longer, listening to the clock tower practice its strike—two strokes, off the hour, the mechanism worn and honest—and thought: *that clock has been lying about the time for a century, a minute at a time, and no one has ever hanged it for it.*

Perhaps paper might be forgiven the same way.

He heard, very faintly, the workshop door below ease open on its swollen hinge—and Odile's bare feet on the stairs she thought he couldn't hear, going down toward the kettle.
