## 8. Curfew Arithmetic

There was an arithmetic to fear, Odile discovered, and she was better at it than she had ever been at the sums Ambrose had tried to teach her as a girl.

One candle-end burned for forty minutes. A patrol passed the bindery's front window on the quarter hour, its boots making a sound like a slow drum in the frozen street. The stove in the soldiers' billet—her billet now, technically, since three men of the Ninth slept in the room that had been the marbling shop—needed feeding every two hours, and a soldier sent to feed it made noise enough to hear through two walls. And sleep, when it came, lasted exactly as long as it dared.

Subtract one from the other, carry what remained, and you had your working night. Some nights the sum came to nothing. Tonight it came to four hours, if the candle held, if the patrol kept its schedule, if Private Ossler—who snored like a sawmill—did not wake hungry.

She lit the candle behind the pressing boards where the light wouldn't reach the window, laid her tools out in their exact daytime order so that any disturbance could be undone in ninety seconds, and got to work on forgery.

It still felt strange to think the word. She had carried secrets for six years without once opening a seal; she had sewn ten thousand signatures without reading past the first line of any of them. *I only carry things,* she had told Ambrose. But carrying, it turned out, was a kind of knowledge too. She knew the republic's correspondence the way a midwife knows a family: by weight, by smell, by the particular way each clerk's hand pressed harder at the descenders. She knew the chancery ledger not because she had read it but because she had rebound it, twice, and a binder knows a book more intimately than its author ever does. She knew how the thread crossed the tapes. She knew the watermarks. She knew which years used the good linen rag paper from the mill upriver and which years, in the chancery's frugal seasons, used the cheaper stock with the flecks of blue fiber in it.

Ambrose could have made this forgery in his sleep, twenty years ago. Ambrose, tonight, was asleep, genuinely asleep, his breath whistling through him like wind under a door, and his hands—the hands that had taught hers everything—shook too badly to rule a straight line. So the arithmetic fell to her.

*Measure twice, cut once.* She measured the true ledger's pages with her thumb-knuckle against a marked stick: 214 millimeters tall, 158 wide, give or take the swelling of damp summers. The false sheets she'd cut yesterday were a half-millimeter proud. No colonel would notice a half-millimeter. But no colonel would notice, either, until the moment he did, and the whole point of craftsmanship was that nobody ever noticed anything at all. That was the joke of the trade, she thought, and almost laughed aloud at the danger of it. A bookbinder's entire ambition was perfect invisibility. The empire had simply given her a bigger commission.

The paper was the hard part. Matching stock was easy enough—there was a ream of the old chancery surplus in Hessel's stationer's down the lane, and she had bought it in three small purchases over nine days, in daylight, with money, like any customer buying paper for any reason, though her heart had hammered so loudly at the counter the third time that she was sure old Hessel would remember her face forever. He hadn't. He had asked after her sister, because Hessel always asked after Marta, and Odile had said *she's gone ahead of us* in the flat voice she used for such things, and he had nodded and wrapped the paper and that was all.

But new paper looks new. It has a brightness to it, a tooth, a memory of the vat. The ledger's pages had been handled by clerks for eleven years, turned by thumbs, blotted, rained on, dried again. They had the color of weak tea and the softness of an old shirt collar.

So: tea.

She had learned aging the way she learned everything, by watching Ambrose and pretending she hadn't. Strong black tea, cooled, applied with a wide soft brush, thin coats, many of them—never one heavy coat, because heavy coat meant tide-marks, and tide-marks meant someone looking closely, and someone looking closely meant hanging. She brushed the first sheet now, working from the edges inward, and the paper drank the tea and went the color of the ledger's earliest folios, and something in her chest unclenched slightly. It looked right. It looked *lived with.*

Smoke was the second lesson. Tea aged the color; smoke aged the smell. A book has a smell the way a house does, and a forged page smells of nothing at all, which is its own confession. She held each dried sheet over the tallow lamp's smoky heart—not the flame, never the flame, only the greasy dark ribbon above it—and let the soot ghost onto it, then buffed it with flannel. By morning the false signatures would smell like the archive itself: dust and tallow and old decisions.

* * *

She was on the fourth signature—sixteen leaves, folded, slit, ready for sewing—when the arithmetic betrayed her.

The boots in the street came early.

Not the quarter-hour patrol. This was heavier, looser, the walk of men who had been drinking, and it stopped outside the front of the bindery, and there was laughter, and then the worst sound in the occupied world: a fist on a shutter.

Odile blew the candle. In the dark she moved the way she'd taught herself to move these last weeks—hands before eyes, eyes barely needed. The signatures went into the hymnal.

It was a hymnal repair, actually, which felt like a small blasphemy or possibly a providence. Widow Kess's family hymnal, its spine gone to lace, had sat on the bench for a month waiting for new endpapers, and its boards were thick, and its hollowed spine—once the new linings went in—would hold sixteen folded leaves of treasonous paperwork as snugly as a vest holds a letter. She slid the signatures into the gap, laid the loose spine leather back over them, weighted the whole thing with the brass finishing press, and stood up just as the shutter banged again.

"Bindery," a voice called, cheerfully drunk. "You alive in there, bindery?"

She counted to ten. It was important, she had found, not to answer too fast. Fast answers were guilty answers. She counted to ten, lit the workroom lamp at its lowest wick, and opened the door on three soldiers of the Ninth, one of whom was holding a bottle by the neck like a dead rat.

"You're the book woman," said the tall one, whose name she believed was Dreyse. He peered at her with red-rimmed friendliness. "We found a book."

"We found a book," agreed the second, who appeared to be supporting the third.

"A *good* book," Dreyse said, and produced from inside his coat a slim volume, and Odile's stomach performed a small cold revolution, because she knew the shape of it even before she read the title. Confiscated novels were supposed to go into the crate with the archive. This one—a romance, some Vellbruck printer's cheap edition, the kind with a woodcut of a swooning lady on the cover—had instead been living in a barracks, and now wanted mending, because Dreyse had read it four times and the binding had surrendered somewhere around the heroine's third fainting fit.

"It's fallen apart," Dreyse said, with genuine sorrow.

"From use," said the second soldier, and they both laughed, and Odile took the book and examined it under the lamp with the gravity of a physician.

"The sewing's broken at three sections," she said. "And the glue's gone brittle. I can resew it. Two days."

"Two days!" Dreyse looked wounded. "Can't you just—" he mimed, vaguely, the application of glue.

"I can glue it," Odile said, "and it will fall apart again by the fifth chapter, and you'll lose the pages in the snow, and then you'll be a man who lost the ending of his own book." She let that sit. Drunken men feared few things, but they feared that. "Two days. Proper sewing. It'll outlast you."

Dreyse considered this, decided it was a compliment to his endurance, and beamed. They negotiated a price—one mark fifty, paid in coin that had recently been somebody else's—one of the soldiers sang two lines of something sentimental about a millwheel, and they went off down the street toward their stove, leaving the night quiet and Odile alone with a hymnal full of contraband and a romance awaiting surgery.

She stood in the doorway a moment longer, letting the cold scourge her face, listening to the town breathe. Across the square the chancery windows were dark, the imperial eagle newly bolted above the door catching what little light the moon offered. Somewhere in that building, in a crate stenciled with a manifest number, lay the true ledger with every name in the network lying in its columns like patients waiting for the doctor to decide. Her name among them. *Nystrom, O., alias "Kestrel,"* under Marta's old working name, because whoever had recruited her had been lazy, or sentimental, or both.

She shut the door, barred it, and went back to work with the hymnal's passengers safe in the press.

* * *

Captain Verren came at an inconvenient hour, which she suspected was policy rather than accident.

He arrived in the gray part of the morning, when the candle had long since died and Odile had had three hours of shallow sleep and looked it. He knocked—Verren always knocked, which unsettled her more than banging would have—and entered stamping frost off his boots, and he was carrying a book.

"You borrowed this," he said. "From the chancery reading shelf. Before." He set it on the bench: a surveyor's treatise, dull as porridge, which she had indeed borrowed in another life, eight weeks ago, when the chancery still belonged to the republic and borrowing a book was not an act requiring receipts in triplicate.

"Thank you, Captain." She took it and began, automatically, to check its condition, because her hands checked conditions the way other people's hands crossed themselves.

"The men have been reading the confiscated novels," Verren said. He said it the way he said most things, evenly, watching her. "I've let them. Morale." He paused. "You mend books for the soldiers now?"

"Widow Kess's hymnal. And a romance one of them ruined."

"Dreyse's."

"You know your men's reading, Captain."

"I know my men." He drifted along the bench, hands clasped behind him, and Odile tracked him the way she tracked weather—as a pressure change, a thing to be prepared for. He stopped by the finishing press. Under the press, invisible, weighing nothing, sat the future of forty-one lives. "My mother bought her prayer book here," he said. "Before the annexation. I remember the shop smelled like this. Glue and leather and—" he searched for it, "—candle smoke."

"Tallow," Odile said. "We burn tallow ends. Wax is for churches."

"Candle smoke," Verren repeated mildly, as though correcting a map. "May I see the hymnal? My mother sang from one like it."

Every instinct she owned stood up and screamed. She smiled the forgettable smile—the one that made customers leave tips and patrols look through her—and lifted the brass weight and opened the hymnal to the broken spine, angling it so that the light fell forward and the shadowed hollow of the binding, where sixteen folded leaves lay flat as sin, faced away from him.

"Kess family, three generations of marginalia," she said. "This one wrote *praise Him* next to every funeral psalm. Even the sad ones. Especially the sad ones."

Verren leaned over the open boards. He was close enough that she could smell boot polish and, underneath it, soap—the plain yellow soap the garrison issued, which meant he washed his own laundry, which meant something, she supposed, about a man, though she couldn't have said what. His finger traced the air above the broken sewing without touching it.

"You'll resew on new tapes?" he asked.

"On the old recessed cuts, with new linen tape. It'll hold another fifty years."

"And the pages inside—" he tilted his head, reading down into the gutter where the shadows pooled deepest, and Odile's heart performed its work silently, steadily, like a well-made hinge— "they're intact?"

"They're psalms, Captain. Psalms survive everything. It's practically their profession."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment, perhaps, of a joke made across a table neither of them would admit sitting at. He straightened.

"My mother's copy had a pressed flower in it," he said. "A cornflower. From some summer before I was born." He looked around the workshop—at Ambrose's empty stool, at the kettle cold on its ring, at the tools in their exact daytime order, all in their exact daytime order at seven in the morning, though Odile saw him notice and said nothing and he said nothing back. "Keep the flower if you find one," he said. "Some things shouldn't come apart."

Then he put on his cap and left, and Odile stood with her hand flat on the hymnal's closed boards, counting her pulse the way she counted candle-ends, and deciding—without quite knowing when she had decided—that the captain had seen exactly as much as he intended to see, and no more.

* * *

Ambrose woke at noon, ate half a bowl of barley soup, and demanded a report on the aging.

"Tea takes," Odile said. "Smoke takes. The watermark's the problem. The chancery stock is single-mill, anchor-and-crown, left margin. Hessel's ream is double-mill, crown only, center."

Ambrose closed his eyes, which was how he consulted the vast untidy library of himself. "Hold the sheet to the glass," he said finally. "Strong light. You can see the double-mill wire-shadow under the crown if you know where to look. So we don't match the watermark. We kill it." He opened his eyes, and there—for one bright moment—was the master of thirty years, the man who had bound state treaties and smuggled pamphlets in the same week and never once gotten ink on his conscience where anyone could see it. "Sizing, Odile. Heavy gelatin sizing, brushed in warm. It fills the paper. The wire-shadows drown. Hold both sheets to the light and they'll show the same nothing."

"That's cheating."

"That's *binding*." He coughed, waved off her hand. "Any apprentice can make a thing. A master makes a thing that cannot be told from the thing it replaces. Now—the tick marks. Show me you remember the tick marks."

She recited them. Vertical tick beside an entry: courier active. Horizontal: route dormant. Diagonal struck through both: contact retired, do not reactivate. And the small cross in the margin corner, which appeared only eleven times in the whole ledger and meant, according to Ambrose, *knows too much, protect.*

"There's a cross by my sister," Odile said. She hadn't planned to say it. It came out the way steam comes out of a kettle.

Ambrose looked at her for a long moment, and his shaking hands went quiet on the blanket, and he said, "Yes. There is."

"What does it mean, Master? For her?"

"It means," Ambrose said slowly, "that someone in the chancery loved her enough to mark her file twice. And that when this is done—if it's done—you'll want to ask me what I know about that, and I'll tell you, and you'll wish I hadn't. Not today, child. Today we drown some wire-shadows."

Odile bent back over the false pages, brush in hand, warm gelatin sliding across the paper, drowning the little crown in the middle of the sheet. But the words stayed with her, the way splinters stay. *You'll wish I hadn't.* She thought of the diagonal ticks, the horizontal ones, the eleven small crosses. She thought of Marta's entry, marked deceased in one column and active in another, like a door marked shut that someone kept quietly opening.

That evening she hid the finished false signatures in the hymnal's spine again and delivered the mended songbook to Widow Kess with the contraband riding inside it, and walked home through the curfew streets doing her sums: four days' work remaining, five hours of darkness a night, one candle per shift, one patrol per quarter hour, one officer who might or might not be counting the same numbers she was.

At the bindery door she found a slip of paper tucked under the shutter, in a neat chancery hand she recognized:

*Inventory verification moved up. Inspectors arrive within the fortnight. — V.*

She stood in the cold a long time, reading it twice, doing the new arithmetic. Then she went inside, woke Ambrose gently, and said, "Master. We're out of nights. Tell me what you know about my sister's cross."
