## 9. Names in the Kettle

The glue kettle had been in the bindery longer than Odile had been alive. It was a squat copper thing, dented on one side where Ambrose had dropped it down the stairs in 1874 and refused ever afterward to admit it, and it sat on its little iron tripod over the back stove like a hen that had gone broody over nothing. Every morning of Odile's working life it had held the same smell: warm hide glue, faintly sweet, faintly animal, the smell she privately thought of as *home* even though she would have died before saying so aloud.

It had never once occurred to her that it might smell like war.

"You understand," Ambrose said from his chair by the window, where he now conducted most of his existence wrapped in two blankets and an air of judicial authority, "that I am too old to be an accomplice. My heart is seventy-one years old and has opinions about excitement."

"You brought it up," Odile said.

"I raised a theoretical question." He coughed, settled deeper into the blankets. "Theoretically, if a person wished to ensure that certain loose papers never reached the imperial clerks' copying desks—and we agree those clerks copy everything, they are paid by the page, it is the only honest incentive in their whole army—then the theoretical options are fire, water, or stomach. And since neither of us can eat that much, and the stove is in full view of six soldiers..."

"The kettle," Odile said.

"The kettle," Ambrose agreed, with the satisfaction of a man whose theoretical question had walked exactly where he wanted it.

Here was the problem, laid out plainly between them like a patient on the bench. The ledger itself—the great bound ledger crated in the archive room, the one that must cross the bridge intact—was safe enough for now, because armies do not read books while books are being verified against inventories. But somewhere in the same crate, tucked in with the chancery's loose correspondence, were duplicate folios: the clerk's working copies, entries recopied in triplicate by a bureaucracy that trusted no one, least of all itself. Names. Routes. Safehouses. Dates. All of it loose, all of it legible, all of it destined for a copying desk in the imperial capital where some diligent junior officer with good handwriting would eventually transcribe every name into a hanging list, alphabetized.

The folios could not be removed from the crate—that would show in the inventory count. But the clerks had been given the chancery's back room as a writing office, and twice already Odile had carried tea in to find them sorting loose paper into piles, humming, contented as mice in a granary.

"Sooner or later they reach the duplicates," Ambrose said. "Sooner, I think. They've finished the correspondence files. It's the only interesting thing left."

"Then I take them out and burn them."

"And the inventory?"

Odile closed her eyes. She had counted the crate herself, pretending to be stupid about numbers, which required more acting skill than she'd known she possessed. "The count stays right if the pages stay right."

"Pages burn thin," Ambrose said. "Ash is ash. Nobody inventories ash." He looked at her over the top of his blanket with eyes that were still, despite everything, quick and rather wicked. "But you'll have to carry them first."

"Carrying is what I do."

"Yes," he said softly. "That's what frightens me."

* * *

The thefts happened by daylight, which felt wrong and therefore worked. Soldiers notice night movements; they barely notice a woman carrying a stack of spoiled paper toward the scrap bin, because binderies are full of spoiled paper, trimmings and misprints and ruined endpapers, and a girl hauling some toward the stove is doing nothing but her tedious job.

The chancery duplicates were not trimmed and were not misprints, but they were the right size and roughly the right color, and Odile had become very good in the past weeks at folding one sheet inside another so that a name traveled hidden inside three blank ones. Two sheets a trip. Never three. On the fourth day she took only one, to break the pattern, and spent the afternoon convinced a soldier was watching her, until she realized he was watching a cat, and that the cat was winning.

The kettle lived behind the workshop partition, out of the soldiers' sightline, and glue hid everything: drop a folio into hot glue and it sank, softened, disintegrated into the amber mass within minutes. Stirred well, a single sheet vanished entirely. A week of sheets turned the glue darker; Ambrose solved that by declaring, loudly and at intervals, that this batch had gone off and he could smell it from across the room, and demanding the soldiers bring him fresh stock from the requisition stores, which they did, because an old man shouting about glue was the most entertaining thing in Vellbruck that month.

But before each sheet went into the copper, Odile read it.

This was Ambrose's condition, set out the first evening with unusual sternness. "Nothing is destroyed unwitnessed," he said. "You look at every name. You say it, once, aloud if you can manage it, in your head if you cannot. These are people who did work worth recording. They don't get to vanish twice."

"I know how memory works, Master."

"You know how *carrying* works. Carrying is easy—you hold a thing, you walk, you put it down. Memory is not a pouch. You cannot put it down. Are you certain you want to be holding forty names when the soldiers ask you a harmless question?"

She wasn't certain. She did it anyway.

* * *

The first name was a woman named Grete Hallvard, who ran the ferry inn at the south landing and who had once, Odile now learned from the folio in her hands, sheltered three chancery couriers during the autumn retreat and fed them goose. Odile knew the inn. She knew the goose, vaguely, from a run two years ago. She stood at the kettle with the page open and the glue beginning to bubble and read the entry twice through—not just the name but the whole record, the careful dates, the notation of payment in salt—and then she said, quietly, to the steam:

"Grete Hallvard. South landing. Goose."

And she put the page in, and stirred, and watched the letters loosen and blur and drown, and felt something in her chest tear slightly and then, strangely, settle.

The second name was easier. By the tenth it was ritual. By the thirtieth it was litany.

She developed a system, because she was her master's apprentice and systems were the family religion. Name, place, one detail. The detail mattered; Ambrose insisted. *"Anyone can memorize a name. A name without a face is just noise by spring. Give yourself the hook the entry gives you—the odd thing, the human thing. Memory keeps people, not data."* So: Pavel Sorel, tanner, whistled badly. The Widow Kess, who passed messages in loaves, rye only, never wheat. A boy listed only as *J., clockmaker's apprentice*, whose contribution was recorded as *eyesight, exceptional*. Odile sat with that one a long moment—a boy valued, in the republic's own hand, purely for seeing clearly—and committed him with particular care.

At night, lying in the loft above the workshop with soldiers snoring two rooms away, she recited them. Not aloud—she was reckless, not suicidal—but moving her lips in the dark, walking the list end to end like a woman pacing a corridor she had built herself, room by room. *Grete Hallvard, south landing, goose. Pavel Sorel, tanner, whistles. The Widow Kess, rye. J., clocks, eyes.* Some nights she got to the end and started over just to hear the doors swing shut properly behind her. Some nights she fell asleep mid-name and woke at dawn with the last one on her tongue like a stone under a pillow.

Her head had never been so heavy. Her head had never felt so much like ammunition.

* * *

"You're doing it wrong, you know," Ambrose said on the ninth day, watching her fold a folio into scrap. "Your guilt. It's crooked."

"There's a right way?"

"Certainly. You're doing it backward. You feel as though reading the names is the sin and burning them the necessity. It's inverted. Burning is nothing—paper doesn't care. Reading is the *work*. You're the last witness these people will ever have. That's not guilt, child, that's office. You've been ordained, unfortunately, by a glue pot."

Odile stirred the kettle harder than it needed stirring. "And what are you, then? You wrote half these entries."

"Yes," Ambrose said placidly. "I'm not the witness. I'm the confessor. We make quite a congregation between us." He coughed, long and dry, and waved off her hand. "Besides, I only wrote them down. You're the one keeping them alive. There's a difference, and the difference is everything, and one day you'll explain it to me properly because I intend to live long enough to hear it."

"You intend no such thing. You intend to die at that bench mid-sentence about headbands, and you think it's witty."

"It is witty," said Ambrose. "I've been polishing it for weeks."

She laughed despite herself, and the laugh cracked something open, and for a moment she had to turn to the kettle and pretend the steam was hard on the eyes. Behind her she heard the old man shift in his blankets, and say nothing, which from Ambrose was the loudest comfort there was.

* * *

The near-disaster came on the twelfth day, and it came, as disasters did in the bindery now, wearing a friendly face.

Corporal Brenner was the youngest of the billeted soldiers, a farm boy with chapped hands and an earnest belief that everyone's questions deserved thorough answers. He had adopted the workshop as his personal museum and Odile as its reluctant docent. On the twelfth day he wandered behind the partition while she was mid-stir, folio folded into her apron pocket still warm from her body, her heart performing gymnastics her face declined to join.

"That smells awful," Brenner announced.

"Ambrose says it's gone off."

"He shouted about it at breakfast. Is it true glue comes from—" he lowered his voice, genuinely troubled, "—horses?"

"Hides, mostly. Sometimes rabbits."

"Rabbits." He stared into the copper with the expression of a man revising his relationship to literature. Books, Odile gathered, had become morally complicated for Corporal Brenner. "And it holds pages forever? However old?"

"Forever," Odile said. "That's rather the point of us."

He nodded slowly, thanked her for the rabbit information, and left—and it was only after his boots had crossed the workshop and mounted the stairs that Odile let out her breath and understood what he had handed her without knowing it: a story. From that day forward, whenever anyone asked about the smell, the answer was ready and true-adjacent: *the batch went off; the old master insists on boiling it out before we take fresh stock.* An army that had accepted an ancient binder's glue tantrum as fact would accept nearly anything.

That night she added three names to the litany, including a schoolteacher from upriver whose recorded crime was teaching geography that included the republic's borders, and she fell asleep angry and steady, which she was learning was a workable combination.

* * *

On the fifteenth day, two things happened in the same hour, and Odile would spend years deciding whether they were connected.

First: the clerks finished sorting the loose correspondence. She carried in their tea and saw the cleared tables, the tied bundles, the smug little piles, and heard one of them say to the other, "Tomorrow the crates, thank God. I want this town behind me by spring." Tomorrow the crates. The duplicates were in the third crate. She had perhaps a day, two at most, to finish.

Second: Captain Verren came to the workshop door while she was elbow-deep in the kettle, and stood there a moment longer than a man needed to stand to deliver a message about firewood allotments.

He was different lately. Harder to read, which troubled her, because she had prided herself on having mapped him like a route: tea at four, restless before inspections, kind in small unobserved ways, guilty in larger observed ones. Now he stood in the doorway looking at the kettle steam as if it had said something to him, and when he finally spoke it was not about firewood.

"How is the old man today?"

"Worse," Odile said honestly.

"And you?" His eyes came to hers, and there was something in them she couldn't file anywhere—something almost like an apology being drafted and redrafted. "How are you, Fräulein Nystrom?"

No one had asked her that in weeks. It cost her more than she wanted to pay. "Busy," she said.

"Yes," said Verren quietly, glancing once—at the kettle, at the darkened glue, at nothing—and then he gave the message about the firewood after all, touched his cap, and went.

Odile stood a long time with her hands in the steam. Then she took the next folio from her apron, read it—*Marta's alias* was not in this one, thank God, though every third entry made her flinch anyway—spoke the name aloud to the copper, and let it go under.

Two days, she told the litany that night, adding the newest names to the corridor and bolting the last door. Perhaps less. Somewhere out there someone was moving schedules around, and the bridge at the edge of town was being strengthened for something sooner than anyone admitted, and her corridor of names would either hold or it wouldn't.

She slept badly. She recited anyway.
