## 10. The Bridge at Dusk

The bridge at Vellbruck had been standing for one hundred and forty years, and Captain Aldric Verren had been standing on it, in one mood or another, for most of the afternoon.

He had come to inspect the engineers' works, which was the official reason, written neatly in his order book: *Capt. A. Verren to survey bridgeworks, report on load capacity and crossing schedule.* But there was a second reason he had not written down anywhere, which was simply that a man could only stand so long in a requisitioned bindery breathing glue fumes and drinking tea with a woman who never spoke unless spoken to, before he needed air that did not smell of hide and calfskin.

The bridge smelled of river stone and cut timber and, faintly, of tar. It was better.

Below him the Vell ran dark and quick between its ice-lipped banks, and the gorge swallowed it toward the low country with the patience of something that had outlasted every government ever foolish enough to claim it. Verren leaned on the parapet and watched the engineers' crews work. There were perhaps thirty men on the span—sappers mostly, local laborers pressed into service alongside them, and a cluster of officers from the Third Bridging Company who wore their competence like good boots: unremarkable until you looked closely.

"Captain." Lieutenant Ossa, the company's junior engineer, came toward him carrying a rolled plan under one arm and a pencil behind his ear where it would not stay. Ossa was twenty-four and had the unfortunate habit of saluting with whichever hand happened to be free. "You got my message, sir? About the schedule?"

"I got a message about the schedule," Verren said. "I confess I read it twice."

"Yes, sir." Ossa unrolled the plan against the parapet and weighted the corners with stones, the way engineers did, as though the wind were an adversary to be formally engaged. "The crossing's moved up. Three days. The division wants the main body over the bridge by the twenty-ninth, not the first. New Year's Eve is now the *tail* of the operation, not the head."

Verren looked at the plan. The ink was fresh; the amendment had been initialed by three people whose names meant nothing to him and one whose name meant a great deal—Colonel Brandt, who would arrive within the week to verify the chancery archive and ask questions with the flat, patient tone of a man counting spoons.

"Three days," Verren said. "Why?"

"Load, sir. The near piers took frost damage in November—the masons found spalling they didn't like. The strengthening work needs another fortnight to be done properly, but the division won't wait a fortnight, so they've split the difference. Lighter columns first, heavier equipment after the turn." Ossa shrugged, a young man shrugging at arithmetic older than he was. "Also, sir, if I may say—there's a superstition about marching troops onto a new century over a bridge. Some staff officer wants the symbolism of the *first* crossing belonging to the old year. Something about continuity."

"Continuity," Verren repeated.

"Yes, sir. Or possibly somebody's birthday. The messages don't say."

Verren studied the amended schedule. He was a careful man; being careful had kept him alive through two campaigns and a winter in the marshes that still visited his left knee whenever it rained. Carefulness meant that when a fact changed, he asked not only what the new fact was but who had carried it to him.

And here was the trouble, sitting quietly in his chest like a stone swallowed hours ago: three weeks earlier, in the first days of the occupation, a message had reached him—the *first* message, the one that had fixed the crossing for midnight, December the thirty-first—and he had been told that date not by his own chain of command but relayed through the chancery clerk, half-drunk and helpful, who had sworn it came from the civil administration's calendar office. It had seemed natural at the time. Everything about the requisition had been conducted through the town's own frightened functionaries; that was how occupations worked. You used the machinery already in place.

But now the official date and the remembered date sat side by side on the engineer's plan, and they did not match, and the discrepancy was exactly three days.

"Captain?" Ossa said. "Is there a problem?"

"No," Verren said. "No problem. Walk me the span. Show me the piers."

* * *

They walked the bridge end to end while the light went long and copper-colored over the water. Ossa talked the way enthusiastic subordinates talked—a steady spillage of information, most of it useful, some of it merely available—and Verren listened with half his mind and gave the other half entirely to the problem of dates.

Someone had fed the town the wrong date. That much was nearly certain. Whether it was error or intention was the question that mattered, and Verren turned it over with the same fingers that turned over everything: slowly, feeling all sides.

Error was possible. Clerks mistyped things; calendars disagreed; the empire itself could barely keep its own schedules straight past the provincial level. He had seen a supply column sent to the wrong war once, or at least to the wrong end of a very long front.

Intention was also possible. And if intention—if someone in Vellbruck wanted the army believing the archive crossed at midnight on the thirty-first when in truth it would cross on the twenty-eighth, or vice versa, or at some hour when fewer eyes stood on the span—then someone had thought carefully about what the bridge would look like at midnight, who would be watching it, and what could be done under the cover of twelve strokes of a bell.

He thought, unwillingly, of tea.

He had taken to stopping at the bindery in the late afternoons. He told himself it was because the billeting order made the workshop part of his responsibility, and because Ambrose Fenn, the master binder, was ill enough that a commanding officer's occasional presence discouraged the small cruelties of bored soldiers. Both things were true. Neither thing explained why he lingered, why he asked questions about marbling and endpapers and the proper way to flatten a warped board, questions whose answers he did not need and could not have explained to a superior if asked.

And the girl—Odile Nystrom, though he'd learned her name only from the wage ledger, never from her own mouth—worked at her bench through these visits with her eyes down and her hands moving, and she answered him in short, precise sentences that gave away nothing except competence. She had the same stillness her sister Marta had had. He had seen Odile only a few times, years ago, across a market square or through a doorway, always at a distance, always beside the brighter sister, and he had thought then: *that one sees everything and says nothing.* He had been a boy himself, and half in love with Marta in the wordless way boys were half in love, and he had noticed the quiet sister only as one notices the shadow that makes a picture legible.

Now the shadow ran a bindery full of sleeping soldiers, and he drank tea in it three times a week, and he could not have said, under oath, why.

"You're frowning at my pier, sir," Ossa said. "It hasn't done anything wrong since Tuesday."

Verren realized he had stopped walking. They stood above the second pier, where the masons had chiseled out frost-cracked stone and set fresh blocks, and the river pushed past below with a sound like a crowd murmuring in another room.

"Lieutenant," Verren said, "when the schedule changed—who brought you the amendment?"

"Column orderly, sir. From division. Why?"

"And before that? When the original date was circulated—did it come to you from division as well?"

Ossa considered, pencil working behind his ear. "No, sir. Funny thing. The original date came through the town office. Civil channel. We assumed division had cleared it." He frowned at his own plan as though it had misled him personally. "Was that irregular?"

"It was efficient," Verren said. "Somebody saved themselves a signature."

He said it lightly, and Ossa laughed, and the moment passed the way moments passed in armies—swallowed whole by the next task. But Verren had seen the shape of it now, clean as a watermark held to lamplight. The false date had entered the machine through the town's own offices. Whoever had placed it there knew how the army consumed civilian paperwork without chewing: uncritically, gratefully, fast.

That was not a clerk's mistake. That was a courier's craft.

* * *

At dusk he reported.

He wrote it up properly, because he was Aldric Verren and proper writing was the spine he had instead of other, more comfortable spines. *Bridgeworks inspected. Frost damage to piers two and four, remediation adequate for staged crossing. Amended schedule confirmed: advance elements 29 December, main body complete by 31 December, 23:00. Bridge closed to civilian traffic from 30 December, 06:00.*

He signed it. He sealed it. He handed it to the column orderly and watched the man mount up and ride off toward division with the true date folded inside his coat, and then Verren stood in the square in the last gray light and felt the strange double weight of having done exactly right and exactly wrong in the same motion.

Because here was what he had *not* written:

*Discrepancy noted between originally circulated crossing date (31 Dec, midnight) and confirmed operational date (advance 29 Dec). Original date appears to have originated via civilian administrative channels. Recommend inquiry into source.*

He had composed that paragraph in his head, fully, twice. It was a good paragraph. It was the paragraph a dutiful officer wrote. And he had deleted it—not crossed out, not softened, but simply never set down—with a deliberateness that frightened him more than any act of carelessness would have.

If he reported the discrepancy, there would be an inquiry. An inquiry would put eyes on every civilian office in Vellbruck, and eyes found things, and things found gallows. He did not know yet who had fed the false date, or why, or whether the reason was anything he could love. Perhaps it was mere graft—smugglers wanting the bridge unwatched for a night. Perhaps it was nothing.

Perhaps it was a bindery.

He walked back through the town slowly, taking the long way along the river road because the alternative was arriving at his quarters and having no excuse left not to think. Lamps were coming on in windows. Somewhere a dog was complaining about the cold with real conviction. On the corner by the cooper's, two soldiers of the garrison were teasing a shop girl with the harmless, tedious persistence of soldiers everywhere, and Verren gave them a look that moved them along, and they went, laughing at nothing, and the girl thanked him with her eyes and hated him anyway, correctly, because his uniform was the reason she needed thanking.

*Order is mercy,* he had written in his own journal the week the army arrived, and he had almost believed it. Occupation without massacre; requisition with receipts; a ledger seized intact rather than a town burned—that had felt, in the first numb weeks, like the gentlest possible version of an ugly thing.

But gentleness was not innocence. He understood that now in his hands before he understood it in his head, the way he understood weather. Every name in that chancery ledger would mean a trial, and every trial would mean a rope or a prison barge, and the army would call each one order, and the sum of all that order would be a silence in Vellbruck that outlasted him.

Unless the ledger did not survive to matter.

He stopped walking. He stood at the foot of the bridge road with the dusk coming down around him and admitted the thought he had been circling for days the way the sappers circled the cracked pier: that somewhere in this town someone was working, patiently and with great skill, to make sure the empire's prize was worthless. And that he had known it—had *chosen* to know it—and that tonight, by omitting one honest paragraph from one honest report, he had stepped off the bank and onto the span himself, mid-river, committed.

Complicity, he thought, was not a door you went through. It was a bridge you walked, plank by plank, telling yourself the far side was still visible.

* * *

He went to the bindery.

He had no orders requiring it. He had no reason he could write down. He knocked the way he always knocked—two raps, unhurried, the knock of a neighbor rather than an occupier—and heard the small scuffle inside that meant a candle being moved, a bench being tidied, the workshop arranging itself into innocence.

Ambrose Fenn opened the door himself, which was effortful for him now, wrapped in two coats despite the stove. Behind him the workshop glowed amber, presses and type cabinets looming in the shadows, and at the far bench Odile looked up from her work—one heartbeat of something unreadable in her face, quickly folded away—and looked back down.

"Captain Verren," Ambrose said, with the ghost of formality. "Come in out of the river wind. It gets into the bones."

"It does," Verren agreed, and stepped inside, and let the warmth close over him like water.

Tea appeared, because tea always appeared; the old man performed the ritual with trembling care, and Odile rose to fetch the cup without being asked, and for a few minutes the three of them existed in the small warm theater of it, nobody saying anything that mattered.

Then Verren set down his cup and said, idly, to the room in general: "I've been on the bridge all afternoon. They're moving the crossing up—the twenty-ninth for the advance columns. The town will notice the extra traffic."

"A busy bridge," Ambrose said mildly. "Well. Bridges are for crossing."

"Mm." Verren turned his cup a quarter-turn on its saucer. "Though I wonder—" He paused, and chose his words the way he had not chosen the ones in his report. "I wonder if anyone in town has been given the wrong idea about the timing. Rumors travel badly in winter. A person might hear a date and believe it, when the truth was different all along."

The clock in the corner ticked. The stove settled. At the far bench, he saw—or believed he saw, and would spend many nights deciding which—Odile's needle pause above her work, just for the length of one breath, before continuing.

"If someone told me a lie about a bridge," Ambrose said slowly, looking into his tea, "I suppose I'd want to know what they wanted me watching instead."

"Yes," Verren said quietly. "I imagine one would."

He stayed another quarter hour. He asked nothing else. When he left, Odile rose to see him to the door—an artisan's courtesy—and as she lifted the latch she met his eyes for the first time in weeks, steadily, and neither of them smiled, and neither of them looked away, and something passed between them that no report would ever contain.

"Good night, Captain," she said.

"Good night," said Verren, and stepped out into the cold, and stood a moment on the step listening to the river, three days wrong and knowing it, walking on.
