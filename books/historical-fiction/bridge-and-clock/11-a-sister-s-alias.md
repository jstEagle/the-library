## 11. A Sister's Alias

The ledger had a smell.

Odile had noticed it the first night she'd been allowed near it—Ambrose had insisted she learn its construction before anything else, and so she had sat with the true volume open on her knees in the dark of the workshop, breathing it in like a suspicious customer at the market. Old glue. Iron-gall ink gone brown at the edges. And underneath all of it, faint as a watermark, the ghost of pipe smoke—Ambrose's particular blend, the one he swore he'd given up every winter since 1884 and never once actually abandoned.

She had expected to find her sister in it the way you expect to find a grave. Quietly. With your hat off.

What she had not expected was the tick.

* * *

It was the third of December, and the soldiers billeted in the bindery had developed opinions about soup. This was, Odile had come to understand, the central fact of occupation: armies did not march on their stomachs so much as complain on them. Corporal Brandt—the youngest, the one who read confiscated novels behind the coal shed and pretended his lips weren't moving—had declared that morning that Vellbruck turnip soup was "a punishment the war hadn't earned."

"You'll take seconds," Odile said, ladling it without looking up, "because there are starving children in the capital who'd fight you for it."

"There are starving children here," Brandt said, taking seconds.

"Then you have something in common. Eat."

He ate. They were not monsters, most of them. That was the trap of it. Monsters could be hated cleanly, the way you hate weather or arithmetic. These boys tracked river mud across her clean floor and sang off-key when they thought no one listened and one of them had quietly fixed the workshop's squeaking door hinge without being asked, which Odile found more unsettling than any amount of menace. It was difficult to plan the destruction of an empire's archive around men who fixed hinges.

Ambrose slept in the back room now more than he worked at the bench. His hands had begun to shake in a way that tea and willpower did not touch, and he'd taken to dictating measurements to her from his chair with the imperiousness of a man who believed precision was a moral quality. He was sleeping this afternoon, which meant Odile had the ledger to herself for perhaps two hours before the evening patrol, and she meant to spend them on the M-through-N signatures, where she had been told—by the ledger's own structure, by Ambrose's careful lessons—certain entries lived.

She had been avoiding them. She knew she had been avoiding them. There is a difference between not knowing where a thing is and refusing to look, and she had been practicing the second kind with real discipline for eleven days.

But craft is craft. The lesson required the whole structure. And so she opened the ledger to the middle gathering, ran her finger down the margin ticks—Ambrose's private grammar, one stroke meaning closed, two meaning watchful, three meaning live—and stopped.

**NYSTROM, MARTA — alias "Fenwick." Deceased, March 1893.**

Beneath the word *deceased*, in the margin, three ticks.

Live contact.

Odile sat very still. The kettle murmured on the stove. Somewhere upstairs a soldier dropped a boot and swore at it with genuine feeling.

Her sister had been dead six years. Odile had carried the grief so long it had worn smooth, like a stone in a pocket—you stopped noticing the weight until someone asked what you were holding. Marta had drowned, officially. A courier lost to the winter river, the republic's own communiqué had said, with a formality that had always struck Odile as the state apologizing for a spilled drink. And here was the ledger agreeing: *deceased, March 1893.*

And beneath it, three ticks, fresh enough that the ink had barely browned.

Someone using Marta's alias—"Fenwick," a name Odile had heard maybe four times in her life, always in her sister's mouth, always half-laughing, *if I'm ever caught I'm Fenwick, remember, plain little Fenwick of nowhere*—was still reporting. Still writing. Six years after the river took her.

Either a ghost kept accounts, or—

Or.

Odile looked at the entry again. Not at the printed name this time but at the marginal notation beside it, the small administrative hand that recorded disbursements: *F., quarterly, via usual channel.* The letters were small, upright, economical. The crossbars of the f's were long. The terminal strokes hooked left, a habit of a man taught to write quickly on a slanted lap-desk.

She knew that hand.

She had spent nine years learning it. She had watched it rule guide-lines down fine paper. She had watched it letter spines in gold. She had watched it sign, exactly once, the flyleaf of her apprenticeship gift—*To Odile, who reads everything twice. A.*

The handwriting of a dead woman's living alias belonged to Ambrose Halloran.

* * *

She did not confront him. She was a courier; concealment was her native tongue. She closed the ledger, wrapped it in its oilcloth, slid it back beneath the false bottom of the paper cabinet where it lived between lessons, and went upstairs to argue with Corporal Brandt about whether novels corrupted discipline (she argued they didn't; she was lying, hers had certainly corrupted something).

But all through supper, and through the evening's mending of a hymnal whose owner had annotated it with such ferocity that three psalms were effectively rewritten, the question turned over in her like a stone in a tumbler.

Why?

The network needed funding. She understood that much from Ambrose's lessons—the ledger wasn't just names, it was money, it was channels, it was the quiet arithmetic of who paid whom and through which glove. A dead courier's alias, kept alive on paper, was a channel that couldn't be killed because officially it had never existed to kill. Letters written as Fenwick could move funds that no imperial auditor could trace to anyone, because Fenwick had drowned, magnificently, with witnesses.

It was brilliant. It was exactly the kind of thing Marta would have loved—her sister had once forged a chancery seal out of a potato and a candle stub purely to win an argument. It was also forgery. Sustained, patient, lovingly maintained forgery in a dead woman's name.

And it meant that for six years, while Odile grieved, someone had been keeping her sister alive as a line item.

She wasn't sure which part hurt worse—that Ambrose hadn't told her, or that some cold clerkish part of her understood completely why he hadn't.

* * *

She waited until the patrol passed at ten. She waited until the house settled into its occupation-sleep, the soldiers' breathing above, the stove ticking below. Then she climbed the stairs to the back room, where a single lamp burned low, because Ambrose had reached the age when sleep came reluctantly and left early.

He was awake, of course. He was always awake. He lay propped against pillows with a book he wasn't reading, and when she came in he looked at her face for approximately one second and said, "Ah. You've found her."

Not *found what*. Not *what troubles you*. Found *her*.

"You knew I would."

"I hoped you wouldn't for another month," Ambrose said. "I wanted your sewing finished first. Grief is very bad for headbands." He set the book aside. His hands, resting on the blanket, trembled faintly, and he folded them to still them, an old magician's gesture—nothing up my sleeves except everything. "Sit down, child. Your standing there makes me feel like a defendant, and I'm too tired to be guilty properly."

She sat. She did not speak. Silence was a tool; her master had taught her that too.

"It was necessary," he said at last, to the ceiling. "You may as well hear the whole ugly architecture of it. When Marta died, the channel she ran—the western fund, the one that fed the safehouses along the river—died with her name. The chancery's rules were strict. Dead couriers' aliases were retired. Closed. Filed." He made a small sound that might have been a laugh. "Filed. As if a name were a coat you hang up."

"So you didn't file her."

"So I didn't file her." He turned his head and looked at her then, and his eyes were clear and old and sorry. "Every quarter, Fenwick writes a report. Every quarter, Fenwick requests funds. Every quarter, Fenwick receives them and distributes them to people who would otherwise be dead by now—there are eleven of those people, Odile, currently alive, eating bread, entirely because a dead girl keeps signing her name. The empire audits everything else. They cannot audit a drowning."

"The tick in the margin. Three strokes. Live contact."

"Three strokes." He nodded slowly. "Your sister's joke, originally. She used to say a lie needs three legs to stand—one for the truth it hides, one for the person who believes it, and one for the teller, who has to live under it." His voice caught on the last words, just slightly, and smoothed itself over with a craftsman's reflex. "I kept her system. It seemed disrespectful to improve on it."

"How many letters?"

"Twenty-four."

"Twenty-four letters in my sister's hand that aren't in her hand." Odile heard her own voice go flat and strange. "Did you practice? Did you sit at that bench and practice her loops until—"

"Fourteen months," Ambrose said simply. "Before I sent the first one. I had her correspondence to study. She wrote beautifully. You both did—your mother's teaching, I expect. I got the f wrong for a year." He held up his trembling right hand and regarded it. "This is why God gives us apprentices, incidentally. Soon Fenwick will need a new hand entirely, and I am running out of quarters and steadiness in roughly equal measure."

And there it was. Laid on the blanket between them, delicate as a knife wrapped in cloth: *soon you will do this.*

"No," Odile said.

"Odile—"

"No. I carry things. I don't *become* things. I told you—I keep my face forgettable and my hands busy, and that is the entire architecture of my survival, and yours, and everyone whose name is in that book, and you want me to sit down and write letters as my dead sister so that—" Her voice cracked, which infuriated her. She pressed on anyway, quieter. "So that what? So I can spend the rest of my life being haunted on a schedule? Quarterly?"

Ambrose was silent a long moment. The lamp flame leaned and recovered.

"Do you know what she said," he finally asked, "the last time she was in this room? Not this room—the old shop, before the fire. She sat where you're sitting and she said, *Ambrose, if I die, don't let them waste me. I'm worth more dead than alive if you're clever about it.*" He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, full of love. "She thought she was being funny. She was always being funny at exactly the wrong moment. But I remembered it. And when they pulled her out of the ice, I sat where I'm sitting now, and I decided that the only way to honor her was to disobey her—no. To obey her. To not let them waste her."

"That's not honoring her. That's keeping her on payroll."

"Yes," Ambrose agreed mildly. "Both things. Most true things are both things. You'll learn that around fifty."

"She's *dead*, Ambrose."

"And eleven people are not," he said, "which she would point out first, and then make a joke, and then steal your bread." He reached over and, with the unsteady tenderness of his ruined hands, touched her wrist. "I am not asking you to forgive me tonight. I am asking you to understand that I never once wrote a letter as your sister without grieving her. Every quarter. Twenty-four funerals. That was the price, and I paid it, and I would pay it again, and when my hands fail entirely I will teach you the f-loop and you will hate me while you learn it, and someday—maybe not soon—someday you'll write the twenty-fifth letter and you'll understand that betrayal and love are not opposites. They're the same stitch, worked from opposite sides of the cloth."

Odile stood. Her legs felt borrowed.

"I need to think," she said.

"Of course." He picked his book back up, opened it to a page he clearly hadn't been reading, and added, without looking at her: "For what it's worth, she'd have laughed herself sick over the whole arrangement. She'd have insisted we split the proceeds and buy you a better stove."

Despite everything—despite the stone in her chest, despite twenty-four forged letters and six years of unknowing complicity—something in Odile's mouth twitched. "The stove *is* terrible."

"I know. Fenwick filed a complaint about it in the spring report."

And that was somehow the worst thing yet, and Odile left the room quickly, before the laugh that was trying to escape her could turn into the other thing.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She sat at the bench instead, in the dark, with no lamp, and let her hands work the only honest thing available—a stack of blank endpapers to be trimmed, nothing secret, nothing coded, just paper and a knife and the small mercies of repetition.

Betrayal and love indistinguishable. She turned the phrase over the way you test a seam, looking for the flaw.

Because here was the thing she kept arriving at, the thing she couldn't trim away: she wasn't only angry at Ambrose. She was angry at the shape of it. At the discovery that the world did not contain clean categories—that her sister could be both a grave and a bank account, that her gentle old master could be both the best man she knew and a forger who had eaten twenty-four funerals, that the occupying soldiers upstairs included a boy who fixed hinges and read novels with moving lips, and that she, Odile Nystrom, forgettable face, busy hands, carrier-not-maker, had just spent an hour realizing that the ledger she was preparing to destroy contained the only place her sister was still, in any sense, alive.

Burn Fenwick, and burn Marta a second time.

Keep Fenwick, and keep feeding the machine that had gotten Marta killed in the first place.

There was no clean stitch. There was only the cloth, and which side you worked from.

The clock tower struck two. Down in the grate, the last of the day's burned folios sat cooling in the kettle ash—names she had memorized and surrendered, each recited aloud so nothing was lost unwitnessed. She thought about tomorrow's lesson. She thought about the f-loop, the long crossbar, the left-hooked terminal stroke. She thought about how her hands already knew the shape of it, because hands remember everything, that was their whole treacherous nature.

She thought about the quarterly schedule, and did the arithmetic she'd been refusing to do all night.

The next Fenwick letter was due in nineteen days.

Nineteen days after New Year's Eve—after the bridge, after the crate, after whatever she and Ambrose and a conflicted officer were building toward across these frozen weeks. Nineteen days, when Ambrose's hands might not manage it at all.

Which meant that somewhere between the forging of a ledger and the burning of a century, Odile Nystrom was going to have to decide—not whether she carried things, but whether she could bear to become one.

Upstairs, a soldier rolled over in his sleep. The kettle ticked as it cooled. And on the bench before her, in the darkness, her hands kept folding the blank endpapers—over, crease, over again—as though rehearsing for a letter they hadn't yet agreed to write.
