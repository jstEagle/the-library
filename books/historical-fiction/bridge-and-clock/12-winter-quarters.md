## 12. Winter Quarters

The soldier's name was Fenner, and he had not wanted to be a reader of confiscated novels, but the army had left him little choice in the matter.

It began with the requisition. A lieutenant with cold hands and colder arithmetic had walked the row of houses on Tanner's Lane, ticking items into a book: one goose, two cords of beechwood, one crate of assorted printed matter—subversive. The crate belonged to the widow Harsch, who ran a lending library out of her parlor and charged a copper a week, and who watched from her doorway with folded arms as four men carried her life's collection into the chancery yard.

"Subversive," she repeated. "That's *Lorna Doone*."

The lieutenant had not read it. Neither, in fairness, had Private Fenner, whose duties now included cataloguing the seized material for burning. The fire was scheduled for Friday. On Monday night, sitting his watch in the chancery cellar with a lantern and nothing else to do, Fenner opened the first crate to count its contents and found himself looking at the face of a girl on a illustrated cover, a girl standing on a moor with her hair blowing, looking like someone who had never once stood watch in a cellar.

He read forty pages before he caught himself. He put the book back. He took it out again. By Tuesday he was three novels deep, reading by lantern-light with his boots off and his greatcoat over his knees like a blanket, and by Wednesday he had developed a system: anything with a plain brown cover went to the burn pile; anything with a picture or a title that sounded like weather (*The Mill on the Floss*, *Under the Greenwood Tree*) went into his knapsack, which by Thursday evening weighed as much as his rifle.

He told himself he would return them after the war. He was nineteen and believed in after the war.

It was the flogging that changed him—not because he saw it, but because he heard it. Nine lashes in the square for a baker's boy caught smuggling letters, and the sound carried down every street in Vellbruck, that flat wet crack and then the silence after it, worse than the crack. Fenner sat in the cellar with *Silas Marner* open on his knee and could not turn a page. The words kept sliding off. He thought about the widow Harsch's face when they carried the crate out—all indignation, no fear, as if theft were simply rude—and he thought about the baker's boy, whom he'd seen once at the market, laughing at something.

On Friday morning, before the burning, Fenner swapped the contents of two crates. The subversive poetry—thin volumes, ugly covers, exactly the sort of thing a man might hide in a hymnal repair, though he didn't know why that thought came to him—went into the crate marked for the far-bank archive, where nobody would look at it for years. The sermons and agricultural almanacs went to the fire.

The flames went up white and cheerful. The almanacs burned beautifully. Somewhere across town, unknown to Fenner, the widow Harsch said a grace over her breakfast porridge, having noticed among the ashes drifting past her window a scrap of cover board she did not recognize, and concluded the army had burned its own paperwork.

Fenner finished *Silas Marner* that night and started *North and South*, and if anyone had asked him what he was fighting for, he would have had to say: I don't know anymore, but I know what I'm reading for.

* * *

The chancery clerk's name was Pelder, and he had discovered that schnapps made the ledgers quieter.

Not silent—he doubted anything would ever make them silent—but quieter, their columns blurring into soft gray rivers, the names losing their edges. It was the names that did it. Eleven years he had entered them: courier fees, safehouse rents, stipends marked only with ticks and initials, all in the republic's service, all perfectly lawful under a government that no longer existed to have done anything unlawful. Now the empire's clerks were going through his work line by line, and Pelder understood, in the way of a man falling who has already passed the last branch, that his careful hand was going to hang people. That some of those people knew his name because he had paid them. That his own name was in at least one ledger, entered by someone else, in the same meticulous script.

He drank at the Crooked Stave, which was closest, and then at the Ferry Bell, which poured heavier, and finally at home, alone, which was where the drinking got honest.

"You're killing yourself by inches," the Ferry Bell's landlady told him, refilling his glass anyway, because Vellbruck's kindnesses had always been practical rather than moral.

"Inches are all that's left," Pelder said, and was surprised to find it funny. She laughed too, the short laugh of the occupied, and charged him half.

The small frictions accumulated around him like snow against a door. The granary requisitioned; the miller's boy arrested for spitting near an officer's horse and released when it became clear the spit had been intended for the ground generally, not the horse specifically. Wood rationed by the army and sold back to the town at a markup so transparent even the soldiers were embarrassed. Every stove in Vellbruck burning green wood that hissed and smoked, so that the whole town breathed the same bitter resin and coughed in the same rhythm, occupier and occupied, until you couldn't tell a soldier's cough from a fishwife's in the dark.

Pelder saw Captain Verren sometimes, at the chancery—the young officer who'd been a boy here before the annexation, who drank tea in the bindery like a man visiting a museum of his own childhood. Once, coming out of the archive room, Verren had paused and looked at Pelder with an expression Pelder couldn't classify. Not contempt. Something closer to recognition, or pity, or the look one drowning man gives another.

"The inventory notes," Verren had said quietly. "You'll keep them current."

"Current," Pelder agreed. His tongue felt like a ledger's spine.

That night he drank two glasses instead of five, poured the rest of the bottle slowly down the privy, and sat with his head in his hands while the room tilted. There was a thought trying to surface, something about paper stock, something he'd seen in the bindery, and he chased it the way a man chases a dream upon waking, and lost it, and was almost glad.

Almost.

* * *

Ambrose Fenn was dying at the speed of a slow-set glue, and it offended him professionally.

"It should be faster than this," he told Odile, on one of his good days, propped in the chair by the workshop stove with a blanket over his knees like a half-finished binding. "Either a man fails quickly or he doesn't fail at all. This business of failing by degrees—it's shoddy workmanship. I'd reject the materials."

"You'd reject your own body," Odile said, trimming endpapers and not looking up, because looking up meant her face doing things she couldn't afford.

"I'd send it back to the supplier with a firm note." He coughed, recovered, waved off her half-rising with two fingers. His hands, which had shaken for weeks now, were nearly still in the warmth, and this frightened her more than the shaking had—the stillness looked less like recovery and more like practice. "The tea-stained folios. Show me."

She brought him three. He held the middle one to the light, tilting it, squinting along the fibers the way other men read faces.

"Too even," he said. "Tea stains in rings, girl, not washes. Nobody's correspondence sits in water evenly. Crumple the next batch first, dampen it, flatten it again under weight. Paper remembers being handled. Make it remember badly." He set it down with exaggerated care, as if setting down something fragile, which perhaps he was. "Your sister used to bring me letters like that. Rain-damaged. Marta claimed the river roads were scenic."

"Marta claimed the river roads were faster."

"She claimed whatever ended the argument in her favor." A smile moved through the old man's face like weather. Then, quieter, watching the stove door's glow: "I wrote eleven names in that ledger myself, Odile. Over the years. Sympathizers who never carried a thing, who only fed couriers and asked no questions. I entered them because the network needed the record and I was the one with the pen. Some nights I tell myself I gave them to history. Most nights I know better. I gave them to whoever opened the book."

Odile put down the knife. Across the workshop wall, faint through the plaster, came the sound of a soldier in the billet humming something that was probably a march and was definitely off-key.

"Ambrose. If the ledger crosses the bridge—"

"It won't." He said it with such flat certainty that she looked at him sharply. He met her eyes, and for a moment the failure in him arranged itself into something that had once commanded apprentices for forty years. "We have a plan. Plans require materials, time, and steady hands. Two of the three we have." He lifted his right hand, watched it tremble, and set it down again with distaste. "Which is why yours will learn the rest. Headbands tomorrow. We start with the simple two-color weave."

"Your hands can hold a needle long enough to teach."

"My hands can hold a needle long enough to teach," he agreed. "Whether they can hold one long enough to finish—" He shrugged, and the shrug cost him, and he covered it with a smile. "Every binding is a wager against time, girl. I've won most of mine. Go see about supper, and take the soldier's boots in to be resoled—they knocked on the wall twice, which is how they knock now, politely, God help us all."

She went. Behind her, Ambrose sat looking at the stove, and his hand, resting on the arm of the chair, was perfectly, terribly still.

* * *

Verren could not sleep, and had stopped pretending it was the cold.

The cold was honest, at least. It came through the chancery windows in sheets off the river and made its demands without ambiguity: more blankets, more wood, endurance. What came through the wall from the bindery was subtler. Sounds. The scratch of an awl at strange hours. Water heating when no one had admitted to heating it. And once—a week ago now, and he had thought of it every night since—the particular muffled clatter of a dropped bench knife followed by absolute silence, the silence of someone listening to find out if they'd been heard.

He had sent a private for firewood the next evening. The private had come back reporting the bindery dark, the master asleep, everything in order. Everything in order. Verren had written *everything in order* in three separate logs since, and the phrase had begun to taste like a coin worn smooth—still spendable, but light.

He got up, dressed, and walked.

Vellbruck at two in the morning was a town holding its breath between snores. Snow squeaked under his boots. At the corner of the square the clock tower stood black against blacker sky, and Verren stopped and looked up at it as he had looked up at it as a boy, when his mother worked the fish stalls below and the striking of the hour meant supper and his father's boots by the door. His father had been a cooper. Annexation had turned coopers' sons into captains by routes that seemed, at two in the morning, entirely arbitrary.

He walked down to the river path. The bridge rose out of the dark ahead of it, scaffolded now, engineers' lanterns strung along its span like a garland of yellow teeth. They had strengthened it for the troop crossing—the whole Third Brigade, guns and wagons and horses, over a span built for carts and pedestrians. New Year's Eve, per the original schedule. Except the schedule had moved. He had reported the change himself, ten days ago, without questioning where the earlier date had come from, and only afterward begun turning the question over like a stone in the pocket.

Someone had fed him the date. Someone in this town had wanted the crossing sooner—or later—or wanted him thinking about dates at all instead of about crates. He had decided not to report his suspicion, which was itself a decision with weight, a coin spent, and he stood at the rail of the bridge in the dark feeling the account of himself grow lighter and lighter.

A sentry at the bridgehead saluted. Verren returned it. From the far bank, a fox barked twice and quit.

He thought about tea. He had taken to taking tea in the bindery—harmless questions, harmless answers, two people performing innocence at each other with increasing fluency. The girl, Nystrom, poured like her sister had, which was to say like someone humoring a guest while mentally reorganizing a shelf. Her hands were remarkable. Binders' hands, ink-stained, precise, and lately—had he imagined this?—scarce with callus fresh enough to bleed, as though she'd been sewing things at speed, at night, in secret.

Everything in order.

He walked back through the sleeping town, past the Crooked Stave where the clerk's lamp burned late and unsteady, past the bindery where a thread of warm air escaped the chimney vent carrying the smell of size and glue and, very faintly, of strong tea—which was odd, at two in the morning, unless one was staining paper, and he made himself stop there, at the edge of knowing, and go inside, and bank his stove, and lie down.

Sleep came at last near dawn, thin and grudging. He dreamed of the bridge, and of a figure walking it alone at midnight with something under her arm, and woke with the clock striking seven and the word *headbands* in his mouth for no reason he could name.

In the bindery that morning, Odile Nystrom counted her remaining candles, checked the false signatures hidden inside the widow's hymnal, and found Ambrose's hands too weak to thread a needle.

"We begin today," she said gently, sitting down across from him, needle ready. "Teach me the weave."

The old man looked at her for a long moment, and then at the bench where the true ledger lay beneath a dust sheet like a body, and said, "First lesson. When a binding falls—when it drops, when it cracks—do not grab for it. Let the pages scatter. Pages can be gathered. A snapped spine cannot." He closed his eyes. "Second lesson. No matter what you hear at that bench—no matter *what* falls—you do not cry out. Not in this house. Not this winter."

Outside, a patrol went by singing. Inside, Odile threaded the needle, and the winter tightened around Vellbruck like boards around a crate, and somewhere above the square the clock tower ticked toward the year's end with the patience of all clocks, which is to say: without mercy, and without hurry.
