## 13. The Cracked Spine

The false ledger had reached that stage Odile privately thought of as *the ugly duckling days*—too finished to be a pile of paper, too unfinished to be a book. It lay on the bench under the south window like a patient halfway through a surgery: sewn, spine-rounded, waiting only for its boards, its headbands, its gilt. Eleven nights of tea-staining and smoke-curing and thread-counting had gone into it. Eleven nights of Ambrose's shaking hands steadied by hers.

She was buffing the spine with a cloth-wrapped stick of bone when Corporal Fenner came through the door carrying two buckets of water and no sense whatsoever.

The soldiers billeted in the bindery had learned, over the weeks, a kind of careful choreography around the workshop. They stepped over the sewing frame. They did not lean on the press. They had absorbed, without anyone explaining it, that the bench by the south window belonged to the little mute bookbinder and was not to be touched—a courtesy Odile suspected came less from respect than from Fenner's superstitious belief that books could hold grudges.

But choreography fails in winter, on worn floorboards, in boots issued by an army that had never once considered the cost of anything. Fenner's heel caught the lip of a raised nail. He pitched forward, buckets swinging, caught himself on the bench—

—and the ledger went up, hung in the air for one horrible weightless moment the way only precious things do, and came down on the corner of the lying press with a sound Odile would hear in her sleep for the rest of her life.

*Crack.*

"Oh," said Fenner, in the small voice of a large man. "Oh no."

Odile did not scream. Screaming would have been a confession that the book mattered, and this book mattered more than her life, more than Ambrose's, more possibly than the entire concept of Vellbruck. What she did instead was make a sound she had invented the previous month for expressing artisanal displeasure at occupying forces: a short, guttural syllable that meant, roughly, *you enormous tragedy of a human being*, delivered with such conviction that Fenner actually took a step backward, water slopping over his boot tops.

"I'll fix it," he said. "I'm good with my hands. I built my mother a fence once."

Odile looked at him. She looked at the ledger, its spine split along three sewing stations, its aged pages fanned across the floor like the cast-off feathers of a very expensive, very illegal bird. Then she looked back at Fenner.

"Or I could just go," he said, and went.

She waited until the door closed. Then she knelt among the scattered signatures and began, with hands that were not entirely steady, to take inventory of the ruin.

The spine had cracked clean through between the fourth and fifth cords. The pages themselves were unharmed—she checked three at random, heart hammering—but the structure was broken, and a broken structure meant resewing, and resewing meant undoing eleven nights of work down to the bare spine, and doing it again before dawn, because the crate sealed in four days and Verren's superiors were expected sooner.

In the corner, from his cot near the stove, Ambrose stirred. He slept most of the afternoons now, in a chair or a cot or once, memorably, upright against the finishing press with a burnishing tool still in his hand. But he woke easily at night, as if sleep were a country he could only visit briefly on a visa.

"What fell?" he asked, without opening his eyes.

"The ledger."

His eyes opened. For a moment he was silent, and Odile could see him doing the same arithmetic she had done—the nights remaining, the hours, the distance between here and catastrophe. Then he swung his legs off the cot, winced, and stood.

"Well," he said. "You always said you wanted practice."

"I never said that."

"You thought it very loudly. Help me up. If I am to supervise a disaster I will do it from a stool like a person of consequence."

* * *

They worked by two candles and the banked glow of the stove, because working past curfew by lamplight visible through a window was the sort of thing that got towns shot at, and Odile had opinions about that. The soldiers' snores came through the partition wall in uneven waves—one man breathing like a bellows, another talking in his sleep about his grandmother's turnips.

Resewing a ledger is not difficult work for a trained hand. Resewing it *identically*—same thread count, same stitch angle, same faint irregularities that a chancery clerk's inventory note would describe—is work for a trained hand with a memory like a customs registry, which is what Ambrose had spent six weeks making of hers.

"Cords four and five," he murmured, watching her strip away the ruined thread. "What did we agree?"

"Four cords on the true ledger's spine, but the false shows five stations because the chancery inventory says five and the inventory lies." She threaded the needle. "Because whoever wrote the inventory counted the kettle-stitch."

"And why does the inventory lie?"

"Because you wrote it," Odile said, "in 1888, when you were rebinding the original and discovered nobody had counted correctly in forty years, and rather than cause trouble you made the record match your own convenience."

Ambrose smiled at the ceiling. "The finest forgers in history have all been, at bottom, tidy men who disliked paperwork. Now. Sew."

She sewed. The needle went through, and through, and through, and the rhythm of it carried her somewhere below fear, into the place where craft lived—that deep quiet room in her where her hands knew things her mouth would never say. Outside, snow ticked against the glass. Inside, the glue pot breathed its animal-smell warmth, and Ambrose kept up his low running commentary like a man narrating a slow boat ride: *tighter at the head, looser at the tail, a book is a held breath, girl, sew it like you mean to let it out.*

It was somewhere past two in the morning that the door opened.

She knew his footsteps before she saw him. She had mapped them weeks ago—heavy at the heel, unhurried, the walk of a man perpetually arriving slightly later than he wished to be. Captain Aldric Verren stood in the doorway with a firewood sack over one shoulder and frost in his hair, and looked at the scene: the girl kneeling at the bench at three in the morning, the old master on his stool, the half-gutted ledger, the needle still in Odile's hand.

Curfew violation. Working after hours. Possession of a document resembling, to any informed eye, the property of the imperial army.

Any of these would be enough. Together they were a hanging.

Verren set down the firewood sack. Very quietly, he closed the door behind him.

For a long moment nobody moved. The soldier beyond the partition snored on, dreaming of turnips.

Then Verren crouched, picked up a single loose folio from where it had slid beneath the stove bench, examined it in the candlelight—tea-aged paper, the republic's watermark, handwriting he almost certainly recognized as a dead woman's—and placed it gently on the bench beside Odile's elbow.

"The wind," he said, "blew some of these under the shelves."

There had been no wind. The windows had been shut for six weeks.

"There was," Odile said carefully, "wind."

"There often is," said Verren, "in this town," and he took off his officer's coat, folded it over a chair, and knelt down to look under the lying press.

* * *

They gathered pages together for the better part of an hour, and neither of them said a single word about what they were doing.

This was the strange arithmetic of it, Odile thought afterward. Everything that mattered passed between them in the negative space. When Verren found a folio and handed it to her, he was saying *I know*. When she took it without meeting his eyes, she was saying *I know you know*. When he glanced at the partition wall and then deliberately continued searching, he was saying *I will not tell*. And when Ambrose, from his stool, offered him a cup of the bitter chicory that stood in for coffee, and Verren drank it without complaint, he was saying something else entirely, something Odile didn't have a word for yet.

"You knew my sister," Verren said at last. It was not a question. It was barely even a statement. He said it to the floor, to the space under the press where his hand was sweeping blind for stragglers.

"Marta Nystrom," Odile said.

"Yes." He found nothing under there; he stayed crouched anyway, as if the floor required supervision. "She used to stand exactly where you're standing. At the counter, in the summer, with the door open. She was reading something and laughing at it, and I was too much of a coward to ask what."

"She'd have told you if you had."

"Do you think so?"

Odile fed the needle through the spine, drew the thread taut. "I know so. She told people things whether they asked or not. It was her worst quality and everyone loved her for it."

Something crossed Verren's face that was not quite a smile and not quite the other thing. "Yes," he said. "That sounds right."

He rose, retrieved his coat, and paused at the door with his hand on the latch. He looked back at the ledger—whole again now, or nearly, its spine rethreaded and waiting only for the morning's paste.

"For the record," he said, "I came for firewood, found the store locked, and left. That is what I will say if anyone asks."

"That's what happened," Odile said.

"That," agreed Verren, "is what happened," and he was gone, leaving behind him the faint smell of frost and officer's wool, and one folio he had found beneath the stove and set silently on the bench where she would be sure to see it: the page bearing the last name in the L section, aged with tea, written in a dead woman's hand.

* * *

Dawn came gray and thin. Odile had not slept. Her eyes felt like sandpaper and her shoulders like someone else's shoulders, borrowed from a much larger and more unfortunate woman. But the ledger sat on the bench whole again—rounded, backed, its spine holding—as if the night had never happened.

Fenner appeared at eight with the water buckets and hovered by the door like a man approaching a dog he had previously kicked.

"Is it—" he began.

"It is fine," Odile said.

"I could pay for—"

"You cannot," said Ambrose pleasantly, from his stool, "afford what that book is worth, corporal, and neither can I, and neither, I suspect, can the army. Fetch your water."

Fenner fetched his water. On his way out he set, inexplicably, a boiled sweet on the corner of the bench, and fled.

Odile looked at the sweet. She looked at the ledger. She looked at the folio from under the stove, which she had not put away, because putting it away would mean deciding what to do about it, and she was not ready to decide.

Because here was the thing she had turned over all night between stitches, the thing that sat in her chest now like a swallowed stone:

A captain of the imperial army had knelt on her floor at three in the morning, among evidence enough to hang the whole bindery, and had helped her gather it up.

That was not ignorance. Ignorance would have reported her, or worse, laughed. That was not carelessness either; Aldric Verren was the least careless man she had ever mapped. That was a choice. He had made it on his knees among the scattered pages, and he had made it knowing what the pages were, and he would have to keep making it every day until the crate crossed the bridge—or he would have to unmake it, and she needed to know which, soon, because four days from now his superiors would open that crate, and if a colonel's eye was sharper than Ambrose's forgery, the first name on the list of the doomed would be the mute bookbinder of Vellbruck, and the second would be the captain who had helped her.

Ambrose followed her gaze to the folio, then to the window, where frost ferned the glass and the bridge's far tower stood gray against the pale sky.

"He'll come back," the old man said. "Men like that always come back. The question, child, is whether he comes back to help us finish—or to ask us, properly this time, what exactly it is we think we're doing. And we ought to decide, before he does, what answer we're prepared to give him when he asks us the truth."

Odile picked up the boiled sweet, unwrapped it, and set it in the jar on the shelf where she kept the small useless kindnesses of the occupation. Then she took up her brush and the warm paste, and opened the ledger to its endpapers, and began.
