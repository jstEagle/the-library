## 14. What Verren Knows

Captain Aldric Verren had a talent, cultivated over twenty years of service, for knowing things and declining to know them. It was not dishonesty, exactly. It was more like the skill of a man walking across a frozen pond who has learned precisely which patches of ice will bear his weight if he does not look at them too directly. He kept his eyes on the far shore and his boots on the sound ice, and he told himself that this was prudence.

For six days now he had known what happened in the bindery after curfew.

He had known it the night he went to fetch firewood and found Odile Nystrom on her knees among scattered pages, candle guttering, needle in her teeth like a seamstress at a funeral. She had looked up at him with an expression he was still turning over in his mind—not guilt, not terror, but something closer to the weary courtesy of a hostess whose guest has arrived early. *You have caught me mid-work. Will you take tea, or shall we both pretend you did not?*

He had helped her gather the pages. That was the fact of it, undeniable as a scar. He had knelt on cold floorboards beside her and picked up sheets of cunningly aged paper and handed them back one by one, and neither of them had said a single word about what those pages were, and when they were all collected she had nodded to him—*nodded*, as though he were the neighbor come to borrow a cup of flour—and gone back to her sewing.

And every day since, Verren had performed the small theater of not knowing. He signed the requisition forms for the bindery's coal without noting how much coal a two-person workshop burned. He reviewed Corporal Fenner's patrol log and struck out the sentence about candlelight seen past curfew, annotating it *verified—bindery work, authorized overtime.* He stood in the archive room while the crate took shape around the false ledger and felt its weight settle into his chest like swallowed river stone.

He knew what he was doing. That was the trouble. A man could excuse ignorance; ignorance was weather, it simply happened to you. But Verren had stopped being ignorant sometime between gathering pages and striking sentences from logs, and there was a word for what he was now, and the word was not *prudent.*

* *

The army has a remedy for men who think too much, and the remedy is paperwork.

On the fourth morning of his knowing, a dispatch arrived from Colonel Hasek's office: *Verify all archive contents against chancery inventory prior to crossing. Discrepancies to be investigated and resolved by responsible officer.* The responsible officer was, by the accident of geography that had put his regiment in Vellbruck, Captain Aldric Verren.

He read the order three times in the chancery's requisitioned front room, standing at the window where the frost had grown ferns on the glass. Across the square, the bindery's chimneys breathed their thin gray smoke into a white sky. Someone was at the glue kettle. Someone was always at the glue kettle.

"Sir?" asked Lieutenant Brasch, his junior, a round-faced young man who believed sincerely in ledgers the way other young men believed in saints. "Shall I bring the inventory?"

"In a moment," Verren said. "First I want a private interview with the civilian bookbinder. The apprentice."

Brasch blinked. "Is there a charge, sir?"

"No charge." And then, because Brasch would keep asking otherwise, and because Verren had discovered he wanted witnesses to nothing: "A routine verification. Off the record."

"Off the record," Brasch repeated, writing it down, which rather defeated the purpose, until Verren gently took the pencil from his hand and pointed him toward the inventory shelves.

* *

They used the chancery's small hearing room, the one with the map of the republic on the wall—a map that had been printed eleven years ago, before the annexation, when Vellbruck still belonged to itself. Nobody had taken it down. Verren suspected nobody could quite bring themselves to, which told him something about his own soldiers he tried not to examine.

Odile Nystrom came in when the corporal fetched her, wiping her hands on her apron, ink in the creases of her knuckles the way there had been ink in them the first time he'd ever seen her, eight years ago, laughing at something his sister-in-law said outside the market while Marta Nystrom stood beside her with her chin up and her collar open in January as though cold were a thing that happened to other people.

She sat when he indicated the chair. She folded her hands. She waited with the patience of someone who had decided some time ago exactly how much this room could frighten her, and had found the number reassuringly low.

"You understand," Verren began, and heard his own voice do the thing voices did in such rooms, flatten and formalize, "that this conversation is not part of any official proceeding."

"Yes, Captain."

"That no record is being made." On the table between them lay a single sheet of paper, blank, placed there for the appearance of procedure. "That anything you say here cannot be used to protect you."

"I understand." She considered the blank page. "It can't be used to hang me either, then. There's a symmetry to it. I like symmetry. It's a binder's habit."

He should reprimand her for flippancy. He found that he could not be bothered to reach for it. Instead he sat down across from her, and the chair creaked, and somewhere above them a soldier's boots crossed the ceiling with the heavy rhythm of a man carrying something he resented.

"I am going to ask you questions," Verren said, "and you are going to find, I think, that they are strange questions, because I am going to ask you to tell me things I already know."

Something moved behind her eyes—the flicker of a woman recalculating. Then, carefully: "All right."

"The ledger in the crate downstairs," he said. "The ledger that will cross the bridge tonight week. It is not the chancery's ledger."

No, she thought later, telling Ambrose about it in the low voice they used now for everything important; no, that was not how he'd begun, he had begun with the paper stock, the thread count, the tea-staining—he had built it plank by plank like a man laying out evidence, except that the evidence convicted himself as much as her. She had understood within a minute that this was not an interrogation at all, or not primarily. It was a confession wearing an interrogation's coat.

But from where Odile sat, in the chair, in the room with the old map, the moment had felt less like either. It had felt like the moment in a card game when both players set their hands face-up on the table and study each other over them.

"Yes," she said. "It is not."

"How long have you been making it?"

"Nineteen days."

"And the original?"

"Burned." Her voice did not waver. "Page by page, in the glue kettle. The ash is in our hearth if your colonel would care to sift it. Every name was read aloud before it burned. Mine among them. Marta's. Ambrose's." She paused, and added, with a precision that cut: "Yours is not in it, Captain. You were never interesting enough for the republic. I'm sorry if that wounds you."

Despite himself—despite everything—he laughed, a short exhale that surprised them both. Then he leaned forward, and put both hands flat on the table the way he had seen magistrates do in his father's time, in this same town, in another country's courtrooms.

"Do you know what you have done? Under the articles I serve under, the destruction of a military archive is—" He stopped. Recalculated. "Do you know what happens to the people in that ledger, if it crosses the bridge intact?"

"Hangings," Odile said. It was the plainest word, so she used it. "Yes. I know. Every name means a hanging, or a prison barge, or a village fined until it starves quietly. I've known since the first night I opened the true ledger and saw my sister marked deceased and active in the same line. That's why it's ash, Captain. Not because I'm brave. Because I couldn't carry it."

"Carrying is your trade, I'm told."

"It is." Her chin came up a fraction. "And I've learned the difference between carrying a thing and holding it for someone who intends harm. One is work. The other is complicity. I don't do the second."

Verren sat back. Outside the frosted window, a supply wagon groaned past, and the clock tower began to strike the hour—ten slow bronze strokes, the town's oldest voice saying the only thing it had ever said. Neither of them spoke until it finished.

"There will be an accounting," he said quietly. "Not today. But the empire is not kind to officers who lose archives, and it is even less kind to civilians who burn them. When this is discovered—and it will be discovered, Miss Nystrom, forgery is patient but it is not eternal—you will be arrested, or fled, or dead. Have you thought about that?"

"Every evening," she said. "Around the time the kettle boils. And then I think about the widow Praus, who carried messages for two years because her son was shot at Kessel Field, and if the ledger crosses the bridge she hangs, and her daughter goes to the state orphanage in Grunstadt, which everyone knows is a workhouse with hymns. And then I stop thinking about the accounting and go back to sewing." She unfolded her hands, refolded them. "You've been in my workshop, Captain. You've watched me sew signatures at midnight and you handed me the pages when they fell. Don't ask me now whether I've considered the cost as though I'm the amateur in the room."

There it was, laid open between them: the firewood, the scattered pages, the silence he had maintained like a man maintaining a lie of omission before a priest. She had known that he knew. Of course she had. She had known for days, and she had let him go on knowing, and the two of them had orbited each other through the cold rooms of the bindery like twin stars keeping a careful, mutual distance.

"Yes," he said. "I suppose I have."

* *

Here is what Verren did not say, and would not say, and carried instead:

He had come back to Vellbruck in October expecting a town that had forgotten him. He had left at fourteen, when the annexation made his father's customs post an imperial office and his father's loyalty a matter of public record, and the family had gone east with what fit in two wagons. He had expected the town to be smaller, shabbier, a museum of a life that had closed over his absence like water. He had not expected it to smell the same—river mud and roasting chestnuts and the particular vinegar-sharpness of the bindery quarter—and he had not expected to stand in the market square and hear, in the crowd's murmur, the vowels of his childhood, and feel the strange grief of a man returning to a place that had gone on without him and done perfectly well.

And he had not expected to see, across the square, a young woman with ink on her hands who walked exactly the way Marta Nystrom used to walk, as though the street were a book she was already halfway through and enjoying.

He had asked, in his first week, an idle question of the chancery clerk—*the bookbinder's girl, is that the Nystrom girl?*—and learned which sister survived. He had told himself the interest was procedural. He had told himself a great many things were procedural that autumn.

Now he sat across a table from her with a blank sheet of paper between them and understood that he had arranged this meeting for one reason, and the reason had nothing to do with verification.

"I have one question," Verren said.

"You've asked dozens."

"One question that matters." He looked at the map on the wall, at the republic rendered in faded inks, rivers blue and borders firm, a country drawn by people who believed in it. Then he looked back at her. "Your sister. Marta. If she were sitting where you're sitting—if she could see what you've done, the burning, the forging, all of it—would she approve?"

Odile went very still.

He watched her think. He watched her refuse the easy answer—the quick yes, the loyal sister's reflex—and he understood, with a sensation like a key turning, that whatever she said next would be the truest thing spoken in this room in years, perhaps in decades.

"Marta would have approved of the burning," Odile said at last. "She'd have done it herself, and faster, and she'd have made terrible jokes about the smell." A breath. "The forging she would have argued about. She'd have said: why make them a beautiful lie? Why give the empire a perfect ledger to file in some archive in the capital, so that in fifty years some historian opens it and believes it? She hated tidy records. She said tidiness was how power forgave itself." Odile looked down at her ink-stained hands. "But she'd have approved in the end. Because Ambrose proposed it, and because I needed the time, and because Marta believed—she really believed—that the point of resistance isn't winning. It's that somebody refused. On the record, off the record, in whatever way the record allows." She raised her eyes to him. "Why do you ask, Captain? Do you want permission? Hers or mine?"

The question landed harder than any magistrate's gavel. *Do you want permission.*

"Yes," Verren said. It came out before he could dress it in procedure. He sat with it, and made no attempt to retrieve it.

Odile studied him for a long moment, and then she did something he did not expect. She reached across the table, took the blank sheet of paper, turned it over, and slid it back to him along with the pencil that lay beside it.

"Then write yourself a note," she said. "Not for the record. For yourself. Something you'll remember in ten years when you try to decide whether you were a coward or the opposite. My master says a binder's job is to make sure the truth survives its binding. Maybe that's an officer's job too, some weeks."

She stood, smoothed her apron, and paused at the door.

"The widow Praus," she added, without turning around. "Her son at Kessel Field. If you're going to choose, Captain, choose before the crate is nailed. After that it's only arithmetic."

The door closed behind her, softly, the way doors closed in houses rather than garrisons.

* *

Verren sat alone in the hearing room for a long while.

The clock tower struck the half hour. Frost continued its slow embroidery on the glass. On the table lay the sheet of paper, blank side up, and the pencil, and a man of forty-one with twenty years of service and a career that had, until this winter, been distinguished chiefly by its freedom from incident.

He thought about the articles he served under. He thought about Colonel Hasek's neat handwriting. He thought about the flogging in the square in December, and the soldier who read confiscated novels, and his father's face the day they loaded the wagons—his father, who had spent his whole life keeping accurate records for a government that changed twice and never once thanked him, and who had said, at the border, looking back at Vellbruck, only: *"Mind, Aldric. Paper remembers."*

Paper remembers. Yes. And paper lies, if the hands are skilled enough, and God help him, the hands in that bindery were the most skilled he had ever seen.

Verren picked up the pencil.

He wrote, in small careful capitals, the way he wrote inventory notes, the way his father had written customs manifests for a republic that no longer existed:

*I knew. I chose.*

He looked at it for a minute. Then he folded the paper into eighths, tucked it into the inner pocket of his tunic, against his chest, where a man keeps letters he means to answer someday, and went downstairs to sign the final verification of an archive that was mostly ash and courage.

At the foot of the stairs, Lieutenant Brasch was waiting with the inventory ledger under his arm and an expression of dawning unease.

"Sir—the clerk. Pellam. He wasn't at his post this morning, and his landlady says he didn't come home last night, and there's—" Brasch hesitated, lowered his voice. "There's a rumor he's been asking after the next boat downriver. Asking about what happens to witnesses, sir. After crossings."

Verren closed his eyes briefly. The clerk. The drunk, frightened, indispensable clerk, who had seen a young woman buy matching paper stock, and who knew exactly what a forged ledger was worth to the men hunting its errors—and what its maker was worth to them.

"Find him," Verren said. "Quietly. Before anyone else does."

Brasch went. Verren stood in the chancery hallway, listening to the clock tower prepare itself for noon, and understood that the margin he had been living inside—the prudent ice, the far shore, the theater of not-knowing—had just become very thin indeed.
