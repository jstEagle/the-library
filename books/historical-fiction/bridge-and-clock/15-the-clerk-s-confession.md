## 15. The Clerk's Confession

The clerk's name was Pell Wexler, and he had been drunk for eleven days by Odile's count, which was generous of her, because she had stopped counting honestly at nine.

She knew his footsteps on the bindery stairs the way she knew the creak of her own bed. Wexler climbed slowly now, one hand dragging the rail, and he paused on every landing to compose himself—which is to say, to lose what composure he had gathered on the previous landing. When he finally pushed through the workshop door, bringing with him a gust of river cold and the smell of cheap brandy cut with cheaper gin, Ambrose did not look up from the sewing frame. Odile did not look up either. Looking up was an invitation, and invitations cost.

"Madame Nystrom." He pronounced it wrong. He had pronounced it wrong for six years, back when the republic still paid him to pronounce things correctly, and he pronounced it wrong now, when pronunciation had stopped being anyone's business but God's. "I would have a word."

"The shop is closed," Odile said, burnishing the edge of a board with a bone folder that had belonged to three masters before her. "Come back in the spring."

"It is December."

"Then come back in December of a better year."

Ambrose made a small sound that might have been a laugh and might have been his lungs giving notice. Either way, Wexler took it as permission, which told Odile everything about how the next hour would go. The clerk lowered himself onto the customer's bench—the good bench, the one by the window where ladies once sat while their Bibles were rebound—and he set both hands flat on his knees as though holding himself down against a wind only he could feel.

"I watched you," he said.

Odile kept the folder moving along the board. Smooth, even strokes. A face you forget, hands that are busy. "You watch everyone. It was your profession."

"At Vasseur's. The paper merchant. The eighth of the month." His voice had the terrible clarity that drink sometimes grants men, like a lantern carried through fog—useless for seeing the road, excellent for blinding everyone else. "You bought stock. Fine wove, cream, twenty-two by thirty-four. Chancery weight." He let that sit in the room like a dropped knife. "And you asked for two quires, and you asked that it not be written in the daybook, and Vasseur—who is a fool, but a fool who remembers money—remembered anyway, because I buy his ink, and we spoke of it, and I put it together."

Outside, a patrol went by on the square, boot-heels ringing on frozen cobbles. Four men, Odile judged, by the rhythm. She counted without deciding to. Counting had become a kind of breathing.

"You put together what?" she said.

"That the ledger can be copied." Wexler leaned forward. His eyes were wet—not with tears, or not only. "That it is being copied. That somewhere in this room there is a second book wearing the first book's clothes, and that when the army opens that crate on the far side of the bridge, they will find a forgery so fine that only a man who kept the chancery's records for nineteen years could say otherwise." He spread his hands. "A man like me."

* * *

Here is what Odile knew about Pell Wexler, and she knew it the way couriers know things—sideways, in passing, filed under routes rather than contents: he had served the chancery since before the annexation, copying treaties he did not understand and inventories he understood too well. He had stayed at his desk after the government fled, because a desk is a country of its own and some men cannot emigrate. He drank because the empire paid him to keep records for a republic that no longer existed, which is the sort of joke that requires brandy to land properly. And he was, in the ordinary way of small frightened men, harmless—

—unless he wasn't.

Unless a man who had spent nineteen years making exact copies decided that history would remember him kindly if he pointed at a forgery. Unless he woke some morning sober enough to trade his silence for a name in the new regime's good books. Unless terror, which had kept him quiet, curdled into the other thing terror becomes when it sits too long: ambition.

"I haven't told them anything," Wexler said, and here his voice cracked down the middle like a bad spine. "Yet. That is why I came. I wanted—I wanted you to understand my position."

"Your position," Ambrose said, from the frame. It was the first thing he'd said, and it landed with the weight of a press screw tightening. The old master set down his needle with great ceremony, the way he did everything now, each motion negotiated with his failing hands in advance. "Sit, Pell. You're sitting. Good. Odile, the kettle."

"We're not doing tea," Wexler said. "This isn't a social call."

"Everything is a social call," Ambrose said. "That is what the young never learn and the old learn too late. Tea, Odile."

She made the tea. It gave her something to do with her face. Behind her she heard Wexler begin, in a low urgent stream, to unburden himself of the fear he had been carrying, and she listened the way she had been trained to listen—for exits, for leverage, for the shape beneath the words. What she heard frightened her more than the threat itself, because Wexler was not threatening them out of malice. He was threatening them out of arithmetic. If the forgery were discovered, the discovery would travel upward, and questions would travel downward, and among the questions would be *who in this town had the skill*, and the answer would be a bindery, and inside the bindery would be an old man and a woman whose name was already written in a ledger destined for the far bank. Wexler was simply informing them that he intended to be the one who answered the question first. To be helpful. To be, when the reckoning came—and in his mind the reckoning was always coming, riding up the river with spring—on the correct side of it.

"There will be tribunals," Wexler said. "There are always tribunals. After the border provinces went over, there were tribunals for eleven years. I read the minutes. I *filed* the minutes." He accepted the tea Odile handed him with a nod that tried for dignity and achieved something closer to apology. "A man wants to be able to show he cooperated."

"A man wants," Ambrose said, settling onto his stool across the workbench, "to survive. There is no shame in it. There is also no shame in naming your price."

Silence. Even the kettle seemed to hold its breath.

Odile looked at her master. In seven weeks of conspiracy she had learned to read him again as she had at fifteen—not his words, which were always circling some indirect country, but his hands, and his hands right now were perfectly still, folded on the scarred bench, waiting. He had gone still the way he went still before opening a binding that might crumble. You did not force it. You offered it a path.

"Name it," Ambrose said.

Wexler stared into his tea. When he spoke, the drunkenness had burned off him almost entirely, leaving something bare and miserable underneath. "Money. I'm sorry. I'm not proud. The silver—the shop does silver repair, I've seen the tray. Enough to cross the border in the spring, enough to start somewhere no tribunal has heard of. And—" he hesitated, and Odile saw the true request surface, the one under the money, the way a drowned cart surfaces wheel-first—"and to be left out of it. Whatever happens. My name nowhere. Not in any account, not in any hand. I was never here."

* * *

Ambrose rose. It took him a long time, and nobody helped him, because helping him required acknowledging that he needed it, and that was a tax none of the three of them could afford just then. He crossed to the cabinet at the back of the shop—the tall one with the false front that had held contraband pamphlets during the last occupation and communion wine during the one before that—and he took from its deepest shelf the silver tray.

It was the last of the workshop's silver. There had been candlesticks once, and a chased ewer, and a set of twelve spoons with the guild mark of Vellbruck binders on the handles, sold off one by one through the autumn as coal got expensive and work got scarce. The tray remained because the tray was the oldest thing in the shop older than Ambrose himself, and because Ambrose had a theory, stated often, that a workshop should keep one beautiful object it cannot afford, as ballast for the soul. Odile had rolled her eyes at the theory for years. Watching him carry the tray now, watching him set it on the bench between himself and the clerk with the tenderness of a man laying down a weapon, she understood that he had been keeping it for exactly this, or for something shaped exactly like this. Ballast for the soul. Spent at last, at need.

"Solid," Ambrose said. "Two and a quarter pounds, Vellbruck assay. Melted down in any border town, it will keep a man in bread for two years and in lies for ten."

Wexler reached for it. Ambrose did not pick it up or pull it away; he simply rested two fingers on the rim, and the clerk's hand stopped, and the whole transaction hung there in the smell of glue and gin.

"But I want to trade fairly," Ambrose said. "And a fair trade means you know what you are buying silence about. All of it."

"Ambrose." Odile's voice came out sharper than she intended. She softened nothing after. "Don't."

"Somebody must," he said. "Or he'll spend the rest of his life imagining worse, and men who imagine worse eventually go looking." He turned back to Wexler, and Odile watched her master do the bravest thing she had ever seen him do, which was considerable, given the competition. "Pell. Nineteen years you kept the chancery's registers. Do you remember the supplementary roll? The one kept apart from the main ledger—not in the chancery safe, not in the archive, but bound in plain calf and stored, let us say, in a place a clever man might guess?"

Odile's stomach dropped through the floorboards and kept going.

Wexler frowned. The frown of a man assembling something. "There were rumors of a second register. We all heard rumors. Sympathizers. Safehouses. Names the main ledger never held." He looked up. "It doesn't exist. The republic fell before—"

"It exists," Ambrose said gently. "It has existed for eleven years. I wrote perhaps a third of it myself."

The clock in the square struck the half hour, muffled by snow. Nobody moved.

"Why," Wexler said. It came out very small. "Why tell me that? Now I'm—now that's another thing I know—"

"Because your name is in it."

The words went into the room like a needle through cloth, taking a thread behind them. Odile watched Wexler's face do several things in sequence—denial arriving first, smooth and automatic, then the faster collapse of it, because Pell Wexler had spent nineteen years around records and he knew a true entry when he heard one. He knew the way a ledger thought. He knew that a network which recorded its sympathizers would record *all* of them, and he knew—he must have known, all these years, in whatever cellar of himself he kept such knowledge—that he had been useful to the republic in ways a desk clerk is useful. Passes stamped. Routes confirmed. One night in the third winter of the resistance, a certain sealed pouch that he logged as blank paper and was not.

"I passed things," he whispered. "Little things. I never—everyone passed little things. The miller. The ferry widow. Half the town passed *little things*—"

"And every one of those names is written down," Ambrose said, "in a hand you would recognize, in a book the empire would very much like to own. Which is why, Pell, the book must never reach the far bank. And why it must never be found to have been forged—because a forgery discovered means a search conducted, and a search conducted means that book found, and that book found means tribunals, yes, but not the ones you're imagining. Not for them." He lifted his fingers from the tray. "For everyone whose name is in it. Including yours. Including—" his eyes flicked to Odile, brief and apologetic and unbearably fond, "—including hers, twice, under two names, one of them belonging to a dead woman."

Wexler sat very still. Then he began, quietly, to cry. It was not dramatic. It was the crying of a man who has run out of brandy's anesthesia and must feel the wound at last, and it was somehow worse for its mildness. He cried the way he had presumably done everything in his life: neatly, at a desk, hoping not to disturb anyone.

"I didn't want to betray anyone," he said. "I want you to know that. I want it known that I didn't. I only wanted to not be hanged. Is that so much? Is that so much to want, at my age?"

"No," Odile said.

Both men looked at her. She realized it was the first thing she had said since handing him tea, and that she meant it completely, and that meaning it completely made her, in Ambrose's phrase, implicated to the eyebrows. No clean hands. She looked down at her own—stained at the knuckle with aged ink, nicked by blades, flexible with craft—and thought that her sister had died with cleaner hands and had not used them half so well.

"No," she said again. "It isn't much to want. It's what everyone in this room wants." She slid the tray toward Wexler. "Take it. Cross the border in spring. Go somewhere no tribunal has heard of. And when historians come asking—and they will come, Pell, empires fall, it is the one law that never fails—you were never here, and you never saw paper bought off the daybook, and you never heard an old man confess to a murder of ink."

Wexler took the tray with both hands, the way you take a sleeping child, or a verdict. He stood, swayed, steadied himself on the good bench. At the door he paused, and Odile braced for gratitude, which is the most dangerous thing a frightened man carries.

Instead he said: "The colonel's aide came to the chancery yesterday. They're verifying the archive ahead of schedule. Verification begins Monday." He pulled his collar up against the cold. "I would finish whatever it is you're not doing in this shop. Quickly."

The door closed behind him. The snow outside took his footsteps almost at once, as snow does, as it takes everything, eventually, and gives it back in spring.

* * *

Odile stood a long moment looking at the empty spot on the shelf where the tray had lived her whole apprenticeship. The dust shadow it left was crisp and pale, a perfect negative of the thing, and she thought absurdly that this was what a name in a ledger was—a dust shadow of a person, an outline that proved something had stood somewhere, once, long enough to matter.

"He's right," she said at last. "About the schedule."

"He's right about most things when he's terrified. It's the only time he's ever honest." Ambrose lowered himself back onto the stool, and this time Odile did help him, taking his elbow, feeling the bird-light fragility of it, hating it. "Three days, then. Perhaps fewer, if the colonel is ambitious."

"Ambrose." She crouched beside him so their eyes were level. "The second register. Where?"

He smiled. It was the smile he wore when a student finally asked the question a lesson had been aimed at for weeks. "There is no second register, child. There never was. But he doesn't know that, and now he never will, and the fear of it will hold him tighter than any chain." He patted her hand, once, with fingers that trembled. "Guilt is the strongest adhesive known to our craft. Stronger than hide glue. Stronger than regret."

"That was cruel."

"That was mercy," Ambrose said, "wearing cruelty's coat, which is the only way mercy travels in wartime." He turned back to the bench, to the false ledger lying there with its spine cracked and re-sewn, its pages tea-aged and smoke-kissed, its gilt awaiting the tool. Three days. Perhaps fewer. "Now. Bring me the finishing leather and the heated brass, and we will make a book so beautiful that no colonel alive will dare believe it a lie. And afterward—" his voice caught, just slightly, on the afterward, and recovered, "—afterward you will make me the headband I showed you. Silk over cord. Two colors. The blue of the river in March. Because a book without a proper headband is a lie even when it tells the truth, and I intend for this one to be honest about everything except its contents."

Odile fetched the leather. Outside, the snow kept falling on the bridge, on the clock tower, on the crate in the archive room that held a book full of dust shadows, and inside the bindery the kettle began, very softly, to sing.
