## 16. Finishing the Work

Three days remained, and Odile counted them the way a miser counts coins that are already promised to someone else.

She had made a list on the back of a customer's receipt—pencil, small letters, folded twice—and then she had burned the list, because lists were how people got hanged, and because by now she knew it by heart anyway. Gilt for the spine bands. The seal press. The tea-staining of the last twelve folios. Thread to match the chancery's peculiar waxy red. And headbands, which Ambrose insisted upon even now, even with the century dying outside the window.

"You cannot send a ledger into the world naked at the head and tail," he said, from his chair by the stove, where he had taken up semi-permanent residence like a cat who had decided the spot was his by ancient right. "A book is judged first by its ends, girl. Everyone looks at the cover. Only the wise look at the headbands."

"The empire is not wise."

"The empire employs clerks," Ambrose said, "and clerks are paid to be wise about very small things. Small things are where forgeries die." He held up one hand—it shook now, constantly, a fine tremor like a tuning fork someone had struck and never damped—and watched it with an expression of scholarly disappointment, as though it belonged to a student of his who had failed an examination. "I cannot hold a needle anymore. So you will do the headbands, and I will tell you everything wrong with them, and you will hate me, and they will be perfect."

"That isn't how hatred works."

"It is exactly how apprenticeship works," said Ambrose, with such satisfaction that Odile threw a scrap of marbled paper at him. He caught it, which surprised them both, and looked quietly pleased with himself for the rest of the morning.

* * *

The mornings belonged to the shop. That was the bargain with daylight: soldiers tramping through for their requisitioned bread and their requisitioned soup and their requisitioned novels mended with requisitioned thread, and Odile smiling her forgettable smile, hands busy, eyes down, while behind the false wall of the drying cabinet the true ledger's last folios waited in a stack thin as a hymnal.

The afternoons belonged to the forgery.

The evenings, when the curfew bell went and the town went dark street by street like lamps being blown out in a long room, belonged to the kettle.

She had developed a rhythm, almost a liturgy. First the names. She would take a folio from the stack—each one holding perhaps twenty entries, names in the republic's meticulous clerk-hand, ticks and marginal codes and safehouse addresses rendered as innocuous-looking numbers—and she would read every name aloud, once, slowly, the way Ambrose had taught her. Not to memorize, he had been careful to say. To witness. A thing spoken aloud by one living mouth could not simply vanish; it had been *entered*, somewhere other than paper.

"Anselm Votek, ferryman, the third house past the weir. His wife knows nothing, mark the tick. Ludmilla Ferrun, midwife—" and so on down the page, her voice low and level, steam rising off the glue kettle where she would feed the folio afterward, torn into pieces the size of communion bread. The kettle ate the names and breathed them out as a sweetish animal smell of hide and heat, and the ash collected in the hearth beneath, ordinary as any winter's ash, deniable as any winter's ash.

At night she recited them again, lying in her narrow bed above the workshop, walking herself through the day's names the way other people said their prayers. Some nights it was a burden so heavy she could hardly breathe under it. Some nights—and this frightened her more than the patrols did—it was a comfort, a rope of names she was hauling hand over hand out of a dark river, and she did not want to reach the end of it, because at the end there would be quiet.

* * *

On the second-to-last day they gilded.

Ambrose propped himself at the bench on three cushions and directed operations with the air of a general who had lost the use of his legs but not his maps. The spine of the false ledger needed five raised bands, and the bands needed gilt, and gilt needed heat, and heat needed patience, and patience was nearly gone from the world.

"Warmer," Ambrose said. "The tool must hiss. Not spit. Hiss."

"It's spitting."

"Because you are rushing. Gold leaf forgives nothing and forgets less. It is the most honest material we work with, which is why governments love it so much on their seals." He watched her lay the leaf with the tip of a wetted knife, breathing through her mouth the way he'd taught her eight years ago over a ruined Book of Hours. "There. Now breathe normally. You are not defusing anything. You are making something beautiful, which is harder, and also the only reason anyone has ever defused anything."

The gold went down clean. Under the burnisher it bloomed, sudden and absurd, a small sunrise on the spine of a lie. Odile sat back and found she was grinning like an idiot.

"There," said Ambrose softly. "That's the whole trade, right there. Everything else is glue."

They tooled the republic's arms into the boards next—the double tower and open gate, the old mark that half the border towns still carved over their doorways out of habit, though the habit was now treasonous. Ambrose talked her through each pass of the fillet blind, his eyes shut, calling corrections from memory: *a hair left, pressure, pressure, lift.* The seal came up sharp and whole. Then, last of all, the warm brass die, heated at the stove's mouth, pressed into its bed of sizing with both their hands on the handle, his shaking ones laid over her steady ones, so that between them the tremor canceled and the impression fell perfectly true.

The republic's seal, warm under their palms, smelling of size and scorch.

"Well," Ambrose said, after a while. "Someone should say something official. We are, after all, the last functioning organ of the government of this republic." He cleared his throat with tremendous ceremony. "By virtue of the authority vested in two binders and one kettle, this document is hereby declared authentic, binding, and entirely false. God save whoever reads it."

"Ambrose."

"I have wanted to say something official my entire life," he said. "You could at least let me enjoy the moment before you take offense at it."

* * *

It was that evening, feeding the last-but-one folio into the kettle, that he collapsed.

There was no drama to it. That was the terrible part, Odile thought later—the way the worst moments arrive looking like nothing. He had insisted on being carried to the bench for the burning, because he had written some of these names himself, in another woman's handwriting, and he would witness them on his feet or not at all. So he stood with his knuckles white on the bench edge and recited the entries with her, his voice thinner now, fraying at the ends of words, and when the folio was gone he reached for the next one and his legs simply stopped being consulted.

Odile caught him under the arms and got him onto the daybed by the stove, and his face was gray as unsized paper, and for one long moment she thought the whole plan had just died on a bindery floor with three days left and no master.

But he came back to her by degrees, the way he always did, grumbling back into his body like a man returning to a cold house. And when he could speak, what he said was:

"Headbands."

"What?"

"Your headbands." His eyes found hers, unfocused but ferocious. "Silk, two colors, the chancery uses a twisted core—you've seen it on their commission volumes. You wind it around a core of the same cord, not cord and thread mixed, they can *tell*—Odile. Odile, look at me. Two colors. Twisted core. Pull the turn-ins snug but never tight, a tight headband will crack a spine in ten years, and that ledger must survive ten years if it's going to survive one week—"

"I know. I know. I've done fifty headbands."

"Not like this one." He gripped her wrist with a strength that astonished her, coming from somewhere below the grayness. "Everything you have ever bound was practice. Every hymnal, every prayer book, every farmer's account book. Your whole life was rehearsal for this one object. Do you understand? Bind it like it's the only book in the world. Because tonight, it is."

Then his grip gentled, and his voice dropped to almost nothing, and he said: "And forgive me. For Marta's name. For the letters I wrote in her hand. I told myself it was for the network. That was true. But it was also because writing her made her alive again for an hour at a time, and I was a coward about burying her properly, and cowardice in a good cause is still cowardice. Forgive me. Not because I deserve it. Because carrying it will be heavier than carrying the names."

Odile knelt by the daybed with her forehead against his knuckles and could not speak at all for a while. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere across the square a patrol laughed at something.

"Yes," she managed at last. "Yes. But you're going to live to see me do the headbands badly at least once, so you can correct me."

"Two colors," Ambrose murmured, already sinking. "Twisted core." And then, from the very lip of sleep, with the ghost of his usual mischief: "Everyone looks at the cover. Only the wise..."

He did not wake again that night.

* * *

The doctor came in the morning—a real one, bribed with the promise of a rebound pharmacopoeia, led in through the alley door—and examined Ambrose and pronounced the word everyone already knew, which was *soon*, and prescribed rest and broth and quiet, and Odile signed for it all with a steady hand because her hands had stopped being able to afford trembling.

Ambrose woke briefly in the afternoon, took stock of himself with the frank appraisal of a craftsman inspecting flawed materials, and issued his final production schedule.

"You'll case in tomorrow night," he said. "Not tonight. Tonight you rest, or your hands will betray you at the moment that matters. Tomorrow night, the covering. Leather you've already pared. Paste, not glue, paste is forgiving. Work slow. The hours will seem long. Good. Long hours make careful ones." He paused, and something in his face eased, and for a moment he looked young, or rather he looked the age he actually was instead of the age his failing body had rushed him into. "I have taught eleven apprentices. Ten of them learned binding. One of them learned why. Do you know how rare that is, Odile? Whole cities full of people go their lives without learning why."

"Don't," she said. "Save it. Say it when the crate is sealed."

"Ah," said Ambrose gently. "We do not always get to choose the timing of our remarks. That is why one should say true things early." He closed his eyes. "Broth, if you please. If I am to die in the middle of a masterpiece I intend to complain about it with a full stomach."

* * *

He lasted until an hour past midnight.

She was at the bench when it happened—she would always be grateful for that, that she was working when it came, that grief arrived and found her already doing the thing. She heard the small change in his breathing from across the room, the way you hear snow begin, and she crossed to the daybed, and held his hand, and he did not wake, and it was quick, and it was quiet, and then the only sound in the bindery was the stove and the river.

She gave herself one hour. She measured it by the clock, because that was the trade she had learned best: measure everything. One hour to sit on the floor beside the daybed with her master's hand going cool in hers and weep the way she had not wept at Marta's funeral because someone had to stay forgettable even then. One hour, and when the church bells marked it, she rose, covered him neatly with the good blanket, opened the window to the winter dark so the room would keep, washed her face in cold water, and went back to the bench.

The leather waited, pared whisper-thin. The paste was mixed. The false ledger—the last true thing Ambrose Verloc ever helped make, and the most elaborate lie either of them had ever built—lay covered in its damp-cloth like a patient under anesthesia.

"All right," Odile said aloud, to the bench, to the stove, to the man under the blanket, to the hundred and forty names walking the walls of her skull. "Two colors. Twisted core."

She worked.

The hours behaved exactly as Ambrose had promised: they stretched, they slowed, they became enormous, and inside their enormity her hands moved with a calm she did not feel anywhere else in her body. Paste the board. Lay the leather. Turn the edges—snug, never tight. Corners, mitred, tucked, pressed. Then the spine, band by band, the leather drawn down into the channels with the bone folder, and the folder whispered along each groove and she heard his voice in it, correcting, encouraging, insufferable.

Somewhere past three o'clock she caught herself talking to him, giving a running commentary on the work in a low voice, the way he used to narrate demonstrations. *"Now the headbands, class. Observe how the apprentice does not rush. Observe how she winds the silk true, two colors, twisted core, because a certain pedantic old man threatened to haunt her."* The stove ticked. The kettle sat cold and empty for the first time in weeks, its work done, its ash banked in the hearth below, one hundred and forty names sleeping in it under a skin of grey.

At the headbands her hands did not shake at all.

She finished casing-in as the sky behind the river hills went from black to iron to the color of a fresh-cut text block. She put the finished volume under weights—boards and bricks, the same humble tyranny that had pressed every honest book this shop ever made—and she cleaned the bench, and put away the tools in their proper places, because Ambrose had beaten that into her in the first month eight years ago and death did not excuse disorder.

Then she sat down on the floor beside the daybed again, in the grey light, with the false ledger pressing itself true under its bricks behind her and her master's blanket rising and falling no longer, and she recited the names. All of them. From Anselm the ferryman to the last entry burned, every one, clear and unhurried, entered somewhere other than paper.

When she finished, the sun came over the hills and lit the gold on the ledger's spine through the window, so that the whole bench seemed briefly on fire.

And that was when the knocking started—heavy, military, far too early, fists on the alley door and then the front, and a voice she knew calling her name.

Colonel's aides did not knock at dawn for mended novels.
