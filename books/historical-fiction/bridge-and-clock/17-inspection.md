## 17. Inspection

The telegram arrived at half past six in the morning, and Captain Aldric Verren read it three times while his tea went cold beside him on the chancery desk.

*Inspection party departs Kreln at first light. Expect arrival Vellbruck by noon. Col. E. Marchetti, Office of Archives and Provenance. Full verification of seized materials prior to crossing. No exceptions.*

No exceptions. He set the telegram down and looked out the window at the square, where the frost had turned the cobblestones the color of old pewter. The bridge stood in the middle distance, its scaffolding wrapped in tarps, engineers already moving over it like ants over a dropped sweet.

They were two days early. Two days in which Odile Nystrom was supposed to have finished the headbands.

Verren discovered, somewhat to his own surprise, that his hands were steady. He had expected them to shake. For a week now he had been carrying the knowledge of the bindery inside him like a coin in a boot—uncomfortable, always present, shifting when he walked—and he had assumed that when the moment of consequence finally came, it would rattle loose. But no. His hands were steady, and his tea was cold, and a colonel of the Office of Archives and Provenance would be standing in this room by noon asking to see thread counts.

He rang for his orderly and asked, very calmly, for fresh tea and for Sergeant Hobb.

Hobb arrived with his helmet under his arm and the expression of a man who had been woken twice in one morning and intended to bear a grudge about both occasions equally.

"Sergeant. How are you at sweeping?"

"Captain?"

"The chancery floor. All of it. And the archive room especially. If there is so much as a paperclip from the republic's government lying under a shelf, I want it found, catalogued, or binned before eleven o'clock."

Hobb considered this. "Binned or catalogued, sir? There's a difference with paperclips."

"There is indeed, Sergeant, and you have just demonstrated a finer grasp of archival science than most of the Office of Archives and Provenance. Catalogue anything with writing on it. Bin the rest." Verren pulled his greatcoat from the peg. "I'm going to the bindery."

* * *

Odile was already at the bench when he came in, and she knew before he said a word—he could see it land in her face like weather. She was sewing a headband at the spine of the false ledger, silk thread pulled taut between finger and thumb, and she did not stop working while she listened, which struck him as either tremendous discipline or tremendous fear wearing discipline's coat. With her he had learned they were often the same thing.

"When?" she said.

"Noon. A Colonel Marchetti. Archives and Provenance." He unwound his scarf, feeling absurdly domestic doing it in her workshop, among the smells of paste and leather and the low mutter of the glue kettle. "He'll verify against the chancery's inventory note. Every volume, every folio count, and—" He stopped.

"Say it," Odile said. "You've been saying it in your head since you read the telegram. It's better out loud. Everything is better out loud; my master says so, and he taught me to bind books, so he knows something about things that hold together."

"The thread count," Verren said. "The inventory note records the ledger's sewing structure. Eighteen supports, five stations. If the forgery differs even by one—"

"It doesn't differ." She set down her needle and looked at him directly, and he thought, not for the first time, that her sister had had the same way of looking at a man as though he were a page worth reading. "Ambrose took the true ledger apart twice before we started. We matched it exactly. Eighteen supports, five stations, linen thread, two-ply, unbleached."

"Then there's nothing to worry about."

"There is never nothing to worry about. That is what worry is *for*." She rubbed her hands on her apron. "But if you're telling me a colonel is coming to count threads, then I want to know what kind of man counts threads."

"A careful one."

"I've met careful men. Some of them buy hymnals."

Verren felt the corner of his mouth move despite everything. "Marchetti isn't buying hymnals. He's a technician. They say he once identified a forged land deed because the wax had been impressed with the wrong temperature of seal. He notices things. That is the entire purpose of him."

"Then we have until noon to make sure there's nothing to notice."

"There is one thing." He hesitated, and then made himself go on, because he had decided somewhere between the cold tea and the cold street that he was finished with half-truths where this woman was concerned—or at least finished with the ones that mattered. "If he finds a discrepancy, I can't explain it away. Not this time. An inspection isn't an inquiry; there's no fog to hide in. It's arithmetic. Either the ledger matches the inventory or it doesn't."

"Then the inventory should match the ledger," Odile said, as simply as if she were recommending a change of glue.

And Verren stood there in the warm workshop with paste fumes rising around him and understood that she had just handed him the whole of it—that whatever he was going to be for the rest of his life was being decided now, in front of a kettle and a woman with thread under her fingernails, and that she had known it before he did.

"You're suggesting I alter an official document," he said.

"I'm suggesting," said Odile, picking up her needle again, "that a man who has already decided which law he serves shouldn't flinch at the paperwork. My sister used to say that. About other things entirely, but it applies."

She bent to her work. He watched her hands for a moment—the small economy of them, the way the silk went where it was told—and then he put his scarf back on and went to find the chancery's inventory book.

* * *

Colonel Emilio Marchetti arrived at twelve minutes past noon, which was punctual enough to be intimidating and late enough to be insulting, and Verren suspected both effects were deliberate. He was a small, dry man with spectacles cleaned so frequently they had worn faint polish-marks at the bridge of the nose, and he carried a leather case containing, presumably, instruments of torture of a purely documentary nature.

"Captain Verren." They saluted. Marchetti's eyes went past Verren's shoulder into the chancery interior and began, immediately and without apparent effort, to catalogue it. "You've kept the archive sealed?"

"Since requisition. Two seals broken for transport preparation, both witnessed. Sergeant Hobb can confirm."

"Hobb. Yes. He swept the floor this morning. Unusual initiative in a garrison sergeant." Marchetti removed one glove, finger by finger. "Or unusual instructions."

"The Office of Archives and Provenance announces a visit, Colonel, and floors get swept. It's a law of nature, like water finding low ground."

Something that might have been amusement moved briefly behind the spectacles and was gone. "Show me the ledger."

* * *

The crate stood in the archive room amid the ordered chaos of the seized collection—folios in crates, maps in tubes, the republic's entire institutional memory stacked and stenciled and waiting for the bridge. Verren pried the lid with Hobb's help, and Marchetti leaned over the crate and looked down at the false ledger for a long, silent minute during which Verren became intimately acquainted with his own pulse.

It was a very well-made object. He could see that now, even untrained as he was. Odile had aged the pages with tea and smoke until they had the soft, exhausted color of documents that had been anxious for years, and the republic's seal sat pressed into the opening leaf with the weary authority of the dead government itself. Somewhere across town, the true names of two hundred people were ash in a glue kettle, and here lay their beautiful, blameless ghost.

Marchetti lifted the ledger out with both hands, the way one lifts a bird.

"Chancery hand," he murmured. "Third clerk, I'd say—the loop on the capital letters. Interesting that they kept the master record here rather than evacuating it with the rest." He opened it. He turned pages. He held one up to the window light and examined the watermark, and Verren watched him do it and thought, distantly: *this is how it ends, then, in a man squinting at paper.* "Watermark's right. Kreln mill, pre-war stock. Whoever packed this archive didn't substitute modern paper." He looked up. "Your inventory note, Captain."

Verren had the book ready. He had had it ready for four hours. He handed it over with the calm of a man handing over a hymnal.

Marchetti read the entry. Then he opened the ledger to its spine, tilted it against the light, and counted.

Verren counted too, silently, along with him. Eighteen supports. Five stations. Exactly as the inventory said, because at four o'clock that morning, alone in the chancery with a pen and a candle, Aldric Verren had copied the original inventory note into fresh ink and written *eighteen* where the chancery clerk's hurried hand had recorded *twenty*, and burned the old slip in his stove, and sat afterward for a long moment watching it curl, feeling absolutely nothing, which was itself the most frightening sensation he had ever experienced.

"Eighteen supports," Marchetti said. "Five stations." He straightened. "Consistent."

"Yes, sir."

"Mm." Marchetti closed the ledger, and then, instead of returning it to the crate, he held it a moment longer, weighing it, and said in a different voice—a quieter one, almost conversational—"There was a bindery in this town. Before the annexation. On the Tanner's Row side. My mother bought prayer books there."

Verren's heart performed a maneuver he declined to acknowledge.

"Nystrom's," he said. "It's still there. The old master's ill, but the trade continues."

"Does it." Marchetti slid the ledger back into the crate and looked at it lying there among its fellows, and for a moment his dry little face was unreadable in a way that made Verren's palms sweat inside his gloves. Then the colonel snapped the case shut on his instruments and said, "Seal it. The manifest goes in my case, the crate goes under guard, and it crosses tonight with the column. I'll want your signature verifying condition at loading, Captain. You'll be responsible for it to the far bank."

"Yes, sir."

"And Verren." Marchetti paused at the door, one glove still off, looking back at the crate with what appeared to be mild distaste. "In thirty years of this work I have learned that archives tell the truth about governments and lies about people, and that the difference is rarely anyone's business but God's." He put the glove on. "Carry it carefully."

He left. Hobb, who had been holding his breath with military discipline for approximately ten minutes, exhaled and asked if he should nail the lid down.

"Yes, Sergeant," Verren said. "Nail it down. Nail everything down."

* * *

The sealing was done properly, wax and cord and two witnesses, and the crate went onto a gun carriage under guard at dusk, and Verren signed the manifest in the blue-gray light with the clock tower looming over the square like a chaperone. The numbers on its face had begun, faintly, to gather themselves toward midnight. Two days early, the schedule had been moved; the column would cross at the turn of the year whether Vellbruck liked it or not, and the whole town seemed to know it, shops shuttering early, smoke standing straight up from the chimneys in the windless cold as if even the fires were holding their breath.

He walked back past the bindery. He had not intended to; his feet had simply made the decision, the way feet do. Through the window he saw lamplight, and Odile's shadow moving against the wall, and the smaller, stooped shadow beside her that was Ambrose, gesturing at something on the bench with the fussy precision of a man correcting a student long after correction has become unnecessary. Teaching headbands, probably. Or forgiveness. With those two it was hard to say where one ended and the other began.

He did not knock. There would be time for knocking tomorrow, or there wouldn't, and either way the work was done and sealed under guard and rolling toward the bridge on a gun carriage, and he had signed his name beneath it in ink that would not wash out. He waited all night for regret to arrive. It sent word instead that it would be along presently, after midnight, and might be some time.

At the door of his quarters he stopped. Down in the square, faintly, the clock tower cleared its throat and struck nine.

Three hours, more or less, until the century ended and the column massed and the crate crossed the span with its beautiful lie inside. Three hours, and somewhere in the bindery a courier who only ever carried things was surely deciding, right now, what she would carry next.

Verren went in, poured himself the tea he would not drink, and began composing, in his head, the fabricated order he was not yet sure he would have the courage to speak aloud.
