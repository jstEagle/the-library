## 18. The Last Delivery

The flaw announced itself the way flaws always did in Odile's experience: quietly, at an hour when no one had the strength to fight it.

It came from Verren, of all people—Verren, standing in the bindery doorway on the twenty-ninth of December with snow melting off his shoulders and a look on his face that Odile had learned to read the way she read watermarks. Not the officer's face. The other one, the one he wore like an ill-fitting coat he kept trying to take off.

"The crate will be opened again," he said, without preamble, without greeting. "On the far bank."

Ambrose looked up from where he sat wrapped in two blankets by the stove, looking smaller every day, like a book whose boards were slowly warping. "Opened."

"Standard procedure for diplomatic archives. There's a records officer at Fort Halmstad—Colonel Dreyse—who verifies every seal personally. He'll count folios. He'll compare thread. He'll hold pages up to the light." Verren's jaw worked. "I've seen him do it. He found a forged customs ledger once because the ink was three months too fresh. A *week*, Mistress Nystrom. Perhaps less. Then they come back across the bridge for everyone who touched it, and they do not come back politely."

The kettle sat cold on its hook. Three days ago it had held the last true folios; the hearth still smelled faintly of scorched iron-gall ink, a smell Odile had come to think of as the smell of names.

"So it was all for nothing," she said. Her voice came out flat, and she was surprised at how little despair there was in it. Just arithmetic. She had been doing arithmetic since the troops arrived.

"Not nothing," Ambrose said mildly. "Nothing would have been leaving it intact."

But his hands were folded in his lap, and Odile knew that look too—the look of a craftsman examining a joint he'd glued wrong and knowing the whole board would have to come apart again.

* * *

They spent the afternoon of the twenty-ninth not solving the problem, which is sometimes how problems get solved. Odile trimmed endpapers for a job she might never finish. Ambrose dozed by the stove and woke to correct her paring angle out of pure habit. Outside, the soldiers went about their occupation the way weather goes about a season—indifferent, pervasive, occasionally violent—and somewhere across town the chancery clerk was presumably drinking himself toward the new year with the enthusiasm of a man who expected it to be his last.

It was near dusk when Odile set down her bone folder and said, "Tell me exactly what happens at the crossing."

Verren, who had stayed—why did he keep staying, why did any of them keep pretending he didn't—straightened against the doorframe.

"At eleven the bridge closes. Civilian traffic stops at ten. The archive cart crosses first, escorted, then the column. On the far bank, the crate goes to Colonel Dreyse before anything else. He opens it under lamplight in the toll house while the column waits." Verren paused, and Odile watched him arrive at the same thought she'd been circling all day. "While the column waits. Which means whatever happens to the crate happens in front of forty officers and two thousand men who are standing around in the cold wishing they were anywhere else."

"Yes," Odile said. "That."

"You're not suggesting—"

"I'm a courier," she said. It felt strange and enormous to say it aloud, after years of saying the opposite. *I only carry things. I don't know what I carry.* "Do you know what the first thing anyone asks when a document goes missing? They ask who had access. And the second thing they ask is whether anyone *saw* it destroyed. Because a missing ledger is a question forever. But a ledger that burned in front of witnesses—" She turned the bone folder over in her hands. "A question with a body buried under it stops being asked."

"That's grim," said Ambrose from his blankets, with evident approval.

"It's bookbinding logic," Odile said. "You don't hide a repair. You make the repair so beautiful nobody thinks to look for the damage underneath."

Verren had gone very still. "What you're describing is walking onto a military span carrying a forged state document past your own escort, and throwing it into a gorge in front of Colonel Dreyse."

"I'm describing a delivery," Odile said. "The last one. It's what I'm for."

* * *

She expected him to refuse. She had prepared arguments the way she prepared sewing stations—all night, in the dark, laying everything out in order. But Verren only stood there while the light failed, and finally he said, "They'll search you at the bridgehead."

"Let them. The forgery should be found on me. That's rather the point."

"And if Dreyse doesn't believe the story?"

"Then I hang for arson and forgery," Odile said, "and every name I memorized stays memorized, and the ash in that hearth says what I say it says, because there'll be no other version left. Captain—Aldric." His name felt strange in her mouth, Marta's old ghost moving through it. "There is no plan anymore in which I am safe. There's only a plan in which everyone else is."

He looked at her for a long moment. Snow ticked against the window glass. Somewhere upstairs a soldier dropped a boot.

"Marta used to argue like that," he said at last, very quietly. "Like the argument was already won and everyone else just needed catching up."

"She got it from our mother, who got it from a milliner who could out-stubborn a bishop."

Something moved across his face—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile lived. "All right," he said. "Here is what I can do. At midnight the column halts for the year-turn. Tradition, since the annexation—no troop moves across the old year into the new. Ninety seconds, maybe more, while the officers take their watches out and pretend they're not sentimental. If a courier came onto the span during the halt claiming to carry a recovered volume from the chancery—a volume the army believed already destroyed—"

"—then whoever carried it would be delivering proof the republic's secrets died in Vellbruck," Odile finished. "In front of the colonel himself."

"And if the carrier happened to drop it," Verren said, "over the rail, into the gorge, in the dark and the crowd and the confusion of a hundred drums—well. Accidents happen at year-turn. People drink. Rails are icy."

"A tragic loss of irreplaceable documents," Ambrose said, "witnessed by two thousand men who will each tell the story differently for the rest of their lives. By spring it will be legend. Legends don't get audited."

Odile looked between them—the dying master and the traitor captain—and felt something loosen in her chest that had been tight since the water-damaged pouch arrived nearly a year ago. Fear, probably. Or its opposite. With her they had always felt similar.

"There's one more thing," she said. "If I'm going to stand in front of Colonel Dreyse and lie with my whole body, I need to know the ledger better than he does. Every quirk. Where the ink blotches on folio sixty-one. That the entries after March change hands twice. You've seen the original inventory notes—tell me everything Dreyse will check, and I'll tell you whether my binding answers."

* * *

They worked through the evening, and it was the strangest lesson Odile had ever given or received: the three of them bent over the false ledger by lamplight, the conspirator teaching the forger how to be caught convincingly.

"Open it to the middle," Verren said. "He always opens to the middle first. Thorough men are predictable men—it's how you survive them."

Odile opened to the middle. The aged pages gave their tea-and-smoke smell, soft at the edges, foxed in the corners just enough. She had sewn them on double raised cords, matched the watermark by holding stock up against the last surviving blank sheet of chancery paper until the lantern light showed the fleur through both at once. She had pressed the republic's seal warm, the way Ambrose had taught her thirty years of masters had done it, wax slightly proud at the lower left where the seal matrix always caught.

"He'll tilt it toward the lamp," Verren murmured, watching her handle it. "Looking for ghost impressions from facing pages."

"There aren't any. I interleaved with felt during pressing."

"He'll count the gatherings."

"Sixteen signatures of twelve leaves, with the last two leaves of the final gathering blank, because the clerk who kept it stopped writing in October and never filled the rest." She said it steadily, though saying it made her throat close, because the real ledger had ended the same way—had simply run out of names, or the clerk had run out of stomach, and the blank leaves at the end had always struck Odile as the most frightening part of the whole book. All that room for what came next.

Ambrose had drifted half asleep, but he surfaced for this. "Show him the headbands," he rasped. "Dreyse was chancery-trained before the army took him. Chancery men check headbands. It's the first thing we teach and the last thing anyone fakes properly."

Odile turned the spine toward the light. The silk headbands were worked in the republic's two colors over a core of rolled paper, tied off in the old style, with the little doubled knot at top and bottom that Vellbruck binders alone used—Ambrose's own invention, forty years old, copied by every apprentice he'd ever trained including the clerk whose drunken terror had nearly undone them all.

Verren studied it a long time. When he looked up, his eyes were wet, and he pretended they weren't, and Odile let him.

"My sister bought her prayer book here," he said. "Marta teased her about the knot. Said it looked like a fly caught in silk." He cleared his throat. "It's perfect. It's so perfect it frightens me."

"Frightens me too," Odile said. "Good. I'll want the fear tomorrow. Fear keeps the hands honest."

* * *

She slept two hours and woke at four with the litany running in her head, as it did every night now—the names, all of them, recited in order like beads on a string. *Anselm Voss, ferryman. The Widow Kreutz, mill road. Brother Emeric, who keeps the school register.* She lay in the dark above the workshop and said them through twice, and then she got up, because there was one thing left to prepare and it wasn't the book.

It was herself.

Downstairs, by the banked stove, she took out the plain gray cloak she wore for deliveries and shook the sawdust out of its hem. She brushed her hair and pinned it plainly, the way a tired courier would. She practiced, in the black mirror of the unlit window, the face she would wear: not innocent—inno­cence would ring false—but harried, dutiful, slightly resentful of the cold, a woman ordered about by men at the end of a long occupation, carrying one more wretched thing across one more bridge.

"You'll want to complain," Ambrose said from his corner, making her jump. She hadn't known he was awake.

"Complain?"

"To Dreyse. About being sent out at midnight. Complaining is the most convincing thing a person can do. Nobody suspects the grumbling." He coughed, long and hard, and Odile knelt beside him and held the cup while he drank. When he could speak again he said, "I wanted to teach you gilding. Proper gilding, gold leaf and the agate burnisher. We never got to it."

"There's time," Odile lied gently.

"There isn't, and we both know it, so listen instead." His hand found hers, papery and warm. "Whatever happens on that span—whatever they do, whatever you have to say to save yourself—you did not fail anyone. Not one name in that kettle. Not Marta. Do you hear me? Craftsmen ruin work by loving the work too much to finish it. Finish it, Odile. Let go at the twelfth stroke. Don't hold on."

"I never hold on," she said. "Holding on is for people with things."

"You have two thousand names," Ambrose said, "and a shelf full of blank books, and a century coming that hasn't read them yet. That's not nothing to hold. That's why you have to practice letting go."

* * *

New Year's Eve arrived gray and windless, the kind of cold that made the river smoke. All day the town heard the army gathering: boots on the frost, wagons groaning, the trumpeters warming up badly in the square. By late afternoon Odile could see from the workshop window the head of the column forming along the quay, torches being lit one by one like a fever spreading through the streets.

At nine, Verren came for the ledger.

He wore his dress uniform, and Odile understood suddenly that he had dressed for his own trial. He stood in the middle of the bindery among the presses and the drying racks and looked around at everything—at Ambrose asleep in his blankets, at the cold kettle, at the empty bench with its worn hollows—and Odile watched him memorize it, the way she memorized routes. Some things you carried even when you couldn't take them with you.

"The crate's loaded," he said. "Cart's at the bridgehead. At quarter to eleven, a corporal named Brack will escort you from the chancery steps to the span. He thinks you're a chancery clerk bringing a recovered volume on Colonel Dreyse's own signed requisition—which I wrote tonight, and which will be discovered to be a forgery sometime next week, by which time I intend to be inconveniently transferred." He held out his hand, palm up.

Odile picked up the ledger.

It weighed almost nothing. That was the terrible joke of it—months of fear, a man's life's craft, a dead woman's alias, a kettle full of burned names, and the thing itself weighed hardly more than a hymnal. She had bound books heavier. She had carried pouches heavier. She had carried, for years, the knowledge of her own sister's name in a stranger's hand, and that had weighed more than any crate.

"Headbands straight?" she asked, handing it over—one last inspection, one last ritual.

"Straight as truth," Verren said, and then winced at his own words, and gave it back.

* * *

At quarter to eleven she stepped out of the bindery for the last time and turned toward the bridge, and behind her the workshop sat dark and honest, its hearth full of ash that would tell the truth to anyone who knelt and looked.

The streets were emptied of everyone but soldiers. Torches threw her shadow long ahead of her, so that she seemed to be walking toward a taller, braver version of herself waiting at the bridgehead. The clock tower loomed against the stars, its face lit, its hands crawling toward the year's end. Under her arm, the false ledger rode warm from the stove-warmed room, and inside her, in the vault Ambrose had built out of her memory, two thousand names stood in their ranks, patient, ready, hers to keep.

A corporal with red ears fell in beside her without a word. Ahead, the bridge stretched black over the smoking gorge, closed at both ends, hung with lanterns, waiting.

The clock tower began, softly, to strike.

Odile Nystrom walked toward the sound, choosing, at last, what she carried.
