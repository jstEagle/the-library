## 19. Midnight on the Span

**Odile**

The last day of the century arrived wearing its best uniform.

From the bindery window Odile could see them massing on the far bank: columns of imperial infantry in grey-blue greatcoats, their breath rising in a single low cloud over the river road, artillery teams watering horses at the troughs that had been, until November, the town's own. The engineers had spent three days lashing fresh timber to the bridge's old bones, and now the planking rang differently underfoot—tighter, newer, a stranger's voice in an old body.

Inside the workshop, everything smelled of glue and scorched paper, which was to say it smelled like every other day of her life, and she was grateful for it.

"You'll want to eat," Ambrose said from his cot by the stove. He had not risen since morning. His voice had the papery quality of a man conserving himself for one last reading aloud. "Fainting on a bridge is undignified. Your sister fainted once at the midsummer fair, do you recall? Into the goose pen."

"I recall you laughed so hard you sat in the pie."

"And I have never regretted it." He smiled, then let the smile go, because there was no use carrying what would not fit through the door ahead of her. "The crate is loaded?"

"Since noon. It sits third from the front, behind the paymaster's strongbox and a case of somebody's wine, which I intend to consider an insult."

"It *is* an insult. Wine travels better than history." He watched her check the ledger one final time—the false ledger, her ledger, four hundred pages aged in tea and smoke, sewn on the republic's five-station thread pattern, tooled with the chancery's double-ruled border, the seal pressed warm two nights ago while Ambrose's hands shook too badly to hold the brass. She had made mistakes in her life. This was not going to be one of them.

Under her arm it weighed nothing. That was the trouble with paper. The weight came later.

"The kettle ash is still in the hearth," Ambrose said. "I stirred it this morning. It looks properly incriminating—a few charred corners, a buckle fragment, enough to satisfy any officer who enjoys being satisfied."

"They won't enjoy it."

"No. But they'll accept it, because the alternative is admitting they guarded a fire for six weeks without looking inside it." He coughed, and she pretended not to count how long the cough lasted. "Odile. When the clock starts, don't hurry. Hurrying is how couriers die. Walk as though the century owes you something."

She bent and kissed his forehead, which was cool as a stone in shade. "You owe me a pie."

"Next summer," he said, in the tone of a man settling accounts he knew he could not settle, "I will buy the whole fair."

* * *

**Verren**

Captain Aldric Verren had commanded men through a winter campaign, a retreat, and the annexation paperwork of two provinces, and he had never once been as afraid as he was standing at the bridgehead at eleven forty in the evening, holding a document he had written himself.

It was a good forgery. He was beginning to suspect forgery ran in the air of Vellbruck like river fog.

The order redirected the archive escort: the crate bearing the recovered chancery ledger was to be carried across separately, before the main column, by a single authorized courier, on account of—here he had allowed himself a flourish—"the documented fragility of the contents." Colonel Brandt had signed things all afternoon without reading them; this one Verren had signed himself, in the name of a major who was currently forty miles away drinking someone else's brandy. If it unraveled, it unraveled on him alone. He found, examining the feeling the way he'd once examined troop dispositions, that he did not mind. There was a strange comfort in finally standing somewhere specific.

Below him the river went black between the ice shelves. Above him the clock tower stood against the stars, its face lit by the two lamps the town council still paid for out of habit, though half the council had fled and the other half were in prison or selling flour to the army.

He had told the sergeant at the barrier to expect a woman from the bindery. He had told no one else anything.

*Ninety seconds,* he thought. *That's what I can buy her. After that, whatever happens happens on the span, in front of God, the garrison, and the entire Third Infantry, who will mostly be thinking about supper.*

He checked his watch. Eleven fifty-one.

Somewhere behind him, in the massed dark of the column, a soldier was singing very quietly, some marching song about a girl and a millwheel, and another soldier told him to shut up, and the first soldier said the century was ending, wasn't it, couldn't a man sing at the end of a century, and the second soldier considered this and began singing too.

Verren let them. He was a dutiful man; he had simply, at long last, chosen his duty.

* * *

**Odile**

The barrier sergeant looked at her pass, then at her face, then at the ledger under her arm, and visibly decided that whatever this was, it was above his pay and below his interest.

"Courier for the archive crossing," Odile said. Her voice came out steady. She was almost annoyed at how steady. All those years of keeping her face forgettable, and here at the end it worked perfectly, when what she wanted was to be remembered by exactly one person.

"Sergeant says you're expected," said the sergeant, contradicting himself without noticing. "Walk quick. Column moves at the bell."

She stepped onto the bridge.

The new timber sang under her boots. Below, through gaps in the planking, the river showed itself in black slivers, moving and patient. Halfway up the far tower the clock face glowed like a held breath. Wind came down the gorge with the smell of snow that hadn't fallen yet but intended to.

Her heart was doing something unprofessional. She made herself count: the bridge was two hundred and ten paces. The clock would begin within minutes. Verren's order bought her the span alone—but orders expired, sergeants remembered their instructions, and somewhere behind her a colonel might at any moment decide to read things after all.

Two hundred and ten paces. She had carried heavier things across worse bridges. She had carried her sister's name for eleven years.

At pace ninety, a voice called from the near bank—Verren's voice, pitched to carry: "*Hold the column!*"

And the column held.

* * *

**Verren**

It worked. That was the astonishing part—it actually worked. A lieutenant rode up demanding explanation, and Verren handed him the forged order and watched him read it with the solemnity of a man reading scripture, because it had a signature and a wax seal and therefore must be true, mustn't it, signatures and seals were how truth traveled in an empire. The lieutenant saluted. The column stood in the cold, stamping feet, breathing steam, and Verren stood at the bridgehead with his heart going like a drummer boy's, watching a small figure walk away from him into the middle of the night.

Eleven fifty-eight.

He thought: *if she drops it, if she turns back, if anyone asks her a question I haven't anticipated*—

He thought: *Marta would have laughed at me. Both of us, probably. Standing at attention for a book.*

The minute hand climbed.

* * *

**Odile**

At the center of the span she stopped, because the center of a bridge is the one place in the world where you belong entirely to neither bank, and she wanted, just once, to stand in that country.

Below her the river ran. Above her the clock gathered itself.

The first stroke fell at midnight exactly, and it went through the timbers and up through her boots and into her chest, and she understood suddenly why towns built towers instead of just telling each other the time: because a bell is the only voice loud enough to say *now*, and mean it.

Second stroke. She lifted the ledger from under her arm.

Third stroke. It was heavier than it had been. Of course it was. Every name she had memorized and recited into the dark, every folio she had fed to the kettle while whispering the litany—*Anselm Kray, the ferryman; Doctor Ilse Vossen; the widow Pern at the tannery; the clerk Rennick, God forgive him and keep him drunk*—all of them had climbed aboard somewhere along the way.

Fourth stroke. Fifth. In the bindery behind her, she knew, Ambrose was listening, counting with her whether he lived to finish or not. Sixth stroke, and the wind took her hair sideways, and she thought of Marta fainting into the goose pen and Ambrose laughing and the pie, the ridiculous pie, and she laughed out loud alone on a bridge full of soldiers, and no one heard it over the bell.

Seventh. Eighth. On the far bank an officer was walking toward the bridgehead with quick, important steps, and beside him Verren turned to meet him, unhurried, a wall of a man built entirely of regulations.

Ninth. Tenth.

Eleventh stroke—and in the silence after it, the whole held century seemed to lean forward.

Odile raised the false ledger over the rail with both hands.

Twelfth stroke.

She threw it into the gorge.

It turned once in the lamplight, gilt edge catching gold, the republic's seal flashing bright as a coin tossed to fate—and then the river had it, and the river closed, and the sound it made was small and final, a period at the end of a very long sentence.

"*The ledger is destroyed!*" she shouted, and her voice cracked across the water like the thirteenth stroke the tower never rings. "*It burned weeks ago in the bindery kettle—the ash is in the hearth—go and read your names in the cinders if you can find them!*"

Silence. Two thousand soldiers silent. The river saying nothing. Somewhere downstream, presumably, a book sinking toward the trout.

Then the far-bank officer's voice: "*What did she say? What burned?*"

And Odile Nystrom, courier, bookbinder's assistant, keeper of a litany of the living, turned and walked back across the bridge at exactly the pace of a woman whom the century owed something, and began, calmly, to collect.

* * *

**Verren**

They arrested her on the span, which was necessary, and gently, which was not ordered. The colonel—Brandt's man, arriving red-faced from the officers' mess with wine on his breath and catastrophe in his eyes—wanted her searched, questioned, and preferably shot in an order he kept revising. Verren stood beside him radiating procedure.

"Sir, the volume she destroyed was the recovered chancery ledger. Its destruction is consistent with the civilian arson already logged at the bindery—I reported the hearth evidence myself, weeks ago." He had not. He was reporting it now, retroactively, fluently, the way a man speaks his native language. "The woman is a binder's assistant who has handled the archive since the requisition. If she chose to destroy state property rather than surrender it, that is treason, sir, and will be tried as such. But the ledger itself—the ledger everyone wants—is ash. It has been ash since December. You may dig the hearth yourself."

The colonel dug the hearth. Well—he sent sergeants to dig the hearth, and they brought back a bucket of kettle ash with charred page corners in it, and a brass buckle fragment, and the unmistakable smell of burnt vellum that no forger on earth can fake because it is the easiest thing in the world to make genuinely.

By two in the morning the report had written itself: the true ledger, damaged in the initial occupation, partially burned by persons unknown in the bindery, the surviving remnant destroyed tonight by a fanatic on the bridge rather than surrendered. The empire had lost its names. The war had lost its paperwork. The colonel had lost his appetite and gone to bed.

Verren stood a long time at the bridgehead, watching the column cross at last, boot after boot after boot, the new timbers ringing.

In the guardhouse, under a lamp, Odile sat where they had put her, hands folded, spine straight as a well-sewn signature. Through the bars she caught his eye.

"Ninety seconds," she said. "You bought me ninety seconds. I only needed twelve strokes."

"Then I overpaid," Verren said.

"Yes," she agreed. "But I know a shop where you can get a book repaired cheap, come spring. Assuming you're still in Vellbruck."

He looked at her a long moment—at the terror and liberation both still fading from her face like weather—and answered carefully, because from tomorrow there would be inquiries, transfers, a career to bury politely:

"I imagine I'm being transferred somewhere with poor libraries."

"Then take up drinking tea," said Odile. "You never learned to do it properly, and you'll have the time."

Outside, the last troops crossed, and behind them the clock tower struck one—unwitnessed, uncounted, striking anyway, the way bells do—and the century that had begun at midnight rolled on toward whatever came next, carrying one small town's secret in the safest vault ever built: the memory of a woman who planned to spend the rest of her life sewing it shut, one blank book at a time.
