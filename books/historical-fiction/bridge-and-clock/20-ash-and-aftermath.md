## 20. Ash and Aftermath

The soldiers found nothing, which was itself a kind of finding.

Verren stood in the doorway of the bindery on the second of January and watched four men from the provost's office move through the workshop with the careful destructiveness of men who had been told to be thorough and were enjoying it. They opened cabinets and shut them harder than necessary. They unshelved books by the armful and let them fall into drifts on the floorboards. One of them, a corporal with a mustache that seemed to have been grown specifically for looking disappointed, poked through the glue kettle with a bayonet and came up with a blade full of gray ash.

"Nothing but paper ash, sir," he said. "Wet down. Cold days old."

"Days," Verren repeated.

"Wet ash keeps its shape, more or less." The corporal turned his blade toward the light, as if the ash might confess something under proper illumination. "Could be anything in it. Could be nothing. Paper burns to nothing fast."

Paper burns to nothing fast. Verren thought of a woman walking a bridge at midnight with a ledger under her arm, and of twelve strokes of a clock tower, and of a book going over the rail into the gorge like a bird finally released from a very long lesson. He said, "Log it."

"Log what, sir?"

"Civilian arson. An unknown party destroyed chancery materials during the New Year's disturbance." He paused. "Note that the disturbance was likely celebratory in nature."

The corporal looked at him. In Vellbruck, on New Year's Eve, the imperial army had closed a bridge, massed three battalions, and lost one ledger to a gorge while the town's church bells rang and someone — no one could say who — set off a string of firecrackers down by the tannery at exactly eleven fifty-eight. The timing had been, Verren reflected, almost courteous. It had given every sentry on the span an excuse to look away toward the noise.

"Celebratory arson," the corporal said, writing it.

"People are strange about a new century."

"Yes, sir." The corporal did not sound convinced, but he sounded willing, which in the imperial service was worth considerably more.

* * *

They searched for two days, because thoroughness is its own reward and because searching gave everyone something warm to do indoors. On the first day they found Ambrose Halloran's bench: unfinished work still clamped in the vice, a half-sewn signature, an awl laid down mid-stroke as though the craftsman had simply stepped out to see about the weather and would be back directly. The old master was not back directly. He was upstairs in the cold room above the shop, under a blanket Odile Nystrom had apparently been changing daily, dead since sometime around the turn of the year — the doctor said days before, in fact, quietly, in his sleep, a heart worn out by decades of work and perhaps a few final weeks of overtime.

Verren signed the death certificate himself. *Ambrose Halloran, master binder, age seventy-one. Cause: natural.* His hand did not shake until he wrote the word *natural*, and then it shook only a little, and he chose to believe this was the cold.

On the second day they found the hearth.

It was Odile who showed them — Odile, who had not left the workshop except to walk to the bridge and back, who answered every question in a flat artisan's voice, who looked at Verren exactly once during the whole proceedings and then only briefly, the way one glances at a familiar piece of furniture to confirm it has not moved. She knelt at the great brick hearth where the glue kettle stood, raked back the wet ash with a poker, and let the officers see.

"Burned folios," she said. "Loose papers, duplicates, the chancery's overflow. We were told to clear the workshop of republic material when the requisition came. My master began burning it in December. Then he died, and I finished." She looked up. "There wasn't much left by New Year's. Mostly I burned it early because the smoke kept the damp out of the leather."

It was a good lie, Verren thought, because it was mostly true, which is the only kind of lie that survives an investigation. The kettle ash did sit in the hearth. It had sat there for anyone to read. And here was the beauty of the thing she had done, which Verren had understood only in fragments and now understood whole: she had made the destruction public before the theft could matter. A stolen ledger that turns out to be the last surviving copy is a treasure. A ledger whose contents are already ash in a civilian fireplace is a clerical embarrassment. No one hangs a town for a book that no longer exists anywhere; one merely files a report and requests additional funding for better locks.

Colonel Brandt, who had come down from the division to supervise the verification of the archive, stood over the hearth for a long time. He was a thick-necked man who smelled of bay rum and believed, sincerely, that the empire's problems were caused by insufficient paperwork.

"So the volume thrown from the bridge," he said slowly, "was already worthless."

"It was the only remaining copy of worthless material," Verren said. "The courier appears to have destroyed it rather than surrender it. Sentiment. These river towns are sentimental about their paperwork."

Brandt grunted. He looked at the ash. He looked at the unfinished bench, at the half-sewn signature with its thread still trailing, at the awl. Something in his face suggested that he had expected conspiracies and been offered instead a dead old man, a grieving apprentice, and a fireplace full of damp mush, and that he found this vaguely insulting.

"The girl," he said.

"Odile Nystrom. Apprentice and courier, formerly of the chancery's contract rolls. Her file shows routine deliveries. Nothing sensitive."

"And her sister?"

"Marta Nystrom. Deceased. Ninety-six." Verren kept his voice level with an effort that cost him more than the falsified inventory had. "A clerk. Illness."

Brandt nodded, satisfied, or satisfied enough, which in January was the same thing. "The town gets a garrison note. The bridge crossing proceeds. And you, Captain—" he glanced at Verren's file, which he held open with one thumb, "—you're marked for commendation. Holding the column at the span under unclear orders, quick thinking, et cetera. Division wants to know why you halted three companies for ninety seconds on a night like that."

Verren had prepared for this question for two days. He had prepared several answers. The true one — *because a woman needed ninety seconds and I gave them to her* — was not among the survivable options.

"There was a discrepancy in the crossing manifest," he said. "I halted to confirm the count of archive crates against the chancery inventory. The count was correct. I resumed."

"You halted a troop crossing to count boxes."

"Yes, sir."

For a moment Brandt's expression hovered somewhere between contempt and grudging respect, the way a man looks at weather he cannot control. Then, mercifully, the corporal with the disappointed mustache dropped a crate lid on his own foot and swore with a fluency that redirected the colonel's entire attention, and the moment passed.

The commendation came through anyway, on the fourth of January. So did the promotion: Major Verren, transferred to division headquarters, effective immediately, with a letter praising his diligence regarding archive security.

Verren wrote back requesting a transfer instead — to the western garrisons, to a supply depot, to anywhere the empire kept records it did not particularly care about. He cited his familiarity with Vellbruck's administration, argued — with genuine feeling — that continuity of local command would serve the occupation better than rotation, and asked, in the humblest language he could manufacture, to remain a captain in a quiet town.

His superiors found this baffling. Men did not decline majors' stars. But the request was technically permissible, the paperwork was in order, and Brandt, who had watched a captain count crates under firecracker fire and decided the man was either a fool or exactly the sort of meticulous nonentity a garrison needed, approved it without comment.

So Verren stayed. Not as a kindness to Odile Nystrom — though it was that, and he did not pretend otherwise to himself at three in the morning — but as penance, which is a different animal wearing similar fur. A promotion would have meant leaving. Leaving would have meant the story ended safely for him and dangerously for everyone else. Staying meant he would spend the rest of the occupation within sight of the consequences of his choice, checking each morning that the town was still standing, that the bindery's chimney still smoked, that no new inquiry had come sniffing after the ashes. Punishment, he had decided, should be administered locally.

Besides — and here he permitted himself the small dishonesty of humor — he had grown fond of the tea.

* * *

He went to the empty workshop once.

It was the eleventh of January, a grey afternoon with the river running high and brown under the bridge, and the garrison's business had carried him past the market square whether or not he arranged for it to, which he had. The bindery door was unlocked. That surprised him until it didn't: a locked door announces that there is something inside worth locking away, and Odile Nystrom had spent a lifetime teaching the world that there was nothing in her shop worth a glance.

Inside, the workshop had been put to rights. The soldiers' wreckage was gone; the books were reshelved; the floor was swept. The great press stood clean. Ambrose's bench had been wiped down and cleared, except for the half-finished signature, which remained clamped in the vice exactly as the old man had left it — and Verren understood that too. Some things are not abandoned. They are kept open, the way one keeps a chair.

The glue kettle sat cold in the hearth, scrubbed.

"You'll ruin your boots," Odile said from the stair.

She came down with a broom in her hand, though there was nothing left to sweep, holding it the way another woman might hold a poker or a verdict. She wore an apron gone grey with use, and her hair was pinned back without ceremony, and she looked, Verren thought, ten years older than she had in November and entirely uninterested in his opinion of the fact.

"I came to offer condolences," he said. "Formally. For your master."

"He'd have laughed at formal condolences." She set the broom against the wall. "But thank you. He liked you, near the end. He said you asked questions like a man reading a book upside down — slowly, and pretending not to notice the print."

"That sounds like him."

"It was him." She crossed to the bench and touched the vice, absently, the way one touches a shoulder. "What do you want, Captain?"

Honesty, he decided, was owed here if it was owed anywhere. "To see it once with my own eyes. The place where it happened. I've written the report, I've answered the colonel, I've declined a major's commission, and none of it feels real yet. I thought standing in this room might make it real."

"And does it?"

He looked around: the shelves, the cold kettle, the open signature. "No," he admitted. "It feels like a bindery."

A ghost of a smile moved across her face and was gone. "That's what he always said, at the end of any job. Whatever we'd done — the fine gilded commissions, the chancery ledgers, the smuggled pamphlets sewn into hymnals — he'd look around the same way and say, well, it's still just a bindery. Books come in ruined and go out mended. Everything else is conversation."

"Is that what it was?" Verren asked. "Conversation?"

She considered him for a long moment. Outside, a patrol passed the windows, boots ringing on the cobbles, and neither of them so much as glanced toward the sound.

"You stopped a column for me," she said at last. "You falsified an inventory in front of a colonel. You threw away a major's star to stay in a frozen river town and drink bad tea within sight of my chimney. I think you can decide for yourself what it was." She picked the broom back up, not because there was sweeping to do but because a woman needs her hands busy, and he recognized the habit and almost smiled. "Don't come again, Captain. Not for a while. A man who visits too often starts being noticed, and noticed men get asked questions by people worse than Colonel Brandt."

"Understood."

"And Captain—" she said, as he reached the door. He turned. She was standing by the bench with the winter light coming grey through the panes, and for a moment she looked less like a courier who had beaten an empire and more like what she was underneath all of it: a tired young woman who had buried a sister and a master in one season and still had a shop to run. "The tea was never bad. You simply drank it too fast."

He inclined his head, which in Vellbruck passed for both agreement and farewell, and stepped out into the cold.

* * *

One thing remained, and he had saved it deliberately, because some errands want doing alone.

The garrison's intelligence annex occupied the old customs house, and among its files — inherited wholesale from the chancery when the republic's clerks fled — was a thin folder marked *Nystrom, M., alias "Lark."* Correspondence received under the alias, ongoing. Source status: active. Verren had pulled it the week before, meaning to burn it, meaning to close the last account his war in Vellbruck had opened.

He had not burned it. Instead he had read every page twice, sitting late with the stove dying, and learned what Odile had learned in December: that the letters in Marta Nystrom's name were in Ambrose Halloran's hand, that the old man had kept a dead woman alive on paper to keep money moving to families the network supported, and that the last letter was dated the nineteenth of December — nine days before the master died — and ended with the line, *The account is nearly settled. Let Lark rest.*

Let Lark rest. Verren had sat with that a long while. Then he had stamped the folder — he allowed himself this one forgery, executed with a steadier hand than he'd managed on the death certificate — *Source deceased. Correspondence terminated. No further action,* and closed it, and felt nothing at all for an hour, and then felt everything.

Now he walked to the annex a final time in his capacity as its departing administrator — his replacement arrived Monday — and took the folder from the drawer, and stood over the stove with it in his hands, and did not burn it.

Because it was not evidence anymore. A dead source, a terminated file, a closed account: the bureaucracy had already forgotten it, and would forget it faster every year. But it was also something else. It was the record of a man who had loved a town enough to impersonate its griefs for years, written in a hand that would never write again. Somewhere in Vellbruck there was a woman memorizing names in the dark, sewing testimony into blank endpapers where no one could read it yet — he did not know how he knew this; he knew it the way one knows the river will rise in spring — and someday, when empires finished falling as empires reliably did, someone would need to know that Marta Nystrom's name had been kept breathing by a friend's hand long after her lungs had quit. That was testimony too. Burning it would be tidier. Tidiness, Verren had concluded, was overrated.

He returned the folder to the drawer. He locked it. He left the key in the lock, because the incoming administrator was a punctual man and punctual men find keys, and because a locked drawer with the key sitting in it is a secret that has decided to be patient.

Outside, the snow had begun again, fat and unhurried. Verren turned up his collar and walked back along the river road, past the bridge standing grey and solid over the gorge, past the clock tower keeping its stubborn time. In the market square, the bindery chimney was smoking.

He did not look at it directly. But he counted, as he passed, the curls of smoke rising off it — one, two, a third — and thought: she's working. Books coming in ruined. Books going out mended.

And somewhere in that smoke, a century was turning over like a page, waiting for its reader.
