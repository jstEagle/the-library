## 21. The Century Turns

They buried Ambrose Ferenc on the third of January, in the small churchyard above the river, where the ground was frozen hard enough that the gravedigger charged double and swore in three languages by the time he finished.

Odile paid him from the bindery's tin box without haggling. Some prices were not worth arguing.

There were eleven mourners. That surprised her; she had expected fewer. The chancery clerk came, sober for the first time since New Year's Eve and visibly suffering for it, wearing a black coat that smelled of mothballs and regret. The soldier who read confiscated novels came too—Corporal Brandt, though she had never asked his name aloud—standing awkwardly at the back in his uniform with his cap in his hands like a man attending the funeral of an enemy's grandfather, which was more or less what he was doing. A handful of townsfolk appeared: the woman who ran the coffee house, two of Ambrose's old customers, a boy Odile half-recognized as having once delivered hides.

The priest was new, appointed by the occupation, young and nervous, and he gave a eulogy that could have fit any dead man of Ambrose's age: dutiful craftsman, long service to the town, beloved by all who knew him. Every word of it true and none of it the truth.

Odile found she didn't mind. Ambrose would have appreciated it. *The best forgery,* he used to say, pressing gold leaf flat with a squirrel's foot, *is the one that tells people exactly what they expected to hear.*

She stood at the head of the grave with her hands folded, the way she had stood at Marta's grave seven years ago, and she did not weep. Not because there was no grief—there was enough of it to fill the gorge under the bridge—but because she had spent the last weeks learning a discipline older than mourning: you do not spill what may yet be needed.

What was needed, she understood now, was everything.

* * *

The army searched the bindery twice.

The first search came the morning after the bridge, four soldiers and a sergeant, thorough in the way of men who expect to find something and annoyed in advance that they won't be the ones credited with finding it. They went through the glue kettle's cold ashes with a sieve. They pulled every book off the shelves and flipped them spine-up, shaking them for loose papers. One of them took Ambrose's burnisher before his sergeant cleared his throat, and he set it back down with the guilty tenderness of a boy returning sweets to a shopkeeper.

Odile stood aside with her hands folded—the same fold as at the graveside—and answered questions in her flat courier's voice.

*Where is your master? Dead. When did he die? The night of the thirty-first. Of what? His heart, or his lungs, or sixty years at a bench; take your pick. Did you know the ledger that was thrown from the bridge? I bound it. I am a binder. I bind what is brought to me.*

The kettle ash they bagged and carried off, which struck Odile later as the funniest thing that had happened all winter: soldiers marching away in solemn single file, each man carrying a portion of burned names, delivering them across town with more care than they had shown the living residents. Somewhere in an office those ashes would sit in a labeled sack until someone decided they were nothing, and then someone else would throw them out, and the names would complete their journey to nowhere in a municipal rubbish cart.

That was fine. That was the plan. Those names had already gone somewhere better.

The second search came ten days later, and it consisted entirely of Captain Aldric Verren walking through the workshop alone for eleven minutes, touching nothing, and leaving without a word except to say that the requisition order on the building would lapse at month's end.

He paused at the door on his way out. Snow was coming down behind him in the lazy, sideways way of snow that intends to keep at it all day.

"Your master's bench," he said. "The vice is loose on the left side. You'll want to tighten it."

"I will," said Odile. "Thank you."

Neither of them looked at the other for longer than a sentence allows. He put on his cap and went out into the weather, and Odile stood listening to his boots crunch away down the lane, and thought about how strange it was that the safest conversation of her life had been the one where both parties knew everything and said nothing at all.

* * *

Running a bindery alone turned out to be equal parts work and arithmetic.

The work she knew. The arithmetic was new: how many repairs a week paid for coal, how much coal paid for flour, how much flour could stretch if she ate with the coffee-house widow in exchange for rebinding the widow's late husband's prayer book, which needed it badly—he had apparently read it in the bath. There was gentle humor in that, Ambrose-style humor, and Odile found herself laughing at odd moments, alone at the bench, which she supposed was either grief or health and decided not to investigate.

Customers came back slowly. First the townsfolk, who had watched her walk onto the bridge and had decided among themselves, in the wordless way of towns, exactly what she was—which was not a collaborator, not a heroine, but *Nystrom's girl*, and therefore trustworthy, because Nystrom's girl had always quoted fair and returned books unharmed. Then, more cautiously, the occupiers. An empire that meant to stay needed its manuals mended like anyone else. A lieutenant brought a water-damaged drill regulations manual. A colonel's wife sent in a book of poetry with a broken spine and a note specifying that the ribbon should be blue, *not the seditious red currently fashionable.* Odile bound it in blue. She charged extra for the ribbon and felt no guilt whatsoever.

Her name, she noted, appeared on no list anywhere now. Not the army's billeting rolls—those had lapsed with the requisition order. Not the chancery's surviving records—that ledger slept at the bottom of the gorge, wearing a forged sister made by her own hands. Not even the town register, which had misspelled her surname in 1887 and never corrected it. She was, administratively speaking, nobody. It was the freest she had ever been, and she intended to use it.

Because there was still the matter of the names.

* * *

One hundred and fourteen.

That was the number. She had recited it so many times that the count itself had become part of the litany, the way the clock tower's first stroke was part of midnight. One hundred and fourteen names, learned folio by folio over the glue kettle, each one witnessed aloud before it burned: couriers, safekeepers, the doctor who had set Marta's leg, the schoolteacher, the ferryman, the clerk whose own signature had purchased his silence with silver and shame. Names with their ticks and marginal marks, live and dead and unknown, the whole careful nervous architecture of a republic that no longer existed except in the head of a twenty-four-year-old woman who mended hymnals.

Ambrose had taught her that a binding was a promise: that the pages would hold, that the shape would endure, that the object would outlive its maker's hands. She had believed him abstractly, the way one believes things about weather. Now she meant to test it.

She began in February, after closing, with the shutters barred and the kettle cold and one candle burning down in its dish.

A blank volume. Good stock, sewn on five cords, the way he had taught her—*the thread sings when it's right, girl; listen for it.* Endpapers of the pale blue laid paper she had left over from the false ledger, because there was something right about using it for this. And in the endpaper, before pasting it down, worked into the paste-warmed fibers with a fine needle point in strokes shallow enough to be invisible until you knew to look:

**ALBRECHT MOSS.**

The ferryman. First name on the list, first name she had spoken into the steam, the first name she had promised not to lose.

She pasted the endpaper down, pressed the book overnight, trimmed it, cased it in plain grey boards with no title and no tooling, and set it on the shelf over Ambrose's bench. One book. One name. A library of exactly one volume that said nothing to anyone, that catalogued as *blank notebook, undated, value negligible*, that could be sold, confiscated, burned, or ignored without betraying anything—unless you knew, unless someone someday knew, that the inside covers of these plain books were not blank at all but crowded, that the shallow needle-cuts spelled out in a private alphabet of Odile's own devising the full testimony of one hundred and fourteen citizens of a country that had died at the turn of the century.

It took her most of a night per book. She had one hundred and thirteen nights' work ahead of her, minimum, plus the ordinary business of staying fed, and she caught herself smiling at the size of it. Marta would have laughed. *You always did bite off more thread than the signature needs.*

*Shut up,* Odile said pleasantly to the empty workshop, and threaded her needle.

* * *

The nights developed a shape.

Close the shop. Eat whatever the evening provided. Bar the shutters. Light the stub of candle. Say the litany—not the whole roll every night, that took hours and she refused to let it become rote; instead a section, five or six names, spoken softly while she chose the paper and waxed the thread, matching each name to the feel of the sheets the way Ambrose had matched watermarks. Then the work: cut, fold, pierce, sew. Listen for the singing of the thread.

Some nights a name caught in her throat. The doctor, who had once splinted Odile's wrist after a fall from a delivery cart and told her she had the strongest grip of any child he'd seen, and had then, presumably, signed something, or hidden someone, or simply been in the wrong ledger at the wrong time. She sewed his name and her eyes swam and she let them, briefly, and then wiped them on her sleeve and kept sewing, because sentiment that stopped the work was just another form of forgetting, and she had been hired—by whom, she couldn't say, but hired nonetheless—to remember.

Other nights were easier, almost companionable. She talked to Ambrose while she worked, in the low running commentary apprentices use. *Your headband trick doesn't hold on octavo, old man, I've had to adapt it. Your vice is still loose. I tightened it anyway, out of spite.* Once, sorting through his tool chest, she found a slip of paper in his handwriting she hadn't seen before—a practice flourish, a single letter done forty times in fading brown ink, the letter M. She looked at it for a long time. Then she put it in the kettle, watched it curl and go to ash, and spoke Marta's alias aloud one final time, retiring it like a flag.

The soldier Brandt came in occasionally, on legitimate errands now that his unit had rotated to garrison duty, bringing books confiscated from suspects and, sometimes, books he had bought himself and pretended were confiscated so he'd have an excuse to visit. He was reading a serialized romance that Odile judged terrible by its condition—it came in with broken spines weekly, as though he wrestled it. She never asked. He never told. Once he lingered at the counter and said, "The library in the capital has rooms they don't catalog. Everything goes somewhere, eventually."

"Everything," Odile agreed, "goes somewhere."

He nodded as if this had been a normal exchange between a soldier and a bookbinder, paid for his repair, and left. Odile stood a moment thinking that the century was going to be stranger than the last one, and got back to work.

* * *

Spring came late and then all at once, the way it does beside rivers. The ice went out of the Vell with a sound like distant artillery, and half the town ran to the banks to watch, and Odile stood with them among the crowd and listened to people cheer at the destruction of winter, and thought that crowds cheered loudest at exactly the things they most feared happening quietly.

By then there were thirty-one books on the shelf.

She fell into a rhythm and guarded it jealously. Repairs by day—hymnals, ledgers of the mundane and taxable kind, a schoolmaster's Euclid held together with hope and paste, the colonel's wife's second poetry book, ribbon color unspecified this time, which Odile chose to interpret as trust. Litany and sewing by night. Sundays she allowed herself the market, an orange if oranges existed, and an hour of doing nothing by the river with her boots off in warm weather, watching the span where she had once walked a lie into the dark and thrown it away.

The town, in its slow way, began to bring her its own histories without meaning to. Old women brought family Bibles for rebinding, fat with births and marriages and deaths in faded inks, and Odile handled them with particular reverence, because here was testimony that had never needed to hide—whole generations recorded openly, sewn in visible thread. Once, unpicking a ruined cover, she found a pressed flower from 1861 and a lock of hair, and she put them back exactly where they had lain, and added nothing, and closed the book gently, like a door on sleeping people.

That was the difference, she decided. Her hundred and fourteen would wait in the dark until it was safe. These others had been lucky enough to live in a time when waiting wasn't necessary. Perhaps the luck would come around again. It seemed to, historically, the way the river came around the bend every morning whether anyone watched it or not.

* * *

On the anniversary of the bridge—she did not call it anything, but her body kept the date—she finished the fiftieth book. Halfway. She sat back on the stool, blew out the candle stub, and let her eyes adjust to the moonlit workshop: the presses, the standing press with its great iron screw, the shelf of grey volumes climbing steadily toward the ceiling beam like a slow brick wall being built against forgetting.

Fifty names in hiding. Sixty-four still living only in her.

She flexed her hands, which ached in the cold months now the way Ambrose's had, and permitted herself the accounting: she was twenty-five, the bindery was hers, the debt at the coffee house was paid, and somewhere under the gorge lay a forged book that had fooled an empire, and somewhere in her skull lay a country that had fooled everyone.

*You built a library no one can read yet,* she told the shelf, in Ambrose's honor.

Then, because he would have wanted the joke completed: *Which puts you one up on the chancery. Theirs couldn't be read either. Wrong reasons, mind.*

The clock tower struck twelve, unhurried, indifferent, correct as ever. Odile counted all the strokes out of habit, waited for the last one to fade over the water, and reached for the next signature.

She had sixty-four names to go, and all the time in the new century—or so she believed, that mild spring night, threading her needle by the light of a candle-end, in a town that thought it had survived its history.

Three days later, a stranger knocked at the bindery door, and asked, politely, in an accent from very far away, whether the shop of Master Ferenc had ever kept records.
