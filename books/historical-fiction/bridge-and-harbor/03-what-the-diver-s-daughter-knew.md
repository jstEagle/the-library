## 3. What the Diver's Daughter Knew

Seven years earlier, in the last summer before anyone had a reason to count the years, Ilse Marchetti learned that her father kept his most valuable pearls not in the company vault but in a tobacco tin beneath the third floorboard of his workshop.

She learned it by accident, the way all important knowledge arrives — she was hunting for twine to mend a kite and pried up the wrong board. The tin was dented on one corner as if it had been dropped down stairs at least once. Inside, resting in a nest of wool shavings, lay eleven pearls, each one wrapped separately in paper torn from the backs of invoices. Her father's handwriting covered the papers: dates, depths, weights. He had catalogued them like children.

She should have put the board back. She didn't. That was the kind of summer it was — the kind where you lifted things up to see what was underneath, because you were fourteen and the war was still just a word grown men used at dinner, and nothing underneath had ever bitten anyone she loved.

* * *

The diving quarter sat below the Marchetti warehouse like a bowl of gray stone that the tide filled twice a day. Ilse came down into it every Tuesday with her father's ledger receipts, because Paolo Marchetti believed his divers should be paid on time even when they didn't ask, and because he believed his daughter should see where the pearls began — not in the vaults upstairs, not in the ledgers, but at the bottom of cold water in the hands of men whose fingers never fully thawed between June and September.

"You'll inherit the trade," he told her, walking the wet cobbles with his hands clasped behind his back like a bishop touring his parish. "Not the pearls. The trade. The knowing of them."

"And what is there to know?"

"That a pearl is only worth something because someone once drowned for it." He said it cheerfully, the way he said everything. "Never forget that, Ilse. Every account in your books has a lungful of harbor behind it."

She was fourteen and thought this was poetry. Later she would understand it was arithmetic.

It was on one of these Tuesdays that she found the Vane boy sitting on the sea wall below her father's warehouse, sorting gravel.

He wasn't sorting it for anyone. He had a small pile of pale stones and a larger pile of darker ones, and he moved pieces from one pile to the other with the concentration of a jeweler, though what he was looking for she couldn't imagine. He was twelve or thirteen, thin in the way of boys who gave their bread to younger siblings without being asked, and he had the specific stillness of a child who had learned that moving draws attention and attention is expensive.

"You're the Vane boy," she said. "Wendell."

He looked up. His eyes did something complicated — first measuring her, the way everyone in the diving quarter measured a Marchetti, then arriving somewhere else entirely. "You're the one who pays late."

"I pay *early*. My father pays on time. There's a difference." She sat down on the wall beside him, which surprised her more than it seemed to surprise him. "What are you doing?"

"Counting."

"There's nothing to count but gravel."

"There's always something to count." He held up a stone. "This one's got a vein of quartz. See? If you were a stupid man you'd think it was a cracked pearl and sell it cheap and be cheated. If you were a clever man you'd know what it really was." He set it carefully aside. "I'm learning the difference."

"Why?"

"Because in this city, the difference between what a thing looks like and what a thing is, is the whole difference." He said it flatly, without self-pity, the way you'd recite a rule of grammar. Then he glanced at her sidelong, embarrassed, as if he'd said too much, which he had. Boys like Wendell Vane were not supposed to have philosophies in front of merchant daughters.

Ilse picked up one of his dark stones and turned it over. "My father says a pearl is only worth something because someone drowned for it."

Wendell was quiet for a moment. Below them the tide worked at the pilings, patient as a clerk.

"He's right," he said finally. "My father dives. My mother used to say the harbor keeps accounts too." He took the stone gently out of her hand and put it back in its pile. "You shouldn't sit here. The stones get hot and your dress is good cloth."

"It's Tuesday. I'm allowed to ruin Tuesdays."

He almost smiled. It was the first almost-smile, and over the following weeks Ilse collected them the way her father collected pearls — one at a time, wrapped carefully, catalogued in a place no one would think to look.

* * *

That summer had a texture she would spend seven years trying to describe and never manage. Salt on everything. Her father singing badly through the open workshop window while he oiled the dive suits. The smell of pitch and tar. The whole city still fat and easy from peace, still believing — as cities do, right up until they don't — that the arrangements of the world were permanent. Corvane's ships came and went on schedule. The magistrates wore clean collars. Nobody had heard the word *registry*, and if an enemy list had been read aloud on the exchange steps that August, it would have contained nobody, because everybody was still just people.

Wendell worked days at the Corvane counting house now — they'd taken him on, indentured, the papers signed by some uncle or other who had signed and vanished — but he came back to the diving quarter evenings, and Tuesdays he waited on the sea wall whether her father sent her down or not. Usually he pretended he hadn't been waiting. Once, caught out, he said, "I was counting," and gestured at the gulls as though they'd asked to be counted.

They talked about nothing, magnificently. They talked about whether fish slept, and whether her father's new apprentice could be trusted with the air lines, and whether the moon pulled on the water or the water pulled on the moon, and which of them would be richer in ten years — she argued herself, he argued arithmetic, and neither of them conceded. He taught her the difference between a natural pearl and a cultured one by feel alone, eyes closed, and made her practice until she got it right three times running.

"Now you know something the magistrates don't," he said.

"What else do I know that the magistrates don't?"

"How to tell when a man's lying about being paid." He shrugged. "Their hands go to their waistcoats. Honest men talk with their hands. Liars hold them still."

"Wendell Vane. You're twelve years old."

"I'm old at twelve," he said, and she didn't laugh, because he was right, and it was the first time she understood that being right could hurt.

* * *

In September her father made his last dive of the season himself — a habit, a vanity, a way of reminding the divers that their employer knew the weight of a weight belt. The water was already cold. Ilse stood on the deck of the little boat and watched the rope pay out and counted in her head the way the divers' wives counted on shore, and at forty fathoms the rope stopped moving longer than it should have, and her heart learned something that day that her mind refused for years afterward: that the whole trade was a wager against drowning, placed fresh with every breath.

Then the rope moved again and her father came up coughing and triumphant, holding his glove closed like a fist, and inside the glove was a pearl the size of a currant, faintly rose-colored, flawless.

"A last-dive pearl," he rasped on the deck, blue-lipped and grinning. "Divers say they're lucky. Superstition. But look at it, Ilse. Look at it and tell me the sea doesn't love us sometimes."

She looked. The sea did.

He meant to put it in the tin under the floorboard, the twelfth child of eleven. But that evening Wendell was on the wall, and Ilse — fourteen, invincible, drunk on the ordinary miracle of a summer ending exactly as summers should — brought the pearl down to him wrapped in invoice paper.

Her father had let her carry it. *Let my daughter learn what value weighs,* he'd said. It weighed almost nothing. That was the astonishing part. Everything that mattered weighed almost nothing.

"What's this?" Wendell asked.

"A last-dive pearl. My father's. Rose-colored. Feel it — no, close your eyes, you taught me, so I'll teach you."

He closed his eyes. He rolled it in his fingers once, twice, and she watched his face do the thing it did when he was counting — the stillness, the attention — and then something else, something unguarded, quick as a fish under a boat.

"This is worth more than my indenture," he said quietly. "Ten times. Why are you showing me?"

"I'm not showing you. I'm giving it to you."

"Ilse—"

"For a promise." She had rehearsed nothing; the words arrived fully formed, the way the truest ones do. "If I ever need you — ever, no matter when, no matter what it costs — you come for me. That's the price. That's the whole trade."

Wendell opened his eyes. The harbor was doing its evening trick, going silver where the light hit it, and his face was very serious in that light, older than thirteen, older than the century.

"You don't want to make trades with me," he said. "My family's luck is bad."

"Luck isn't inherited."

"No?" He turned the pearl over in his palm. "Then what is?"

She didn't have an answer then. She would spend years finding one.

"Say the terms," he said finally.

"If I ever need you, you come."

"If you ever need me, I come." He said it like a man reading a contract aloud, clause by clause, except his voice cracked slightly on *come*, and he was thirteen, and the sun was going down behind Corvane's warehouses turning the whole harbor the color of the pearl in his hand. "And what do I give?"

"The pearl holds your side. It always knows where you are."

"That's superstition."

"My father says all contracts are superstition. Paper only works because we agree to pretend." She took his hand and closed his fingers over the pearl, both of hers around his one, sealing it the way you seal wax, the way you seal a letter. "So pretend."

For a long moment neither of them moved. A gull screamed somewhere. Somewhere across the harbor a ship's bell rang the hour, indifferent and perfect, and the water slapped the pilings in the rhythm of a thing that had been happening forever and believed it would happen forever more.

"All right," Wendell said, so quietly she nearly missed it. "Pretend."

But she felt his fingers tighten, and she knew — the way she could feel a pearl through paper, the way her father knew the bottom of the harbor through forty fathoms of black water — that it wasn't pretend, and never had been.

* * *

They had four more weeks after that. Four weeks of Tuesdays, of gravel sorted into piles, of arguments about the moon. Then the weather turned, and the diplomatic turns followed the weather, and by spring the word *war* had changed from a noun into a verb, and the ships began carrying different cargo, and the lists began.

Ilse Marchetti kept the knowledge of that promise the way her father kept his pearls: wrapped, catalogued, hidden beneath a floorboard of the ordinary. Through the war years she unwrapped it sometimes, at night, and checked that it was still there. Still round. Still rose-colored. Still pretending to be only a stone.

Seven years later, standing at the window of a sealed warehouse with her family's name newly printed on a government registry, she would take it out again and turn it over in her hands.

And far across the city, in a counting house that smelled of ink and salt, a courier was handing a sealed letter to a young man who did not yet know that the past had learned to write.

But that morning, seven years ago, there was only the harbor, silvering, and two children sealing a bargain with the smallest valuable thing either of them would ever own — not knowing yet what promises cost, only that they weighed almost nothing, and that everything that mattered never weighed more.
