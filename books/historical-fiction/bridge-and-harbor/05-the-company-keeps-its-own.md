## 5. The Company Keeps Its Own

Aldous Corvane kept two ledgers.

The first sat in the iron safe behind his study, bound in green calfskin, audited twice a year by men who wore worried expressions and carried the company seal. It recorded pearls bought and sold, freight contracted, wages disbursed to the two hundred and eleven souls on Corvane's payroll, Wendell Vane among them at nine shillings a week plus lodging in the counting-house attic. That ledger was a public fiction, and Aldous Corvane maintained it the way he maintained the façade of the counting house itself — repainted every spring, brass polished, nothing behind it but more wall.

The second ledger lived in his head.

He reviewed it most evenings after the clerks had gone, when the light off the harbor turned the color of old pewter and the tally-rooms fell quiet enough that he could hear the water slapping pilings through the half-open window. He would pour one measure of brandy — never two; clarity was an asset and he hoarded it like any other — and turn the pages of memory with the same care his father had turned the pages of the calfskin book before him.

Tonight's entry was a boy.

*Wendell Vane. Nineteen years of age. Indentured seven years past, papers held by the company, no kin of record. Father: Josiah Vane, pearl diver, drowned or fled — the distinction has never mattered. Mother: Livia Vane, née Kestrel, deceased eight years. No debts outstanding except the debt of his indenture, which is to say, all of him.*

Corvane sipped and considered the entry the way a jeweler considers a stone that has come onto the market unexpectedly. Not yet cut. Not yet priced. But with something inside it that caught the light wrong — or right, depending on what you wanted from it.

* * *

The trouble — or the promise — of Wendell Vane was that he had learned to be invisible in a building designed to make men visible.

Corvane had noticed it first three years ago, in the way a man notices a draft: not the thing itself but the evidence of it. A clerk's error in the autumn shipment manifest, four hundred marks misassigned between the Harwich account and the Saltmere depot, corrected overnight without any hand appearing to have touched it. The correction was elegant. The correction was deniable. And the correction had been made by a fourteen-year-old apprentice whose job description did not include reading manifests at all.

Corvane had not congratulated him. Congratulation teaches subordinates that they have been seen, and being seen is the beginning of either loyalty or fear, and Corvane preferred to choose which a man would feel and when. Instead he had ordered the manifests moved to the far shelving, out of the boy's reach, and watched.

The boy had stopped correcting them. Immediately. Without a single visible sign of having noticed the change. That, more than the correction itself, was what Corvane filed away under the heading of *investment*. Most clever people could not resist demonstrating their cleverness. This one could. Which meant that when he finally chose to demonstrate it, the demonstration would be deliberate, and worth whatever it cost.

Since then, Corvane had collected the boy the way other men collected specimens. Not physically — physically the boy remained exactly where he had been placed, third desk from the window in the tally-room, cataloging pearls he would never own. But Corvane collected facts about him, and facts were the only currency that never debased.

*Fact: the boy can hold his breath longer than any man on the payroll. Four minutes, tested informally by the dock foreman on a bet, though the foreman does not know Corvane knows.*

*Fact: he sends money quarterly to a woman named Maren Oduya in the old diving quarter. Small sums. The kind of sums a proud person sends when they cannot send gratitude instead.*

*Fact: he carries something small and hard in his left coat pocket, always, and turns it over in moments of stress. The dock foreman's wife, who mends the tally-room coats, has described it as round and the size of a thumbnail. Corvane has never needed to see it to know what it is. Divers' sons keep pearls the way soldiers keep bullets — as proof they once stood in front of something dangerous and came back.*

*Fact — and here Corvane set down the glass and let the fact sit alone on the page of his mind for a long moment — the boy is not Josiah Vane's son.*

* * *

This last item required care, because it was the load-bearing fact, the one the whole structure rested on, and Corvane had built enough structures in his life to respect a good foundation.

Josiah Vane had run cargo during the war. Everyone in the diving quarter knew it and no one in the diving quarter said it, which was the arrangement cities make with their poorer districts: your crimes are permitted so long as they remain unspoken. Corvane himself had known, because Corvane knew everything that moved on the water, and for two years he had quietly permitted Vane's little boat to pass his wharves unmolested, taking a tithe in pearls that appeared in neither ledger, in exchange for the silence that let the man feed his family. It had been good business. It had also been leverage, banked against a future need, because Aldous Corvane did not do favors; he made deposits.

Then Josiah Vane's boat had gone down with a hold full of contraband and a crew of one, and the leverage had seemed lost, and Corvane had written it off as he wrote off all drowned assets.

Except.

The widow Livia had come to the counting house once, eight years ago now, with the boy trailing her — a thin, watchful child of eleven who stood exactly one pace behind his mother's shoulder and looked at nothing and saw everything. She had asked, in her careful diver's-wife English, for the indenture of her son. Asked for it, mind — not begged. She had offered the last of Josiah's dive-earnings against the boy's term, seven years, and Corvane had agreed because agreeing cost him nothing and purchased a claim on a promising asset.

But it was not the transaction Corvane remembered. It was what she had said at the door, thinking the child too far away to hear, in a voice pitched for one listener only: *Tell him his father will be proud, if ever you must tell him anything.*

And the child had heard. Corvane had seen it in the boy's back — a stillness that was not a boy's stillness but a man's, the stillness of someone absorbing a blow without letting it show. Stillness like that, in a child, meant the sentence had landed on ground already prepared. The boy had known, or half-known, or suspected in the way children suspect what adults believe they have hidden.

And there was this further wrinkle, which Corvane had verified through channels he did not commit to paper: Josiah Vane had spent the last eighteen months of his life running cargo for buyers whose names led, through three intermediaries and one dead ship's chandler, toward the Marchetti warehouse on the east quay. Toward old Marchetti himself. Toward the Marchetti daughter, who would have been twelve at the time and learning her father's trade the way divers' children learn theirs — early, completely, and in silence.

Two families, then, braided together by cargo and pearls and a drowned man, and only one of them knowing it.

* * *

Corvane rose and crossed to the window. The harbor lay dark now except for the riding lights of the coastal steamers and the pale scar of the customs house across the water. Somewhere out there, in sealed warehouses and stalled ministries, the armistice was being negotiated clause by clause, and each clause was a door closing, and Aldous Corvane had spent forty years making sure he was always inside the room before the door shut rather than outside it.

The war had been good to him, in its sideways fashion. Neutral shipping, neutral flags, neutral accounts in neutral banks — he had kept Corvane Trading scrupulously, provably clean on paper while his profits grew fat on the gray margins where paper and truth diverged. But peace was coming, and peace brought auditors, and auditors asked questions about certain entries, and certain entries could be answered only by men who were conveniently dead.

Unless they weren't dead unless someone needed them to be. Unless a name could be produced, posthumously, attached to cargo that had passed through Corvane's own wharves. A smuggler's name. A dead smuggler's name, with a living son holding company paper, and a living son's testimony worth exactly as much as the company allowed it to be worth.

That was the first use of Wendell Vane: insurance. If the auditors came sniffing at the gray margins, the trail would end at Josiah Vane's grave and a grieving family of suppliers, and Aldous Corvane would stand in the wreckage wringing his hands about how badly he had been deceived.

But insurance was passive, and Corvane had never cared for passive assets.

The second use was active, and it concerned the Marchetti name, which had surfaced that very morning on a provisional registry drafted by the ministry — *pending final armistice terms*, the phrase that meant everything and guaranteed nothing. Enemy property. Seizable property, while the registry stood. The Marchetti warehouse alone — stone-built, deep-water frontage, cranes and cellars and title unencumbered since the old man's death — was worth more than Corvane Trading's entire pearl book. And if the armistice signed soft, if the registry dissolved, that property would slip back into the hands of a foreign family and be forever beyond reach.

The window was narrow. Windows usually were. The trick was to know which side of it you stood on, and to move before anyone else understood there was glass at all.

To take the Marchetti holdings cleanly, Corvane needed three things: legal cover (the registry, already secured), a buyer of record (himself, through the blind trust in Harwich, already arranged), and — this was the piece still missing — a reason for the seizure that would survive scrutiny if the terms changed. Smuggling ties. Wartime supply chains. Documentary evidence linking Marchetti to contraband cargo.

And there, like a pearl rising in an oyster, was the Vane boy. Son of a supplier. Living witness. A letter placed in the right hands could set the whole mechanism turning — the boy frightened, the boy investigating, the boy drawing the ministry's eye inevitably toward the Marchetti warehouse. The company need never appear. The company merely needed to leave the right doors unlocked.

It was elegant. Corvane permitted himself a small pleasure in its elegance, the way he permitted himself one measure of brandy.

There was a risk, of course, and he was honest enough with himself — honesty with oneself being the only honesty he practiced — to name it. The boy was not stupid. The boy had corrected a manifest at fourteen and hidden the correction inside an ordinary Tuesday. If the letter smelled of the company, the boy might bolt, or worse, might go looking upriver for whoever had sent it, and Corvane's river had bodies in it that he would prefer stayed theoretical.

Which was why the boy would be watched. Closely, quietly, and by a professional.

* * *

Tobias Grell arrived at nine, shown up not by the stairs but by the back passage that let one enter the study without passing the tally-room. Grell had a face like a closed ledger and a way of standing that suggested he had been paid not merely to observe but to become difficult to remember. Corvane had used him twice before, both times for work that required patience and left no receipts.

"The Vane boy," Corvane said, without preamble. "I want to know where he goes, who he speaks to, and what changes in his habits. Especially what changes."

Grell nodded slowly, filing the instruction somewhere behind his eyes. "Anything particular I'm watching for?"

"Contact with the Marchettis. The daughter especially — Ilse. She'll be in the city still, pinned to the warehouse by the registry. If the boy moves toward her, I want to know within hours, not days." Corvane turned to the window, giving Grell the view of the dark harbor, which was as close as he ever came to showing a man anything. "And if anyone approaches the boy — anyone at all, however trivial — that I want faster."

"And if he does meet the girl?"

"Nothing. You report. Nothing else." Corvane let the silence stretch just long enough to make the point. "The boy is an investment, Mr. Grell. Investments are protected. They are not handled, not hurried, not damaged. An investment ripens on schedule, and my schedule is not yours to adjust."

"Understood." Grell paused at the door, one hand on the frame. "The girl. If the registry signs and she's shipped out —"

"Then the window closes and we've wasted our money on watching. If it doesn't sign," Corvane said, "she becomes the most useful enemy of the state in Saltmere, because she gives my investment a reason to act. People are never so predictable as when they're keeping a promise."

Grell's face did not change, but Corvane had the impression, fleeting and unwelcome, that something behind it did — a flicker, a private calculation, the smallest adjustment of a man deciding what portion of his employer's business was worth remembering and what portion might profitably be misplaced. Corvane noted it and let it pass. Watchers were like knives; you didn't inspect the edge every day, you simply kept your free hand ready.

When the door closed, Corvane returned to his desk and, from the locked drawer, took out a single sheet of cream-laid paper. On it, in a clerk's hand he had hired precisely because it resembled no hand in his employ, was the draft of a letter addressed to *the Vane family* — a name no official document had spoken in eight years.

He read it once more, weighing each line the way he weighed a stone: color, weight, the likelihood it would catch light correctly in another man's eyes. Names of ships. Dates of runs. A list of suppliers with one name circled. Enough to terrify a careful young man, and shaped so that every road of inquiry it opened led east, toward the quay, toward the warehouse, toward the prize.

He would not send it tomorrow. Timing was the whole art. The letter must arrive when the boy was already unsettled, already listening for his own name in other people's mouths. A week, perhaps two. Let Grell's reports tell him when the ground was ready.

Until then, the letter went back in the drawer, and Aldous Corvane went back to his ledger of debts and favors, where the entry for Wendell Vane waited with the patience of everything he owned.

*An investment,* he wrote in his mind, closing the day's accounts. *And investments, unlike people, always yield.*

Out in the tally-room attic, above two hundred sleeping ledgers, the investment was turning something small and hard over in his pocket — and Corvane, blowing out his lamp, fell asleep content in the certainty that he knew exactly what it was.
