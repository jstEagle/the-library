## 6. Salt in the Wound

The diving quarter still smelled of salt and tar, though the boats were gone.

Wendell felt it before he saw it — the way a man feels rain coming in his knee. Seven years since he'd walked these streets, and his body remembered them better than his mind did: the slope of Cobbler's Lane where the cobbles went slick with spray, the low arch of the fish-gate, the particular gray of the harbor water here, shallower and greener than at the company docks, where Corvane's cranes had taught it to run dark.

He had told himself he was walking for air. He had told himself a great many things that morning, standing at the counting-house window with the burned letter's ash still ghosting through him like a fever. The letter was destroyed. The words were not. *Suppliers to the ring — the Vane family — cargo moved under dive-boat cover.* He could recite it now with his eyes shut, the way other men recited scripture or debt schedules. His father's name was in it. His mother's name was in it. And the handwriting had been half-familiar in a way he couldn't place, which was worse than unfamiliar, because familiarity meant someone who knew them, and someone who knew them meant this wasn't paper — it was memory, weaponized.

So he walked. Past the net-menders' shed, boarded now. Past the chandler's, which had become a pawnshop, which said everything about the war years without saying a word. The silence he'd practiced for seven years sat in him differently out here. In the counting house, silence was armor. Here it felt like an accent — the local tongue of a country he'd been deported from.

Maren Ostergaard lived where she'd always lived, in the narrow house at the end of Gull Row with the blue door that had faded to the color of old bruises. Wendell stood across the lane a long while, watching smoke rise thin from her chimney, working up the nerve of a man about to knock on his own past.

She'd been his mother's nearest friend. She'd mended nets beside her on the seawall every morning of Wendell's childhood, two women with their skirts pinned up and their hands moving, talking in the low continuous way of women who have outlived enough to talk plainly. When the Vane family was broken apart — father drowned, mother taken by the coughing sickness two winters later, boy indentured off to settle the debts — Maren was the one who'd packed his single trunk. He remembered her hands on his shoulders at the gate. He remembered what she'd said, because it was one of maybe six sentences from that year that survived intact:

*Say little, Wendell. Companies keep ledgers on mouths.*

He knocked.

* * *

She opened the door before his knuckles fell the third time, as if she'd been standing behind it since dawn, waiting for exactly him.

"Well," she said.

"Maren."

"You've got his shoulders." Her eyes went over him the way she'd once gone over a torn net, finding every weakness without pity. "And your mother's way of standing like the ground owes you nothing. Come in before the whole lane sees a Corvane collar at my door."

Inside smelled of kelp tea and lamp oil and, beneath it, faintly, of brine that no amount of years would scrub from a house this close to the water. The room was small and clean and crowded with the furniture of long habit. On the mantel sat a row of things that stopped his breath for half a second: a dried sea-star, a brass diving weight worn smooth, and a single pearl in a cracked dish, dull as a fingernail.

"Sit," she said. "Tea?"

"Thank you."

She poured without ceremony and sat opposite him, and for a moment neither of them spoke, and the silence between them was different from any silence Wendell had learned in seven years. It was an old silence. A shared one. It had room in it for both of them.

"You came about the letter," she said.

The cup nearly slipped in his hand. He set it down too hard; tea jumped the rim.

"I don't know what you—"

"Wendell." She said it the way his mother used to, flat and final, closing a door gently so as not to wake anyone. "You're twenty-three years old and you walk into Gull Row wearing a company coat with your collar turned up and your eyes doing inventory of every window on the lane. You came about something. There's only one thing left in this world that would bring you here." She folded her hands. "A letter. Paper with names on it. Am I wrong?"

He looked at her for a long time. Then he heard himself say, quietly, the thing he had not said aloud to anyone:

"They named us. The Vane family. Suppliers to the smuggling ring. It said cargo moved under dive-boat cover during the war, and after, under armistice cover. It named my father."

"And you burned it."

"How did you—"

"Because you're your mother's son, and your mother burned things too. Letters, receipts, anything with a name she didn't choose." Maren drank her tea. "Burning is a prayer, in this quarter. We've been saying it for years."

Wendell's chest felt tight. "Is it true?"

He hadn't meant to ask it so baldly. He had rehearsed a dozen gentler approaches on the walk over — oblique questions about the war, about the boats, about who kept what records. But sitting in the blue-doored house with the dull pearl on the mantel, all his careful silences collapsed at once, the way a bad wall collapses, all in one piece.

Maren set down her cup. She looked at him for a long moment, and whatever she was deciding, she decided it fully, the way she seemed to decide everything.

"Yes," she said.

One word. It went through him like cold water through cloth.

"Your father ran cargo," she said. "From the second year of the war until the last month of it. Not guns, mostly. Medicine, mostly — quinine, iodine, surgical silk when we could get it. Some of it went to the naval hospital. Some went to people in this quarter whose children would be dead otherwise, and I'll name three of those children right now if you want me to, because they're grown and alive and they buy their bread on this street." Her voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about it. It stayed level as a tide table. "And some of it was just cargo. Coffee, tobacco, silk, things that made other men rich while your father took a diver's cut to keep bread on your table. That part's true too. I won't dress it for you."

"He never—" Wendell stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "He never said anything."

"What would he have said? 'Wendell, tonight Papa will be committing a crime so you can eat'?" She shook her head slowly. "Children are the first thing smugglers protect. From the knowledge. Your father used to say the boats carried ballast. Everyone on the quay understood what ballast meant and nobody said so. It was a whole language of not-saying, and your family spoke it fluently, because they had to."

"The letter said the Vane name is on manifests. Real ones."

"It would be. Names get written down. That's the cruelty of paper — it remembers only that a thing happened, never why." Maren leaned forward. "Listen to me now. Your father was not a criminal the way the magistrates mean it. He was a man with two mouths to feed in a port the navy had blockaded, and the blockade didn't care whether the Vane table had food on it. He dove for pearls by day and ran cargo by night because pearls paid pennies and cargo paid pounds, and because a man will do almost anything rather than watch his wife count out the same three potatoes and pretend it's a meal."

Wendell found he couldn't look at her. He looked instead at the brass diving weight on the mantel, worn smooth by decades of palms. His father's palm had worn part of that smoothness. His own hands, soft from seven years of ledger ink, suddenly felt like a betrayal of something.

"I spent seven years being ashamed of them," he said. The words came out before he could weigh them, which was how the truest ones always came. "Of the name. At Corvane they teach you shame like arithmetic. Every clerk knows the indentured boy's family ended badly, and nobody says it, and the not-saying is the lesson. I built myself out of not-saying. And now there's a letter telling me the worst thing I believed about them is true, and I don't—" His voice caught. He hated it. He pressed on. "I don't know which shame is the real one anymore."

Maren was quiet for a while. Outside, gulls argued over something in the lane.

"Come here," she said finally, and rose, and went to the mantel.

She picked up the brass weight and put it in his hands. It was heavier than it looked. Cold at first, then warming against his palms.

"That belonged to your grandfather, and his before him. It has been in this family of the harbor longer than Corvane has been in Saltmere." She tapped the pearl dish with one finger. "And that was your mother's. Your father brought it up on his last dive — the last honest one, some said, though I never thought there was such a thing as a dishonest dive. She gave it to me the week before she died. Said, 'Keep it where someone will remember us without charging interest.'"

Wendell held the weight and said nothing, because there was nothing in his trained vocabulary that fit.

"Here is what I know about shame," Maren said. "It's a tax the comfortable levy on everyone else. Corvane collects it in the counting house. The magistrates collect it in their courts. They take your family's hunger and they write it down as greed, because paper can't hold hunger, only numbers, and numbers make it easy to call a starving man a thief." She took the weight back from him gently and returned it to its place, and her hand rested on it a moment, as if steadying it. "Your father fed people. In a war. Under a blockade. With the sea trying to kill him every night and the law waiting to hang him every morning. If that's a crime, then this whole quarter is a prison, and half of Saltmere should be in irons."

"And the other half owns the prisons," Wendell said.

"Now you sound like your mother."

He laughed — a short, cracked thing, but a laugh. Then it left him, and what remained underneath was colder and more precise, and he heard his own voice go flat the way it went flat in the counting house when the numbers wouldn't reconcile.

"Maren. The letter wasn't sent to warn me. It wasn't blackmail, not yet. It was addressed to *the Vane family* — like a summons, like bait. Someone wants me to react. Someone wants the Vane name moving again, visible, attached to something." He paused. "Someone at Corvane."

Her face changed. Not fear exactly — recognition. The look of a woman watching a tide come in that she'd predicted months ago.

"Sooner than I thought," she murmured.

"What does that mean? Sooner than you *thought*?"

Maren looked at him a long moment. Then she rose and crossed to the dresser in the corner — an old thing of whalebone joints and salt-swollen wood — and stood with her hand on the drawer, not opening it.

"When the war ended and the investigations started, a lot of people in this quarter got nervous. Manifests, receipts, letters. Things with names." Her fingers drummed once on the drawer. "Some people burned theirs. Some people buried theirs. And some people — fools, or saints, I've never decided which — *kept* theirs. Kept accounts of who did what, and who ordered what done, and who got paid, all through the war years. Private ledgers. Because somebody ought to remember the truth even if the truth never gets its day."

Wendell felt his heart begin to move faster. "Whose ledger?"

"A woman doesn't survive forty years in this harbor by handing out answers to a boy in a company coat." But her eyes, when they met his, were not unkind. They were the eyes of someone measuring a rope before trusting it with weight. "You say someone at Corvane sent you a letter naming dead men to bait a living boy. I believe you. And I'll tell you something for free, Wendell Vane, because I loved your mother and I owe her a debt no bank ever managed to collect: whoever wrote that letter knows this quarter keeps memories. And if they know that, they'll come looking for the memories too. Sooner than later."

"Then tell me what you know before they do."

"Paper doesn't protect people." She said it softly, like a proverb she'd paid for. "People protect paper. Remember that, whatever happens next. And remember this door opens to you."

She didn't open the drawer. Instead she poured him a second cup of tea he didn't want and talked for an hour about small things — the weather, the price of coal, which of the old net-menders had died — and Wendell understood he was being dismissed and kept, both at once, and that the keeping mattered more.

At the door, she caught his sleeve.

"Your father once told me something, the night before his last dive. He was frightened — I could see it, though he smiled through it the way your mother did. He said: 'If the sea takes me, tell my boy the water doesn't care what name you carry. It only cares whether you can hold your breath.'" Her grip tightened. "Hold your breath, Wendell. Whatever comes next. Don't spend it all in one place."

He walked back up the lane with the harbor at his shoulder, and the shame he'd carried for seven years sat differently in him now — not lighter, but re-cut, the way a jeweler re-cuts a stone and finds it was worth more in its true shape. His father had run cargo to feed him. His mother had burned paper to protect him. Everything he'd been taught to call disgrace was survival, worn smooth as the brass weight, passed hand to hand down the years to arrive in his palms tonight.

Behind him, in the blue-doored house, Maren Ostergaard stood at her window until he turned the corner.

Then she opened the drawer, took out a ledger bound in oiled canvas, and began, by lamplight, to read.
