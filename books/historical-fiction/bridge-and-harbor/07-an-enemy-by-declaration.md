## 7. An Enemy by Declaration

The registry notice went up on the Exchange door at half past eight on a Tuesday morning, and by nine the whole harbor had read it twice.

I heard it before I understood it. I was in the counting house with the ledgers open and the scale weights laid out in their ranked rows, and through the shutters came the sound of Saltmere doing what Saltmere always did when something changed: talking it to death. Voices stacking on voices out in the yard, the words too broken up by glass and distance to arrive whole. I might have ignored it. Seven years teaches you that most noise is weather.

Then Pell came through the door with his coat still buttoned wrong and said, "They've posted a new list," and his voice had a crack down the middle of it, and I stopped being able to pretend it was weather.

"What list?"

"Enemy registry. Provisional." He set a stack of invoices down without looking at them, which was how I knew he'd already forgotten he was carrying them. "Pending final terms, whatever that means. Eleven names. Fishermen first, then merchants." He swallowed. "Marchetti's on it."

The room did not change. That was the strange thing. The scale weights stayed in their rows, the brass arms of the balance hung exactly where they'd hung, the ledger lay open at the page of pearls I would never own, and outside a gull screamed its one stupid scream, and none of it moved at all. Only I moved. My hand drifted to my boot cuff, where the memory of a letter lived now instead of paper, and pressed flat against nothing.

"Pell," I said, and my voice came out level, which surprised me, because underneath the levelness something had begun to run — not panic exactly, but motion, like water finding the crack in a hull. "When did this go up?"

"This morning. There's a crowd at the Exchange already. Some of the names are old men. One of them's dead, far as anyone knows — the Kessler fellow, lost off the Shelf two years back." He laughed, a short ugly sound with no humor in it. "They're declaring war on ghosts."

"And the Marchetti warehouse."

"Sealed yesterday. Wax on the doors, customs men on the gate. You can see it from the quay if you—" He caught himself. He looked at me then, really looked, the way people look at a man when they suddenly remember there's a person inside the clerk. "Wendell. I didn't think. Your—"

"It's fine," I said. "Log the invoices. The Corvane shipment from the north bank needs countersigning by noon or we eat the penalty."

He stood there another moment, wanting to say something kind and knowing kindness would cost us both, and then he turned back to his desk and the room resumed its ordinary arithmetic. That is the shape of survival in a place like Saltmere: someone tells you terrible news, and you both agree, without a word passing, to pretend it arrived in a language neither of you speaks.

I counted pearls for four hours. I know I did, because the tally sheet exists somewhere with my initials on it and the sums are correct. I have no memory of a single one of them. My hands worked and my mind was elsewhere, running the streets of this city in loops, measuring distances the way a drowning man measures the surface above him.

* * *

Here is what everyone in Saltmere knew about the Marchettis, and what I knew better than anyone:

Emil Marchetti had come to the harbor thirty years earlier with a cargo manifest, a wife, and a daughter on the way, and had built a warehousing business on the simple principle that a man's goods should be safer with him than anywhere else. He kept his promises the way other men kept ledgers — obsessively, in ink, initialed. During the war years, when every nation on the coast was hungry for cargo that could pass quietly, half the trading houses in Saltmere had come to him with offers wrapped in patriotism, and Emil Marchetti had turned them all away and posted his neutrality on the warehouse door like a lamp in a window. *This house trades with no flag.* It cost him contracts. It bought him the right to still look at himself in a mirror, which in those years was a currency rarer than pearls.

And Ilse. Ilse, who at fourteen could name every ship in the harbor by the sound of her engine. Ilse, who had once argued a customs inspector out of a wrongful seal with nothing but the correct regulation read aloud from memory. Ilse, who that last peacetime summer had stood on the loading bridge with her hair coming loose from its braid and told me that when she ran her father's business someday, the first thing she'd do was raise wages and the second thing she'd do was never, ever learn to lie about numbers.

She was in that sealed building. Or near it. Trapped behind wax and customs men in a city that had just voted, on paper, that her name belonged to the enemy.

*Pending final terms.* Everyone repeated the phrase like an incantation, as if saying it enough times would make it mean something merciful. But I had grown up in the counting houses; I knew what *pending* meant. It meant the blade was raised but not yet dropped, and everything in the interval — every arrest, every seizure, every auction — could be done in the shadow of a signature that hadn't landed yet. Pending wasn't mercy. Pending was a clock.

* * *

At midday I took my meal hour and walked, because sitting still had become impossible.

I didn't decide to go toward the harbor quarter. My feet decided, and I let them, which is a thing I had trained myself never to do in seven years at Corvane. But the news had knocked something loose in me, some bolt deep in the keel, and the discipline that had kept me alive leaked out through the gap.

The Exchange square was thick with people reading the posted list. They stood in a rough crescent, hats in hands, lips moving over the eleven names like mourners at a graveside — and they were mourners, though no one had died yet. A woman beside me copied the names onto a scrap of paper with a pencil stub, pressing so hard she tore through in two places. An old stevedore read the list aloud to a companion whose eyes had gone; he pronounced each name slowly, giving each one its full weight, the way you ring a bell.

"...Marchetti, Ilse, of the Harbor Road warehouses..."

Hearing her name in a stranger's mouth did something to me that seeing it in print could not have done. Print is quiet. A voice makes it real. Somewhere under my ribs a door I had kept shut since I was seventeen swung open on its hinge, and out of it came a summer.

I had made her a promise once. I had sealed it with a pearl. I had been a boy then, and boys make promises the way they throw stones — for the pleasure of the arc, never imagining where the stone will land. But the stone lands. The promise waits at the bottom of the water, patient as anything drowned, until the day your hand goes into your pocket and finds it still there.

I walked past the Marchetti warehouse at a pace designed to be unremarkable, and looked at it the way you look at a thing with your whole body while your face does nothing.

The customs seal sat on the main doors, a disc of red wax the size of my palm, stamped with the double anchor of the port authority. Two men in customs grey stood at the gate, bored in the particular way of men guarding nothing yet — because the warehouse was empty of people, officially, its contents impounded pending terms. The windows were shuttered. The loading bridge that reached out over the water like a wooden arm hung locked and chained.

Officially empty. Officially sealed.

But I had grown up in the diving quarter, where children learned early that the sea shows you one surface and keeps another underneath, and I looked at those shuttered windows with a diver's eyes, and I saw what the customs men, bored behind their wax, had not troubled to see: that the third-floor shutter on the seaward side did not sit flush. That it breathed, very slightly, with the wind off the water. Shutters on an empty building do not breathe.

Someone was living in there. Someone who knew which windows faced away from the gate, and how to move quietly across floorboards, and how to keep a light so dim it looked like dusk even at midnight.

Someone like a girl who had spent her childhood in that building, who knew its bones better than the men who'd sealed it.

I walked on. I did not slow my step. Every instinct that had kept me alive for seven years screamed at me to keep walking, to go back to the counting house, to log the afternoon inventory and let the tide carry this away the way it carried everything, because Wendell Vane did not get noticed, did not get involved, did not let his face tell anyone anything.

But the letter in my boot cuff — burned, memorized, living now in the tissue of me like shrapnel — had named my family among smugglers, and the registry on the Exchange door had named hers among enemies, and both lists had come from the same inkwell, I would have bet the seven years of my life on it. Someone was arranging these papers the way a hand arranges pieces on a board. And if I let the hand finish its arrangement, Ilse Marchetti would end up a casualty of somebody else's arithmetic, filed and forgotten, and I would spend the rest of my life being exactly what I had always been: a man who knew which silences kept people safe, and chose them, every time, until the choosing was all he was.

No. Not again. Not her.

* * *

I went back to Corvane. I signed what needed signing. I answered Mr. Pell's careful, sideways questions about my health with the flat pleasant nothing he needed to hear. I catalogued a consignment of south-sea buttons and a crate of navigational instruments bound for a buyer whose name I will not write here because it does not matter anymore. I was, for six hours, the perfect clerk: invisible, accurate, silent.

All the while, under the silence, a plan was assembling itself the way plans do — not in words but in weights and measures. Distances. Times. The interval between the watch change at the customs gate, which I had learned years ago out of pure professional habit and which now unspooled in my memory like a gift: forty minutes, sometimes fifty, when the evening shift overlapped and both men retreated to the guard hut against the cold. The loading bridge, chained — but chains are only as strong as their nearest staple, and staples rust in salt air, and I knew which ones. The water below the bridge, deep enough at high tide to bring a small boat right under the timbers. The tide tables were public. So was the moon.

I left at six. The fog was coming in off the harbor the way it did in autumn, softening the masts along the quay into a forest of grey pencils, muffling the city into something almost kind. I turned north, toward the diving quarter, because there was one more thing I needed before nightfall and only one person in Saltmere who could give it to me: Maren Ostrand, my mother's old neighbor, keeper of other people's histories, who had watched the Vane family disappear into Corvane's ledger books one signature at a time and had never once asked me a question I couldn't survive answering.

If I was going to do this — if I was going to walk toward a sealed warehouse in the dark, past customs men, under the nose of whatever intelligence had written my family's name into a smuggling plot — then I refused to do it blind. Maren had lived sixty years in this harbor. She knew how seals were enforced and how they were evaded, which patrols drank where, and, I suspected, more than she had ever admitted about the letters that moved between the trading houses like rats in a wall.

The fog thickened as I climbed. By the time I reached the diving quarter the lamps were haloes and the cottages swam up out of the white one at a time, and standing at the mouth of the lane where I'd been born, I felt the old fear and the new resolve grind against each other like two stones making fire.

Somewhere behind me, back down the hill, I had the prickling certainty — the diver's sense, the feeling of water pressure changing before any visible current — that I was not entirely alone on the road. I stopped. I listened. Fog gives back nothing but your own breath.

Nothing. Probably nothing.

Maren's window glowed dull orange through the mist. I knocked, and while I waited for her footsteps I stood very still and made myself one final promise, smaller than the first one but harder: that whatever I found in the sealed dark of the Marchetti warehouse tonight, I would not walk away from it again.

The latch lifted. And Maren, looking at my face, said, "Before you ask me for anything, boy — come in, and tell me why there's a man been standing at the end of this lane for the last hour."
