## 9. Through the Sealed Door

The loading bridge of the Marchetti warehouse had been built for men carrying crates, not for a clerk in borrowed boots trying not to make the boards confess. Wendell crossed it at low tide, when the harbor's breathing was quietest and the gap between the seawall stair and the bridge's iron lip shrank to something a man might clear without prayer. He had measured that gap with his eyes three times from the water side over two nights. Tonight he closed it.

His palms found cold iron. His boots found wood. And then he was inside the estate of a family declared enemies of the state, standing on planks that smelled of tar and salt and, beneath all of it, faintly of coffee — the smell of the old trade, the smell of Giorgio Marchetti's warehouses before the war made cargo a crime.

The seal on the main door had been wax and ribbon, official as a gravestone. But seals only close doors, and doors are not the whole of a building. The loading bridge led to the upper gallery, the long loft where the finest stock had once been sorted under north-facing windows. If Ilse was anywhere, she would be there. He knew this the way he knew the tides: not by reasoning but by having grown up beside them.

He moved along the gallery by feel. The city's blackout discipline — half precaution, half theater now that the fighting had stopped everywhere but in the signing rooms — left the interior black as the inside of a boot. His fingers read the wall: a crate stenciled in his memory even before his hand found the letters. *MARCHETTI & SONS — MOTHER-OF-PEARL, GRADE A — SALT MERE.* The stencils got older as he went. One of them, near the gallery's end, had been painted over twice, and beneath the paint his thumb caught the groove of an even older word. He didn't need light for it. He had been eleven years old the day that crate came in, standing on his father's shoulders to see the unloading, because his father and Giorgio Marchetti had been friends in the way divers and traders sometimes were — one risking his lungs, the other refusing to cheat him for it.

At the gallery's far end there was a faint line of gray. Not a window. A shutter, imperfectly closed, letting in the harbor lights' reflection off the low clouds.

And against that gray, a shape. Sitting very still.

"Ilse," he said.

The shape did not move for so long that he heard his own heart twice. Then a voice came out of the dark, level and quiet, the voice of someone who had rehearsed being brave alone.

"If you're here for the inventory, magistrate, you're late by four days. Everything worth taking has been taken."

"It's Wendell."

Another silence. Different in texture. He could hear her deciding things.

"Show me your hands," she said.

He held them up into the thin gray light, palms out, empty. She rose from whatever she'd been sitting on — a crate, he thought; she had been sitting on a crate like a queen refusing a throne — and came toward him slowly, and stopped an arm's length away, and looked at his face the way people look at photographs they suspect have been altered.

"They said you were still there," she said. "Corvane's. Seven years."

"The paper says seven. The paper lies about most things but not that."

"You grew."

"So did you."

It was a stupid thing to say. It was also true, and trueness mattered more than elegance tonight. The girl who had stood on the pier with a pearl pressed into her palm had been all edges and laughter. The woman in front of him had been sanded down somewhere in the years between — not softened, exactly. Weathered. Like timber that had decided which grain it wanted to keep.

"You shouldn't be here," she said.

"No," he agreed. "Probably not."

She laughed then — one short breath through the nose, almost nothing, and yet it undid him more than any embrace could have. Because it was the same laugh. Seven years, two wars' worth of paper, an enemy registry with her name on it, and the laugh had survived all of it like a coin in a wreck.

They sat on the crates with a plank's width between them, then less, then none. Her shoulder against his felt like a fact established after long dispute.

* * *

She told him in pieces, the way people tell things they've told no one.

Her father had died in the second winter of the war — not at sea, which would have been a clean grief, but in a magistrate's waiting room, waiting to answer questions about a shipment he had never sent. His heart had simply concluded its business early, Ilse said, in the manner of a merchant closing a bad account. By spring the warehouse was sealed. By summer the registry notice went up on the customs house door, and her mother took the younger children inland to cousins in Veldt, and Ilse stayed, because someone had to stay, because a sealed building that is entirely empty gets auctioned within the month, but a sealed building with a light moving behind the shutters becomes a legal question, and legal questions move slowly.

"So you haunt your own house," Wendell said.

"I keep my own house." Her chin lifted. "The distinction matters to me."

"How?"

"There's a rain barrel on the roof. The harbor patrol checks the ground floor weekly and the gallery monthly, on Thursdays, at the change of watch. I know their boots better than my own. I've lived worse, Wendell. I lived well until quite recently."

But her hands, when he took them, were cold past the point of pride, and there was a bread crust folded in a handkerchief beside her crate that had gone hard as slate, and he understood that *lived worse* was a sentence spoken in the dark, in the past tense, to keep it from becoming present.

"Why didn't you send for me?" he asked. "I told you—"

"I know what you told me."

"—seven years ago. I said if ever—"

"I know." She pulled her hands back, not in anger but to fold them, and he watched her choose her words like coins counted out of a tight purse. "A promise is a heavy thing, Wendell. I didn't want to spend yours. Every week I thought, next month the terms will be signed, the registry will be revised, we'll be merely foreign again instead of enemies, and I'll walk out of here upright and owe nobody. Next month. Next month." She looked at her folded hands. "Then last week a letter came under the door. Not to me. To the estate. Official seal. It said the property would be posted for auction unless outstanding claims against the family were resolved. Outstanding claims. My father has been dead four years and the claims are growing in his grave."

Something turned over in Wendell's chest. "What kind of letter? Who sealed it?"

"I don't know. It had no name. That's what frightens me — everything else in this city comes with names attached. Even the arrests."

He was silent a moment. Then, because silence had been his profession for seven years and he was tired of practicing it where it wasn't needed: "Ilse. I received a letter too."

He told her. All of it — the courier, the address written to *the Vane family*, the name no one had spoken aloud since his father's boat came back empty, the words he had memorized and burned. Suppliers to a ring still running cargo under armistice cover. His father named. His mother named. Himself named, a boy of nine, which meant whoever wrote it either didn't care about accuracy or cared very much about terror.

When he finished, Ilse was looking at him with an expression he couldn't read in the gray light.

"When did it come?" she asked.

"Nineteen days ago."

"And the auction letter came under my door twelve days ago." Her voice had gone flat and quick, the trader's daughter doing sums. "Yours first. Mine second. Yours names your family as suppliers. Mine threatens seizure of assets. Two letters, three weeks apart, aimed at a pearl diver's son and a dead importer's daughter, neither of whom owns anything worth the wax on the envelope." She shook her head slowly. "Unless the letters aren't about us at all. Unless we're the weight on the scale, and something heavier is being weighed."

"What could be heavier than a family?"

"A warehouse full of Grade A shell on the best harbor frontage in Saltmere," she said, "two weeks before the armistice terms turn enemy seizures into theft."

The words settled over both of them, cold and clarifying. Somewhere out on the water a buoy bell rang once, and neither of them moved.

* * *

They talked until the gray light strengthened toward silver, and what began as information became, without either of them marking the moment, conversation, and what began as conversation became the particular pleasure of two people discovering that the person they had promised things to had kept growing on the other side of the years, like a garden visited rarely.

She wanted to know about Corvane's. He gave her the counting house in small vivid strokes — the ledgers ruled in red for debts and black for holdings, Mr. Pryce's cough that meant *mind the third drawer*, the way pearls passed across the scales in little velvet troughs like eggs in nests that belonged to someone else. He did not tell her about Aldous Corvane studying him too long in the corridor, the look like a thumb pressing fruit at market. There was no reason to tell her that. He filed it away in the place where he kept things that had no use yet.

She told him about the crates around them, and this became a kind of tour conducted by voice, since the dark forbade anything else. This stack, she said, was her father's final purchase — shell from the deep beds off Kessel Point, bought from a diver whose name her father always spoke respectfully, never *my supplier* but *my colleague*. That long row was finished work that never shipped: buttons blanks, knife handles, inlay panels, all the small immortal luxuries of a peacetime world, crated and waiting out the war like soldiers in winter quarters. And against the east wall —

Against the east wall there was one crate apart from the others, smaller, and she was quiet next to it for a moment before she said, "This one I opened. You're allowed to open your own father's boxes, I decided. Nobody wrote a law against it."

"What's in it?"

"His ledgers. The real ones. Not the ones the customs men copied." She tapped the lid once, softly, like knocking at a door she didn't expect answered. "Every dive he ever bought, every diver, every fair price and every price he quietly corrected upward because the man's boat needed repair. Fourteen years of decency in accounting ink." Her voice thinned. "If they auction this building, Wendell, the crates go to whoever pays. The ledgers go to nobody. That's the part I can't — that's the part that keeps me here. Not the shell. The proof that he was good."

Wendell reached out in the dark and found her hand among the shadows, and this time she let him keep it.

"He was good to my father," Wendell said. "My father dove for other buyers who shorted the weight and paid late. He dove for your father eleven seasons and never once came home angry. In our house that counted as a miracle. My mother used to say Giorgio Marchetti weighed honest because he was honest all the way down, and it saved everyone the trouble of watching him."

Ilse made a sound that was half laugh, half something wetter, and pressed her forehead briefly against his shoulder, and he held very still, the way you hold still when a wild thing has chosen to land near you and the wrong movement could undo the choosing.

"Wendell," she said into the cloth of his coat. "The promise. Do you remember the exact words?"

"I've never once forgotten the exact words."

"Say them anyway. I want to hear them in your grown-up voice. The boy's voice was all sea air and no idea what he was saying."

He smiled in the dark where no one could tax him for it. "'If ever you need me,'" he said, "'send nothing and say nothing. I'll come. I'll know.'" A pause. "You added the part about the pearl. I just promised to come. You made it binding."

"That's what girls do," she said. "We notarize."

Outside, distantly, a whistle sounded — one note, then a second answering from farther along the quay. Change of watch. Not Thursday. Early, or rerouted, or curious.

Ilse's head came up off his shoulder like a deer's. In the silver seam of the shutter-light he watched her become a different animal entirely — silent, total, listening with her spine. Boots on the quay stones. Not many. Three pairs, maybe four, walking without hurry, which was somehow worse than running. Lantern-glow began to breathe against the shutter slats, thin gold lines sliding across the crates, across her face, across the stencil of his own name in a memory thirty feet away.

"Ground floor or bridge?" she breathed.

"Bridge," he said. "I came in over the water. I'll leave the same."

"They don't check the bridge at night."

"They checked everything tonight, apparently."

The lantern lines crawled up the far wall. Someone outside tried the main door — Wendell heard the wax seal crack further, heard the lock take the strain and hold. A voice muttered. Another answered. The boots did not leave. They distributed themselves instead, unhurried, the way men do when they plan to wait.

"Ilse." He kept his voice below the sound of the harbor. "Thursday. They come Thursday. Is that still true?"

"It was true yesterday."

"Then hold until Thursday. I'll come back over the bridge with more than promises. There's a ship — I'll find out schedules, papers, whether neutral tonnage is still calling here. There's a way out of this. I'm going to find it and bring it to you in pieces if I have to."

Her hand found his wrist and gripped it, hard, harder than tenderness, the grip of someone transferring a weight. "Wendell. If those lanterns belong to whoever wrote your letter, then coming back isn't bravery, it's delivery. You'd be walking the evidence to the post."

"Then I'll come back differently each time," he said. "They'll never collect the same man twice."

She laughed again — that single breath, there and gone — and then she pulled him to her and kissed him once, fast and fierce, tasting of cold and hunger and four years of sealed rooms, and before he could arrange his heart around it she was pushing him backward along the gallery, toward the bridge, toward the gap, toward the water.

"Thursday," she whispered. "Bring the way out. Nothing else is worth the risk."

The lanterns swung wide outside and threw the shutter-slats into motion, bars of light sweeping the gallery like the ribs of some enormous fish, and in the last of them he saw her face set and pale and certain, and then he was through the door onto the bridge with the wind off the harbor hitting him like a hand.

He dropped from the bridge's end into the tide channel, surfaced beside the seawall, and hung there in the black water with his borrowed boots dragging at him, looking up at the warehouse while the patrol's voices murmured above. One window glowed faintly where a shutter had shifted. Then it, too, went dark, and the building became again what the papers said it was: sealed, enemy, forfeit.

He swam for the shallows, climbed the rocks at the point, wrung out his coat in the shadow of the pilot's hut, and started the long walk back to the clerks' quarter with his heart doing something that was no longer fear, or no longer only fear.

Thursday. Three days. And somewhere in this city sat a man with steady handwriting, a fondness for official wax, and reasons of his own — and Wendell intended, before Thursday's tide, to learn his name.
