## 10. Terms Not Yet Signed

The lantern had to be turned down to a sliver before Ilse could speak above a whisper. Even then, she kept her voice low, the way you keep your voice in a sickroom, because the warehouse was sick — sealed, stamped, dying by decree — and sound carried through it like fever through a body.

"Sit," she said.

Wendell didn't sit. He stood among the crates with his cap in his hands, turning it the way he used to turn things over as a boy, and she remembered that about him: Wendell Vane never sat down when he was frightened. Sitting was surrender. Standing was somewhere to run from.

"There's no patrol for another hour," she said. "I've counted them. Eleven o'clock and three, and the eleven o'clock man is lazy. He cuts through Fishmonger's Row to warm his hands at the brazier there. Sit down."

He sat on an upturned crate beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched, and the touch went through her like current through water. Seven years. Seven years of letters that stopped coming, of rumors that came instead, and now here he was smelling of ink and counting-house dust, alive, real, looking at her as if she might dissolve if he blinked.

"You said you'd explain," he said.

"I said I'd try." She pulled her coat tighter. The warehouse was cold in a way buildings are cold when no one is allowed to light a stove in them — a legal cold, an official cold. "First you have to understand what's actually been done to us. Because everyone in this city thinks the war ended in November, and everyone is wrong."

"The armistice—"

"Is not a treaty." She held up a hand, ticking fingers the way her father used to when he taught her the pearl trade. "It's a pause. A holding of breath. Nothing has been signed, Wendell. The terms are still being argued over in rooms we'll never see, by men who've never smelled salt water. Which means everything the provisional government has done since November is provisional too. Including the registry."

"Your name is on the registry."

"Our name is *provisionally* on the registry." She laughed, and hated the sound of it, a dry cracked thing. "That word does more work than any word I've ever known. Provisional enemy status. It means we can be seized from today and un-seized tomorrow. It means everything the magistrates have taken from us is being held in a kind of legal fog — ours, but not ours; stolen, but not yet officially stolen. It means—" she made herself say it plainly, because plainness was all she had left— "it means our status isn't a judgment. It's a *tool*. Whoever holds it can sharpen it or sheathe it whenever it suits them."

Wendell was quiet. She watched him work something through, his jaw tight, and she thought: he was always the one who listened like other people talked.

"So it could be revoked," he said finally.

"It could be revoked. If the final terms are lenient, if the Marchettis are reclassified, if some clerk finds an error in the file — poof. We're citizens again. Our property comes back. Our warehouse unlocks." She gestured at the dark around them, the ghost-rows of crated goods her father had spent thirty years assembling: nacre, shell, mother-of-pearl blanks, the tools of a trade that had fed half the diving quarter once. "Or it could be confirmed. Hardened. Made permanent. And then everything my father built gets auctioned off to the loyal and the well-connected, legally, cleanly, with the magistrate's own stamp on it."

"And nobody knows which it will be."

"Nobody knows. The negotiators are stalled — that much even the newspapers admit. Every week they don't sign is another week the provisional orders float free, unanchored, doing whatever the people who wrote them want them to do." She leaned forward, elbows on knees. "Do you understand what that means? It means right now, in Saltmere, in this exact season, an enemy declaration is worth whatever someone will pay for it. It's currency. It's leverage. It's a knife that hasn't been assigned an owner yet."

* * *

She had worked it out over six weeks of nights in this warehouse, listening to patrols, reading the auction notices posted and torn down and reposted, talking to the one guard at the seal who took bribes in coffee and gossip. She laid it out for Wendell now the way her father had taught her to lay out lots for a buyer: worst first, best last, nothing hidden.

"Someone wrote you a letter," she said. "Telling you your family ran cargo during the war."

His face changed. In the lantern sliver she saw it happen — the blood leaving, the stillness arriving. "How do you know about the letter?"

"Because of the shape of things. Tell me about it first. Who addressed it? When did it come?"

"It was addressed to *the Vane family.* Not to Corvane, not to me. To a name I haven't heard out loud since I was ten years old." His voice dropped further. "It named my father as a supplier to a smuggling ring. Wartime cargo runs. It said the ring is still operating. Under armistice cover."

"And you'd never heard any of it before?"

"I didn't say that." A long breath. "I didn't know it. There's a difference. My father died when I was small. What he did to feed us — nobody talks about those years in the diving quarter. They bury them."

"Then someone dug him up," Ilse said. "Out of a grave seven years deep. Ask yourself why. Ask yourself *when*."

She let the silence sit. She had learned silence from him, ironically — from the boy who'd survived Corvane's counting house by mastering it. Now she spent it deliberately, giving him room to arrive where she needed him.

"The timing," he said slowly. "It came the same week the Marchetti registry notice went up."

"It did. And here's the rest of the shape." She stood, restless, and paced between two crates of shell blanks she'd slept beside for a month. "My family's assets are frozen under the provisional order. Frozen — not forfeited. There's a difference, and the difference is time. While the armistice terms hang unsettled, the seizure isn't final. But there are men in this city who cannot afford to wait for the final terms. Do you see? If the terms come back soft, the Marchetti holdings revert. Everything my father owned walks back into my family's hands, free and clear. So anyone who wants those holdings has to act *now*, while the law is still soft enough to push through and hard enough to hide behind. They have to convert provisional into permanent before the pen comes down on the actual treaty."

"Auction."

"Auction." She nodded. "A forced sale under enemy-property rules. Legal theft, fast, irreversible — because once it's sold to third parties, even a favorable armistice can't give it back without a hundred lawsuits against every buyer in Saltmere. The men who buy at those auctions know exactly what they're buying: permanence. Finality. A fait accompli wrapped in a magistrate's ribbon."

"But what does that have to do with my father? With me?"

"That's the part I had to work hardest to see." She crouched in front of him. "Think like a ledger, Wendell. You told me yourself you've spent seven years learning how Corvane thinks. Someone wants the Marchetti warehouse. To get it, they need the registry entry hardened — need a *reason* for the magistrates to move from provisional seizure to full forfeiture. Enemy property alone might not be enough if the terms wobble. But enemy property *connected to ongoing smuggling*?" She tapped the crate between them. "That's a different animal. Smuggling under armistice cover is a hanging offense for a family already marked. Evidence of it doesn't just seize a warehouse. It poisons everything attached to it — associates, suppliers, debts, names. It gives whoever holds that evidence power over everyone in the chain."

"And the Vane name is in the chain."

"The Vane name is in the chain. Your father ran cargo — Maren could have told you that, and I'd wager she did. Whoever forged or found that letter knows it. And whoever receives the letter — you — sits *inside Corvane*. Indentured. Trusted with inventory. Trusted with codes, eventually, papers, shipping routes." She watched his eyes and saw him arrive ahead of her, saw the horror bloom. "You're not just a target, Wendell. You're an instrument. Someone needs a scandal pinned to the Marchettis, and needs a man inside Corvane compromised or discredited or blackmailed into providing it. One letter accomplishes both. It threatens you, it threatens me, and it hands whoever wrote it a lever long enough to reach from the diving quarter to the counting house floor."

* * *

Wendell put his head in his hands. She let him have the moment. She knew what it cost — she'd had her own version of it six weeks ago, standing in front of the sealed doors, understanding for the first time that her family's ruin had authors, that it wasn't weather, wasn't fate, wasn't the vague cruelty of history. It had a hand on the pen.

When he looked up, his eyes were wet but his voice was level. "You think it's Corvane himself."

"I think it's someone at Corvane." She made herself be precise; precision was the only weapon she had left. "I can't prove it yet. But look at what's knowable, and by whom. Whoever wrote that letter knows the Vane history — which means access to old harbor records, diving-quarter informants, wartime cargo manifests. That's company material. Corvane handled the shipping insurance on half the coastal trade during the war; if anything documented your father's runs, it lives in their archives. Second: whoever wrote it knows the Marchetti file is weak without corroboration, which means they've read the registry filings. Third: whoever wrote it chose to send it to *you*, inside the company, rather than straight to the magistrates — which means they wanted the threat private first. Blackmail before denunciation. That's not a patriot's move, Wendell. Patriots denounce. This is a buyer's move. Someone building pressure toward a transaction."

"Blackmail toward what transaction?"

"Toward whichever one pays best. Either you deliver them something valuable from inside Corvane — codes, manifests, routes — or you refuse, and they send the same letter onward to the magistrates with your name added as a conspirator's son, and use the resulting scandal to drag the Marchetti file down with the Vane name. Heads they win something, tails they win more." She paused. "And there's a fourth thing. The ugliest one."

"Say it."

"If the Vane name goes to the magistrates as smugglers' kin, and the Vane son is indentured to Corvane Trading — the company inherits the scandal too. Unless the company distances itself. Unless the company makes itself the injured party, the honest house betrayed by a bad indenture. And what does an honest house do, when it discovers its own records show Marchetti goods moving through channels touched by Vane cargo?" She spread her hands. "It petitions for accelerated forfeiture. It demands the auction, to cleanse its books. The company becomes the prosecutor of the very plot it planted."

The lantern guttered. She trimmed the wick with a fingernail and the light steadied.

Wendell stood. He walked to the loading bridge door and looked out at the black water of the harbor, and she watched his shoulders and knew he was doing what he always did — carrying it, weighing it, deciding which silences to spend.

"My father ran cargo to feed us," he said at last, not turning around. "And now his corpse is a key. Somebody picked the lock of the past to steal the future." He turned. "I need to know who."

"So do I. And I'm closer than you think — my father's ledgers were seized with everything else, but the guard who takes coffee also takes copies of shipping notices, and three lots in the provisional inventory list don't match anything my father ever bought or sold. Someone added weight to the manifest to justify a higher reserve price at auction. Someone has already priced my family's bones."

"Then we watch the auction notices," Wendell said. "And we find out who bids."

"No." She stepped close to him, close enough to see the lantern sliver reflected twice in his eyes. "We're past watching. Whoever wrote that letter set a clock running, and I don't know yet where the hour hand points — but I know it points at you. The next thing that arrives with that handwriting won't be information, Wendell. It'll be a demand."

Three days later, when the second letter came, she would be proven right.
