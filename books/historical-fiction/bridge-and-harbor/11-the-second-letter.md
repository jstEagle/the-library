## 11. The Second Letter

The first frost came to Saltmere on a Tuesday, and the second letter came the day after.

I want to say I was surprised. I want to say I opened my locker at the counting house and felt the ordinary shock of a man finding something where nothing should be. But the truth is uglier and more useful: I had been waiting for it. All week my hands had known before my head did. They fumbled latches. They miscounted strands. Twice I caught myself standing at the high window over the harbor, watching the gray water, listening to my own breathing the way you listen to a floorboard in a dark house — waiting for it to creak.

The envelope sat between my spare shirt and a coil of measuring twine, white as a fish's belly, sealed with plain wax this time. No crest. No courier's stamp. Addressed not to the Vane family but to *W. Vane, Corvane Counting House, Third Stair* — a name and a place so precise it could only have been written by someone who watched me climb those stairs every morning.

Someone inside.

I did not touch it right away. That was the discipline, the thing seven years had carved into me: when the trap is already around your leg, do not thrash. Thrashing is what tells the whole reef you are caught. So I finished my morning ledger entries with hands that only shook a little, and I carried the letter against my ribs all day like a coal wrapped in cloth, and I said yes sir to Mr. Pellam twice and counted forty-one Tahitian pearls into the strongroom without dropping one.

By the time the lamplighters made their rounds, I was on the loading bridge behind the warehouse, alone, tearing the wax with my thumbnail.

*You have read the first letter. You have told no one — good. Silence serves us both.*

*The terms are now these: within seven days, deliver to the undersigned the Corvane shipping codes — the winter rotation, all four harbors. Leave them where instructed. Do this and the Vane name never leaves your pocket.*

*Fail, and the magistrates receive everything: names, dates, cargo manifests, your father's signature on his own confession. A diver who ran enemy cargo while honest men drowned in the Channel. They hanged men for less, Vane. Your father was lucky. You may not be.*

*Seven days. The tide does not wait, and neither does history.*

No signature. The same hand as the first letter, though. I knew it now the way you know a voice through a wall — the hard downstroke of the *V*, the way the writer crossed their *t*s with a single impatient slash, the small crowding of letters at the end of each line, as if the words were afraid of open space. Whoever wrote it had written it slowly, carefully, in a hand trained by clerical work. And whoever wrote it had made one mistake the first time that they did not make now: there was no false warmth, no pretense of warning me for my own good. This letter was naked. It wanted something.

It wanted me to betray the company that owned my papers.

I read it three times, standing on the bridge with the harbor wind pulling at my collar, and each reading closed another door.

Here is the arithmetic nobody teaches you in the counting house. If I refused, the Vane name went to the magistrates, and the Vane name — whatever my father actually did, whatever Maren had told me about cargo run to feed children — would drown under the weight of paper. Paper does not care that my father is nine years dead. Paper does not care that he fed four children on what he earned in those runs. Paper only cares that the signature is real, and Maren had seen it, and it was.

If I obeyed, I handed the winter codes — the schedules, the routes, the insurance seals, the whole circulatory system of Corvane's shipping — to someone who wanted them badly enough to commit forgery and blackmail to get them. And the moment Corvane discovered the leak, and Corvane always discovered leaks, he would look for the clerk nearest the water. He would find me. He would find me holding the codes, or having given them, and either way I would hang from a different set of charges with the same neck.

And if I went to Corvane himself, laid both letters on his desk and said, *Sir, someone is doing this* — then what? Then I confessed that I had received the first letter a week ago and told no one. That I had burned it. That I had spent that week asking questions in the diving quarter about wartime smuggling and my father's part in it. Every silence I had kept to stay safe was suddenly a thread in a noose, and the man I'd be confessing to was a man who studied me too long across a counting table, who called boys like me *investments*, and who had, I now understood, reasons of his own to know exactly what the Vane name was worth.

Every exit was a door into another room. Every room had the same walls.

I folded the letter along its creases and held the corner near the lantern I'd brought, and stopped. My hand wouldn't do it this time. With the first letter, burning had felt like erasure — destroy the evidence and the danger dies with it. Now I understood the fire had only ever destroyed my copy of the truth. Somewhere out there, the original still existed, and its twin existed, and probably a dozen copies besides, sitting in whatever drawer the writer used, patient as debt. If I burned this one, I would be alone with the demand, unable to prove what had been asked of me, unable even to prove the deadline. A man accused without evidence has only his word. A man blackmailed without evidence has nothing at all.

I folded it small instead and pushed it into the lining of my boot, beside the ghost of the first letter, and stood a while longer on the bridge watching the lights of the harbor.

Seven days. Four hundred and eighty hours, if you counted the way I counted, which was badly, which was constantly.

* * *

The strange thing about terror, I have since learned, is how orderly it makes you. The morning after the second letter I arrived at the counting house early and did something I had never done in seven years: I began to study the company instead of serving it.

The winter codes lived in Mr. Corvane's wing of the building, in a locked cabinet behind his private office, changed quarterly, copied in the senior clerk's hand. I had carried sealed dispatches containing fragments of them maybe six times in my life, and each time my instructions had been the same: do not read, do not linger, do not be noticed. I had followed those instructions the way other men follow scripture. Now I walked the corridors that led to that office and mapped them without meaning to. Which doors locked. Which stairwell the night watchman skipped because its steps groaned. Where the coal chute entered the building, wide enough for a thin man. I hated myself a little for how quickly it came, how naturally the traitor's geometry arranged itself in my head — but fear is a good draftsman, and mine drew fast.

At midday I took my lunch hour and did not eat. Instead I went to the harbor master's public boards, where ship arrivals are posted, and stood in front of the registry with my hands in my pockets like any idler watching the gulls.

The *Meridian*. Neutral flag, out of Tessel. Berthed at the east quay, scheduled departure — the posting gave no date, neutral ships never did, but her manifest agent kept an office on Cordwainer Row, and agents talk when you buy them a coffee. I did not let myself think about why I was checking. Ilse's face did the thinking; I was just the legs.

Because here was the other wall of the room, the one I had been refusing to measure. The Marchetti name was on the enemy registry. Provisional, pending final armistice terms — everyone said so, everyone hoped so, but *pending* is a word that can mean a week or a year, and meanwhile the Marchetti warehouse sat sealed and the Marchetti daughter sat inside it like a pearl in a shut shell, and the city's patience with enemy names was thinning by the day. If the registry hardened — if the final terms came down cruel — then names on that list would not merely lose their warehouses. They would lose their right to be in the harbor at all. Enemy aliens were being moved east of the marsh line in two coastal towns already. I had read the notices and thought, *that is terrible for strangers*, the way you think things about fires in other people's houses.

Now my house was on fire too, from two directions, and I stood in front of a ship registry understanding that the second letter and the registry were not separate disasters. They were the same disaster wearing two coats. Someone wanted Corvane's codes, and someone wanted the Vane name ruined, and someone wanted the Marchetti holdings vulnerable — and the handwriting on the blackmail matched the timing on everything else too well for coincidence to carry the weight. Coincidence is what small men blame when they haven't yet found the design. I had spent seven years learning to see designs, because designs are what get quiet boys noticed.

I bought the coffee. The agent talked. The *Meridian*, he said, would likely clear the harbor within a fortnight, armistice weather permitting, and she was taking passengers of verifiable neutral association, and no, he didn't ask personal questions, that was rather the point of a neutral flag, wasn't it?

A fortnight. My deadline was seven days. The numbers did not overlap, and I stood on Cordwainer Row feeling the walls of the room come closer, close enough to touch on both sides.

* * *

That evening, Tobias Grell found me.

I should say Grell found everyone eventually; it was his profession. He was a soft-footed man with a face like a closed ledger, and he had appeared in my life sometime after the first letter — I couldn't name the day, which is itself the tell. Watchers who want to be seen are dismissed. Watchers who don't become furniture, and then one evening the furniture follows you home.

He fell into step beside me on Harrow Street, under the gas lamps, and spoke without looking at me.

"Long hours at the counting house."

"Season," I said. "Inventory."

"Mm." He adjusted his scarf. "Mr. Corvane values thoroughness in the young men. Thoroughness and discretion. The two together, that's rare."

We walked ten paces. My heart was behaving, mostly. Seven years of practice will hold a face steady even when the mind underneath is running through every document it has ever hidden in a boot.

"Discretion especially," Grell went on. "A discreet man doesn't go asking questions in the diving quarter. Doesn't visit harbor registries on his lunch hour. A discreet man receives correspondence and disposes of it properly." He glanced at me then, just once, mild as milk. "You'd be surprised what ends up in the wrong hands, Vane. Letters especially. Letters travel."

"I burn my correspondence," I said.

"I expect you do." He stopped at the corner where our ways divided, and straightened his hat, and delivered the rest like a man reciting freight rates. "Mr. Corvane likes the young men to know they're valued. Valued men don't need to look elsewhere for... opportunity. Whatever a man thinks he needs, the company provides. Safer for everyone." A pause. "Good night, Vane."

He went one way and I went the other, and I made it four streets before I had to stop and put my hand against a wall and breathe.

He knew about the diving quarter. He knew about the registry. Whether he knew about the letters themselves I couldn't tell — the wording was careful, watcher's wording, deniable — but it didn't matter, because the effect was the same. Corvane's eyes were on me. Which meant that whatever I decided about the codes, about the blackmail, about any of it, I would decide it under glass. And if the writer of the letters was who I was beginning to fear it might be — someone close to Corvane, someone whose interest in the Marchetti warehouse and the Vane name could coexist in the same ambitious skull — then Grell's little speech wasn't a warning at all.

It was pressure. Applied precisely, timed precisely, to a man with seven days and no exits. Squeeze the boy, and see which way he breaks: toward loyalty, toward treason, toward the magistrates' door. Any of the three would tell the watchman something useful.

I stood in the cold with my back against the wall, and I thought about my father underwater, in the green dark, running cargo he shouldn't have because the alternative was watching us starve, and how he must have felt the whole city closing on him the way it was closing on me, walls becoming rooms becoming a box becoming a coffin. And I thought: he chose anyway. Even trapped, he chose. That's what no one tells you about the drowning — you still get to pick which way you sink.

Seven days. Maybe five now; I'd lost track, and losing track was itself a kind of defeat.

There was one person left in Saltmere who might know how deep this water ran, one person who had kept accounts through the whole war and owed my mother a debt she'd never named aloud. Maren had given me the truth about my father once already. She would hate me for coming back.

I pushed off the wall and turned toward the diving quarter, walking fast, and behind me the gas lamps threw my shadow long and doubled down the wet cobblestones — one shadow going where I sent it, the other lagging half a step behind, like a man who hadn't yet decided whether to follow.
