## 13. Grell's Bargain

Tobias Grell had learned long ago that the best place to catch a man was not in an alley, where he expected to be caught, but in a crowd, where he believed himself invisible. Saltmere on a market morning was made for the purpose — fishwives shouting down the gull cries, crates of ice melting into brown puddles, a hundred shoulders to lose yourself between. Grell moved through it the way water moves through gravel: without haste, without sound, filling every gap.

He had been watching Wendell Vane for nine days now, and in those nine days he had assembled a picture of the boy that Corvane had never bothered to take. The boy walked to the counting house by one route and came home by three, chosen at random — good instinct, that. He checked reflections in shop glass without turning his head — better. He had gone twice to the sealed Marchetti warehouse after dark, and once to the diving quarter to sit with the widow Maren over tea, and once, for two full hours, nowhere at all, which was the most telling thing of all, because a man with nothing to hide does not practice being nowhere.

Corvane wanted reports. Grell gave him reports. But a report is a map, and a map is not the territory, and Tobias Grell had survived twenty years in the territory by understanding that every employer is also a door that can close on you.

So he had begun keeping his own ledger.

The omission he'd left out of his last report — the boy's third visit to the warehouse, the one where a lantern burned behind the boards until nearly midnight — was not sentimentality. Grell did not deal in sentiment. It was capital. A detail withheld is a coin in the pocket, and coins buy exits, and exits were the only currency Grell truly trusted.

Today the coin he wanted was different.

He had seen it by accident, which was how all the true things were seen. Nine days ago, at the warehouse, when Vane slipped through the loading bridge under cover of fog, Grell had followed onto the span itself — quiet work for a man of his weight if the wind was right — and through a gap in the planking he had watched the boy kneel before the Marchetti girl and press something into her hands. She had pressed it back. There had been words too low to catch, a refusal, an insistence; then the object passed from his palm to hers and back again, refused a second time, and finally closed inside the boy's fist like something alive.

Grell knew pearls. Twenty years in a harbor city will teach you pearls the way a priest learns psalms. And he knew this pearl in particular, because he had done his reading — because Grell always did his reading. A diver's last find. A girl's father's final dive before the war took him. A promise made in peacetime, sealed in nacre. Sentiment, yes. But sentiment has a price, and everything with a price can be bought.

The trick was knowing what a man would not sell.

* * *

He caught Vane coming out of the fish market, where the boy had stood for ten minutes doing nothing but looking at the boats — a habit of his father's people, Grell supposed, the diver's habit of reading water. The crowd was thick enough to hide a murder. Grell fell into step beside him so smoothly that three paces passed before Vane realized anyone was there, and when he did realize, Grell watched the boy's face do exactly what it had been trained to do: nothing. Seven years in the counting house had taught him well. But nothing held is its own confession.

"You walk like a clerk," Grell said pleasantly, "and look like a man being followed."

"Maybe I am."

"You are." They turned up Rope Walk together, two men strolling, harmless as weather. "Three routes home. Good. Glass-checking. Better. But you always look at the harbor when you think no one's watching, and a man who looks at the harbor wants to leave it, and that's the tell I could never train out of myself either."

Vane said nothing. Kept walking. Grell approved. Panic was noise; silence was arithmetic.

"I have a proposal," Grell said.

"I don't make bargains in markets."

"You'll want to make this one." Grell steered them left, off Rope Walk, into the shadowed cut between the sailmaker's and the chandler's, where the noise of the market thinned to a mutter and the smell was tar and old rope. He stopped, and put his back to the wall where he could see both ends of the passage, and let his face do what faces did for men like him: open slightly, like a till. "In nine days there is going to be an auction," he said. "You know this. In eight days, your deadline expires — and yes, I know about the letter, boy, I've read your mail more carefully than you have. And on the seventh night, a ship called the Meridian sails on the evening tide, and she is flying neutral colors, and there is a girl living in a sealed warehouse who means to be aboard her."

For the first time, Wendell Vane's face moved. It was small — a tightening around the eyes, a fractional stilling of breath — but to Grell it was as legible as a shout.

"There it is," Grell said softly. "There's the coin."

"If you go to Corvane—"

"Corvane pays me to watch you. Watching is what I'm paid for. Telling him everything I see—" Grell shrugged, a loose rolling of heavy shoulders. "A man sells his eyes once. If he spends them wisely."

"What do you want?"

Straight to it. Good. Grell had no patience for haggling dressed up as honor. He held up one hand, thumb and forefinger almost touching. "The pearl."

The silence that followed had texture to it. Grell felt it settle over the passage like fog off the water.

"The what?" Vane said.

"Don't insult us both." Grell kept his voice level, almost kind. "The pearl from her father's last dive. The one you gave her and she gave back, seven years ago, on a summer afternoon when the war hadn't eaten anyone yet. The one you keep in your pocket and touch when you think of her, which is often, which is why I know it's still there. Left breast pocket. Inner coat. You check it the way other men check their watches."

Vane's hand did not move toward the pocket. That, too, was discipline. But his breathing had changed, and Grell marked it.

"Lose me," Grell went on. "That's the offer. You give me the pearl, and from tonight forward I develop a limp, bad eyes, and a memory full of holes. I report that Vane walks to work, buys herring, goes home. I saw nothing, I know nothing, the trail goes cold. When the Meridian sails, whoever stands on that gangway does so without my shadow on him. You buy your freedom and hers for the price of a stone."

"It's not a stone."

"No," Grell agreed. "It isn't. That's why it's worth what I'm asking."

They stood in the tar-smell and the half-dark, and Grell waited, because waiting was most of his trade. He had made this offer perhaps a dozen times in his career, in one shape or another, and he had learned the pattern of the answer. Men reached for pockets. Men wept, sometimes, quietly. Men asked how they could know he'd keep his word, which was the wrong question, because the right question — the question that mattered — was whether the thing being sold was the sort of thing that survives being sold.

Vane looked at him for a long moment. Then he said, "How do I know you'd keep your word?"

And there it was — the wrong question. Grell almost smiled.

"You wouldn't," he said honestly. "But I've never broken a bargain, because broken bargains end careers, and I intend to die somewhere warm. Ask anyone in this city who's dealt with me. I am a great many ugly things, Vane, but I am not a cheat at my own table."

"And if I refuse?"

Grell spread his hands. "Then I keep watching. And watching turns into reporting, and reporting turns into a name given to Mr. Corvane on the sixth day instead of the seventh, and then there's a patrol at the warehouse gate and a constable at the gangway, and the Meridian sails light, and you and the girl discover what Corvane's gratitude looks like from the inside." He paused. "I'm not threatening you. I'm describing Tuesday."

* * *

He expected the boy to weaken. They always weakened — not out of cowardice but out of love, which was heavier than cowardice and bent men lower. Love made men sell anything, because love could always find a reason: *it's only a ring, only a watch, only a stone, and she is worth all of it.* Grell had built his career on the arithmetic of love. It was the most reliable currency in any port.

But Wendell Vane stood in the passage between the sailmaker and the chandler, and Grell watched something happen in the boy's face that he had seen perhaps three times in twenty years and had never once learned to predict.

The boy straightened. Not much. An inch, maybe. But it was the inch that mattered.

"No," Vane said.

"Think again. Eight days, boy. After that the choice stops being yours."

"I said no."

"The pearl is just a stone," Grell said, letting a little steel into his voice now. "It sits in a pocket. It weighs less than a coin. You're going to trade your life, maybe hers, for a pebble with a story attached?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

And here was the strange part, the part Grell would turn over for years afterward on warm evenings with money in his pocket and no answers in it: the boy didn't hesitate. He didn't construct an argument. He didn't dress the refusal in logic the way clever men did, the way Grell himself would have. He simply said, in a voice flat and certain as a sounding line hitting bottom:

"Because it's the only thing I own. Not the papers, not the name, not the room I sleep in — Corvane owns all that. Everything else I've ever had was taken or rented. That pearl is the one piece of my life that nobody can seize, because it isn't property. It's a promise. And if I give it to you, you don't get a pearl. You get everything I am, and what's left of me wouldn't be worth following. You'd have bought an empty coat."

Grell studied him. Somewhere beyond the mouth of the passage the market roared on, indifferent, selling and selling.

"You understand," Grell said slowly, "that I can take it. You're half my weight. I could take it and you couldn't stop me, and there's no magistrate in Saltmere who'd ask after a counting-house boy's missing pebble."

"I know."

"And you're not afraid?"

"Oh, I'm afraid." Something moved at the corner of Vane's mouth — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would eventually be built. "I've been afraid for seven years. Fear isn't the thing that decides. It just watches while something else decides."

Grell let out a long breath through his nose. He found, to his own surprise, that he was not angry. He had offered fair terms and been refused fairly, and there was a cleanliness in that which he respected the way a gambler respects a man who loses without reaching for the knife. More than respect. Recognition. Once, a lifetime ago, in another harbor, Tobias Grell had been offered a similar price for a similar stone — not a pearl, a letter, a single letter that would have bought him out of a burning situation — and he had refused, and the refusing had cost him eleven years and most of his teeth, and he had never once regretted it, because the letter had been the last piece of himself he owned, and a man who trades that is finished even if he walks free.

"You're a fool," Grell said.

"Probably."

"You'll hang for a pebble."

"Maybe. Or maybe I'll stand on that gangway with it in my pocket, and she'll sail, and I'll still be somebody." Vane met his eyes, and for a moment the clerk's trained blankness fell away entirely, and Grell saw the diver's son underneath — the boy who had grown up holding his breath. "You asked what I'd trade. Here's my counteroffer. Nothing. But I'll owe you, and I pay debts. Ask around about that too."

Grell laughed — a short, rusty sound, like a hinge remembering oil. "Everyone pays debts, boy. The question is what kind."

He pushed off the wall and walked past Vane toward the market noise, then stopped, shoulder to shoulder with him in the narrow dark.

"For what it's worth," Grell said quietly, "I'm not going to Corvane tomorrow. Not yet. A watcher who reports everything is worthless — even Corvane knows that. You've bought yourself three days of my blindness, and you haven't paid for it, and don't ask me why, because I don't fully know." He turned his head. His voice dropped to almost nothing. "But hear me, and hear me plain. Three days. After that, someone else will come asking, and they won't offer terms, and they won't care what you keep in your pockets. Whatever you're going to do — do it fast."

He stepped out into the light and the market roar and was gone between the stalls, water through gravel.

Behind him, in the tar-dark of the passage, Wendell Vane stood a moment longer with his hand flat against his breast pocket, over the small hard shape of the only thing he owned.

Then he turned — not toward the counting house, not toward home — toward the harbor, walking fast now, a man who had just learned exactly how little time remained, and who had discovered, in refusing to sell the smallest thing he had, precisely how large he was.

Grell did not look back. But he heard the footsteps quicken, and he smiled at nothing, and began, in the ledger of his head, to write the entry he would never show anyone:

*The boy keeps what he keeps. God help whoever comes to take it.*
