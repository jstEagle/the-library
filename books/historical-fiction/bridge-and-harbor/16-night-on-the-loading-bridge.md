## 16. Night on the Loading Bridge

The lanterns came at half past eleven, the way they always did, two of them swinging from the belts of men who had learned to walk slowly because slow walking looked like authority. Ilse knew their rhythm now. She had been living inside her father's warehouse for nineteen days, and in that time she had learned the whole grammar of the sealed door: which boards creaked, which windows still opened, how long a patrol's lantern light would lie across the floor of the loading bridge before it moved on.

She counted. She was good at counting. Her father had taught her that too — *count everything twice, little bird, and trust the third number* — though he had meant pearls and cargo manifests, not patrolmen.

Tonight the lanterns passed at 11:31 by the cracked pocket watch she kept beneath her pallet of sacking. They would not return until 12:40. Sixty-nine minutes.

Wendell was already on the loading bridge when she climbed up to him, materializing out of the dark the way he did — quietly, entirely, as if he had been assembled out of harbor fog and patience. He sat with his back against the bridge rail, knees drawn up, and for a moment neither of them spoke. This was another thing she had learned: Wendell Vane did not fill silences. He let them settle, like sediment in water, until you could see what was actually at the bottom.

"You found a ship," she said.

"I found a ship." He turned the words over carefully before releasing them, the way he handled anything fragile. "The *Meridian*. Neutral flag — Ostmark registry, though half her crew is ours. She loads at Pier Nine, departs the night after next. After that, the harbor closes for the armistice review. No neutral traffic for weeks."

"The night before the auction."

"The night before the auction." He said it again, and this time she heard what was underneath it, the thing he was turning over and over like a pearl with a flaw in it.

Ilse pulled her coat tighter. The warehouse was cold in a way buildings are cold when they were built to hold goods, not grief. Below them, the crates stood in their long ranks — her father's trade reduced to inventory, each crate chalked with a magistrate's mark. Somewhere under the tarpaulin was the last diving suit he had ever owned. She had not opened that one.

"Say the rest of it," she said. "Whatever you carried up this bridge in your pocket, say it."

Wendell was quiet a moment longer. Then: "There's room on the *Meridian* for two."

The words hung there between them, and Ilse felt something in her chest do a complicated thing — a lifting and a dropping at once, like a swell that can't decide which way to break.

"No," she said.

"Ilse—"

"No." She said it more softly the second time, because shouting on the loading bridge was a way of dying, but she said it all the same. "No, Wendell."

He uncurled himself and faced her fully. Even in the dark she could find his eyes; they were the color of deep water over sand, and they had always been too honest for his own good. Seven years in the counting house had taught him to hide everything except that.

"There's an auction in ten days," he said, low and even. "And a letter-writer who needs a Vane in front of a magistrate before then. If I stay, I don't stay free. I stay as bait, or I stay as a fugitive, or I stay as whatever Corvane decides to make of me when he's finished using me. There is no version where I remain here and remain whole."

"There's no version where you leave and remain whole either." She heard her own voice sharpen. "You have papers, Wendell. Papers that belong to Corvane. You walk onto a neutral ship without release from your indenture and you're not a passenger, you're stolen property. They'll wire ahead. They'll have a name waiting in every port from here to Ostmark. At least if you stay, you know the streets. At least if you stay, you have Maren, you have Hale's confession, you have—" She stopped herself, because the list was running out and they both knew it.

"I have a ledger," Wendell said. "Unsigned. And a forged page bought from strangers. Ilse, listen to me. I've spent seven years learning exactly how much paper protects people, and the answer is: almost nothing. People protect people. So let me come with you and be someone who protects you instead of someone who guards pearls he'll never own."

It was a beautiful sentence. That was the trouble with Wendell — he had spent so many years being silent that when he finally spoke, he spoke like a man who had composed everything in advance, every word load-bearing.

"That's exactly why you can't come," she said. "Because you'd be doing it for me, and not for yourself. There's a difference, and the magistrates will hear it. A Marchetti daughter fleeing enemy declaration is a story they already know how to tell. But a Corvane indentured man running? That's a scandal with legs. That's a manhunt. You wouldn't just lose your freedom, you'd hand Corvane the perfect proof that the Vane family was guilty all along. You'd confirm his forgery with your feet."

For a long moment he said nothing. Out on the water, somewhere toward the breakwater, a ship's bell rang the hour wrong — or maybe it was right and the cracked watch was wrong; the harbor kept its own time and never apologized for it.

"I made you a promise," Wendell said finally.

"And I'm releasing you from it."

"You can't release me. It wasn't yours to release. It was mine to keep." His voice stayed level, but she saw his hands close against his knees. "That summer, when we were fifteen, you asked me for one thing. Not safety. Not wealth. Just — come for me. If it ever goes dark, come for me. I gave you a pearl to seal it. I've carried that pearl through seven years of counting house and silence, and I will not carry it onto a ship while you sail away alone into a world that has declared you nothing."

"Then give me the pearl," Ilse said, before she could stop herself.

The words came out wrong — came out cruel, or at least shaped cruelly, and she watched them land. Wendell went very still. It was the stillness of the counting house, she realized. The stillness he wore like armor when something hit close enough to matter.

"I didn't mean that," she whispered.

"Didn't you?" He said it without accusation, which was worse. "You might be right to mean it. Take the pearl, take the ship, forget the boy from the diving quarter. It would be the sensible thing. I've watched sensible people survive my entire life, Ilse. They're very good at it. They're also very alone at the end of it."

"That's not fair."

"No," he agreed quietly. "Very little of this is."

* * *

They quarreled properly after that — as properly as two people could quarrel in whispers on a loading bridge above a sealed warehouse with patrols on forty-minute rounds.

She told him he was romanticizing his own destruction. He told her she was doing the same and calling it strategy. She said the promise had been made by children, in peacetime, by a boy who dove for pearls and a girl who thought the war would end like weather, gently, with everyone still standing in the same garden. He said promises didn't age out; that was the whole point of them; otherwise they were just contracts, and God knew he'd had enough of contracts to last three lifetimes.

"You want to know what I learned in that counting house?" His voice dropped lower, tighter. "I learned that every silence I kept made someone else's life easier and mine smaller. Every single time. I kept silent about the mislabeled crates, and a clerk kept his post. I kept silent about what I saw in Corvane's ledgers, and I kept my rations. Silence kept me alive, Ilse, but it never once kept me *safe*. There's a difference, and it took me seven years and one sealed letter to learn it."

"And coming with me isn't silence, it's—" She groped for it. "It's running."

"Running is a kind of speech," Wendell said. "It says: this, this thing you did to us, we decline it. We decline the auction and the registry and the letters. We decline the whole arrangement."

She wanted to laugh at him — *we*, as if there were a we large enough to hold both their ruinous situations — and she wanted to weep, and she did neither, because the watch in her head was counting down to the next lanterns and because she was suddenly, unbearably aware that these might be among the last words they ever exchanged in the same air.

"Wendell." She made her voice as steady as she could. "If you come aboard that ship, they will chase us both. If you stay, they chase only me — and I am fast, and I am angry, and I have a father's contacts in three ports who owe him more than Corvane owes anyone. Let me be the one who runs well. Please. I am asking you. As the person the promise was made to: let me keep it by leaving you alive to make new ones."

He looked at her for a long time. Down in the yard, wind moved across the tarpaulins with a sound like a held breath released.

"You know what's in my pocket," he said at last. "You know I've carried it since the day you gave it back to me on the dock. I could give it to you tonight and it would mean goodbye. I could keep it and it would mean I failed you. Or—" and here his voice did something she had never heard it do, crack slightly, like ice accepting weight, "—or we could stop pretending that either of those is the only choice."

"It is the only choice," Ilse said. "One of us gets out. That's arithmetic."

"Arithmetic," Wendell repeated softly. "My father used to say the sea doesn't do arithmetic. It does tides. Things go out and things come back, and you can't always tell which is which from the shore."

She reached for his hand because she could not reach for any argument left. His fingers closed around hers, cold from the bridge rail, familiar past all sense.

* * *

The quarrel ended the way real quarrels do — not with victory but with exhaustion, the two of them sitting shoulder to shoulder against the rail, whispering themselves down from the high ground.

Somewhere in the middle of it, without either of them deciding, they had turned toward each other, and Ilse found her forehead resting against his jaw, and his arms around her shoulders, and the cold of the warehouse seemed suddenly far away and irrelevant, the way cold always is when you are holding the one warm thing.

"Don't do anything stupid," she murmured into his collar. "I know that's an enormous request. I know the request contradicts your entire character."

She felt him smile more than saw it. "I'll restrict myself to moderately stupid."

"Wendell."

"Ilse." He said her name the way other people said prayers — not expecting an answer, just putting it into the dark where something might hear it.

Above them the sky was the color of wet slate, harbor-colored, home-colored. She breathed him in — ink and salt and the faint chemical ghost of the counting house — and tried to memorize it the way her father had taught her to memorize water conditions before a dive: thoroughly, deliberately, as information she would need later in a place she couldn't yet see.

"The *Meridian* sails the night after next," Wendell said into her hair. "Pier Nine. Second tide."

"I know."

"If you're not there, I'll assume you found a better ship."

"If *you're* not there," she said, "I'll assume nothing. I'll come looking for you. That's what the promise means from my side, in case you've forgotten. You made it, but I intend to enforce it."

He laughed then — a small, broken sound, quickly muffled against her hair, and she held him harder, and for a while there were no more arguments, only breathing, only the harbor doing its slow tidal work below them, taking things out and bringing things back.

When the lanterns came at 12:40, they had already separated. By the time the light swept the loading bridge, Ilse was alone on her pallet of sacking among the crated memories of her father's trade, eyes closed, pulse loud, listening to the faint sound of Wendell Vane going silently down into the dark toward the city.

She lay awake long after, doing arithmetic she hated: two tickets, one ship, one name on a registry, nine days.

And it was only near dawn, drifting at last toward sleep, that the thought surfaced — cold, clear, and unwelcome as a body rising from deep water:

Wendell hadn't argued that hard. Wendell, who fought for every silence and every word, who had quarreled with her like a man defending a fortress — he had given in too easily at the end. *I'll restrict myself to moderately stupid,* he'd said.

She sat up in the dark, heart beginning to hammer.

He was planning something. He had been planning something the whole time they argued, and it wasn't coming aboard the *Meridian* with her.

It was something else. Something he hadn't told her.
