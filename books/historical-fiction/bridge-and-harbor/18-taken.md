## 18. Taken

The lamp in Corvane's study burned past midnight, and Tobias Grell counted the ways a man could leave a room.

There was the door, which creaked on its second hinge and had done so for eleven years — Grell had reported it once, early in his employment, and been told to stop noticing things that cost money to fix. There was the window above the garden wall, four feet of drop and a rosebush that would tear a man's coat and his dignity together. And there was the servants' stair, narrow and unlit, which smelled of tallow and old fish because the kitchen below still stored salt cod in winter, a habit no one had broken since before the war.

Wendell Vane chose the servants' stair.

Grell had expected nothing less. Seven weeks of watching the boy had taught him that Wendell Vane did not make mistakes so much as he made calculations, and most of them were good ones. He came down the stair at half past one in the morning with a roll of papers inside his coat, moving at the speed of a man carrying nothing worth stealing, which was itself a kind of skill. His boots made no sound. His breathing was even. If Grell had not been sitting in the dark of the linen closet two floors down for three hours with his hat off and his knife loosened in its sheath, he would have missed him entirely.

But Grell had been paid to miss nothing.

He let the boy reach the ground floor. He let him cross the passage where the floorboards went pale from forty years of feet. He waited until Wendell's hand touched the latch on the yard door — because there is an art to taking a man, and the art is this: never take him where he can run forward. Take him where he must turn around.

Then Grell opened the closet door and said, quietly, "Evening, Mr. Vane."

* * *

To the boy's credit — and Grell gave credit rarely, and never aloud — he did not flinch. He turned the way a man turns who has rehearsed turning, slow, hands visible, face arranged into something between apology and boredom. The lantern outside the yard door threw their shadows long across the flagstones.

"Mr. Grell." A pause, measured out like sugar. "You keep late hours."

"I'm paid for late hours." Grell stepped into the light, letting the boy see all of him: the broad shoulders gone soft at the edges, the coat too warm for the season, the face that dockworkers described as *a closed ledger*. "Question is what you're paid for, coming out of the master's study at one in the morning with your shirt buttoned wrong."

It wasn't true. The shirt was fine. But Wendell's hand moved an inch toward his collar anyway — a flicker, a hair's breadth of doubt about his own body — and Grell filed it away the way he filed everything. Small doubts were levers. You didn't need to pull them tonight. You only needed to know they were there.

"I was returning a book," Wendell said.

"Carrying papers under your coat."

"Returning papers, then."

Grell smiled, or performed the shape of one. "Let's not do this in a doorway. Doors have ears and yards have windows and I've a bottle upstairs that doesn't like being kept waiting."

He jerked his head toward the stairs and, after a moment that lasted exactly as long as Wendell needed it to last — long enough to consider the yard door, the lock, the distance to the gate, the odds — the boy followed him up.

* * *

Grell's room sat above the coach house, a single square with a bed, a stove, a table, and three locks on the door, which was two more than the house paid for. He poured whisky into two glasses without asking, because offering is a courtesy and courtesies are contracts, and contracts are how you own people politely. Wendell took the glass and didn't drink. Also correct. Grell was beginning to think the boy might be worth more than Corvane believed, which was a dangerous thought to have about anyone, and he set it aside the way you set aside a live coal.

"Sit," Grell said.

Wendell sat. He placed the glass on his knee. In the lamplight his face was young and calm and utterly unreadable, and Grell felt the old familiar pleasure rise in his chest, the pleasure of a difficult job going well.

"You've been in the harbor city seven years," Grell said. "I've been here nineteen. Before that I was army, and before that I was nothing much. You know what I learned in the army?"

"That officers lie."

"That everyone lies," Grell said. "Officers just lie on paper. It's the same everywhere. Ledgers, letters, confessions, armistice terms — all paper, all lies wearing ink. The only honest thing in Saltmere is the tide." He drank. "So when I catch a clerk climbing out of Aldous Corvane's study at one in the morning, I don't ask myself *is this boy a thief*. Thieves I understand. I ask myself *what does this boy know that makes theft worth hanging for*."

"And what answer did you find?"

"That's the interesting part." Grell leaned back until the chair complained. "Because here's what I know about you, Wendell Vane. I know you were indentured at nine years old, which means your father died owing someone, and the someone was us. I know you can read figures better than men who've been clerks twenty years. I know you visit the diving quarter on your half-days and sit with an old woman named Maren who keeps her accounts in a language she thinks nobody remembers. I know you walk past the Marchetti warehouse every evening like a clock striking, regular as sin."

Wendell said nothing. Good. Silence was a language too, and the boy spoke it fluently.

"And I know," Grell continued, "that six weeks ago, Mr. Corvane asked me a strange question over his brandy. He asked me if anything had ever left this house that shouldn't have. Shipping schedules. Harbor codes. Anything." He let that sit. "And I told him no. Because that was my answer then."

The lamplight ticked. Somewhere below, a horse shifted in its stall.

"And now?" Wendell said.

"Now I'll tell you a story, and you'll listen, and then we'll both decide what happens next." Grell set down his glass. "Three years ago, a packet of Corvane shipping codes appeared in the possession of a blockade runner working the northern channel. Not copied. Not guessed. The actual codes, in Corvane's own cipher rotation. The runner used them twice and then vanished, and the company lost two cargoes and nearly lost its neutrality charter. Corvane tore this house apart looking for the leak. Searched desks. Searched servants. Had me follow three clerks for a year." He smiled again, thinner this time. "He never once looked at the man he hired to do the following."

The silence that followed was the kind Grell liked best: the kind where the other person does all the arithmetic.

"You," Wendell said at last. Flat. Not shocked — recalibrating. That was the thing about the boy; he didn't waste time on surprise. Surprise was a luxury for people whose lives weren't on the table.

"Me," Grell agreed. "Nineteen years in this house. I know every code rotation, every safe combination, every night Corvane drinks past his sense. You want to know why a watcher watches? Because watching is the only trade where the goods come to you. Everything in this city passes by my eyes eventually. Letters, ledgers, cargoes, secrets. All I ever had to do was not report some of it." He shrugged. "Corvane pays me to be his eyes. He never understood that eyes can be rented to a second tenant."

"Why tell me this?" Wendell's voice stayed level, but Grell saw the calculation running behind it, fast, the boy doing sums at the speed he read columns. "You could turn me in right now. Papers, hour, circumstances. Corvane would have me in irons before dawn and grateful to you besides."

"I could," Grell said. "And tomorrow you'd hang or rot or vanish into the magistrates' paperwork, and I'd be exactly where I am, in a room above a coach house, waiting for an old man to die so I can retire on whatever crumbs the estate throws. Whereas—" he spread his hands, "—whereas there's another arrangement available. To a man of imagination."

"The codes."

"The codes."

"You want me to steal them for you."

Grell laughed, short and genuine. "Steal them? Boy, you've just climbed out of his study at one in the morning. Whatever's in your coat, you've already stolen *something*. All I'm proposing is that we make our thefts agree."

* * *

Here is what Grell knew, and what he watched the boy realize, layer by layer, like frost forming on glass:

Wendell Vane had gone into that study for documents. Grell didn't know which documents, but he knew the shape of the errand. Six weeks ago a letter had arrived in this house addressed to a family name that hadn't been spoken aloud in years, and Corvane had read it twice and burned it and smiled in a way that made even Grell's neck prickle. Since then the boy had changed. He'd started walking like a man with two shadows. And two days ago, Grell had watched him meet a salt-crusted courier behind the rope walks and pass over coin, and the courier had passed back a folded page, and Grell — who followed everyone, including himself, on principle — had noted the exchange and wondered.

So when Wendell reached slowly into his coat and drew out the roll of papers, Grell was not surprised. When the boy held them a moment, weighing, Grell nodded once: *go on.* And when Wendell unrolled them across the table — pages of cipher rotations, code groups, the seasonal key tables, Corvane's cramped initials authenticating each sheet — Grell felt something loosen in his chest that had been tight for three years.

The northern codes. Current. Complete.

"Well now," Grell said softly, and reached for them.

Wendell's hand came down flat on the pages first. Light, almost gentle. But final.

"One thing first," the boy said. "You get these, and I walk out of this house clean. No report. No memory of tonight. And if Corvane ever asks you what I was doing in his study, you were asleep above the coach house, same as every night, and saw nothing, same as always. Those are the terms."

"Terms." Grell tasted the word. "You're negotiating with me."

"Somebody has to."

And there it was — the flicker Grell had been waiting for all night, not fear but something under the fear, a spine of stubbornness that reminded him unpleasantly of the divers he'd known in the old days, men who went down into green dark on a single breath and came up either rich or dead and considered the arithmetic fair. He found he respected it. He also found it irrelevant. Respect was a luxury too.

"Agreed," Grell said. "Clean walk, silent tongue. Now give me the pages."

Wendell lifted his hand.

Grell gathered the sheets and read them the way a priest reads scripture — not the words but the truth beneath them. Cipher groups in sequence. The autumn rotation, which had changed in September. The harbor pilot signals. He checked the third page against a fragment he carried in his head from three years ago, an old group number he'd sold to the runner, and it matched the new series perfectly, the way a forged chain matches its links. Whoever had built these pages knew the house's cipher habits down to the handwriting.

*Maren's contacts*, Grell thought, and almost smiled. The old woman in the diving quarter kept accounts older than the war. She'd have had access to a clerk's discarded drafts, or worse, a bought memory. It didn't matter. What mattered was that the pages were good enough to sell, and Grell intended to sell them north within the week, before the armistice terms landed and made the whole harbor either lawful or worthless, depending on which rumor you believed.

He rolled the pages, tucked them inside his coat, and raised his glass.

"To honest thieves," he said.

Wendell stood, untouched glass left on the table, and walked to the door. At the threshold he stopped, and for a moment the lamplight caught his face full-on, and Grell saw something there he hadn't seen in seven weeks of watching: not fear, not relief, but a kind of settled grief, the look of a man who has paid a price and is already counting what's next.

"Mr. Grell," the boy said. "One piece of advice, free of charge. When you spend those codes — spend them fast, and far from this harbor. Because whoever forged the letters that started all this, whoever's using my father's name as bait — they're not finished. And when the whole scheme comes apart, it won't come apart at the top. It'll come apart at the middle. At the men in rooms like this one, holding things they shouldn't hold."

Grell kept his face still, but the words went in deep, because they were his own thoughts wearing the boy's voice, and hearing your own secret spoken aloud is a particular kind of cold.

"Is that a threat?"

"It's arithmetic," Wendell Vane said, and went out, closing the door softly behind him, the way a man closes a door he intends never to open again.

* * *

Alone, Grell sat for a long while with the pages against his ribs, listening to the house settle.

The boy was right. That was the trouble. Grell had spent nineteen years believing he was the smartest predator in the tank, and tonight a pearl-diver's son had walked into his room, handed him exactly what he wanted, and walked out again — and somewhere between the doorway and the stairs, Grell had begun to wonder whether he'd been given the codes or *fed* them.

He got up, fetched the pages out, and read them a third time by candlelight, hunting the flaw.

He didn't find one. But as he rolled them up, a slip of paper slid free from between the third and fourth sheets — thin, unsigned, written in a small careful hand:

*Check the date on the September rotation. Then ask yourself who wanted you holding these.*

Grell sat down slowly.

Outside, the tide was turning, and somewhere across the black water a ship's bell rang once, counting the hour toward an auction nine days away.
