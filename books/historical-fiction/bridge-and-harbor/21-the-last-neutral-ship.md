## 21. The Last Neutral Ship

The city did not sleep that night, and neither did I.

Word of the auction's collapse ran through Saltmere like dye through water. By midnight the counting house steps had been swept clean of the magistrate's men, and by two o'clock someone had smashed the Corvane sign with a boat hook and no one had bothered to stop them. I stood at a window on the third floor of an empty office overlooking the harbor and watched the company that owned seven years of my life become, in the space of one tide, simply a building with its lights off.

Aldous Corvane was ruined. That word had a taste to it, salt and iron. He'd been dragged from his own auction floor under escort, his ledgers impounded, his name already being chiseled off the manifests. Both men fall together — I'd known it even as I raised Maren's ledger above my head. Some truths are not weapons you can aim. They are floods, and when you open the gate you had better be standing somewhere you can afford to drown.

I could not afford to drown anywhere. But Ilse could afford to leave.

The *Meridian* sailed at dawn. Neutral registry, Dutch papers, bound south for a port whose name changed depending on who said it. She sat in the outer roads with her lamps burning low, taking on the last of her passengers by tender, because the magistrates had closed the main quay pending "clarification of seizure authority" — which meant nobody knew anymore what the law was, and so everyone was free and no one was safe, all at once.

Charges were pending against me. That was the phrase the clerk had used, *pending*, as though they were packages waiting at a depot. Forgery, theft of correspondence, conspiracy to defraud a trading concern. The irony was that every charge was true. I had stolen Corvane's letters. I had conspired — with Maren, with Hale's unsigned confession, with a magistrate too frightened or too honest to look away. And forgery, well. Forgery was the family trade, apparently. My father had run cargo under false flags; I had run truth under one. It seemed only fitting that we should both hang for our honesty in the end.

Except I wasn't going to hang. Not tonight. Tonight I was going to keep a promise seven years old, made in peacetime by a boy who didn't know what promises cost, and I was going to pay the full asking price without haggling.

I left the office before the lamplighter finished his rounds, moving through streets I had memorized the way a diver memorizes currents — not by looking but by feeling. Grell was nowhere, which either meant he was dead, drunk, or paid better elsewhere. After what happened at Corvane's study, after he took my false codes and vanished into the crowd like smoke deciding to be elsewhere, I suspected all three. I owed him nothing and he owed me less, which in Saltmere passed for a settled account.

The diving quarter was silent. Maren's door was barred; I set my hand against the wood once, in thanks, and did not knock. There would be time for goodbyes that could survive daylight, if any goodbye ever could. In my pocket, wrapped in a scrap of oilcloth, was the pearl — small, gray-white, imperfect, worth almost nothing on any market in the world. Her father's last dive. The first thing I ever gave away and the only thing they never managed to take.

Until now. Until I gave it freely, which is different.

* * *

The tender left from a shingle beach below the old pilot station, where a man with a lantern and no questions took coins from people who walked bent under bags. I counted eleven others on the sand: families mostly, a clerk still wearing his ink-stained cuffs, a woman holding a hatbox the way other people hold children. Everyone had learned this week that paper decides everything — passports, registries, seizures, auctions — and everyone had also learned that paper can burn. So they moved quietly, quickly, toward the one ship whose flag meant nothing yet meant everything.

I found Ilse at the top of the gangway.

She was wearing her traveling coat, the gray one, and her hair was pinned up in the practical way she used when she meant to work and not to be looked at. Behind her, the deck of the *Meridian* was already crowded with shapes and murmurs. Below us, black water moved between hull and pier, patient as arithmetic.

She saw me and did not smile. That was right. Smiling would have made it a reunion, and it wasn't one. It was a transaction, the last and oldest one between us, conducted across seven years and one gangway.

"You're late," she said.

"The auction ran long."

"I heard." Her eyes went over me the way she used to check crates — manifest first, damage second. "They're saying you stood up in front of forty buyers and a magistrate and accused Aldous Corvane to his face."

"They're saying worse about him."

"They're saying charges are pending against you." She used the word carefully, tasting it. *Pending.*

"Paper," I said. "And paper can burn, or it can wait. Either way it doesn't hold me the way it holds you. Your name is on a registry, Ilse. Provisional enemy. Revocable, maybe, someday, by men who have never been wrong in their lives. Mine is just a debt in a city where the creditor is gone broke. Stay here and you wait years for a committee to decide what you are. Go, and you decide for yourself."

"And you?" Her voice cracked down the middle and she hated it, I could tell, hated the crack the way she hated rust on good brass. "What do you decide?"

Here is what I had rehearsed on the walk down, in the dark, through the streets: I will come with you. I'll stow away, buy passage with Hale's money, talk my way past the harbormaster, whatever it takes. We made a promise in peacetime and peacetime is coming back and I intend to be there when it arrives.

Here is what I said instead.

"Someone has to stay and answer the questions. If we both vanish, they don't look for a diver's son who skipped out on a bond. They look for two conspirators who fled together, and everything we did becomes exactly what Corvane said it was. But if I stay — if I stand in front of the inquiry and give them the whole shape of it, names, dates, the forged entries, Hale's confession — then it gets written down correctly. The Vane name comes out of this as victims, not accomplices. Your name comes out of this as a merchant's daughter who was robbed, not a smuggler's partner. Paper, Ilse. Someone has to make sure the paper says true things, or it will say lies forever."

"That isn't fair."

"No," I agreed. "It's arithmetic."

"I never asked you for arithmetic." She was crying now, without sound, without permission, the tears arriving like weather. "I asked you for a promise. One summer, one pearl, one stupid boy swearing on a beach that if I ever needed him he'd come. And I needed you, Wendell, and you came — through a sealed door, through Grell, through Corvane himself — you came. So don't stand there telling me the promise ends with arithmetic."

"It doesn't end." I took the oilcloth from my pocket. "It changes hands."

She looked at the little bundle and understood immediately, because she had always understood things before I finished saying them. Her hand came up, half to take it, half to stop me.

"Your father's pearl."

"Her father's pearl," I corrected gently. "Dived by Marchetti, promised by Vane. It was always yours. I've just been carrying it for seven years the way you carry something while the owner is away."

"Wendell—"

"It's not a farewell gift. Don't take it like that. Take it like a receipt." I pressed it into her palm and folded her fingers over it, and her hands were cold, and mine were shaking, and we stood like that, two people gripping a stone over black water. "You'll get where you're going. You'll open a warehouse somewhere the registries haven't reached. You'll write it all down, every crate, every price, because you're your father's daughter and numbers are your native language. And one day, when the armistice is old news and the magistrates here have nothing left to seize but their own dignity, a letter will arrive in Saltmere with a harbor postmark I don't recognize. And I will read it. That's the rest of the promise. Memory doesn't need papers. Memory doesn't clear customs. Nobody on this dock can seize it, tax it, or enter it into evidence."

"And until then?"

"Until then I dive. My father's trade. Maren says the beds off the point are still working." I tried to smile and mostly managed it. "Turns out I'm better at holding my breath than anyone guessed. Seven years in a counting house. Practice."

She laughed — wet, broken, real — and pulled me to her, and for a moment the gangway, the harbor, the charges, the whole rotten beautiful city ceased to be. There was just the smell of her coat and the hard small shape of the pearl between our joined hands, and the gulls beginning their arguments overhead because dawn was coming whether anyone approved or not.

"If you drown," she said into my shoulder, "I will never forgive you. And I am very good at never forgiving. Ask anyone in the shipping trade."

"Noted. No drowning. It's in the terms."

"Terms not yet signed," she whispered, and it was our old joke and it wasn't funny at all, and then the mate at the bottom of the gangway called the last call in three languages, none of them ours.

* * *

She went up the gangway the way she did everything: fast, straight-backed, refusing to make it harder by lingering. At the top she stopped once. Turned. Held up the pearl between finger and thumb so the lantern light caught it — a small gray moon, imperfect, priceless — and then closed her fist around it, keeping it, keeping me, and went aboard.

I stayed on the dock while they cast off. I stayed while the tender lines came in, while the tug nosed her bow around, while her sails took the early wind one seam at a time. The sun came up over the eastern mole the color of new brass, and the *Meridian* grew smaller against it, and I stood in the cold with charges pending against me and nothing in my pockets but air, and I have never in my life felt richer.

The harbor closed behind her. Water heals over everything; that is the first thing a diver learns. By noon there would be no wake left at all. But I would know where it had been. That is the whole trade, really — knowing where the water has been, what passed through it, what it carried down and let go.

I turned back toward the city. Somewhere up those streets, an inquiry waited with my name on its docket, a magistrate with Maren's ledger, an unsigned confession that needed signing, and a junior clerk named Edmund Hale who might yet find his spine. Work to do. True things to write down.

In my pocket, where the pearl had lived for seven years, there was now nothing but a small square of oilcloth, folded empty.

It weighed more than the pearl ever had.

I climbed the stairs from the docks as the gulls wheeled and cried, and at the top, leaning against the pilot station wall with his hat in his hands and his face carefully blank, stood Tobias Grell.

"Well," he said. "You'll want to hear this. The inquiry opened early this morning — and the first name they called wasn't yours."
