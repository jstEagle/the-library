## 1. The Ledger of Small Names

The River Sante in December moved the way an old man walks to church: slowly, with great dignity, and entirely without regard for anyone's schedule. It had frozen at the edges by the first week of the month, a lacework of white climbing inward from both banks, while the dark middle current kept on going about its business as though winter were somebody else's problem.

Ivo Okafor had lived beside it for fourteen months now, and he had come to think of the river as the only honest thing in the garrison. It took what was given to it and carried it away — no ceremony, no paperwork, no amendments.

"Okafor." Sister Amara's voice came from the supply room, pitched exactly between command and conversation, which was how she said most things. "If you are talking to that window again, the men will begin to worry about you."

"I am not talking to the window," Ivo said. "I am negotiating with it. The frost and I have reached an agreement. It keeps to its corner; I keep to mine."

"And the middle of the pane?"

"The middle of the pane is under discussion."

She came out with an armful of rolled bandages and gave him the look — the one that had, over the months, trained him the way a bell trains a dog. He took the bandages without being asked twice and went down the ward.

There were thirty-one beds in the long hall, and this morning twenty-nine of them held men. The other two held the space where men had been, and Ivo had learned that those empty beds required more care than the full ones. Not from him — from everyone else. An empty bed invited questions, and questions at the Sante garrison had a way of being answered wrong.

He started at the far end, as he always did, with Private Callum Reeve, nineteen years old, shot through the shoulder at a place whose name the orders never gave. The orders spoke of "positions along the frontier" and "elevations of strategic interest," as if the war were being fought among the clouds.

"How's the shoulder this morning, Private?"

"Lying to me, sir," Reeve said cheerfully. "It tells me it's healing, but I've caught it in several falsehoods already."

"Well, shoulders are notorious liars. Mine once swore it could carry a piano."

"Did it?"

"It could not. We do not speak of the piano." Ivo unwound the old dressing, checked the wound, and was pleased by what he found beneath. Clean edges, no heat in the skin. "You'll be telling your children you got this falling off a cart, I expect."

Reeve's grin flickered, just slightly. "Something like that, sir."

*Something like that.* Ivo wrote the new dressing in the ward book — *Pvt. C. Reeve, gunshot wound to left shoulder, dressing changed, improving* — and felt the familiar small friction behind his breastbone. Because the official roll, the one kept in Colonel Brase's offices across the yard, did not say *gunshot wound*. It said *accidental injury, non-combat*. And more to the point, the official roll said Private Callum Reeve of the Fourth Border Regiment was not wounded here at all, because Private Callum Reeve was, according to that same roll, present at a skirmish forty miles upriver on the twelfth of November, where his name appeared in a column of casualties.

Ivo had seen the casualty list himself. He had been carrying it to the stove as scrap, one of the small indignities of infirmary work — officers' paper became kindling, always — when a name caught his eye the way a fishhook catches a sleeve. He had stood there in the cold anteroom with the list half-unrolled, reading it twice, three times, while the stove ticked and waited.

Private C. Reeve. Killed in action, 12 November.

At that moment, Private C. Reeve had been upstairs complaining about the porridge.

Ivo had fed the list to the fire in the end. What else was there to do? But that night, lying in his narrow cot in the nurses' annex, listening to the river grind its ice, he had taken out a plain schoolboy's notebook he had bought in a market town before the war and had done a small, private, probably treasonous thing.

He had written: *Callum Reeve, Fourth Border. Alive 14 November. Listed dead 12 November.*

Then, because it seemed a poor sort of ledger with only one entry, he had added the others he could remember — men who had died in the ward whose names had never appeared anywhere, men struck from rolls for reasons written in pencil and then erased even from the pencil's memory. Eleven names. Some he knew only by their first names or their regiments, and he had left honest gaps where honesty was all he had, little blank spaces like missing teeth.

*Thomas W., Lancers. Died of fever, 3 September. No record.*

He could not stop the dying. He had learned that in his first month, thoroughly and painfully, and the learning had cost him two nights of weeping into his pillow with his coat over his head so the other orderlies wouldn't hear. But the forgetting — the forgetting was a different matter. Forgetting, he had decided, was not an act of God. It was an act of clerks. And clerks could be contradicted by other clerks.

It was a modest rebellion. He knew that. A notebook hidden inside the lining of his trunk, under a false bottom that also held a bottle of gin he was saving for a better occasion than any the garrison was likely to provide. But every morning when he copied the day's truths into it — the real causes, the real names, the real dates — he felt something in his chest straighten, like a man remembering to stand tall after years of stooping under a low doorway.

*They may take the men,* he thought, tucking the notebook away each time with the guilty speed of a boy hiding sweets. *They cannot take the arithmetic.*

* * *

Morning rounds proceeded in their usual rhythm. The ward smelled of carbolic and wet wool and, underneath everything, the particular sweetness of the stove that burned whatever fuel the quartermaster issued — this week mostly peat and, apparently, one unfortunate boot.

"Who put a boot on my fire?" demanded Corporal Hask from bed twelve, lifting his head. "I can smell it. That's someone's boot."

"That is peat, Corporal," said Ivo.

"That is a boot, Okafor. I know boots. I polished boots for two years before they let me carry anything heavier than a brush."

"Then consider it a tribute to your former profession. Smell it proudly."

Hask lay back, satisfied. He had a bullet lodged near his hip that the surgeons had examined and pronounced "inadvisable to pursue," a phrase Ivo had privately begun collecting. *Inadvisable to pursue.* As if the bullet were a fox and the surgeons tired huntsmen. In the ward book it was recorded as *shrapnel, superficial*, which was a lie of only moderate size, the kind of lie that fit comfortably in a single line.

By eleven o'clock the frost on the window had lost its negotiation and retreated to the corners, and the light came through thick and pale, laying long rectangles on the floorboards. Ivo moved down the rows with his basin and his dressings and his store of terrible jokes, which he rationed carefully — one per man, no more, because humor, like morphine, lost its potency with overuse.

Bed seventeen was young Ferro, the orderly, who wasn't a patient at all but sat on the edge of it anyway whenever his legs needed a rest, which was often, because Ferro worked harder than anyone in the building and ate less than anyone in the building, including, on occasion, the stove.

"You look gray," Ivo told him. "Lie down properly."

"If Sister sees me lying down, she'll give my rounds to Doyle's lot."

"If Sister sees you gray, she'll give you to me, and I'll leech you. Lie down."

Ferro lay down with the deep sigh of the very young, and within ninety seconds was asleep, mouth open, one boot dangling off the mattress. Ivo pulled the blanket over him and stood a moment watching the boy breathe. Nineteen? Twenty? Nobody had ever asked Ferro for papers; he had simply appeared at the garrison the previous spring, following a supply wagon, and made himself so useful that asking questions would have been like interrogating a chair. He was part of the furniture now, and the furniture was exhausted.

Ivo added him mentally to no list at all. Some names, thank God, still belonged only to the living.

* * *

In the afternoon the snow came — not the dramatic sort, but the patient colonial kind, fine as sifted flour, that arrived without wind and settled on everything with an air of permanent intention. Through the ward windows the bridge across the Sante turned white and ghostly, its lantern posts dimming early. There was a ferryman who worked the ford below the bridge in bad weather, and Ivo watched his flat boat push through the slush ice, lantern swinging, and thought, as he often did, that somewhere on the far side of that river was a world where ledgers told the truth.

"Sugar," said Amara at his elbow, making him jump.

"Beg pardon?"

"You're smiling at the ferryman. Either you want sugar, or you want to marry him, and either way the answer requires discussion. Sugar. The stores came up from Marrow Junction this morning, first convoy in three weeks. Actual sugar. Don't tell the men until it's distributed or there will be rioting, and I include the surgeons in that estimate."

"Your secret is safe with me, Sister."

"It had better be. You're the worst gossip in this garrison, Okafor. You simply do your gossiping silently, which makes it worse." She handed him a small paper sack, and her voice dropped, and the warmth went out of it the way heat goes out of a room when a door is left open. "Two new men came in on the convoy. Frostbite, exposure. They were found at the ford. And there's a third — a civilian, they're saying. Half-dead. Doyle has him at the checkpoint house, but the surgeon wants him moved here tonight if he lives."

"A civilian? Out here?"

"In this weather, out here means dead, ordinarily. So either he's extraordinarily lucky or he had business worth dying for." She looked at Ivo a moment longer than the sentence required, and then the door of the ward opened, letting in a blade of cold air and Sergeant Doyle, and Amara said, in her ordinary voice, "Sugar. In the tin with the chipped lid," and was gone.

Doyle crossed the ward with the rolling, deliberate walk of a man who wanted to be heard approaching — a courtesy, Ivo supposed, or a tactic, depending on the day. He stopped at bed nine, glanced at its occupant, and then let his eyes travel the room the way a customs officer's hands travel a suitcase.

"Ward's full," he observed.

"Ward's nearly full, Sergeant."

"Nearly." His gaze snagged briefly on Ferro, sleeping. "Boy works too hard."

"He does."

"Hm." Doyle adjusted his gloves, finger by finger, a small ritual he performed when thinking. "Colonel's office sent down a revised casualty annex this morning. For the infirmary copy. You'll see it's been corrected in several places. The earlier version contained errors."

"Errors," Ivo said.

"Clerical errors." Doyle smiled without any involvement of his eyes. "Names appearing that shouldn't appear. Names absent that shouldn't be absent. The Colonel takes accuracy seriously. You'll initial the corrections at the bottom, confirming the infirmary has received them."

"Confirming the corrections are accurate, Sergeant, or confirming that I received them?"

Doyle looked at him for a long moment. Outside, the snow ticked softly against the glass, and somewhere down the ward a man moaned in his sleep and was quiet again.

"Confirming receipt," Doyle said at last, "is generally the wiser profession." He set the annex on the desk, touched two fingers to his cap, and rolled away down the corridor, boots marking time on the boards.

Ivo stood alone in the anteroom. The annex lay on the desk, four pages, closely typed, with a red line drawn through certain entries and fresh ones inserted in the margins in the adjutant's neat hand. He read it once quickly, then again slowly, memorizing rather than moving — because Doyle was right about one thing: receipt was a trap, but memory was not yet a crime.

Three names struck. Two inserted. One of the struck names was Thomas Wexler, Lancers, whom Ivo had personally carried to the burial ground on the third of September, and who had, according to this document, died in July, at a place Ivo had never heard of, of wounds received in action — which sounded honorable enough until one remembered that Wexler had died of fever in a cot six feet from the stove, alone except for an orderly reading psalms badly.

That evening, after the lamps were trimmed and the ward had settled into its chorus of breathing, Ivo waited until Amara was occupied with a bad chest in bed four, and slipped into the anteroom, and took from the lining of his sleeve the little notebook, and wrote:

*Wexler, Thomas. Lancers. Fever, 3 Sept. Annex says July, says action, says elsewhere. Corrected today, 19 December. By whom? By whom?*

He hesitated over the question mark. Then, because a ledger owed truth even to its own uncertainties, he left both questions standing.

Below them, after a moment, he added the day's small tally — the true tally, the one that existed nowhere else on earth:

*Twenty-nine in the ward this morning. Two beds empty and unexplained. One annex of lies initialed by no one yet. One civilian coming up from the ford tonight, if he lives. Sister says he had business worth dying for.*

He blew out his match and sat a moment in the dark, listening to the river.

Somewhere down the road, through the snow, he could hear the wheels of the cart bringing the stranger up from the checkpoint house, and the creak of the lantern on the bridge, and under all of it the Sante going about its patient, honest work, carrying everything given to it toward the sea.

Ivo slid the notebook back into his sleeve and went to prepare a bed for a man nobody had officially identified, in a ward that officially held no such man, on a night that would turn out — though he could not yet know it — to be the last ordinary night of his life.
