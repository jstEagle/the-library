## 2. What the Colonel Signs

The frost came down from the hills in the night and by morning the River Sante wore a skin of grey glass along its edges, thin as a communion wafer. Ivo Okafor noticed such things because noticing was most of his work. A nurse who did not notice was merely a man in an apron; a nurse who did could feel a fever arriving the way you feel weather in an old door hinge.

He noticed, that morning, that Private Callum had stopped moaning in his sleep — a good sign at last, after nine days of it — and that Sister Amara had rearranged his ward stores again, moving the laudanum higher "so certain hands stop helping themselves," by which she meant Ferro, the orderly, who was seventeen and had a sweet tooth for anything bottled.

"It's medicinal curiosity," Ferro protested, hauling water.

"It's theft with a Latin accent," Ivo said. "Carry the water."

The garrison at Sante Ford held perhaps two hundred men: infantry companies rotated through on their way to nowhere much, a supply depot, a hospital of forty beds that never quite emptied, and the bridge — the lantern bridge, the men called it, though the lantern was more ceremony than light now, lit each dusk by a rotation of privates who drew straws for the privilege of standing in the river wind. Beyond it lay the hills where the war actually happened, and beyond the hills, nothing anyone in London wished to think about.

On the third morning of the frost, Colonel Brase came to inspect the hospital, and behind him, unannounced, came a carpenter's crew with a platform.

"A platform," Amara said flatly, watching them carry it past the window like a table being brought into a room that already had one.

"For the ceremony," Ivo said.

"What ceremony?"

"The colonel is going to say some words."

Amara snorted — a sound like a kettle deciding against boiling. She had been at Sante longer than Ivo, longer than Brase himself perhaps, and she regarded military ceremonies the way a farmer regards hail: as weather to be endured under a solid roof. "Then we'll want the screens up for the men who shouldn't see it."

"Which men shouldn't see it?"

"The dying ones," she said simply. "Let them rest."

But there was no preventing it. By noon the yard before the infirmary filled with every ambulatory soldier in the garrison, drawn up in ranks that kept drifting out of true because the ground was frozen iron and nobody's boots wanted to stay planted. A detail raised the garrison colours. The band — four men, two of whom could play — attempted something solemn and achieved something damp. And Colonel Brase mounted the platform in his greatcoat with the collar turned up against the wind, and began, in a voice built for parade grounds, to speak about the fallen.

Ivo stood at the edge of the assembly, half in the shadow of the infirmary porch, because he had learned that officers preferred nurses visible but not near. He listened the way he did everything: carefully.

"—and we honour today the memory of those whose bodies the river and the hill country declined to return to us," Brase was saying. "Lieutenant Harrow. Sergeant Meech. Corporal Dill. Private Ansel Rooke. They fell in the action at the upper fords, and though no grave bears them, every stone of this garrison does. Let their names be struck—" here a small hesitation, a cough, "—let their names be *carved* into our memory, that they may never be lost."

Applause, mittened and muffled, like distant surf made of wool.

Ivo did not applaud. He was doing arithmetic.

Lieutenant Harrow he knew — or thought he knew — a nervous young man with ink-stained cuffs who had passed through the ward in October with a bullet through the shoulder and left walking, refusing the wheelchair, insisting he'd take his tea standing up like a soldier. He had joked with Ferro about the laudanum. He had written a letter home sitting up in bed number eleven, and Ivo had posted it for him, and remembered the address because the handwriting slanted hard left, like a man leaning away from his own words.

Sergeant Meech. Ward seven, November. Fever, not wounds. Loud, funny, impossible man who had conducted an ongoing feud with the cook over the question of whether army stew legally qualified as soup. Discharged to duty on the twelfth, complaining that the only thing wrong with him was the army.

And Corporal Dill — Dill he was almost certain of. Quiet Welshman, cough that would not resolve, kept three extra days purely out of Ivo's stubbornness. Had left on the twentieth of November pale but upright, thanking him with such formality it had embarrassed them both.

Three men. Alive in his ward within the last month. Walking out of it on their own feet, past his own hands.

And now dead — honoured dead, unrecoverable dead, carved-into-memory dead — in a speech delivered forty feet from the beds they had slept in.

It was possible, he told himself, turning it over while the band attempted its damp hymn again, that he was mistaken. Names repeated themselves in armies; men shared surnames the way rivers shared water; clerks transposed ranks and regiments with cheerful incompetence. He had seen a man listed dead twice in one dispatch and alive in a third. Paper was a sloppy animal.

But he did not believe he was mistaken. He had held Harrow's shoulder while it healed. You do not confuse a shoulder you have held.

Afterward Brase came through the wards on the customary tour, saying customary things — "Good air in here," to a ward with the windows shut all winter; "This one has a fighter's face," to a man unconscious since Tuesday — and shaking no hands, because the cold gave even colonels an excuse. At the door he paused beside Ivo, which was unusual enough that the whole ward felt it.

"Okafor," the colonel said, reading the name off his apron as if confirming it against a list he carried somewhere behind his eyes. "You've been at Sante how long now? Two years?"

"Two years and a season, sir."

"Long enough to know your trade." A smile that did not involve any neighbouring muscles. "Keep the men comfortable. That is the whole of nursing, I find. Comfort." And he went out into the bright cold, leaving Ivo with the peculiar sensation of having been patted down for weapons.

"Well," said Amara, appearing at his elbow with the sixth sense she kept in the same pocket as her scissors. "What did the great man want?"

"Comfort," Ivo said.

"Hm." She looked after the colonel with her nostrils working, a woman tasting air for smoke. "Men who talk about comfort usually mean quiet."

That evening the orders began to matter to him.

They had always passed through the infirmary, of course — that was the thing he realized, sitting very still on his bunk while the others played cards. Quartermaster requisitions for dressings. Duty rosters for stretcher parties. Sanitary inspections, coal allotments, the endless paper sediment that settles in any building where men are stationed. The infirmary saw it all, because everything at Sante eventually needed either bandages or an excuse for why bandages weren't available, and both arrived in writing.

He had never read them. There was reading, and there was reading.

Now he read.

He started modestly, the way a man tests ice. The requisition book sat open on the desk in the duty room while he "finished the entries," and his eye travelled down columns that had nothing to do with him. Dressing linen, forty yards — crossed out, re-entered as twenty-five. Quinine, twelve ounces — crossed out entirely, with a note in a clerkish hand: *redirected per C.O.* Where redirected? Not said. The wounded at the upper fords had been asking for quinine since September; Ivo had written twice to ask why the answer was none, and received twice a courteous reply explaining supply difficulties in terms so general they explained nothing at all, like a sermon.

Twenty-five yards of linen for two hundred men. He thought of the dressing he had changed that morning on Private Callum's chest, cutting it close because the roll was low, telling himself the quartermaster would be along with stores any day.

The quartermaster would not be along. The quartermaster's stores were being *redirected per C.O.*

He closed the book gently, as though it might bruise.

Over the following days — the frost tightening, the river glass thickening until the ferryman complained he could neither sail nor sleep for the cracking — Ivo taught himself a new discipline. He learned which papers passed openly and which arrived folded, which clerks were careless and which were careful, which hours the duty room stood empty (mid-morning, during sick-call; the hour after supper, when everyone with rank found somewhere warmer to be). He learned that Sergeant Doyle of the provost detail had a habit of materializing in doorways without footsteps, like a cat with a commission, and adjusted accordingly.

Doyle was not a bad man — Ivo had seen him carry a drunk private to bed rather than to the cells, and once sit all night with a fevered muleteer because the man had no one — but he was a thorough one, and thoroughness in an army is a loaded weapon laid across a chair. Doyle noticed things. Ivo resolved to be less noticeable, which, for a six-foot nurse with a foreign name and a habit of looking directly at people, was a project.

He told himself he was only reading. Reading is not a crime. Half the Empire ran on men reading what they should not and calling it diligence.

But reading, he discovered, leaves a residue. It accumulates in a person like silt in a river bend. He read the movement order routing a company upriver to "the upper fords (advanced post)" and remembered Harrow's slantwise handwriting. He read the court of inquiry summary — one page, heavily abbreviated — concerning "the engagement of 14 October, casualties: nil confirmed," and thought of the fourteen men he had treated in the forty-eight hours after the fourteenth of October, several of whom had declined, in writing and out loud, to confirm being nil.

Casualties: nil confirmed. It had a kind of genius, that phrase. Not *no casualties*. Only that none were *confirmed*. The truth could stand in the middle of the sentence and be walked around, like a hole in a road everyone agreed not to mention.

On the ninth day of the frost, Amara caught him at it.

He heard her before he saw her — there was no other way anyone ever heard Amara — and by the time her shadow reached the desk his hand was already flat over the page, a gesture so guilty it convicted them both.

"Copying?" she said.

"Finishing entries."

"With your left hand." She came closer. On the desk, in plain view because God protects fools and nurses, lay the court of inquiry summary. She looked at it for a long moment. Her face did the thing Ivo had never fully mapped: nothing moved, and yet everything relocated, the way furniture changes in a room when the light shifts.

"Harrow," she said quietly. "Meech. Dill."

"You heard the speech too."

"I wrote the discharge papers for two of them." She pulled out the chair opposite and sat, which she never did, and folded her hands, which she always did. For a while the only sound was the stove ticking and, far off, the river ice booming like a slow drum. Then she said, in the voice she used for reporting deaths, "My mother used to say: when the powerful begin praising the dead, count the living."

Ivo looked at her.

"There are names in these papers," she went on, tapping the summary without quite touching it, "and names in that speech, and they don't agree, and I don't think it's clerks anymore. Clerks make honest mistakes. This—" she turned the page toward him, toward the neat abbreviation, the nil confirmed, "—this is somebody signing his name under a lie and initialling it for safety."

Brase. *Per C.O.* The colonel's initials rode through the requisitions like a watermark.

"So what do we do?" Ivo asked. It came out smaller than he intended. He was thinking of Harrow standing to drink his tea. Of Dill's formal thank-you. Of Callum upstairs breathing shallow but breathing, needing twenty-five yards of linen that had become fifteen by now, probably, or ten.

"We do what nurses do," Amara said, rising, retrieving her authority along with her scissors. "We write down what actually happened. Everything else in this garrison can be ordered to say otherwise, but a written record doesn't take orders." She paused at the door. "Not aloud. Not together. Not where Doyle walks. Slowly, Okafor. Ice first, then weight."

She went out. Ivo sat alone in the duty room with the stove ticking and the paper lying there like something washed up.

He understood, sitting there, that he had crossed some line whose existence he hadn't known about — not a line of law, armies have lawyers for lines, but a line inside a man, between *I suspect* and *I intend*. Suspicion is a visitor; intention moves in and unpacks. He had been a nurse for eleven years. His entire craft consisted of noticing what was wrong and then acting as if noticing obliged you to do something about it. He had simply never before applied it to the officers.

That night, after supper, during the empty hour, he took a cheap exercise book — the kind sold to soldiers for letters home, twopence, blue cover going grey — and cut the regimental stamp off the cover with his razor so it was only a book. In the front, in small careful script, he wrote the date and the words *Kept for the record*, and beneath that, because honesty deserved a little decoration, *(unofficial)*.

Then he made his first entries. Not the patients — the patients were already in the official books, however mendacious the margins around them. These were the others. Lieutenant J. Harrow, discharged fit, 9 October. Sergeant W. Meech, discharged fit, 12 November. Corporal E. Dill, discharged fit, 20 November. Each followed by the same line, written slowly, the nib scratching like a mouse in the wall:

*Named among the dead by Col. Brase, [date], body never recovered. Last seen alive by me.*

Last seen alive by me. He wrote it three times and found, by the third, that his hand had steadied instead of shaken. There was a strange firmness in it, like setting a bone. Somewhere in London or Cardiff or a village upcountry there were women named Harrow and Meech and Dill who had received, or would receive, printed condolence cards describing heroic deaths in actions that never occurred. When — *if*, he corrected himself, *if* — those women ever asked their questions, someone ought to be able to answer with more than silence.

Someone ought to be able to say: last seen alive by me.

He hid the book that night inside the false bottom of the instrument cabinet, beneath the spare stethoscope nobody used because everybody preferred ears, wrapped in oilcloth against damp and discovery alike. Lying in his bunk afterward, listening to the frost boom on the river and Ferro dreaming audibly of bottled things, Ivo rehearsed the dangers with professional calm. If found: charges, certainly. Prison, likely. Worse, possibly — the upper fords were not far, and men who erased the living were not famously tender with witnesses.

Against this he set a single image: Corporal Dill standing at the gate, pale as candle wax, thanking him with that terrible formality. *Thank you, Nurse Okafor, for the extra days.* The extra days that had apparently been borrowed against a death certificate.

He slept better than he expected to. Purpose, he reflected somewhere on the border of sleep, is a warmer blanket than fear is cold.

*

For a week nothing happened, which is the week he should have enjoyed.

He refined his method. He copied sparingly, choosing documents the way he chose dressings — only what was needed, nothing to invite infection. He grew fluent in the duty room's rhythms and nearly invisible in them. Twice Doyle drifted past the doorway and twice found Ivo doing exactly, boringly, what a nurse ought to be doing, and moved on with the disappointment of a cat finding the mouse asleep.

On the eighth evening, Amara touched his elbow in the corridor and said one word: "Bridge."

He went at dusk, ostensibly returning from the laundry, and stood at the rail while the ferryman lit the lantern with the ceremony of a man who has done it four thousand times and complains about every one. The river ran black under its rim of ice. Beyond the water, the hills. Beyond the hills, whatever the papers would not confirm.

"Ferro heard something," Amara said quietly, joining him, eyes forward. "Carrying trays in the mess. Two staff captains, passing through. Talking about January."

"January what?"

"That's the thing. Not January something. Just *January*. As in: 'it can all wait till January,' and 'come January there'll be no paper worth reading anyway.' And then they saw the boy and talked about the weather, which in my experience means they'd been talking about everything else." She watched the lantern swing out over the water. "New Year's, I'd guess. Turn of the century. They're making a great festival of it — there's to be a bonfire of old records, officially. Housekeeping, they call it."

"Housekeeping," Ivo repeated.

"Mm. Every garrison burns its dead paper when the year rolls. But this one's been ordered *thorough*." She turned to go, then added, without looking at him: "Whatever you're keeping in the instrument cabinet, Okafor — keep duplicates of the important parts somewhere that isn't this fort."

He stared at her. She had known about the cabinet the whole time, of course. She had probably known before he bought the book.

"Sister," he said, "you are a terrifying woman."

"I'm a nurse," said Amara, and went in to supper.

Ivo stayed at the rail a while longer. Below him the lantern burned its small stubborn yellow above the black water, and the ferryman grumbled about ice, and the hills kept whatever they kept.

Come January, there'll be no paper worth reading. He turned the phrase over the way he'd turn a stone to see what lived under it. Every record gone — including, conveniently, discharges and duty rolls and the testimony of ward eleven. A clean century. The powerful praised, the dead properly buried, and the living — the living erased so smoothly that even the erasure itself would burn.

Well. He had a twopenny book with the stamp cut off, and a hiding place, and a beginning.

It was two nights later, in the middle of that quiet, that the sentry's whistle shrilled from the checkpoint and the duty runner burst into the infirmary shouting for blankets and brandy — a traveller down at the ford, half-frozen, pulled from under the bridge.

Ivo reached for his coat, and paused, one arm in, one arm out, aware of a feeling he couldn't immediately name.

It took him until he reached the door to name it: the feeling of ice bearing weight. Something coming across the river toward him, and no way yet to tell how thick it was.
