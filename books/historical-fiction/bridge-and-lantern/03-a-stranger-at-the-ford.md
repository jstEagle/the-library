## 3. A Stranger at the Ford

The frost came early to the Sante that winter, and it came the way bad news comes — quietly first, then all at once. By the second week of December the river had gone grey and sullen along its edges, laced with ice that creaked at night like old floorboards, and the men in Ward Two had taken to complaining about their fingers and toes in the cheerful, competitive way soldiers complain about anything.

"Lost two toes, Nurse," said Private Hennegan, holding up his bandaged foot with the pride of a man presenting a medal. "Two. That's a tenth of them."

"You have ten toes, Hennegan, so mathematics was never your strong suit either," Ivo said, tucking the blanket back over him. "And you'll keep the rest if you stop waving that foot about like a flag."

"There's a draft," Hennegan insisted.

"There is always a draft. There is a draft in heaven, I expect. Suffer it."

It was good, this small banter. It kept the ward alive in a way that carbolic and cod-liver oil could not. Ivo had learned early — before the war, back when he'd been a plain hospital orderly in a civilian town with no opinions worth recording — that men did not die of wounds alone. They died of being treated like bundles to be stacked. So he joked with them, scolded them, remembered their mothers' names, and when they slept he opened his ledger and wrote down who they were, properly, in case anyone upstream decided otherwise.

The ledger lived inside the false bottom of the linen cupboard, beneath a stack of pillowcases no one had audited since the summer. It was not a grand thing. It was a stationer's notebook, water-stained, its cover soft as chamois from handling. In it were forty-one names now. Forty-one men whom the official rolls had struck through or reclassified or reassigned into silence, mostly because dead-deserted drew no pension questions and dead-of-wounds required explanations. Ivo wrote their names, their regiments where he knew them, the dates they stopped breathing on his ward, and — where he could manage it — something true. *Hennegan's brother. Sang badly, constantly. Feared water.* Small things. The things that made a name more than an entry.

He was adding one that evening — Corporal Whitlock, who had lasted four days after the shelling and spent all of them asking about his dog — when the bell at the checkpoint bridge began ringing.

Not the alarm bell. The other one. The dull, perfunctory clang that meant: *someone has arrived, deal with it.*

* * *

The sentry brought word up to the infirmary within the hour, stamping snow off his boots in the corridor and leaving gray puddles that Amara would make him mop himself.

"Fella at the ford," he said. "Collapsed right at the guardhouse door. Half-dead already, near enough. The sergeant on duty says bring him in or bury him in the morning, and it's cold work digging."

"What's wrong with him?" Ivo asked, already reaching for his coat.

"Cold, mostly. And something else. Talking nonsense. Ferryman pulled him off the near bank — says he walked out onto the ice like he intended to cross it on foot."

"On foot? It's nearly frozen through."

"Yes, sir, well." The sentry shrugged with the magnificent fatalism of a man describing someone else's problem. "Some people are peculiar."

Some people are peculiar, Ivo thought later, was possibly the truest military report ever filed.

They laid the stranger in the small side room they kept for fevers — the one with the iron cot and the window that didn't quite shut, which was a flaw in summer and a mercy in winter, since fevered men wanted air even if the air wanted them dead. He was perhaps forty, though it was hard to say; exposure erases the years between thirty-five and fifty with impartial cruelty. His clothes were good once — a wool traveling coat, city-cut, now soaked stiff and smelling of river mud — and his boots had been expensive before the river finished them. Somebody's clerk, Ivo thought. Somebody's accountant. A man who dressed for offices and had somehow wandered into a war.

His lips were blue-grey. His hands, when Ivo chafed them, were cold clear down past the wrist, the way a drowned man's are. But he would not stop muttering.

Ivo caught fragments as he worked. "...must reach... before the first..." Then, more urgently, twice, three times, the same phrase: "*before the century turns.*"

"Hmm," Ivo said aloud, to no one. "Before the century turns. Very dramatic. You'll forgive me if I aim for something humbler, like *before your heart stops.*"

The man's eyes half-opened then — brown, bloodshot, startlingly present for all the delirium — and fixed on Ivo for a long moment before sliding away again.

Amara came in with the hot-water bottles and the brandy ration she guarded like a dragon guards gold. She looked the stranger over with her nurse's arithmetic — pulse, breath, color, the particular slackness of the mouth — and pronounced him, "Half-dead, entirely foolish, and no danger to the ward if we keep him isolated. What's his name?"

"He hasn't offered one."

"They never do." She tucked a bottle against his ribs with brisk tenderness. "Papers?"

"The guardhouse took his satchel for the sergeant to look through. Standard procedure."

"Mm." Amara's mouth thinned slightly. Sergeant Doyle's procedure. She said nothing further about it, but Ivo noted the thinning of the mouth and filed it away, because Amara's silences were more articulate than most men's speeches.

By midnight the stranger's fever climbed, and by two in the morning it broke, and somewhere in the sweating interval between the two he gave them a name, gasped out to the ceiling as though reporting to some invisible superior: "Pell. Mr. Pell. Nothing — nothing else — only Pell—"

"Well, Mr. Pell," Ivo murmured, wringing out a cloth. "You picked a fine month to walk across a frozen river."

* * *

The trouble began, as trouble usually did at the garrison, with paperwork.

Sergeant Doyle came round at dawn with the stranger's satchel under his arm and an expression of aggrieved diligence that Ivo had come to think of as Doyle's natural face — the face of a man perpetually disappointed that the world contained objects requiring him to look at them.

"Property of the detainee," Doyle said, setting the satchel on the infirmary table as though it might bite him. "Colonel's orders: medical staff to inventory personal effects of any patient unfit to do so himself. You're to sign for it."

"Inventories of half-frozen men's luggage now fall to nursing staff," Ivo said mildly. "The army's reach is a marvel."

"The army's reach," said Doyle, without humor, "is exactly as far as it needs to be. Sign there."

Doyle lingered while Ivo unbuckled the satchel — lingered being perhaps generous; he loitered the way a cat loiters near a mousehole, with apparent idleness and total attention. Inside, the contents were unremarkable enough at first glance: a razor, wrapped; a spare shirt gone grey at the folds; a tin cup; a Bible with the spine cracked at Psalms; a comb missing three teeth; a canvas roll of papers tied with string.

It was the canvas roll that mattered, though Ivo did not know it yet. He lifted it out, felt the weight of paper within, untied the string with the careful hands of a man who has learned that documents in wartime are never merely documents.

Letters. Perhaps twenty of them, folded and refolded so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth. They were in no order. Some were dated; some weren't. The handwriting varied — sometimes cramped, sometimes loose and sprawling — but the signature at the bottom of each page was the same, and when Ivo saw it, the room went very quiet around him, the way a forest goes quiet.

*A. Vane.*

He knew that hand. Every man at the Sante garrison who had been there longer than a winter knew that hand, because Captain Aldous Vane had written the requisitions, the rosters, the condolence notes — all of it in that looping, theatrical script, each capital letter flourished like a man taking a bow. Vane had signed supply orders with more style than the colonel. Vane had once signed Ivo's own leave pass, two years ago, adding beneath his name a small drawn lantern — his private mark, his little joke, *the light stays lit*. The men had loved him for such vanities. Officers were not supposed to have jokes.

Then Kessari Crossing had happened, in whatever form it had actually happened — the official account was a heroic engagement, the unofficial accounts were rumors, and the rumors had a habit of going quiet whenever officers entered a room. And Captain Aldous Vane, officially, had died in that engagement. There had been a ceremony. Brase had spoken. A plaque had gone up in the mess hall with VANE carved into it, and his effects had been inventoried, and his name had passed from speech into stone.

And here was his hand, looping and bowing across twenty letters, carried through the winter by a man who called himself Pell and walked on rivers.

"Nurse." Doyle's voice, flat. "You've been reading that roll for some time."

"I'm inventorying," Ivo said, and was pleased at how steady it came out. "That's the task you assigned me. Razor, shirt, cup, Bible, comb, papers." He retied the string with fingers that felt like someone else's fingers. "Approximately twenty letters. Unsigned addressees. I can't read the addressees because there don't appear to be any."

"Do you know whose hand that is?" Doyle asked. His eyes had gone very still, and Ivo understood suddenly that Doyle was not asking casually. This was the question under the question, the trapdoor under the rug.

"It's a very fine hand," Ivo said. "Whoever owns it wastes ink professionally."

Doyle looked at him a moment longer. Then he took the canvas roll, tucked it under his arm, and said, "The colonel will want these held. The patient's property remains confiscated pending review. Sign the inventory."

Ivo signed. Doyle left. The door closed behind him with its usual indifferent click, and Ivo stood alone in the corridor feeling the way one feels when the ice underfoot makes its first small sound — not yet breaking, but announcing its intention.

* * *

Through the following day the stranger — Pell, when he was anything — drifted between sleep and fever, and Ivo found reasons to be in the side room far more often than the ward required. He took the man's pulse at intervals too close together. He changed water glasses that did not need changing. He straightened blankets that were already straight. Amara watched him do all of this on her afternoon round and said nothing, which from Amara was a paragraph.

The truth was simpler than his excuses: Ivo could not stop thinking about the letters. Twenty letters in a dead man's hand. And underneath that thought, older and colder, sat another: three weeks ago Colonel Brase had stood on the parade ground honoring fallen heroes whose bodies were never recovered, and Ivo had recognized three of the honored dead among the living men of his own ward, struck from the rolls by the same pen that had signed the tribute. If a garrison could erase living men, what couldn't it do with a dead captain?

At dusk, when the ward lamps were lit and the noise of the day had settled into coughs and snores, Ivo went into the side room ostensibly to check the hot-water bottles and found Pell awake.

Truly awake, for the first time. Propped on one elbow, eyes clear and dark and appraising, watching Ivo the way a card player watches the dealer's hands.

"You're the one who pulled me through," Pell said. His voice was rough gravel but functional. "I remember a voice. Telling me about drafts in heaven."

"That was likely me, yes."

"Thank you." He said it simply, without performance. Then, before Ivo could answer: "My satchel. Where is it?"

"Confiscated. Sergeant Doyle has it. Letters and all."

Something crossed Pell's face — not fear exactly. More like a man hearing a floorboard give way and calculating, instantly, how far the drop is. "All of them?"

"All twenty, near enough. Signed *A. Vane* in every one." Ivo kept his voice level, conversational, the tone he used for telling men difficult truths gently. "Which interests me, Mr. Pell, because Captain Vane has been dead two years. There's a plaque. I've seen it. Very tidy carving."

Pell was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the wind worried at the ill-fitting window, and somewhere down the ward a man laughed in his sleep at some dream joke none of the living would hear.

"Fevers loosen tongues," Pell said at last. "So do near-drownings. Mine is loose enough tonight to tell you that you ought to be careful, Nurse. Asking questions with that face — the honest face — in a place like this. It's a dangerous habit."

"I've been told. I continue anyway."

"Yes," Pell said softly, and there was something in it — recognition, appraisal, almost warmth. "Yes, I can see that you do."

Ivo should have left it there. He wants me to leave it there, thought Ivo, and stayed precisely because of it. He lowered himself onto the stool beside the cot and folded his hands.

"I've nursed a great many men," Ivo said. "I have watched officers write reports about them afterward. And I have noticed, over time, that the reports and the men do not always resemble one another. So understand me, Mr. Pell: I am not curious the way idle people are curious. I am curious the way a man is curious about a sound in the night. Either you tell me what those letters are, or I will spend every sleepless night until spring inventing worse possibilities than the truth. I promise you my imagination is thorough."

Pell studied him. The lamplight moved across his face, and for a moment — just a moment, in the angle of the shadow, in the tilt of the head — Ivo thought absurdly of the mess hall plaque, of the flourished capitals, of the little drawn lantern. Then it was gone, and there was only a soaked, exhausted traveler in an army cot, looking at him with eyes too old for his face.

"Mr. Pell," Ivo repeated. "Is that even your name?"

A pause. The wind. The sleeping laughter down the ward, fading.

"No," said Pell quietly. "But it will do for now. And you're right about one thing, Nurse — your imagination isn't thorough enough. Not nearly."

He turned his face to the wall, and would say nothing more that night. But as Ivo rose to go, dousing the lamp, the voice came once more from the darkness behind him — dry, faint, and carrying, unmistakably, the ghost of a smile:

"Leave the light burning, would you? I find I have a fondness for lanterns."

Ivo stood very still in the doorway, hand on the lamp handle, and did not sleep well at all.
