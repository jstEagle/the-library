## 4. The Hand of a Dead Man

The fever broke sometime before dawn, and by the time the grey light came seeping through the infirmary shutters like something ashamed of itself, Mr. Pell was sleeping the ordinary sleep of an ordinary man — which was, Ivo had decided over the course of a long night, the most suspicious thing about him.

Ordinary men did not carry letters in the hand of dead captains.

Ivo had turned the satchel's contents over in his mind perhaps two hundred times since yesterday, and each turn had produced the same cold click of recognition. He knew that handwriting the way a priest knows his mother's face on a prayer card. Captain Aldous Vane had been a patient at the River Sante garrison hospital for eleven days in the spring of 1897 — a flesh wound to the thigh sustained in what the reports called *a minor engagement*, though Ivo had dressed enough wounds from that engagement to know there was nothing minor about anything fought along the Kessari road. Vane had been a charming patient, which was rare. Most officers were either tyrants or cowards when laid low, and Vane had managed a kind of theatrical humility, thanking Ivo extravagantly for every bandage change, calling him "the true general of this garrison," and writing letters at the little table by the window for hours at a stretch.

It was those hours at the window that had burned the handwriting into Ivo's memory. The letters looped. That was the thing about them — the capital letters performed, like opera singers. The *V* in his own signature swept down below the line and curled back up as if reconsidering. The *A* of *Aldous* had a crossbar that floated free of both legs, confident it could do better on its own.

And now that same floating crossbar sat folded in a stranger's satchel, twenty-three letters deep, addressed in some cases to no one at all.

Ivo pulled the blanket higher over Pell's shoulders. The man had come across the ford half-frozen, which in December was less an accident than a decision. Nobody crossed the Sante in winter by mistake. His boots were good boots, resoled recently. His coat was expensive and had its insignia unpicked with care rather than torn away in haste. And under the fever, under the shivering, he had a soldier's stillness — the particular way he lay without twitching, listening even in sleep.

"You are not an ordinary clerk," Ivo told him quietly. "Whatever you say you are."

Pell did not answer, being asleep. It was one of the more pleasant conversations Ivo had had all week.

Sister Amara moved past behind him with a basin, and touched his elbow — her way of saying *eat something* without wasting words. She had noticed the satchel too. Ivo had seen her notice. But she had said nothing, and Amara's silences were longer and more considered than most people's speeches, so he filed her silence away alongside his own suspicion and went to fetch bread and dripping, because fainting nurses made poor guardians of secrets.

* * *

Two years ago, almost to the week, Ivo had stood in the officers' ward and listened to Colonel Brase announce that Captain Aldous Vane was dead.

It had been done with flags. Brase liked ceremony the way other men liked brandy, and he had arranged the announcement beautifully: the garrison drawn up in the yard, the Union Jack run to half-mast, and the Colonel himself reading from a paper in a voice built for parade grounds. *"It is my sorrowful duty to report that Captain A. Vane, while conducting reconnaissance upriver, was lost in the flood waters of the Sante during the storms of November last. His body has not been recovered. He is survived by the gratitude of his Queen."*

Gratitude of his Queen. Ivo had stood at the back with his ledger of small names pressed against his ribs and thought: *what a strange river this is, that drowns only captains and never their witnesses.*

Because there were things wrong with the death, and Ivo had spent two years turning those things over like stones.

First: Vane had gone upriver on reconnaissance with a full escort of twelve men, and not one of those twelve had drowned, fallen ill, or been heard from again. Twelve men did not simply evaporate. Second: the flood story had arrived three weeks after the storm it described, which was either very bad administration or very good patience. Third — and this was the stone Ivo returned to most often — Vane had come to the infirmary two days before departing, healthy and cheerful, and had shaken Ivo's hand and said, with that performing charm, *"Nurse Okafor, if ever you hear I've died, don't believe the paperwork."*

Ivo had laughed at the time. Officers joked about death the way boys threw stones at windows — carelessly, delightedly, certain nothing would break.

Then the autumn after the announcement, a corporal named Hewitt had been carried into the ward with a gut wound and a fever, and in the delirium Hewitt talked. He talked about a crossing. About orders given at the Kessari ford — orders that no village, no age, no condition of surrender would have excused. He talked until Sister Amara gently sedated him, and he survived four days, and on the fourth day his file was stamped *deceased* six hours before he actually died, and by morning the file was gone from the cabinet entirely, and Ivo had learned that paperwork could be a kind of drowning too.

Hewitt had said one phrase that stayed: *the captain watched from the far bank.*

The captain. Not *Captain Vane*. Delirious men do not edit themselves, but they do reach for titles, and the title had been spoken with something worse than fear — with awe.

So when the flags went to half-mast, Ivo had done the arithmetic quietly, in the place where he kept everything else: Vane had ordered something unforgivable at Kessari Crossing; Vane had been allowed to die on paper instead of standing trial; and twelve witnesses had been retired along with him. The river had swallowed exactly the right number of people.

He had written none of it in the ledger. The ledger held names of soldiers, small names, erased ones. But that night, two years ago, he had written a single line at the back page, small enough to be missed: *If Vane lives, someone chose that he should.*

Now the someone's handwriting was sleeping three beds down, breathing in careful rhythm, wearing a coat with its buttons changed.

* * *

At noon, Amara looked in on Pell, pronounced the fever "broken but rude," and instructed Ivo to let him sleep. Ivo agreed with her warmly and then, the moment she was occupied with Private Callahan's dressing, pulled a chair to Pell's bedside and began, with great deliberation, to sharpen a scalpel.

He was not going to use the scalpel. He wanted that understood even if only by the room.

The infirmary was quiet in the way he loved best — the long afternoon hush, snow-light through the shutters striping the floorboards, someone's slow breath, the tick of the water clock Amara had liberated from the dispensary years ago. Ivo worked the whetstone along the blade in long, unhurried strokes. *Shing. Shing.* It was a sound that meant different things to different patients, and he was curious what it would mean to this one.

On the fifth stroke, Pell's breathing changed. Not waking-breathing — *listening*-breathing. Ivo had nursed enough feigned sleepers to know the difference. A genuine sleeper breathes like weather; a pretending one breathes like a performance, slightly too regular, slightly too considerate.

"Mr. Pell," Ivo said pleasantly, continuing to stroke the blade. "You gave us quite a fright at the bridge."

*Shing.*

"Fever of forty degrees. In December. Crossing the ford alone." *Shing.* "In my experience, men catch such fevers from bad luck. You do not strike me as a man who has bad luck. Bad luck requires a man to be surprised by events, and you—" Ivo set the scalpel down on the tray with a small musical clink, "—you do not look like a man who is ever surprised."

Silence. Then, because honesty is sometimes the best disguise, Ivo picked up the satchel from where it hung on the bed frame and drew out the topmost letter, holding it where the light fell across the paper.

"I found these when I cut your coat off you. Standard procedure for hypothermia; wet wool kills faster than fever. I was looking for wet wool." He unfolded the letter halfway, carefully, not reading it aloud. "Instead I found this. Which is interesting, Mr. Pell, for a number of reasons. The least interesting reason is that you carry another man's correspondence. Clerks do that. The second-least-interesting reason is the quality of the paper, which is officer's stock, not clerk's. But the reason that interests me most—"

"The crossbar on the A," Pell said.

His voice was rough with fever but perfectly lucid. He had not opened his eyes.

Ivo felt the hair rise on his forearms. "You're awake."

"I've been awake since the whetstone." Pell opened his eyes at last. They were pale grey, unglamorous, tired eyes — eyes that had decided some time ago to stop performing. Up close, without the frost and the collapse, he was harder to read than any man Ivo had met. Somewhere north of forty, lean in the way of men who had been hungry on purpose. "You keep lists too."

Not a question.

The room got very small and very loud with its own quiet. Ivo became aware of his own heartbeat, of the water clock ticking, of Private Callahan snoring faintly two beds over — the whole ordinary machinery of an afternoon that had just stopped being ordinary.

"I don't know what you mean," Ivo said.

"Yes, you do." Pell shifted up against the pillows, wincing, and looked at Ivo with the frank appraisal of a man taking inventory. "You searched my satchel for wet wool and then you sharpened a scalpel beside my bed for ten minutes hoping I'd wake up frightened. Frightened men confess. That's not nursing, that's interrogation. Nurses who interrogate are men who need to know things. Men who need to know things at River Sante, in this year, are men keeping records the Colonel doesn't know exist." He paused, and something almost gentle crossed his face. "Also, you have ink under your fingernails. Green-black. Indigo cake, the cheap kind that doesn't wash out. Officers' clerks get proper ink. Cheap indigo is what a man buys himself, privately, to write things he can't ask anyone to write for him."

Ivo looked down at his hands. Two years of scrubbing, and the ghost of the ink still lived in the quick of his nails like a bruise.

"You might simply be observant," Ivo said.

"And you might simply be a nurse," Pell agreed. "We might both simply be many things." He reached out slowly — telegraphing the movement, Ivo noted, the way you move around injured animals and armed men — and tapped the letter in Ivo's hand. "You recognized the hand. I watched your face when you took the satchel, before the fever pulled me under. You looked at that envelope like a man seeing a ghost at his own dinner table. So you knew Aldous Vane. And knowing Vane at this garrison means you know how he died, which means you know how he didn't."

The scalpel lay between them on the tray, gleaming and suddenly absurd. Ivo left it there anyway. Props have their dignity.

"Sit properly," Ivo said at last, "and I'll take your pulse, because if you've lied about the fever breaking I'll know within thirty seconds, and then we'll continue this conversation with you actually conscious, which will make a pleasant change."

Pell complied. His pulse was steady, a little fast, honest enough. Ivo counted it while neither of them spoke, and in the counting found himself making the oldest calculation in his ledger-keeper's life: what does this man know, what will it cost me, and who else knows it?

"Here is my situation, Mr. Pell," Ivo said quietly, releasing his wrist. "Three months ago I watched Colonel Brase give a speech honoring fourteen men whose bodies were never recovered. I had personally bathed three of those men the previous month. They died of dysentery, badly, complaining about the soup, and they were declared lost in action so that their pensions would go where the Colonel wished and their names would not appear anywhere near the word *Kessari*. I cannot stop the dying. I cannot stop the lying. What I can do—" he tapped his own chest, where the ledger lived against his ribs like a second heartbeat, "—is refuse the forgetting. That is all. I am not a spy. I am not brave. I am a nurse with a notebook."

"And I," said Pell, "am a clerk with a satchel."

They regarded one another. Something passed between them that Ivo would spend years trying to name — not trust, nothing so clean, but the specific relief of two men discovering the other is also carrying contraband. It is easier to hide a thing in a crowd of identical hidden things.

"Twenty-three letters," Ivo said.

"Thirty-one. You missed eight sewn into the lining."

"Why does a dead man write thirty-one letters?"

For the first time, Pell smiled, and the smile was tired in a way that had nothing to do with fever. "Because a dead man," he said, "has until the first of January to become a live one again — or stay dead forever. On New Year's Day, by order of the Colonial Office, every record older than five years in every garrison from here to the coast goes into the fire. Dispatches. Hospital files. Courts of inquiry. Private correspondence, if the Colonel's supplementary order means what I think it means." He let that settle. "Everything Aldous Vane ever signed stops existing in nineteen days, Nurse Okafor. Except in two places in the world." He raised one finger. "This satchel." A second finger, aimed politely at Ivo's chest. "And whatever you've got under your shirt."

The fire crackled. Down the ward, Callahan muttered something in his sleep about his mother's kitchen.

"Who are you?" Ivo asked. "Truly."

"That," Pell said, settling back into the pillows and closing his eyes with the composure of a man who had decided to trust precisely one inch further and no more, "is chapter two. First tell me, Nurse Okafor, since we are trading inventories: how many names in that book of yours?"

Ivo hesitated. Every instinct honed by two years of secrecy said *lie*. But the man had already counted his ink and read his face, and besides — there was a strange, shameful comfort in saying the number out loud, in the open air, to a witness.

"Four hundred and six," Ivo said.

Pell was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke again his voice had dropped, and the dryness in it had cracked somewhere down the middle.

"Aldous wrote in one of these letters — the last one — that the number he was responsible for was beyond counting, and that the men sent to bury it couldn't count past their pensions. Four hundred and six." He exhaled slowly. "You've outnumbered them."

Before Ivo could answer, boots sounded in the corridor — the particular brisk, deliberate boots of Sergeant Doyle, whose footsteps Ivo could identify at forty paces the way a sailor identifies weather. Doyle's voice carried ahead of him, cheerfully asking an orderly something about supply inventories, and getting closer.

Pell's eyes snapped open, and Ivo saw the transformation he had half expected and fully dreaded: the sick man vanished, the clerk vanished, and something alert and old and military lay flat against the pillows in their place.

"Nurse," Pell murmured, barely moving his lips, "your sergeant is two days early for his rounds. Either that's coincidence—" the grey eyes flicked to the satchel, "—or somebody at this garrison already knows a dead man's mail just walked across your bridge."

Doyle's boots stopped outside the infirmary door.
