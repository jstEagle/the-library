## 5. Pell's Account

The fever broke sometime before dawn, and I woke to find a nurse sitting in the chair beside my bed with a ledger open on his knee, watching me the way a man watches a fire he has not yet decided whether to put out or let spread.

"You keep lists too," I said again, because it had served me well the night before, and because my throat still felt like someone had rung it out like laundry.

"I asked you a dozen questions while you were sleeping," he said. "You answered none of them."

"That's very disciplined of me."

"You were delirious. You sang half of a hymn and then argued with your mother about boots."

"Ah." I considered this. "Then you know everything of consequence already. The hymn was *Abide With Me*, the boots were brown, and my mother was right, as usual. What else is there?"

Ivo Okafor — for so the duty roster pinned to the tent flap had told me, along with the small useful fact that he signed himself *Nurse-Orderly, First Class*, a rank that existed nowhere in any army manual and was therefore exactly the sort of rank a man invents when he intends to stay invisible — closed the ledger but did not put it away. He rested his hand flat upon it, the way a gambler rests a hand on his cards.

"There is the small matter," he said, "of the letters in your satchel."

I had wondered when we would arrive there. I had hoped, honestly, that we might circle the town a few more times first — take some tea, discuss the weather, which had turned properly vicious overnight, sleet ticking against the canvas like a slow applause. But circling was a luxury. The calendar was not.

"The satchel," I said, "belongs to Mr. Pell."

"And who does Mr. Pell belong to?"

"No one, since 1896. I was made redundant by a bankruptcy and then re-hired by a dead man, which does remarkable things for one's job security. Dead men make few demands. They never reschedule meetings."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, more the place where a smile would be built if anyone ever chose to build one there. "You are Vane's clerk."

"I was Captain Aldous Vane's clerk for two years and four months. I filed his dispatches, mended his pens, forged nothing, and witnessed a great deal. Then he died at Kessari Crossing, heroically, according to the dispatch I myself copied out in fair hand — and I went home to London, where I discovered that heroic death pays no pension to clerks." I shifted against the pillows. My ribs ached from the river; the ford at night in December is not an experience I recommend to friends, and I have very few of those left to recommend anything to. "And then, eleven months later, letters began arriving in my landlady's care, in a hand I would know blindfolded, asking me to come back."

* * *

He made me tell it in order, which was cruel of him, because order is precisely what the story lacks.

"You understand," I said, "that I can't give you order. Order implies that someone arranged events sensibly. What happened at Kessari Crossing was not sensible. It was arithmetic. Someone wanted a number reduced and used the cheapest method available."

"Whose arithmetic?"

"Patience. In order means starting with the pens."

So I started with the pens. I told him about the morning in September of '97 when Vane sent me down to the company storehouse with a requisition for forty rounds per man instead of the regulation twenty, and how I thought nothing of it, because captains are always requisitioning double ammunition for exercises they intend to fudge the reports of. I told him about the orders that came through two days later, unsigned, directing the column to cross at the Kessari ford before dawn rather than at the bridge — the bridge being where the village's elders, midwives, and goat population tended to congregate at market hour, and the ford being where nobody was.

"Except," Ivo said quietly, "the villagers crossed at the ford."

"Everyone crosses at the ford. Bridges are for armies and idiots. That is rather the whole point of the thing." I heard my own voice go thin and stopped, and looked at my hands, which were still the wrong color from the cold. "I didn't know. You must believe I didn't know. I knew the route had changed. I copied the change into the order book in my neat little clerk's hand, the same hand that now copies out his letters, God help me, and I went and ate my breakfast. Two hundred and six people died in the time it took me to eat a boiled egg and complain about it, which I did, because it was underdone. I have thought about that egg every day since. There should be a special hell reserved for men who are judged by their breakfasts."

Ivo said nothing for a long moment. Outside, the sleet kept up its polite applause.

"And afterward?" he said.

"Afterward, the reports wrote themselves the way reports do. *Engagement with hostile forces.* * regrettable casualties among non-combatants caught in the line of fire.* *Captain A. Vane, who ordered the crossing, killed in the subsequent action.* Only there was no subsequent action, Nurse Okafor. There was a boat, and a civilian coat, and a captain who had been promised — I have seen the letter promising it, I hold it in my satchel at this moment — that if he vanished cleanly, certain names above his name would remain unexamined. Certain signatures. Certain signatures belonging to men who are currently eating well and being promoted."

"Brase."

I did not answer directly. I had learned something in the last two years about answering questions directly, namely: don't. Instead I said, "In three days' time — well. Thirty-one days' time, if my arithmetic has suffered from the fever, and it may have — every record this garrison holds goes into the fires. New century, new paper. That includes the crossing orders. It includes the ammunition requisition in my fair little hand. It includes whatever private correspondence certain officers have been careless enough to keep, because the burn order — and I have seen the burn order — is written broadly enough to swallow a cathedral. On the first of January, the massacre becomes a rumor, and rumors about dead heroes are easily dismissed as the spite of living nobodies."

"And you intend to stop that."

"I intend to make it inconvenient. There is a journalist in London, a Mr. Halloran of the *Dispatch*, who has been chewing on this story for a year without ever getting proof past the censors. I am carrying proof. Testimony, correspondence, the original crossing order with its signature intact. Enough paper to hang a reputation, if not a man — reputations hang more easily than men, they're strung up on weaker hooks."

* * *

Here is where I must be honest about the nurse, because honesty is the only currency I have left and I am trying to spend it carefully.

I had watched him for two days before I let myself collapse at that checkpoint. Deliberately. The fever was genuine — the river saw to that — but the collapsing was strategy. I had read his file, or rather Vane had read it for me and quoted it in his letters, because Vane, wherever he is, keeps files on everyone, and his file on the River Sante infirmary contained one line I could not stop thinking about: *The orderly writes things down.*

That line appeared in the fourth letter. It was not an instruction. Vane's letters never instructed; they simply noticed things, the way a lamp notices a room. But I understood it well enough. If I was going to move his paper through three checkpoints guarded by men paid to find it, I wanted at my side the one man in this garrison who had already been committing the identical crime, quietly, for who knows how long.

So when Ivo set his ledger on the blanket between us and said, "Before you tell me anything further, we are going to compare notes," I felt something I had not felt since 1897. Relief. Frightening, improper relief.

"Compare notes," I repeated.

"If your story is true," he said, "then it will agree with mine. And if it agrees with mine, then neither of us can afford to report the other." He said it without heat, almost apologetically, the way a man apologizes for the weather while closing the shutters against it. "I don't say that to threaten you, Mr. Pell. I say it because it is the only basis on which two strangers in this place can trust each other. Mutual destruction is a very sturdy foundation. Better than love. Love forgives, and forgiveness leaves doors open."

"My word," I said. "They told me nurses were gentle."

"We are. Gently is the only way to hold a knife."

He opened the ledger.

I will not describe everything in it — some things I witnessed secondhand and some I have no right to carry, having carried enough already — but I will tell you what it did to me. It was a list of names. Dead men, mostly. Soldiers struck from the rolls, erased from dispatches, buried under initials or wrong regiments or no regiment at all, each entry dated, each annotated in a small furious hand. Three weeks of entries alone covered men who had officially *never been posted to Sante*, though Ivo had pulled their splinters and held their heads and heard their mothers' names.

"Cross-reference it," he said, and passed me the ledger like a man passing a live coal.

I did. Fever-shaky, reading by the grey light of the tent's one lamp, I matched his entries against the story I had carried across a frozen river in a satchel strapped to my chest. The ammunition figures agreed. The dates of the erased wounded agreed — men treated at Kessari's aftermath and then subtracted, their injuries reassigned in official papers to other companies, other months. One name, *Okonkwo, P., pvt.*, appeared in his ledger under November of '97 with the note *died of wounds, no record exists* — and there, in my memory, sat the same name in Vane's casualty adjustment, the original of which was folded in oilcloth six inches from my knee.

"It agrees," I said at last. My voice came out strange. "Every line of it. Nurse Okafor, you have assembled, in a notebook hidden — where, may I ask?"

"In a hollowed-out Bible."

"— in a hollowed-out Bible, a case that three separate tribunals would weep over. Do you know what you've done? You've done the part I crossed a sea and a river and a checkpoint to find someone else to do. Proof needs a witness outside the machine. I'm inside the machine; my testimony is contaminated; I was his clerk, they'll say, of course he says these things. You're a nurse. Nurses are believed. It's one of history's few kindnesses to your profession."

"And you," he said, "have done the part I could never do. I know *that* names were erased. I did not know *who* signed the erasure, or why, or that the man responsible was alive and posting letters." He took the ledger back, and for a moment his thumb rested on the page as if on a wound. "Which brings us to the question I have been circling since the ford."

"Ask it."

"Pell is not your name."

"How do you know?"

"Because last night, delirious, you answered to *Tobias* twice, and because no landlady in London writes her lodger's true name on the envelope when the lodger is hiding." His eyes were very steady. "Who are you, and where is Aldous Vane?"

* * *

I looked at him for a long time. The sleet had softened to snow, big flakes drifting past the tent flap, and somewhere across the compound a bugle was attempting reveille and mostly achieving noise.

There were perhaps three answers to his question. One was the truth. One was a lie. And one was the truth bent just enough to pass through a narrow door, which is the shape most truths take in wartime, and which I chose, because I liked him, and because lying to a man who keeps a ledger of the erased seemed like a poor long-term investment.

"Tobias Wren," I said. "Late of Fenchurch Street, late of Vane's service. And where Aldous Vane is, Mr. Wren cannot swear. I receive letters. I obey them. I deliver what they tell me to deliver, to whom they tell me to deliver it. If you want to know whether the dead man writes them himself —" I shrugged, and my ribs objected. "A hand is a hard thing to fake for two years. But not impossible. Not for that man."

"So he might be alive."

"He might be anything. He might be the ghost of this whole war, taking dictation." I leaned back. "What I know is this: the letters come, the hand is his, and the last one contained a sentence that has kept me walking north through winter. Shall I recite it? *'Tell the nurse who writes things down that his book is safer with us than with him.'*"

The lamp guttered. Ivo did not move for the space of three heartbeats — I counted them, it is a clerk's habit, counting other men's silences — and when he spoke, his voice was as level as a frozen pond.

"He knows about the ledger."

"He knows about everything. He knew about the ford before the ford knew about itself." I reached slowly, deliberately, and laid two fingers on the satchel strap where it lay across the chair. "Thirty-one days, Nurse Okafor. Three checkpoints between here and the coast, and Sergeant Doyle already looks at me like a dog looks at a thief. Your book and mine agree on every line, which means neither of us can hang without the other. So."

He looked at the satchel, and then at the Bible-shaped hollow where his own evidence slept, and I watched him arrive at the only conclusion the arithmetic allowed — the same conclusion, I suspect, that Vane had arrived at years before either of us existed, and which is the reason the man has survived every side of every story.

"All right, Wren," Ivo said. "We plan the route together. Starting with the bridge."

It was at that precise moment that Sergeant Doyle's boots came to a stop outside the tent flap, and stayed there.
