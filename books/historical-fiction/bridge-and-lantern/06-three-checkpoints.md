## 6. Three Checkpoints

The infirmary at night had a grammar of its own. The snorers held the long vowels; the feverish supplied the punctuation; and somewhere around two in the morning the whole ward settled into a low, communal hum that Ivo had come to think of as the building's honest voice. It was under cover of that voice — with a stub of candle shielded inside a chipped enamel basin, so that its light fell only on his hands and the ledger — that he sat across from Mr. Pell and listened to him ruin a perfectly good map.

"No, no," Pell said, tapping the oilcloth with a pencil stub. "You're thinking of the ford at Kessel's Bend. That's been closed since the spring floods."

"Then why did you draw it?"

"I drew it hopefully." Pell scratched it out. "Hope is cheap on paper."

Ivo watched him work. There was something almost pleasant about it, he admitted — this quiet conspiracy of two men hunched over a map by stolen candlelight, like boys planning to steal apples from an orchard that happened to be guarded by the entire colonial army. He was careful not to enjoy it too much. Enjoyment, in his experience, preceded disaster the way lightning preceded thunder, except backwards.

"The route is simple," Pell went on. "That's what makes it terrible. Three checkpoints, no way around any of them. Bridge, rail depot, customs house. Everything else is river or swamp or swamp pretending to be river."

"Everything else is also winter," Ivo said.

"And swamps in winter are merely cold swamps, whereas checkpoints in winter are armed ones. Agreed. So we go through them. All three." Pell drew three small, neat crosses along the road like a man marking graves. Ivo tried not to notice how natural the gesture looked on him.

* * *

They began, sensibly enough, with the bridge.

"The guard post changes shift at dawn and dusk," Ivo said. "Eight men on days, six at night, because Sergeant Okafor—" he caught himself, "—because somebody once decided night duty was less important. The corporal in charge of papers is a boy called Renwick. He checks documents the way a man checks whether milk has turned: he holds them up, squints, and decides based mostly on mood."

"You know him?"

"I've bandaged his hand twice. Once from a knife, once from his own enthusiasm with a bayonet drill. He calls me *sir*, which tells you everything about how seriously the army takes nurses."

Pell grinned. It was the first time Ivo had seen him do it properly, and it changed his whole face — made him look younger, and more tired, both at once. "And your papers? What would a nurse need to cross the bridge for?"

"Medical supply runs happen twice a week. The infirmary orders from the depot at Marrow Junction. Someone signs the manifest, someone carries it, someone waves it through." Ivo hesitated. "Usually Ferro does the carrying. He's young. He gets carsick on the ferry and complains about it the entire way, which is somehow worse than the crossing itself."

"Ferro." Pell wrote the name down, then looked at what he'd done and crossed it out with a small grimace. "Habit. Vane used to make me write down every name in every garrison. Said names were the only currency that never devalued."

"He sounds like a charming employer."

"He sounds," Pell said carefully, "like a man whose handwriting you've seen."

Ivo let the silence sit there between them, fat and awkward, like a cat that had decided to occupy exactly the space where neither of them wanted it. Then he said, "The manifest needs two signatures. The quartermaster's and Colonel Brase's adjutant. I can get the first. The second—"

"The second we forge."

"I don't forge. I copy. There's a moral distinction and I intend to keep clinging to it."

"Copy, then. Copy beautifully." Pell slid the manifest template across the basin-light toward him. "You have a clerk's hand. Better than mine."

"That's because mine is honest."

"All handwriting starts honest. It only goes wrong when people start signing things they shouldn't." Pell said it lightly, but something underneath it wasn't light at all, and Ivo found himself looking up sharply — and finding Pell already looking away, very busy with the pencil stub, very interested in the closed ford at Kessel's Bend.

* * *

The rail depot came next, and it came with bad news attached, the way most things did.

"Marrow Junction," Pell said, "is the problem. Not because of the guards — there are only four, and they're mostly interested in contraband tobacco — but because of the schedule. One train south per day, leaving at eleven at night, arriving at the coast by morning. If you miss it, you stand on a frozen platform until the next one, holding a ledger full of treason, becoming progressively more memorable to everyone who passes."

"So we don't miss it."

"Wonderful. Say it with more confidence and I might believe you." Pell tapped the timetable. "The depot requires a travel warrant. Military personnel travel free on warrants; civilians pay and are searched. We want the first category, obviously."

"Obviously."

"But a warrant has a regimental number, a company stamp, and a countersignature. And here is my professional assessment of our position regarding those three items:" Pell paused, with the timing of a man who had delivered this joke before, in better rooms, "we possess none of them."

"We could buy them."

"From whom? The quartermaster sells boots, blankets, and grudges. Warrants are above his market."

"Then we steal one."

Pell looked at him over the candlelight with an expression Ivo couldn't quite parse — surprise, maybe, or the particular delight of a teacher whose worst student has just answered correctly. "Nurse Okafor. You continue to astonish me."

"Don't be impressed yet. I haven't thought about how."

"That's usually the order of operations," Pell said. "First the sin, then the logistics. Vane worked the same way."

"You keep doing that."

"Doing what?"

"Talking about him in the present tense."

Pell's pencil stopped moving. For a moment the only sound in the infirmary was Corporal Hesse, two beds over, dreaming loudly about whatever it was corporals dreamed about — probably forms, filed correctly, forever. When Pell spoke again his voice had lost its performance quality entirely, gone flat and quiet as the river in January.

"If I told you the truth about who I am," he said, "you would do one of two things. Report me, and lose the only chance those names have of ever being read. Or believe me, and spend the rest of your life wondering which parts you'd chosen to believe." He turned the pencil over in his fingers. "I'm offering you a third option. Don't ask. Help anyway. Judge me after the century turns, when it won't matter anymore."

Ivo considered this. He considered the letters with their looping, unmistakable hand, folded now at the bottom of Pell's satchel like coiled snakes sleeping off a meal. He considered the ledger beneath the loose floorboard under his own cot, forty-one names deep and waiting for a forty-second reason to matter.

"Judge you after," he agreed. "But I reserve the right to judge you *harshly*."

"Oh, I'd expect nothing less. Now — the customs house."

* * *

The coastal customs house was, according to Pell, the worst of the three, and he described it with the weary intimacy of a man describing a former mother-in-law.

"It sits on the harbor road where the mail coach stops before boarding the packet boat. Every parcel, every letter, every bag opened on the table in front of Officer Grange, who is either extremely corrupt or extremely honest, and I genuinely cannot determine which, because both produce the same expression."

"Which expression?"

"The expression of a man who has already decided how this ends and is simply waiting for you to catch up."

Ivo pulled the map closer. The coastal road ran thin and red across the oilcloth, ending in a small ink smudge where Pell's elbow had sweated against it. "So we cannot carry the ledger through the customs house. Not whole. Not hidden in a coat lining, not sewn into a collar, not anywhere on a body, because bodies get searched last and most thoroughly."

"Correct."

"So it goes separately."

Pell nodded slowly. "The mail coach carries parcels to the packet boat without further inspection once they're stamped at the house. A parcel already stamped is sacred. Even Grange wouldn't reopen one — it would mean admitting his own stamp could be forged."

"So we need one stamp."

"One stamp," Pell agreed. "And here is the beautiful, horrible symmetry of it: everything we need exists somewhere in this garrison. A manifest for the bridge. A warrant for the junction. A stamp for the coast. The empire is a machine for producing paper, Nurse. We are simply going to redirect some of its output."

"For a while I thought you were a clerk," Ivo said. "Now I think you're a poet with a filing system."

"My mother hoped for worse."

Ivo laughed before he could stop himself — a real laugh, brief and startling in the dark ward, quickly smothered under a cough when Corporal Hesse muttered in his sleep. But it left something behind, that laugh. A loosening. He noticed that Pell noticed it too, and that neither of them mentioned it, and that this was perhaps the beginning of trust between them: not the grand confessions, but the shared embarrassment of having briefly been human together.

* * *

They planned until the candle burned down into the enamel basin and drowned itself. By the end, the scheme sat on the oilcloth in Pell's tidy annotations: the bridge crossed by medical run, papers copied in Ivo's honest hand; the junction boarded at eleven sharp on a warrant acquired by means still marked, in Pell's writing, with a single optimistic question mark; the coast reached with the ledger divided into parcels, each one small enough to be innocent, each one addressed to the same London address Pell recited from memory like a prayer he'd been given by someone braver than himself.

*The Argus,* Ivo repeated to himself, memorizing it. *Care of the Argus.*

"And if any single piece fails?" he asked. "If Renwick's mood turns sour, if we miss the eleven o'clock, if Grange opens one envelope?"

"Then we lose a piece," Pell said. "Not the whole. That's the design. Forty names in forty envelopes means the empire has to catch forty separate miracles." He began rolling the map, and then stopped, and looked at Ivo directly for the first time all night. "You understand what this costs. Not the risk to us. I mean—" he gestured vaguely at the sleeping ward, at the bandages, at the whole dim economy of care Ivo moved through daily, "—this. Once the pages leave, they're out of your hands forever. They'll be read by strangers. Printed. Argued over by men who never smelled the river. Is that what you want for them?"

Ivo thought about Private Adeyemi, whose name existed nowhere now except in his ledger and in his memory. About the colonel's speech, the empty chairs, the medals pinned to air. About the burn order he hadn't heard yet and would hear soon enough, though he didn't know that tonight — tonight he knew only the shape of it, gathering somewhere above them like weather.

"They were never mine," he said. "I was only holding them. Holding isn't keeping."

Pell studied him for a long moment. "Vane wrote something once," he said quietly. "In the last letter. He said there was a nurse at Sante who kept accounts the empire couldn't balance. He said if anyone could get the truth past three checkpoints, it would be a man who'd already spent years getting it past himself."

The candle was dead. The dark felt suddenly full of ears.

"Get some sleep, Mr. Pell," Ivo said, standing, folding the map into his sleeve. "We start copying tomorrow. And you should know—" he paused at the edge of the basin-light, "—Sister Amara counts the candles. She counts everything. She'll know we've been sitting together half the night, and she will ask, and she is not a woman you lie to successfully."

"What would you suggest?"

"Tell her the truth," Ivo said. "It's faster."

He was halfway to his cot, the map a small hard rectangle against his ribs, when he saw the figure standing at the far end of the ward — motionless in the doorway where the lamplight from the corridor fell, arms folded, watching them with the patient, unblinking attention of a woman who counted everything.

Sister Amara did not say a word.

She simply looked at the dead candle, and then at Ivo, and then at the stranger in the visitor's chair — and then she held up one finger, meaning *one moment*, and turned, and walked away down the corridor with footsteps as soft and as final as a closing door.
