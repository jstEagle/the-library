## 7. Sister Amara Notices

There were three certainties in Amara Okonkwo's life, and she had arranged them like stones in a garden: that God was patient, that men were foolish, and that Ivo Okafor could not lie to save his soul — which was precisely why he was going to need saving.

She had known him for two years, since the day he arrived at the River Sante garrison with his careful hands and his careful face and a trunk of medical texts he had already half-memorized. He apologized to patients before he hurt them. He thanked the orderlies for carrying bedpans as though they had carried him across a flooded river. And when the dying men called out for mothers who were four thousand miles away, Ivo would sit with them and answer in their mothers' voices — badly, in a whisper, his accent hopeless — until they went quiet.

A man like that, she had decided long ago, was constitutionally incapable of deception. Which made it all the more alarming that he had gotten so good at it.

She had been noticing things for weeks. The way he lingered over the morning dispatches that came through the infirmary for the surgeons' signatures. The way he'd developed a sudden devotion to copying out supply requisitions — twice, neatly, "for the file, Sister, in case of audit." The way he wrote at night by the stub of candle he thought nobody saw, hunched over something small enough to vanish into his sleeve.

And there was the matter of Mr. Pell.

* * *

The stranger had been in the fever ward nine days now, occupying the cot in the corner that Amara privately called *the parish*, because everyone placed there either recovered miraculously or died with unusual dignity. Pell was recovering, which under the circumstances she found suspicious. Fever did not usually spare men who looked at wards the way Pell did — counting exits, noting faces, cataloguing the room like a merchant pricing stock.

He claimed to be a clerk. She believed him about as far as she could throw a piano.

What interested her more was Ivo's behavior around him. Her gentle, guileless Ivo had taken to visiting the fever ward on pretenses so flimsy they practically curtsied. Checking a dressing that didn't need checking. Taking a pulse Pell hadn't asked for. Once she'd caught them simply sitting together, not speaking, both wearing the expression of two men sharing an umbrella in a storm neither wished to name.

Amara had buried a husband and raised three younger brothers, and she had learned that the fastest route to truth is not confrontation. It is patience, positioning, and the element of surprise.

So on a grey Thursday afternoon, when the ward was quiet and the snow was coming down like ash, she walked into the records alcove behind Ivo, stood close enough that her shadow fell across his page, and said pleasantly:

"Write my name next. You've got a lovely hand."

Ivo froze the way a rabbit freezes — completely, instantly, and with total conviction that this rendered him invisible.

"Sister," he managed. "I was only—"

"Copying the December movement orders." She reached past his shoulder and turned the page toward herself, reading her own name among the transfers. *Nurse Okonkwo, A., attached infirmary, Sante, per Col. Brase.* "You misspelled nothing. Disappointing. I hoped you'd given me a promotion."

"I can explain."

"You've been explaining nothing for six weeks. It's been very educational watching you try." She pulled the second stool from beneath the desk, sat down, and folded her hands. "Sit down, Ivo."

"I have patients—"

"Who are asleep. Sit."

To his credit — or his ruin, depending on how one counted — he sat. He set the little ledger on the desk between them, squared it, and then covered it with both hands, which struck her as rather like locking the barn after the horse had not only left but written home describing the countryside.

"Whatever you think—" he began.

"I think," Amara said, "that Sergeant Doyle thinks you are stealing stationery. And Doyle is wrong, because Doyle believes you're a coward, and cowards don't steal stationery; they steal blankets. So I have spent some time asking myself what a man like you does with military paper at midnight." She let the silence do its work. It was a good silence. She'd had years of practice on brothers. "And then I remembered Kessari Crossing."

His face did something she had never seen it do: it went entirely still, and behind the stillness something moved, quick and frightened, like a fish under ice.

"What do you know about Kessari?" he said quietly.

"My brother Joseph was there." She said it evenly, because she had practiced saying it evenly for two years, and even now the practice only mostly held. "Second Sante Rifles. He was twenty-three. He wrote me a letter the week before — I have it still. He said the captain was driving them too hard upriver, and he said — this part I know by heart, Ivo, so don't look away — *'if anything happens, it will be the water or worse than the water.'*" She smoothed her apron. Two folds forward, one back. A habit, a rosary of cloth. "The official record says forty-one men drowned in a crossing accident. Forty-one men cannot drown in an accident, Ivo. I grew up on a river. Rivers take one, sometimes two, when they are feeling greedy. They do not take forty-one."

"No," Ivo said. "They don't."

The lamp between them guttered and steadied. Somewhere down the ward a man coughed, turned over, slept on.

"I keep names," Ivo said at last. It came out small, almost apologetic, and then something hardened in him and it came again, larger. "The ones they strike from the rolls. The ones Brase signs away as *unrecovered* or *deserted* or *died of fever* — fever, when I personally held their arms while they bled. I can't stop any of it. But I write them down. Who they were. Where they fell. What actually killed them, where I know it." He turned the ledger over and pushed it toward her, an offering. "Joseph is in here. Page eleven. I put him in myself, from the muster lists. *Okonkwo, J., private, second rifles.*"

Amara had prepared, on the walk over, for several outcomes. She had prepared to be firm. She had prepared, if it came to it, to threaten him with Doyle. She had not prepared for page eleven.

She read it twice. Then once more, though the words did not change.

"He liked to swim," she said, and her voice betrayed her only slightly, on *swim*. "When we were children. Our mother beat him for it every Sunday after church. He would say God made the water first, so the water was older than the rules." She closed the ledger with both hands, gently, the way one closes a hymnal. "And they put him in one."

"Yes."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Outside, the snow ticked against the shutters. Down the corridor, someone laughed at something in the mess — distant, oblivious, human. The world went on being ordinary, which Amara had always found to be grief's cruelest feature.

"Well," she said finally, wiping her eyes briskly with the heel of her hand, as though drying dishes. "Then you'll need help, won't you. Because you are a terrible conspirator, Ivo Okafor. Truly dreadful. You visit that man in the corner as though you were courting him. Half the ward thinks you've fallen in love; Ferro has a wager running."

"Ferro has a *what*—"

"Two shillings says you propose by Christmas. I advised him against it, on the grounds that you'd never work up the nerve." She allowed herself the smile, because grief and humor had lived in the same house inside her for two years and had long ago agreed to share the kitchen. "Now. Mr. Pell. Tell me about Mr. Pell. And do not tell me he is a clerk, because clerks do not have soldiers' posture, and clerks do not flinch when the gate gun fires, and clerks most certainly do not carry letters in the hand of a dead man. Because I have seen those letters too, Ivo, when he was delirious and I went through his satchel looking for identification, which is my right and my job, whatever anyone says."

Ivo stared at her. "You went through his—"

"I went through everyone's satchels during the typhoid season and I will go through yours if you give me cause. Don't change the subject. A. Vane."

"A. Vane," Ivo confirmed, and told her.

He told it low and fast, glancing at the door between sentences — a habit she now recognized as new, and hated. The captain dead two years. The letters in his unmistakable looping hand. The claim of the clerk Pell, sent to carry proof of the Crossing to a journalist in London before New Year's Day, when the garrison's paper shame would be fed wholesale into the fires along with the century. And underneath it all, the question Ivo clearly carried like a stone in his boot: whether the clerk was the captain, wearing another man's name like a borrowed coat.

"And you believe him?" Amara asked.

"I believe the letters are genuine."

"That isn't what I asked."

Ivo looked at his hands. "No," he admitted. "I don't suppose I believe anyone. Not fully. Not anymore." He said it without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "But he knows things only Vane or Vane's own clerk could know. And either way — either way — the proof exists, and it's sitting in our fever ward, and on the first of January it becomes ashes. So does my ledger, probably. Everything they signed, everything we buried without names — gone, and the century turns, and it's all clean. As if it never happened."

Amara sat with that. Through the alcove's open arch she could see Pell's cot, the blanket rising and falling with his breathing, deceptively peaceful. A sleeping snake looks peaceful too, her mother used to say. Mind the grass.

"There's something else," she said, and now she dropped her voice lower than grief, lower than prayer. "You should hear it from me and not from the walls. Doyle has been asking questions. About Pell — how he came to the ford with no regimental papers, why no one at the bridge checkpoint logged him. Yesterday he pulled Ferro aside in the linen store and talked to him for ten minutes. Ferro told me himself; the boy was shaking. Doyle is not clever, but he is a dog, Ivo, and dogs don't need to be clever. They only need to keep their nose down."

"Doyle," Ivo repeated. "Doyle audits the stores. He counts bandages."

"Doyle counts everything," Amara said. "That's the whole trouble with that sort of man. He counts because he cannot bear that the world doesn't add up, and he'll make it add up on somebody, anyone, and better yet on a foreigner with no papers lying in our ward with a dead man's letters." She rose, straightened her apron — two folds forward, one back — and picked up the ledger, pressing it back into Ivo's hands and closing his fingers over it with her own. Her hands were stronger than they looked. Midwives' hands. "From tonight, I'm in. Whatever you're planning — and from your face, it's something mad — you'll plan it with me. I've smuggled contraband cocoa past three quartermasters and delivered two babies in a artillery tent. You need someone who can lie."

"You can?"

"I'm a head nurse in a colonial garrison, Ivo. I lie professionally, every day, to colonels. 'The men are comfortable, sir.' 'The fever is abating, sir.' 'Your surgeon general sends his regards.'" She sniffed. "It's exhausting work, but I'm considered gifted."

That startled a laugh out of him — the first real one she'd heard from Ivo in weeks, and she noted with satisfaction that it changed his whole face, opened it like a window. Grief makes people forget they have faces.

They bent together over the desk, heads close, and began, quietly, to talk. The route south. The checkpoints. Papers, forged or found. She contributed what she knew — the depot schedule at Marrow Junction, the customs house at the coast, the fact that the mail sack left every Tuesday whether inspected or not, because the postmaster played cards with the inspector and inspection cut into the game. Ivo listened the way he listened to patients: completely, writing nothing down, remembering everything.

It was nearly dark when she stood to go, and nearly dark when she said the last thing, the thing she had carried to the alcove and had not known she would say aloud until it was already leaving her:

"When they read the record someday — and they will, Ivo, I've decided they will, I've decided it the way I decide things, absolutely and in advance — when they read it, there should be a woman's name somewhere near it. Joseph would never forgive me otherwise. He was always saying I'd end up in the newspapers." She touched the ledger's cover once, lightly, a benediction. "He wasn't often right. He was right about that."

Ivo looked up at her, and for once had nothing careful to say, and said none of it, which was correct.

She was halfway down the corridor, already composing the evening's fiction — *Sister, you were in the records alcove forty minutes.* "Inventory, Sergeant. Dreadful state of the splint stock.* — when she saw him.

Doyle stood at the far end of the fever ward, lantern in hand, doing nothing at all. That was the trouble with Doyle; he was never doing anything at all, and it always meant something. His eyes went from her face to the alcove arch to the corner cot where Mr. Pell lay sleeping, and stayed there a long, thoughtful moment.

Then he smiled at her, tipped his cap, and walked on, his boots making no sound at all on the stone.

Amara stood very still in the gathering dark, and thought: *two days. We have perhaps two days.*

She went to wake Ivo.
