## 8. Doyle's Questions

The morning Sergeant Doyle came to audit the infirmary supplies, Ivo was elbow-deep in a bowl of barley gruel and lying about its virtues.

"It builds blood," Ivo told Private Hessler, who had lost two fingers to frostbite and most of his faith in army cooking besides. "That's what the manual says."

"Does the manual say anything about it tasting like wet chalk?"

"The manual is very thorough on the subject of chalk." Ivo pressed the spoon back into Hessler's good hand. "Eat, or I'll read you the chapter aloud."

Hessler ate. It was an old trick of the ward — give a man something to complain about and he'd forget, briefly, to despair. Ivo had learned it from Sister Amara, who could make a whole roomful of dying men grumble their way back toward life with nothing but bad soup and worse jokes. He missed having her in the ward that morning; she was at the laundry, which meant he was alone when Doyle's boots came down the plank walk with the particular heavy evenness of a man who wanted to be heard arriving.

Ivo did not look up quickly. That was the first rule. A man who looks up quickly has something on the table he shouldn't.

"Sergeant." He kept stirring. "We've nothing worth auditing unless you count the gruel, and I'd advise against it."

"Colonel Brase wants a full accounting of medical stores before the new year." Doyle stood in the doorway, filling most of it, snow melting off his shoulders onto the boards. He had the kind of face that gave away nothing except patience, and he had a great deal of patience. "Every bandage. Every vial. Every ledger."

The word went through Ivo like a splinter finding its depth. *Every ledger.* But Doyle said it flatly, administratively, the way he'd say every blanket, every boot, and Ivo reminded himself that the supply ledger chained to the dispensary desk was exactly where it ought to be, honest as a hymnbook, recording three dozen lost thermometers and one regrettable incident with the ether.

"Then you've come to the right grave," Ivo said. "Mind the puddles."

Doyle stepped inside and began, methodically, to destroy Ivo's morning.

He counted. That was the terrible thing about him — other auditors would have walked the aisles, glanced at shelves, initialed a form and gone to warm their hands. Doyle counted. He counted the morphine vials twice, then matched them against the dispensary book, then looked up at Ivo over the page with those grey eyes and said, "You're two short."

"We're not. We're one short and one broken. Private Callum dropped one Tuesday; I wrote it down." Ivo turned the book toward him without being asked, without hurrying, and watched Doyle run his thumb down Ivo's neat column of figures. His handwriting there was upright, unremarkable, the handwriting of a man with nothing hidden. Somewhere under the floor of the world, in a place no audit would ever reach, lay another ledger written in the same hand, holding forty-one names the army had decided never existed.

"Hm," said Doyle, which seemed to be his longest opinion.

Ivo resumed his rounds while the sergeant worked, because standing still would look like waiting, and waiting would look like worry. He changed Hessler's dressing. He propped young Whitcomb higher on his pillows so the fluid in his lungs might find somewhere else to go. He spoke to each man by name, and heard the names come back to him like small answers — *Nurse Okafor, Nurse Okafor* — and thought, not for the first time, how strange it was that a ward should be full of men insisting on their names while the army worked so hard to subtract them.

The dead occupied the little annex off the east wall: two of them, waiting for the burial detail that the frozen ground had delayed for six days now. Private Enns, twenty-two, typhoid. And Gunner Mallow, whose heart had simply quit in the night the way old clocks do, quietly and without appointment. They lay under sheets in the cold annex, and Ivo passed their doorway several times that morning and felt them there the way you feel a draft.

It was on the fourth pass that the idea came to him, arriving fully formed and horrible, the way the true solutions always do.

Doyle was working down the aisle toward the annex. There was nowhere else for the search to go. The ledger was — Ivo ran the calculation in a flash of cold arithmetic — eleven feet away, rolled into the false bottom of his own footlocker, wrapped in an oilcloth that would not fool a man who counted everything twice. Doyle had already been past the footlocker once. He would come back to it. Men like Doyle always circled back.

Eleven feet. Doyle's back was turned. Six days of delay on the burial detail.

Ivo had never stolen so much as a lump of sugar in his life. He had lectured orderlies about pilfered brandy. And here his hands were moving before the rest of him had agreed to anything, lifting Mallow's sheet at the corner, sliding the oilcloth roll into the hollow between the gunner's arm and his ribs, drawing the shroud smooth again, tucking it with the same careful hospital corners he'd learned as a probationer. Mallow weighed nothing anymore. He looked, under the sheet, like a man folded politely away, and Ivo heard himself say inwardly, *forgive me, friend, I'll keep you one more hour,* and understood that he had just asked a dead man to lie for him, and that Mallow — steady, unhurried Mallow, who had once spent an entire convalescence teaching card tricks to half the ward — would probably have found it funny.

"Who's in the annex?" Doyle's voice, directly behind him.

Ivo turned around at a speed he chose with great care. "Two gentlemen who are in no condition to answer questions. Enns and Mallow. You may audit them if you like; they're our best-behaved patients."

Doyle looked at him a moment too long.

Then he went into the annex, and Ivo followed him, and the next ninety seconds were among the longest of Ivo's life.

Doyle lifted the first sheet, noted the name from the tag, wrote it down. Of course he wrote it down — a supply audit included mortal stores, the army's grim inventory, and Ivo saw the pen move and thought absurdly, *he's counting you, Mallow, you're about to become a number twice.* Doyle moved to the second cot. He took the corner of the sheet.

Somewhere out in the ward, Hessler began complaining about the gruel again, loudly, to no one, and a couple of men laughed, and the sound came through the doorway like weather from another country.

Doyle lifted the sheet to the chest. Ivo watched his own hands hang at his sides, relaxed, useless, and marveled distantly that they were not shaking yet. Under the sheet, inches from Sergeant Doyle's fingers, lay a roll of oilcloth containing the names of forty-one men the army had erased — including, though Doyle did not know it, a name that would have interested him more than all the others combined. Ivo had written it in October, copied from a casualty telegram that passed through the infirmary and was then recalled and burned: *Lance-Corporal T. Doyle, Kessari Crossing, struck from rolls, administrative correction.* He had never learned if this Doyle and that Doyle shared a hearth or merely a surname. He had wondered. He had never dared ask.

Doyle looked at the dead man's face for a moment. Something crossed his expression — not softness exactly, but the shadow where softness had once lived and been posted elsewhere.

"Gunner Mallow," he read, and wrote it, and let the sheet fall.

"Yes," said Ivo, when the silence had stretched long enough to need filling. "Heart. He didn't suffer, if that's the next question. He was asleep."

"I didn't ask."

"You were going to. Everyone asks eventually. It's how we reassure ourselves death has manners."

Doyle almost smiled. It was a small movement, quickly repossessed, like a coin slipped back into a pocket. He finished his column of figures, blotted the page, and straightened. "Stores account for. Two thermometers unaccounted."

"Three."

"Three," Doyle agreed, and for just a moment there was something in his voice that might, in a different man, on a different day, have been humor. Then he looked around the infirmary once more — the shelves, the stove, the footlockers, the ward windows furred with frost — with the thorough gaze of a man closing a ledger of his own, and Ivo felt the near-miss land in him all at once, delayed like gunfire across a valley.

"Clean ward, Nurse Okafor," Doyle said. "Colonel Brase will be pleased."

"He has a great deal to be pleased about these days," Ivo said, before his mouth could be stopped. "Medals coming, I hear. And a new year."

Doyle paused at the door, snow-light behind him. "Everyone's pleased about the new year," he said. "Fresh page and all that." And he went out down the plank walk, boots marking time, until the snow took the sound.

* * *

Ivo made it through two more dressing changes before his hands started to shake.

They shook quietly, professionally, the way a man's hands do when he has ordered them to wait, and he finished Whitcomb's bandage with small precise tremors and then walked, not fast, into the annex and shut the door and stood with his back against it in the cold blue light.

Mallow lay where he had left him, patient as stone. Ivo drew back the sheet and took out the oilcloth roll and found it exactly as he'd placed it, dry and safe, warmed faintly by the last heat leaving the gunner's body, and that detail undid him more than anything Doyle had done. He sat down abruptly on Enns's empty cot, clutching the roll like a hot coal he couldn't drop, and shook in earnest for the better part of a minute.

It was not fear of Doyle, he realized. Or not only. It was the arithmetic of what he had just become. An hour ago he had been a nurse who kept an illegal notebook — a romantic offense, a private rebellion, something a court-martial would barely bother to spell correctly. Now he was a man who had used a corpse as a hiding place, who had lied by stillness and by timing, who had felt relief wash through him when a sergeant's hand hovered over the wrong body. The ledger was supposed to be the clean thing. The one clean thing. And survival had just taught him its going rate, and the rate was paid in exactly the currency the ledger was meant to protect.

"You'd have laughed," he told Mallow softly. "I hope you know that. You'd have laughed yourself sideways."

The dead man kept his counsel, as dead men do, but Ivo chose to take it as agreement.

He rewrapped the roll and considered his options with a steadier eye. The footlocker was finished — Doyle had marked it, Ivo was sure of it, had filed it away behind those grey eyes for a second visit. The mattress was the second place everyone looked. The chapel, the laundry, the woodpile: all of them places, which was the problem. Places could be searched. What he needed now was not a hiding place but a principle, and the principle the morning had taught him was brutally simple: *the safest hands are the ones already carrying something worse.* Nobody audits the dead. Nobody searches a shroud twice.

So the ledger would travel with the ward's mortality, from shroud to shroud, whenever danger came near — borne by the one company of soldiers no officer ever wanted to inspect. There was a bleak fitness to it that Ivo suspected Amara would appreciate and God, if He was paying attention, might forgive. The forgotten names riding with the forgotten men. He would get them both to the ground together someday, properly, with words said over them.

He washed his hands, because ritual is a kind of courage, and went back out to the ward, and was nearly himself again — enough so that when Hessler demanded to know whether the manual recommended the gruel as food or as punishment, Ivo answered that the manual classified it under *fortification*, and Hessler said "of walls, maybe," and the ward chuckled, and the ordinary mercy of small jokes closed over the morning like water over a dropped stone.

He was restocking the bandage shelf in the late afternoon when Amara came in with the linen basket and a look on her face that put him instantly on guard. Amara's faces were legible as telegrams; this one read *urgent, not here.*

"Ferro took a fall on the ice steps," she said, loudly, setting down the basket. "Banged his head. He's asking for you."

Ferro was fine; Ivo established this within thirty seconds of reaching the boy, who was sitting on the dispensary cot with an egg-sized bruise and a theatrical expression. But Amara stayed close, folding bandages with unnecessary attention, and murmured, low, under the rhythm of her work:

"Doyle was at the laundry after your infirmary. Asking the washerwomen what gets sent out and what stays in. Then he walked the perimeter. Twice." Her hands never stopped. "He wasn't auditing stores, Ivo. Stores were the pretext. He was mapping the buildings."

"He counted the morphine twice."

"He counts everything twice. That's the trouble with Doyle." She looked up then, and her eyes were steady but not calm. "Whatever you're carrying, it needs to be somewhere he can't map. Tonight, Ivo. Before dark."

"I've found a place," Ivo said. "It's not comfortable. But it's very difficult to search."

Amara studied him a moment, and whatever she read in his face made her press her lips together — disapproval and understanding arriving simultaneously, as they tended to with her. "Just don't," she said quietly, "let it be a person."

And Ivo, arranging bandages that did not need arranging, did not quite meet her eye — because the plan forming in him, quick and terrible, involved exactly one person: himself, summoned or not, walking into the lion's dining room.

Because it was at that moment that an orderly appeared in the doorway, breathless from the climb, with a note in Colonel Brase's own hand:

*Nurse Okafor is requested at the officers' table tomorrow evening, New Year's Eve, in recognition of his faithful service to the garrison. Dress uniform. The Colonel wishes to toast the century — and the men who served it.*

Amara read it over his shoulder. For a long moment neither of them said anything at all.

"Well," Ivo said at last, folding the note with steady hands. "Apparently the century and I are both coming to an end, and only one of us intends to see 1901."

He touched the place under his jacket where the ledger's absence lay, twelve feet away in a cold annex, keeping company with the dead — and understood, with a calm that frightened him more than any shaking had, that tomorrow night he would sit across the cloth from the man burning the past, smile, pour his wine, and memorize every word.
