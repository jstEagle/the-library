## 9. The Colonel's Dinner

The summons arrived on a Tuesday, which struck me as an odd day for ceremony. Tuesdays at the Sante garrison were for lancing boils and arguing with the quartermaster about bandage allotments; one did not ordinarily dress for them.

"Colonel Brase requests your presence at table tonight," said the runner, a boy of perhaps sixteen with a voice still deciding between boyhood and manhood. "As medical attendant."

"Medical attendant," I repeated. "Is someone ill?"

"Not that I'm told." The boy shrugged with the magnificent indifference of youth to all matters not immediately involving supper. "He says you're to come in your good coat."

I owned exactly one good coat, and it had been good in 1894, when I'd bought it secondhand from a tailor who assured me it had belonged to a diplomat. The diplomat, presumably, had been taller through the shoulders and shorter through the arms, but it was black and it buttoned, and on the rare occasions I wore it people assumed I was respectable.

Sister Amara found me holding it up against myself in the dispensary's little mirror — a glass so flawed that it flattered everyone, fogging us all into soft watercolors of ourselves.

"You've been summoned," she said. It was not a question; the whole infirmary knew by now. News traveled through our ward faster than fever.

"I have been *requested*," I corrected. "There is a distinction. A summons implies I might refuse."

"And can you?"

"No."

"Then it is a summons wearing better manners." She took the coat from me, held it at arm's length, and made a small sound in her throat that meant she was suppressing commentary. She was, as ever, mostly unsuccessful. "You'll want the collar turned down. And Ivo—" she paused, and something in her face shifted from amusement to that watchfulness she carried like a stone in her pocket these days, "—Brase doesn't invite nurses to dinner out of loneliness. Listen more than you pour."

"That is generally my practice at dinners."

"Listen even when they think you're only pouring." She smoothed the collar flat with two efficient strokes, the way she'd smooth a sheet over a body. "Especially then."

* * *

The officers' mess occupied what had once been a trading company's counting house, back before the river war gave everyone new occupations. It had high windows meant for watching cargo and now curtained for watching nothing, and a long mahogany table polished to the color of strong tea. Candles burned in jars along its length because the garrison's supply of proper candelabra had gone, like so many things, into general administration.

I arrived early, as servants should, and was set to pouring wine by a mess sergeant with a cold and a grievance, both of which he shared with me liberally.

"Twelve officers tonight," he said, thumping down another bottle. "And him." He tilted his head meaningfully toward the far end of the table, where Colonel Brase stood receiving Major Cleese's compliments the way a wall receives weather.

Him. I had seen Brase perhaps six times since arriving at the Sante, always at a distance or across a desk, delivering speeches or signing things. Up close he was smaller than his reputation, a compact man going silver at the temples with the careful grooming of a portrait subject. He had the kind of face that looked honest until you realized it simply looked *nothing*, and honesty had been your own addition.

The officers came in by degrees, shaking off the evening cold, stamping their boots, unwinding scarves. I knew most of their faces from the ward — not the wounded ones, but the visitors, the men who came through between campaigns smelling of horse and tobacco, asking after their friends with the particular dread of men who hoped the answer would be quick. Captain Harrow, red-faced and loud. Lieutenant Mowbray, young and earnest in a way that hadn't yet been beaten out of him. Major Cleese, whose mustache entered rooms several seconds before he did.

And Sergeant Doyle, posted by the door because sergeants do not sit at the colonel's table, and because Doyle seemed to consider every room his post regardless of where anyone else wanted him. His eyes passed over me once — a slow, inventory-taking pass — and moved on. I poured wine and thought about nothing, which is the hardest thinking there is.

The dinner began well enough. There was soup that had achieved a flavor through sheer persistence, and a roast of some animal I chose not to identify, and the talk ran along ordinary grooves: the state of the road east, the state of the horses, the state of Major Cleese's digestion, which appeared to be poor and was discussed with a frankness that would have startled the animal we were eating.

Then Captain Harrow raised his glass.

"Gentlemen — to the century."

"To the century," they echoed, and drank, and Harrow pressed on with the momentum of a man who had rehearsed this at his toilette. "Dead at midnight, God rest it. And what a century it's been. Steam, telegraph, vaccination, the abolition of four kinds of cruelty and the invention of six new ones—"

"Harrow," said Cleese warningly, glancing at me.

"Oh, the nurse has heard worse, haven't you, Okafor?" Harrow beamed at me with the genial cruelty of a man who had never once needed stitches. "You've heard worse in your ward, eh?"

I said, "I hear everything, sir. It's the job."

That got a laugh — even Mowbray laughed, and quickly hid it in his napkin. Harrow pointed his glass at me approvingly, as one points at a clever dog.

"There it is! That's why I like this one. Well then, nurse, you'll appreciate the toast properly. To the old century — may it take its paperwork with it!"

"*That,*" said Brase, and the table quieted the way water quiets when a large fish moves beneath it, "is actually a matter under discussion tonight. Sit down, Harrow, and stop performing."

Harrow sat. Brase let the silence do a moment of his work for him — he was skilled at silence, I'll grant him that — and then unfolded his hands on the tablecloth like a man preparing to discuss accounts.

"As most of you know, General Order Forty-One takes effect at midnight on the thirty-first. All garrison records of the eastern campaign — muster rolls, dispatch logs, court documents, correspondence — are to be inventoried and destroyed by the first of January. The century turns, the paper goes with it. New era, clean ledger." He permitted himself the smallest smile at his own phrase. *Clean ledger.* I felt the words land somewhere behind my ribs like a swallowed pin.

"Clinical records too?" asked Mowbray, who was young enough to ask real questions.

"The infirmary ledgers transfer to the new colonial authority," Brase said. "They're considered ongoing. Everything else burns." He paused, and added, with the air of a concession: "Private correspondence included. Officers' letters home, requisitions marked personal, anything filed outside the official sequence. If it's paper and it predates November, it's fuel."

There was a general murmuring at that. I kept my eyes on the wine bottles and poured where glasses were empty, and I listened the way Amara had told me to listen, which is to say with everything except my face.

"But sir," Harrow again, emboldened by wine, "my letters from my wife—"

"Will burn, Harrow, along with everyone else's. You may keep photographs. Photographs don't say anything."

"They say plenty, in my experience," muttered Cleese, and there was laughter, and Brase allowed it, and the dinner rolled on into dessert — a custard of heroic ambition and disappointing execution — and the talk slid away from orders toward shooting, and dogs, and whether the new year ought properly to begin in 1900 or 1901, a question they settled twice and reopened three times.

I stood at my station with the claret and thought: *private correspondence.*

I thought about Pell's satchel. About letters signed in that looping, unmistakable hand, letters that were, as of tonight's revelation, scheduled for destruction — not because anyone knew where they were, but because the order swept so wide that existence itself became evidence. Vane's confession did not need to be found to be erased. It merely needed to be paper, and old, and somewhere within reach of a match.

And I thought about my ledger, tucked in its place among the ward's pharmaceutical records, disguised as tallies of quinine doses. My ledger was not garrison property. It was not correspondence either. It existed in no category the order recognized — which meant, I realized with a coldness that had nothing to do with the draft from the high windows, that its safety depended entirely on nobody ever looking at it closely enough to wonder.

Brase had said *clean ledger* and smiled at his own wit, and he didn't know — couldn't know — that the true clean ledger of this campaign was currently folded inside my good coat, because I had not dared leave it in the infirmary tonight, and would not dare again.

"Nurse." Brase's voice, suddenly near. He had crossed to the sideboard for port and stood beside me, turning a glass by its stem. "My compliments on the service. You pour without spilling, which is more than I can say for some of my officers' marksmanship."

"Thank you, Colonel."

"How long have you been at the Sante? Eighteen months? Two years?"

"A little over two years, sir."

"Long time, in this business." He watched me rather than the glass he was turning. His eyes were pale gray and perfectly level, the eyes of a man who had learned that looking at people directly made them confess things. "You must have seen a great deal. Kept busy."

"Infirmaries are never idle, sir."

"No. No, I imagine they aren't." He lifted the glass, sniffed the port, apparently found it acceptable. "When the records go, the ward's papers will need sorting before transfer. I'll want someone thorough for that. Someone who knows what belongs where." A pause, thin as paper itself. "You strike me as a thorough man, Okafor."

"I do my best with what's given me, Colonel."

"Mm." Something moved behind the gray eyes — not suspicion exactly; suspicion has heat to it, and this was colder. Inventory, maybe. A man adding a figure to a column. "Well. Enjoy the evening. Have some port. It's terrible, but it's free."

He returned to his chair. I poured port for those who wanted it and refilled water glasses and smiled the small, serviceable smile of a man who is only furniture, and inside that furniture a single thought sat burning like a lamp:

*Someone knows what belongs where. He wants to know if I know.*

* * *

The dinner broke up near eleven. Officers departed in twos and threes into the frozen dark, voices carrying strangely over the snow — Harrow singing something obscene about a gunner's wife, Cleese telling him to hush, the crunch and fade of boots. Doyle left last, after a word with Brase at the door, and I noted that the word involved a glance in my direction, brief as a struck match.

I walked back to the infirmary through streets blue with moonlight, breath smoking, ears aching, and my mind moved in orderly, terrible steps the whole way.

All records burn. Private correspondence burns. On the first of January, every scrap of proof that Kessari Crossing happened as it happened becomes ash — the orders, the reports, the letters, the muster rolls with their neat erasures, two years of careful forgetting sealed with fire and called renewal. Brase will stand at midnight with his glass raised to a clean century, and the smoke will smell of nothing at all, because paper burns that way, innocently, like any other fuel.

Unless it was already gone. Unless, somewhere on the far side of three checkpoints, the truth sat safe in a mailbag addressed to London, beyond the reach of any general order.

Pell. The letters. Whatever game the man was playing — and I still did not know, could not know, whether Mr. Pell was a clerk or a ghost wearing a clerk's name — his errand and mine had just become the same errand, racing the same calendar. Twenty-three days.

Amara was awake when I came in, sitting by the stove with her feet on a stool and her mending abandoned in her lap. She looked up, read my face in one pass, and put a finger to her lips, nodding toward the ward where eleven men slept and breathed and coughed in the dark.

"Well?" she whispered, when we'd stepped into the dispensary. "How was the great man's table?"

"He's burning everything," I said. "On the first. Every record of the campaign. Dispatches, rolls, courts-martial." I pulled off the good coat and hung it, and heard myself add, flatly: "Private letters too. Even the things people wrote home."

Amara went very still. I watched her understand it — watched the arithmetic move behind her eyes the same way it had moved behind mine on the walk home. Somewhere in that bonfire were the last documents naming her brother. The last official acknowledgment that Daniel Amara had existed, had served, had died badly at a crossing whose name was scheduled for oblivion.

"The old paper shame," she said softly. "So that's what they call us."

"Us?"

"Our dead. Our missing. The pages that tell the truth are *shame* now, and shame gets burned so the new century can feel clean about itself." She picked up her mending, looked at it without seeing it, set it down again. "Ivo. Your book—"

"Is one book. Against a garrison's worth of matches."

"Books travel better than you'd think." Her jaw set in the way I had learned meant she had finished grieving for the moment and begun organizing. "If everything burns anyway, then nothing is lost by moving it. There's no safer place than inside the thing they're destroying — they never look there."

"It needs to reach the coast," I said. "Before the first. Past the bridge, past Marrow Junction, past the customs house. Three checkpoints, twenty-two days, and a man in my ward who carries a dead captain's letters and won't say what he really is."

"Yes," said Amara. "And Sergeant Doyle spent ten minutes today asking the night orderly how long 'Mr. Pell' intended to stay convalescing, and whether he had papers, and where he'd slept before the bridge." She met my eyes. "He's pulling threads, Ivo. Quietly. Which means whatever you and Pell intend to do—"

A sound stopped her. Not from the ward — from the corridor. Footsteps, unhurried, stopping just outside the dispensary door. Then a knock, polite as a bill collector's.

Amara blew out the lamp in a single motion, and in the sudden dark I heard her say, pleasantly, at normal volume, "Who is it?"

"Beg pardon, Sister." The night orderly, Tam. Young, reliable, terrified of Amara. "There's a gentleman below asking after Mr. Pell. Says he's a friend from the ford. Says Mr. Pell's expecting him."

I stood in the dark of my own dispensary, heart hammering against the ribs of the good coat hanging beside me, and thought of a pale-eyed colonel taking inventory, and a sergeant pulling threads, and a stranger asleep two wards away who might be the most dangerous man in the colony or the only honest one in it.

"Tell the gentleman," Amara said, with iron gentleness, "that visiting hours are at eight."

Tam's boots retreated. In the darkness, neither of us moved.

"Well," Amara murmured at last. "It seems Mr. Pell's friends are finding him faster than we expected."

And I thought: *or his hunters are.*
