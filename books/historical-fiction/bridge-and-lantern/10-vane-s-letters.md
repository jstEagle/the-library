## 10. Vane's Letters

There is an art to reading a dead man's handwriting, and I had learned it the hard way, four years of taking dictation from Captain Aldous Vane. His hand was beautiful in the way that certain snakes are beautiful — looping, deliberate, each capital letter coiled at the top like something about to strike. When he wrote *proceed*, the word looked like it was already advancing. When he wrote *disregard prior orders*, which he did more often than any officer I ever served under, the ink seemed almost apologetic.

So when Ivo Okafor laid the letters out on the infirmary's little supply table — after locking the door, after drawing the shutter, after positioning the lamp so its light fell only on the paper and not out through the window cracks — I knew them the way a man knows his own mother's voice in a crowd of strangers.

"Where did you get these?" Ivo asked.

I kept my hands flat on the table, because if I didn't give them something to do they would start shaking, and I had decided somewhere on the road that this man would never see me shake. "Vane kept carbons. He was vain about his correspondence. He said — and I quote him across four years and one continent — 'a well-written order outlives the fool who obeys it.' He kept copies of everything in a tin box under the floor of my quarters, and when he died, or vanished, or whatever we are calling it this winter, the box became mine."

"And you carried them here." Ivo looked at me the way a surgeon looks at a wound he did not make but must nevertheless treat. "Through the mountains. In December."

"I carried them here," I agreed.

"Why?"

"Because on January the first, every scrap of paper in this garrison burns. Every order, every roll, every requisition for ammunition that was spent at Kessari Crossing on a day the official record says nothing happened at all." I tapped the topmost letter. "These are the only copies left in the world, Mr. Okafor. After New Year's, they are the only memory anyone will be permitted to have."

He pulled a stool over and sat down across from me, and I noticed — because by then I noticed everything about Ivo Okafor — that he brought his ledger with him, holding it against his chest like a breviary.

"All right," he said. "Read them."

* * *

The first letter was dated the eleventh of October, two years gone. Vane's hand, crisp even by lamplight:

*To Col. E. Brase, commanding, River Sante garrison. Sir — The situation at the ford deteriorates. Kessari villagers have given shelter and provision to irregulars; whether willingly or under threat I cannot determine, and frankly no longer consider it material. I request authority to make an example. You will understand the term as I mean it.*

"Make an example," Ivo repeated. His voice was very level, the way water is level right before it goes over a ledge. "Of civilians."

"Of a village," I said. "The next letter answers it."

The second letter was Brase's reply, and I confess I had expected better of the Colonel's penmanship. It was cramped, sideways-slanting, the hand of a man writing quickly in a warm room about cold business:

*Authority granted. Conduct the operation with such discretion as the circumstances allow. No reports through regular channels; you will report to me directly and alone. The men engaged will be drawn from companies whose muster rolls are due for revision regardless.*

"'Due for revision regardless,'" Ivo read aloud, slowly, tasting each word like something spoiled. "He chose the men first. Men whose deaths could be arranged."

"Men who were expendable on paper before they were ever shot at," I said. "Yes. That was the arrangement. The soldiers who did the shooting were mostly men the army had already stopped counting properly — deserters reinstated, criminals commuted into service, fellows whose names had errors in them nobody wanted examined. If any of them died, there would be questions nobody wanted answered, and so no questions would be asked."

Ivo opened his ledger. He did not explain himself; he simply turned pages until he found what he was looking for, and turned the book around to face me.

Six names. Dated the seventeenth and eighteenth of October, two years gone. Struck from the rolls — I recognized now the particular double-strike mark the garrison clerks used, the one that meant *erased*, not *deceased*. Beside three of the names, in Ivo's small careful script: *ward, Oct. 4–9. Fever. Recovering.*

"Some of them came back," he said quietly. "Some of the men from Kessari Crossing. They came through my ward first. Wounds, fever, one fellow who hadn't slept in nine days and wept whenever the lamps went out. I nursed them. And then the rolls came down and their names were gone, and when I asked Sergeant Doyle where Private Enfield had been posted, Doyle looked at me as though I'd asked him where the rain goes." He closed the ledger. "I started writing them down that night. All of them. Everyone the paper eats."

We sat with that for a moment. The lamp hissed. Somewhere out in the ward a patient coughed twice and settled.

"The third letter," I said, because someone had to.

* * *

The third letter was dated the nineteenth of October. Two days after the Crossing. It was longer than the others, and Vane's hand had changed — still beautiful, but faster, the loops stretched, as though the pen had been running.

*Col. Brase. It is done as authorized. I will not flatter either of us with euphemism: forty-one souls, of whom perhaps six were of military consequence. The remainder were villagers, chiefly women, children, and elders too slow to run the river ice. My men performed poorly, several refusing further orders after the first hour; two are dead of their own hands this morning. I hold you personally responsible for having armed me with an instruction rather than an order, so that responsibility may now slide off your signature onto mine. Very well. I will wear it.*

*What I require of you in return: passage. You know what awaits me at a court of inquiry, and you know equally well what testimony I would be obliged to give at one. We are men of documents, Colonel, and I am a document you cannot afford to have read. Arrange my disappearance with the same discretion as before. I will keep copies of our correspondence as insurance, as is my habit, and you will find my habit reassuring.*

"There," Ivo said softly. "That's the sentence, isn't it. That's the whole thing. *You know what testimony I would be obliged to give.* Vane wasn't threatening him exactly. He was just being honest. And honesty was threat enough."

"Brase couldn't court-martial him without a trial," I said. "And a trial means witnesses, and witnesses mean the Crossing gets spoken aloud in a room with journalists outside it. So instead the Captain drowned. Officially. Fell from the ferry at Lantern Bridge in high water, body never recovered, honors rendered, widow consoled by return of post." I paused. "There was no widow. I wrote the condolence myself. He dictated it while packing. He thought it was funny."

Ivo looked at me for a long moment. "And what did you think?"

"I thought it was funny," I said, "which is the sort of thing a twenty-four-year-old clerk thinks, and I have spent two years learning otherwise at the pace of one slow lesson per night." I sorted the remaining letters into a stack. "There are seven more. Requisitions. Ammunition counts. A bill for lime — quicklime, Mr. Okafor, forty sacks of it, signed for by a quartermaster who has since been promoted. Shall I go on, or would you like to lie down first? Most people do by the fifth letter."

"Go on," Ivo said, and moved the lamp closer, and did not lie down.

* * *

It took us the better part of two hours, and I will not reproduce all of it here even in memory, because some arithmetic deserves to stay where it was done. But by the end we had reconstructed Kessari Crossing entirely from paper — the dates, the companies, the ammunition drawn (six hundred rounds, returned: ninety-one), the boats hired and never logged back, the forty sacks of lime, and finally, dated the sixth of November, the letter that Brase had written and Vane had copied, the one that closed the account:

*Your disappearance is arranged. You will depart on the night ferry of the ninth. Your effects will be inventoried and your file sealed. Consider our correspondence concluded, and consider also, Captain, that a man who keeps copies of letters is a man who has generously provided the rope for his own hanging should it ever be required. Yours in continued confidence, E. Brase.*

"He threatened him back," Ivo said.

"He threatened him back," I agreed. "Two men holding identical pistols to identical temples, and both of them smiling about it. That is what I served, Mr. Okafor. Four years of that. Fetching the ink for it."

Ivo gathered the letters into their stack, squared the edges, and for a moment simply rested his hand on top of them, the way one rests a hand on a sleeping animal one does not entirely trust.

Then he went still.

"Pell," he said. "This one." He slid a letter out from mid-stack — the fourth chronologically, dated the twenty-eighth of October, which I had nearly skimmed because its opening paragraphs were logistics. "Read the second page. Read the last paragraph."

I took it. Vane's hand again, but relaxed now, chatty almost, a man writing to a partner:

*[...] Further, regarding the matter of the infirmary: your head nurse is sentimental and your new assistant is worse. The latter — Okafor, the one with the ledger habit — records everything. Deaths, fevers, the weather, I believe his own dreams. Ordinarily I would call it an virtue in a medical man, but you will recall that virtue, like documentation, is a question of whose purposes it serves. If our arrangement requires future tidying, be aware there is a small dark book in that ward that contradicts your official paper at every seam. It would take little to arrange its loss along with mine. — A.V.]*

The lamp hissed. Out in the ward, somebody groaned in their sleep, and neither of us moved.

"He knew about the ledger," Ivo said. His voice sounded strange — not frightened yet, just far away, like a man reporting weather from another country. "Two years ago. Before I'd filled ten pages of it. How—"

"You talk in your sleep?" I offered, and immediately wished I hadn't, because gentle humor is only gentle when the ground beneath it isn't collapsing. But Ivo huffed — half a laugh, no more — which told me something about him: even at the bottom of a dread, he could find the one loose thread of absurdity and pull it.

"I don't sleep enough to talk in it," he said. "Pell. Who else knows?"

I made myself think slowly, which is difficult when every instinct is sprinting. "Vane knew. Brase knew, because Vane told him, and Vane told him because that letter was itself a kind of gift — *here, Colonel, I've noticed your garrison's other loose thread for you.* Whether anyone acted on it—" I stopped.

"Doyle," we said, together. Which was its own small horror, that we had arrived there in unison.

"The audit," Ivo said. "Last week. He searched the supply room for two hours over a discrepancy of one dozen bandage rolls. Nobody audits a dozen bandages, Pell. He was looking for something, and he was looking *at me* while he did it."

"Or he was looking for me," I said. "Or for letters. Or for a small dark book. Or for all three." I rubbed my eyes. The lamp flame had begun to stutter; the oil was low, and the shadows in the corners of the supply room had grown ambitious. "Here is what I can tell you about soldiers who watch quietly, Mr. Okafor, having been employed by one for four years: they watch for a long time, and then very suddenly they stop watching and start doing. The gap between those two conditions is not announced."

Ivo stood. He tucked the letters back into their oilcloth, wrapped the string around it with fingers that were steadier than mine would have been, and then — this was the part I would remember later, in cells and courtrooms and on bridges at midnight — he picked up his ledger, opened it to a fresh page, and wrote.

"What are you doing?" I asked.

"Recording," he said. "Dated tonight: *read the letters. Confirmed Brase's authorization. Forty-one souls.* And underneath that—" his pen scratched on, small and unhurried— "*note appended to official record: someone has been watching this book since before it mattered. Let the record show it mattered anyway.*"

He blotted the page, closed the book, and looked up at me, and in the guttering lamplight his face held something between fear and an extraordinary calm, the calm of a man who has just learned the flood is coming and has decided, on reflection, to keep gardening.

"Now," he said pleasantly, "we leave tomorrow. Ahead of the schedule. Ahead of Doyle. Ahead of whoever else has been reading over my shoulder for two years." He blew out the lamp, and the dark came in close around us, smelling of carbolic and lamp oil and old paper. "And Pell — in the morning, you're going to tell me exactly how a man carrying the most dangerous letters in the colony let himself get half-frozen on a bridge within sight of the garrison that wants them burned."

"That," I said into the darkness, "is a story involving brandy, a mule with opinions, and a ferryman I intend to sue."

"Good," said Ivo, feeling his way to the door. "Wake me if anyone tries to kill us before dawn."

Outside, along the frozen river, a sentry's boots crunched past the window and did not stop.

But farther off — much farther, out where the lantern-light thinned to nothing along the water — something else crunched too, once, and then went quiet, as if it had heard us listening.
