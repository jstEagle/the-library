## 11. First Checkpoint: The Bridge

The plan, such as it was, had been drawn up in the dispensary three nights earlier, in the blue hour between the last round of dressings and the first snoring of the ward. Pell had sketched it on the back of a requisition form using a burnt matchstick for a pencil, which struck me as exactly the sort of economy a clerk learns and a soldier never does.

"You are the diversion," he said, tapping the little X he'd made at the bridge's near end. "I am the parcel."

"And if the parcel is opened?"

"Then the parcel has very good papers." He smiled his thin, unreadable smile — the one that made me think, not for the first time, that whatever Mr. Pell had been before Vane's clerk, he had been something patient. A man who could sit still while the world walked past him.

The papers were good. That was the trouble with them; they were *too* good. Amara procured the medical chit from a supply sergeant who owed her eleven years of small favors, and Pell aged it with tea water and turned the ink of the signature just slightly wrong — wrong in the direction of haste, not forgery. "A perfect document frightens them," he explained, holding the paper over the lamp until the edges took on the honest grime of the road. "Nobody questions a thing that looks tired."

He was right, of course. He was right about most things, which was one of the several reasons I did not entirely trust him.

The diversion was my part, and I will confess here — since this account is for no one but myself, or perhaps for everyone someday, which amounts to much the same thing — that I hated my own part of it. Not the danger. The danger was almost a relief after weeks of holding still. What I hated was that it required an audience, and an audience requires collateral.

"A staged emergency," I'd said doubtfully, when he first proposed it.

"The best kind," said Pell. "All of the urgency and none of the dying."

I should have written that sentence down and framed it above my bed.

* * *

We chose the evening of the twenty-eighth, when the garrison was thick with year-end inventories and every clerk on the river was counting things — cartridges, blankets, years. Sergeant Hobbes held the bridge checkpoint, and Hobbes was a gift: a man who believed in procedure the way other men believe in God, and who therefore could be counted upon to behave exactly as his regulations told him to, even when the universe misbehaved around him.

At six minutes past seven by the infirmary clock, Private Ferro wheeled the dispensary cart out of the yard at a pace just short of running, and I came behind him shouting.

"Clear the bridge! Fever case coming through! Clear the bridge!"

Ferro lay back in the cart under a blanket, gray-faced — he had taken a dram of ipecac for authenticity, against my advice and Amara's better judgment — and I carried the basin and the smell did half the work before anyone looked twice at us. There is nothing like the sound of a man losing his stomach to make a sentry decide he has seen enough.

Hobbes stepped out of his hut with his ledger under his arm and his face already arranged into its expression of grievance. "What's all this? No crossing without inspection—"

"Fever ward, you absolute walnut!" I snapped, in the voice I used on orderlies who fumbled a stretcher. It is a strange education, nursing; it teaches you that authority is mostly volume plus confidence, and that both can be manufactured like bread. "Do you want Kessari coughing on your paperwork?"

That did it. Nothing moves a man like the mention of fever moving toward *him*. Hobbes waved us through with both arms, actually waving, his ledger flapping, and Ferro produced a retching sound so convincing that a corporal near the far rail turned away with his hand over his mouth.

And behind us — this was the beauty and the horror of it — the whole checkpoint stopped to watch. Every eye on the bridge went to the cart, the basin, the performance of catastrophe. Nobody saw the tall man in a porter's coat come up the footpath on the north side with a crate of "chloroform tins" and papers that looked tired, and nobody stopped him, because Hobbes was busy holding open the barrier with his own two hands and shouting for someone to fetch vinegar.

Pell crossed the bridge inside ninety seconds. I know because I counted, the way I count everything now — pulse, breaths, seconds, names.

We got Ferro to the far side, dosed him with nothing stronger than mint water and apology, and I stood on the south bank with my heart going like a drum corps and felt, for perhaps four full seconds, magnificent.

It worked. It worked *barely*, which I have since learned is the only way anything worth doing works.

* * *

The curdling began an hour later, back in the dispensary, where Pell sat eating cold rice with the untroubled appetite of a man who has recently been a parcel and found the role agreeable.

"It went well," he said.

"It went well," I agreed.

Neither of us said anything for a moment. Then Amara came through the door without knocking, which she never did, and her face told me the rest before her mouth got there.

"There's been an accident at the yard gate," she said. "During your — during the fever business. One of the yard orderlies went for the vinegar trolley when Hobbes shouted. The trolley was on the ramp and the ramp iced over."

"How bad?" My voice sounded very far away, as though someone else were asking.

"Broken leg. Badly broken — the bone came through. They've taken him to the surgical hut." She paused, and Amara never paused unless she was choosing between truths. "It's Ferro, Ivo. Young Ferro."

The room tilted in the small, sickening way rooms do when a debt arrives that you did not know you had signed for. Ferro. Sixteen stone of cheerful incompetence, nineteen years old, who sang hymns off-key while scrubbing bedpans and called me *sir* despite my telling him weekly that nurses outrank no one. Ferro, who had lain in a cart retching theatrically on my instruction, and then, hearing the shout for the trolley, had done the one genuinely heroic thing of the entire evening — he had tried to help.

"He was meant to stay in the cart," I heard myself say stupidly.

"People rarely stay where we put them," Amara said gently. "That's rather the whole of medicine."

Pell set down his rice. For once he had nothing clever filed under any of his names.

I walked to the surgical hut through the freezing dark, past lanterns swinging on their hooks, rehearsing excuses I knew I would not use. The hut smelled of carbolic and wet wool. Ferro was on the table, white as the sheets, his leg splinted and his eyes glassy with the ether they'd given him — not enough to put him under, only enough to make him honest, which is worse.

"Ivo." He grinned when he saw me, the little wretch. "Did the fever fellow live?"

"He lived."

"Good." He winced, tried to raise himself, thought better of it. "Went down on the ramp like a sack of Bibles, didn't I. Heard the leg go. Sounded like green wood." He laughed, a thin breath of a laugh. "Sergeant says I'll likely keep it. Says I'm too stubborn to lose parts."

"You will keep it," I said, and pressed his hand, and fled.

* * *

He asked me the next morning. Of course he asked me the next morning; Ferro had the kind of mind that worried at things like a dog at a knot, and the ether had worn off overnight along with everyone else's version of events.

They were sitting him up by then, propped on pillows, his splinted leg a long pale shape under the blanket, and when I brought his breakfast he caught my sleeve with fingers surprisingly strong for a boy full of laudanum.

"Ivo. Tell me straight. The fever cart — that was staged, wasn't it. You and the cart. For someone to get across."

The ward went very quiet around us, or perhaps it only went quiet inside my ears. Twenty men pretending to sleep, and every one of them listening, because that is what wards do.

"What makes you ask that?" I said, and I heard it as it left me — smooth, warm, practiced, the exact voice of a man lying kindly — and I despised it more than I have despised any sound in my life.

"Because I did the retching on purpose," Ferro said, frowning up at me with the terrible earnestness of the young. "Because you gave me the ipecac. Because you're asking that question instead of answering it."

I looked down at him — at the splinted leg that would heal crooked, at the face that trusted me with the whole guileless weight of itself — and I lied.

"You were feverish and confused, lad. You took a tumble on ice. That's all there is, and all there will be."

He held my eyes a moment longer than was comfortable. Then he nodded, slowly, and let go of my sleeve, and something in his face closed a small door — not suspicion, quite, and not forgiveness either. Something between them, with the light off.

"All right," he said. "If you say so."

I said so. God forgive me, I said so twice.

* * *

I went back to the dispensary and sat with my ledger open on my knees for a long time before I wrote anything.

This book of mine has rules, unwritten ones, the way all consciences do. Names of the dead whom the army erased. Dates. Places. Causes where I could learn them, blank lines where I couldn't. It has never contained a lie, because the dead don't need lies, and it has never contained a living name, because the living can still speak for themselves.

Except they can't, can they? That is the discovery of this winter. The living can't always speak. Sometimes the living are nineteen years old with a crooked leg and a question, and the truth would put them in front of Colonel Brase, and so the truth must go somewhere, and the somewhere is me.

So I opened to a fresh page, and beneath two years of the dead I wrote:

*Ferro, J., orderly, b. 1880. Injured 28 December, yard ramp, in service of this book. Leg broken. Lie told on 29 December by I.O. May it be the last.*

A living name. The first. And beside it, because the ledger is honest even when I am not, the record of my own dishonesty — filed like a wound, cleaned and dressed and left open to the air, because the alternative was letting it close over something rotten.

Pell found me there an hour later, sitting with the book on my knees and the lamp burned low. He read the entry upside down, which I let him do, which was itself a kind of answer.

"You wrote the lie down," he said.

"I wrote everything down. That's the point of it."

He considered that for a while. Outside, the wind came off the river carrying the first smell of the year-end bonfires being stacked in the compound yard — old crates, old forms, old years, all waiting for a match.

"Do you know what Vane used to say?" Pell said at last, quietly, in the voice he only used when speaking of the captain. "'A record that only holds the pleasant truths isn't a record. It's a portrait.'" He nodded at the ledger. "Yours just became a record."

"It became a ledger with a price in it," I said. "I traded that boy's leg for your ninety seconds on the bridge."

"Yes," said Pell, simply. "You did. And you'd do it again, and so would I, and the names in that book are worth a hundred crooked legs — but knowing that doesn't stop it costing, does it?"

"No," I said. "It doesn't stop it costing."

We sat in the lamplight, the two of us and the book, while the wind stacked itself against the shutters. Two checkpoints left. Marrow Junction, where the trains ran south with the mail and the military police ran thicker with every day nearer the first. And after that the customs house at the coast, where every parcel gets opened and everything gets read.

Ninety seconds on a bridge. One boy's leg. One lie in the book with the rest of the truth.

I was beginning to understand what the century was charging us for its turn.

It was Amara who brought the news that evening, coming in fast with snow still on her shawl and her eyes bright with the particular fear she reserved for things already gone wrong.

"Ivo," she said. "There's a telegram on the board. Marrow Junction. They've transferred a sergeant down the line ahead of schedule." She swallowed. "It's Doyle."
