## 12. The Price of the Diversion

Ferro slept like a boy pretending to sleep — eyes too still, breath arranged rather than easy. Ivo knew the performance because he had invented it himself at nineteen, lying in a charity ward in Colverna while a surgeon decided whether his left hand would keep its fingers. He had kept his hand. He had lost other things instead.

He pulled the chair closer to the orderly's cot and set down the basin of water he had no honest reason to bring. Ferro's leg was splinted from ankle to knee, the bone cracked clean where the supply cart had pinned it against the bridge rail. A fracture, nothing more. In a war that fed men into a grinder by the hundred, a broken leg was almost a gift.

Almost.

"You breathe differently when you're awake," Ivo said quietly. "It's a tell. You might want to work on it."

Ferro opened one eye. Then both. "Sister Amara says you were born knowing when people are faking."

"Sister Amara flatters me. I was born knowing when *I'm* faking, which is a narrower education." Ivo unwound the bandage at the top of the splint and checked the skin beneath — pink, warm, uninfected. Good. "How is the leg this morning?"

"It throbs."

"Throbbing means it's alive. Numb means we worry." He soaked a cloth and wiped the grit from Ferro's shin, working around the splint with the ease of long habit. Around them the ward murmured its usual morning liturgy: coughing, groaning, one sergeant near the window arguing in his fever with a horse that had been dead since spring. The stove ticked. Snow slid off the roof in soft thumps.

"I keep thinking about it," Ferro said.

"About what?"

"The cart." His voice had gone careful, the way voices do when they've been rehearsing something. "The rope slipping. The way it came off the ramp all at once, like somebody had cut it."

Ivo's hands did not stop moving. This was the first discipline of nursing he had ever learned, before dressings, before dosages: hands never stop moving, whatever the face is doing. "Ropes slip," he said. "Especially army ropes. Half of them were woven before the war and the other half were woven by men who hated weaving."

"It didn't just slip, Mr. Marchetti." Ferro turned his head on the pillow. He was twenty-one, maybe twenty-two, with a farm boy's square jaw and a cowlick that stood up through the bandage on his forehead where he'd cracked it against the planks. "I've hauled carts since I could walk. That rope was tied off. I tied it myself, and then Corporal Voss checked it, and then—"

"And then it was a busy morning," Ivo said, "with a medical run to fake for reasons nobody explained to you, an officer shouting, and a stranger in a borrowed coat standing on the ramp. Busy mornings eat ropes."

The lie went out of him smooth as thread through a needle's eye. That was the terrible part — not that he lied, but how little friction there was. He had expected lying to feel like breaking something. Instead it felt like tidying.

Ferro studied him. He had the slow, thorough way of looking that suggested he had grown up judging weather and livestock and would not be hurried in judging a man.

"That man," Ferro said. "Pell, they said his name was. He wasn't sick, was he? Not really fever-sick."

"He was half-frozen when they carried him off the bridge. Fever or no fever, he'd have died in the snow."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Ivo agreed mildly. "It isn't." He began rewrapping the bandage, tucking the ends with more attention than the task required. "Here is your answer, though, and you may keep it: whatever happened on that ramp, you are in this bed with a straight-breaking bone, a good prognosis, and a nurse who intends to have you walking by Easter. There are men in this ward who would trade places with you down to their souls. Some of them are beyond trading."

"I know that." Ferro's jaw worked. "I'm not ungrateful. I just want to know if I broke my leg because somebody needed me to break my leg."

And there it was, laid out between them on the gray blanket like a card turned face up. Ivo finished the bandage. Tied it off. Put his hands in his lap where they could be trusted.

"Ferro," he said, "if I told you yes, what would you do?"

The boy blinked. It was, apparently, not among the answers he had rehearsed. "I — report it, I suppose. To Sergeant Doyle."

"To Doyle." Ivo let the name sit a moment. "And what do you imagine happens then? To me, perhaps a reprimand. To Pell, a cell, or worse. And to you?" He leaned forward. "You become the orderly who asked questions about an accident on a garrison where officers erase men from the rolls like ink blots. You would spend the rest of your service being noticed by exactly the people one hopes never to be noticed by. I've seen what noticing costs. I write it down every night."

Ferro had gone pale under his tan. "Erase men from the—"

"Forget I said that." Ivo rose and adjusted the blanket, brisk again, the door of conversation swinging shut with professional grace. "Morphine at noon. Broth if you can keep it down. And if you must think about the cart, think about it this way: the cart is gone, the leg will mend, and the man who may have caused it spends his nights watching boys die and writing their names in a book so that no one can pretend they never existed. If that man owes you a debt, Ferro, believe me, he is paying interest on it already."

He was three steps away when Ferro spoke again.

"You said 'a book.'"

Ivo stopped.

"I didn't say anything about a book," Ferro said slowly. "You said a book."

The ward went on coughing around them. The feverish sergeant's horse had apparently won the argument, because he had begun to weep gently. Ivo stood with his back to the cot and felt the particular cold of a man who has survived an avalanche only to step on his own bootlace.

"Nurses keep records," he said, turning with a smile he could feel sitting slightly wrong on his face. "Temperatures. Doses. It is a very dull book full of numbers."

"Then why did your face just do that?"

"Do what?"

"Fold up." Ferro pushed himself up on his elbows, wincing, refusing to lie back down. "Mr. Marchetti. I'm not going to tell Doyle anything. My brother died at Kessari Crossing and my mother got a letter saying he was *lost in transit*, which is a strange thing to lose a whole man in, and I have spent two years not asking questions because not asking questions felt like staying safe. But I'm lying in this bed anyway, aren't I? With my leg in pieces. So staying safe doesn't seem to be on offer." His voice cracked and he steadied it, furious at it. "I just want to know if it was for something. If I'm lying here for something true."

There is a moment in every nurse's life — many moments, if the nurse lasts — when the correct thing and the kind thing pull in opposite directions like two horses, and the wagon between them is a human being. Ivo stood in that moment and felt it stretch.

What he wanted to say was: *yes, boy, I untied your rope with my own hands while Pell walked past under forged papers, because a dead captain's confession has to reach London before New Year's Day or every name I've ever saved from forgetting burns with the rest of the paper shame, and you were the cheapest price available, and I have been tasting copper in my sleep ever since.*

What he said was: "Rest now. Morphine at noon."

Because the truth was not his alone to give. Because Pell's life hung on the story holding together. Because — and this was the thought that made him ill, the thought he kept turning over at night like a stone with something underneath it — because Ferro believing it was an accident was *safer for Ferro*, and safety made such a convenient coat for cowardice.

He escaped to the dispensary and shut the door and put his back against it.

Amara looked up from the inventory shelf, a bottle of iodine in each hand, and read him the way she always did — instantly, completely, and without mercy. "The boy asked you."

"How could you possibly—"

"Everyone asks eventually. It's the leg's fault; it gives men time to think." She shelved the bottles. "What did you tell him?"

"A beautiful, seamless, well-constructed falsehood."

"And how did it feel?"

Ivo opened his mouth to say *fine*, and found the word wouldn't come out, which was answer enough. Amara nodded once, unsurprised, and returned to her counting, and that was somehow worse than an argument would have been. She had lost a brother at Kessari Crossing. She had joined them with her grief converted to iron. She did not lecture him, because she understood — better than he did some days — that they were all spending themselves, coin by coin, and the only sin was to stop counting.

But the ledger counted. That was the whole point of the ledger. Every entry in it was a refusal of the garrison's arithmetic, the arithmetic where men vanished into *lost in transit* and massacres became *engagements* and reputations compounded like interest. And now Ivo had written a false entry into the living world: a boy with a broken leg and a tidy story about a slipped rope, filed away in the great record of things that never happened.

That night, after lights-out, he sat on his cot with the ledger open on his knees and the shuttered lantern leaking one blade of light across the page.

He had a rule — he had believed it was a rule — that only the dead went in the book. Names struck from the rolls, men erased so that pensions could go unpaid and reports could stay clean. Writing the dead was a duty. Writing them cost nothing but ink.

Ferro was alive. Ferro was snoring faintly across the ward, leg splinted, prognosis excellent, entirely capable of one day reading whatever Ivo wrote.

Ivo dipped the pen.

His hand hesitated over the page longer than it ever had. He thought of all the names above the empty line — Kessari men, deserters shot for example, fever cases recorded as battle wounds — each one entered in the same steady script, each one a small stone laid against the current. And he thought: if I write Ferro here, in this book of erased men, what am I saying? That the garrison erased him too? Or that I did?

But the alternative was worse. The alternative was a book with a hole in it, a conscience with a convenient gap, and he had seen where convenient gaps led. They led to Colonel Brase toasting the century's end while ledgers burned. They led to *lost in transit*. A record that only held the comfortable truths was not a record; it was a mirror.

So he wrote it honestly, because honesty was the last thing he had left to spend:

*Ferro, Tomas. Orderly, 2nd class. Not dead — God forgive me, not even close. Leg broken 27 December on the lantern bridge, by my arrangement, so that a stranger might cross with another man's name. He asked me directly whether I had caused it. I told him no. Both facts belong in this book. Only one of them does him credit, and it is not mine.*

Below it, after a moment, he added: *Brother died at Kessari Crossing. Mother informed he was 'lost in transit.' Ask him his brother's name. It should be in this book too.*

He closed the ledger and pressed his palm flat against the cover, the way he did every night, a small superstition — as if he could hold the names in through the binding, the way you hold warmth in a stone.

Across the ward, Ferro stirred. "Mr. Marchetti?" His voice was thick with the edge of real sleep.

"Yes?"

"My brother's name. If you're keeping a list of the ones they lost properly — his name was Anselm Ferro. Private, Third Company. He went in at the crossing with forty others and the report says eleven." A pause. "Eleven, out of forty. Nobody ever said where the rest went."

Ivo sat very still in the dark.

"Would you write him down?" Ferro asked. "In your dull book of numbers?"

"I will write him," Ivo said, and found his voice held, barely, "in letters you could read across a courtroom."

The boy made a sound that might have been thanks, or relief, or the beginning of understanding, and slid under at last into honest sleep.

Ivo took out the ledger once more and wrote *Anselm Ferro* in his steadiest hand, and beneath it drew the small mark he used for the erased — and then, after a moment, beside young Ferro's entry, he drew something he had never drawn before: a second mark, a thin line linking the living name to the dead one. Brother to brother. Debt to debt. The book was growing teeth, or roots, or both.

He blew out the lantern.

Tomorrow — he knew it before Amara tapped twice on the dispensary door and slipped him the note, before he read the single penciled line in Pell's careful clerk's hand — tomorrow the route would move again. *Doyle transferred to Marrow Junction. Someone is ahead of us. Must board tonight.*

Tonight, then. He would have to arrange Ferro's broth, brief Amara, and walk out onto the snow with a book of stolen names sewn against his ribs, past the very sergeant whose questions he had spent a week dodging.

He looked across the dark ward at the sleeping boy whose trust he had spent like a counterfeit coin, and made himself one promise, small and hard as a seed: *when this is done, whatever it costs me, Ferro gets the truth.*

Then he buttoned his coat, took up the ledger, and went out into the cold to buy its passage with whatever he had left.
