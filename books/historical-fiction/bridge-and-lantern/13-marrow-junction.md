## 13. Marrow Junction

The trouble with sewing a ledger into one's coat lining, Pell had discovered, was that one could never again forget the coat. Most men wore a coat the way they wore their own indifference — without thought. Pell now wore his the way a sinner wears guilt: aware of every seam, every stitch, every moment it brushed against a doorframe or a stranger's knee. Fifty-one names pressed against his ribs like a sleeping cat that occasionally woke and dug in its claws.

He had done the sewing himself, by candlelight in Amara's linen room, with Ivo watching over his shoulder and offering commentary in the manner of a man who has decided to be helpful by being critical.

"Tighter," Ivo had said. "Doyle will pat you down like he's checking a goose for fat."

"I have been patted down before," Pell had replied. "By professionals. In three countries."

"And did you have fifty-one dead men sewn into your lining on those occasions?"

"No. Only debts."

Ivo had not laughed. Pell had come to learn this about him — that the nurse's humor was a small lamp he lit only when the ward was quiet, and never when the stakes were showing. But Amara, threading needles with those quick dark fingers of hers, had snorted into the basting thread, and Pell had counted that as an audience won.

Now, at Marrow Junction, the coat was all he thought about.

* * *

The junction was less a station than a wound in the hillside — a gash of rails cut through black pines, a water tower leaning like a drunk against the sky, and one wooden platform where the wind off the Sante valley came through a man's clothes as though the clothes were a rumor. Snow fell in that half-hearted way of snow that cannot decide whether to commit.

Pell stood at the ticket window with his collar up and bought two third-class tickets south: one for himself, one for the fiction of Mr. Pell's traveling companion, who had unfortunately taken ill and would not be traveling after all. It was cheaper than explaining why a clerk of no particular importance needed to look unremarkable in a crowd, and refunds were for optimists.

The train was due at nine. It would rattle through the night and reach the coastal line by dawn. Twelve hours. He repeated it to himself like a prayer: twelve hours, then the sea, then envelopes, then London, then done. Behind him, somewhere back along the river road, Ivo was already preparing his own journey with the duplicate ledger and that terrible calm of his, the calm of a man who has decided what he is willing to lose and finds the list shorter than most.

Pell checked the clock on the station wall. Eight-forty.

And then he saw the sergeant.

Doyle stood at the far end of the platform under the one lamp that worked, talking to the duty officer with the easy posture of a man who belonged wherever he happened to be standing. His greatcoat was buttoned to the chin. His kit bag sat at his feet like a dog that had learned not to wander. Steam rose from his breath in short, economical clouds.

Pell did not move. Movement, he had learned long ago in less reputable professions, is confession. He finished his transaction, took his tickets, turned with the unhurried air of a man considering whether to buy a paper, and looked at the departures board as if it contained the mysteries of the age instead of two trains and a goods service.

*Transferred ahead of us,* he thought. *Of course. The man travels faster than bad news.* Brase had moved his hound down the line to wait at the narrows — or perhaps Doyle had arranged it himself, which was worse, because it meant instinct rather than orders, and instincts could not be forged.

There were eleven minutes until the train. There was no other train. There was no other road that mattered; the coastal customs house did not care about courage, only about dates, and January the first sat on Pell's chest like a stone. The ledger could not go by post from here — he had checked, twice, and both times the postal clerk had looked at him with the flat suspicion of a man whose cousin worked for the provost.

Which left one option, and the option was absurd, and Pell found, examining it honestly, that he was almost smiling.

He was going to spend the night in the same train car as the man hunting him.

"Well," he murmured to the departures board, "it wouldn't be the first time I've dined with the tax collector."

* * *

He boarded early and chose his seat with the care of a chess player. Second car, smoking compartment, the bench against the window — because a man with his back to a wall can watch a door, and Doyle would sit with his back to a wall, which meant Pell could take the seat across from him rather than behind him. Behind read as pursuit. Across read as coincidence, provided the coincidence was dressed well enough.

Pell dressed it. He bought a paper from the boy on the platform — a provincial thing full of harvest figures and a speech by some colonel about the century's end — and he folded it with the crisp authority of a gentleman who reads harvest figures for pleasure. He arranged his small valise at his feet. He let his shoulders settle into the shape of a tired clerk, which required no acting whatsoever.

Doyle came down the car at nine sharp.

Pell felt him before he saw him — there is a particular displacement of air that a large careful man makes, a pressure in the ear — and kept his eyes on the paper. Harvest figures. Barley, remarkably, was up.

The bench creaked opposite him. Doyle settled into it, laid his kit bag between his boots, and unwrapped with visible pleasure a pipe and a rubber pouch of tobacco.

"Evening," Doyle said.

"Evening," said Pell, and turned a page. Wheat stable. Oats, disappointingly, unreported.

He knew Doyle recognized him. Of course he did — Pell had been the feverish stranger carried through the checkpoint gate in a handcart, and sergeants do not forget faces they have already begun to distrust. The question was whether Doyle would say so, and if so, in front of whom. The compartment filled around them: a wool merchant asleep before the wheels turned, two conscripts sharing bread, an elderly woman knitting something gray and endless. Witnesses, of a sort. Doyle struck a match and lit his pipe, and the little flame lit his face from below, giving him the look of a man being told a secret by the fire.

"Going south?" Doyle asked, conversationally, smoke curling.

"For the coast," Pell said, folding his paper a precise quarter-inch. "I find I do my best work near the sea."

"Do you."

"The fish don't argue."

That earned him a grunt that might, in a more generous reading, have contained humor. The train lurched and began to move, and Marrow Junction slid away into the snow — the lamp, the leaning tower, the dark pines swallowing the rails behind them — and Pell understood, with the peculiar clarity that arrives precisely too late to be useful, that he had just nailed shut his own door. Twelve hours. One compartment. A coat full of names and a nose full of pipe smoke, and the hunter sitting close enough that Pell could count the gray threads coming into the red of his beard.

He opened the paper again. Barley, he decided, was going to carry them both through the night.

* * *

An hour out, the conscripts got off at a fueling stop and did not return, and the wool merchant proved to possess the gift of sleeping as though embalmed. This left four souls: Pell, Doyle, the knitter, and the train itself, which complained its way upward through the pass with the sound of an enormous animal being reasoned with.

Doyle smoked. Pell read. Every forty minutes or so the conductor came through and punched something, and both men performed the same ritual of producing papers, and both men's papers were found to be in order, and neither man believed the other's relief.

It was, Pell reflected, rather like dancing with a partner who knows all the same steps and learned them from the wrong teacher.

"You were sick," Doyle said, around ten o'clock. Not looking up. Tamping his pipe. "At the garrison. Fever. They brought you in off the ford like a sack of turnips."

"Turnips," Pell said, "at least get a doctor."

"Sergeant said you nearly died."

"Sergeants say many things." Pell turned another page. "As it happens I recovered, thanks to the infirmary, and owe my life to better men than either of us, I suspect."

Something shifted in Doyle's jaw — the smallest movement, a door tested and left shut. He looked out the black window, where nothing looked back but their own faint reflections and, beyond them, the dark rushing pines.

"They're burning everything at New Year," he said, to the glass. "Down at the coast depot too. Whole archive. Twenty years of paper. Command says the new century wants a clean desk."

Pell's hands, he noted with distant interest, had gone entirely still on the newspaper. He made them move. He smoothed the page flat, an idle gesture, a man merely tidying.

"Efficient," he said.

"Wasteful," said Doyle. "Some of that paper's the only honest thing the army ever wrote."

The words hung in the smoke between them like a coin nobody would claim. Pell studied the sergeant's reflection in the glass — the heavy brows, the burn scar along the neck disappearing into the collar, the eyes that were doing arithmetic while the mouth told jokes.

"Have you lost much time to dishonest paper, Sergeant?"

Doyle turned from the window then, and looked at him directly, and Pell felt the look land like a hand on his chest — right on the coat, right on the lining, right on the fifty-one names.

"My brother," Doyle said, "is written down somewhere in that archive. Kessari Crossing. Except he isn't, because there's nothing to write down. No grave. No report. Just his name gone missing from a muster roll like a button off a coat." He knocked his pipe out against the window frame, unhurried. "Funny thing about buttons. You don't miss them till the coat falls apart."

The train rocked them both in silence. The old woman's needles clicked on, steady as a metronome, knitting her gray endlessness. Pell became acutely aware that his heart was making noise at a volume he would previously have described as impossible for an internal organ.

"You keep lists too."

Ivo's words, in Pell's memory — the first thing the fevered man had ever said to him. And now here was the echo wearing a sergeant's greatcoat, and Pell could not tell anymore which of them was the ghost.

"That's a strange sentiment for a serving soldier," Pell said quietly.

"It's a strange army," said Doyle, and closed his eyes, and appeared to sleep.

He did not sleep. Pell knew it the way animals know weather. For three hours they sat in the swaying lamplight, each pretending unconsciousness with the dedication of actors in a bad company, each listening to the other breathe, and the ledger lay sewn against Pell's ribs like a heart that was not his but had been entrusted to him anyway.

Somewhere past midnight, unable to bear it, Pell spoke into the dark.

"If a man carried something," he said, "something true — proof of what happened at the Crossing — and he were carrying it to people who'd print it, people in London—" He stopped. Started again, humbler. "If he offered to share whatever blame came of it with the men who'd earned none, would that be worth anything? To anyone?"

A long silence. The wheels found a stretch of straight track and ran smooth, almost kind.

"There's a sergeant," Doyle said at last, without opening his eyes, "who's been ordered to find out what a certain stranger is carrying and take it off him. And that sergeant is wondering why the stranger would tell him all this, unless the stranger thinks the sergeant has already guessed — and is choosing, out of everyone on God's earth, him."

"Perhaps," said Pell, "the stranger is a fool who trusts arithmetic. A brother at Kessari. A coat missing a button."

The needles clicked. The train plunged on into the hills.

"Don't talk to me again about it," Doyle said, "where there's lamps."

Then he actually slept, or did an excellent portrait of it, and Pell sat with his paper of barley and wheat and watched the black windows and did not sleep at all.

* * *

Dawn came the color of dishwater, seeping slowly into the compartment as the line dropped down out of the pass toward the coast junction. The knitter disembarked at some nameless halt, leaving her gray thing unfinished on the bench like an abandoned animal; the wool merchant woke, gathered his sample case, and shuffled off without once having been truly present. By six o'clock it was only the two of them, and the mountains letting go of the train by degrees, and far ahead a thin silver line that might have been cloud or might have been the sea.

Doyle stretched, checked his watch, and stood. He swung his kit bag onto his shoulder with the ease of a man carrying nothing heavier than laundry.

"This is me," he said. "Coastal detachment. New posting." He looked down at Pell, and for a moment the sergeant's face held something unspeakably tired. "Train goes on to Harrow Quay without me. You'll want the second carriage when you change lines. The first one's got military police in it. Been there since Marrow Junction."

He said it the way a man reports the weather. Then he walked the length of the swaying car, stepped down onto the platform of the coast junction, and stood there in the rising light with his pipe unlit in his hand, watching the train as if seeing off a friend.

Pell sat very still for a long moment. Then he began, very carefully, to laugh — the silent, shaking laughter of a man who has just discovered the floor under him was thinner than anyone thought, and held.

Military police. First carriage. Since Marrow Junction. Which meant Doyle had known the whole night — known, perhaps, since the platform — that his quarry was riding the same train under official escort's nose, and had spent eight hours sitting across from him saying nothing, guarding him, in his way, with his silence.

Or it meant the trap had simply been given better walls.

The train slowed for the change of lines. Ahead, past the last headland, the sea showed itself at last — gray, enormous, indifferent, smelling of salt and endings. Pell gathered his paper, his valise, his coat with its paper heart, and stood.

Whatever waited at the customs house, he would not be taking the first carriage.
