## 14. Night Train Confessions

The train to Marrow Junction had exactly one smoking car, one lamp that worked, and — as of eleven minutes out of the last water stop — one sergeant of the Colonial Border Regiment sitting three seats away, pretending very hard to read a newspaper that was four days old.

Pell knew the newspaper was four days old because he had read it himself, twice, in the waiting room at Hallow Ford, mostly for the shipping notices and partly for the serialized adventure story about a man eaten by a tiger, which had not improved on the second reading either.

He considered his options.

Option one: stay in his seat, keep his collar up, and hope that Sergeant Doyle's eyesight was worse than his memory. This was unlikely. Doyle had once identified Pell across a market square from the way he *bought apples*, according to Doyle himself, who had mentioned it with something like fondness.

Option two: move to the next carriage like a man with nothing to hide. Also suspicious. Innocent men did not flee smoking cars; guilty men did not flee them either; everyone simply sat where they were and suffered.

Option three: walk over and offer him a cigarette.

Pell chose option three, because he had learned — first as a clerk, then as whatever he was now — that the most dangerous thing you could do with a frightened man was leave him alone with his arithmetic. Better to interrupt the sum before it finished. Better to be a person than a problem.

Doyle looked up as the shadow fell across him. For a long moment neither man spoke, and the train rocked them both gently, like a mother who suspected her children of something.

"You," said Doyle.

"Me," said Pell, and held out the cigarette case. "You look cold."

"I'm always cold. It's the uniform. They issue it thin so we don't fall asleep." Doyle took the cigarette anyway. His hands were enormous, red-knuckled, careful around the small paper the way a man might handle a bird he didn't trust himself not to crush. "Funny thing," he said, striking the match. "I was thinking about you just now."

"People rarely think about me kindly."

"No," Doyle agreed, blowing smoke at the ceiling, which was grimed with the soot of a hundred such conversations. "Not kindly. Curiously. Like a coin you found in the road that's the wrong currency. You want to spend it, but you're not sure who'd take it."

"That's the most flattering description of me I've heard all year."

"Sit down, Mr. Pell." He said the name with a small pause before it, the way a man sets down a glass he suspects is rigged. "Or should I say the other name. I'm told there's another name."

* * *

The lamp above them buzzed. Outside, the winter dark poured past, stitched here and there with a farmhouse light, a signal fire, a frozen field catching the moon like the bottom of a spoon. The train was half empty: two sleeping soldiers across the aisle, an old woman with a cage of pigeons who had regarded both men throughout with the flat professional disinterest of someone who had seen every kind of man and preferred pigeons.

Pell sat. He folded his hands. Under his coat, against his ribs, the ledger's weight was sewn into the lining — Nurse Okafor's small, furious handwriting pressed against his heart like a second heartbeat, which, he supposed, was exactly what it was.

"Here's my difficulty," Doyle said conversationally. "Two years ago, a clerk named Elias Pell disappears from the garrison rolls the same week Captain Aldous Vane dies — officially dies — at Kessari Crossing. Very sad. No body recovered, but these things happen, rivers being what they are." He drew on the cigarette. "Then this winter a man walks onto the bridge at Hallow Ford half-dead, carrying letters in Vane's hand. Letters I've been ordered to keep quiet about by men whose orders I've spent six years obeying without asking what for. And the man looks like a drowned clerk pulled out of a river and dried wrong."

"The river does change a fellow," Pell said. "I recommend it highly. Very clarifying. All your debts float away."

"Don't jest at me, Mr. Pell."

"I'm not jesting at you. I'm jesting near you, which is different, and I'll stop if you tell me what happens when you finish being curious."

Doyle was quiet for a while. The old woman's pigeons shifted and muttered. Somewhere behind them, in the mail van, letters were rattling in their sacks toward the coast, toward London, toward lives none of them would ever hear about — including, if everything went well tonight, several envelopes containing pages that would end careers and possibly begin trials.

"What happens," Doyle said finally, "is that I put you off this train at Marrow Junction, wire the garrison, and Colonel Brase sends men who are not curious at all. Men who ask nothing. And you go into a room, and afterward there isn't a you anymore, and the paperwork says 'escaped' or 'fever' or whatever word they like this season. I've written those words myself. They come easily. That's what frightened me when I noticed how easily they came."

Pell kept his face still, but something in his chest turned over — not fear exactly, or not only fear. Recognition. This was not the voice of a hunter. It was the voice of a man arguing with himself in public and calling the other party *Mr. Pell*.

"And the other option?" Pell asked quietly.

"The other option requires me to believe a story. And I'm a bad audience for stories. I had a friend who could tell them beautifully. He ended up believing his own." Doyle flicked ash into the tray. "So tell me the true version. Not the pretty one. Not the one you practiced on the train platform rehearsing your wounded expression. The ugly one."

"The ugly one costs you something."

"Everything worth hearing does."

* * *

Pell took out his cigarettes again, more for the ritual than the tobacco, and let the silence arrange itself before he spoke.

"Elias Pell was real," he said. "That's the first thing. He was a real clerk, and I was him, and I did the work clerks do — requisitions, pay ledgers, casualty returns. Neat columns. I liked neat columns; they made the war look like mathematics, which was restful. And then one day in the autumn of '97 the mathematics stopped adding up, because Captain Vane asked me to make them stop adding up, and I did it, because I was a coward with good penmanship."

He watched Doyle's face. Nothing moved in it, but the sergeant had gone very still, the stillness of a man listening with his whole spine.

"Kessari Crossing," Pell went on. "You know the official story. Ambush during a ford maneuver. Twelve dead. Heroic rearguard action, captain lost. Brase wrote the citation himself — you can still find it framed somewhere, I expect, all those adjectives doing the work the facts wouldn't. Here's the unofficial story. There was no ambush. The village at the crossing was listed as hostile on the strength of a report that was falsified to cover a supply theft further up the chain — grain, mostly, and some rifles sold through Marrow Junction, though the rifles were never proved. Vane was ordered to clear the crossing. The order said *clear*. He chose to interpret it the way ambitious men choose to interpret things. Forty-one people died, Mr. Doyle. Farmers. Two midwives. Children in a granary that burned. And when the reports went up the line, Brase looked at what his order had produced, and instead of a court-martial he saw a mirror — because the falsified report that started it all traced back to men under his own protection. So he buried it. Buried the village, buried the truth, and gave Vane a death with no body attached, which is the most convenient kind, because a man with no grave can also have no trial."

The train swayed. The lamp hummed its one dim note.

"And you," Doyle said. His voice had gone rough at the edges. "Where were you while this burial was happening?"

"In the next room, copying it. That was my job afterward — making the false version neat. Vane kept the true version. Letters. Orders. His own hand, describing what he'd done, because — and this is the part I still can't decide whether to admire or despise — he wanted it written down. He couldn't stop having done it, so he refused to let it become tidy. Then he vanished, and I vanished, and for two years I've been a courier of paper that could hang a colonel." Pell smiled without much humor in it. "There's your ugly version. I helped bury forty-one people under neat columns, and now I'm digging them up with the same pen. I don't offer that as virtue. Only as fact."

"Why now? Why not burn the letters and live?"

"Because on January the first, every record in that garrison burns. Officially, it's the century turning — new era, clean books. Unofficially, it's the last loose page going into the stove. After New Year's Day, Kessari Crossing never happened at all, except in the memories of anyone still alive who saw it, and the century has a way of outliving those." Pell leaned forward. "But that's only half the answer. The true half is smaller and worse. I carried those letters for two years and did nothing, because doing something meant dying for paper, and I am, as established, a coward. Then I met a nurse who keeps a ledger of names the army strikes from its rolls. He writes down every forgotten man in a hand like a schoolmaster's, and he hides the book inside a corpse's shroud when Doyle comes searching, and he has never once — not once — decided that survival was more interesting than the truth. And standing in that infirmary watching him, I understood that my two years of caution were not prudence. They were just the cowardice wearing a better coat. So. Now."

For a long moment Doyle said nothing. He looked at his cigarette as though it had said something surprising.

"Nurse Okafor," he said at last. "The quiet one. Ferro talks about him like a saint."

"Ferro talks about everyone like a saint. It's one of his injuries."

A sound escaped Doyle that might, in a kinder setting, have been a laugh. It died quickly. He turned the cigarette over, studying the ember, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped to nearly nothing, beneath even the noise of the wheels.

"My brother was at the Crossing."

Pell did not move. The old woman's pigeons muttered. The train took a curve and the lamp swung its little shadow around the ceiling like a slow pendulum.

"Tomas Doyle," Doyle said. "Private, third company. Twenty-three years old. He wrote home every week — terrible handwriting, worse spelling, endless questions about the dog. The last letter arrived after the telegram announcing he was dead. It said the river was high that week and he'd learned to swim, and wasn't that funny." He crushed the cigarette out with great precision. "Twelve dead, the report said. Twelve heroes of the ambush at Kessari Crossing. I stood at the memorial service and I believed it, because believing it was easier than being the kind of man who doubted his own officers. My mother got a medal and a letter signed by Brase. She keeps both on the mantel. She polishes the medal."

"Doyle—"

"I checked, later. When I got promoted and could see the returns. Twelve dead at the Crossing, but the muster book showed thirty-one men went down to the ford and nineteen came back. Twelve dead, twelve missing, and nobody in the whole apparatus of the army thought that arithmetic was strange except a corporal who was too junior to matter." He looked up, and his eyes in the lamplight were terribly level. "So here's my question, Mr. Pell, and you'll answer it straight because your life depends on the quality of your honesty, which I understand is poor but might improve under pressure. Is Tomas in your papers? Is my brother a name in that confession of yours, or is he already erased?"

"He's in them," Pell said. "All of them. Every man at the ford that day, ranked and named. Vane insisted the dead be listed individually — he called it *the debt register*. I copied it four times." He paused. "Tomas Doyle is on the third page. He's listed as having tried to reach the granary. There's a note in the margin — Vane's hand. It says *this one fought back. Remember that.*

Doyle closed his eyes.

It lasted perhaps ten seconds. To Pell it felt like watching a building decide, slowly, whether to fall. When the sergeant opened his eyes again, they were wet, and he did not trouble to hide it, and somehow that undid Pell more than any threat could have.

"All right," Doyle said hoarsely. "All right. Here's what happens now, and you'll listen carefully because I'll only say it once. At Marrow Junction, I put you in a cell. That part isn't theater — there are two other garrison men on this train, and if I don't produce you, they will, and they don't argue with themselves the way I do. In the cell, you'll wait until morning relief. Morning relief is Corporal Finch, who owes me money and asks no questions. After Finch's shift begins, the cell door will be found unlocked and the guard will be found asleep, and Mr. Elias Pell will have escaped into the night, which will reflect poorly on me and I will accept the reflection."

"Why?" Pell asked. It came out almost angrily. "Why would you do this? Your brother's dead either way. Paper doesn't bring anyone back."

"No," Doyle agreed. "Paper doesn't bring anyone back." He looked at the dark window, at his own reflection ghosted against the flying fields. "But paper brought my brother to me tonight. First time in two years anyone's said his name out loud in my hearing. Everyone else got *the fallen*, *the brave twelve*, *our losses* — words like snowfall, covering everything the same. And then you sit down and give me one name, mine, particular, with a note in the margin saying he fought back." His jaw worked. "Names are all the dead have left, Mr. Pell. Somebody's collecting them properly, at last. I'm not the man who stands between the collector and the collection."

Pell found, to his embarrassment, that he could not immediately speak. He covered it by producing the cigarette case again; his hands were not entirely steady, and Doyle pretended not to notice, which was its own kindness.

"One condition," Pell managed.

"Bold of you, a fugitive setting conditions."

"It's about the nurse. Okafor. If they come for him — and they will, they're already watching him, there's a line about him in one of Vane's letters —"

"I know about the letter."

"Then you know he's the one in actual danger, not me. I chose this. He just wrote down what was true. Whatever happens at Marrow Junction tomorrow, whatever report gets filed — Sergeant Doyle, I am asking you, man to man across the world's worst cup of coffee—" Pell nudged the abandoned tin mug on the armrest, "—keep that nurse off their lists. Keep him *unnamed*. Let him be one more forgettable man in a white apron. Because the ledger has to reach the coast, and he's the only one who can carry it if I don't."

Doyle studied him for a moment, and something shifted in his broad, weary face — the look of a man revising a ledger of his own.

"You care about the book more than yourself," he said. "That's either noble or stupid."

"They're not mutually exclusive. I'd ask my brother clerk, but he's fictional."

"Hm." Doyle rose, tugging his tunic straight, becoming once more the sergeant, the instrument, the thin uniform issued against sleep. But at the end of the carriage he paused, one hand on the door strap, and said without turning around:

"The note in the margin. *This one fought back.* Does it — would there be a copy of that page? Something a man could send to his mother?"

"There'll be copies," Pell said softly. "When it's done, there'll be copies enough for every mantel in the county."

Doyle nodded once, a short sharp motion, and went through into the next carriage.

* * *

Pell did not sleep. He sat with his collar up and watched the dark unspool, feeling the ledger press against his ribs with each jolt of the wheels — patient, patient — and rehearsed, in a whisper under the rattle, the small theatrical cries he would make in the morning when his cell was found empty.

The train slowed for Marrow Junction at ten past four, sighing steam into a darkness lit by one swinging oil lamp and the breath of waiting soldiers. Doyle walked him down the steps at gunpoint, loudly, professionally, for the benefit of the other garrison men on the platform — and in the moment the shackles went on, quick and cold, he bent close as if checking them and murmured, so low the wind nearly took it:

"Finch's shift starts at six. The lock sticks — you'll need to lift, not pull. And Mr. Pell." A pause. The lamp swung. "Third page. Marginal note. Thank you."

Then the night swallowed them both, and the train stood empty on its rails, and somewhere up the line, in a warm infirmary smelling of carbolic, a nurse was waking early to check whether the world had yet discovered what he carried.

By noon, word would be moving back along the line faster than any train: *Pell taken. Pell in a cell. Pell—*

And by dusk, the cell would stand open, and the story would belong to Ivo.
