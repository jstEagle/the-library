## 15. Betrayal at Dawn

The news came with the milk.

That is to say, it came badly — watered, half-frozen, and carried by someone who did not want to be the one carrying it. Private Hollis, a boy of perhaps nineteen whose chief military distinction was an ability to sleep standing up, arrived at the infirmary before the lamps had been turned down, stamping snow off his boots and holding an envelope as though it might go off.

"Nurse Okafor. Colonel's compliments." He said this in the flat way of a man delivering somebody else's bad news, which was, in fairness, the only kind the Colonel ever sent anywhere.

Ivo wiped his hands and took the envelope. Inside was a single sheet in the adjutant's careful hand: *Detained at Marrow Junction, one male traveler answering to the name Pell, papers irregular. Sergeant Doyle has been commended. All persons having correspondence with the above will report to the Provost.*

*All persons having correspondence.* The sentence sat there like a hook left in a stream, patient, certain that sooner or later something hungry would swim past.

"Thank you, Hollis."

"Yes, sir. Nurse." Hollis shifted. "It's bitter out. Thought you'd want it early, on account of—" he lowered his voice to something that would not have survived a gentle cough, "—on account of the fellow what come through the ford last week. They're saying he's a spy."

"Are they."

"Yes sir. Doyle's saying it loudest." Hollis glanced at the ward behind Ivo, at the long rows of sleeping men, the breath-fog over their blankets rising and falling like slow tide. "Mind how you go, Nurse."

He went. He was, whatever else could be said of him, a boy who knew which way the wind was pointing without needing to lick a finger.

Ivo stood in the corridor for a long moment, the paper in his hand, listening to the garrison wake around him: the clatter of the kitchen, the far-off bugle cracking on its high note as it always did because Corporal Nye had never once managed the ascent, the groan of frost letting go of the window glass. Ordinary sounds. Sounds from a world that still believed tomorrow would resemble today.

Pell had been taken at Marrow Junction. Taken, which meant questioned, which meant — Ivo made himself think the word plainly, because flinching never yet saved anyone — which meant broken, eventually. Everyone broke. He had seen men hold out against pain better than most people hold out against cold tea, and he had seen them all, in the end, tell the truth they'd sworn to carry to the grave. The body simply got tired of being a locked room.

And in Pell's coat lining, sewn there by Amara's steady hand while Pell held the lamp and joked grimly about tailors, had been the duplicate ledger. Not the true one. But a ledger nonetheless, copied in Ivo's own hand, page after page of names the army had decided did not exist.

They had it. And soon enough they would know whose handwriting it was.

* * *

Amara found him in the linen store, which was where he went when he needed to be somewhere small. She did not ask why he was counting bandages he had already counted. She took the second end of the bolt from him and began folding, and for a while the two of them worked in the particular silence of people who trust each other enough not to fill it.

"Doyle's been commended," Ivo said finally. "For a train station."

"For catching your Mr. Pell."

"He's not my Mr. Pell."

"No," Amara agreed. "He's nobody's Mr. Pell, most likely. That's rather been the trouble all along." She set a fold sharp as a creased sermon. "Ivo. We need to talk about the book."

"There's nothing to talk about—"

"There is everything to talk about, and you will listen to me say it, because I have buried a brother and I know exactly what grief costs and I am not willing to add yours to the bill." Her voice stayed low — she was head nurse; low was her native register — but there was iron under the wool of it. "They have the copy. Within two days, three at most, some clerk with good eyes and no soul will match that handwriting to your requisition slips and your ward reports, and then Doyle will walk through that door with men behind him, and everything you are, everything you've kept, will be taken from you in one morning. Burn the true ledger. Today. Now. Before breakfast."

"I can't."

"You *can*. It's paper. Paper burns beautifully — I've watched the army prove it a hundred times. You feed it to the stove, you keep your hands, and you stay here, where sick men need you, and where you can do more good alive than any book can do burning in a drawer."

"The names, Amara."

"I know what's in it," she said, quieter now, and her hands stopped moving. "My brother's name is in it. Tomas Amara, Kessari Crossing, listed as *drowned during river operations*, which is a lovely way to describe what happened, isn't it? Drowned. As if the river had opinions." She looked up, and her eyes were dry in the way that cost her more than tears would have. "Do you imagine I don't lie awake composing his eulogy? Do you imagine a night passes when I haven't read every name you keep, in my head, in order, like prayers? The book matters to me more than to almost anyone alive, Ivo. And I am telling you to burn it."

The stove in the corner ticked. Somewhere beyond the wall, Nye attempted the bugle call again and failed in exactly the same place.

"Why?" Ivo asked. It wasn't defiance. He genuinely wanted to know — wanted the shape of her reasoning laid out where he could look at it honestly, because he respected her enough to fear she might be right.

"Because dead men write nothing," she said. "Because if they take you, they take the ledger *and* the nurse, and then it's all just evidence in a drawer, read out once in a courtroom nobody attends, and then burned on the first of January anyway. Because survival isn't surrender, Ivo — it's the only position from which anything gets done. I learned that watching women run hospitals through two wars before this one. The ones who died heroically were mourned beautifully. The ones who lived quietly changed things."

"That's a very persuasive speech."

"It's the best one I have. I've been rehearsing it since midnight."

"And it nearly works." Ivo set down the bandage. His hands were not quite steady, and he looked at them with a kind of clinical annoyance, the way he might regard a patient's tremor. "Nearly. But listen to what's actually happened, Amara. Pell is taken — and what does that mean? It means the army believes it has caught the whole conspiracy. Doyle gets a commendation. Brase sleeps soundly. Nobody is looking for a second ledger, because nobody knows there *is* a second ledger. If I burn mine now, they win twice — once at Marrow Junction, and again in this linen closet. Everything we paid for the crossing, everything Ferro—" his voice caught briefly on the name, and he pushed through it, "everything Ferro paid for, would buy nothing at all."

"They'll trace the handwriting regardless."

"From the copy, yes. Eventually. Which is why I'm leaving tonight or tomorrow, ahead of any warrant, and taking the true ledger to the coast myself." He said it aloud for the first time, and saying it made it solid, the way a diagnosis becomes real when spoken to the family. "The route is mapped. Two checkpoints remain. The mail goes out of the customs house daily if the weather holds. Four days, five at worst."

Alone, the plan sounded thin as ice in March. He watched Amara hear it and knew she thought the same.

"You'll travel as who?" she demanded. "You're the most recognizable man in this garrison south of the Colonel himself. Half the ward owes you their legs and the other half their bowels. You cannot walk into a depot without being greeted like a returning priest."

"Then I won't walk in as myself."

"And as whom, precisely?"

Ivo was quiet for a moment. He had been turning something over since the news arrived, something he had not yet dared examine in full light, because examined, it became a decision, and decisions had consequences, and he was already so heavily in debt to consequence. But there was nothing for it now. He had spent two years writing down the names the army erased. It seemed there was one final use for them.

"A name from the ledger," he said. "One of Vane's dead officers. A man with rank enough to have an aide, and no family near enough to contradict me. Lieutenant Harrow, possibly. Or Captain Merrick. Merrick drowned at Kessari with his papers dry in his saddlebag, according to the official account, which means somewhere there is a record of him having an aide-de-camp, and nowhere at all is there a record of what that aide looked like." He met her eyes. "The army forgot these men once, Amara. Deliberately, on paper, with signatures. I'm going to borrow the forgetting and turn it round on them."

Amara stared at him. Then, slowly, terribly, she began to laugh — not the laugh of amusement but the laugh of a woman recognizing the exact dimensions of a thing she could no longer stop.

"You dreadful, brilliant man," she said. "You're going to impersonate a ghost's servant using a ghost's own paperwork."

"I'm going to make their erasure work for the living for once. Yes."

"And if Merrick has a cousin at Marrow Junction?"

"Then I shall be very sorry, and very arrested, and you'll have burned nothing and hidden nothing and the true ledger will still be safe."

"With whom?"

"With you." He watched her face close like a door. "Don't shake your head yet. Hear the whole of it. If I'm taken on the line, they search me, and they find nothing, because I will carry nothing worth finding. The book stays here, under your floor, beneath your brother's photograph, in the one place no sergeant will rummage because nurses frighten even sergeants. If I reach the coast, I send word — a plain card, *the patient improves* — and within the week a laundry van comes to the infirmary, and the book travels in the clean linen, which nobody in the history of armies has ever inspected. And if I don't reach the coast—" he paused, and finished it honestly, because she deserved honesty more than comfort, "—if I don't reach the coast, then in ten years, when this war is a bad memory to everyone but us, you take the book out, and you give it to whoever is still asking questions. However long it takes. Promise me that part, at least."

"I don't want your ten years, Ivo. I want your four days."

"Then pray for my four days," he said gently, "and keep my ten years in reserve. That's the whole of the strategy. Faith forward, memory in reserve."

She was silent a long time. Through the store's small window the sky was going from slate to pearl, and the frost on the glass was beginning to weep down the panes, and somewhere out in the yard a driver cursed amiably at a mule with strong opinions about mornings. The world went on being ordinary, which Ivo found, oddly, steadying. Ordinary was what he was protecting, in the end. Ordinary, and the names of the men who used to live in it.

"If you die on this road," Amara said at last, "I will put your name in that ledger myself, and I will write underneath it *he was told*, in capital letters, so that posterity understands."

"That seems fair."

"And you'll eat a proper breakfast before you go. I won't have the man who saves the century fainting at a checkpoint for want of porridge."

"Your tenderness overwhelms me."

"It's not tenderness, it's logistics," said Sister Amara, and folded one last bandage with the violence of a woman strangling her own grief, and left him alone in the little room with the ticking stove and the weeping windows and the enormous, quiet weight of what remained to be done.

* * *

He packed in the hour before noon, when the ward was drowsy with broth and the officers' side of the garrison slept off the previous evening's toasts. Two shirts, his medical bag, the forged papers Pell had prepared for exactly this contingency — the man had been maddeningly thorough, and Ivo prayed now that thoroughness survived interrogation — and a letter of introduction signed with a colonel's stamp that Amara had lifted from the adjutancy eight months ago and kept, she confessed, for no reason except that she was the sort of woman who kept things.

At the bottom of his bag, wrapped in a spare cassock, went the true ledger. Small thing. Barely a hand's width of bound paper. Two years of nights, forty-one names — forty-one men who had existed, damn it, whatever the rolls said — and one name at the back, written fresh three days ago and still the heaviest entry in the book: *Ferro, Elias. Living.*

He had promised himself he would not open it again before leaving. He opened it anyway, one last time, in the grey light of his cell-like room, and ran his thumb down the columns of names like a priest counting beads, and felt what he always felt, which was not courage exactly but something adjacent to it — the stubborn animal refusal of a man who has decided that forgetting is the one sin he will not commit, not today, not at any price the century cares to name.

A soft knock. Amara stood in the doorway with bread and cheese wrapped in cloth and an expression that forbade comment.

"The laundry van leaves Thursday," she said. "If there's no card by Saturday, I go to the journalist myself."

"You don't know the address—"

"Pell wrote it on the inside of his cigarette case while pretending to be drunk. I read upside down professionally, Ivo. It's a nurse's second gift." She pressed the parcel into his hands. "Go by the ford, not the bridge. Doyle watches the bridge now; everyone knows it, including, presumably, Doyle, which is why he'll be at the depot instead. Go at dusk. And Ivo—"

She stopped. Whatever she had meant to say — and there were clearly several things queued behind her lips, arguments she had lost and blessings she didn't know how to phrase — she compressed it all, finally, into two words.

"Come home."

He kissed her cheek, which surprised them both, and went out into the falling dark with the book against his ribs, feeling its small heartbeat there like a second pulse, like the pulse of every man inside it, walking toward the ford and the coast and whatever the new century intended to do with the truth.

Behind him, the garrison lamps flickered on one by one, and somewhere in the officers' mess, uncorking wine for toasts he would never hear, Colonel Brase slept soundly, unaware that his entire carefully burning century was already walking out the door.
