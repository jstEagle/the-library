## 16. Second Checkpoint: Alone

The true ledger went into Amara's care wrapped in a linen bandage, the way one might carry a wound out of sight.

She sewed it into the lining of her nursing bag herself, sitting on my cot in the infirmary after lights-out, working by the light of a candle stub she had no business burning. Her needle went through the canvas with small, vicious precision, and every few stitches she would glance at me as though I were a patient who had insisted on leaving the ward against medical advice — which, in a manner of speaking, I was.

"You understand," she said, not looking up, "that if they search me and find this, I will say I have never seen you before in my life. I will say you are a fever dream I had during the typhus winter. I will be very convincing."

"That seems fair."

"It is not fair. It is strategy." She bit the thread. "Fairness is for people who can afford it. Hold still, this knot is difficult."

I held still. Outside, the frost had come down hard on the River Sante, and the ice on the shallows made a sound like distant crockery whenever the current shifted beneath it. Somewhere across the water the garrison clock struck eleven, and we both paused to let it finish, the way one pauses for a sneeze.

"There," Amara said. She weighed the bag in her hand, then handed it back to me — which startled me until I understood: she wanted me to feel the weight once more before I gave it up forever. The ledger was lighter than I remembered. Four years of dead men's names, and it weighed less than a loaf of bread.

"You'll take the duplicate," I said. "But listen to me. The duplicate is only half-true. It has the names through autumn, but not the orders, not the copies of what Brase signed. If anything happens—"

"Nothing will happen."

"Amara."

"Nothing will happen," she repeated, with the serene authority of a woman who has said that over open graves, "because you are going to walk onto that train wearing the face of a man who does not exist, and no inspector in the colonial service looks twice at a clerk. Clerks are furniture, Ivo. You have spent four years being invisible in an infirmary. This is simply the same trick performed standing up."

I could not argue with that. It is a strange thing, to spend years wishing someone would see you, and then to stake your life on their refusal to look.

* * *

I left before dawn, when the frost smoke lay so thick on the water that the bridge existed only as sound — the creak of its ropes, the drip of ice melting and refreezing along its rails. Amara walked me as far as the ford road, which was further than prudent, and stood with her hands folded into her sleeves like a woman waiting for a bus she had no intention of boarding.

"The name," she said. "Say it again."

"Lieutenant Josiah Crake. Fourth Sante Rifles. Aide-de-camp to Major Halloway, deceased."

"And he is dead?"

"He is dead." I had checked three times, because I am the sort of man who checks things three times, and because I owed him this much accuracy. Josiah Crake, twenty-six years old, died of wounds at Kessari Crossing — died twice, in fact: once in the mud outside the crossing, and again two months later when his name was struck from the rolls so that his widow's pension could be redirected to cover a quartermaster's shortfall. He existed now only in my ledger, in my handwriting, in ink that was already beginning to brown. And from tomorrow morning onward, he would exist in the flesh, walking through Marrow Junction with his coat buttoned wrong and his papers in order.

It seemed to me a kind of resurrection. It also seemed to me a kind of theft. I could not decide which, and I suspected I never would.

"His widow," Amara said. "Do you know where she is?"

"I wrote to her. In October. Unsigned." I hesitated. "I told her how he died. That he was carrying a wounded private when it happened. I thought she should know he wasn't alone."

Amara was quiet for a moment. Then: "You see, this is why I sew slowly. If I sewed quickly I would have time to think about what company I keep." She pressed the nursing bag — the false ledger, the decoy, the thing meant to be found — into my hands. "Go. And Ivo — when you pass the inspection, do not apologize. Men apologize when they lie, and inspectors smell apology the way horses smell fear. Be bored. Boredom is the great disguise."

"Boredom," I said. "Four years in a garrison infirmary. At last, my training tells."

* * *

Marrow Junction in winter was a place that seemed to have been designed by someone who hated travelers specifically. The depot sat in a bowl of frozen mud between two hills, with a single platform, a coal stove that heated approximately one cubic foot of air, and a stationmaster who had achieved a state of communion with his own boredom that bordered on the religious. The morning train to the coast came through at nine; the afternoon train at nine as well, weather permitting, which it rarely did.

I arrived at half past seven dressed in Crake's spare uniform — or rather, in a uniform adjusted to fit me, since Crake had been broader through the shoulders, and Amara had spent an evening letting out seams while muttering about the vanity of dead men. The tunic sat oddly on me, tight at the collar, loose at the waist, and I kept catching myself tugging at it the way a man tugs at clothes that belong to someone else, which of course they did, in every sense.

The papers were good. Pell had made them, and whatever else one said about Mr. Pell — and I had by then said a great deal of it, mostly silently — the man could forge documents the way other men write letters home: fluently, warmly, with convincing smudges. Lieutenant J. Crake, aide to the late Major Halloway, traveling to the coast on compassionate leave following his commanding officer's death. There was even a mourning band for my sleeve, cut from black ribbon Amara produced without explanation and refused to explain.

At eight o'clock the military police set up their inspection table at the mouth of the platform, and my stomach performed a maneuver I had previously only read about in naval accounts.

There were three of them. Two were young and merely thorough. The third was thorough in the way that ice is thorough — a thin lieutenant with spectacles and a ledger of his own, God help us all, in which he recorded each traveler's particulars in a hand so small it looked like the page had grown fur.

I joined the queue behind a farmer taking pig iron to the coast, and I did what Amara had taught me without knowing she was teaching it: I was bored. I looked at the inspection table the way one looks at rain. I thought about inventory lists. I thought about the particular tedium of counting bandages, and found, to my surprise, that I did not have to pretend.

"Lieutenant Crake." The spectacled officer did not look up. "Purpose of travel."

"Escorting effects," I said. "Major Halloway's personal effects, to his family. And leave." I let my voice go flat on the word *leave*, as though leave were an administrative burden I resented having to take.

"Effects manifest?"

I produced it. Pell had forged this too — a list of a dead major's possessions: watch, prayer book, two shirts, a miniature portrait, various mundane sorrows. The officer read it with his tiny pen hovering, and I stood there wearing another man's name and felt the strangest sensation come over me, which was not fear.

It was grief.

Because I knew these items. Not Halloway's — Crake's. I knew what a dead man's family waited for, because I had written to them, dozens of them, unsigned letters that said *he was not alone, he spoke of you, he did not suffer long* — and some of that was even true. Standing at that table, I was not impersonating a stranger. I was impersonating a man whose last words I had carried, whose mother I had lied to gently, whose name I had written in a book because the army had erased him and someone, someone had to refuse.

"Lieutenant Crake." The officer looked up at last. "You've been listed as deceased."

I want it recorded that I did not sway, although inwardly every structure I owned leaned hard against the wind. "I beg your pardon?"

"Clerical error, apparently." He turned his ledger toward me with a small dry satisfaction, the satisfaction of a man whose paperwork has caught something interesting. "Struck from the rolls in October. Pension redirection. Someone's been careless." He sucked his teeth. "Happens more than you'd think, at the end of a war. Everyone's in a hurry to burn things."

"They misspelled my name, I expect," I heard myself say. "Crake. They always get it wrong. It's C-r-a-k-e, not—" I waved a bored hand. "I've sent four letters about it. Nothing comes of letters out here."

Something in him softened — the universal softening of a bureaucrat meeting a fellow sufferer of bureaucracy. He made a note, a tiny furious note, presumably titled *clerical incompetence, again*, and stamped my papers.

"Coast train, second carriage. Keep your manifest visible at the customs house, they're worse than we are." He waved me through. "Give my regards to the century, Lieutenant."

I walked past him onto the platform, each step carefully ordinary, and it was only when I reached the coal stove — that one warm cubic foot — that I understood what I had done, standing there in the cold with a dead man's name in my mouth:

They had erased Josiah Crake so thoroughly that even his erasure was filed under clerical error. And I had just used that erasure — the insult of it, the ease of it — as my ticket through. He had carried a wounded private out of the mud at Kessari Crossing, and now he had carried me past an inspection table, and neither of us had been thanked.

"Well," I said quietly, to the stove, to the frost, to him, "we're even. Or we will be. When they read your name aloud."

* * *

The train was late, which gave me forty minutes to sit on a bench and rehearse being Josiah Crake, and to discover the unexpected difficulties of the role. It is one thing to invent a man; it is another to keep him fed. Every question became a small ambush.

*Where are you stationed?* — I had settled on "attached to garrison administration, lately," vague enough to survive scrutiny. *How long with the Major?* — "Two years." True enough; two years ago Crake had been alive, and I had been writing his name down for the first time, though neither of us knew it yet. *Family?* — and here I faltered, because Crake had a widow, and I had written to her, and there was a horror I had not anticipated in answering *no* on behalf of a man whose wife still set, I imagined, an extra cup in the cupboard out of habit.

In the end I answered nothing, because nobody asked. The farmer with the pig iron talked instead — at me, around me, possibly through me — about the price of coal, the dishonesty of coastal merchants, and the coming century, which he regarded with the personal suspicion of a man who had not cared for the present one either.

"They say they're burning everything at the garrisons," he offered, apropos of nothing. "Old records. Start clean, new century, new paper. My brother-in-law says they'll burn the debts too." He chuckled. "Imagine. All the debts, gone up the chimney."

"All the debts," I agreed, and thought of names going up with them, and smiled the smile of a man agreeing about weather.

The train came in at nine-twenty, hissing like something offended, and I boarded the second carriage with my manifest visible and my heart behaving badly. The compartment filled gradually: the pig-iron farmer, two supply officers arguing amiably about mules, a fat civilian merchant who fell asleep before the first hill. I took the corner seat, back to the engine, mourning band on my sleeve, and watched the frozen country unroll — the river appearing and disappearing between hills, the Sante going down to the sea the same way I was going, patiently, under cover of ordinary weather.

Somewhere behind me, miles back now, Amara was sitting in her office with the true ledger sewn into her bag, being deliberately unremarkable. Somewhere ahead, at the customs house, an officer waited to open every parcel I owned. And somewhere between those two points, in a cell or a cart or a grave, was Pell — taken at Marrow Junction, if the rumor that had reached us was true, carrying the duplicate I had planted on him, believing, perhaps, that I had abandoned him, or worse, understanding perfectly well that I hadn't.

I had used them both. Doyle, Pell, Crake, even Amara. I turned it over in my mind as the wheels took up their rhythm, looking for a way it wasn't true, and found none, and decided — in the way one decides things on night trains — that I would carry that too, along with everything else. The ledger had room. It was, in the end, a book about what men cost.

The conductor came through checking tickets, glanced at my mourning band, and murmured condolences for a major I had invented feelings about. I thanked him gravely. The supply officers offered me a flask. The century's last afternoon went gold and then grey over the hills, and somewhere past the sixth milestone out of Marrow Junction, the train slowed — not stopping, but slowing, the way trains slow when someone has flagged them down.

A lantern swung on the line ahead. Blue glass. Military.

The supply officers stopped talking about mules. The merchant snorted awake. And in the doorway of the next carriage, silhouetted against the lamp, I saw a figure step aboard with the unhurried confidence of a man who owns every checkpoint between here and the sea —

— and my stomach dropped through the floor of the world, because I knew that silhouette, that roll of the shoulders, that way of removing his cap as though the train ought to thank him for it.

Sergeant Doyle had escaped Marrow Junction too.

And he was walking toward my carriage.
