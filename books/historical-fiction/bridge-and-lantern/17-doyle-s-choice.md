## 17. Doyle's Choice

Sergeant Pius Doyle had interrogated men before. He had done it in tents at Kessari, in the guardhouse at Marrow Junction, once — memorably — in a pig shed outside Fort Aldren where the acoustics were terrible and the pig had opinions. He knew the trade: keep your voice level, keep your hands visible, let the silence do most of the work. Silence was cheaper than a rubber hose and twice as effective.

The trouble with the man called Pell was that he seemed to have read the same manual.

He sat in the cell at Marrow Junction depot with his coat folded neatly beside him — they'd taken his belt and laces but nobody had thought to take his dignity, and he wore what remained of it like a dress uniform — and when Doyle came in with the lamp and the stool and the official notebook, Pell simply said:

"You'll want the chair nearer the wall. The floor's wet by the bars."

Doyle looked down. The floor was wet by the bars.

"Thank you," he said, before he could stop himself, and then hated the thank-you for the rest of the night.

* * *

They had taken Pell on the eleven-forty, two stops out of the junction, a clean arrest that Doyle had not personally made because fate — or Colonel Brase's efficiency with telegraph wire — had transferred him ahead of the very man he was hunting. Doyle had arrived at the depot to find the job already done by a corporal who smelled of onions, and Pell already in the cell, and the satchel already inventoried.

The inventory troubled Doyle. It listed letters, papers, a tin cup, a spoon, a hymn book with three pages dog-eared. It did not list anything sewn into a coat lining, because nobody had thought to look into a coat lining, because nobody but Doyle knew that people who carry secrets sew them into linings. His brother Tom had taught him that, years ago, over a smuggled ham. *Anything worth carrying is worth sewing, Pius. Pockets are for honest men.*

Tom had died at Kessari Crossing. That fact sat in Doyle's chest every day like a swallowed stone, and tonight it rolled.

He waited until the corporal went off duty. Then he asked for Pell's coat — "inventory verification," he said, which was true the way a map is true; accurate, and missing everything that matters — and carried it to the lamp table while Pell watched him with an expression of polite interest, as though watching a man examine a horse he might buy.

Doyle worked the seam with a fingernail, then a penknife, feeling along the lining the way you'd feel along a riverbank for a drowned thing. And there it was. A flat bundle, oilcloth-wrapped, hand-sewn with stitches so fine they'd have shamed a seamstress. He cut three of them and drew out the contents.

A ledger. Small, cheaply bound, water-spotted. He opened it to the first page.

It was Ivo Okafor's handwriting. He recognized it from the infirmary requisitions — that careful, upright script, each letter standing at attention. But this wasn't requisitions. This was names. Columns of them. Dates. Regiments. Causes of death written in small, merciless phrases: *fever, untreated. Wound, left shoulder, bled. Killed after surrender, per witness.*

And there, on the third page: **Thomas Doyle, private, 4th Rifles. Killed at Kessari Crossing, 14th of September. Official record: missing, presumed desertion.**

The lamp flickered. Or perhaps Doyle did.

*Presumed desertion.* For two years he had carried the other version — the official version, the one delivered by a lieutenant who wouldn't meet his eyes — and he had hated his brother a little for it, quietly, shamefully, all these miles and months. Tom had run. Tom had left him holding the name.

The ledger said otherwise. The ledger said what some animal part of Doyle had always known and refused to know.

"You've found it," Pell said mildly, from behind the bars. "I wondered how long the stitching would fool anyone. I sewed it badly on purpose, you understand."

Doyle didn't turn around. "You sewed it badly."

"I sewed it so it would be found. Not immediately. By the right person, eventually." Pell's voice had the unhurried quality of a man laying cards face up. "The question, Sergeant, is whether you are the right person. I confess the evidence is promising so far. You haven't called for the corporal."

"Do you know what this is?" Doyle held up the ledger without looking at it. He couldn't look at it again just yet.

"It's a copy," Pell said. "A decoy. The real one is elsewhere — where, please don't ask, because I would lie badly and we're getting along." A pause. "Okafor gave it to me to carry. Or rather — " and here Pell laughed softly, a sound almost like embarrassment — "rather, Okafor let me believe I was carrying it, knowing I'd be taken, knowing whoever took me would search me, find nothing, search harder, find the duplicate, and report the matter closed. He used me as a false bottom. I used myself as one too, but that's a longer story."

Doyle turned then. The man behind the bars looked tired and cold and entirely unafraid, and Doyle understood, with the particular clarity that arrives only at the worst moments, that everyone in this affair had been moved across the board except him. Okafor had used him. The quiet nurse with the steady hands and the sorrowful eyes — Doyle had liked him, God help him, had shared bread with him at the garrison, had thought *here is an honest man in a dishonest place.*

"He passed inspection," Doyle said slowly, working it forward. "At Marrow Junction. On my watch or near it. As somebody's aide."

"Lieutenant Havelock's aide. Lieutenant Havelock being dead, and being on page four of that ledger under 'died of wounds officially recorded as killed in action, dates adjusted.'" Pell tilted his head. "You see the elegance. He traveled under one of his own names. Who checks papers against the dead? The dead have no objections to make."

Doyle sat down on the stool. The interrogation had somehow become a conversation, and the conversation had somehow become a confession, and he could not for the life of him remember authorizing either.

"Why tell me all this?" he said. "You're handing me enough to hang you twice."

"Because of how long you looked at your brother's name before you turned around." Pell said it gently, and that gentleness was worse than any threat. "I'm a clerk, Sergeant. Reading people is the whole of my profession. And I think you came into that cell tonight to decide something, and you brought the ledger question with you the way a man brings a Bible to a deathbed — hoping it will tell him what to do."

* * *

Doyle went out into the depot yard and stood in the cold and tried to be a soldier.

It should have been simple. That was the bitter joke of it. Twenty-two years in the army and the rules had always been simple: report what you find, follow your orders, keep your own name clean. He had built his whole life on the principle that a man could stay honest by staying inside the lines, even when the lines enclosed something rotten. Especially then. *Not my rot,* he'd told himself at Kessari. *I held the road. I didn't hold the order.*

But the ledger held the order. Page seven, if he remembered right — he'd looked only once and the page had burned itself in — *order signed Brase, countersigned Vane, withdrawal of support, 400 yards from ford, survivors subsequently.* The ink trailed off there because Ivo Okafor's hand, so upright everywhere else, had apparently failed him at that sentence, and resumed three lines down with a single word: *afterward.*

Tom had been 400 yards from the ford. Tom had been exactly where the order abandoned him.

Doyle walked the length of the yard twice. A sentry saluted him and he returned it mechanically. Somewhere a train whistled, long and mournful, the sound the century made when it cleared its throat.

Here was what he knew now, laid out like kit for inspection: the nurse had played him. The stranger had played him. Both of them had looked at Sergeant Pius Doyle — reliable, rule-keeping, line-standing Doyle — and seen a tool, or a fool, or a door. And both of them had been right about the door part, because here he stood in the cold with the choice in his hands, and the choice had been in his hands since the moment he read *presumed desertion* and felt two years of hatred curdle into grief and shame.

If he reported the ledger, it burned on January the first with everything else. Tom's name would go into the fire as a deserter and come out as smoke, and there would be no correcting it ever, not in this world.

If he didn't —

If he didn't, he was committing treason. Not the dramatic kind, with trumpets; the small kind, the paper kind, the kind that happened in depots at midnight and never made speeches. He thought of the pig shed at Fort Aldren and almost laughed. He'd interrogated a man through a pigshed wall for stealing army bacon, and here he was deciding to steal an empire's conscience.

There was a third option, of course. There was always a third option, and it was the worst one: report it, and spend the rest of his life telling himself he'd had no choice. Doyle had watched men do that. They aged quickly.

He went back inside.

* * *

Pell was asleep when Doyle unlocked the cell — genuinely asleep or performing it, the man was hard to read either way — and woke the way soldiers wake, instantly, saying nothing, watching.

"The corporal changes watch at four," Doyle said quietly. "He takes twenty minutes for his tea. The yard gate sticks unless you lift it as you push — lift, then push, mind, or it'll sing like a church bell. There's a milk cart at half past four, driver named Okonkwo, deaf as a post and blind to anything he isn't paid to see. Two shillings gets you to the coast road."

Pell sat up slowly. "Sergeant — "

"Don't." Doyle held up a hand. "Don't thank me and don't explain and don't tell me where the true ledger is, because if I don't know it, I can say so honestly, and I intend to say things honestly for approximately one more hour of my career." He set the duplicate ledger on the cot. "This stays with you. It'll be found destroyed — burned in the stove, partially, pages recovered, identification confirmed by me. I'll write the report myself. I've a fair hand for official lies; I've read enough of them."

"Why?" Pell asked. Just that. No artistry in it, no clerk's cleverness. Why.

Doyle looked at the ledger on the cot, at the oilcloth, at the neat stitches he himself had cut.

"My brother's in there," he said. "Page three. My brother's been in there the whole time, and I didn't know it, and neither did anyone else, and if that book burns, then the last true thing anyone ever says about Thomas Doyle is a lie told by cowards." He found, to his surprise, that his voice stayed level throughout, which was more than he managed in the confessional at Easter. "I spent two years being ashamed of him. I won't spend thirty more being ashamed of myself."

Pell stood and, absurdly, straightened his ruined coat as though dressing for an audience, and then extended his hand. Doyle shook it. The man's grip was a clerk's grip, ink-stained and warm.

"For what it's worth," Pell said, "Okafor didn't use you carelessly. He spoke of you — before Marrow Junction — as the one soldier he trusted to be exactly what he appeared to be. He said honesty was rare enough to plan around. I believe he meant it as a compliment."

"He planned around my honesty," Doyle said. "That's the compliment, is it?"

"It's the highest one I know." Pell smiled faintly. "Most men can't even be reliably bribed."

At ten past four, Doyle began his report. At twenty past four, a figure crossed the yard in the dark, lifted the gate, pushed, and was gone, and the gate did not sing. At half past, a milk cart rattled out through the coast road, its driver humming tunelessly to a passenger he had not seen board and would not remember.

Doyle fed four pages of a cheap ledger into the depot stove and watched them curl. He wrote *destroyed by fire during search, remains consistent with subject's described documents* in his best administrative hand, and signed it, and blotted it, and sat for a moment with his face in his hands.

Then he took out his personal notebook — a real one, private, the kind pockets were for — and on a fresh page, in his own careful script, he wrote:

**Thomas Doyle, private, 4th Rifles. Died at Kessari Crossing. No desertion. Remembered by his brother.**

It wasn't much. It wasn't evidence. But it was a start, and a man had to start somewhere, and besides — he had one more letter to write tonight, to a garrison downriver, warning a certain sergeant's replacement to keep a respectful distance from a certain bridge where a certain nurse would soon be traveling.

Some treasons, he was learning, came in families.

He sealed the report. Outside, toward the coast, the sky was going grey at the edges, the color of paper waiting to be written on.
