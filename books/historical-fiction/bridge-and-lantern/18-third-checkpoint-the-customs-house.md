## 18. Third Checkpoint: The Customs House

The sea announced itself before I saw it. For two days the road had been climbing and falling through grey pine country, and then the trees thinned all at once, as if they had agreed among themselves to step aside, and there it was: a long cold ribbon of light lying flat against the sky, smelling of salt and tar and something older than either.

I had not seen the sea in four years. I had forgotten that it moved even when the wind did not — that it was never entirely still, never entirely asleep, the way the river at the garrison sometimes seemed to be. The Sante lay under ice for half the year, patient and obedient. This water had no master.

"You're staring," I told myself aloud, because there was no one else to talk to, and because my voice sounded better than the silence. Three days of solitary travel will do that to a man. You begin narrating your own life like a cheap novel, and then you catch yourself doing it and feel ashamed, and then you do it again anyway.

I walked down into the port town of Halloway Cross with my collar up and my satchel light. That was the whole trick of it, Amara had said, pressing her hand flat against my chest one last time on the garrison steps — *carry nothing they would want.* The true ledger lay sewn into the hem of her apron back at the Sante, wrapped in oilcloth, sleeping among the lint and the laudanum bottles as if it were just another piece of hospital property. What I carried instead was paper — dozens upon dozens of loose pages copied in my own careful hand, folded small, and a doctor's bag full of envelopes that looked, to any reasonable eye, like medicine going out to patients who could not come in.

And what I carried that could not be hidden at all was the name I had stolen. Lieutenant Edmund Harrow, aide-de-camp, dead these fourteen months of a fever nobody recorded. His papers rode in my inner pocket, warm against my heart like a second heartbeat. I had written his name in my ledger myself, two winters ago, in ink I mixed from soot and gin. Now he wrote himself through my hands. It was the strangest work I had ever done — walking around inside a dead man's coat, breathing with a dead man's lungs, signing a dead man's name at gates he would never have reached alive.

*You keep lists,* Pell had said to me that first feverish night. *Well, Mr. Harrow keeps lists now too,* I thought. Perhaps somewhere in whatever country the dead inhabit, Edmund Harrow was keeping a list of the things I did in his name, and perhaps he forgave me, and perhaps he laughed.

* * *

Halloway Cross consisted of perhaps forty buildings huddled together against the wind, one church with a crooked weathervane, three public houses, and — the reason I had come four hundred miles — a customs house squatting at the end of the pier like a toad in a good coat.

Through its windows I could see the mail sacks. That was what undid me, standing there in the street with my breath steaming. Not the officer, not the soldiers, not the notice pasted beside the door announcing rewards for deserters and seditionists. The sacks. Great slumped canvas bodies of them, waiting by the door for the mail boat that came, according to a fishwife I asked, "when it comes, love, weather permitting, which it mostly doesn't."

Inside those sacks was London. Inside those sacks was a journalist named Mr. Calloway whose address I knew by heart and whose face I had never seen. Inside those sacks, God willing, was every name.

"Move along or come in," said the customs officer from the doorway, "but pick one."

He was a small man, round as a bollard, with side-whiskers of an astonishing orange colour and the weary eyes of someone who had been standing in doorways telling people to move along since roughly the reign of George the Third. His cap badge marked him as customs; his hands, I noticed, marked him as something else. No sailor's hands. Clerk's hands. Ink-stained, precise, the fingers of a man who read things for a living.

I went in.

The interior smelled of lamp oil and damp forms. A soldier stood by the far wall picking his teeth with great dedication, and behind a counter scarred by decades of cargo crates sat the orange-whiskered man, who produced a ledger of his own — everyone in this war kept ledgers, it occurred to me; the only difference was what we chose to write in them — and looked me over the way a horse-trader looks over a lame mare.

"Purpose of travel?"

"Medical courier," I said, and set the bag on the counter. "Under orders of the garrison surgeon, River Sante. Medicines and correspondence for transport south."

"Name?"

"Lieutenant Harrow. Aide to Colonel Brase." I produced the papers with exactly the right degree of boredom. Boredom, I had learned on the road, is the finest disguise there is. Guilt makes men interesting, and interesting men get searched.

The officer — his tag read **MR. TIBBS, EXAMINING OFFICER** — read the papers front and back, twice, running one stained finger beneath each line. Then he set them down, squared their edges neatly, and looked up at me with genuine regret.

"Very well, Lieutenant Harrow. You'll understand that everything gets opened."

"Of course."

"All of it." He tapped the medical bag. "New orders came down from the coast command a fortnight ago. Every parcel, every packet, every letter leaving the district gets opened and read before it goes in a sack. There's a man—" he jerked his thumb toward a curtained alcove from which came the sound of someone clearing his throat with tremendous self-importance, "—who reads the correspondence. I do the parcels. We've caught two spies already." He said this with the flat pride of a man who has caught no spies at all but enjoys the phrase.

My stomach performed a slow, deliberate revolution, the way a ship turns in a heavy swell.

"That seems thorough," I managed.

"It seems *ruinous*," said Tibbs, and for one mad moment I thought he had guessed everything. But his eyes were on the mail sacks, and his expression was that of a drowning man looking at more water. "Do you know how many letters go out of this port in a week? Four hundred. Five hundred. Christmas just past, six hundred a day, every one of them to be opened, read, logged, resealed, and entered in triplicate." He pulled a form book toward himself and glared at it. "There are men in London who believe the century can be audited. I have begun to fear they are correct."

Something loosened in my chest — carefully, the way you loosen a knot you cannot afford to snap. Here was a thing I had not counted on: not every instrument of the machine hated what it was being used for. Some of them merely resented the paperwork.

"I'll need a receipt for the medicines," I said. "The surgeon's very particular."

"The surgeon can write to the Admiralty and complain, and the Admiralty can lose the letter, which is what Admiralties are *for*." Tibbs beckoned. "Bag on the table, Lieutenant. Let's see what the River Sante is sending out into the world."

* * *

The alcove reader was named Prew, and he was everything Tibbs was not: young, keen, and armed with a magnifying glass he used with the solemnity of a bishop handling relics. While Tibbs worked through my bag with surprising gentleness — lifting each bottle to the lamp, sniffing the stoppers, muttering "quinine, honest quinine, well I never" — Prew sat across the room with a growing pile of my letters and the magnifying glass, and read.

I stood between them and did arithmetic.

Twenty-two packets. Twenty-two names, each folded inside a medicine envelope of the kind the garrison used by the thousand — brown paper, printed header, the little box for dosage instructions left blank. Each addressed to Mr. J. Calloway, care of a journal office in Fleet Street, London. To any inspector, twenty-two envelopes of medicine bound for a newspaper office would look like nonsense. That had been the idea: nonsense is camouflage. But Prew was reading them, and Prew was young, and young men with magnifying glasses find nonsense suspicious precisely because it fails to make sense.

"He writes very small," Prew observed, holding a page to the lamp. His voice had the bright, pleased edge of a man who believes he has found something. My heart went down into my boots and began digging.

Tibbs didn't look up from the quinine. "They all write small. Doctors write like spiders drowning. Priests too. It's a professional affliction."

"This isn't a doctor's hand." Prew adjusted the glass. "It's too regular. Look — every letter the same height. And there's a sort of... a list-like quality. He numbers things."

The room went very quiet, or I did. Somewhere outside a gull screamed once and gave up.

"What sorts of things does he number?" Tibbs asked mildly, still examining bottles.

"Supplies, sir, it looks like. Ward stores. 'Lint, twelve rolls. Splints, four.' It's an inventory." Prew frowned at the page. "Addressed to a newspaper."

Ah, bless you, Ivo Okafor, I thought, with a gratitude close to religious feeling. Two years of writing supply lists into a ledger to disguise what the ledger held, and here the habit saved me a third time. Every page I had copied bore, at its head, three lines of perfectly innocent ward inventory — real inventory, actual splints, honest lint — before the first name appeared below. Camouflage above, evidence beneath. Any reader who stopped at the lint would see only a fussy quartermaster. Any reader who continued—

"Perhaps the newspaper publishes a medical column," Tibbs suggested.

"Why would a medical column want splint counts from a colonial river?"

"Why does *anyone* want anything? Man's dying of boredom in a garrison, he writes long dull letters to London, the editor prints the dull parts and burns the rest. Half the mail I read is exactly that." Tibbs finally turned, took the page from Prew's fingers without asking, and studied it. Then he looked at me.

It was not a searching look. It was worse. It was the look of a tired, intelligent clerk who has spent thirty years reading other people's paper and knows, in his ink-stained soul, that paper lies the way rivers run downhill — constantly, and for excellent reasons.

"Your surgeon," he said, "numbers his lint?"

"Meticulously," I said. "He once made me recount a crate of bandages three times because the total ended in seven instead of zero."

That, at least, was true. Sister Amara had made me do it, and I had complained about it for a week, and she had said — I heard her voice as clearly as if she stood beside me in this lamplit room — *"Precision is a virtue, Ivo, because most days it's all the virtue you can afford."*

Tibbs grunted. It might have been a laugh. He set the page down, and his finger rested on it a moment longer than it needed to, and then he squared the edges again — he liked squaring edges, this man, it was his form of prayer — and pushed the whole stack back toward me.

"Reseal them," he said. "Prew, give the lieutenant his wax. Lieutenant, you'll sign the examination log — item count, destination, your name and rank. Standard form. Then your letters go in tonight's sack, and you can stand on the pier and watch the boat take them if you like. It's a long wait, in this wind, but some people find it satisfying."

I signed the log as Edmund Harrow for the last time, and my hand did not shake, and I have rarely been prouder of anything.

* * *

The resealing took an hour. Wax, seal, fold, wax again — Prew supervised each envelope with his magnifying glass, and I let him, and smiled at his findings, and felt each click of the seal like a small door closing behind a fleeing child. Twenty-two times. By the twentieth my fingers ached and my face ached from smiling and the lamp had burned low, and Tibbs had given up pretending to inspect anything and was telling me about his daughter in Portsmouth, who was studying to be a schoolmistress and wrote him letters "smaller than yours, Lieutenant, and twice as numbered."

"She sounds formidable," I said.

"She is. She takes after the postmistress on her mother's side." He sighed with enormous contentment. "When this audit madness ends I shall retire and grow cabbages and read nothing but seed catalogues, which lie less than government forms."

At last the envelopes went into the sack — all twenty-two, dropped in a single loose armful like laundry, landing among bills of lading and love letters and a grocer's complaint about cheese, indistinguishable, anonymous, safe. Or nearly safe. Or as safe as anything becomes once you surrender it to the care of strangers, which is to say: out of my hands at last, which is its own species of safety, and its own species of grief.

Tibbs walked me to the door. The wind off the water hit us both and he tucked his whiskers into his collar with practiced dignity.

"You'll be staying the night, Lieutenant? The Anchor holds rooms."

"No. I'll wait for the boat."

"Thought you might." He looked out at the black water, where a lantern swung on the pier-end post, throwing its small stubborn circle of light on the waves. "Odd thing," he said, conversationally, to the sea rather than to me. "Thirty years opening other folks' post. You develop an eye. Most of it's nothing — laundry lists and complaints about the weather. But once in a great while a letter comes through that matters. You can feel it in the paper. Heavier than it looks." He shrugged. "Tonight's sack feels heavy."

My heart stopped, politely, and waited.

"Christmas arrears," I offered.

"Christmas arrears," Tibbs agreed comfortably. "Well. Good luck to your surgeon and his lint, Lieutenant Harrow. Mind how you go." And he closed the door of the customs house behind me with the soft finality of a man closing a book he has decided, for reasons of his own, not to finish.

* * *

The mail boat came at half past eleven, late, its single light bobbing up out of the dark like something the sea had decided, after consideration, to return. Two deckhands hauled the sacks aboard one by one, and I stood at the pier-end beneath the lantern and watched each canvas body swing up over the rail and disappear below. Ordinary. Heavy. Nothing.

I counted them. All accounted for. The last sack went over the rail, and a voice below shouted something about the tide, and the light began to draw away, and I stood in the wind with an empty medical bag and a dead man's papers and the strangest sensation I have ever known: that I was lighter by twenty-two names, and heavier by every one of them, simultaneously, and would remain so for the rest of my life.

Somewhere south of here, a train. After that, a ferryman, and a bridge with a lantern on it, and a road home I already suspected I would not finish.

I touched my cap brim to the departing light — Edmund Harrow's last salute, I hoped, though I should have known better than to hope anything that December — and turned toward the Anchor, and the single warm window, and whatever the night intended to send me next.

It sent Sergeant Doyle.
