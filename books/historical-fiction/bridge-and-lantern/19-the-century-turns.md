## 19. The Century Turns

The last day of the century arrived the way most difficult days arrive at River Sante — with fog, a shortage of clean linens, and Sister Amara telling me to eat something before I argued with anyone.

"Toast," she said, pressing a heel of bread into my hand as though it were medicine, which in a sense it was. "You've been living on ledger ink and worry for three weeks. It shows."

"Does it?"

"It shows in your handwriting. Even your forgeries have gotten shaky." She said this lightly, but her eyes did not join in the joke. She had not been light-eyed since Pell was taken at Marrow Junction. None of us had.

I bit the bread. It tasted of sawdust and kindness, which is to say garrison bread. Outside, the fog sat on the river like something sleeping, and beyond it the bridge stood with its lantern already lit, though the sun would not set for hours. The ferryman kept it burning early on feast days. Perhaps he felt, as we all did, that this particular evening deserved a head start.

Word had come down from headquarters two days prior, read aloud at morning muster in Colonel Brase's best parade voice: by order of the General Staff, all records of the preceding administration were to be ceremonially destroyed at midnight, December the thirty-first, eighteen hundred ninety-nine, in honor of the new century. Ledgers, muster rolls, correspondence, requisitions — everything older than the year itself. A fresh page for a fresh hundred years. The men had cheered. The clerks had not. I had watched young Corporal Whitcombe, who spent his days copying supply manifests in a hand like bird tracks, go pale as a christening gown.

"They're going to burn my whole life's work," Whitcombe whispered to me afterward. "Six years of ink. Six years."

"You'll make new work," I said.

"That's not the same thing at all, is it, Mr. Okafor?"

It is not. That is rather the entire point, and the entire crime.

* * *

The preparations for the burning had begun at dawn and taken on, by afternoon, the air of a parish fete. Soldiers stacked crates of documents in the yard behind the commissary like men building a bonfire for midwinter, which in every respect but legality they were. Someone had strung bunting between the mess hall and the armoury — actual bunting, red and white, as though paper needed decorating before it burned. Sergeant-Major Hollis directed the stacking with the solemnity of a man laying out a cathedral foundation. I watched him pause over one crate, open the lid, and remove — I am nearly certain — a single folder, which he tucked inside his coat with the practiced discretion of a magician.

I did not blame him. By nightfall half the garrison had performed some version of the same small theft. Orders one wished to forget; orders one could not afford to. The century's paperwork was being sorted into two piles: the shameful and the sentimental, though I suspect the categories overlapped considerably.

Amara found me at the infirmary window watching this.

"They'll want medical staff down there tonight," she said. "Brase insists. He wants the whole garrison present. Witnesses, he calls them." Her mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "A fire needs witnesses the way a lie needs listeners."

"And we are both fluent listeners."

"We are." She lowered her voice. The ward behind us held four sleeping men and the smell of carbolic. "Ivo. The copies you sent — they went out with the Tuesday post?"

"With Ferro's help. In medicine envelopes, addressed to a teaching hospital in London that owes me a favor. From there—" I stopped. Even now, even with her, I could not finish sentences about it. Some precautions you keep wrapped even from allies, the way you keep a wound covered even from the surgeon who dressed it.

"Good," she said, and then, softer: "I still think you should burn yours."

"I know you do."

"The ledger. The original. Whatever's gone to London is gone; nothing can unsend it. But if they find that book on you, Ivo, they won't court-martial you. Brase will simply lose you, the way Vane was lost. Quietly. In the fog somewhere."

She was right, and I told her so, and she looked at me the way she looks at soldiers who remove their bandages early.

"I tended those names," I said. "Some of them I held while they went. If the book burns and the copies fail, then those men died twice, and I was the instrument of the second death. I'd rather be the instrument of my own."

Amara was quiet a moment. Then she took the empty bread crust from my hand, brushed the crumbs from my sleeve like a mother, and said, "Then wear your good coat tonight. If a man is going to be foolish, he should be foolish in wool."

* * *

The fires were lit at eleven o'clock, an hour before midnight, so that the records might burn precisely as the century turned — a piece of theatre Brase had insisted upon personally, and which I will grant him this: he understood ceremony the way some men understand weather, instinctively and without mercy.

The whole garrison assembled. Four hundred men drawn up in the yard, breath rising in silver columns, faces lit orange from below like a congregation at candlelight service. Three great pyres blazed where the crates had been stacked, and the heat came off them in waves you could feel through your greatcoat, and above them rose sparks and ash and — once, horribly, briefly — a whole page, intact, lifted on the thermal current, turning over and over as it climbed. I watched it rise. So help me, every man in the front rank watched it rise. It hung against the black sky for a full three seconds, white and legible, and though I was too far away to read a word of it, I knew somehow that it was a muster roll, and that somewhere on it was a name no one would speak again.

Then it caught, midair, and came down as flame, and the band struck up a march, because God forbid a man should think.

Brase stood on the commissary steps in dress uniform. Promotion had come through that very week — brevet colonel to full brigadier, effective with the new year, the King's Birthday list arriving early like a bill before the meal — and he wore the news in his shoulders. He gave a speech. Of course he gave a speech. Brase would have given a speech at his own drowning.

"Men of Sante," he began, and his voice carried beautifully off the commissary wall; I have always suspected he rehearsed against it. "Tonight we close a book. Not merely the ledgers behind me, not merely the files and requisitions and returns of six years' soldiering — but a chapter in the history of this empire. The old century takes with it its quarrels, its confusions, its necessary sorrows. The new century asks only our clarity. Our purpose. Our forward faces."

Necessary sorrows. I turned the phrase over like a coin I suspected of being shaved. Behind him the third pyre shifted and collapsed in a gout of sparks, and the crowd murmured, and Brase waited for it to settle with the patience of a man who knew the fire was punctuating his remarks better than any artillery could.

"There are those," he continued, "who say a soldier should look back. Who cherish what they call memory. But I tell you memory is a poor quartermaster — it issues grief where courage is wanted, and doubt where discipline has been paid in full." Scattered approval. "We do not forget our fallen. We honor them. We carve their names where they belong, in stone, in bronze, in the grateful silence of a job completed. And the paper—" he gestured, a wide sweep, taking in three pyres and six years, "—the paper was never the thing, gentlemen. The paper was only the shadow the thing cast. And shadows, when the lamp moves on, ought not to follow us into the new room."

He said it warmly. That was the unforgivable part. Any tyrant can burn a library with a sneer; it takes a certain gift to do it smiling, to make four hundred cold men feel warmed by the flames eating the evidence of what was done to their friends.

And then he did the worse thing.

"But before the century turns," Brase said, "I wish to recognize the quiet heroes. The men whose service never makes the dispatches." His eyes found me in the third rank — I felt it physically, like a hand on the collar. "Corporal Okafor. Front and center, if you please."

Four hundred heads turned. My boots moved; I give them credit; they moved without consulting me.

"Corporal Okafor has served this garrison's hospital through fever seasons, through the winter crossings, through more long nights than any man here knows," Brase said, and clapped a hand on my shoulder, and the warmth of it went through my coat like the pyre-heat, all surface and no depth. "I have seen him work. I have seen him write — reports, returns, observations. A careful man. A thorough man. A man who understands that what is written down properly may be trusted, and what is written improperly—" here he smiled at the ranks, and some of them smiled back, and I stood very still under his hand, "—is best consigned to the same century that produced it. Corporal, the garrison thanks you. The new year thanks you. Lead us into it with your example."

The men cheered. They cheered *me*. Ferro, who had come back to light duty with his arm in a sling and a limp he pretended not to have, was cheering hardest of all, and I could not look at him. Amara, two ranks back, was not cheering, and I could not look at her either, so I looked at the fire.

And there — I will record it here because I recorded it nowhere else — I felt the bitterest irony of my life assemble itself, neat as a kit inspection: Brigadier Brase, his hand on my shoulder, thanking me publicly, sincerely, for exactly the quality he feared most in me. Thoroughness. A man who writes things down. He was honoring his own destruction and calling it loyalty, and the terrible comedy of it was that he was not entirely wrong to — because everything he praised me for was true. I *was* careful. I *was* thorough. I had learned both at his expense.

"Thank you, sir," I heard myself say. "It has been — an education."

Something flickered behind his eyes. Whether he heard the double edge or merely the cold air, I could not tell you. He squeezed my shoulder once, hard, and released me back into the ranks, and the band played again, and the century went on dying.

* * *

At five minutes to midnight the chaplain prayed, briefly, for peace, which drew a respectful silence and, I noticed, one or two glances toward the pyres that suggested the prayer had been filed under fiction.

At midnight the church bells of the mission across the river began — first Saint Agnes's cracked old bell, then the smaller ones joining, then, faint and late as ever, the bell at the ford chapel, so that the sound rolled over the water in ragged waves, unsynchronized, human, each bell insisting on its own count of the hour. The men embraced. Hats went up. Someone produced a barrel of the good rum and the sergeants made only token efforts to prevent its consumption. On the bridge, far off, the lantern swung gently in the wind off the water, and I thought of the ferryman keeping his own midnight, unlisted, unthanked, moving whatever needed moving from one bank to the other regardless of what year it was.

Ash fell on the celebration like grey snow. It settled on shoulders, on caps, in the rum cups. A private near me wiped it from his sleeve and said, contentedly, "Clean slate, eh, Corporal?" and I said, "Very clean," and we both drank.

Amara found me at the yard's edge, where the light from the pyres gave way to the blue dark.

"He thanked you," she said.

"He thanked me thoroughly."

"Ivo—"

"I know." I watched the fires. They had begun to settle into themselves, great red heaps now rather than towers, and by morning they would be warm gravel and the wind would take the rest. "Amara, listen to me. Whatever happens in the new year — whatever comes across that bridge or down from Marrow Junction — you were at the fete tonight with everyone else. You toasted the brigadier. You sang."

"I don't sing."

"Pretend harder than usual, then." I took her hand, brief as a thief. "The envelopes are gone. The rest is only waiting."

She squeezed back, hard, and let go, and returned to the light with her chin up like a woman walking into weather.

I stayed at the dark edge a while longer. Behind me the bells finished their argument and agreed, at last, on midnight; somewhere in the yard four hundred men sang *Auld Lang Syne*, badly, warmly, most of them holding hands with men they'd have died for and couldn't name a relation of, and I confess the song undid me slightly, because it asks, after all, whether old acquaintance should be forgot — and there I stood, having built two years of my life on answering *no*, never, not one, not even the smallest name, not even mine.

The last thing the fires ate, just before they sank, was the correspondence. I saw the crates go in — private letters, diaries, the small paper shame — and I thought of Vane's looping hand somewhere in those flames, or copies of it, or orders that referenced it, the web of a vanished man burning strand by strand while the spider accepted congratulations on the steps.

Except that it wasn't all burning. That was the thought I carried away from the yard like a coal in my pocket, cupped and alive: it was not *all* burning. Somewhere south, in a London sorting house or a doctor's study, several ounces of paper sat in the dark, patient as seeds. The century had turned. The order had been obeyed. And it had changed nothing that mattered.

I walked back to the infirmary past the smouldering pyres. The watchfire smell would be in our clothes for weeks; Amara would complain of it; the men would call it the smell of the new age. I let myself believe, for nearly the length of the walk, that the worst was behind us — that the evidence was safe, the names were loose in the world, and all that remained was to grow old quietly around the truth.

I was halfway down the corridor when I saw the figure waiting outside the ward door, cap in hand, lamplight catching the stripe on his sleeve.

Sergeant Doyle.

"Ivo," he said — my Christian name, which he had never used before, and which landed between us like a dropped instrument. "We need to talk. Before someone else does."
