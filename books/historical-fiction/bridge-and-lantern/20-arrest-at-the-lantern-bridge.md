## 20. Arrest at the Lantern Bridge

The last morning of the century arrived wearing grey like a borrowed coat.

I had slept badly, which was to say I had slept exactly as much as a man sleeps who has spent three days waiting for a knock that he knows is coming. It is a strange thing, awaiting one's own arrest. The body does it better than the mind. The body says: eat, wash, mend that button on your cuff, the tea will be cold otherwise. The mind says: everything you have done for two years ends today, and possibly before lunch.

I ate the porridge. I mended the button. Sister Amara watched me do both from across the ward with an expression I could not at first decipher, and then could, and wished I hadn't. It was the expression of a woman watching a man fold his own shroud and press the creases neatly.

"You could take the coast road," she said quietly, pretending to inventory bandages. "The northern one. Doyle won't think of it until noon."

"Doyle thought of it in November," I said. "He's simply been kind enough to wait."

"And you've been fool enough to stay."

"Yes," I agreed. "Fool enough." I tied off the thread with my teeth, which Amara considers barbarous, and which is therefore one of my small private pleasures. "Amara — if I run, they burn me as a fugitive and the ledger becomes the mad scribbling of a coward who fled. If I stand on a bridge in full view of God and Sergeant Doyle, I become something else entirely. A story. Stories travel further than men do."

"Stories don't serve two years in Kessel Prison," she said.

"No," I admitted. "They have all the luck."

She came around the table then and did something she had never done in four years of shared wards and shared winters: she took my face in both her hands, nurse's hands, cool and certain, and looked at me the way one looks down a patient's throat — thoroughly, and without sentiment, and lovingly anyway.

"You wrote my brother's name," she said. "Third page, second column. You didn't know me then. You didn't know him. You wrote him anyway."

"I write everyone," I said. "It's practically a compulsion. The doctors should look into it."

"God keep you, Ivo Marchetti." She released me, brisk again, already turning away so I would not see whatever her face did next. "And leave the lantern lit. He'll be coming in the dark."

* * *

I want to set down honestly how I spent those last free hours, because history — if it ever bothers to read this far — will assume I spent them heroically, drafting final thoughts or composing letters of noble defiance. In fact I spent them doing rounds. Private Okafor had a fever that broke around ten o'clock and asked, for the fourth time, whether his mother's letter had come, and I lied to him for the fourth time, gently, and hated myself in the familiar comfortable way. Young Whitlock needed his dressing changed. A sergeant with a splinter the size of a church nail insisted it was a wound and required sympathy, which I administered in generous doses.

It was ordinary work. It was the work. And somewhere in the middle of it I understood, standing there with iodine on my fingers, that I was not saying goodbye to any of it. Whatever happened on the bridge, the work would go on without me or with me, and either way the names were safe. That is a strange freedom, knowing the important thing is already done. It makes the unimportant things very clear. It makes porridge taste like porridge.

At half past three the light began to fail — December does its failing early and without apology — and I put on my coat, and took the lantern from its hook by the dispensary, and walked out through the garrison gate past the sentry, who saluted me because I was a nurse and nurses are saluted by everyone and respected by no one, an arrangement I had always found fair.

The Lantern Bridge crossed the Sante at its narrowest point, a hundred and forty feet of weathered planking and iron rail that the engineers kept promising to replace and the ferryman kept praying they wouldn't, since his living depended on the bridge being perpetually one bad winter from collapse. At its center stood the lantern itself — old, black-green with salt, tended by whoever remembered — because the river fog came up fast after dark and the ferryman Pollo claimed, with complete sincerity, that a light on the water meant the difference between finding the far bank and finding the other side of forever.

Pollo was on his landing below, mending a net that did not need mending, which was his way of thinking.

"You're walking slow, Nurse," he called up.

"I'm walking deliberately," I said. "There's a difference."

"There is," he allowed. "One of them gets you somewhere."

I hung the lantern on its hook at the bridge's center and trimmed the wick, and then, having nothing left to arrange, I arranged myself instead — hands folded, back to the rail, facing the garrison road — and waited for Sergeant Declan Doyle to come and arrest me.

* * *

He came on foot, alone, at twenty minutes past four, with the fog just beginning to rise from the water in pale ropes. He walked the way he always walked, like a man carrying a sack of grievances up a hill he had personally purchased, and he stopped six paces onto the bridge, where the planks still creaked under military boots and civilians' alike.

"Nurse Okafor," he said.

"Sergeant Doyle." I nodded toward the town behind him. "You didn't bring anyone."

"Didn't need anyone." He shifted his weight. Doyle shifting his weight is a whole conversation; most men require words. "You knew this was coming."

"I've known since Marrow Junction. Since before. I'd begun to feel neglected, actually. A man plans his own arrest for three days and nothing — not a knock, not a summons. One begins to wonder if one matters."

"Don't make this clever, Ivo." He used my Christian name the way he did perhaps four times a year, each occasion memorable. "I have orders. Colonel Brase's own hand. Detain and hold for transport to Kessel."

"Kessel," I repeated. "In winter. For two years, if I'm fortunate, and indefinitely if I'm not."

"That's the shape of it."

"Well." I looked out over the rail at the darkening water. Below us, Pollo's lantern came alive on his landing, a small yellow eye in the fog. "Then let's not pretend either of us enjoys the ceremony. Say the words, Sergeant, and we'll walk in together like civilized men."

But Doyle did not move. He stood there working his jaw, a man chewing on something that refused to go down, and finally he took one step closer and lowered his voice to the register he probably believed was private, though the fog carried voices on the water the way a tray carries cups.

"Pell got out," he said.

"I'd heard a rumor to that effect."

"He got out because I opened the door. And I told Brase the ledger burned at Junction. Both of those are treason, and I have committed them, and now they're sending me to arrest *you*, which means somebody above Doyle suspects Doyle, which means I'm hanging by a thread same as you." He exhaled through his nose, long and slow, steam rising off him like a kettle. "My brother died at Kessari Crossing. Thomas Doyle, second company, nineteen years old. Your book has him on page five."

"Fourth line," I said. "He was carrying water to the wounded when the order came. Two witnesses saw it. I spoke to both of them."

Doyle closed his eyes briefly. When he opened them they were wet and furious, which is a combination I have seen often on the faces of men who lost people at the Crossing, fury being the only grief the army issues alongside the uniform.

"So here is what I cannot work out," he went on. "You gave Pell the real book. You walked into Junction wearing a dead man's name. You mailed your pages out piecemeal from the customs house — yes, I know about the envelopes, I've known for a week, I chose not to see them. Every step of the way you made yourself smaller and the book bigger. And now they've caught nothing and no one except you, and you're standing on a bridge in the fog like a man waiting for a ferry." His voice cracked slightly on the last word and he hated himself for it, I could tell. "*Why?*"

It was, I thought, a fair question, and deserved better than cleverness. I considered giving him the true answer, which was complicated, and settled instead on the true answer, which was simple.

"Because they can burn every record in the garrison tonight, Declan. They can burn the rolls and the orders and the correspondence and the paybooks, and they will, and by tomorrow morning none of it will ever have existed. They can burn me too, eventually, in the slower way prisons manage it. But they cannot burn what's already across the water. There are pages in London right now with Thomas's name on them, in my handwriting, and by spring a journalist will read that name aloud to strangers who never heard of the Sante, and some of those strangers will repeat it, and some of *them* will write it down, and so on, hand to hand, like a flame passed along a line of people standing in the dark." I shrugged. "You cannot arrest a thing once everyone is holding it."

"They'll try."

"They'll certainly try. But it's a large country and they are few and their handwriting is terrible."

That startled a laugh out of him — short, unwilling, gone immediately, but real. Somewhere below us Pollo began singing to himself, an old river song about a bride who married a heron, which made about as much sense as most old songs and considerably more than the war.

"Brase wants you held quietly," Doyle said at last. "No spectacle. He's promoted on the first of January — General Brase, can you credit it — and he doesn't want a nurse's trial making noise under his fireworks."

"Ah," I said. "Well. He's welcome to his quiet. Quiet has never been on my side anyway."

"Ivo." Doyle rubbed the back of his neck, and for a moment he looked not like a sergeant but like a farm boy from Marrow county, which is what he had been before the army improved him. "If you run — right now, off this bridge, down to Pollo's boat — I'll report you escaped north. I'll swear it on my mother. You'd have a night's start and the fog."

And there it was: the door, held open, for the second time by the second man. I looked at it the way one looks at all doors one will not walk through, with gratitude and a certain distance.

"Declan," I said gently, "do you remember what you asked me in October, when you searched the infirmary and found nothing but honest linen?"

His ears went red. "I remember."

"You asked whether I understood what would happen to me if the ledger were found. I told you I did. I want you to understand that I understood about more than the prison. I understood that the moment I started, two years ago, writing the first name — Private Enos Mbala, struck from the rolls on the ninth of May — I had already bought my ticket to wherever this ends. Everything since has just been the journey." I held out my wrists, palms up, an absurd theatrical gesture, and immediately felt foolish, and let my arms drop. "Take me in, Sergeant. Do it properly. Let Brase have his quiet arrest, and let him sleep badly on it, which he will."

"Why will he sleep badly?"

"Because powerful men always know when the ground has moved under them, even before it opens." I turned to the rail and lifted down the lantern, warm and heavy, its little flame steady against the fog. "Before we go — one request. Not a bargain, not a trade. A request."

"Name it."

"The lantern stays lit. Tonight and every night after, whoever passes — trim the wick, fill the oil. Pollo will show them how." I hooked it back in place, and the light fell yellow across the planks between us, and across his face, and mine, and the black water sliding underneath. "It's for the ferryman. He swears it guides him home. I've never had reason to doubt him about the river, and I find I've run out of reasons to doubt him about anything else."

Doyle looked at the lantern a long moment. Then he straightened, and became a sergeant again from the boots upward, though his eyes took longer than his spine.

"Ivo Okafor," he said, formal and rough at once, "you're detained by order of Colonel Brase, to be held for transport." He did not take my arm. We simply began walking, side by side, off the bridge and toward the gate, two men going in out of the fog. Behind us the lantern burned on its hook, small and stubborn, throwing its little circle of gold onto the water where Pollo's boat slid past, and Pollo raised a hand to us both as though we were merely passengers he'd see again on the return crossing.

"For the record," Doyle muttered as we passed the sentry post, "you're the most infuriating prisoner I've ever arrested."

"You haven't arrested me yet," I pointed out. "You've detained me. It's a different form."

"It'll be a different cell too, if you keep correcting paperwork."

We came through the gate into the garrison yard, where the fires were already being built for the midnight burning — great stone-ringed pyres stacked high with two years' worth of paper, and soldiers lounging beside them with nothing to do but wait for the century to end. As we crossed the yard, heads turned. Word travels faster than arrests. And at the infirmary steps, wrapped in her coat, stood Amara, and beside her young Ferro with his arm still in its sling, and neither of them saluted, and neither of them looked away.

Doyle noticed. So, I am quite certain, did the dozen soldiers pretending not to.

"Cheerful lot," Doyle said under his breath. "For a man under arrest."

"Give them until spring," I said.

I did not tell him why I was so sure. I did not need to. Somewhere over the grey Atlantic, in mailbags and satchels and coat linings, the names were already moving — page by page, envelope by envelope, hand to hand — and no order signed in this yard, not even by a general, could call them back.

What happened next happened quickly, and I will set it down as it deserves: in the next chapter of this account, though it happened to me in a courtroom rather than a prison, and the first voice I heard reading my own handwriting aloud was not the prosecutor's at all.
