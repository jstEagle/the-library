## 21. The Trial of Paper

The courtroom at Woolwich had been a drill hall in a former life, and it had not entirely given up the habit. The rafters still smelled of boot polish and old sweat, and the judge advocate's bench — a great slab of black oak salvaged from some decommissioned man-of-war — sat where the parade ground's dais used to be. Amara decided, on the fourth morning of proceedings, that she did not mind this. It was fitting, somehow, that the army should be tried in its own gymnasium, among the ghosts of its own exercises.

She had been given a seat in the second row behind the press gallery, on account of her being what one young barrister called "a material witness of the most delicate sort." She had asked him whether that meant she was important or merely fragile, and he had gone the colour of boiled ham, and she had liked him a little ever since.

Nine months. Nine months since the lantern bridge, since Ivo had gone quietly into Sergeant Doyle's custody while the mail boat carried ninety-one envelopes out across the grey water toward London. Nine months of letters — hers arriving to him irregularly, his arriving to her not at all, because convicted men awaiting court-martial were permitted to write only to family, and the army had declined, thus far, to recognise two nurses and a dead captain's clerk as anybody's family.

But today they would read the ledger. Today, at last, everyone in this hall would hear what she had carried folded inside her apron through three inspections, what she had kept sewn into a mattress for a winter, what she had nearly burned at Ivo's own stubborn insistence and had never once truly considered burning.

"Mrs. Okonkwo-Bell." The barrister — his name was Fenwick, and he had a voice like warm treacle poured over gravel — found her at the recess. "You're quite certain you can bear it? The reading. Some of the families have been asked to wait outside."

"The families," Amara said, "have waited two years and a war. They can wait in the room where it happens, same as me."

Fenwick smiled down at his shoes. "I told them you'd say something like that."

* * *

The morning session opened with paperwork, as everything in the army did, even the trial of the army's own conscience. A clerk with a cold read aloud from the garrison's requisition logs, and Amara let her mind drift over the faces around her.

The press benches were full — fuller than anyone had expected when the first pages appeared in the *London Chronicle* under the headline NINETY-ONE NAMES THE WAR DENIED. There had been a hunger for it, she thought. That was the thing no one had predicted. The public had swallowed the official version of Kessari Crossing for years the way one swallows a large bone: painfully, and only because it was put in front of them. But once the names began appearing — ordinary names, misspelled in places, Ivo's careful hand rendering *Adeyemi* where the rolls said *Adams*, *Whitlock* where the rolls said nothing at all — once the names came, letters had flooded the Chronicle's offices from mothers and widows and one indignant retired schoolmistress who had taught three of the dead men Latin and wished it entered into the record.

Colonel Brase sat at the defence table like a man attending the funeral of a distant acquaintance: correct, composed, faintly bored. He wore his dress uniform with all its ribbons, which Amara privately considered a tactical error, since every ribbon now read to the gallery less as decoration than as an exhibit list.

Beside him, oddly, sat his counsel — and next to the counsel, an empty chair. The empty chair had its own placard: *Capt. A. Vane (in absentia)*. Vane himself had surfaced in Marchessa, of all places, sunburnt and going by the name of a wine merchant, and the extradition had taken four months and consumed more diplomatic cable than some small wars. He would arrive, Fenwick said, within the fortnight. Amara found she was not eager to see him. She had imagined the moment often enough — the man whose orders had emptied her brother's world — and in every imagining, he was smaller than the hand that wrote those looping letters.

The clerk with the cold finished the requisitions. The judge advocate, a dry stick named Halloran who reminded Amara of a schoolmaster she had once defeated at chess by refusing to be intimidated, removed his spectacles.

"We will now proceed to Exhibit Fourteen."

The room changed. It was subtle — a settling, like wind moving through wheat — but she felt it, and she saw Ivo feel it too.

He sat in the dock in a plain grey suit that Amara had chosen for him herself, over the objections of his military escort, who had wanted him in prison uniform. *He is not on trial for being a prisoner,* she had told the escort. *He is on trial — well, he isn't on trial at all, but if any of us were dressed honestly today, colonel, it would be him.* The suit had been her late husband's and was slightly wrong across the shoulders, but Ivo wore it the way he wore everything, as though he had borrowed it from a kinder man and meant to return it in good condition.

Exhibit Fourteen was the ledger itself. Not the original — the original lay somewhere in the Chronicle's vaults under the care of an editor who had reportedly slept beside it for a week — but a certified copy, page for page, made under court supervision. It looked so small in the clerk's hands. A soldier's pocket book, canvas-bound, water-stained at one corner from the River Sante. Amara had seen it a thousand times; she had dusted around it, hidden it, threatened to burn it. She had never seen it look small before.

"Begin at the first entry," Halloran said.

The clerk cleared his throat, sneezed, apologised, and read:

"*Private Tomas Bell, 2nd Rifles. Fever, 14 December. Official roll: deserted, 11 December.*"

Amara closed her eyes. Tomas Bell. She had held his hand through that fever. He had been nineteen and frightened of the dark, in the polite way that some grown men are, asking her to leave the lamp lit and apologising for the trouble. Deserted, the roll said. His mother had died believing it.

"*Private Emeric Whitlock. Wounds received at Sante Ford, 19 December. Died 23rd. Official roll: no record.*"

On they went. The clerk's voice was flat and reddened with his cold, and that was right, Amara thought. It should not be dramatic. Drama was Brase's currency; drama was speeches about fallen heroes and bodies never recovered. What was happening now was something else — a slow, patient accounting, name after name after name, each one laid on the scales like a coin.

She watched the gallery. An elderly woman in black held a hymnbook upside down in her lap and did not appear to know it. A man in a railwayman's coat wrote each name into a notebook of his own, and Amara understood that somewhere outside this hall there would soon be another ledger, and another, and that this was how a thing like this spread — not like fire but like seed.

At the forty-third name, the clerk faltered.

"*Corporal Joseph Doyle,*" he read, "*killed at Kessari Crossing, 3 October. Official roll: listed among the missing, status unresolved.*"

In the row ahead of her, a broad-shouldered man in civilian clothes — she had noticed him before, sitting very straight, saying nothing — put his head briefly into his hands. Sergeant Doyle, discharged and decorated in the same week, come to listen for his brother's name. He had let Pell out of a cell and lied in an official report, and here he sat, paying whatever price a man pays for doing the only right thing available to him too late. Amara wanted to touch his shoulder. She did not. Some griefs preferred their own company.

* * *

The afternoon belonged to cross-examination, and Brase's counsel was good — she granted him that grudgingly. Good the way a knife grinder is good.

"Mr. Okafor," he said, pacing, "you will agree that a field hospital is a place of great confusion."

"A hospital is a place of great clarity," Ivo answered. "It's the paperwork that gets confused."

Laughter from the gallery, quickly suppressed. Halloran's gavel tapped without conviction.

"You recorded these names," the counsel continued, "in secret. Without authority. Without verification beyond your own observation. Is that consistent with the conduct of a trusted member of the medical service?"

"It is consistent," Ivo said, "with the conduct of someone who has watched a man die twice — once in his bed and once on paper."

Amara saw Halloran's mouth twitch at that, and thought: *there, that one went into the record, and the record will keep it.*

They tried other angles. They suggested sentimentality, then forgery, then — delicately, disgustingly — that a nurse of Ivo's particular background might have political motives incomprehensible to Englishmen. At this, Fenwick rose and observed that the entries matched the burial registers of the Sante mission chapel, which the court had subpoenaed, entry for entry, date for date, and that if Mr. Okafor had forged anything, he had done it two years in advance and in two continents. That ended that line of questioning.

Through all of it Ivo remained Ivo — courteous, precise, occasionally so gently funny that the stenographer had to ask him to repeat things because the gallery was still laughing. Once, asked whether he had considered the consequences of his actions, he replied, "Every day, sir. That is rather the point of a ledger."

But when the session closed and the escorts came to take him back to the detention wing, Amara caught his eye across the room, and what she saw there frightened her more than any of the prosecution's insinuations. He looked *light*. Unburdened. Like a man who had set something heavy down and did not much care what happened to his hands now that they were empty.

"He knows what sentence is coming," Fenwick murmured, following her gaze. "His counsel has prepared him. Two years, likely. Perhaps eighteen months with remission."

"Two years," Amara repeated.

"For ninety-one resurrections," Fenwick said, "it could be worse."

"It could also," Amara said, "be exactly as bad as it is, which is bad enough. Two years is nine hundred days of a man eating prison bread while Colonel Brase dines wherever colonels dine." She pulled her gloves on finger by finger. "Tell me about tomorrow."

"Tomorrow," Fenwick said, "they read the Vane letters into evidence. And the day after, the extradition hearing concludes, and we learn whether the man himself takes that empty chair."

* * *

That evening she wrote to Ivo, because letters to the detained were permitted if inspected, and she had long ago stopped caring what a censor read.

*Ivo,* she wrote. *Today I heard your handwriting spoken aloud. Do you know what it sounds like? It sounds like the chapel at Sante in the early mornings, before the wounded wake — that quiet, that steady. The clerk mispronounced Adeyemi and I corrected him under my breath and the woman in front of me turned around and thanked me. Her son was number thirty-eight.*

*Brase's people say he will take the fall alone, that the Crown wants it contained. They say Vane will hang or won't, depending on treaties nobody in this room understands. None of that is yours to carry anymore. Yours was only the writing down, and the writing down is done, and it worked.*

*Do not go gentle in there, and do not go bitter either. Both are wastes of a man. I am keeping your books. All of them, even the terrible poetry. Ferro sends regards; he has been promoted to orderly-in-charge, God help us all, and asks me weekly when you'll be back to inspect his bandaging. I tell him two years. He says he'll have the whole infirmary spotless by then, which we both know is a lie.*

She hesitated, pen hovering, and then added the thing she had been circling:

*One more matter. The Chronicle printed the final names last week — the ones from your second notebook, the ones you never showed me. Ninety-one in the ledger, and eleven more besides. Among them: Ferro, D. Orderly. Living. You wrote him down while he still breathed, and now every reader in London knows an orderly by that name survived the Sante garrison and was hurt in an accident that was not an accident. He came to see me yesterday, Ivo. He stood in the doorway a long time. He didn't ask whether you caused it. He asked whether you'd written anyone else down alive, and when I said no, he laughed until he cried and said: "Then I'm the only one. Then I get to visit."*

*He means it. Expect him.*

She sealed it, addressed it, and set it on the table by the candle. Outside her lodgings, London went about its fogbound business — hansom bells, a newsboy shouting something about Marconi, the river smell of coal and cold stone. Somewhere in that city, ninety-one envelopes' worth of pages were becoming a book that would outlast everyone in the courtroom, including, eventually, herself.

Amara blew out the candle and sat a moment in the dark, listening to the city, thinking about the empty chair with its placard, and the ship that was bringing its occupant across the water at last.

The trial resumed in the morning. Vane arrived within the fortnight — and when Amara finally stood in the same room as the author of those looping letters, she discovered she had been wrong about one thing all along.

He was smaller than his handwriting. And he knew her brother's name.
