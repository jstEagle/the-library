## 22. Every Name Read Aloud

The bridge was finished in the spring of 1904, and Ivo read about it in a newspaper he had bought for the obituaries.

He was fifty-one years old now, and he had learned to read newspapers the way other men read weather — scanning for what might fall on him. But there in the third column, between an advertisement for a cure for baldness and a report of a railway strike in Yorkshire, was a small notice: *The Lantern Bridge at Sante Ford, destroyed during the late hostilities, has been rebuilt by public subscription. A ceremony will be held on the evening of December 31st.*

Public subscription. He read that twice. Not military funds, not colonial office money — ordinary people, shopkeepers and schoolteachers and widows, had sent shillings across an ocean to raise those pilings again. It seemed the century had turned over a new leaf after all, or at least turned far enough to glance back at the old one.

Ivo folded the paper, finished his tea, and told his landlady he would be away over the holiday.

"Going anywhere nice?" she asked.

"A bridge," he said.

She was used to his answers being like that — technically true, oddly shaped — and let it be.

The journey south took four days by rail and one by ferry, and every mile of it Ivo felt like a man returning to the scene of both his crime and his salvation, which were, as it happened, the same scene. The war was five years gone. There were new settlements along the river road, a school with a bell tower, children running alongside the ferry cart shouting prices at him for fruit he did not need. He bought two oranges anyway, mostly to have something to do with his hands.

The river was exactly as he remembered it, which felt almost like an insult. Men had burned records on its banks; men had drowned in it carrying papers sewn into their coats. And here it went on being a river, silver and unhurried, as though none of it had ever been asked to remember anything.

Then he came around the bend and saw the bridge, and understood why they called it the Lantern Bridge still.

It was a modest thing — timber and iron, wide enough for a cart, long enough to need three piers. But at each end stood an iron post crowned with a glass lantern case, and from the lanterns, chains of smaller lamps swung down along the railings like strings of low stars. Someone had lit them early against the coming dusk, and their light lay doubled on the water, so that the bridge seemed less built than grown there, a bright thing that had put down roots in the dark.

At the near end, set into the stone abutment, was a plaque.

Ivo walked to it slowly. He knew before he could read it what it would say, because Amara had written to him about it two years ago, her letter full of that dry amusement she wore like armor: *They argued all winter about what to put on the plaque. Names, no names, how many names, whose names. In the end they chose nothing, which I consider the first honest decision any committee has ever made.*

The plaque said:

**THE LANTERN BRIDGE**
*Rebuilt by subscription, 1903–1904*
*In memory of those who crossed, and those who did not.*

No names. Deliberately, mercifully, no names.

He stood before it a long time, and found he was not sad. That surprised him. He had expected the blank bronze to feel like one more erasure — the whole long habit of his life had been fighting erasures, after all. But this blankness was different. This was not forgetting. This was a doorway left empty on purpose so that anyone could walk through it carrying whoever they wished. Every man who had ever been struck from a roll could stand behind that inscription and fit there perfectly, because the inscription refused to say who was excluded. It was, he thought, the only kind of monument that could never lie.

"You're staring at it like it owes you money," said a voice behind him.

Ivo turned, and there was Ferro.

He had aged, of course — everyone had — but Ferro had done it loudly. His limp was real now, permanent, the leg that had been crushed under the toppled supply cart having decided, in its slow way, never to fully forgive him. He leaned on a cane he clearly resented, and his beard had come in patchy and black, and he was grinning the same grin he'd had at nineteen, when he'd asked Ivo point-blank whether he had caused the accident and Ivo had lied to his face.

"Ferro," Ivo said. "You got my letter."

"I got your letter." Ferro's grin softened into something more careful. "You wrote that you weren't sure I'd want to see you."

"I wasn't."

"I know." Ferro looked past him at the plaque, at the lanterns coming up along the railing as the light failed. "I spent about four years deciding. Then I heard they were rebuilding the bridge and figured I'd decide standing on it."

"And?"

"And I'm still deciding," Ferro said. "But I came." He shifted his weight off the bad leg with a small hiss. "Amara's here. She arrived yesterday and has already reorganized the entire ceremony committee. They didn't know they needed reorganizing, but she explained it to them."

"That sounds like Amara."

"She says the reading starts at full dark. She says—" Ferro squinted at a card he pulled from his coat, "—she says, and I quote, 'Tell Ivo if he is late I will assume the river took him and proceed without sentiment.' She's in fine form."

Ivo laughed, and the laugh surprised him by feeling easy. Five years ago — even two years ago, walking out of that military prison with two years served and a pardon signed by men who would have preferred he drown quietly — he had not been able to imagine laughing on this riverbank.

* * *

The crowd gathered as the dark settled. Perhaps two hundred people, Ivo guessed — more than he expected, drawn from the new settlements and, judging by the accents, some from much farther. He recognized faces he could not place names to, and names he could not place faces to, and wondered briefly if that was how Colonel Brase had felt delivering his speeches about unrecovered bodies, except that Ivo's confusion was honest.

Brase himself was long gone — disgraced in '02 when the ledger pages surfaced in London, court-martialed in absentia while he sat in a comfortable house in Kent insisting the documents were forgeries until the handwriting experts finished with him. They had not finished gently. Vane was extradited the following spring; the trial still limped through the courts, kept alive by journalists who had learned, rather late, that dead men's letters sold papers. Doyle — Ivo had received exactly two letters from Doyle in five years, both brief, both unsigned beyond an initial — had written last month only: *They will ask me to testify again. I will tell them what I told them before. It remains true.*

And the pages themselves — his pages, his cramped, careful, lamp-lit handwriting — lived now in the public record, reproduced in broadsheets, read aloud in Parliament, printed in a book that sold better than any novel Ivo had ever managed to write under his own name. There was an irony in that he had stopped trying to explain to people at parties.

A small wooden platform had been built at the bridge's midpoint. On it stood Amara, straight-backed as ever, her hair gone fully grey and worn like a flag of surrender nobody had accepted. Beside her stood perhaps thirty children, arranged in a line, holding lanterns on short poles. They ranged from seven or eight to nearly grown, and they fidgeted and whispered and elbowed one another with the universal restlessness of children awaiting a duty.

Amara surveyed the crowd with the expression of a woman confirming the ward was clean, then spoke. Her voice carried without effort — a hospital skill, Ivo thought, the voice that reaches the bed at the end of the row.

"Five years ago," she said, "a book of names was carried down this river against considerable opposition. Some of you know the story. Most of you know only the names." She paused. "Tonight we are going to read them. Not because a government ordered it. Not because a plaque demands it. We are going to read them because these children have learned them, and because a thing spoken aloud by the young cannot be buried by the old."

She turned to the children. "As practiced."

The smallest girl at the end of the line lifted her lantern higher, took an enormous breath, and began.

"Abel, Thomas. Private, Third Sante Rifles."

"Aduba, Kwesi. Private, Second Column."

"Adeyemi, Samuel. Corporal."

On down the line, name after name, rank after rank, the children passing the list among themselves like a bucket line, each voice taking up where the last left off. Some stumbled over spellings and corrected themselves. One boy sneezed magnificently mid-name and resumed without shame. The crowd stood absolutely still. The lanterns swung. The river moved beneath them all, taking the light and giving it back.

Ivo listened the way he had once written: name by name, refusing to hurry. He knew every entry. He had written most of them by candlelight in a supply closet with his heart hammering, and copied others from orders he should never have touched, and a handful had come to him half-remembered from dying men's mouths — misspelled, uncertain, precious precisely because they were imperfect. Hearing them aloud in children's voices, scrubbed clean of fear, made something happen in his chest that he had no clinical term for.

Near the middle of the list came a name he did not expect.

"Marchetti, Lior," recited a solemn girl of about ten. "Civilian clerk, attached garrison headquarters."

Ivo blinked. Then, beside him, Ferro snorted.

"I may have added that one," Ferro murmured. "For services rendered. Don't look at me like that — the man wrote a famous book. He counts."

"He was a fictional person, Ferro."

"All clerks are fictional. Ask anyone who's dealt with the postal service." Ferro's grin faded into something quieter. "There's one more coming. Toward the end. Wait for it."

The list went on. Okafor, Ivo — *that* one drew a ripple through the crowd, a turning of heads, and Amara's eyes found Ivo's across the water-dark air and held there, unembarrassed, until the next child took over. Pell, James, occupation unknown, date unknown — which Ivo found fitting, since to this day he did not know whether Pell had ever truly been Pell, or only ever Vane wearing a borrowed name, or something stranger entirely: a clerk who had chosen, once and finally, to be the hand instead of the shadow.

Then the last child in the line, a boy with his lantern held very straight, cleared his throat.

"Ferro, Elias," he said. "Orderly, garrison infirmary. Status —" the boy glanced down at his card, frowned, and evidently decided the card was wrong, because he looked up and announced, "— living."

The word went out over the water and did not come back.

Ferro's hand found Ivo's shoulder and gripped it, hard, the way a man grips something to stay upright. "Status living," he repeated quietly, just for Ivo. "Every year. That's the tradition now. Amara started it — anyone on the list who's still breathing gets their status corrected out loud, every year, forever. She calls it the most subversive sentence in the language."

"It is," Ivo said, and found his voice rougher than he intended. "It absolutely is."

Because that was the thing he had learned in prison, in the long nights, counting cracks in the ceiling: the ledger had never really been about the dead. The dead were past his help; he had known that from the first entry, written with shaking hands while a man with no name on any roll bled out three beds away. The ledger had always been a wager — a bet that naming people honestly, completely, including the living and the guilty and the inconvenient, would outlast the men with the matches. Brase had burned the records. Brase was a footnote now. The children on the bridge could recite his victims the way they recited their prayers, and could correct the record for Ferro, cheerfully, annually, indefinitely.

Memory had outlived power. It hadn't even been close, in the end.

* * *

Afterward the crowd thinned toward the festival fires on the far bank, and the three of them — Ivo, Amara, Ferro — stayed on the bridge, leaning on the railing in a row like the world's most improbable fence, watching the lamps swing.

Amara produced a flask from somewhere in her coat, as she had produced flasks from nowhere on a hundred night shifts, and poured three measures of something that smelled medicinal and tasted worse, which meant it was authentic.

"To the committee," she said. "May they continue making honest decisions by accident."

"To the plaque," said Ferro. "Best blank page in the empire."

Ivo raised the flask. "To status living."

They drank. Downriver, the fires on the bank threw sparks up into the black, and somewhere a fiddle had started, badly, hopefully.

Amara studied him sideways. "You're leaving soon," she said. Not asking.

"After New Year's. There's a publisher in London who wants — of all things — a novel. Historical fiction, he says. Bridges, lanterns, wars, that sort of nonsense." Ivo shrugged. "I told him I'd dabbled."

"You'll write it under Marchetti again?"

"Oh, certainly. Marchetti sells." He smiled, but it faded into something more thoughtful, and for a while none of them spoke. The river talked instead, softly, under the boards.

"There's one thing I never asked," Ferro said at last. "That night at the customs house. Thirty envelopes, one by one, while that officer breathed down your neck. Weren't you afraid?"

Ivo considered it honestly, the way he had promised himself he would always consider things now.

"Yes," he said. "Terrified. Every envelope. My hands shook so badly on the twentieth that the officer offered me a chair and asked if I had fever." He turned the flask in his fingers, watching the lamplight swim in the glass. "But here's what I learned, Ferro, and it took me a prison cell and five years to learn it properly. Fear doesn't go away when you do the right thing. It just stops being in charge. You carry it the way the ferryman carries his lantern — not because it lights much, but because you refuse to cross in the dark."

Amara made a sound that was either a laugh or a dismissal; with her they had always been the same sound.

Below them, at the foot of the near pier, a small boat slid out of the shadows — a ferryman, older than the war, poling his way toward the landing with a single lantern hung at his bow. The children's ceremony had ended hours ago; there should have been no one to carry. But the bridge lanterns were lit anyway, all along the railings, because the town had voted on it — actually voted, minutes recorded, one dissenting alderman overruled — that the lanterns would burn every night, forever, for whoever might still be crossing.

Ivo reached into his coat and brought out the ledger.

It was the original — the true one, the one Amara had hidden in the linen stores through the whole long winter of '99, through his arrest, through the trial, through everything. Its cover was cracked, its spine repaired with surgical tape in Amara's unmistakable neat stitching, its corners soft as cloth from handling. He had asked for it back only once the verdicts were read and the appeals exhausted, and the court, in one of its rare gracious moods, had released it to him with instructions that it be preserved.

Preserved. As if a book could be pickled. Books stayed alive or they died; that was the whole bargain.

He opened it to the last written page. Ferro's name was there — the first living name he had ever entered, written in '99 with guilt sour in his mouth — and beneath it, in the margin, the small annotation he had added after the trial: *Forgave me before I forgave myself. See: status living.*

There was room below that. Room for one more line, perhaps two, before the page ran out.

Amara watched him. "What are you going to write?"

Ivo uncapped the little pen he still carried — a nurse's pen, short, practical, the twin of the one confiscated at his arrest — and thought about it.

Not another casualty. Not another erased man. The ledger had done its work as evidence; the trial record held every page in facsimile; the children had the names by heart. What remained was only this: to keep the book honest. To keep adding to it, year after year, whatever deserved the dignity of being written down truly — births as well as deaths, returns as well as losses, the whole unruly census of people the official paperwork would never quite get right.

He bent over the page, shielding the paper from the river wind, and wrote in his careful, cramped, once-condemned hand:

*Okafor, Ivo. Nurse, River Sante garrison. Status — living. Present at the Lantern Bridge, 31st December 1904, in the company of friends. All accounts reconciled.*

He read it over. It would do.

Then he closed the book, and the river went on talking beneath the lanterns, patient as testimony, and far upstream — so faint he might have imagined it, and chose not to — the bells of the new year began.
