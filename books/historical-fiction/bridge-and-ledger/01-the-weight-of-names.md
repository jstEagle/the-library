## 1. The Weight of Names

The wind came off the sea that night the way it always came in December — not as a guest but as a claimant, rattling the glazing of the lantern room as though it had papers to prove the light belonged to it. Ansel Thackeray climbed the ninety-three steps with the oil can in one hand and a stub of candle in the other, counting them without meaning to. He had counted those steps so many times in nineteen years that the number had worn a groove in him, smooth and automatic, like the groove his boots had worn into the treads. Ninety-three up. Ninety-three down. A man could live his whole life inside such arithmetic, he thought, and some men did.

The lamp was burning steady. He checked it anyway.

That was the whole of his religion now: check the lamp anyway. Wicks trimmed at four and again at midnight, the clockwork that turned the lens wound every two hours, the glass wiped inside and out so no salt haze could thicken the beam. His father had kept lights on the Suffolk coast before him, and his grandfather before that, and somewhere in the long descent of Thackerays there had presumably been a man who tended a flame for a living because he loved God or loved the sea or loved the terrible beauty of a storm seen from a warm stone tower. Ansel had never found that man's romance in himself. He kept the light because it was there, and because if he did not, ships would die on the point, and because the work filled his hands from dusk to dawn so thoroughly that his heart had less room to remind him what it lacked.

He wound the clockwork. The great lens turned above him with a sound like a slow breath, sending its blade of white out over the water — three seconds bright, three seconds dark, the character of the Point Aldery light, printed in the almanacs, known to every skipper between here and Calais. Out beyond the headland the winter sea rolled black and seamed with phosphorescent foam, and far off, almost at the edge of seeing, the riding lights of the herring fleet sat clustered like a village that had decided to float away from England altogether.

"Steady," he said to the lamp, because Maren used to say it, and because saying it was easier than not saying it.

Maren had been dead fourteen months. He did not think of it that way — *dead* — most hours. He thought of it as *gone up the path*, which is what she had done one grey morning in October of the year before, walking down through the gorse to meet the doctor's cart from Millbeck, and had sat down halfway back, on the flat stone by the second stile, as though tired, and had not got up again. Her heart, the doctor said. Quick as a snuffed wick. Ansel had been up in the lantern room when they brought her home, keeping the light through a fog so thick the beam came back at him like a wall, and when he came down she was laid out on the bed he had made for her with his own hands when they married, and the first thing he had noticed — this detail would pursue him all his days — was that her boots were muddy, and someone ought to clean them.

He cleaned them. He did not know why. It seemed the only task in the world that mattered, and he had done it kneeling on the cold floor with a cloth and a bit of tallow soap, weeping in the silent way he wept, which was to say not at all, only his hands shaking until the brush chattered against the leather.

Now he finished his rounds, logged the weather — wind south-southeast, freshening, glass falling — and went down the inside stair to the rooms at the tower's base where he lived, and where the ritual waited for him that he could not break, though he had tried.

* * *

The ledger lived on the dresser between the bread crock and the tin of lamp keys, which was not where a book of any consequence should live, but Maren had put it there and Ansel had never moved it. It was a ship's blank logbook once, bought for sixpence from the marine stores in Millbeck, its printed columns ruled and waiting for courses and distances; she had ruled new lines across the old ones and made the pages hers. The cover was green canvas, gone soft as cloth at the corners from handling. On the spine, in her upright, patient hand: *Names.*

Ansel ate his supper standing — bread, cheese, the last of the pickle she had put down in September of the year she died, which he rationed like a miser because when the jar was empty there would be no more pickles in the world made by those hands — and then he dried his hands on the towel, properly, fingers and thumb, because you did not touch the book with wet or greasy hands, and he took it down, and he carried it to the chair by the fire, and he began to read.

He read it aloud. He had not decided to do this. It had simply become the shape of the thing, the way the tide has a shape. His voice in the low room, the fire ticking, the wind worrying the tower overhead, and Maren's words moving through the dark at the pace of reading aloud.

"*Saved, the eighth of March, 1889.*" His own voice sounded strange to him, older than he was. "*Thomas Cregg, boy, fell from the harbour wall at Millbeck on the ebb. Pulled out by James Renney with the boat-hook. Broken arm. Living.*"

He turned the page. The paper whispered. Outside, the sea said what the sea always said.

"*Lost, the twenty-second of November, 1891. The* Speedwell *of Aldery Haven, six souls. Names as follows—*" and here they were, written one beneath another in her careful letters, each with whatever she knew appended to it, because Maren had not been able to bear a name standing alone: *Peter Quayle, master, forty-four years; his wife's name was Bessie; he grew dahlias. Matthew Curnow, mate, thirty-one; sang bass in the chapel. William Tregaskis, twenty, had a new coat that winter, his mother said, and wore it the day he sailed and would not take it off even in the rain—*

Ansel stopped there sometimes. Not tonight. Tonight he read through it, every word, because the ritual demanded the whole of it, the saved and the lost alike, and because he had learned — slowly, the way you learn anything worth learning — that grief was not a thing you got over but a thing you carried correctly, like a full pail, level and careful, and the carrying was done here, in this chair, at this hour, with this book open on his knees.

There were sixty-one names under *Saved* and eighty-eight under *Lost*, and Maren had known something personal about nearly all of them, because she had been the sort of woman who asked the second question — not *what happened* but *who else*, not *how old* but *what were they like*. She had begun the book in their first winter at the point, after the *Mary Stothard* went ashore with all hands and the bodies came in along the tideline for a week, and Ansel had found her sitting where he sat now, writing, her face wet, and when he asked her what she was doing she had said:

"Somebody has to keep them somewhere. The sea keeps them wrong."

He had not understood it then. He understood it now the way he understood the lamp: completely, and without comfort.

* * *

Near midnight he wound the clockwork again, and coming back down the inside stair he did not go to his bed but out the low door into the yard, as he did every night, crossing the frozen mud to the workshop.

It had been a net-store once, before their time — a long low building of stone and timber roofed in tarred felt, smelling forever of pitch and hemp. Maren had claimed it in their second year and slowly, plank by plank and drawing by drawing, transformed it into her own country. Inside, the smell was different: sawn pine, linseed oil, the chalk-and-vinegar of her sizing, and underneath it all the ghost of pipe-clay from the model boxes. Shelves ran the length of one wall, and on them stood her models — little bridges, a dozen of them or more, arched and flat and cantilevered, built at the scale of inches out of matchwood and glue, each one labeled in her hand with a date and a verdict. *Too heavy. Spans wrong. Wind would take it.* She had been the daughter of a Falmouth shipwright and had absorbed timber knowledge with her mother's milk, and she had spent the last years of her life — though Ansel had not fully grasped it until after — designing a bridge.

Not a great bridge. Nothing for carts or cannon. A footbridge: from the rocks below the point, where the lighthouse path ended, across the narrow gut of water to the beginning of the mainland cliff-path, so that in any weather, at any hour, a person could come and go without waiting for the tide to drop and the causeway stones to show. Twice a day the sea closed the point off entirely, and twice a day it opened it, and everyone who lived here arranged their lives around that breathing. Maren had nearly died twice on that causeway herself — once swept to the waist, once stranded overnight with a broken wrist — and her drawings, pinned now in tidy rows along the workshop's north wall, showed what she had made of her grievance: measurements, loadings, a bill of timber, joint details drawn in section with notes in her small square script. *Mortise and tenon throughout. No nails where wood can hold. Oak for the piles, larch for the deck.*

And beneath the drawings, in the corner, wrapped in sacking against the damp, the timber itself — what he had gathered so far. Some bought from Millbeck with money he did not have to spare. Some salvaged, honestly and otherwise, from the beach. Enough, perhaps, if the winter allowed. He had begun cutting joints in October, badly at first, his hands remembering slower than his mind wanted them to, and then better, the chisel finding the line again the way a voice finds a tune. He worked an hour most nights. Not more. He did not let himself think about why he worked slowly, except that some part of him understood that the bridge, once finished, would be the last thing left to finish, and he was afraid — though he would never have put it in those words — of the morning when there was nothing left of Maren's design still waiting to be built.

Tonight he uncovered the oak and looked at the pile-work, half-cut, and touched the tenon he had pared that evening before his shift, checking it with his thumbnail the way his father-in-law had taught him. Sound. He wrapped it all up again against the cold.

On the bench lay her pencil, sharpened, waiting. He moved it half an inch so it lay parallel with the bench edge, the way she always left it, and went out and locked the door, and stood a moment in the freezing yard looking back at the tower, where the beam swung and swung above him, patient as conscience, three seconds bright, three seconds dark.

* * *

Back in the warm room, he set the ledger on his knees again for the last entries of the night. This was the part of the ritual he dreaded and needed in equal measure, though he would not have called either thing by its name. The last page under *Lost*. The names there were not strangers'.

His brother-in-law, Joss Ravensworth — no, not Ravensworth; that was nothing to him; Maren's brother, Joss Merren — was written there in Maren's own hand, dated eleven years back, and below it the facts she had collected with terrible patience over two years of letters and enquiries: *Lost off Harker's Rill, June 1888, aged twenty-six. Deckhand, the* Gannet. *No body recovered. There was no quick way to bring help that night — the causeway was under, and by the time the boat launched from Millbeck it was hours. Hours.*

Ansel read the entry as he read it every night, and every night the same sentence rose in him unbidden, Maren's sentence, spoken once in this room with a calm that had frightened him more than weeping would have: *"If there had been a bridge, Ansel, there would have been time."*

He closed the book. His hands rested on the green canvas cover, and the fire had burned low, and outside the wind had backed and freshened, and the glass — he glanced at the barometer on the wall — had fallen again, further than falling weather usually cared about in the first week of December.

He entered the night's figures in his own log in his plain keeper's hand — *Midnight. Wind SSE freshening, squally. Glass 29.1, falling. All well.* — and paused, as he did every night, over the small official lie of *all well*. The light was well. The tower was well. The machinery was well.

He blew out the candle, and lay down in the bed that was too wide, and listened to the sea working at the point below, and slept the shallow sleep of a man who keeps a list.

In the morning there would be oil to haul and wicks to trim and the tide to watch, and none of it would be unusual, and that was the mercy and the terror of his life in equal parts. But three days later, on a morning of hard frost, he would come out to find soldiers on the cliff-path with nailed papers and a captain on a grey horse, and the world he had built out of routine and remembrance would begin, plank by plank, to be taken apart.
