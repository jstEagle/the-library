## 2. Requisition Orders

The soldiers came on a Tuesday, in the grey hour after dawn when the sea and the sky are the same colour and a man cannot tell where his work ends and the world begins.

Ansel Thackeray had just come down from the tower, the lamp doused and the lens covered, his fingers still smelling of brass polish and paraffin. He was standing at his kitchen table with a mug of tea going cold between his hands, watching the steam unravel the way Maren's breath had unraveled at the end — not gone all at once but thinning, thinning, until it was only the room breathing and not her — when he heard the wheels.

Not cart wheels. Something heavier. Iron-rimmed, military, the sound of a thing that expected the road to be there for it.

He went to the window. Down the coast road, past the crooked line of houses that made up Harrow Point, came a column of men in army blue-grey, forty of them perhaps, with two waggons behind and a mounted officer at the head whose greatcoat was buttoned to the chin against the February wind. Behind them, out on the water, a steam pinnace was working its way toward the landing slip, towing a barge stacked with timber that even at that distance looked wrong — squared, numbered, stamped. Timber that had never known a tree's own shape.

Ansel set down his mug. He did not finish the tea. He had learned, in thirty years of tending a light, that when something changes on the horizon you go out to meet it while it is still far enough away to be met.

* * *

The column halted in the square before the fish-cellars, where three roads met and the war memorial stood — a granite sailor, his face worn featureless by salt, gazing eternally at a horizon that had taken better men than it had given back. The officer dismounted. She was younger than Ansel expected, and a woman, which surprised him more than the uniform did; her cap sat square, her boots were polished to a black mirror, and she moved with the economy of someone who had already measured every distance in the town from the saddle without appearing to look.

She carried a leather document case under one arm and a hammer in her other hand, and a soldier beside her carried a canvas satchel bristling with printed notices.

"Captain Marsh," she said, when Ansel came down the lane toward them. Not an introduction. A designation. "Army Corps of Bridges and Defences. You're the lighthouse keeper?"

"Thackeray. Ansel Thackeray." He looked past her at the waggons, the timber, the faces of the townsfolk beginning to gather in doorways like swallows before weather. "What's all this, then?"

"The Crown is constructing a fortified bridge across the narrows," she said. Her voice was level and clear, a voice trained to carry over guns. "The point is to be fortified against landing. These premises fall within the defensive perimeter." She drew a paper from the case and held it out, and her eyes did a curious thing as she did so — flicked once, quickly, up the cliff path to where the lighthouse stood white against the pewter sky, taking in the gallery, the lantern, the state of the paint, and returning to him before the blink finished. "You'll find the terms explained. Compensation schedules are attached."

Ansel took the paper. It was good stock, heavy in the hand, printed in four colours of ink, and it said everything and nothing: *by order of*, *under the authority of*, *requisitioned for the purpose of*, *all persons residing within the designated perimeter will*. Words like fencing, driven into the ground around him.

"There's people live here," he said. "Fishing families. Sixty souls if it's sixty."

"Seventy-one," said Captain Marsh, and there was something in the flatness of it — she had counted them herself, or caused them to be counted, house by house. "They'll be relocated. The schedule is in your notice."

"They've fished these waters a hundred years."

"The narrows are to be closed to civilian traffic during construction."

"And after?"

She did not answer that. She turned instead to her sergeant and said, "Begin with the cellars and work east," and the machine of her moved into motion around Ansel as though he were a post in the ground — useful for tying things to, otherwise to be ignored.

The hammering started. That was the sound he would remember afterward, more than any of it: the flat, authoritative knock of nails going into doors, one after another down the crooked street, each notice squaring itself against the weathered wood like a warrant. Mrs. Tregale at the bakehouse came out with flour still on her hands and read hers aloud in a voice that cracked on the long words. Young Pellow, whose boat the *Merry Alice* was his father's before him and his grandfather's before that, stood in front of the paper nailed to his door for a long time without moving, the way a man stands at a graveside.

Ansel walked back up the lane to his own door, and found a soldier already there, hammer raised.

"I'll take that," Ansel said, not unkindly, and held out his hand for the nail, and drove it home himself, because it seemed important somehow that his own door be nailed by his own arm. Then he stood and read what his government thought of him.

*Notice of Requisition.* *Defence of the Realm Consolidation Act.* *All documents, registers, and records pertaining to persons resident or sojourning within the perimeter shall be surrendered to the requisitioning authority for verification.*

He read that last clause twice. Something cold began, very quietly, in the pit of him.

* * *

They came to the lighthouse at midday.

There were three of them — Captain Marsh, the sergeant, and a clerkish corporal with a ledger-satchel of his own, which struck Ansel as a joke the universe had gone to some trouble to arrange. Marsh knocked at his door, though she had seen him watching from the window, and waited the two seconds politeness requires before proceeding.

"You keep records here, Mr. Thackeray," she said. He noticed she did not make it a question. "Logbooks, obviously. And anything beyond them. Registers of residents, visitors, salvaged goods, wages paid. Persons who have passed through your keeping."

"It's a lighthouse," Ansel said. "Ships pass. People don't."

"The act is broad." She stood in his kitchen without being invited, and her eyes went over the room the way they had gone over the tower: measuring, filing. They stopped at the dresser. At the drawer where the oilskins hung above the folded wool of Maren's old shawl. At the book that lay on the corner of the table, because Ansel had been reading it that morning and had not put it away, because he never put it away anymore, because it lived on the table the way a lamp lives on a table, lit whenever the room was dark.

The ledger. Maren's ledger. Blue cloth boards gone soft at the corners, the spine mended twice with sail-thread, the pages inside written in a hand that slanted left and pressed hard, as though the writer were afraid the ink might not hold unless she bore down on it.

"What is this?" Marsh said, and reached for it.

"That's mine," Ansel said. "That's private."

"All documents within the perimeter—" the corporal began, reciting, and Marsh lifted two fingers and he stopped as if a valve had shut.

She opened the ledger. Ansel watched her do it and felt something tear in his chest that had taken him eleven months to knit closed. She turned the pages slowly. Too slowly for inventory; too quickly for reading. Her face gave nothing away, but her thumb paused once, halfway through, on a page, and Ansel knew which page it was without looking — August 1894, the *Providence* of Padstow, nine souls off the rocks and eight brought ashore, and at the bottom in Maren's hardest-slanting hand: *Thomas Wrenner, drowned. 34 years. Left a wife, Cissy, and a daughter born the following spring who will never know him but will.*

"Names," Marsh said quietly. "Dates. Vessels." She looked up. "What is this record, Mr. Thackeray?"

"A keeper's book."

"It isn't in the standard format."

"My wife kept it her own way."

"Your wife." A pause, no longer than a heartbeat, and her eyes went to the shawl folded on the dresser and came back. "She's not resident."

"She died last January."

Something crossed the captain's face then — small, quickly governed, but Ansel had spent thirty years reading weather off water and could read weather off a face. Sympathy, maybe. Or recognition. Whatever it was, it lasted less than a breath, and then the uniform came down over it like a shutter.

"Then the document has no living author," she said, "and falls squarely under the registration provisions. Unregistered persons cannot remain undocumented within a defensive perimeter. The names in this book must be verified against civil rolls, and the book itself is required evidence pending verification." She closed the ledger and held it against the document case, and the two blue-leather things looked like cousins. "You may apply for its return after review."

"After review." Ansel heard his own voice and did not recognize it — low, careful, each word set down like stone on stone. "Captain. Those aren't unregistered persons. Those are drowned men and saved men. Some of them are ash under the churchyard wall. Half of them are nothing at all now but what my wife wrote down. That book is—"

He stopped. He had meant to say *it's all I have of her*, and he found he could not say it in front of the corporal and the sergeant and the iron-shod boots on his floor, could not hand them that and watch them write it in their satchels.

"That book is the history of this point," he finished. "Every soul lost off these rocks since '81, and every soul pulled off them. My wife wrote it. She spent eleven years writing it. You can't requisition the dead, Captain."

"No," said Elowen Marsh. "But I can requisition the paper they're written on."

And there it was — not cruelty, that was the terrible part of it. No relish, no malice. Just the arithmetic, clean as a compass bearing: a perimeter required documentation, documentation required surrender, surrender required his wife's eleven years folded into a satchel and carried down to a tent among the fish-cellars. She held out her hand for his receipt, and he took it without reading it, and he watched her carry the ledger out of his kitchen and down the lane, and the whole time the sea went on making its ordinary noise below the cliffs, indifferent, enormous, keeping its own accounts.

* * *

He followed them as far as the square, because his legs would not agree to anything else.

The notices were up on every door now, forty-odd sheets of printed paper flapping in the onshore wind like a strange pale flock come to roost. The clerk-corporal had set up a trestle table by the war memorial and was entering items into a register: *one (1) ledger, cloth-bound, approx. 200 ff., mixed entries, condition fair.* Ansel stood at the edge of it all with his hands hanging at his sides, and the townsfolk gathered around him the way water finds the low place — Mrs. Tregale, young Pellow, the Widow Curnow with her shawl over her head, old Tam Budge who had not spoken a full sentence since the storm of '93.

"They've taken Maren's book," Ansel said. His voice carried further than he intended. The square went quiet enough that he could hear the timber creaking on the barge out at the slip.

"They can't do that," said the Widow Curnow.

"They've a piece of paper says they can."

"But those names," she pressed, and her old voice shook. "My Jory's in that book. Drowned off the Gull Rock, '89. If they take the book—"

She didn't finish. She didn't need to. Ansel understood, looking around that square at their faces, exactly what she couldn't say: that the dead did not live in the churchyard, whatever the vicar said. The churchyard held what was left over. The dead lived in the ledger, in the ink, in the pressure of a leftward-slanting hand that had borne down hard on every page so the names would hold. Take the book and you did not confiscate a document. You confiscated seventy people who had already died once and did not deserve to die twice.

"Mr. Thackeray," said Captain Marsh.

She had come back across the square alone, the document case under her arm, the ledger nowhere visible — already filed, already shelved in some tent among the cellars, already begun its long sleep in the army's paper dark. Up close, in the flat light, she looked younger than she had at dawn, and tireder, and Ansel hated himself for noticing.

"You'll want to apply for return of the document through the proper channel," she said. "Form K-11. The corporal can issue you one."

"Will form K-11 give me back my wife's handwriting?"

A muscle moved at the corner of her jaw. "It will enter your application into the review queue."

"How long is the queue?"

"Review concludes when the perimeter is secured. Construction completes in autumn." She said it evenly, without apology, and that was the worst of it — she was not lying to him, she was simply telling him the truth of her world, in which everything was scheduled and grief was not a line item. "I'm sorry for the necessity, Mr. Thackeray. The invasion threat is real. What we build here may save more lives than that book ever recorded."

"Then you'd think," Ansel said, "that a book about saving lives would be the first thing your army wanted to read."

She looked at him for a moment — a long moment, longer than regulation, long enough that the corporal at the table glanced up from his register. The wind came off the sea and moved the hem of her greatcoat.

"The light," she said, "remains your responsibility. Requisition doesn't touch the station operations. Keep it burning, Keeper. That's your duty, and I won't have it neglected for... private griefs."

She turned and walked back toward the cellars, and Ansel stood in the square with Form K-11 in his hand and the notices flapping on every door, and above him on the cliff the lighthouse stood white and blind in the daylight, waiting for evening, waiting for him, its lamp cold and its purpose unchanged while everything around it changed.

*Private griefs.* He turned the words over as he climbed the cliff path, and by the third turn of the switchback they had stopped hurting and started doing something else entirely — hardening, setting, taking an edge.

Because here was the thing Ansel Thackeray knew, standing on his own doorstep as the afternoon dimmed, that Captain Elowen Marsh did not yet know: the book was paper, yes. Paper could be confiscated. But paper was only where the names had been put down, not where they were kept. They were kept somewhere else. They were kept in a keeper's head, thirty years of nights, name after name read by lamplight until the lamplight and the names were woven through him like thread through canvas — Thomas Wrenner, 34 years, and Cissy's daughter who would never know him but *will*; the boy off the *Kestrel*; the three Frenchmen in the November of '87 who spoke no English and wept anyway when the brandy came out; Maren's own careful tally in the back pages, sums done in her hard leftward hand, saved against lost, year against year, as if love were an account that could be balanced.

He had memorized that ledger without ever meaning to. He had read it every night for eleven months because he could not help it, and every reading had carved the lines a little deeper.

They could take the book. They could not take the keeper.

Ansel went inside, trimmed the wicks, filled the reservoirs, laid out fresh paper and the good pen with the steel nib Maren had bought him in Falmouth, and waited for dark, and for the moment when the tower would need him and the work of remembering could begin — one name, one date, one vessel, recovered page by page from the only archive the army could not reach.

Down in the square, the camp lamps were lighting among the fish-cellars. Somewhere among them, in a crate or a cabinet, his wife's ledger lay closed in the dark.

He meant to have it open again by spring. Every page. Every name. Even if he had to write the whole of it twice.
