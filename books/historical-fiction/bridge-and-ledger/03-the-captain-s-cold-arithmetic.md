## 3. The Captain's Cold Arithmetic

Captain Elowen Marsh had been taught, from her first day at Woolwich, that an inventory was a form of honesty. A thing counted was a thing understood; a thing understood could be commanded. She rose before the garrison bugle on the second morning of the occupation and stood at the window of the requisitioned customs house while the town assembled itself below in the grey light — slate roofs furred with frost, chimney smoke standing up straight as signal masts, the long black spine of the point running out to where the lighthouse lifted its lantern like a man raising his face into weather.

She spread the survey maps on the trestle table and began.

The arithmetic of the operation was simple, and simplicity was its virtue. The War Office required a fortified crossing at the narrows before spring; spring meant timber, labour, level ground, and clearance. The town of Harrow Point contained forty-one dwellings, three stores, a chapel, a boatyard, two net-drying sheds, a smithy, and one lighthouse with its attendant structures. Of these, nineteen buildings fell inside the demolition line drawn in red ink by a colonel who had never seen the sea. The rest would be spared or repurposed. Every door, every hearth, every family — she recorded them in a ledger of her own, ruled and numbered, each entry answering only to itself.

*Customs house: requisitioned. Occupant relocated to quarters on Fore Street.*

*Chapel: retained for garrison use. Minister cooperative.*

*Boatyard: partial requisition. Slipway required for construction traffic.*

Her pencil moved without pause. That was the discipline — not to think of each line as a household, but as a figure. Figures were clean. Figures added up. She had learned long ago that if you let yourself see the faces behind the figures you became slow, and slowness in an officer was not mercy but dereliction. Somewhere across the water, an enemy fleet was being counted by other officers with the same efficiency, and the side that counted best would keep its people alive. Duty was arithmetic performed correctly. She believed this the way she believed in the load-bearing qualities of stone.

By eight o'clock she had catalogued the eastern half of the town and ordered tea she did not drink. Sergeant Bray waited at the door with the morning's confiscation manifests stacked square as bricks.

"The outbuildings list, ma'am. And this came from the lighthouse." He set it apart from the others, slightly, the way a man sets down something he'd rather not touch.

It was a slim book bound in marbled board, cloth spine gone soft with handling, corners rounded not by accident but by years of hands finding the same places. *Register of Persons, Unofficial* — that was what the seizure order called it. Unregistered persons. Names not entered in any parish roll or census the army could cross-reference. On paper it was exactly the kind of document the regulations required her to take: an unofficial record of individuals, kept without authority, on a military installation's perimeter, during a state of alert.

She opened it at the worktable with her manifest beside it, prepared to enter it as *Item 47, one ledger, contents miscellaneous*, and be done.

The handwriting stopped her.

Not the first page — the first pages were fair copies, tidy, almost printed, a careful hand establishing its own rules. Two columns. *Saved. Lost.* Dates in small figures. But somewhere past the middle of the book the hand loosened, as though the writer had stopped performing and started simply writing, and it became something else entirely — a slanting, urgent script, letters leaning into the wind of their own making, the descenders of the g's cut short as if the pen could not spare time for flourish. Entries crowded the margins. Small annotations bloomed in the spaces between lines: *third son of the widow Corve; the one who whistled; came ashore with rope still round his wrist and lived to be an old man.*

Elowen read three entries without meaning to. Then a fourth.

*11 March '93 — the Greyling, lost with five. Wrote what I could learn. God knows it is little.*

Below it, in different ink, older: *He was kind to my sister on the Yarmouth packet. He deserved more than little.*

She sat back. Her tea went cold on the manifest and left a ring, and she found herself looking at that ring instead of the columns.

There was no question of the book being what the order presumed. She had seen registers of dissidents, of smugglers, of men whose names were dangerous to hold. This was not that. This was a woman's book — she was certain it was a woman's hand; she had spent her girlhood copying out her mother's recipes and knew the particular economy of a female script, the way it made room for itself in a narrow world — and it recorded nothing seditious. It recorded the sea's accounts. Who came back. Who did not. It was, if anything, the opposite of an unregistered-persons document: it was a register of persons the parish had never troubled to count because they were too poor or too drowned to matter.

And yet the regulations said seize. And regulations were how an army kept its promises to itself.

She closed the book and looked at it for a moment longer than an officer should look at Item 47. The marbled cover had a coffee-coloured stain shaped roughly like a bird in flight. Someone had carried this book down to a cold shore, over and over, year after year, and written in it with fingers that must have been stiff, because the ink wavered in the winter entries and steadied again in summer ones. You could read the seasons in the ink. Twenty-some years of seasons.

"Ma'am?" Bray said.

"File it," she said. "Evidence annex. Not to be destroyed."

"Yes, ma'am. And the keeper? He made a protest, sir — well, objection. Says the book is his late wife's. Personal effects, he calls it."

"I heard his objection." She had heard it yesterday at the light station, delivered in a voice like a door closing slowly — no heat in it, only finality, and underneath the finality something she had chosen not to name. "Regulations don't distinguish between a wife's effects and a stranger's when the wife is deceased and the contents are unregistered."

Bray nodded and wrote it down, and Elowen picked up her pencil and returned to her survey, and discovered that her pencil had stopped being automatic. At the top of the page she had written *Net shed No. 2:* and then nothing, because she was thinking about ink that wavered in winter.

She forced herself through the morning the way one forces a march through rain — head down, next step, next step. Boathouse. Coal store. Signal platform, proposed. She walked the point itself at midday with two corporals and a measuring chain, wind coming off the water hard enough to make the flags of her coat crack, and she assessed the ground the way she had been trained: drainage, bearing, access, fields of fire. Excellent ground. Ground that wanted a bridge across the narrows and earthworks on the rise and the clutter of civilian life cleared away from it like undergrowth before a survey.

The lighthouse keeper watched her from the gallery of the tower, a small dark figure against the white of the paint, and she felt the watching like a weight between her shoulder blades all the length of the point.

* * *

In the afternoon the cracks in the arithmetic began — hairline, hairline-wide, the kind a surveyor notes and a builder ignores at his peril.

The first appeared at the chapel, where the minister's wife asked her, with terrible gentleness, whether the graves inside the demolition line were to be moved or merely built upon. Elowen checked the map. The graveyard was not on the map. Maps of this coast, done by the Ordnance Office, showed contours and soundings and nothing else that mattered to artillery. "That will be addressed in due course," she said, and heard her own voice perform the phrase, and hated it, faintly, the way one hates a draft that will not bear rereading.

The second appeared at the boatyard, where an old shipwright stood among his half-built hulls and answered every question with perfect courtesy and eyes like wet slate, and when she noted the slipway for requisition he said only, "You'll want to know the tide table then, miss. Army engineers never do." And she, who had spent four years proving she could do everything army engineers were thought unable to do, wrote down *slipway* and could not write down anything about the tide table because she had not thought to ask.

The third appeared at the customs house, after dark, when she laid out the day's manifests in order and found her eye going, again and again, to the folder marked Evidence Annex, in which Item 47 lay shut.

She told herself the professional course was simple: transcribe the names, cross-reference against parish rolls, discharge the document as a curiosity. That was what the regulations intended. She even took out fresh paper to begin the transcription, and wrote the heading, and then turned to the ledger itself to copy the first entries fairly — and read, instead, the whole middle section through, by lamplight, the wind worrying the shutters of the customs house like a dog at a bone.

It was the annotations that undid her. Any record could be copied. What could not be copied was the knowledge between the lines: that the sea took the young men first; that the widows of this coast could identify their dead by a boot heel or a scarred knuckle; that someone — Maren Thackeray, the keeper had said at the seizure, *my wife's book, she kept it eleven years, she died in March* — someone had gone out in all weathers to gather these facts because no one else would, and had died with the book unfinished, and the book had gone on being written anyway, in a different hand, the husband's hand, heavier and more careful, trying to imitate the slant of the letters and not quite managing, the way a mourner tries to imitate a laugh.

Two hands in one book. Eleven years of the first, months of the second. She could read the grief in the change of ink the way she read contour lines, and she resented that she could, and read on regardless.

At some hour past midnight she set the book down and did what she had been trained to do with disordered facts: she reduced them to an orderly statement.

Statement: the document contains approximately two hundred names, none of security significance. Statement: its seizure serves no operational purpose beyond compliance. Statement: compliance is the foundation of command; an officer who selects which orders to honour has surrendered the authority to give them. Statement: the point must be cleared by spring regardless of one ledger, one keeper, one widow's handwriting.

All true. She tested each statement the way she tested a girder, loading it, looking for deflection. None buckled.

And yet there was a hairline something, a tremor under the fourth statement, as if the words *regardless* and *one ledger* were not quite the same weight when spoken aloud in a dark room above a town whose lights were going out one window at a time, obediently, because she had ordered it.

She snuffed the lamp. In the dark she listened to the sea working the point below, patient as a clerk, and thought about arithmetic — real arithmetic, the kind she had loved as a girl before she learned it was also a weapon. Arithmetic did not care what you felt. That had always been its comfort. Two and two made four for the general and the drowned alike.

But somewhere in the sum tonight a carry had gone wrong, some small digit moved from one column to another, and now the total would not sit still under her inspection. She was an honest officer. She wrote in her own journal, before sleep, the day's summary in her usual dry style, and then — a sentence she crossed out, rewrote, crossed out again, and finally left:

*Item 47 is not what the order says it is. Filed pending clarification.*

Pending. In eleven years of service she had never once written the word pending. Pending was indecision wearing uniform. Pending was what happened to officers who flinched.

She blew out the candle and lay listening to the sea, and told herself the word meant only that she was thorough.

Out past the point, faint through the shutter-louvres, the great lamp turned and turned, sweeping its blade of light across the water every few seconds — steady, indifferent, keeping its count of the darkness. Somewhere in the tower the keeper was awake with it, as he would be every night she was on this coast, holding his own kind of inventory against the sea.

Tomorrow she would inspect the light station formally. Tomorrow there would be questions to ask him — proper questions, about oil and wicks and the structural report she was obliged to file on the tower. She slept at last with the manifest squared on the table and the ring of cold tea drying on it like a small brown tide-mark, evidence of something spilled and not yet wiped away.

In the morning, the keeper's workshop stood first on her list of outbuildings — a low roofed structure between the tower and the cliff, marked *stores* on the old surveys, flagged for assessment. She had walked past it twice already and noticed nothing remarkable: a building like any other.

She did not yet know what was drawn on the papers pinned inside its door.

But the wind, coming up the cliff path that night, rattled those papers once, softly, like a hand testing a latch — and held.
