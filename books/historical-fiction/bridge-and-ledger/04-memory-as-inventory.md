## 4. Memory as Inventory

They gave him seven days.

The corporal who delivered the order — a boy with chapped hands and an accent from the eastern valleys, the kind of boy who should have been mending nets or courting a girl with ribbons in her hair instead of carrying requisition papers up a lighthouse stair — could not meet Ansel's eyes when he said it. He read from the paper in a voice that cracked twice on military phrasing: *all unregistered persons recorded in confiscated documents to be accounted for within seven days, particulars of name, date, and circumstance, under penalty of* — here the boy stumbled — *under penalty of whatever they put after that.*

"Under penalty of law," Ansel finished for him, and the corporal nodded gratefully, as if Ansel had done him a kindness, and fled back down the stairs into the grey afternoon.

Seven days. As though grief could be itemized. As though twenty-three years of drowning and rescue could be entered into a column like sacks of flour.

Ansel stood at the window of the lamp room while the boy's boots faded down the spiral, and watched the army's bridge take shape below — the great fortified span creeping out from the mainland on stone piers, its skeleton already dark against the water. They were building it a half-mile north of Maren's point, where the channel ran deep and the government engineers had decided the invasion would come, though no invasion had come in living memory and the fishermen joked bitterly that the only thing threatening England that winter was the weather. The jokes had stopped when the notices went up on the doors. Jokes were not registered persons either, apparently.

He turned to the lamp. It wanted trimming; it always wanted trimming. His hands went through the motions without him — wick, cloth, the sweet mineral smell of the oil, the brass fittings wiped until they held small distorted versions of the room. Hands busy. That was the trick of it, the trick he had learned in the two years since Maren died: keep the hands busy and the heart has less room to make trouble. Wind the mechanism. Polish the lens. Log the weather. Do not think about how she used to sit on the third step of the stair with her sketchbook open on her knees, pencil in her mouth, watching him work and pretending she wasn't designing something.

The light went out over the water at dusk, as it had for a hundred years before him and would after, and Ansel descended to the keeper's cottage and sat at the kitchen table where the ledger used to lie, and looked at the pale rectangle of clean wood where it no longer lay.

That was when the fear arrived. Not before — during the daylight, during the orders and the corporal and the trimming of wicks, there had been too much to do. But now the cottage was quiet, the tide was turning with its long gravel sigh below the point, and there was nothing between Ansel and the empty rectangle but himself.

They had taken it on the second day of the requisition. Captain Marsh herself had carried it out — not a soldier handed off roughly, but the captain, in her grey greatcoat, holding the ledger the way you hold a bird with a broken wing, both hands, careful. Ansel had thought, in that first mad moment, that she understood what it was. Then she had written in her own book — *one (1) bound volume, handwritten register, contents unverified* — and the understanding had gone out of her face like a lamp turned down, and the ledger had gone into a canvas satchel, and the satchel had gone into a cart, and the cart had gone down the road toward the garrison, and Ansel had stood in his own doorway listening to the wheels and understanding that he had just watched his wife's soul get inventoried.

*Contents unverified.* Maren would have laughed at that — a short, delighted, dangerous laugh, the one she saved for pomposity. *Unverified,* she'd have said. *Twenty years of verification, sir. I verified every one of them myself.*

Now he had seven days to verify them again, alone, from the inside of his own skull.

He lit the lamp on the table. He took paper — good paper, hoarded paper, the stock he kept for the annual reports — and a fresh nib, and he inked it, and then he sat there with the pen poised above the page like a man poised above cold water.

And found that he was afraid of the blankness.

Not of the army. Not of penalties. Of the blankness. Because to write the names was to admit they lived now only in him, and if he wrote one wrong — if he transposed a letter, misremembered a date — then some part of some soul would be lost twice, once to the sea and once to his failing memory, and he did not know if he could bear to be the instrument of that second loss.

The fire ticked in the grate. The kettle began its low murmur toward boiling. Outside, far off, the garrison's night shift hammered at the piers, iron on stone, patient as erosion.

Ansel dipped the pen. And wrote:

*Tobias Vane, lost 14 March 1877, the *Merry Alice*, trawler, swamped off the point in a northeast gale. Twenty-two years old. The Vane boy. His mother pulled his oilskin from the line every spring for six years and finally gave it to young Pellow next door, who grew into it.*

He stared at what he had written. His hand had not hesitated. The date had come up whole, the way a stone comes up out of shallow water, complete, wet, exact. Fourteen March 1877. He had not thought of Tobias Vane in years — decades, nearly — and yet there the boy was, entire, with his crooked grin and his hat too big for him and the way the sea took him within sight of the harbor mouth so that his father had rowed out and found him and carried him home in the boat he'd taught the boy to fish from.

Ansel wrote the next line before he understood he was doing it.

*His father, Josiah Vane, never fished past the point again. Sold the *Merry Alice*'s gear to Pellows. Died 1891, winter, in his sleep. Registered. Decent end, which the sea denied the son.*

Then: *Ellen Pellow, saved 9 October 1881* — and the circumstances came with the name, attached like the tag on a specimen: gig overturned returning from the mainland, the ebb running hard, Ansel out in the station boat alone because Maren was laid up with the fever and couldn't help launch, and Ellen Pellow half-drowned and screaming curses at the sea even as he hauled her over the gunwale, cursing it all the way to shore, alive, gloriously alive, and she'd sent him a goose every Christmas afterward until the year she died.

Page after page. Name after name. The kitchen filled up with lamplight and scratching and the low tide-sound, and Ansel wrote until his wrist ached and the candle guttered, and when he finally stopped and counted, his hand was shaking and there were thirty-one names on four sheets, each one with its date, its vessel, its circumstance, its small human freight — the brother who came asking, the widow who moved inland, the man who never spoke of it, the woman who wouldn't stop speaking of it.

Thirty-one. And the ledger had held — he made himself count backward through the years, made himself walk its pages in his mind the way he'd walked the gallery deck in high wind, hand over hand, certain of every footing — the ledger had held better than two hundred entries across twenty-three years. Saved and lost. Saved and lost. Two hundred souls, give or take, and he had thirty-one.

It should have crushed him. Sitting there at midnight with thirty-one names against two hundred, he waited for the crush to come.

Instead, something else arrived. It came quietly, the way important things do, dressed in ordinary clothes, and it was this:

*He knew where the rest were.*

Not the names themselves — not all of them, not yet — but where they lived inside him. He discovered that his memory of the ledger was not a memory of a book at all. It was a memory of a coastline. Each name had its ground. The lost clustered in the storm years — '77, '84, '91 — dark reaches of the shore where you had to search cove by cove. The saved spread along the calmer decades in long populated strands. Some names stood alone like beacon towers: *Maren Thackeray, saved 1876* — his own wife, pulled from the water the year before they married, the entry that had started the whole ledger, because she'd said, dripping on the beach with his coat around her shoulders, *someone ought to write these down, Ansel. The sea keeps no books. Someone has to keep the other side of the account.*

The sea keeps no books. But he did. He always had. He saw it now — saw that for twenty-three years, without knowing it, he had been doing this very work, every night, every walk along the point, every conversation at the harbor. When old Colley told the story of the *Providence* for the hundredth time, Ansel had not merely listened; he had filed it, dated it, cross-checked it against the weather log. When Mrs. Dunmore mentioned her sister's anniversary, some part of him had quietly confirmed a date against a page. Twenty-three years of tending a light and tending a book, and it turned out the two disciplines were the same discipline: attention. Keeping watch. Refusing to let the dark take what you had once clearly seen.

They could confiscate the paper. They had confiscated the paper. But they could not requisition what he had become in the writing of it.

Ansel rose, stiff, and banked the fire, and stood a moment at the window looking north toward the garrison camp, where the watch fires burned in a ragged orange line and the half-built bridge showed blacker than the night around it. Somewhere over there, in a satchel or a crate or a drawer with a lock, Maren's handwriting sat in the dark, unverified, unread, perhaps already misfiled among stores. Let it sit. Paper slept. What mattered was awake, and walking, and writing by lamplight, and it was him.

He went back to the table and wrote at the top of a fifth sheet, in letters as steady as he could make them:

*Register of persons saved and lost off this point, being a true copy from memory of the ledger of Maren Thackeray, begun by her in the year 1876 and continued by A. Thackeray to the present day. This copy made December 1899, the original having been taken. Every name herein belongs to someone. Let them be accounted for.*

The defiance in it surprised him. It was a small defiance, ink-thin, visible to no one but himself and God and the tide — but it rang in him like a struck bell. For two years his grief had been a private thing, hands busy, heart empty, a man polishing brass while his life went quiet around him. Now the grief had found work worthy of it. Every name he raised from the dark was a blow struck — not against the soldiers, who were mostly boys, not even against Captain Marsh with her careful hands and her closed face, but against the great dull machine that believed a thing ceased to exist when its papers were seized. He would show them otherwise. He would fill five sheets, and ten, and twenty. He would write until the century turned, if that was what it took, and every name would arrive on this side of the divide documented, witnessed, real.

He worked the next four nights as he worked the first: light at dusk, rounds at full dark, then the table, the pen, the rising of the dead and the rescued out of the deep water of memory. By the fourth night he had ninety-six names. Some came easily, swimming up at a touch. Others he had to hunt, and the hunting taught him his own methods: begin with the weather, and the year will follow; begin with the boat, and the family will follow; begin with who wept at the harbor mouth, and the name will come, because grief is the best index ever kept.

Only twice did he truly fail. Once was a fisherman, lost in '88, whose Christian name would not surface — he had the surname, the boat, the widow's face, everything but the one word, and he sat with it for an hour, jaw tight, before writing the surname with a blank space left for the rest, and the blank space hurt worse than any scar he carried. The other failure had a shape he refused to look at directly yet: an entry near the ledger's end, in the last year of Maren's life, in her hand grown thin and looping, that he could feel waiting for him at the bottom of the memory-sea like something standing on the bottom, patient, face upturned. He was not ready. He would be, but not tonight.

On the fifth evening, coming down from the light with the wind rising pleasantly out of the southwest, he found a folded paper wedged under the cottage door, weighted with a stone.

Not army paper. Wrapping paper, brown, torn from a parcel. Inside, in a crabbed, hurried hand he almost recognized:

*Thackery — they say you are making yr list. My husband Peter Cole saved 22nd Aug 1888, capsize off the kelp rocks, you know the date better than I do. Put it down right. Also his brother Samuel, lost same day, and do not let them confuse the two, the army will want one Cole where there were two. — R. Cole, and I am not sorry to sign it*

Ansel stood in the doorway a long time, the paper trembling slightly in the wind and in his hand, reading it three times. *They say you are making yr list.* So it was known. Word had gone out along the harbor the way word always went out, under doors and over fences and in the spaces between women at wells, past the notices and the patrols and the registers, faster than any army could move, because the army had never learned the speed of widows.

He turned the sheet over. On the back, in different hands — three, four different hands, some pencil, some ink, one in what looked like charcoal nub — more names had been added in transit, the paper passed along like a collection plate:

*Martin Drew, saved 1893, his leg broke on the ladder rocks.*
*The Hearn child, 1891. You were there. You know.*
*Ask Widow Tregale re: the two Frenchmen, July '85 — she has the dates.*

Ansel looked up. The point stretched away toward the village, smoke rising straight from a dozen chimneys in the still evening air, and somewhere among those chimneys people were remembering, on his behalf and on their own, and passing the results to his door like coal to a neighbor in a hard winter.

He thought of his blank space — the fisherman of '88 whose Christian name would not come. He folded R. Cole's paper with great care and put it inside his shirt, against his heart, where the important papers went.

Ninety-six names, and counting. A town remembering with him. And somewhere across the water, in a locked drawer, a ledger sleeping in the dark, unaware that it had already been resurrected.

Ansel went in, lit the lamp, sharpened the nib, and began to write. He was three lines into the Coles — *Peter saved, Samuel lost, and God forgive the confusion of anyone who mistakes one brother for the other* — when he heard boots on the path outside. Measured boots. Military. Coming up the point in the dark, stopping at his door, followed by a knock, firm and official, at half past eight on a winter's night.

He set down the pen, capped the ink, and hid R. Cole's paper under the hearthstone before he opened the door.

Captain Elowen Marsh stood in the wind with a lantern raised, and behind her, dim on the path, stood two soldiers — and in her gloved hand, unmistakable in the lantern light, she held a single loose page of faded, looping handwriting that Ansel knew anywhere in the world.

Maren's. A page torn out.
