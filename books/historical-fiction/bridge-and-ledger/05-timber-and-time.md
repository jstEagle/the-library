## 5. Timber and Time

The demolition began on a Tuesday, with the fog still thick enough to drink.

Ansel heard it before he saw it — the groan of old timber giving up its hold, then the flat crack of it coming down, muffled by the mist the way a bell is muffled by deep water. He was climbing the tower stair with his arms full of oiled wicks, and he stopped on the third landing and stood very still, counting. Boathouse first. That would be the boathouse; he knew its voice, that particular long complaint in the joists that had sounded like Maren's name if you listened wrong. Then the smokehouse. Then, he supposed, the net-drying racks that had stood by the shingle since before his father kept this light.

He climbed the rest of the way because the wicks needed oiling and the lamp did not care what fell down in the town below it. That was the arrangement he had made with his own heart these four years: hands above, grief below, and never the two meeting where anyone could see. He trimmed, he polished, he wound the clockwork that turned the great lens, and through the glass he watched three army wagons standing small and grey on the shore road while men moved over the point like ants over a dropped loaf.

By noon the boathouse was a heap of planks and broken slate, already being sorted into burnable and reusable by soldiers who worked with the cheerful efficiency of men who had been told nothing about what they were destroying and had not thought to ask. Ansel walked down past them with his ledger-face on — the blank, civil expression he wore now whenever he dealt with the garrison — and stood at the edge of the rubble of the smokehouse, where Mrs. Prynne's kippers had hung every autumn for thirty years, and where her son's boots had been left to dry one October night in 1887 before he went out and did not come back.

Nobody had asked him about that either.

"You'll want to keep clear, sir," said a corporal, not unkindly. "Falling timber."

"It's already fallen," Ansel said.

The corporal looked at him, decided the remark was just an old man's oddness, and touched his cap. Ansel turned and walked along the shore road toward the last of the outbuildings still standing: the lighthouse workshop, squat and stone-walled at the landward end of the point, where the ground rose toward the causeway path.

* * *

The workshop had been Maren's kingdom more than his.

That was the truth of it, though the deed and the stores-inventory both carried his name. She had claimed it in their second year, when the damp got into the boat shed and she declared — with the particular bright stubbornness that had made loving her feel like standing in weather — that a keeper's wife who could not mend a winch ought at least to have somewhere decent to fail in. He had laughed. She had drawn plans. Within a month there were sawhorses under the roof and drawings pinned to the door of the old store cupboard, and within two years there was a bench along the north wall worn pale where her forearms rested, and wood-shavings in the cracks of the floor that smelled, even now, even in winter, faintly of cedar.

He had not gone inside since the requisition notices went up. He told himself it was because there was work enough at the light. The truth was smaller and worse: he had been afraid of the room. A room is only walls until you stand in it alone; then it becomes everything that happened in it, all at once, without mercy.

But the soldiers were working down the row of buildings methodically, east to west, and the workshop stood at the western end, and so Ansel opened the door that afternoon because the alternative was to watch someone else open it.

Inside, the cold had kept everything. Her drawings were still pinned where she had left them, corners curling, the graphite soft with age. The good saw hung on its pegs. On the bench lay a half-finished model no longer than his forearm: a footbridge, slender and arched, in white pine, its deck planked in strips thin as book-bindings, its rail pierced with a repeating pattern like waves cut through card. He picked it up the way a man picks up a sleeping child.

She had designed it the winter before she died. He knew that much without needing the papers — he remembered her at this bench, lamplight on her jaw, tongue caught between her teeth, telling him that a bridge to the mainland path was not sentiment but arithmetic. *The path floods at spring tides, Ansel. Old Widow Cade can't cross for days sometimes. And if a man goes down on the rocks in January and the tide's in, help has to come the long way round by the cliff road — forty minutes. Forty minutes.* She had tapped the little model with one finger. *Forty minutes is the difference between saved and lost. And I have watched you write the word lost too many times.*

Saved and lost. The ledger's whole vocabulary, reduced to minutes.

He set the model down gently and heard, outside, the sound of another building coming down.

* * *

Captain Marsh found him there an hour later. Ansel heard her boots on the stone step and put himself between the bench and the door without deciding to, the way a man puts himself between a dog and its food.

"Mr. Thackeray." She stood in the doorway with her cap under her arm and a clipboard of some kind against her hip, and her eyes went past him around the room — the drawings, the saw, the model — with a thoroughness he could almost hear, like a scale weighing coins. "You've a fine workshop here."

"You've a fine habit of destroying them."

Something crossed her face — not anger. Something quicker and more private. "The requisition covers all outbuildings within the works perimeter," she said. "The schedule runs east to west. I'd expect your building to come down within the fortnight."

"There are lighthouse stores in it."

"I'm sure there are."

"Rope. Chain. Spare ironwork for the gallery rail. Oakum. Paint stocks worth more than most of what your wagons carry." He heard his own voice go flat and precise, the ledger-voice, each item set down in its column. "The light is a government installation, Captain. Its stores are government property. You're welcome to inventory them, catalog them, and move them wherever suits you — but I'd want that in writing, from your office, before a single plank of this roof comes off. Because if a storm takes the gallery rail in February and the light fails for want of a spare bolt, that failure will be in my logbook. Signed by me. Addressed to Trinity House, the Board of Trade, and whoever signs your pay."

It was a long speech for Ansel Thackeray. He surprised himself with it. What surprised him more was that none of it was a lie — the stores genuinely were there, Maren had kept the inventory herself, in her tidy hand, and half the reason the light ran so well was that everything needed was always within thirty feet of the tower.

Marsh studied him. Up close her eyes were grey-green, the color of the sea before it decides about weather, and they did not blink often.

"You'd make a quartermaster weep, Mr. Thackeray," she said. "Stores for lighthouse use. Very well. The workshop stands until—" she consulted the board, and something behind her eyes did arithmetic he could not see— "the thirty-first of December. Not a day after. The perimeter wall goes through this ground on New Year's Day, and I will not delay a fortification for a storeroom."

"The thirty-first," Ansel agreed.

"And the stores will be removed to a place I designate before then."

"They will be accounted for," he said, which was true, and meant something else entirely, and he did not think she missed it. But she only nodded, put her cap back on, and went down the steps into the fog, and Ansel stood listening to her boots fade until he could no longer tell them from the sound of the sea.

Until the thirty-first of December. Eleven weeks. He leaned against the doorframe and felt the number settle onto his shoulders like wet rope.

* * *

That night, after the lamp was lit and turning, he came back down to the workshop with a candle stub in a tin and Maren's plans unrolled across the bench.

She had drawn the full bridge properly — he had known this too, but knowing and looking are different things. Fourteen feet of span, though the gap to the mainland path wanted nearer twenty at high water; she had solved it with a causeway footing of stacked stone at the near end, dry-laid, that the tide could pass through but boots need not. The arch was shallow, almost a straight camber, built of laminated ribs — pine over oak core — because, she had written in the margin in her quick slanted hand, *there is no oak on this coast worth bending and no money to buy it, so we shall make the wood do what solid wood cannot.* The rail was her extravagance: the wave-pierced pattern repeated all along both sides, purely useless, purely beautiful, and beneath one section, on the drawing, a small annotation he had never noticed before tonight:

*Name here.*

No explanation. Just those two words, in pencil, underlined once.

He sat down on her stool — it was exactly his height, because she had been taller than most women and had adjusted it for herself — and he understood, with the slow cold settling of a tide turning, what the annotation meant and therefore what the bridge meant entire. It was not only forty minutes saved. It was a ledger in wood. Every plank a name. A record the sea could not confiscate.

And the army was going to feed it to a boiler in eleven weeks unless it existed somewhere else first.

He blew out the candle and sat in the dark a moment, listening. The demolition crews would be back at dawn. They worked east to west, and the eastern sky showed no interest in anything yet. So: nights. Nights were his. The lamp took him four hours of every evening and two of every morning, and he slept badly at best — Maren had died in March and his body had never relearned the habit of full nights. There would be time. Barely. The way a drowning man barely has time.

He lit the candle again and went to the timber rack along the south wall, and began, by candlelight, sorting.

* * *

The cutting started two nights later.

He learned quickly what secrecy cost in craft. Work that should have taken an hour took three, done quietly — the saw wrapped in sacking where the blade met the wood, the mallet strikes muffled with leather, the plane strokes timed between gusts of wind so the sound carried out to sea instead of inland toward the barracks. He worked by two candles because one gave a poor judge of grain, and he burned them down to the sockets and pinched them out with his fingers, and the burns on his thumb and forefinger joined the old ones there, a small constellation of the trade.

He started with the pattern ribs, because they were the heart of the thing and the hardest, and if the hard part proved impossible he wanted to know now rather than in December. Maren's laminations called for thin stock — quarter-inch pine over a three-quarter oak spine — bent over a form and glued and pegged. He had no glue worth trusting in the cold. He had pegs, though, hand-cut from blackthorn, and he had patience, which Maren had always said was his one material advantage over the rest of creation.

The first rib fought him. Pine does not forgive a dull blade or a hurried hand, and his hands had spent four years doing nothing finer than winding clockwork and trimming wicks, and they had gone stupid with grief the way unused things do. He ruined two lengths of good clear pine before midnight, and swore once, quietly, in the voice he used for gulls, and then sat down on the stool and held Maren's model in both hands and studied it the way he had studied her face in the last week, memorizing.

Then he sharpened the saw. Then the planes. Then he tried again.

Somewhere past two in the morning the third rib came off the form clean — a fair, sweet curve, springy as a living branch, holding its shape without a single check or split — and Ansel held it up to the candlelight and looked down its length the way he had once looked down the polar axis of a lens, and something happened in his chest that he had thought was finished happening. Not joy. Nothing so simple. More like a lamp being turned up a few threads in a room he'd resigned himself to living in the dark.

He set the rib aside, marked it *No. 3* in pencil on the underside where no eye but his would find it, and reached for the next length of pine.

* * *

The days took on a doubled shape after that. By daylight he was the keeper: logs written, wicks trimmed, brass polished, the garrison's sentries saluted with his ledger-face, the stores inventory copied out fair in his best hand and delivered to Captain Marsh's office as promised, every item listed, every item true, and between the lines of it nothing at all about twenty feet of laminated ribs and pierced railing hidden under tarpaulin and sacking in the western bay of the workshop, under the honest labels *MISCELLANEOUS ROPE* and *BROKEN STONE — BALLAST*.

By night he was something else. A shipwright of one. A man assembling his wife's last sentence, word by wooden word.

He learned the rhythm of it the way he had once learned the rhythm of weather: which nights the wind covered his noises, which hours the patrol passed the workshop on its circuit and how long the lantern-light took to cross the window. He learned to sleep in fragments, two hours here, ninety minutes there, and to wake sharp. He was fifty-eight years old and he had not felt this since he was a boy mending crab pots for the promise of a farthing — this animal greediness for the work, this wanting of the dawn to be further away.

There was a cost, and he paid it without complaint. His supper went uneaten twice a week. His hands split at the knuckles from pine resin and cold. Once, coming up the tower stair at dusk, he miscounted the landings and stood a full minute on the wrong one before his feet corrected what his mind had fumbled, and he gripped the rail and made himself a promise: the light comes first. Always the light first. Maren would have said it herself — she had loved the bridge, but she had married the light same as he had, and she'd have flayed him for letting a plank outrank a wick.

So he kept the discipline. Lamp first, ledger-second — his nightly hour with the reconstructed names, the pages he was rebuilding in a copybook bought in town and kept wrapped in oilcloth under a floorboard — and the bridge third, and sleep last, in whatever crumbs remained.

The copybook worried him more than the bridge did, if he was honest. Wood could be explained away. Rope and ballast, salvage and stores. But pages of names in a careful hand, rebuilt from memory — that was a different cargo entirely, and if it were found, no technicality of stores-inventory would cover it. He thought sometimes of the drawer where he hid it and felt the way he imagined a smuggler feels, except that what he smuggled was not brandy or silk but the dead, and the revenue men were the whole British Army, and there was no cove remote enough.

Still he wrote the names. Still he cut the ribs. By the middle of November he had seven of fourteen, and the forms stood along the workshop's north wall like a row of pale ribs of some great fish that had swallowed the building whole, and Ansel walked among them each night before leaving, touching each one, saying nothing, because there was nothing to say that the wood did not already know.

On the last Friday of the month, he came down from the lamp at half past nine and found, wedged under the workshop door where the frost had frozen it fast, a folded scrap of paper.

He tore it free. Inside, in a crabbed hand he half recognized — a fisherman's hand, one of the Prynne cousins perhaps — were five names, five dates, and a line of detail beside each: *drowned off the Gull Rock, 1871; body never recovered; his mother called him Tam to the day she died.*

No signature. No message. Just names, delivered in secret to a door in the dark, the way provisions reach a besieged city.

Ansel stood in the cold workshop with the paper trembling slightly in his fingers, and out on the point a sentry's lantern swung past the frosted window, and he thought: they know. Somehow they know. The town is writing to me.

And then, closer behind the first thought, quieter and heavier, like a following sea: *and if the town knows what I keep behind these walls, who else does?*

Above the bench, Maren's drawings stirred in the draft from under the door, and somewhere down the coast, far out beyond the Gull Rock, the wind was beginning to rise.
