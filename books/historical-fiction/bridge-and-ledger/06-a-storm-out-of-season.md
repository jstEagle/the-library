## 6. A Storm Out of Season

The glass had been falling for two days.

Ansel Thackeray had learned to read the barometer the way other men read scripture — not word by word but in its whole countenance, the way the needle hesitated at nine hundred and ninety before sliding down through it like a man stepping off a ledge he had long contemplated. Maren used to say the instrument was honest the way a doctor is honest: it told you the truth only after you had already begun to dread it. By the morning of the ninth of November, with the year still shy of its winter and the equinox gales three weeks distant, the needle had settled into territory he associated with wrecks, and the sea off the point had turned the colour of gunmetal left out in the rain.

*A storm out of season*, his father would have called it. The old keepers had a superstition about them — that the sea, cheated of its expected storms by some softness in the summer, came early to collect what it was owed. Ansel did not hold with superstition as a rule. But he had spent thirty years on this rock, and there were debts the water kept books on, and he knew a creditor's knock when he heard one.

He spent the daylight hours the way his hands insisted on: trimming wicks, filling the oil reservoirs until the paraffin sloshed amber and sweet-smelling in the lamp room, testing the clockwork that turned the great lens with its slow patient revolution. He checked every pane of the lantern glass for flaws, running his thumb along each seam the way a man might check a child's forehead for fever. Outside, the wind came in long shoves now instead of gusts, bending the coarse grass flat, tearing white rags off the crests beyond the harbour mouth. The garrison's new works — timber staging, piles of dressed stone, the skeletal beginnings of their fortified bridge — stood grey under the racing cloud, and he watched a sentry's coat snap around him like a flag refusing an order.

They had been on the point since September. Their notices were still nailed to doors. Their requisition ledger — for they had ledgers too, cold ones, ruled and stamped — sat in Captain Marsh's quarters holding everything his own ledger once held, or so he feared. He had not let himself think of Maren's book in three days, because thinking of it was like pressing a bruise to see if it still hurt. It always hurt. But the storm gave him something better than forgetting: it gave him work, and work was the only prayer he trusted.

By four o'clock the light was lit, though the dark would not properly arrive for another hour. He climbed down and ate standing up — bread, cold ham, tea gone lukewarm in its cup — because on nights like this eating was maintenance, not pleasure, and he had long ago stopped pretending otherwise. In the workshop below the tower, Maren's drawings lay where he had covered them with sacking, and the first cut timbers of her footbridge rested on trestles, smelling of resin and sawdust and, faintly, of the future she had imagined for them. He touched the nearest beam once, the way you'd touch a sleeping shoulder, and went back up.

The dark came early and total, a lid closing. And then the sea stood up.

* * *

He had seen storms all his life, but they never grew familiar; they only grew legible. This one spoke plainly from the first: a south-easterly with nothing between the point and the Bay of Biscay to slow it, piling water against the headland in strokes he felt through the soles of his boots. The tower hummed. Not shook — hummed, a low note in the stonework like a held breath, and the rain arrived not as drops but as a horizontal flood, silver in the beam, so that the light seemed to be swimming through weather rather than shining over it.

At half past eight the fishing fleet should have been safely in. He knew their boats — the *Gannet*, the *Two Sisters*, the *Kittiwake* — knew their hull colours and their skippers' habits, knew which of them pushed their luck and which came home at the first shadow on the water. Old Hobbes with the *Gannet* had fished these waters forty years and read the sky better than any barometer. They would be in. They would be in.

At a quarter past nine, through the lull between two seas, he heard it.

Not a horn. Not a gun. A bell — the small cracked handbell the *Two Sisters* carried, ringing not steadily but in bursts, arrhythmic, desperate, the sound of a man ringing with both hands while the deck kicked under him. It came from seaward, from the wrong direction entirely, from the reef line north of the point where the water shoaled over the Tarn Rocks and no boat wanted to be in any weather, let alone this one.

Ansel's body moved before his mind finished the arithmetic. Lamp room. The light was turning, steady, faithful — he had done everything right, and it did not matter, because the *Two Sisters* was not off the point waiting for guidance; she was on the rocks, and a revolving light told a drowning man nothing about where the deep water lay.

There was an arrangement — Maren had drawn it, God help him, she had drawn everything — a fixed low light for the fairway, a second lamp mounted in the gallery's lee to burn steady over the northern channel, so that boats running for shelter in bad weather could hold a course between the point and the Tarns. He had built the mounting two winters ago from her plans and never, in all that time, needed it. The fittings were in the store room. The lamp itself, a small brass thing with a lens no bigger than a loaf, sat wrapped in oilcloth on the shelf above his ledger shelf — above the empty space where the ledger had been.

His hands were sure and his stomach was ice. He carried the lamp up the ninety-one steps with the wind trying to peel him off the stairwell wall at every landing, and in the gallery, crouched below the parapet with the sea coming over in green tons and smashing down again, he bolted the bracket, wired the lamp to the accumulator, trimmed its wick with fingers that should have shaken and did not, and struck the match inside the shelter of his own coat.

It caught. A small warm star, fixed and low, burning out over the black northern channel.

Through the rain he thought he saw it answered — a flare, red and brief, swallowed whole.

* * *

The light failed at twenty minutes to eleven.

He would learn afterward exactly how the mechanics of it worked, would take the assembly apart piece by piece in the days that followed, but in the moment there was only the sound — a change in the tower's hum, a clunk from the machinery far below, and then the great lens stopped turning. Stopped, and the beam froze where it pointed, out over the empty southeast, useless as a signpost in a field. And then the burner guttered. The draught had reversed. Some fluke of the wind, backing suddenly as the storm's centre passed, had driven air down the flue instead of up, and the flame bent double, smoked, and died.

Darkness. Total, absolute, the darkness of the inside of the earth. Ansel stood in the lantern with the rain hammering the glass and felt the century drop away beneath him — every keeper before him, every night, all of it gathered into this single minute in which the light was out and the sea was full and somewhere behind him men were drowning in the noise.

Eleven minutes. That is what the record would eventually say, written in someone else's hand, in someone else's book. Eleven minutes. He knew it differently. He knew it in matches — seven of them, cupped, spat out by the wind. He knew it in the climb down and the climb up, in the valve he bled and the airway he cleared with a wire brush and his bare arm, in the second when the flame took properly and roared upright and the lens began, blessedly, horribly slowly, to turn again.

Four of those eleven minutes the small fixed lamp in the gallery had burned alone — one small star against the whole roaring dark — and it was those four minutes, not his great restored beam, that mattered. Because when the light came back and he swept the glass with his eyes across the black water, he found the *Two Sisters* not on the Tarn Rocks at all but in the northern channel, aground on the outer spit where the sand shifts yearly, heeled hard over, her mast gone, and six heads in the water among the broken water — five, no, six — and a line of men on her hull clinging like barnacles, working toward the bow, toward the shallower water, toward the small fixed light that said *this way, this way, the deep water is not here.*

He could not launch alone. No man could. But he could burn everything the tower had.

He fed the lamp like a starving thing — doubled the wick, opened the draught past every safe mark — and dragged every spare lamp, every signal flare, every hurricane lantern from the store onto the gallery rail until the point wore a crown of fire. And below, in the town, in the garrison, other lights began to answer. Windows. Torches on the new staging. The rocket apparatus cart, its wheels screaming on the wet stone of the road, hauled by soldiers who had drilled for this on dry summer grass and now learned what a rope weighs when the wind is a fist.

They got the breeches buoy lines to the spit by one in the morning. Ansel watched it all from the gallery, unable to leave the light, forbidden by his own conscience from leaving it, while the rockets arched gold and dying over the water and the men of the *Two Sisters* came ashore one by one inside the canvas breeches, swung through the surf like parcels, dropped, hauled, carried up the shingle. Five of the six. The sixth — young Tam Ellery, seventeen years old, whose name was not yet in anyone's ledger — was pulled from the shallows near the spit by two fishermen wading chest-deep into breaking seas, coughing seawater, alive, alive, alive.

By three o'clock the wind had backed and steadied. By dawn the sea was still furious but honest again, and the *Two Sisters* lay on the spit with her belly open to the sky, and six exhausted men sat in the garrison cookhouse drinking tea that shook in their cups, wrapped in army blankets, and Ansel Thackeray climbed down from a tower he had not left for nineteen hours and stood in the workshop doorway with his hand on the frame because his legs were not entirely his own.

* * *

They came for him at noon. Not soldiers with rifles — worse. A clerk, with a board, and Sergeant Doyle with his red, competent face, and behind them both the tall figure of Captain Elowen Marsh, her collar wet from the morning's inspection round, her expression giving away precisely nothing.

"Keeper Thackeray." The clerk opened his board. "The station log records an interruption of the light between twenty-two forty and twenty-two fifty-one hours last night. Duration, eleven minutes. You are required to provide a written account of the failure and its causes within forty-eight hours."

"A written account," Ansel said. His voice sounded like someone else's, scraped raw by salt wind and shouting. "Six men came off that boat alive."

"The account, Keeper." The clerk's pencil hovered. "Cause of failure."

"Draught reversal. The flue choked and the burner drew down. I cleared it and relit." He looked at Doyle, at Marsh. "You want to log something, log the four minutes the small light burned alone over the channel while your rocket crews were still finding their boots. Log that it was lit at all. There's no mounting on any naval chart for a light there. I put it up myself, last night, in that."

For the first time, something crossed Captain Marsh's face — quick, quickly mastered. Her eyes went to the gallery above, to the small brass lamp still bolted where he had fixed it, its glass streaked with salt, and stayed there a moment longer than procedure required.

"The account will note the failure regardless," the clerk said. "It goes into the garrison record."

"Then put it all in," Ansel said. "Put in the eleven minutes and put in the six men. Your record ought to be able to carry the weight."

Doyle wrote something down. Marsh said nothing at all. But as they turned to go, she paused at the workshop door, and her gaze travelled — briefly, professionally, he told himself, and yet he felt it like a hand on his skin — across the sacking-covered trestles, across the smell of fresh-cut timber, across the shavings still bright on the floor.

"You'll want to secure that door properly," she said quietly. "Weather like this gets into everything."

She walked on. Ansel stood in the doorway a long time after they were gone, listening to the sea spend itself against the point, and understood with perfect clarity that whatever she had seen or suspected, the next knock on his door would not be a clerk with a board.

It would come soon. He had eleven minutes' worth of reasons why.
