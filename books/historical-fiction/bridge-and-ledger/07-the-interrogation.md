## 7. The Interrogation

The summons went out under her own signature, which struck Elowen Marsh as a small absurdity: she had come to this point to take inventory of boats and barrels and able-bodied men, and instead she found herself composing, in her careful engineer's hand, an order for a lighthouse keeper to present himself at the garrison office concerning *irregularities in the operation of the navigational light*. Eleven minutes. The failure log said eleven minutes, and the fishing crew of the *Damaris* said their lives had hung in the space of them.

She had read the report four times. It was thin. Lamp mechanisms failed; that was the nature of lamp mechanisms — carbonized wicks, fouled oil lines, glass crazed by salt. But something in the margins of the thing bothered her the way a splinter bothers a finger: you could not see it whole, only feel it catch. The keeper's written account was precise about the mechanics and silent about everything else. Eleven minutes, no explanation of cause, no note of distress. As though he had simply recorded what happened and declined to say why, the way a man declines to explain a scar.

She set the paper down and looked out the window of the requisitioned harbormaster's office, which still smelled of its former occupant — pipe smoke and tar — beneath the newer smells of ink and boot polish. Below, the town lay under its first hard frost, chimney smoke standing up straight in the windless cold. And beyond the harbor wall, on its black promontory, the lighthouse stood with its painted band dull white against a colorless sky, patient as something waiting.

*Come now*, she thought, *let us see what you are made of.*

She told herself it was procedure. She was very good at telling herself things were procedure.

* * *

He came at the appointed hour precisely, which she noted without surprise. Men who lived by tides and lamps did not run late; lateness was a luxury of people whose mistakes did not drown anyone. Ansel Thackeray filled the doorway the way weather fills one — not imposing, exactly, but total. He was taller than she'd registered, thinner too, with the particular leanness of a man who ate because the body required it rather than because meals interested him. His hands were enormous and gentle-looking, scarred across the knuckles, and they hung at his sides like tools set down carefully.

"Captain." He did not salute. She hadn't expected him to; he was civilian, and besides, there was something in his face that suggested saluting was one more ceremony the sea had long since burned off him.

"Mister Thackeray. Sit."

He sat. The chair was too small for him — garrison furniture, built for clerks — and he folded himself into it with the unhurried competence of a man boarding a small boat. He looked at her directly. His eyes were pale grey, the color of the winter sea an hour before snow, and they held nothing she could read. No guilt. No fear. That itself was information, though she wasn't yet sure of what.

"You know why you're here."

"The light failed." He said it flatly, a fact laid on the table like a coin. "Eleven minutes. I've written it in the log."

"I've read the log." She let the silence do some work. Outside, a soldier called something to another, boots on frozen gravel. "What the log does not tell me is why."

"The burner assembly fouled. Salt in the oil line, ice in the feed. I cleared it and relit."

"That's the mechanism. Not the reason." Elowen leaned forward. "You keep that light, Thackeray. You have kept it, by my count of the station records, for nineteen years. In nineteen years there is no prior failure longer than ninety seconds. Then, in the same month that my engineers find your station ledger missing pages — pages you were ordered to preserve — your light goes dark over a working crew. I am not a superstitious woman, but I am a suspicious one, and those two facts sitting next to each other look less like coincidence than like arithmetic."

Something moved behind his eyes then — not fear. She would learn later that she might never see fear in that face; whatever replaced it in him was older and quieter. It looked almost like grief deciding whether to be anger.

"You think I let my light fail," he said slowly, "on purpose."

"I think it is possible that a man who resents the army taking his town, his neighbors' homes, and his wife's private papers might allow his attention to drift from his duty. Or that a man might arrange matters so that the army's presence here looks costly. Failure makes arguments, Thackeray. I have used it myself."

For the first time he smiled, and the smile was terrible — not cruel, but worn through, like fabric gone threadbare at the elbow. "You think the dark was an argument." He turned his hands over in his lap, studying the palms as if the answer were written there in the callus. "Captain, do you know what was on that water the night the light failed? Four men. Tom Hedley and his brother-in-law, two boys from up the coast, and a lad called Wren who isn't eighteen. When the light went out, they were coming home in the dark past a point that has drowned better sailors than any of them. If I wanted to argue with the army, Captain, I would not use living men as punctuation."

The room was very quiet. The stove ticked as it cooled.

"Then tell me how it happened," she said, "in detail. Because your log doesn't."

And he did. He talked with the same unhurried precision he'd shown in the doorway, but underneath it she began to hear something else — a depth of familiarity with the machinery of the light that approached intimacy. He described the oil line's failure the way other men described a friend's illness: symptoms, history, temperament. Ice had gotten into the feed reservoir — how, he couldn't say; perhaps driving spray through the ventilator, perhaps a cracked seam he hadn't found yet. The line had choked. The burner had guttered. He'd gone up with tools and hands he knew blind, in the dark, and cleared it, and the eleven minutes were the eleven minutes between darkness and flame, and every second of them had been spent with his fingers in the guts of the lamp.

"If there's a crack in the reservoir seam," he finished, "I haven't found it. Which means either it doesn't exist, or it's somewhere I'll find it on the worst night of the year."

"And which do you believe?"

"I believe in finding things before they find me." He looked at her, and for a moment the flat calm of him shifted, and she saw down into something vast and cold and careful. "I'll strip the whole feed system in January. I'd have done it sooner, but someone has been requiring me to write out lists of names."

The words landed between them like a dropped tool.

Elowen sat back. She kept her face still — she had learned long ago that her face, with its sharp bones and cooler coloring, frightened subordinates when it went expressive, so she had trained it into neutrality — but inside her chest something performed a small, involuntary calculation, the kind she did when a load figure didn't match the sum of its parts.

*Names.*

"There were forty-one names in that ledger," she said. "When it was confiscated, it was complete. My clerk catalogued it. Forty-one entries in a fine, consistent hand. Yet when the document was transferred to divisional archive, the receiving officer noted the binding broken, the final signatures loose, several leaves unaccounted for." She rose, walked to the window, and spoke to the frost on the glass. "Pages go missing from documents all the time, Mister Thackeray. Usually near the end, usually where the writing is most recent. Usually because someone wants them."

"Do they." His voice gave away nothing.

"They do." She turned. "So here is my question, and I will ask it once, and I ask you to consider that your answer determines whether this remains an irregularity or becomes an offense." She let the pause stretch until the stove ticked again. "Are you rebuilding it?"

The question hung in the lamplight. She watched him take it, watched him weigh it — not whether to lie, she realized, but something else entirely. He was deciding how much truth she could carry. She had seen officers make that calculation about maps and ammunition counts. She had never been on the receiving end of it.

Ansel Thackeray looked down at his great scarred hands for a long moment. When he looked up, his face had settled into something she could only call honest, and it was worse than any evasion.

"Every name in that book," he said quietly, "is a person. Not a number. Not an entry. A man who came off the rocks with the skin gone from his palms and my wife sewing them with sailcloth needle. A woman who waited in this harbor office — this room — while we dragged for her son's boat. A boy buried above the point with a wooden marker because there was nothing else to bury." He unfolded himself from the chair and stood, and did not loom over her, though he could have; he simply stood, the way a tree stands. "You took the book. Fine. You're the army; you take what you like. But the names aren't in the book, Captain. They're in me. They're in the widows on the hill and the fishermen who won't meet your soldiers' eyes. You can confiscate paper. You cannot confiscate remembering."

The room seemed smaller and larger at once. Elowen became aware of her own heartbeat, steady and traitorous.

"That is not an answer to my question," she said.

"It's the only one I have."

They regarded each other across the clerk's desk, and Elowen Marsh understood, with the clarity she usually reserved for structural failures, that she stood at a junction. On one rail lay her duty: report him. Reconstructing a confiscated document was obstruction at minimum, possibly sedition given the invasion panic that had sent them all here. One paragraph in her evening dispatch and the man would be removed, the light staffed by garrison soldiers who could not read a tide table, and the matter closed. Clean. Correct. Her orders fulfilled down the line, her record unblemished, the point cleared by spring as instructed.

On the other rail lay — she did not know what. Something in his face. Something in the phrase *you cannot confiscate remembering*, which was either the most dangerous sentence she'd heard since arriving or the truest.

"Sit down, Mister Thackeray," she said, and heard her own voice come out differently than she intended. Quieter.

He sat.

She opened the folder before her and drew out the catalogue sheet her clerk had prepared: forty-one entries, transcribed, cross-referenced against parish rolls. Forty-one names in Maren Thackeray's hand — she had examined that hand herself, alone, at night, turning the confiscated book under a lamp: the letters upright and generous, the numerals graceful, a hand that had taken pleasure in forming itself. Beside each name, in the original, small annotations. Dates. Boats. *Saved, the 9th of March.* *Lost with the* Sweet Alice. *Survived; died the following winter anyway.* She had read those annotations three times and had not been able to say why.

"Forty-one names," she said. "My clerk transcribed all of them. The army's interest in this document ended with the transcription. The original sits in a crate in the divisional archive at Corvenna, and will sit there until some junior officer misfiles it, which is the fate of everything the army archives." She met his eyes. "Which means that if a man wished to reconstruct a document nobody is actively guarding, no one would stop him. Provided his light never failed again, and provided no such reconstruction ever crossed my desk. Do you understand me?"

His brow furrowed — the first genuine uncertainty she'd seen on him. "You're letting me go."

"I'm declining to manufacture a case. It's different."

"Why?"

And there it was: the question she could not answer. She heard herself begin — *the light is critical to garrison shipping, and replacing you midwinter would be operationally unsound* — and stopped, because it was true and insufficient, the way a bridge that holds is not the same as a bridge that is sound. The real reason stood in front of her with frost-melt on his shoulders and his dead wife's handwriting in his memory, and it had something to do with the fact that in nineteen years he had asked the army for nothing while giving it a working light, and she had arrived and taken his book.

"I don't know," she said.

The honesty of it startled them both. Ansel Thackeray studied her for a long moment, and whatever he found in her face, it seemed to satisfy some test of his own, because something in his shoulders eased — not trust, nothing so generous, but a kind of truce between watchfulnesses.

"My wife used to say," he offered, rising, pulling his coat closed, "that a ledger tells you what a place is worth. Not in money. In names." At the door he paused, half-turned. "The light won't fail again, Captain. I'll find that crack if it's there to find. That part you can put in your report."

He left. His footsteps faded down the corridor, unhurried, and the outer door thumped shut, and the cold came in briefly behind him like a witness.

* * *

She did not write the report that night.

She sat at the harbormaster's desk with the blank dispatch form squared before her, her pen uncapped, the ink well open, and wrote the heading — *Report of interview, keeper A. Thackeray, re: light failure of the 14th* — and then nothing. The stove burned low. Frost feathered up the windowpanes, and somewhere below, the lighthouse beam swung its slow arc across the water, steady as a heartbeat, sweeping past her window once every fourteen seconds. She found herself timing it. Fourteen seconds of light, moving on; fourteen seconds of returning. A rhythm like breathing. Like a page being turned, and turned again.

*You cannot confiscate remembering.*

Her orders were clear. Clear orders were the whole architecture of her life; she had climbed from a provincial childhood through the engineers' college — one of three women in her intake, the only one to commission — on the strength of exactness. Give her a specification and she would meet it. Give her a boundary and she would hold it. Duty presented itself to her, always, as a kind of virtue, and she had never had cause to examine the foundation until now, sitting in a stolen office with a pen full of ink and nothing to say.

Because here was the fault line, and she could trace it plainly: the keeper had confessed, in substance, to defying a lawful requisition. And the ledger — she had read it, all forty-one entries — was not evidence of unregistered persons. She had known that within pages of opening it. It was a register of rescues. Of wrecks. Of the drowned and the nearly-drowned and the saved-by-someone's-hands, kept in a widow's careful script because someone had believed names mattered, that a town should be able to count its losses in persons rather than rumor. The army had confiscated a gravestone and filed it as contraband.

And she had signed the requisition order.

Elowen capped the pen. She took the dispatch form, with its single written line, folded it once, and set it in the drawer beside her copies of the demolition schedules — the outbuildings slated to come down through December, workshop after workshop, the point scraped clean for her fortifications. Her finger paused on the schedule as she closed the drawer. *Thackeray — workshop and stores shed — review prior to clearance.*

Review. There would have to be a review. Procedures existed; she was good at procedures.

She put on her coat and went out into the freezing dark, past the sleeping barracks, down through the empty streets to the harbor wall, and stood looking at the point. The beam swept past. Fourteen seconds. The sea breathed against the rocks below her, black and enormous, and she thought about four men on the *Damaris* feeling their way home in the dark, and about a man with his fingers in the guts of a lamp, and about a hand — upright, generous — writing *saved, the 9th of March* by candlelight, and she could not, for the life of her, locate the enemy in that arithmetic.

Behind the lighthouse, half-hidden in the shadow of the stores shed, she thought she saw movement — lantern-light where no lantern should be, at an hour when a keeper should be asleep. It guttered and vanished, quick as a struck match cupped from the wind.

Elowen stood a long while in the cold, watching the place where it had been, and did not go to look.
