## 8. What Maren Built

The first beam went down on a Tuesday, in the grey hour before dawn when the garrison's sentries changed watch and the point belonged to no one but the gulls.

Ansel had measured the space a hundred times with his eyes and twice with his tape, kneeling on the frost-stiffened grass between the workshop's back wall and the rocks where the mainland path began its climb away from the cove. Forty-one feet of nothing — a gap of tussock grass and loose stone that could be crossed in a minute by a strong man walking carefully, or in three minutes by a frightened one carrying something heavy, or never at all by someone hurt and bleeding and waiting for help that came too slowly because there was no way to bring the help to them quickly.

Forty-one feet. Maren had written the figure in her sketchbook in her upright, patient hand, underlined once, as though she were daring the distance to argue.

He worked alone, which was the only way it could be done now. The timber he'd cut in the autumn lay stacked under sailcloth behind the workshop: seasoned oak for the sills, larch for the decking, a length of greenheart salvaged from an old jetty that he had bought off Tam Pellowe for the price of two bottles of brandy and the promise never to say where it came from. The army inventory recorded none of it. The army inventory recorded, officially, a quantity of lighthouse stores requiring dry storage until December the thirty-first, after which date the structure known as the keeper's outbuilding would be razed and the ground incorporated into the approaches of His Majesty's fortified bridge.

His Majesty's bridge would carry guns and gun-carriages across the mouth of the cove. Maren's bridge would carry people.

Ansel set the first sill into the trench he had dug the night before, tamping the shingle beneath it with the flat of a spade until the oak sat level as a sermon. Then he knelt, laid his palm flat against the grain, and said, to no one, to the one person who might still be listening from somewhere beyond weather and years:

"Well. Here we start."

* * *

She had come to the point in the spring of 1874, a cartographer's daughter with ink stains she never quite scrubbed from her knuckles and a way of looking at coastlines as though they were arguments waiting to be settled. He was twenty-six and already keeper, already solitary, already so accustomed to his own silence that her voice on the first evening sounded like a foreign language — quick, amused, asking questions he didn't know how to answer. Why was the light lit before dark? Why did the fog signal sound in patterns rather than one long moan? Why did the path to the mainland road take forty minutes when the distance was barely a mile?

Because of the cliffs, he'd told her. Because of the way the land fell away between the point and the high ground.

That's not an answer, she'd said, laughing. That's just the same question wearing different clothes.

They were married by the August. He learned, over eleven years, all the shapes of her: the way she drew while she talked, filling the margins of letters and bills and the backs of seed packets with little precise sketches — the lamp mechanism, the gulls, the curve of the cove seen from the gallery rail; the way she woke before him in winter and stood at the window watching the light sweep the water as if counting its heartbeats; the way grief sat on her, in the year after the news came from Falmouth, not loudly, not dramatically, but like ballast — low, permanent, altering how everything moved.

Her brother. Corwin. Ansel had met him exactly once, a big easy-going man with their mother's long face, home from the sea for a fortnight and full of stories. The *Providence Girl*, a coaster out of Falmouth, had gone onto the ledges near his own harbour town in February '75, in a south-westerly that gave the crew perhaps twenty minutes between the first grounding and the breaking up. Twenty minutes is nothing, if help can reach you; it is an eternity, if it cannot. Corwin Marsh — no relation, the name is common enough on that coast — had been pulled from the water alive, they later learned, had made it to the foot of the cliff below the town, broken-legged, half-drowned, calling up at the houses above him. And the houses above him could hear him, could see him in the surf-torchlight, and could do nothing quickly, because the only way down to that beach was the winding cliff stair, three hundred steps, which in that wind and that dark took the rescue party nearly an hour to descend with their gear. He was alive when they reached him. He died while they carried him back up.

Maren had read the letter from her mother at this very table, in this very room, with the lamp trimmed low. Ansel had watched her fold it along its creases, once, twice, three times, until it was a small hard rectangle she could have closed her fist around. She hadn't wept. She had said, in a voice he would hear for the rest of his life: *It shouldn't take an hour to cross a hundred feet.*

He hadn't understood, then, what she meant. He understood later, when the sketchbooks began.

* * *

The second beam was heavier than the first, and he was older than he liked to admit, and by the time he had it seated and checked against his spirit level the light was coming up properly and he had maybe an hour before the garrison's working parties stirred and someone might walk the path behind the workshop out of idle curiosity. He sat on an upturned crate, hands raw, and let himself remember — because the remembering was the work too, now. He had learned that in the long nights since the requisition. The ledger had been taken, but memory was a kind of timber: it held if you jointed it right.

Maren's design was not a grand thing. She had never intended it to be. She called it — he found the phrase in her sketchbook after the funeral, in a note tucked between drawings of scarf joints and handrail profiles — *a mercy bridge*. Not for carts, not for cattle, not for soldiers. For a stretcher, carried at a run. For a lantern party going down to the water without having to pick their way through wet rock. For the minutes that decided whether a man lived.

Every element of it answered some failure she had catalogued in her neat hand, as though she were preparing evidence for a court. The width — four feet six inches — so two bearers could walk abreast with a stretcher between them. The handrails, double-height, close-spaced, so a man hauling himself along in a gale had something to grip at every point. The gentle camber, draining spray inward to channels rather than letting it sheet the deck. The non-slip battens, spaced to the stride of a running man. She had even specified the colour — a flat, unromantic grey — *so that it does not glimmer in a rescuer's torch and distract the eye from the water.*

And at the far end, where the mainland path climbed away toward the road, she had drawn a small square platform with a bench. In the margin beside it, in pencil that had gone silvery with age, she had written: *for whoever is waiting.*

Ansel read those words more times than he would ever confess. For whoever is waiting. She had been the one waiting, all those years of marriage — waiting whenever he went down to the boat in bad weather, waiting whenever a wreck came onto the ledges and the keeper's duty pulled him toward it. She knew both ends of every rescue: the hands in the water and the hands on the rail. Her bridge served them both.

She had begun cutting stock for it in the summer of '86. He remembered the sound of her saw in the evenings through the workshop wall, irregular, unhurried, a woman working at her own pace on her own project while he polished brass and filled log columns and did, he understood now, the smaller part of keeping people alive. He had teased her about it once, gently — *you're building a bridge to nowhere, my girl; there's naught at the other end but gorse* — and she had not laughed, which was unlike her. She had looked at him with an expression he could not read then and could read now, thirty feet above his own knees, in the grain of the third beam as he guided it home.

There is always somebody at the other end, Ansel. That's the whole trouble with coasts.

She died in the November of '93, quick and unfair, a fever that burned through her in four days while he rode for the doctor and back twice across that same forty-minute path, and he had thought of it even then — the path, the minutes, the distance she had spent years measuring against death — and had hated it, and had been unable, in the numb months that followed, to touch her tools. They had hung on their pegs like sleeping birds. The sketchbook had stayed shut. The stack of cut timber had waited under its sailcloth through four winters, patient as she had been patient.

Now the army wanted the workshop gone, and the deadline had done what grief could not. It had put a date under the work. And Ansel Thackeray, fifty-one years old, widower, keeper of His Majesty's Light at Gullwrack Point, discovered that he could be moved to treasonous carpentry by the same force that moved most men: the fear of running out of time.

* * *

By noon the frame's first bay stood true: sill, beam, brace, all fastened with draw-bored mortise and tenon, the oak pins whistled home with a beetle borrowed — permanently, he supposed — from Tam Pellowe. It was joinery of a kind the army's engineers would have scorned, all hand-cut, no iron except where iron had to go, and yet it would outlast their prefabricated lattice-work by a century, and he knew it, and the knowledge warmed him like a stove.

He heard footsteps on the path behind the workshop and did not turn, because turning would look like guilt, and guilt was a thing he had decided he would not perform.

"You've been busy, Keeper." Sergeant Hale's voice, flat with suspicion. "Thought you kept a light, not a shipyard."

"I keep whatever keeps," Ansel said, straightening, wiping his palms on his apron. "Lighthouse stores want staging. You're welcome to inspect the inventory."

Hale inspected, which meant he looked at timber and saw only timber, saw only an old man's clutter, and wrote nothing down, because there was nothing in the regulations about oak. Ansel watched him go with a face like a shut door, and behind the door he was doing arithmetic: five bays remaining, thirty-one days, weather permitting, strength permitting, luck permitting.

Luck. He looked at the little platform site at the far end, where the bench would go. For whoever is waiting. He wondered whether Maren had ever guessed that the one who would wait longest, and finish last, and stand at the end of her bridge holding the lantern, would be him.

In the afternoon a knock came at the cottage door — Mrs. Tregose the postmistress's widow, breathless from the climb, with a scrap of paper in her mittened hand. "My sister-in-law's boy," she said. "Lost off the ledges in '81. You'll not have forgotten him. He was twelve." Ansel took the paper. On it, in a child-uncertain hand copied from some old parish record, was a name and a date, and he felt the ledger rebuild itself inside him, plank by plank, name by name.

He was laying out the second bay's mortises by lamplight that evening when he saw, through the workshop's salt-fogged window, a figure standing out on the path above — a tall figure in an officer's greatcoat, motionless in the wind, watching the workshop with the particular stillness of someone counting things.

Captain Marsh. Come at dusk, alone, without her sergeant.

Ansel set down his marking gauge, left the lamp burning plainly in the window — hiding it would be worse than showing it — and waited for the knock.
