## 10. Orders to Demolish

The orders came up from the south on the morning mail sloop, wrapped in oilcloth and smelling faintly of the engine room — coal smoke and machine oil, the smell of the new century arriving early. Elowen Marsh took the packet into her office at the requisitioned customs house, set it on the desk beside the lamp, and did not open it at once.

She had learned, in eleven years of service, to read a packet's weight before its contents. This one was thin. Thin packets were never good. Thick ones contained surveys, returns, the ordinary sediment of administration. Thin ones contained decisions already made by men who had never seen the place those decisions would unmake.

She slit the wax with her penknife and read.

*By direction of the Chief Engineer's Office, Southern District: schedule of demolitions revised. Owing to acceleration of bridgehead works and anticipated spring mobilization, all outbuildings on the point designated for clearance shall be demolished no later than 15 December. This supersedes prior instruction of 3 November.*

The list followed. The fish-curing shed. The old net store. The Cooper's cottage, roofless these six years. And fourth on the list, typed in the same indifferent ink as all the rest:

*Thackeray workshop (designated: lighthouse stores annex) — clearance by 15 December.*

Mid-December. Eleven days from today.

Elowen read it twice, then a third time, as though repetition might alter some clause she'd missed. It did not. The words sat on the page with the flat permanence of type, each letter identical to its neighbours, nothing in them that could be argued with or softened. That was the nature of printed orders. They arrived like weather.

She rose, went to the window. Below her, the town lay under its first proper snow, roofs whitened, chimneys standing up their smoke into a windless grey sky. Farther out, past the last of the houses, the lighthouse held its white column against the pale morning, and below it — she knew exactly where, though from here it was only a dark smudge among other dark smudges — stood the workshop.

She thought of Thackeray's face when she had served him the first notice in November. Not anger. She had prepared herself for anger; she had received it often enough, from farmers and harbourmasters and vicars, and knew its grammar. But Ansel Thackeray had only gone very still, the way a man goes still when something long-feared finally puts its hand on his shoulder. As if the paper confirmed something he had known since the day his wife died.

And now the date had moved. The army wanted its bridgehead, and the point between here and spring was simply an obstacle in the way of concrete and steel, and everything on it — every shed, every wall, every ledger of names — would be cleared like scrub before planting.

She sat down again and drew the requisition forms toward her.

There was a procedure. There was always a procedure. She could file an objection citing the annex's designation as stores, request a surveyor's assessment, note that the lighthouse keeper required covered storage through winter — and every one of those filings would be correct, defensible, and futile. Objections went south. Decisions came north. The channel between them swallowed nuance the way the sea swallowed boats.

Her pen hovered over the acknowledgment line.

Here is what Captain Elowen Marsh believed, and had built eleven years upon: that duty was not a garment one put on and took off but a spine, load-bearing, invisible, and that a soldier who bent it once found it bent forever after. Her father had been a man who kept his word even when keeping it ruined him; she had inherited the belief along with his jaw and his habit of waking before dawn. Orders were how an army remained an army rather than a mob with rifles. Every concession to sentiment was a crack, and cracks propagated.

She signed.

Her signature came out smaller than usual, cramped at the end, and she looked at it with distant distaste, as one looks at a thing done by another hand. Then she countersigned the distribution copies, sealed what needed sealing, and gave the packet to Corporal Voss for delivery, and by half past ten in the morning the whole business was finished and there remained only the rest of the day to get through, which she did the way she got through all days: inventories, rosters, the interminable arithmetic of supply that filled her ledgers — *her* ledgers, clean and impersonal, columns that asked nothing of her but accuracy.

At four o'clock the light began to fail. At five, full dark.

She told herself she would stay in. There were reports to draft. The stove needed less wood if a man sat still, and she had developed a soldier's talent for sitting still. But at seven she found herself pulling on her greatcoat without having decided to, and letting herself out the back of the customs house into air so cold it tasted of iron, and setting off along the cliff path with no destination she was willing to name.

* * *

The snow had crusted since noon and held her boots with a dry squeak. Overhead the sky had scoured itself clear, and the stars came down to the horizon without shame, thousands of them, the kind of sky no city man saw twice in a lifetime. The lighthouse beam swung its slow metronome above her — flash, and dark, and flash — and she timed her walking to it without meaning to, four strides to a revolution, the rhythm of a coast that had kept its own time for centuries before anyone thought to fortify it.

The path curved past the last cottages. Past the boat slip, where the fishing fleet lay hauled up and shrouded for winter, their masts bare and black against the snow. Past the boundary stone where, by the terms of the requisition, military jurisdiction began and the town's ended — a line drawn by clerks across living ground.

Beyond the stone, the wind found her. It came off the sea flat and searching, working through the seams of her coat, and she turned her collar up and walked on because turning back now would mean admitting she had come out for something, and she had not yet admitted that even to herself.

The workshop stood at the head of the slip, a low stone building with a slate roof furred white. Dark. As it should have been, at this hour, in this cold. She stopped perhaps forty paces off, in the shadow of the net store, intending — she supposed — only to look at it. To fix in her mind the shape of the thing she had condemned. That was a discipline she practised: whenever she signed an order that would cost someone, she made herself see what it cost. Her first posting, a young lieutenant, had wept after clearing a tenant farm in Ireland, and an older captain had told him: *Never sign blind. Sign with your eyes open, or you'll become the sort of officer who signs anything.* She had taken it as gospel. She was looking at the workshop now with her eyes open.

That was when she saw that the door stood ajar, and light leaked from within — a thin yellow seam, trembling slightly, candlelight or a shaded lamp.

Someone was inside.

Her first instinct was procedural. Curfew on the military zone ran from dusk; unauthorized entry to a designated structure was an offence; she should knock, identify herself, note the name. Her hand had actually begun to rise toward her collar, toward the whistle she carried, before some other instinct — quieter, slower, wearing the same uniform but marching out of step — stopped it.

She knew who it would be. There was no one else it could be. And she found that she did not want to catch Ansel Thackeray at anything. She wanted, with a hunger that startled her, to know what he was doing.

She crossed the remaining distance without sound, boots finding the snow's crust the way she'd learned to find it on night manoeuvres, and put her eye to the gap of the door.

* * *

The interior had been transformed.

The workshop of November, which she had glimpsed once during initial survey — a cluttered, grieving place, benches under dust-sheets, tools hung like a mothballed ship — was a ship no longer mothballed. Dust-sheets folded and stacked. Floor swept. And down the centre of the room, where workbenches had been shoved against the walls, lay the beginning of something she could not immediately name.

Timber. Good timber, oak, far better than a stores annex required — stacked, sorted, marked in chalk. Across two trestles lay a beam perhaps twelve feet long, and along its edge ran a row of shapes cut and chiselled with geometric precision: notches, teeth, interlocking cavities, each one fitted to its neighbour in a chain of wooden promises. Beside it, a second beam waited, its cuts half-finished, a paring chisel resting in the last notch like a pen laid down mid-word.

Above the bench hung drawings. Not army drawings — she knew army drawings, orthographic, soulless. These were drawn in a fine, sure hand, ink faded brown with age, annotated in a small slanted script with measurements and, in the margins, small notes that were not measurements at all. She could read some from where she stood. *Let the rail sit proud — hands need something to hold.* And lower, on the sheet showing the full elevation: a footbridge. Slender, single-arched, crossing — she traced the ground beyond it through the window-shape of the drawing — the gully below the workshop, the black rain-gash that separated the point from the mainland path. The gully that flooded at every spring tide, that forced the townsfolk to walk three miles round by the road when the sea came up. The gully that, she now understood with a cold settling in her stomach, had very likely figured in more than one of the deaths recorded in a confiscated ledger she had locked in her own office cabinet.

Ansel Thackeray stood at the trestle with his back to her, working the second beam. He worked slowly, deliberately, the way she imagined a monk copied scripture — each cut preceded by a pause, a marking, a checking against the drawing above. His coat hung on a nail by the stove; the little iron pot-belly threw a low red warmth across the floor, and the smell of oak shavings and coal smoke was so particular and domestic that it reached her through the door-gap and did something to her chest she refused to examine.

He was building her design. That was the truth of it, plain as joinery. Maren Thackeray — the wife, dead these three years, whose handwriting filled the ledger Elowen had seized as evidence of unregistered persons — had drawn this bridge, and the husband was building it by candlelight, in secret, against the clock Elowen herself had just shortened by a fortnight.

She should announce herself. She should log this. Unauthorized construction on requisitioned ground, materials of undeclared provenance, a structure that would stand — she measured the span with a surveyor's eye — squarely athwart the army's access route for the bridgehead works. Any competent officer would seal the workshop tonight. Any honest one would report it tomorrow.

She stood in the snow and watched him check a cut against the drawing, blow the shavings from the notch, run his thumb along the grain the way a man touches a face he knows by heart.

She thought about the ledger. She had read fragments of it — professionally at first, cataloguing the document, then less professionally, unable to stop. *Saved, March '91: the Gracie boy, eleven years old, swept from the herring net. Lost, October '88: Tomas Aune, going for the doctor, the gully in flood.* Tomas Aune. Going for the doctor. Three miles round by the road, over ground this bridge would cross in two minutes flat. She had written that name into her catalogue in her neat clerk's hand and slept badly after, and had told herself the sleeplessness was indigestion.

Now she stood watching the answer to that entry take shape under a widower's chisel, and understood that she was looking at the most illegal thing on the point and the most righteous thing she had seen in eleven years of service, and that they were the same object.

The stove ticked. The beam swung overhead, visible through the small salt-hazed window — flash, and dark, and flash — and in each flash the shavings on the floor glowed briefly gold, and went out.

Thackeray paused. Set down the chisel. Stood a moment with his head bowed over the beam, not praying, she thought, but listening — as if the wood had something to tell him and he had all night to hear it. Then his shoulders squared, and he reached for the chalk, and wrote on the beam's hidden face a mark she could not read from the door.

A name. She was almost certain it was a name.

She stepped back from the door.

The cold rushed back into her, thorough and moral. Snow was beginning again, fine and dry, ticking softly on her shoulders, erasing her bootprints behind her even as she watched. By morning there would be no evidence she had stood here. That fact presented itself to her not as misfortune but as an arrangement — offered by the weather, accepted without negotiation.

She walked back along the cliff path with the beam sweeping above her, four strides to a revolution, and made herself think it through like a soldier.

If she reported the bridge, it would be destroyed. Not stored, not documented — destroyed; the demolition teams were behind schedule and took their frustrations out in kindling. If she did not report it, and it was discovered anyway — and things on this point had a way of being discovered; a sergeant had already noted timber moving where timber shouldn't — then it would be discovered with her knowledge unrecorded, and concealment was a career-ending word in the service, and worse than that, it was a crack. She had felt the spine bend tonight, actually felt it, the way one feels ice flex underfoot a half-second before deciding whether it holds.

And yet her feet had carried her away from the door. Her hand had come down from the whistle. Whatever she decided in the morning, the decision tonight had already been made by her body, quietly, without consulting her rank.

At the boundary stone she stopped and looked back once. The workshop window showed its thin line of gold. The lighthouse turned above it, patient, indifferent, keeping faith with every boat on the water regardless of whose flag flew over the shore. She thought: *that is the difference, and I have been on the wrong side of it.*

Then she went home to her clean ledgers, and sat before the stove a long while without lighting it, and in the dark she drafted, mentally, three separate reports — and burned all three the way the snow was burning her footprints, and slept, or something near it.

In the morning there would be eleven days left, a sergeant asking questions about moving timber, and a widower at his bench who did not yet know that the woman sent to end his work had stood in the snow outside his door and, saying nothing, become its accomplice.
