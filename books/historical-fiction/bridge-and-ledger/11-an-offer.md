## 11. An Offer

The knock came at half past nine in the evening, and Ansel knew before he opened the door that it would be her.

There was a quality to Captain Elowen Marsh's presence that carried across a room, across a yard, across the dark space between the lamp tower and his cottage — a stillness that was not quiet but *attention*, as though she stood always at the center of some instrument of her own devising and everything that moved did so within her field of measure. He had watched her cross the point in the weeks since the requisition, and she never hurried and never dawdled; her stride was a sentence with every word accounted for.

He opened the door. Wind came in behind her, smelling of salt and cut pine from the demolition works down the shore, where the Hendersons' boat shed had been reduced to a skeleton of grey timber that afternoon. She stood with her cap under her arm, her hair pinned tight, and behind her the night was clear and cold enough that the stars seemed to have been counted by someone official.

"Mr. Thackeray," she said. "May I come in?"

"It's my house," he said. "You've already got the ledger."

Something crossed her face at that — not guilt, he thought, or not quite; more like a note made against herself. She stepped over the threshold.

* * *

The cottage was warm in the way his cottage was always warm: the stove banked low and steady, the kettle within reach, everything squared away as though a boarding officer might inspect it at any hour, which for eleven years had been Maren's habit and for two had been his. He poured tea without asking. She took the chair opposite him, the one that still pulled slightly to the left on its uneven leg because he had never fixed it, because Maren had said it gave the chair character and he could not bring himself to plane the leg now even though he could fix it in four minutes with the tools three steps away in the workshop.

She looked at the room the way she looked at everything — taking inventory. He let her. There was nothing here to find. The reconstructed pages were not beneath the floorboards or sewn into the mattress; they were in the workshop, wrapped in oilcloth inside the false back of Maren's plan chest, and if Elowen Marsh knew that — and he believed, sitting there watching her eyes travel the shelf above the stove, that she suspected it the way a compass suspects north — she had not yet proved it.

"You've been burning lamp oil past your allotment," she said.

"I have."

"The garrison issues oil by the gallon against recorded hours. You're drawing nearly double."

"I'm an old man who doesn't like the dark," Ansel said. "Charge me what you like."

She turned her cup a quarter-turn on its saucer. Her hands were steady, well-made hands, ink-stained at the side of the right forefinger where a pen rested too long. Outside, the wind worried the eaves.

"Mr. Thackeray," she said, "I am going to speak plainly, and I'd ask you to hear me out before you answer, because I won't offer twice."

Ansel folded his own hands around his cup and waited.

"Your ledger was confiscated under standing orders regarding unregistered persons. That classification was applied by a clerk in the quartermaster's office who read the title page and nothing else." A pause, precise as a mark on a chart. "I have read more than the title page."

The fire ticked in the stove. Ansel kept his face still, but something in his chest went tight and cold, the way it did when the light guttered and his hands were already moving before his fear caught up.

"There were fragments," Elowen went on. "Pages separated from the binding during cataloguing. Entries dated back thirty years. Names, dates, weather, tide. And in the margin of one page, in a different hand — yours, I think — a notation: *"Pulled four from the water below the point. All lived.""*

"Maren wrote the book," Ansel said quietly. "I only ever added the weather."

"No," Elowen said. "I think you added the names."

* * *

He set his cup down. His hands were not entirely steady now and he saw that she saw, and he found he did not much care.

"Say what you've come to say, Captain."

"My orders are to clear this point by spring and deliver a full accounting of all persons, documents, and structures to garrison command. In that accounting, a privately maintained register of names — unverified, unsanctioned, containing the particulars of persons the Crown has no record of — is a liability. To me, and to you." She leaned forward slightly. "But I am also empowered to classify documents in the field when their evidentiary status is ambiguous, pending review. A review can take months. It can be delayed by any number of administrative necessities. The winter, for instance. The storms. The demands of the new bridge works."

"And in exchange?"

"You give me the ledger. The whole of it. Whatever you have rebuilt, whatever pages remain hidden — I know they exist, Mr. Thackeray; I have watched you carry oil to a workshop that stores nothing but rope. You surrender them voluntarily, tonight. They go into garrison custody under my signature, classified as records pending review, sealed, untouched. No clerk reads them. No order destroys them. They sit in a locked drawer in my office until the review concludes — and reviews of that kind, properly managed, do not conclude quickly."

Ansel sat very still. For one treacherous moment the offer looked like rescue: Maren's book safe, sealed, *untouched*, spared the fire or the pulping or the slow bureaucratic erasure that happened to confiscated papers all along the coast. Safe in a drawer, like a body in a tomb.

Then he heard what she had actually said, and the moment passed.

"In a drawer," he said. "In your office. Under lock. Where nobody reads them."

"Where nobody has to read them."

"And what happens to the names, Captain? Do they stop being dead because the paper is in a drawer? Do the widows on the point stop coming to ask me whether I remember the date the *Cormorant* went down, because the Crown has filed it?" He shook his head slowly. "You're offering to bury them deeper and call it mercy."

"I am offering to keep them from being destroyed."

"They cannot be destroyed." It came out of him with more force than he intended, and he let it stand there in the warm room between them, ringing. "That's the thing you don't understand yet, Captain Marsh, and I think you're beginning to, which is why you're sitting in my kitchen at nine o'clock at night making bargains instead of sending a sergeant with a warrant. Those names are not in the book. The book is just where we keep them so we can find each other."

Elowen's face did not move. But her thumb had gone to the rim of her cup and stayed there, pressing lightly, and Ansel thought: *there. There is the crack.*

"Explain that to me," she said.

* * *

He rose and went to the window. Below the cottage, past the black shape of the workshop with its one shuttered window, the sea worked at the base of the point with its old patience. The great fortified bridge the army was building stood off to the east, a confusion of scaffolding and caisson lamps, iron bones in the dark. Two bridges, then, on this coast now: theirs of iron and command, and hers —

"Twenty-two years ago," Ansel said, "before I took the light here, I was a fisherman out of Harrow Quay. My brother-in-law — Maren's younger brother — was Tomas. Tomas Vane. He drowned off a headland very like this one, forty minutes from help, because there was no path quick enough and no boat launched fast enough and no light bright enough. He was nineteen." He heard Elowen's breath change. "Maren was seventeen. She never spoke of it in the first ten years we were married, and then she never stopped speaking of it. Every name in that ledger is a name she wished someone had been able to write down alive instead of dead. Some of them are saved. More than you'd think, given this coast. She used to say the sea takes its tithe but the light argues about the amount."

"And the ones it takes anyway?"

"Are owed remembrance," Ansel said. "Not storage. Remembrance. There's a difference, Captain, and it is the whole difference. Your drawer keeps the paper. It doesn't keep the thing the paper is *for*. When the Henderson girl comes to my door in February and asks when her father was lost, because she was two years old and wants to put flowers on the right day — the answer lives in a living memory or it lives nowhere. If I hand you those pages, the town loses them. They become evidence. Evidence belongs to whoever holds the case. Memory belongs to everyone who carries it." He turned from the window. "I will not make your report easier to write. I will not hand over the dead of this point to be filed in the same office that nailed notices to their doors."

Elowen was silent for a long moment. Then she said, with a care that surprised him, "If command learns you've rebuilt a confiscated document — and they will learn it, Mr. Thackeray; sergeants notice lamp oil and midnight candlelight same as I do — the consequences will not fall on me. Reconstruction of seized materials is obstruction. It could mean detention. It could mean the workshop goes first, tomorrow morning, and everything in it."

"Then you'll have a busy morning," Ansel said.

"Why?" It was almost gentle, and that gentleness frightened him more than any threat. "Why does it matter so much, that it's worth this? The names are remembered by you. You said yourself the memory is in the carriers. Write them a fair copy. Give me the original. What difference—"

"Because a copy is a copy," Ansel said. "And because—" He stopped. He had not meant to say the true thing. He stood there at the window with the sea behind him and the captain's clear grey eyes on him, and he said it anyway, because she had come alone and unarmed and offered a bargain instead of a search, and some debts were paid in truth. "Because Maren's hand is on those pages. Thirty years of her hand. The last entry is in her writing and she was dead four days later. If I give them up, I give up the last thing on this earth her fingers touched, and I will not do it, Captain. Not for safety. Not for the workshop. Not even to keep the peace. You may put that in your report."

Elowen Marsh looked at him for a long time. The wind rose outside and fell again, the way wind does when it is deciding.

"I haven't written my report," she said at last. She stood, took her cap from the table, and set it on. At the door she paused, her back to him, and said: "The demolition schedule for the outbuildings is under review. Reviews, as I said, do not conclude quickly. I'd advise you not to waste the time."

The door closed behind her. Ansel stood in his warm kitchen and listened to her boots fade down the frozen path toward the garrison lights, and he understood two things at once, and they did not fit together: that she had come to take the ledger and had left having taken something else instead — his measure of her, and perhaps hers of him. And that whatever mercy she had just extended, she had extended it against her own orders, alone, in the dark, and would deny it if asked.

He banked the stove, took down the lamp, and went out into the cold toward the workshop, where under oilcloth the true ledger lay with its rebuilt pages and its thirty years of Maren's hand, and beside it, waiting under shavings, the first cut timbers of her bridge.

The night was clear. From the east, faintly, came the sound of the army hammers driving iron into the sea.

Three days later, the barometer began to fall.
